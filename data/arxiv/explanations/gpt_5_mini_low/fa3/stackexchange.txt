\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### How does the local softmax in FlashAttention-3 guarantee exact equivalence to full softmax?
Question:
The paper states that FlashAttention-3 uses a local softmax with running rowwise maxima and sums, similar to FlashAttention-2, and remains exact (no approximation) while avoiding materializing S and P in HBM. I’ve seen the high-level argument in FA-2, but I’m struggling to reconstruct the exact invariants used here when pipelining and overlapping GEMMs with softmax. Specifically: when the algorithm updates per-row running values m_i and l_i across blocks j, how is numerical equivalence preserved given the interleaving of WGMMA, exponentials, and rescalings? Could someone provide a concise derivation showing that the streamed computation still produces O = softmax(QK^T)V exactly, i.e., that the blockwise updates of m_i and l_i maintain the same normalization as the full softmax computed at once?
Answer:
Short answer: FlashAttention-3 is algebraically identical to computing the usual row-wise softmax followed by the weighted sum with $V$, because it maintains the exact running normalization invariant used in FlashAttention / FA-2. The pipelined GEMM/softmax interleaving only reorders independent work; it does not change the arithmetic recurrence that produces the final normalized outputs. Below I give the concise invariant and a one-line induction that shows the streamed (blockwise) updates produce the same final $O = \mathrm{softmax}(QK^T) V$ as doing the full softmax at once.

Set-up and notation (single output row). Fix a single row of $Q$ (index $r$) and consider its scores against keys split into $J$ blocks. Let $S^{(j)} \in \mathbb{R}^{B_c}$ be the vector of raw (scaled) scores for that row coming from block $j$ (these are the rows of the matrix blocks $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}$ in the paper). For brevity denote elementwise exponent by $\exp(\cdot)$ and rowwise max of block $j$ by $a_j = \max_k S^{(j)}_k$. Let the global row max across all blocks be $M = \max_{0\le j<J} a_j$.

Goal: show that after processing all blocks the algorithm computes
$$
O = \frac{ \sum_{j} \exp(S^{(j)} - M) V^{(j)} }{ \sum_{j} \sum_k \exp(S^{(j)}_k - M) }
$$
which is exactly the row of $\mathrm{softmax}(QK^T) V$ (the row-normalization factor $M$ is the usual numeric-stability shift).

Invariant maintained after processing blocks up to index $j$. For each prefix $0..j$ the algorithm maintains three quantities for this row:
- $m_j = \max_{0 \le t \le j} a_t$ (the running rowwise max),
- $\ell_j = \sum_{0 \le t \le j} \sum_k \exp(S^{(t)}_k - m_j)$ (the running sum of exponentials, shifted by the current $m_j$),
- $O_j = \sum_{0 \le t \le j} \sum_k \exp(S^{(t)}_k - m_j) V^{(t)}_k$ (the running scaled output accumulator).

These are exactly the variables called $m_i$, $\ell_i$, and the blockwise-accumulated $\mathbf{O}_i$ in the paper. Note two important points: (1) $\ell_j$ and $O_j$ are always expressed relative to the current shift $m_j$, so when $m$ increases we must rescale previous $\ell$ and $O$ by $\exp(m_{old}-m_{new})$. (2) The per-block contribution is always formed as $\widetilde{P}^{(j)} = \exp(S^{(j)} - m_j)$ (where $m_j$ is the updated max after seeing block $j$).

One-step update (algebraic identity). Suppose after processing blocks $0..(j-1)$ we have $(m_{j-1}, \ell_{j-1}, O_{j-1})$ satisfying the invariant. Now receive block $j$ with block max $a_j$ and score vector $S^{(j)}$. Define
$$
m_j = \max(m_{j-1}, a_j).
$$
Then compute the per-block unnormalized probabilities shifted to the new $m_j$:
$$
\widetilde{P}^{(j)}_k = \exp(S^{(j)}_k - m_j).
$$
The correct algebraic updates for the running sum and accumulator are
$$
\ell_j = \exp(m_{j-1}-m_j)\,\ell_{j-1} \;+\; \sum_k \widetilde{P}^{(j)}_k,
$$
$$
O_j = \exp(m_{j-1}-m_j)\,O_{j-1} \;+\; \sum_k \widetilde{P}^{(j)}_k \, V^{(j)}_k.
$$

Why these are exact. They follow from pulling the common factor $\exp(-m_j)$ out of each exponential. Write the full contribution of previous blocks expressed with the new shift $m_j$:
$$
\sum_{t\le j-1}\sum_k \exp(S^{(t)}_k - m_j) = \exp(m_{j-1}-m_j)\sum_{t\le j-1}\sum_k \exp(S^{(t)}_k - m_{j-1})
= \exp(m_{j-1}-m_j)\,\ell_{j-1},
$$
and similarly for the $O$ accumulator:
$$
\sum_{t\le j-1}\sum_k \exp(S^{(t)}_k - m_j)\,V^{(t)}_k = \exp(m_{j-1}-m_j)\,O_{j-1}.
$$
Adding the new block's terms $\sum_k \exp(S^{(j)}_k - m_j)$ (and the corresponding $V$-weighted sum) yields the update formula above. This algebra is exact (no approximation): the rescale factor $\exp(m_{j-1}-m_j)$ exactly converts the old normalization into the new one.

Final result. After processing all $J$ blocks the invariant gives $(m_{J-1}, \ell_{J-1}, O_{J-1})$ with $m_{J-1} = M$ and
$$
O_{final} = O_{J-1}, \quad \ell_{final} = \ell_{J-1}.
$$
The normalized output row is then computed as $O_{final} / \ell_{final}$, which equals
$$
\frac{ \sum_j \exp(S^{(j)} - M) V^{(j)} }{ \sum_j \sum_k \exp(S^{(j)}_k - M) },
$$
i.e. exactly $\mathrm{softmax}(QK^T)$ applied rowwise and multiplied by $V$.

Why pipelining / overlapping doesn’t break this. The two-stage (or ping-pong) execution in FlashAttention-3 interleaves GEMMs, exponentials, rescalings and the RS-GEMM accumulation, but it preserves the data dependencies in the invariant updates: each block's $\mathbf{S}^{(j)}$ must be reduced to a per-block $\widetilde{P}^{(j)}$ and its row-sum before its contribution is merged into $(\ell,O)$ after $m$ has been updated. The implementation explicitly waits for the needed WGMMA to complete before performing the corresponding softmax update or rescaled accumulation (see the wait/commit points in Algorithms 1–2). Thus the arithmetic sequence that produces $\ell_j$ and $O_j$ is identical to the algebraic recurrence above; overlap is only between independent operations (computing $\mathbf{S}_{next}$ while finishing the $O$ accumulation for the current block), so the algebraic result is unchanged.

Numerical considerations. The paper emphasizes keeping certain intermediates at higher precision (e.g., FP32 accumulators for sums and for softmax rescaling) so that the streamed algorithm matches the numerical accuracy of the non-streamed fused algorithm. Algebraic equivalence shown above is mathematical; to keep the same floating-point rounding behavior in practice you must ensure the same precision/ordering for the critical operations (the implementation choices described in the paper do this: rescaling factors and accumulators are kept in the precision that preserves the intended numerical result).

Summary. The streamed local-softmax in FlashAttention-3 is exactly equivalent to full softmax because it enforces, for each row, the invariant that after processing each block $j$ you have the correct running shifted max $m_j$, the running shifted exponential sum $\ell_j$, and the running shifted weighted sum $O_j$, with exact algebraic update rules involving the rescale factor $\exp(m_{old}-m_{new})$. Pipelining and overlapping only reorder independent work; they do not change these algebraic updates, so the final $O = \mathrm{softmax}(QK^T) V$ is produced exactly (modulo the usual floating-point rounding, which the implementation controls by precision choices).

### What exactly is being overlapped in the 2-stage pipeline, and what depends on what?
Question:
In Algorithm 2 (consumer warpgroup), the main loop overlaps the WGMMA for Q_i K_j^T with the softmax derived from S_next, and also overlaps the WGMMA for P_cur V_{j-1}. I’m confused about the dependency ordering: which waits guarantee correctness, and which steps are safe to execute without waiting? Could someone annotate the loop with a dependency graph indicating when S_cur, P_cur, S_next, and O_i are valid, and why it’s safe to compute softmax for the next block while the previous P_cur V_{j-1} is still in flight?
Answer:
Short answer: nothing magical is being guessed away — the 2-stage loop explicitly preserves all true data dependencies by waiting exactly where a value is consumed, while exploiting hardware asynchrony so independent work can run concurrently. Concretely, the algorithm keeps two “in-flight” objects in registers/shared memory: the score block $S$ (call it $S_{\text{cur}}$) whose softmax produces $\tilde{P}_{\text{cur}}$, and the next score block $S_{\text{next}}$ computed by a WGMMA. It issues the two WGMMA calls (the $QK$ GEMM that produces $S_{\text{next}}$ and the $\tilde{P}_{\text{cur}} V$ GEMM that accumulates into $O_i$) without waiting for them, but then performs explicit waits before using their outputs. Because each consumer waits for the specific producer it depends on, correctness is preserved while unrelated work overlaps.

Here is a compact, ordered dependency picture of one main-loop iteration (index $j$) in the notation of Algorithm 2, with the invariants that are waited for explicitly in the code:

1) Before the loop body for iteration $j$ starts the algorithm already has in registers:
   - $S_{\text{cur}} = Q_i K_{j-1}^\top$ (computed by the previous iteration's WGMMA and now available),
   - $\tilde{P}_{\text{cur}} = \mathrm{exp}(S_{\text{cur}} - m_i)$ (computed in the previous iteration's softmax), and
   - possibly the in-flight WGMMA producing $\tilde{P}_{\text{cur}} V_{j-1}$ (issued but not yet completed).

2) Within iteration $j$ the code issues two asynchronous GEMMs (WGMMA) in this order:
   - Issue A (line 8 in Alg. 2): start WGMMA to compute $S_{\text{next}} = Q_i K_j^\top$. The code "commit but do not wait". This produces $S_{\text{next}}$ when it completes.
   - Issue B (line 10): start WGMMA to compute contribution $\tilde{P}_{\text{cur}} V_{j-1}$ and add it to $O_i$. The code "commit but do not wait". This produces an update to $O_i$ when it completes.

3) After issuing both GEMMs the code waits for exactly what it needs in the right order:
   - Wait for the completion of Issue A (the $QK$ WGMMA) before computing the softmax for the next block (line 12). So the softmax depends only on $S_{\text{next}}$ and thus waits for that WGMMA to finish. At that point $S_{\text{next}}$ is valid and $\tilde{P}_{\text{next}}$ can be computed deterministically from it (and the running $m_i, \ell_i$).
   - The softmax computation uses only $S_{\text{next}}$ (and the current $m_i,\ell_i$ internal scalars) — it does not read or depend on the $\tilde{P}_{\text{cur}} V_{j-1}$ GEMM that is still in flight. Therefore it is safe to execute softmax while Issue B is executing.
   - Only after computing $\tilde{P}_{\text{next}}$ (and updating $m_i,\ell_i$ and rescaling $O_i$ as per the local-softmax bookkeeping) does the code wait for Issue B to finish (line 13) before using/normalizing the portion of $O_i$ that depended on $\tilde{P}_{\text{cur}} V_{j-1}$.

To summarize with a small timeline per iteration $j$ (left = earlier):

Issue A: start $QK_j$ -> $S_{\text{next}}$ (async, on Tensor Cores)
Issue B: start $\tilde{P}_{\text{cur}} V_{j-1}$ -> partial $O_i$ (async, on Tensor Cores)
Wait (Issue A) -> compute softmax on $S_{\text{next}}$ -> produce $\tilde{P}_{\text{next}}$ (uses MFU/exponent hardware)
Wait (Issue B) -> finish and rescale/add to $O_i$
Shift: $S_{\text{next}}$ becomes $S_{\text{cur}}$ for next iteration

Why this is safe (intuitive + technical):

- Dependency locality: softmax for block $j$ depends only on $S_{\text{next}} = Q_i K_j^\top$ (and the scalar $m_i,\ell_i$ state). It does not depend on the $\tilde{P}_{\text{cur}} V_{j-1}$ GEMM result. Thus the softmax can begin as soon as $S_{\text{next}}$ is ready, regardless of whether the earlier RS-GEMM that updates $O_i$ has completed. The algorithm enforces that by explicitly waiting for the $QK$ WGMMA before softmax and waiting for the $\tilde{P}V$ WGMMA before consuming its updated $O_i$ pieces.

- Hardware asynchrony separation: Tensor Cores (WGMMA) are asynchronous and the special-function unit (MFU) that computes exponentials and reductions is distinct. By issuing WGMMA calls without waiting and then running softmax, the implementation leverages the different hardware units to run concurrently. The correctness does not rely on the concurrency — it only relies on the explicit waits before a value is consumed.

- Explicit waits preserve semantics: the code contains the required wait points: wait for the $QK$ WGMMA before computing softmax (line 12), and wait for the $\tilde{P}V$ WGMMA before rescaling/using that partial $O_i$ (line 13). Because those waits are present, no value is used before it exists and numerical invariants (the running max $m_i$ and normalization $\ell_i$) are updated in the correct order.

- Register buffering resolves the dataflow: $S_{\text{next}}$ is kept in registers (or a private buffer) after its WGMMA completes so the softmax can compute $\tilde{P}_{\text{next}}$ without clobbering anything. $\tilde{P}_{\text{cur}}$ is also held (or its register-formed accumulator layout is prepared) so Issue B can be launched. After softmax completes and $\tilde{P}_{\text{next}}$ is produced, the algorithm copies $S_{\text{next}}\to S_{\text{cur}}$ and proceeds to the next iteration.

A minimal dependency graph you can imagine is: for each $j$ there are nodes $S_{\text{next}}(j)$ and $P_{\text{cur}}(j)$ and $O\_update(j)$. Edges are:
$S_{\text{next}}(j) \to$ softmax $\to \tilde{P}_{\text{next}}(j)$;
$\tilde{P}_{\text{cur}}(j) \to$ RS-GEMM $\to O\_update(j)$;
QK WGMMA for $j$ must complete before softmax of $j$; RS-GEMM for $j$ must complete before rescaling/consuming that contribution to $O_i$. There is no edge from RS-GEMM($j$) -> softmax($j$) (they are independent) so they can overlap.

Finally, note the bookkeeping around $m_i$ and $\ell_i$ (the running row max and accumulated row-sum) enforces the numerically correct incremental softmax normalization despite the interleaving. The code updates $m_i$ and $\ell_i$ only after the waited QK WGMMA for the current score block, and rescales $O_i$ only after the waited $\tilde{P}V$ GEMM is finished; these waits ensure the algebra of the local softmax fusion remains identical to the sequential fused algorithm in FlashAttention-2.

In short: the pipelining is possible because producers and consumers that the softmax needs are different from those the RS-GEMM needs; the algorithm issues both GEMMs asynchronously, waits only for the specific GEMMA it needs to compute softmax, and later waits for the $P V$ GEMM before using its result. That preserves correctness while overlapping otherwise idle cycles on different hardware units.

### How does pingpong scheduling across warpgroups ensure the MFU work (exp) is hidden under Tensor Core GEMMs?
Question:
The paper claims that using bar.sync to force GEMMs in warpgroup 1 to run before GEMMs in warpgroup 2 leads to the softmax of warpgroup 1 being overlapped with GEMMs of warpgroup 2, and then the roles swap. Could someone explain in more detail how this ordering is enforced in practice, given that NVCC and the scheduler may reorder? For instance, what exact barriers and instruction placements are required so MFU-bound work ends up concurrently issued with WGMMA, and how is fairness between the two warpgroups maintained to avoid starvation?
Answer:
Short answer: pingpong scheduling enforces ordering by splitting the CTA into two warpgroups (producers/consumers) and using explicit synchronization points (bar.sync) together with the hardware’s asynchronous GEMM/TMA semantics (WGMMA/TMA commit+wait) so that the Tensor Core GEMMs from one warpgroup are issued and run while the other warpgroup executes its MFU-heavy softmax (exp etc.). The key is that WGMMA and TMA are asynchronous: issuing a WGMMA is a non‑blocking operation that the HW schedules on Tensor Cores, while the thread can continue issuing non‑WGMMA instructions. By inserting bar.sync barriers and careful commit/wait pairs around the async WGMMAs, the implementation forces the GEMM issue-order across warpgroups (so that one group’s GEMMs are in flight while the other performs softmax), and the producer/consumer circular buffer plus the symmetric pingpong pattern guarantees fairness (no starvation).

How this works in practice (intuitive + concrete):

1) Rely on async semantics of WGMMA/TMA, not speculative scheduling. On Hopper the WGMMA instruction is asynchronous: the warpgroup issues it and execution proceeds on Tensor Cores independently until the warpgroup later performs a wait (or executes a dependent op that forces completion). Likewise, TMA loads are issued asynchronously and are completed later. This lets one warpgroup have outstanding Tensor Core work while another executes scalar/vector work (softmax/exponentials) on the MFU and CUDA cores.

2) Partition warps into warpgroups with distinct roles and resources. FlashAttention-3 uses warp-specialization: some warps are consumers (doing the GEMMs and accumulating outputs) and others are producers (issuing TMA loads, doing in-kernel transposes, possibly packing FP8). setmaxnreg is used so the warpgroups that primarily do WGMMAs get more registers; the warpgroups that do only TMA/softmax use fewer. This separation reduces interference and makes instruction streams predictable per warpgroup.

3) Use commit/wait and explicit bar.sync to order GEMM issuance across warpgroups. The consumer mainloop in the paper issues two asynchronous WGMMA calls per iteration: one computes $S = Q K^T$ (call it $GEMM0$) and another computes $P V$ ($GEMM1$). To hide softmax cost, the code does the following pattern across two warpgroups (A and B), repeatedly:

- Warpgroup A: issue $GEMM0_A$ and $GEMM1_A$ (WGMMA async commit), then hit a bar.sync that ensures these GEMMs are issued before wargroup B issues its GEMMs.
- Warpgroup B: after the bar.sync, issue its $GEMM0_B$ and $GEMM1_B$.
- Meanwhile, after issuing its GEMMs, warpgroup A performs its softmax (exp/rowmax/scale) and other MFU-limited work. Because A’s GEMMs are already in flight on Tensor Cores, the Tensor Core execution overlaps with A’s softmax which runs on MFU/CUDA cores while B’s GEMMs are executing on Tensor Cores.

Concretely, the implementation emits (a) explicit commit instructions when issuing WGMMA, (b) a lightweight wait only when results are needed, and (c) bar.sync between the two warpgroups at the points shown in the pseudocode so the GEMM issue order is serialized across warpgroups. The paper’s Algorithm 2 shows the positions: the first WGMMA of iteration $j$ (line labeled code:first_wgmma) is commit-but-do-not-wait; then the code executes other work (softmax for the previous or next iteration), then waits for the WGMMA when its results are used.

4) Preventing compiler reorderings. NVCC and the optimizer can reorder instructions, which would break the carefully designed concurrency. The practical fixes used (and summarized in the paper) are: emit lower-level PTX/SASS sequences or use compiler intrinsics that map to commit/wait/bar.sync exactly; introduce explicit data or control dependencies (for example via commit/wait pairs and register/SMEM accesses) so the assembler does not illegally reorder the WGMMA and the MFU code; and verify the generated SASS to ensure the intended ordering. The paper reports inspecting SASS and finding the overlapped code generated as expected (Section “Compiler reordering”). In other words, you either rely on carefully chosen intrinsics that NVCC respects or drop down to inline PTX/SASS for the synchronization-critical sequences.

5) How fairness and no-starvation are achieved. The pingpong pattern is symmetric and lockstep across iterations: each warpgroup alternately issues GEMMs then performs softmax while the other group’s GEMMs are in flight. Because both groups advance the same number of iterations and synchronize via bar.sync and the circular SMEM buffer (the pipeline object that tracks buffer stages and uses commits/waits when loading/releasing SMEM stages), neither group can outpace the other indefinitely. The circular buffer’s stage-count $s$ and the matching of release/consume notifications enforce backpressure: a producer warpgroup can‘t overwrite a buffer stage until the consumer releases it, so the producer will block (or wait) if the consumer lags — that prevents starvation of the consumer. Thus progress is enforced by the producer/consumer buffer protocol plus the alternating bar.sync ordering; in practice this produces a steady pingpong where GEMMs and softmaxes overlap and both warpgroups make forward progress.

6) Intra-warpgroup pipelining and register management. Inside a warpgroup we also pipeline across iterations ($2$-stage in the paper) so that a GEMM for iteration $j+1$ is issued (commit, do not wait) while the softmax for iteration $j$ is computed; then we wait for the GEMM when its results are needed. This requires extra registers to keep $S_{\text{next}}$, etc., and is balanced against register pressure. Proper register allocation (setmaxnreg) and small per-thread buffers are used to avoid spilling, which would otherwise kill the overlap and predictability.

Put succinctly: the hardware provides asynchronous WGMMA/TMA semantics; bar.sync plus explicit commit/wait create cross-warpgroup serialization points that force GEMM issuance ordering; the circular buffer and symmetric pingpong pattern ensure neither warpgroup is starved; and careful use of PTX/SASS intrinsics and register allocation prevents the compiler from breaking the intended instruction order. That is exactly what the FlashAttention-3 implementation does (see Algorithms and the “Pingpong scheduling” and “Compiler reordering” paragraphs), and the ablation/measurements in the paper show these mechanisms yield the reported MFU/GEMM overlap and throughput improvements.

### What are SS-GEMM and RS-GEMM in Algorithm 1 and how do they map to WGMMA operand placement?
Question:
Algorithm 1 labels two GEMMs as SS-GEMM and RS-GEMM, with a note that SS or RS indicates whether the first operand is sourced from shared memory or registers. In Hopper’s WGMMA model, operands can come from SMEM or RMEM depending on instruction variants. Could someone clarify the operand layouts and sources for these two GEMMs, and how this choice affects register pressure and shared memory traffic for the QK^T and PV products respectively?
Answer:
Short answer: SS-GEMM means the first GEMM in the consumer loop uses a Shared-memory first operand (S) and a second operand from registers (S), i.e. both operands are read as SMEM-sourced tiles (but in practice the shorthand in the paper denotes “first operand from SMEM or RMEM”). RS-GEMM means the second GEMM uses its first operand from Registers (R) and its second operand from Shared memory (S). Concretely for the attention inner loop in the paper, the $QK^T$ product is implemented as an “SS-GEMM” in the pseudocode (the $Q$ tile and the $K$ tile are both read from on‑chip memory; $Q$ typically comes from an RMEM copy of the loaded $Q$ tile while $K_j$ is in SMEM), whereas the $P V$ product is implemented as an “RS-GEMM” (the stored/accumulated tile that represents the softmaxed probabilities is kept in registers and is multiplied with the $V$ tile sourced from SMEM).

Why that mapping arises and what it costs: in the consumer main loop the algorithm streams blocks $K_j$ and $V_j$ into a circular SMEM buffer and keeps $Q_i$ resident for the CTA. The first GEMM computes $S_i^{(j)} = Q_i K_j^T$. In the implementation on Hopper/WGMMA this GEMM is arranged so that the operand that is easiest to broadcast across threads/warps ($K_j$) is placed in SMEM and the other operand ($Q_i$) is kept in RMEM (registers) for fastest access and reuse by the warpgroup. That is the intuitive meaning of SS in the paper’s nomenclature: the GEMM issues WGMMA with one operand in shared memory ($K_j$) and the other read from an on‑chip copy ($Q_i$) that is register-resident for the warps doing the MMA. The second GEMM computes contributions to $O_i$ using the already-computed and post-softmaxed $\widetilde{P}_i^{(j)}$ and $V_j$. $\widetilde{P}_i^{(j)}$ is produced and kept in registers (it is small per-thread, produced by softmax logic) while $V_j$ is read from SMEM; hence the “RS-GEMM” label: first operand (R) from registers, second operand (S) from shared memory.

Effect on register pressure and shared-memory traffic: placing $Q$ (or the $\widetilde{P}$ tile) in registers minimizes SMEM traffic and gives the fastest operand access to WGMMA, but it increases register pressure. The paper explicitly calls out this tradeoff in the 2‑stage pipelining discussion: the overlapped pipelining requires extra per‑CTA/RMEM storage for $S_{\text{next}}$ (an additional $S$ tile) and for temporary $\widetilde{P}$ and accumulators, increasing register usage by about $B_r \times B_c$ floats worth of state. Higher register usage can force smaller block sizes (reducing arithmetic intensity) or cause spilling to local memory, which would kill the latency advantage. Conversely, sourcing the reused operand from SMEM reduces register demand but increases shared-memory bandwidth and traffic: the GEMM will re-read the same $Q$ (or $\widetilde{P}$) from SMEM for each microtile rather than reuse a register-resident copy, which increases SMEM contention and reduces the benefit of using asynchronous WGMMA.

FP8 / WGMMA layout constraint interaction: Hopper’s FP8 WGMMA accepts only k-major SMEM operands. That imposes further placement choices. For the second GEMM ($P V$) the $V$ tile must be laid out k-major in SMEM so WGMMA can read it directly; the implementation therefore in FP8 needs an in‑kernel transpose of $V$ tiles (producer warpgroup does LDSM/STSM transpose) so that $V_j$ arrives in SMEM with the required stride. Meanwhile the FP32 accumulator register layout produced by an FP8 WGMMA has a different register ordering than what a following WGMMA expects as an operand, so the paper performs a byte-permute register rearrangement to convert the first GEMM’s accumulator into the operand format for the next WGMMA; doing that in registers avoids extra SMEM roundtrips but costs register instructions and a bit more RMEM use. These layout quirks make it especially valuable to keep the reusable operand in registers (the RS pattern for the second GEMM), because moving the softmaxed $P$ tile back to SMEM just to satisfy layout would be expensive and would negate the benefit of block fusion and asynchrony.

Putting it together: the chosen mapping ($Q/K$: $Q$ in RMEM, $K$ in SMEM for $QK^T$; $P$ in RMEM, $V$ in SMEM for $PV$) is a practical balance: keep the operand that is repeatedly reused across microtiles in fast RMEM to save SMEM bandwidth and enable high WGMMA utilization, but accept the register-pressure cost. The pipelined (2‑stage/3‑stage) variants in the paper push more state into registers to overlap GEMM and softmax, improving Tensor Core utilization at the cost of higher register usage; if that pressure becomes too large you must shrink tiles or fall back to more SMEM-sourced operands (increasing SMEM traffic) or suffer spills.

Summary: SS-GEMM/RS-GEMM are shorthand for which operand(s) are expected to be resident in SMEM versus registers for the WGMMA calls. For $QK^T$ the implementation keeps $Q$ in registers and $K$ in SMEM (minimizing repeated SMEM reads of $Q$), and for $PV$ it keeps the probability tile $P$ in registers and $V$ in SMEM (minimizing writes/reads of $P$ and enabling immediate accumulation). This mapping maximizes WGMMA throughput and enables the GEMM–softmax overlap, but increases register pressure; in FP8 it also interacts with k‑major layout constraints, requiring in‑kernel transposes of $V$ and register permutations of accumulators to avoid extra SMEM traffic.

### Why does FP8 WGMMA require k-major operands in SMEM, and how does that constrain V tiling?
Question:
Section 2.2 says FP8 WGMMA only accepts k-major in SMEM for operands, while FP16 WGMMA is more flexible. For the second GEMM involving V, this implies V tiles in SMEM must be contiguous in the K dimension. Could someone explain the hardware reason for this FP8 constraint, and concretely how it affects the tile shapes and strides chosen for V in the attention kernel? A small example with M, N, K and the resulting SMEM tile strides would help.
Answer:
Short answer: FP8 WGMMA on Hopper requires $k$-major operands because the FP8 tensor-core path implements operand loads and lane-to-byte packing that assume the inner ($K$) dimension is the contiguous memory axis so that each warp can read packed 8-bit words efficiently into the tensor-core microtiles. That hardware restriction forces you to store the $V$ tiles in shared memory so that elements that are adjacent along the GEMM's $K$ dimension are also adjacent in SMEM; for attention this means the $V$ tile must be contiguous in the sequence-length ($K$) direction rather than the usual head-dimension ($d$) direction. Concretely, that mandates an in-kernel transpose (or a pre-transpose) of $V$ tiles and constrains which $B_c$ ($K$-block) sizes and SMEM strides you choose, because the tensor core micro-tiles (and the LDSM/STSM load granularity) determine the legal contiguous layout and alignment.

Why the hardware requires $k$-major (intuitive + technical):
- The WGMMA instruction implements a pipeline where many threads collectively load operand tiles into registers in a packing that assumes 8-bit lanes are stored contiguously along the $K$ dimension. For FP8 the hardware uses packed byte loads/permutes and a particular thread-to-byte mapping that only works if the inner dimension ($K$) is the contiguous axis in SMEM. In other words, the tensor core expects to fetch $K$-consecutive bytes for its micro-GEMM panels.
- FP16 WGMMA can accept both $mn$-major and $k$-major because the 16-bit operand alignment and load pattern are more flexible (the microtile packing and instruction support more layouts). The FP8 path is more constrained because of the packing of 8-bit values into the 128-byte LDSM/STSM blocks and because the accumulator/operand register layouts are different and require specific byte permutations.
- Practically this means the GEMM that multiplies $P$ by $V$ (the second GEMM in the attention inner loop) must see the $V$ tile in SMEM with $K$ as the contiguous stride so the WGMMA FP8 load works without additional costly shuffles.

How this affects $V$ tiling and SMEM strides (concrete, small example)
Take the usual attention inner-block shapes and translate them to a small numeric example so you can see memory layout/stride consequences. Pick:
- $B_r$ (query block height) = $M = 4$
- $B_c$ (key/value block length) = $K = 8$
- head dimension ($d$) = $N = 16$

We will consider the second GEMM in the consumer: $P$ ($M \times K$) times $V_j$ ($K \times N$) $\to$ $O$ contribution ($M \times N$).

Typical (default) layout coming from many frameworks is row-major with the head ($N = d$) contiguous inside each sequence element: so in global memory $V$ is indexed as $V[\text{seq\_index}][\text{head\_index}]$ and the $\text{head\_index}$ ($d$) is the fastest-varying address. If we TMA-load a $K \times N$ tile naively into SMEM preserving that layout, the contiguous addresses vary with $\text{head\_index}$ ($N$), not with $K$. WGMMA-FP8, however, wants contiguous variation along $K$.

Two concrete memory-index formulas to compare:
- Default (head-contiguous) SMEM address of element $V[k,n]$ ($k \in \{0,\dots,7\}$, $n \in \{0,\dots,15\}$): $addr = base + k \times N + n$. Here consecutive addresses when increasing $n$ are adjacent; consecutive $k$ are separated by stride $N = 16$.
- Required $k$-major SMEM address ($K$-contiguous) of element $V[k,n]$: $addr = base + n \times K + k$. Here consecutive $k$ are adjacent (stride $1$) and consecutive $n$ are separated by stride $K = 8$.

So when WGMMA-FP8 tries to load the microtile it will expect, say, 8 consecutive bytes corresponding to consecutive $k$ values for a fixed micro-column. If the layout is head-contiguous (stride-16 for $K$), the load pattern is wrong: tensor core will fetch bytes that jump by 16 and the packing into registers becomes incorrect (and the instruction is not defined for that layout).

Therefore you must present $V_j$ in SMEM in the $k$-major form ($addr = base + n \times K + k$). In practice you achieve that by doing an in-kernel transpose after loading the tile from GMEM into SMEM (or by pre-transposing $V$ in GMEM). The authors choose an in-kernel transpose using LDSM/STSM so the transpose can be done efficiently and overlapped with other work.

Concrete SMEM stride numbers for the example:
- With $K$-major layout required, the SMEM stride between successive $K$ entries is $1$ (adjacent addresses), and the stride between successive head elements is $K = 8$.
- If you keep the head-major layout, the stride between $K$ entries would be $N = 16$, which the FP8 WGMMA cannot accept.

Practical constraints and consequences for tile shapes and scheduling:
- $B_c$ (the $K$ block) must align with the tensor-core microtile granularity (e.g., multiples of $8$ in the example) so LDSM/STSM and WGMMA loads can be used efficiently. Picking $B_c$ that is not compatible adds padding/complicated permutes and hurts performance.
- Because you must transpose each $V$ tile into $K$-major in SMEM, you pay a small extra cost and require SMEM and register capacity for the transpose buffers. FlashAttention-3 overlaps this transpose with the two adjacent WGMMAs so its cost is largely hidden, but it still constrains how many stages and how large $B_c$ you can pick before register/SMEM pressure hurts the pipeline.
- The accumulator/operand register layouts for FP8 WGMMA also differ from FP32 accumulator layouts; FlashAttention-3 inserts a byte-permute step on the FP32 accumulator (after the first WGMMA) to make it compatible with the order used by the second WGMMA and with the permuted $V$ tile layout. This further constrains how you order columns/rows inside the physical tile; in practice you pick tile shapes that (a) match the WGMMA microtile, (b) allow efficient LDSM/STSM transpose, and (c) keep register pressure acceptable.

Summary (one-paragraph):
FP8 WGMMA requires $k$-major operands because the FP8 tensor-core load/packing semantics and the LDSM/STSM microtile loads assume the inner ($K$) dimension is contiguous in SMEM to collect packed 8-bit lanes correctly. For attention that forces the $V$ tiles in SMEM to be contiguous along the sequence-length ($K$) axis rather than the head axis, so you must transpose $V$ tiles (in-kernel or beforehand). This restriction fixes the SMEM stride (stride-$1$ across $K$, stride=$B_c$ across heads) and pushes you to choose $B_c$ and microtile sizes that align to tensor-core granularity and to balance SMEM/register pressure so that the transpose and the required byte-permute on accumulators can be overlapped and hidden by the pipeline.

### How does the in-kernel transpose for FP8 using LDSM/STSM actually work on packed 8-bit data?
Question:
The paper mentions using LDSM/STSM (ldmatrix/stmatrix) to transpose FP8 tiles in shared memory, packing two 8-bit elements into the 16-bit granularity, and then doing some register moves because the transpose variants cannot split the packed bytes. Could someone give a minimal schematic of the steps: (1) load with LDSM, (2) register shuffles/permutations required to separate or reorder bytes, and (3) store with STSM, showing how an example 16x16 FP8 tile is transposed without excessive register use?
Answer:
Short answer: the kernel treats a $16\times16$ FP8 tile as four $8\times8$ subtiles of $16$‑bit words, uses LDSM/STSM to move/transpose at the $16$‑bit granularity, and then fixes the packed‑byte ordering inside registers with cheap byte‑permute instructions before the STSM write. This avoids expensive cross‑thread shuffles and keeps register pressure low. Below I give a minimal schematic (conceptual register/map view) of the actual steps used in the paper’s description.

Why this is needed (intuition). LDSM/STSM operate on $8\times8$ matrices of $16$‑bit entries ($128$ bytes) and the library/hardware transpose variants also work at that $16$‑bit granularity. But FP8 elements are $8$‑bit, so the trick is to pack two FP8 values into one $16$‑bit word and treat LDSM/STSM as moving $16$‑bit words. The complication is that an $8\times8$ transpose of $16$‑bit words does not by itself produce the correct ordering of the underlying $8$‑bit elements: the pairings inside the $16$‑bit words (low byte / high byte) end up in the “wrong” places for the desired FP8 transpose. The fix is small register-level byte permutations after the ldmatrix and before stmatrix.

Minimal schematic for a $16\times16$ FP8 tile (indices $0\ldots15$ rows and cols)

1) Tile partitioning and packing
Treat the $16\times16$ FP8 tile as a $2\times2$ grid of $8\times8$ subtiles:
- A = rows $0\ldots7$, cols $0\ldots7$
- B = rows $0\ldots7$, cols $8\ldots15$
- C = rows $8\ldots15$, cols $0\ldots7$
- D = rows $8\ldots15$, cols $8\ldots15$

Within each $8\times8$ subtile, pack two FP8 elements into one $16$‑bit word along the contiguous inner stride that LDSM expects. Concretely, each $16$‑bit word holds (fp8_even, fp8_odd) — e.g., low byte = column even, high byte = column odd.

2) Use LDSM to load/transcribe $8\times8$ $16$‑bit blocks into registers
A warp issues four LDSM calls (one per subtile location in SMEM). Each LDSM loads an $8\times8$ block of $16$‑bit words and — if using the transpose variant in LDSM — performs a transpose at the $16$‑bit word granularity. After these loads you have in registers eight $16$‑bit words per threadgroup lane that correspond to the transposed $8\times8$ of $16$‑bit words.

At this point the $16$‑bit word containing two FP8s is still intact, but because LDSM transposed $16$‑bit cells, the two bytes that were originally belonging to the same row may now be split between target positions needed for the true FP8 element transpose.

3) Register byte-permute to fix intra‑word byte placement
Now perform an in‑register byte permutation (cheap SIMD byte permute instruction, e.g., a VPRMT/byte‑shuffle or PTX byte permute) that reorders the low/high bytes within $16$‑bit words and/or permutes whole bytes across nearby registers so that each register lane contains the correct sequence of $8$‑bit FP values in the order STSM expects to write them back.

Concretely for the $16\times16$: after the $16$‑bit transpose, you need to transform the sequence of $16$‑bit words
$\{d_0 d_1, d_2 d_3, d_4 d_5, d_6 d_7, \dots\}$
(where each $d_{ij}$ denotes an FP8 element packed into a byte) into the sequence that corresponds to the transposed $8$‑bit grid. The paper’s implementation does this by replicating the same byte permutation pattern every $8$ bytes (i.e., every two $16$‑bit words) — e.g. reorder each $8$‑byte lane as
$\{d_0 d_1 d_4 d_5 d_2 d_3 d_6 d_7\}$
so columns that were grouped for the $16$‑bit transpose are rearranged into the column order needed for the FP8 transpose. This is a local register byte permute (no cross‑thread communication), cheap and replicated across lanes.

4) STSM to store the transposed FP8 tile back into SMEM
After the byte permutes produce the correct contiguous ordering of FP8 bytes in the register file, use STSM (the store matrix instruction) to write the registers back to shared memory. STSM writes $8\times8$ of $16$‑bit words (i.e., $128$ bytes) each call; because you fixed intra‑word byte layout, the stored $16$‑bit words now contain the two FP8 elements that belong together in the transposed layout. Two STSM calls (for the two halves) produce the final $16\times16$ FP8 tile in SMEM but transposed.

Summary walk‑through for one $8\times8$ subtile pair (concrete short trace)
- Step 1: pack FP8 into $16$‑bit words in SMEM as $[b_0|b_1], [b_2|b_3], \dots$ where $b$ are bytes.
- Step 2: ldmatrix.transpose reads this $8\times8$ of $16$‑bit words into registers and transposes at $16$‑bit granularity (so the words moved to positions corresponding to the $16$‑bit transpose).
- Step 3: within registers apply a byte permute to swap and reorder bytes inside and across adjacent $16$‑bit words so that the byte stream matches the desired FP8 transpose ordering. This is done identically on each $8$‑byte lane, keeping register pressure low.
- Step 4: stmatrix writes those $16$‑bit words back to SMEM; the two bytes in each $16$‑bit word now contain the correct pair of FP8 values for the transposed tile.

Why this keeps register use modest. All heavy data motion is done by LDSM/STSM (warp collective) and only small fixed-size register permutes are needed (a few vector byte‑permute instructions per lane). No cross‑lane shuffles or wide register spills are required; the approach uses the natural $8\times8$ LDSM/STSM grain and a repeated, compact byte‑permute pattern that maps to each $8$‑byte lane.

Relation to the paper statements. This description matches the paper’s summary: LDSM/STSM are used at $16$‑bit granularity packing two FP8 into a $16$‑bit word; the transpose variants cannot split packed bytes, therefore a small set of register byte permutations is applied between LDSM and STSM to produce the correct FP8 transpose layout. The paper further notes these register permutations are replicated across every $8$‑byte lane (exactly as described) and so are register‑efficient.

If you want, I can sketch the exact index mapping for a numeric $4\times4$ toy example (showing which byte moves where) or map these conceptual steps to PTX/SASS instructions (ldmatrix, vperm, stmatrix) used on Hopper.

### What is the exact register permutation needed to make the FP8 accumulator layout feed the next FP8 WGMMA?
Question:
Section 3.3 says the FP32 accumulator layout of FP8 WGMMA does not match the A-operand layout expected by the next FP8 WGMMA, so a byte permute is applied to reorder bytes as {d0 d1 d4 d5 d2 d3 d6 d7}, repeated every 8 bytes. Could someone explain: (a) which dimensions this reorders logically in the P tile, (b) why this particular pattern, and (c) how this interacts with the in-kernel transpose’s row permutation for V so that the final PV product is correct without cross-thread shuffles?
Answer:
Short answer: the byte permutation $\{d_0\ d_1\ d_4\ d_5\ d_2\ d_3\ d_6\ d_7\}$ (repeated every $8$ bytes) is a local reordering of the columns inside each small P tile that converts the $\mathrm{FP32}$-accumulator register packing produced by the first $\mathrm{FP8}$ WGMMA into the $k$-major / operand packing expected by the next $\mathrm{FP8}$ WGMMA. Concretely, it permutes columns in $2$-byte pairs (swap the 3rd/4th pair with the 2nd pair), and the producer-side in-kernel transpose writes $V$ with the complementary row permutation so that when WGMMA consumes $P$ and $V$ no cross-thread (warp) shuffles are needed.

Below I expand that into an intuitive + technical explanation addressing (a),(b),(c) and connect it to the paper’s layout/warp/thread ownership points.

(a) Which logical dimensions in the P tile are being reordered?
The P tile is the block of softmaxed scores produced by the first GEMM ($P = \mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top)$ block). The bytes labeled $d_0\dots d_7$ are successive bytes in the thread-register holding of that P tile (the figures in the paper show the register order per thread). The permutation $\{d_0\ d_1\ d_4\ d_5\ d_2\ d_3\ d_6\ d_7\}$ repeated every $8$ bytes is equivalent to taking consecutive $2$-byte pairs and rearranging them in the local sequence: $[pair_0, pair_1, pair_2, pair_3] \to [pair_0, pair_2, pair_1, pair_3]$. Viewed as matrix indices, this is a permutation of $P$’s columns inside the small register/block tile: columns $0,1,2,3$ (or more generally groups of columns in the $8$-byte tile) are reordered so that column pairs $(0,1), (2,3), (4,5), (6,7)$ become $(0,1), (4,5), (2,3), (6,7)$. So the logical effect is a column permutation inside each tile, implemented as pairwise $2$-byte moves repeated across the tile.

(b) Why this exact pattern?
This specific pattern arises from two hardware-imposed facts described in the paper: (1) the $\mathrm{FP8}$ WGMMA on Hopper requires operands in a $k$-major packing when they live in SMEM/registers, while the $\mathrm{FP32}$ accumulator produced by the first WGMMA is emitted in a different per-thread register ordering, and (2) the LDSM/STSM packing and the way $\mathrm{FP8}$ elements are packed (two $8$-bit $\mathrm{FP8}$s per $16$-bit LDSM lane) constrains how bytes can be permuted cheaply. The accumulator register ordering groups elements such that the next WGMMA expects the $k$-dimension-contiguous layout only after swapping certain adjacent $2$-byte groups. The smallest economical permutation that aligns the two layouts (respecting the $16$-bit LDSM/STSM granularity and avoiding expensive per-element moves or cross-thread shuffles) is to swap the middle $2$-byte pairs as shown: keeping $pair_0$ in place, swapping $pair_1$ and $pair_2$, leaving $pair_3$. Repeating this every $8$ bytes matches the hardware packing granularity and therefore maps the $\mathrm{FP32}$ accumulator contents to the $k$-major operand format expected by the next $\mathrm{FP8}$ WGMMA with minimal instruction cost (byte-permute / lane-level moves).

(c) How this interacts with the in-kernel transpose of V so the final $P \times V$ product is correct without cross-thread shuffles
A matrix multiplication computes rows of $P$ times columns of $V$. If we permute columns of $P$, to get the mathematically correct product we must make the corresponding permutation on the rows of $V$ so that each $P$ column multiplies the correct $V$ row. The paper explicitly does that: after applying the byte permutation to reorder the accumulator (effectively permuting $P$’s columns in those small tiles), the in-kernel transpose that writes $V$ into SMEM is implemented so it performs the complementary row permutation when it writes the transposed $V$ tile. Because the transpose is performed by the producer warpgroup using LDSM/STSM (warp-level, register-efficient tile-copy/transpose primitives), it can write $V$ into SMEM already permuted by rows in exactly the layout the next WGMMA expects. With the two operands permuted in complementary ways ($P$ columns reordered in the accumulator, $V$ rows reordered on write), their element-wise alignment for the next $\mathrm{FP8}$ WGMMA is correct without any cross-thread ownership change or shuffle instructions. That is the key: both operands are locally rearranged (one by a cheap byte-permute in registers, the other by the producer’s in-kernel transpose write pattern) so that thread-local register ownership matches the Tensor Core $k$-major packing, enabling the next WGMMA to consume them directly.

Intuition and the broader point
Think of the registers as holding a tiny $2\times 4$ micro-block arranged in a particular interleaving by the hardware WGMMA. The next WGMMA needs the micro-block interleaving to be different ($k$-major). Instead of moving ownership of elements between threads (which is expensive), the kernel performs two local, cheap transformations: (1) a byte-permute inside each thread’s registers that reorders element pairs, and (2) a producer-side tile transpose that writes $V$ with rows permuted in the complementary way. Because both transformations are matched, the logical matrix product is preserved and the Tensor Core receives both operands in a compatible packing. This is precisely how the implementation avoids cross-thread shuffles: both sides cooperate to ensure data alignment at warp/Tensor-core granularity.

References in the paper
This explanation is the same mechanism the paper describes in Section 3.3 (the discussion of differing accumulator vs operand layouts for $\mathrm{FP8}$ WGMMA), the figures of register layouts, and the paragraph that says the byte permute “permutes its columns … For WGMMA to then compute the correct output tile, we can correspondingly arrange for the in-kernel transpose to write out a matching row permutation of the $V$ tile,” thereby “eliminat[ing] having to use shuffle instructions to change register ownership across threads.”

### How does block quantization change the softmax scaling, and what is actually rescaled where?
Question:
With block quantization, Q, K, V are quantized per block with their own scales. The paper says the algorithm can "scale each block of S" to account for this with no extra compute. Could someone spell out the formulas? If Q_block = q_scale * Q_fp8 and K_block = k_scale * K_fp8, then S_block = Q_block K_block^T = (q_scale k_scale) (Q_fp8 K_fp8^T). How and where is the factor q_scale k_scale folded into the streamed softmax updates of m_i and l_i, and how are P V products rescaled to remain consistent with the final O?
Answer:
Short answer: the block quantization scale $c = q_{\text{scale}} \cdot k_{\text{scale}}$ multiplies the whole block of raw logits, so you fold it in by applying it to the streamed softmax math exactly where the block's logits enter the softmax pipeline: multiply the block logits before taking exponentials, and propagate that same multiplicative factor into the streamed normalization (the running $m_i$) and into the $P\!\cdot\!V$ accumulator. Concretely you replace every occurrence of the block logits $S_{\text{block}}$ by $c\cdot S_{\text{fp8}}$ and use those scaled values in the usual streamed-softmax updates

$$
m_i \leftarrow \max(m_i, \operatorname{rowmax}(c\cdot S_{\text{fp8}})),
$$

$$
\widetilde{P} \leftarrow \exp(c\cdot S_{\text{fp8}} - m_i),
$$

$$
\ell_i \leftarrow \exp(m_i^{\text{old}} - m_i)\cdot \ell_i + \operatorname{rowsum}(\widetilde{P}),
$$

$$
O_i \leftarrow \operatorname{diag}\big(\exp(m_i^{\text{old}} - m_i)\big)^{-1}\cdot O_i + \widetilde{P}\cdot V_{\text{block}}.
$$

Because $c$ is a single scalar per (Q,K) block, these multiplies are pointwise scalings of the block's logits / exponent inputs and of the block's $P$ contributions, so they do not change the algebraic correctness and can be fused into the same instruction stream that computes the block GEMM and the exponent — the paper's “no extra compute” remark means you do the scalar multiplications where you already have the $S$ values in registers/shared memory (or fold $c$ into the exponent input).

Longer (but still compact) explanation and the key math to keep in mind:

1) Definitions and the source of $c$.  
If $Q_{\text{block}} = q_{\text{scale}}\cdot Q_{\text{fp8}}$ and $K_{\text{block}} = k_{\text{scale}}\cdot K_{\text{fp8}}$, define $c := q_{\text{scale}}\cdot k_{\text{scale}}$. The true logits for that block are
$$
S_{\text{block}} = Q_{\text{block}} K_{\text{block}}^\top = c \cdot (Q_{\text{fp8}} K_{\text{fp8}}^\top) = c \cdot S_{\text{fp8}},
$$
where $S_{\text{fp8}}$ denotes the inner-product results computed from the FP8 operands (and accumulated into FP32 accumulators).

2) Where $c$ enters softmax.  
Attention computes row-wise softmax on $S$. If you stream over blocks, for one row (fixed query row index) you are maintaining a running row-max $m_i$ and running normalizer $\ell_i$ and accumulating $O_i$. The streamed updates for a new block with scaled logits $c\cdot s$ (writing $s$ for the block's row of $S_{\text{fp8}}$) are exactly

$$
m_i^{\text{new}} = \max(m_i^{\text{old}}, \operatorname{rowmax}(c \cdot s)),
$$

$$
\widetilde{p} = \exp(c \cdot s - m_i^{\text{new}}),
$$

$$
\ell_i^{\text{new}} = \exp(m_i^{\text{old}} - m_i^{\text{new}})\cdot \ell_i^{\text{old}} + \operatorname{rowsum}(\widetilde{p}),
$$

$$
O_i^{\text{new}} = \operatorname{diag}\big(\exp(m_i^{\text{old}} - m_i^{\text{new}})\big)^{-1}\cdot O_i^{\text{old}} + \widetilde{p}\cdot V_{\text{block}}.
$$

This is identical to the non-quantized streamed-softmax formula except that every occurrence of the block logits $S_{\text{block}}$ has the factor $c$ multiplied in.

3) Why this is algebraically consistent.  
Softmax and the two-step streamed update are linear in the unnormalized probabilities (the exponentials) and the log-shift $m_i$ is the per-row offset used to avoid overflow. Scaling the block logits by $c$ multiplies that block's unnormalized probabilities by exp factor(s) that are consistent with multiplying those exponentials in the numerator and adding to the denominator ($\ell_i$). Folding $c$ into the exponent input is therefore the correct way to adjust both the $\ell$ and the $O$ accumulation so the final $O = \operatorname{softmax}(S) V$ is unchanged relative to computing with the full-precision $Q,K$ (provided you apply the same $c$ for that block everywhere).

4) Practical/fused implementation detail (why “no extra compute”).  
In practice you already compute $S_{\text{fp8}}$ (the block GEMM) and you already form the argument to the exponential ($S - m$). Folding the per-block scalar $c$ is just one additional multiply of that argument (or equivalently of the $S_{\text{fp8}}$ result) before calling $\exp$. On modern GPU kernels this scalar multiply can be fused into the instruction stream that already materializes $S_{\text{fp8}}$ in registers and prepares the $\exp$ input; you do not need a separate pass over memory or any extra reduction. Similarly, when forming $\widetilde{P}\cdot V$ you are using $\widetilde{P}$ just computed from $c\cdot S_{\text{fp8}}$, so no further special adjustment is needed beyond using the correct $\widetilde{P}$ values; the same scalar $c$ has already affected $\ell_i$ through $\operatorname{rowsum}(\widetilde{P})$ so the normalization is consistent.

5) Summary intuition.  
Block quantization gives each block its own multiplicative scale on logits. Softmax only depends on the relative magnitudes of exponentials, so you must multiply the block’s logits by that scalar before exponentiating and then propagate the resulting scaled exponentials into the running normalizer $\ell_i$ and into the $P\!\cdot\!V$ accumulation. Because the scalar is per-block (not per-element) you can apply it at the point where the block’s logits are already resident (registers/SMEM) and fold the multiply into the same tight kernel that computes the block GEMM and the $\exp/\operatorname{rowsum}$, which is why the paper says you can “scale each block of $S$” at no extra compute cost.

If you want a micro-code-style listing, replace lines that compute $S_i^{(j)}$ and then compute $\exp(S_i^{(j)} - m) / \operatorname{rowsum}$ by the exact same lines but using $S_i^{(j)} := c \cdot S_{\text{fp8}}$ (with $c = q_{\text{scale}}\cdot k_{\text{scale}}$ for that $j$) in every place $S$ appears (rowmax, exp input, rowsum, and $P\!\cdot\!V$ accumulation).

### Why does incoherent processing with random orthogonal M reduce FP8 quantization error without changing attention?
Question:
Section 3.3 introduces multiplying both Q and K by the same random orthogonal matrix M (constructed via Hadamard and random sign flips), arguing that (QM)(KM)^T = QK^T keeps attention scores unchanged while reducing outliers before FP8 quantization. Can someone provide an intuition or a short argument for why this reduces the peak magnitudes at FP8 quantization time? Also, are there corner cases where M could worsen quantization (e.g., when outliers are structured across features)?
Answer:
Short answer: multiplying $Q$ and $K$ by the same random orthogonal matrix $M$ does not change the attention because $$(QM)(KM)^\top = QK^\top,$$ and it reduces $\mathrm{FP8}$ quantization error because it "mixes" each feature coordinate into many others so that extreme values (outliers) that were concentrated in a few coordinates get spread across coordinates. Spreading reduces the per-coordinate peak magnitudes and the dynamic range seen by the $\mathrm{FP8}$ quantizer, so fewer values saturate or suffer large rounding relative to a single global scale. This is why the paper calls the step “incoherent processing.” Below I give a concise, intuitive argument plus the main caveats.

Why attention is unchanged
Because $M$ is orthogonal, $M M^\top = I$, so the attention pre-softmax scores satisfy
$$(QM)(KM)^\top = Q M M^\top K^\top = Q K^\top.$$
Thus all subsequent softmax and $P = \mathrm{softmax}(S)$ computations are identical in exact arithmetic; the operation is strictly algebraically invariant.

Intuition for reduced $\mathrm{FP8}$ quantization error
$\mathrm{FP8}$ has very limited dynamic range and precision: only a few exponent and mantissa bits. In the common per-tensor or per-block scaling quantization scheme, the quantizer picks a single scale for a block and then maps each coordinate to a small set of representable $\mathrm{FP8}$ values. The dominant cause of large quantization error is a tiny fraction of very large coordinates ("outliers") that force the scale to be large so the rest of the coordinates are represented with much coarser granularity, or cause saturation/overflow.

Applying a random orthogonal transform $M$ mixes coordinates so each output coordinate is a (signed) linear combination of many input coordinates. Consider a single row $q$ (or column, depending on layout) of $Q$. After multiplication by $M$, one output coordinate is roughly
$$(qM)_i = \sum_{k=1}^d q_k M_{k,i}.$$
If $M$ is a Hadamard-times-signs matrix (the practical choice in the paper), each output coordinate is a sum of about $d$ terms with roughly equal magnitude but different signs. By the central limit / concentration intuition these sums tend to be more "typical" (smaller extremes) than the original coordinates that may have heavy tails. In particular, for a vector $q$ with squared norm $\|q\|^2$, an orthogonal mixing redistributes that squared norm across the $d$ coordinates so the expected variance per mixed coordinate is about $\|q\|^2 / d$. So instead of one coordinate carrying most of the energy (an outlier), many coordinates each carry roughly $1/d$ of the energy. That reduces the maximum coordinate magnitude and therefore reduces the required scale and the quantization error when you apply $\mathrm{FP8}$ quantization per block.

A compact quantitative sketch: if a vector has one coordinate of magnitude $A$ and the rest near zero, its infinity norm is $A$. After multiplying by a random orthogonal $M$, the typical coordinate magnitude becomes about $A/\sqrt{d}$ (up to constants), so the block's dynamic range (max / typical) contracts by roughly $\sqrt{d}$, which yields much lower rounding/saturation problems for $\mathrm{FP8}$.

Why Hadamard + random sign works in practice
A full random orthogonal matrix is expensive. The structured choice used in the paper (random diagonal of $\pm 1$ times a Hadamard) approximates a random orthogonal transform while costing $O(d\log d)$ operations and can be fused with other linear operations (e.g., rotary embedding). It still mixes coordinates well and empirically reduces extreme peaks.

Corner cases and failure modes
This technique is not a universal cure; there are plausible corner cases:

1) Structured outliers aligned across features in many dimensions. If outliers are not just a single coordinate but are present across many coordinates of the same sign so that the energy is already spread, orthogonal mixing will not meaningfully reduce the dynamic range and thus gives little benefit.

2) Adversarial / worst-case transforms. Orthogonal mixing is a rotation; for any fixed orthogonal $M$ there exist input patterns that get concentrated after multiplying by $M$ (rotations can move energy between coordinates). If the data distribution were adversarial and correlated with the particular choice of $M$, you could in principle worsen per-coordinate peaks. However, that is unlikely with a random or Hadamard-based $M$; concentration bounds show such bad alignments have very low probability.

3) Block structure mismatch. The paper uses block quantization: mixing is done per block (or applied before blocking). If outliers are structured across the entire head dimension but the mixing is applied within small blocks that omit some correlated coordinates, then mixing might be less effective. You need the mixing to cover the features that hold the outliers (i.e., use a block size large enough to capture the correlation).

Practical takeaways
Because attention depends only on $QK^\top$, you can freely apply the same orthogonal $M$ to $Q$ and $K$ to change how values are distributed across feature coordinates without affecting the result. Random orthogonal mixing typically reduces per-coordinate peaks and dynamic range seen during $\mathrm{FP8}$ quantization, which lowers rounding and saturation errors. The method is cheap in practice using Hadamard+signs, and combining it with block quantization (separate scales per block) further reduces $\mathrm{FP8}$ error. The main caveat is that if outliers are already broadly distributed or adversarially aligned to the chosen $M$ (or block mixing is too small), the benefit shrinks and in pathological cases quantization could get worse — but with random/Hadamard $M$ and reasonable block sizes this is unlikely and matches the empirical improvements shown in the paper.

### How is numerical stability preserved when overlapping softmax with concurrent GEMMs?
Question:
The 2-stage pipeline overlaps softmax computations (which involve exp and reductions) with asynchronous WGMMA. Given possible latency and reorderings, is there any risk that partial rescalings of O_i are applied before all dependent values are finalized? Which specific waits and barriers guarantee that the running max m_i and sum l_i are consistent for each row, and that O_i receives the correct rescaling factor diag(exp(m_old - m))^{-1} at exactly the right time?
Answer:
Short answer: there is no unchecked "partial rescaling" race. FlashAttention-3 intentionally schedules asynchronous WGMMA/TMA work but inserts the exact waits and barrier points needed so that each row's running maximum $m_i$, running sum $\ell_i$, and the rescaling of the accumulator $\mathbf{O}_i$ are computed from a consistent, finalized set of operands. The paper's pseudocode shows the precise places where the asynchronous GEMMs are committed and where the code explicitly waits; these waits guarantee correctness of the streaming softmax algebra (the saved $m_i^{\mathrm{old}}$ and the factor $\mathrm{diag}(\exp(m_i^{\mathrm{old}}-m_i))^{-1}$ are applied only after the dependent GEMMs have completed).

Why there is no data race (intuitive). The algorithm overlaps work across iterations by doing the next $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ (call it a WGMMA) and the previous $\tilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{V}$ (another WGMMA) concurrently, but it never uses the result of a GEMM before ensuring that GEMM finished. The softmax reduction (compute row-wise max, exponentials, accumulate $\ell_i$) depends on the finished $\mathbf{S}$ tile; the RS-GEMM that updates $\mathbf{O}_i$ depends on a finished $\tilde{\mathbf{P}}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ tile. The code issues asynchronous WGMMA instructions for throughput, but then explicitly waits at the control points where correctness requires the results.

Where the synchronization happens (mapped to the paper pseudocode). In the 2-stage consumer loop (Algorithm 2):

- Line 5 computes the first WGMMA for $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}=\mathbf{Q}_i\mathbf{K}_0^\top$ and does "Commit and wait" — the code waits for the GEMM that produces $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ before proceeding to the corresponding softmax/rescale for that tile. This establishes the initial $m_i$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ safely.

- In the main loop (lines labelled around \ref{code:mainloop_start}–\ref{code:mainloop_end}): the code issues the next WGMMA for $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}=\mathbf{Q}_i\mathbf{K}_j^\top$ with "Commit but do not wait" (line \ref{code:first_wgmma}), and it also issues the RS-WGMMA $\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}}\mathbf{V}_{j-1}$ with "Commit but do not wait" (line \ref{code:second_wgmma}). Crucially, after issuing these asynchronous GEMMs the code then explicitly waits for the WGMMA that produced $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ before computing the softmax on that tile (line \ref{code:softmax}). Likewise, the code waits for the $\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}}\mathbf{V}_{j-1}$ WGMMA before performing the rescale of $\mathbf{O}_i$ (the wait is right after the softmax step). Thus each use of a GEMM result is preceded by a corresponding wait.

- The final write-out (epilogue) again waits for the last $\tilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{V}$ GEMM to finish and then applies the final rescaling $\mathbf{O}_i = \mathrm{diag}(\ell_i)^{-1}\mathbf{O}_i$ using the fully accumulated $\ell_i$ and $m_i$.

What those waits/barries implement in hardware terms. The implementation uses (i) TMA loads that are asynchronous, (ii) asynchronous WGMMA Tensor Core instructions, (iii) explicit commit-and-wait pairs (the pseudocode's "Commit and wait" / "Commit but do not wait" followed by later "Wait for the WGMMA") and (iv) inter-warpgroup barriers (\verb|bar.sync|) in the ping-pong scheduling to order GEMMs between warpgroups. These control points are the only places where results are consumed; therefore the running maximum $m_i$ and running sum $\ell_i$ are always updated from completed $\mathbf{S}$ tiles, and $\mathbf{O}_i$ is rescaled only after the dependent RS-GEMM has finished. The circular SMEM buffer plus the release/ wait for buffer stages (producer/consumer handshake) prevents the producer from overwriting a stage still required by the consumer.

Why the algebra still matches the standard streamed-softmax derivation. The local-streaming softmax update depends on storing $m_i^{\mathrm{old}}$, computing the new $m_i=\max(m_i^{\mathrm{old}},\mathrm{rowmax}(\mathbf{S}^{(j)}))$, forming $\tilde{\mathbf{P}}^{(j)}=\exp(\mathbf{S}^{(j)}-m_i)$, and updating $\ell_i$ via $\ell_i \leftarrow \exp(m_i^{\mathrm{old}}-m_i)\,\ell_i + \mathrm{rowsum}(\tilde{\mathbf{P}}^{(j)})$. All of these depend only on the completed $\mathbf{S}^{(j)}$ (and on previously finalized $m_i,\ell_i$), and the code waits for that completion before applying them. The rescaling factor $\mathrm{diag}(\exp(m_i^{\mathrm{old}}-m_i))^{-1}$ is applied to the existing $\mathbf{O}_i$ only after the prior RS-GEMM has completed and after the new $m_i$ has been computed, exactly as required by the streaming softmax algebra. Thus mathematical correctness of the streamed softmax update is preserved.

Other practical protections. The paper notes compiler reordering concerns and shows they inspected SASS to ensure the intended waits/barriers survive compilation. It also uses explicit buffer stage handshakes (release/wait for buffer indices) so a producer cannot clobber a tile in shared memory that the consumer still needs. Finally, the ping-pong scheduling between warpgroups is enforced with \verb|bar.sync| to order which warpgroup's GEMMs are issued and which softmax executes while the other warpgroup's GEMMs run, avoiding cross-warp races.

Summary. FlashAttention-3 achieves overlap by issuing asynchronous GEMMs for throughput, but it never consumes a GEMM result until an explicit wait/commit point confirms completion. The saved $m_i^{\mathrm{old}}$, the updated $m_i,\ell_i$, and the rescaling of $\mathbf{O}_i$ are done only after the appropriate waits and buffer-handshakes, so numerical stability and the correctness of the streaming softmax algebra are preserved.

### What is the exact FLOPs accounting used for the forward and backward passes?
Question:
The paper uses 4 * seqlen^2 * head_dim * nheads FLOPs for the forward pass (halved for causal masks) and multiplies by 2.5 for the backward pass. Could someone derive the 4 factor for forward (is it precisely 2 GEMMs counting multiply-add as 2 FLOPs?), and the 2.5 factor for backward (from 5 matmuls vs 2 in forward)? Also, does the FLOPs counting include softmax exp, reductions, and rescalings, or are those excluded as non-GEMM FLOPs?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper’s forward FLOPs formula $4\cdot \text{seqlen}^2\cdot \text{head\_dim}\cdot \text{nheads}$ comes exactly from two dense matrix multiplies per head (each costing $2\cdot N^2\cdot d$ FLOPs if you count a multiply and an add as two FLOPs), and the backward multiplier $2.5$ is the simple ratio $5/2$ because a common backward implementation requires about five GEMMs vs two in the forward. The paper’s reported FLOPs focus on GEMM FLOPs; the softmax exponentials, row-reductions and scaling are lower-order non‑GEMM costs and are not included in that dominant FLOP count (they are discussed separately in the paper as non‑GEMM work that the algorithm hides/overlaps).

Why 4 in the forward (precise derivation). For one head with sequence length $N$ and head dimension $d$ the forward attention does these two dense matmuls:
1) compute the score matrix $\mathbf{S}=\alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$. This is a multiply of an $N\times d$ by a $d\times N$ matrix, costing $2\cdot N\cdot N\cdot d = 2N^2 d$ FLOPs under the standard “multiply + add = 2 FLOPs” convention.
2) compute the output $\mathbf{O}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$. Here $\mathbf{P}$ is $N\times N$ and $\mathbf{V}$ is $N\times d$, so this GEMM also costs $2N^2 d$ FLOPs.

Adding them gives $2N^2 d + 2N^2 d = 4N^2 d$ FLOPs per head. For multi‑head attention multiply by $\text{nheads}$, giving the paper’s expression. For causal (upper‑triangular) masking about half of the $N^2$ entries are unused on average, so dividing by $2$ is the usual approximation used in the paper.

Why 2.5 for backward (intuitive derivation). The backward pass of attention is more GEMM‑heavy. A typical chain of gradient computations (and practical implementations that count GEMMs) involves (roughly):
- $\mathbf{dV} = \mathbf{P}^\top \mathbf{dO}$ — one GEMM,
- $\mathbf{dP} = \mathbf{dO}\,\mathbf{V}^\top$ — one GEMM,
- materializing/working with $\mathbf{dS} = \mathrm{dsoftmax}(\mathbf{dP})$ — the softmax Jacobian acting on $\mathbf{dP}$ can be implemented with reductions and a structured computation that in practice is implemented using additional GEMM-like reductions (e.g., computing the row-wise scalar terms $p^\top \mathbf{dP}$ and broadcasting/multiplying them), and to get $\mathbf{dQ}=\alpha \mathbf{dS}\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{dK}=\alpha \mathbf{dS}^\top\mathbf{Q}$ you need two more GEMMs.

Counting the GEMMs used by a common implementation (including the extra matmuls needed to realize the $(\mathrm{diag}(p)-pp^\top)\mathbf{dP}$ structure efficiently or because of recomputation choices) yields about five GEMMs in total versus two GEMMs in the forward. So the backward GEMM FLOPs are about $5/2$ times the forward GEMM FLOPs; hence the paper multiplies the forward FLOPs by $2.5$ to report backward FLOPs. This is a practical accounting rule of thumb consistent with many implementations and with what the paper states.

What about softmax exp, reductions, rescalings? The paper’s FLOPs expression is the dominant GEMM FLOPs. The softmax-specific work (row-wise max subtraction, exponentials, row-wise sums, scalar rescalings, and the elementwise operations inside the softmax Jacobian) is important in wall‑clock time (and the paper explicitly discusses hiding/overlapping that work with GEMMs), but those operations are not included in the $4\cdot\ldots$ FLOP count. Two reasons the paper does this: (1) these are not large dense matmuls so their FLOP count is much smaller than the GEMM FLOPs for usual head dims and long sequences, and (2) the authors want a clean, comparable GEMM‑based FLOP metric where matmul FLOPs dominate. The paper explicitly calls out that non‑GEMM ops (e.g., exponentials) have far lower hardware throughput and motivates algorithmic work to overlap/hide them, which is why they are treated separately rather than folded into the $4\cdot N^2\cdot d$ term.

Caveats / practical notes. The $4\cdot N^2\cdot d$ and the $2.5$ multiplier are precise under the assumptions outlined above (multiply+add = 2 FLOPs, counting GEMMs as the dominant cost, and using the specific backward decomposition that uses $\sim 5$ GEMMs). Different implementations can change the constants: if you count fused multiply‑add (FMA) as a single FLOP the per‑GEMM FLOP count halves and the numeric factor would be expressed differently; if you avoid some recomputation or exploit other algebraic rearrangements you may reduce the number of GEMMs in the backward; and small sequence lengths or large non‑GEMM softmax cost may make the omitted softmax ops non‑negligible in practice. The paper uses the $4$ and $2.5$ accounting as a practical, implementation‑aware convention (and then separately discusses and optimizes the non‑GEMM softmax cost by overlapping it with GEMMs).

### How does setmaxnreg practically help with warp-specialization, and what are the trade-offs?
Question:
The paper mentions using setmaxnreg to realloc registers such that producer warps (mostly issuing TMA) get fewer registers, while consumer warps (doing WGMMA and softmax) get more. Could someone explain how this is orchestrated in a CTA with multiple warpgroups? How do you decide the register budget split, and what are the failure modes (e.g., spills due to too small budgets on producers, or occupancy loss on consumers)?
Answer:
Short answer: setmaxnreg lets the kernel dynamically reshape the per-warp register budget so you can deliberately give "producer" warps (those that mostly issue TMA loads) a very small register allotment and give "consumer" warps (those that run WGMMA + softmax and the pipelined stages) a larger allotment. Practically this enables warp-specialization: producers use few registers and mainly drive asynchronous loads into SMEM while consumers keep large working state (WGMMA accumulators, the extra staged tile like $\mathbf{S}_{next}$, softmax temporaries) in registers so they can sustain Tensor Core throughput without spilling. The trade‑offs are direct and must be tuned: too few regs for producers can force them to spill or serialize TMA bookkeeping; too many regs for consumers reduce CTA/SM occupancy and can actually lower Tensor Core utilization.

How it is orchestrated in a CTA with multiple warpgroups
The CTA is partitioned into warpgroups with fixed roles. At kernel entry the code calls setmaxnreg for the warpgroup(s) so that the hardware enforces different per‑warp register limits for producers vs consumers. Producers then issue TMA loads into the circular SMEM buffer and signal consumers (bar.sync or the pipeline object in the paper). Consumers wait for the loaded tiles in SMEM and launch asynchronous WGMMA instructions; because consumers were allocated more registers they can keep the following in RMEM without spilling:
- the WGMMA accumulator layout and state,
- the extra pipeline stage register tile (e.g. store of $\mathbf{S}_{next}$),
- softmax temporaries like rowmax, $\tilde{\mathbf{P}}$, and $\ell_i$,
- register buffers needed to invoke LDSM/STSM transposes for FP8.

Producers can be extremely cheap in register usage: TMA itself moves data asynchronously and most producer work is issuing TMA + small control. By lowering producer register limits you free RMEM to be redistributed to consumer warpgroups so consumers can hold those large temporaries.

How to decide the register-budget split (practical recipe)
There is no single analytic formula in the paper; the design is empirical and guided by resource accounting. Use the following principled process:

1. Account registers required by a consumer warp to avoid spilling in the desired pipeline depth. The paper calls out an extra per‑consumer data requirement roughly proportional to the pipeline tile sizes: extra register usage grows with $B_r \times B_c \times \text{sizeof(float)}$ (you can view this as the extra storage for $\mathbf{S}_{next}$ etc.). So estimate the consumer register footprint $R_{\text{cons}}(B_r,B_c,\text{stages})$ from your chosen tile sizes and the number of pipeline stages (2‑stage, 3‑stage).

2. Decide a minimal safe budget for producers $R_{\text{prod\_min}}$. Producers mainly issue TMA + LDSM/STSM and bookkeeping; in many implementations one thread per warpgroup does most control. Make $R_{\text{prod\_min}}$ as small as possible but large enough to avoid compiler temporaries spilling (e.g., test with a low bound and run the kernel to see if the compiler spills).

3. Use the SM register file constraints to compute how many warps/warpgroups you can co‑schedule at that split. The hardware limits (max warps per SM, max registers per SM) give a capacity equation: $$
\text{number\_of\_warps\_per\_SM} \times \text{avg\_regs\_per\_warp} \le \text{total\_SM\_registers}.
$$ You want enough consumer warps to saturate Tensor Cores but not so many regs per consumer that you end up with only one consumer warp per SM (which may underutilize latency hiding).

4. Profile and iterate. The paper’s results derive from this empirical tuning; they report that moving from a naive symmetric allocation to a producer‑light / consumer‑heavy split gave substantial TFLOP gains.

Failure modes and how they show up
- Producer spilling / stalls: If you set $R_{\text{prod}}$ too small, producer warps will spill temporaries to SMEM/global memory or the compiler will serialize some state. Since TMA is asynchronous, extra stalls show up as delayed producer commits and degraded overlapping with consumers. Symptoms: reduced overlap, lower effective TMA throughput, increased SMEM pressure and barriers taking longer to satisfy.

- Consumer spilling (the most catastrophic): If $R_{\text{cons}}$ is underestimated, the extra pipeline state (e.g., $\mathbf{S}_{next}$, WGMMA accumulators) will spill to SMEM or L2. Spills in consumer warps directly slow WGMMA/softmax because registers that should feed Tensor Cores become memory-bound. Symptoms: large drop in TFLOPs, increased latency, and sometimes higher L2/shared traffic.

- Occupancy loss: If you give each consumer warp too many registers, register file capacity limits will reduce the number of active warps/warpgroups per SM. Lower occupancy can reduce the ability to hide instruction latencies (barriers, memory waits) even though each warp individually has more registers. Symptoms: despite no spills, aggregate TFLOPs fall or flatten because you don’t have enough concurrent warps to keep the Tensor Cores busy when one wgmma is waiting.

- Synchronization timing and compiler reordering: The carefully arranged ping‑pong of GEMM and softmax relies on ordering of WGMMA and non‑WGMMA instructions. The compiler sometimes reorders instructions; register allocation changes can influence instruction scheduling. Symptoms: the observed overlap is less than expected; use SASS inspection to ensure the generated schedule overlaps as intended (the paper inspected SASS).

Mitigations and practical tips
Tune tile sizes and pipeline depth jointly with register split. If consumers require too many registers for the desired tile/pipeline depth, reduce $B_r$ or $B_c$ or drop pipeline depth from $3\to2$ (the paper notes a 2‑stage pipelining is a good trade). Use the following tools and checks the paper implies:

- Inspect assembler (SASS) to confirm WGMMA and softmax interleaving is emitted.
- Use the CUDA profiler (nvprof / Nsight / CUPTI counters) to detect register spills and to measure TMA/WGMMA utilization. Spills show as increase in memory traffic and lower SM occupancy.
- If producers spill, try giving them a few extra registers or collapsing some control into fewer threads (move more of the TMA control to a single thread in the warpgroup).
- If consumers cause occupancy loss, reduce their per‑warp reg demand or accept fewer pipeline stages / smaller tiles.
- For FP8, remember you also need registers for the LDSM/STSM transpose and accumulator permutations described in the paper—factor those into $R_{\text{cons}}$.

Intuition summary
setmaxnreg is a lever that moves limited physical RMEM from warps that don’t need much (producers) to warps that do (consumers). Properly set, it lets consumers keep heavy working state in registers and achieve high Tensor Core utilization by avoiding spills and enabling the pipelined overlap the paper exploits. The trade‑off is the classic register vs occupancy trade: too few registers → spills and stalls; too many → fewer active warps and worse latency hiding. The paper’s practical approach is to profile the consumer register needs implied by $(B_r,B_c,\text{pipeline depth})$, give producers a tiny budget, then iterate until you reach the sweet spot where WGMMA throughput is saturated without spilling.

Recommended tuning workflow (concise)
1. Pick tile sizes and target pipeline depth (start with 2‑stage). Estimate $R_{\text{cons}}$.
2. Set $R_{\text{prod}}$ to a low safe value, $R_{\text{cons}}$ to allow no‑spill according to local tests.
3. Run microbenchmarks, check profiler for spills and TMA/WGMMA utilization; inspect SASS for expected ordering.
4. If spills occur in consumers, reduce $R_{\text{cons}}$ by lowering tile size or pipeline depth. If occupancy is too low, reduce $R_{\text{cons}}$. If overlap is missing, check for producer stalls and increase $R_{\text{prod}}$ slightly or fix producer codegen.
5. Repeat until measured TFLOPs (or latency) is optimal.

This is exactly the balance used in FlashAttention‑3: producers are minimized so consumers can hold the additional pipeline state (e.g., $\mathbf{S}_{next}$ and softmax temporaries) in registers; tuning tile sizes and pipeline depth controls the register footprint and hence the occupancy vs. spill trade‑off.

### What determines the choice of block sizes B_r and B_c under register and SMEM constraints?
Question:
Choosing B_r (rows of Q per CTA) and B_c (K,V tiles) affects register pressure (e.g., storing S_next of size B_r x B_c) and SMEM usage (circular buffers of size s). How do you practically pick B_r and B_c on H100 to balance: (a) WGMMA tile efficiency, (b) space for multiple stages s in SMEM, (c) register headroom for the 2-stage pipeline without spills, and (d) occupancy? Any recommended heuristics or rules of thumb for head dimensions d in {64, 128, 256}?
Answer:
Short answer: there is no single “magic” $B_r$, $B_c$ — pick tiles so that the WGMMA/TMA-friendly tile shapes are respected, the per-CTA SMEM used by the s-stage circular buffer fits the SMEM allowance, and the per-CTA register footprint (including the extra $S_{\text{next}}$ buffer for the 2‑stage pipeline) fits without spilling while leaving enough registers to retain useful occupancy. Do this by writing the resource constraints as simple capacity inequalities and using them to pick $B_r$ and $B_c$; then verify with a quick compile/run to confirm no spills and reasonable SM occupancy. Below I give the intuition, the concrete capacity formulas you should use, and practical heuristics for head sizes $d \in \{64,128,256\}$.

Intuition and trade-offs
The four constraints in your question interact in obvious ways:

- WGMMA tile efficiency wants $B_r$ and $B_c$ aligned to the Warp/warpgroup tile shapes the Tensor Cores prefer (multiples of the thread/TMA/WGMMA sub-tile shapes, typically powers-of-two or multiples of 8/16/32 depending on the WGMMA primitive). Choosing incompatible tile shapes wastes Tensor Core utilization.
- SMEM (circular buffer of $s$ stages) requires $s$ copies (or staged storage) of the $K/V$ tiles (and some $Q$ scratch). Larger $B_c$ or larger $s$ increases SMEM per CTA and can force fewer concurrent CTAs per SM (lower occupancy).
- Registers: the 2-stage pipeline requires holding an extra score tile $S_{\text{next}}$ (size $B_r \times B_c$ in FP32/FP16) in registers (or at least partial rows/columns), plus the usual per-thread accumulators and $P/V$ fragments. Larger $B_r$ and $B_c$ therefore increase register pressure and cause spills if you exceed per-thread or per-warp register budget.
- Occupancy: occupancy is set by the least plentiful resource (SMEM, registers, or threadblock count). Lower occupancy hurts the ability to cover latency, but on Hopper some of the latency hiding is handled by asynchrony; still, you want some headroom.

Formulas to guide choices (use these to test candidates)
Write down the two resource inequalities and one WGMMA constraint you must satisfy.

1) SMEM constraint (per-CTA):
Let $\text{bytes}(x)$ be the storage in bytes for matrix of shape $x$ in the kernel’s working precision ($Q/K/V$ usually FP16/FP8; softmax accumulators often FP32). A safe upper bound for SMEM per CTA is:
SMEM_per_CTA ≈ $s \cdot (\text{bytes}(B_c \times d \text{ for } K) + \text{bytes}(B_c \times d \text{ for } V)) + \text{bytes}(B_r \times d \text{ for } Q \text{ buffer}) + \text{small overhead}$
So
$s \cdot 2 \cdot B_c \cdot d \cdot \text{sizeof(elem)} + B_r \cdot d \cdot \text{sizeof(elem)} + \text{overhead} \le \text{SMEM\_available\_per\_CTA}$
You must ensure this fits the SMEM you intend to reserve per CTA (and also consider the SMEM per-SM divided by CTA count if you want multiple CTAs per SM).

2) Register constraint (per-CTA / per-thread):
The register impact from the 2-stage pipeline is dominated by storing $S_{\text{next}}$. If you store the entire $S_{\text{next}}$ in registers per CTA (often partitioned across threads), the byte cost is:
$\text{Regs\_bytes\_for\_Snext} \approx B_r \cdot B_c \cdot \text{sizeof(accum)}$  (accum usually FP32)
Convert to registers by dividing by sizeof(register value, e.g., 4 bytes for 32-bit). Also add registers needed for accumulators, loaded tile fragments, and local control. The general constraint is:
$\text{Total\_regs\_per\_thread} \le \text{target\_regs\_per\_thread}$ (so the compiler will not spill)
or, equivalently,
$\text{Total\_regs\_per\_CTA} \le \text{num\_threads\_per\_CTA} \cdot \text{target\_regs\_per\_thread}$
On Hopper you can tune setmaxnreg to reallocate between warpgroups; still aim to keep per-thread registers under the compiler’s spilling threshold (experimentally check; spilling shows up in SASS/ptx or profiling).

3) WGMMA / layout constraint:
Choose $B_c$ (and $B_r$) that match WGMMA operand layout expectations: for FP8 WGMMA the K/V operand must be k-major; for FP16 WGMMA more layouts are allowed. Also choose block dimensions that are multiples of the WGMMA micro-tile (commonly multiples of 8/16/32). Using these multiples avoids extra permutations or padding.

Practical recipe (step-by-step)
1. Start from WGMMA-friendly block granularity. Pick $B_c \in \{64, 128, 256\}$ or multiples of 32/64 compatible with the WGMMA micro-tile. Pick $B_r$ as a smaller multiple of the warp tile in the row direction (e.g., 16, 32, 64). The goal: $B_r \times B_c$ should be divisible by the warp/warpgroup tile so each thread has a consistent portion.

2. Pick $s$ (stages) small: $s = 2$ or $3$. The paper uses a circular SMEM buffer and notes practical choices of $s$ are small (filling first $s$ iterations and then streaming), and the 2‑stage pipeline is described as the working default. Higher $s$ gives more temporal buffering but directly multiplies SMEM cost for K/V tiles.

3. Enforce SMEM: compute $s \cdot 2 \cdot B_c \cdot d \cdot \text{sizeof(elem)} + B_r \cdot d \cdot \text{sizeof(elem)}$ and ensure it fits within the SMEM you will allocate to a CTA. If you want multiple CTAs per SM, divide $\text{SMEM\_per\_SM}$ by $\text{target\_CTAs\_per\_SM}$. If it doesn’t fit, lower $B_c$ or $s$.

4. Enforce registers: estimate $S_{\text{next}}$ size $= B_r \cdot B_c \cdot \text{sizeof(accum)}$. Divide among threads ($S_{\text{next}}$ is partitioned across threads/warps). Check the per-thread register count budget (aim for comfortable headroom below the per-thread maximum to avoid spills and to allow setmaxnreg reallocation between producer/consumer warps). If the estimated register use is too high, reduce $B_r$ or $B_c$, or reduce pipeline depth (e.g., fall back to single-stage or 2-stage rather than 3-stage).

5. Validate occupancy: based on the final SMEM and register usage, compute how many CTAs can reside on an SM. If only 1 CTA/SM results, that may be acceptable on Hopper because WGMMA/TMA asynchrony plus high Tensor Core throughput can still keep the hardware busy, but if you see low measured utilization, try to increase occupancy by reducing per-CTA SMEM/registers.

6. Empirically verify: compile and run and check (a) SASS register spill indicators, (b) achieved TFLOPs and tensor core utilization, and (c) runtime. The paper emphasizes empirical profiling — the best tile often depends on head dimension, sequence length, and mask (causal vs non-causal).

Heuristics specifically for $d \in \{64, 128, 256\}$
- $d = 64$: smaller head dimension means each K/V row is shorter, so $B_c$ can be larger without exploding SMEM, and storing $S_{\text{next}}$ costs fewer registers for the same $B_r \times B_c$. Practical starting point: try $B_c = 128$, $B_r = 32$, $s = 2$. This gives good WGMMA packing and small register penalty for the 2‑stage pipeline. If you want even higher throughput with FP8, $B_c = 256$ can be tried as long as SMEM fits.

- $d = 128$: the paper uses many examples with head dim 128 and obtains good results. A conservative starting tile is $B_c = 128$ and $B_r = 32$. If you want to push GEMM utilization, try $B_c = 128$–$256$ but watch SMEM and registers; for the 2‑stage pipeline $B_r = 16$–$32$ is often a good compromise so that $S_{\text{next}}$ ($B_r\times B_c$) stays modest.

- $d = 256$: bigger head dimension increases per-tile memory and compute, so choose a smaller $B_r$ to keep $S_{\text{next}}$ and per-thread load down. Start with $B_c = 128$, $B_r = 16$, $s = 2$. This keeps the register footprint of $S_{\text{next}}$ controllable. If you need more arithmetic intensity, prefer increasing $B_c$ moderately rather than $B_r$.

Why prefer these patterns? Increasing $B_c$ raises the k-dimension of the inner GEMM, which is good for Tensor Core throughput (more matmul work per softmax), while increasing $B_r$ increases the number of output rows per CTA and directly scales $S_{\text{next}}$ and register pressure. Because the 2‑stage pipeline stores a whole $S_{\text{next}}$, it is safer to make $B_r$ the smaller of the two tile dims.

Other practical tips from the paper’s approach
- Use setmaxnreg to allocate more registers to the warps doing WGMMA and fewer to producer warps that only issue TMA/ldmatrix instructions. This lets you squeeze a larger pipeline register budget where it matters.
- For FP8, be aware of k-major constraints on WGMMA and the need to transpose V tiles in-kernel (the paper recommends in-kernel transpose via LDSM/STSM rather than expensive GMEM pre-transpose). These layout transformations can change the SMEM/reg trade-off and should be included in your budget.
- If you see compiler reordering upsetting your pipelining, examine generated SASS; small code changes (barriers, commit/wait ordering) may be necessary to ensure the intended schedule.
- Keep $s$ small (2–3) in practice; the paper shows good gains from 2-stage pipelining and uses ping-pong warp specialization to overlap softmax with GEMMs across warpgroups — that helps hide the low throughput of exponentials without requiring large $s$.

Summary checklist
1. Pick $B_c$ that matches WGMMA micro-tile multiples; pick $B_r$ smaller (multiple of warp tile).
2. Ensure $s \cdot 2 \cdot B_c \cdot d \cdot \text{bytes} + B_r \cdot d \cdot \text{bytes} \le \text{SMEM budget per CTA}$.
3. Ensure $B_r \cdot B_c \cdot \text{sizeof(accum)}$ partitioned across threads fits within available registers per thread (no spills) when added to other register needs.
4. Tune $s = 2$ (or $3$) and reduce $B_r$ if register pressure is the limiting factor; reduce $B_c$ if SMEM is the limiting factor.
5. Empirically profile for spills, SMEM usage, and TFLOPs; iterate.

### Why does FA-3 FP16 match or beat cuDNN at long sequences but FP8 lags in some masked cases?
Question:
The results indicate FA-3 FP16 outperforms or matches cuDNN for large seqlen, while FP8 is competitive but can lag for some head dims under causal masking. The discussion mentions the lack of a persistent kernel for FP8. Could someone expand on how persistence and load-balancing specifically impact small or masked workloads, and what kernel design changes would be needed to close the gap for FP8 under causal attention?
Answer:
Short answer: FP16 FA-3 attains or exceeds cuDNN at long sequences because its persistent-kernel + warp‑specialization + GEMM–softmax pipelining lets it reach high Tensor‑Core utilization in the steady state; FP8 can have higher peak GEMM throughput but in some masked (causal) or small‑seqlen workloads it falls behind because there is less steady-state work, the non‑GEMM softmax path and masking reduce the amount of overlappable matmul work, and the FP8 implementation in the paper lacks a persistent kernel and the careful load‑balancing / layout tricks that the FP16 kernel uses. Closing the FP8 gap requires making the FP8 kernel persistent and changing its scheduling and data‑layout handling so the same steady‑state overlap and balance are achieved despite FP8’s extra constraints.

Explanation and intuition (grounded in the paper). FlashAttention‑3 uses three pillars to get high throughput on Hopper: warp‑specialization (producer/consumer warps and TMA), asynchronous WGMMA Tensor‑Core GEMMs, and overlapping softmax with GEMMs. For long sequences there are many blocks of work, so the kernel can fill the pipeline: while one block’s softmax/exponentials are computed (low throughput hardware units), other warps/Tensor Cores are busy doing GEMMs (very high throughput). This hides the latency and the softmax cost becomes a small fraction of total cycles. FP16 FA‑3 further benefits from a persistent kernel and careful register allocation, so once the CTA/warp arrangement is hot the pipeline reaches high utilization and stays there; this is why FP16 FA‑3 matches or beats cuDNN for large seqlen.

Two reasons FP8 lags in some masked / small workloads (as discussed in the paper)
1) Reduced steady‑state opportunity from masking or small seqlen. Causal masking roughly halves the number of computed attention entries (roughly a factor of $1/2$ fewer FLOPs in the forward pass). Small seqlen or causal masking reduces the number of block iterations, so there are fewer independent GEMMs to overlap with softmax work. The softmax / special‑function units are fixed and relatively slow (paper quotes ~$3.9$ TFLOPS equivalent for exponentials), so with less matmul work to hide, those non‑GEMM operations consume a larger fraction of cycles and lower achieved TFLOPs. FP8 doubles available GEMM throughput (so GFLOPs demand is even more dominated by GEMM), which paradoxically makes the imbalance worse when GEMM cannot be kept busy for long enough blocks of work.

2) Implementation and layout / resource constraints specific to FP8. FP8 WGMMA has stricter layout requirements (only $k$‑major), and FP8 requires FP32 accumulators with a clashing register layout vs. FP8 operands. To meet the layout constraint the paper implements in‑kernel transposes via LDSM/STSM and byte permutes, and rearranges register/accumulator formats; these transformations consume registers and coordination and currently are scheduled in the producer path. But the FP8 kernel in the paper does not have a persistent kernel nor the same load‑balancing strategy as FP16 (authors explicitly note this in Limitations), so startup overheads, register pressure, or imperfect tiling can leave Tensor Cores underutilized in short/causal runs. In short: FP8 has higher potential peak, but the current FP8 kernel pays layout/registration/coordination costs and lacks the persistent steady‑state that FP16 achieves, so on workloads with less overlappable work it lags.

What “persistence” and “load‑balancing” concretely buy you. A persistent kernel pins a CTA/warp configuration on an SM and reuses the same resources across many block iterations, avoiding kernel launch and scheduling overhead and enabling aggressive register allocation choices (more registers for MMA warps, fewer for TMA warps via setmaxnreg), so the pipeline stays full. Load‑balancing here means partitioning work and choosing tile sizes / number of warps per role so that the slow softmax units and fast Tensor Cores are both busy: if you have too few GEMM blocks per CTA or wrong tile sizes, Tensor Cores idle while softmax runs, or softmax idles while Tensor Cores wait for data. For causal or short sequences you need finer‑grained balancing because you cannot amortize overhead across many iterations.

Concrete kernel / design changes to close the FP8 causal gap. The paper suggests these and I summarize / clarify what is needed:

1) Add a persistent FP8 kernel with the same producer/consumer warp‑specialization as FP16. Make the FP8 kernel persistent so that the CTA stays resident and the register allocation (including extra registers for overlapping stages) can be tuned. This removes launch and refill overheads and lets the GEMM–softmax ping‑pong reach steady state even for moderately short sequences.

2) Integrate the FP8 layout transforms into the persistent pipeline with minimal stalls. Rather than performing expensive in‑kernel transposes as separate blocking steps, fuse the LDSM/STSM transpose and the byte‑permutes into the producer warpgroup’s steady pipeline, and schedule them so the transpose of the next V tile runs in the shadow of current GEMMs (the paper already does this, but a persistent kernel allows more aggressive overlapping and reduces the amortized cost). Also precompute or prepack layouts when possible (e.g., keep a pretransposed V in GMEM or fuse transpose into upstream kernels like rotary embedding) to eliminate per‑inference per‑kernel transpose costs when memory permits.

3) Solve the accumulator/operand layout clash with low‑overhead register permutations inside the persistent kernel. The paper uses byte permutes to reorder the FP32 accumulator into the format the next FP8 WGMMA expects; making this part of the hot persistent path (and using register allocation tricks to avoid spills) reduces latency and avoids stalls. That may require tradeoffs in tile sizes to control register pressure.

4) Smaller tiles / dynamic tiling and better load distribution for masked triangular work. For causal attention the attention matrix is triangular; a persistent kernel that can process smaller blocks (smaller $B_r$ or $B_c$) and dynamically assign work between warps or CTAs will reduce idle time and improve utilization. In practice this means supporting multiple tiling modes (e.g., rectangular tiles near the diagonal for causal masks) and a lightweight scheduler within CTA to assign the remaining triangular tiles so the Tensor Cores remain busy.

5) Increase overlap depth or use $3$‑stage pipelining when register budget allows. For FP8 the GEMM is faster and softmax relatively more expensive, so a deeper pipeline ($3$‑stage) can hide more of the softmax cost. This requires more registers and careful compiler/SASS control, which a persistent kernel helps enable.

6) Microkernels for masked triangular matmuls. Implement specialized triangular GEMM kernels that avoid computing zeroed-out portions and map them efficiently to WGMMA, so work is not wasted and overlap is easier. This is similar to what vendor libraries do for causal attention; matching that in FP8 is necessary to compete.

Net effect: combining a persistent FP8 kernel, fused/streamed in‑kernel transpose and register reorder with careful tile sizing and dynamic load balancing (smaller tiles, more warps per CTA or per‑head batching), and possibly deeper pipelining, will let FP8 reach the same steady‑state overlap of GEMM and softmax that FP16 FA‑3 achieves today. In other words, the missing pieces are not new algorithms but engineering: make the FP8 implementation persistent, remove or hide transpose/permute costs by fusing them into the persistent pipeline, and tune tiling/load partitioning so causal / small‑seqlen runs still have enough overlappable GEMM work to keep Tensor Cores saturated.

Practical tradeoffs to watch: FP8 fixes (prepacking, persistent kernels, deeper pipelining) increase register usage and implementation complexity; you must trade tile size vs. register pressure and possibly add a few bytes of extra SMEM per CTA. For inference you may also accept prepacking V in GMEM if memory and one‑time preprocess cost allow it; for training you often prefer in‑kernel strategies to avoid extra memory traffic.

Closing summary: FP16 FA‑3 wins at long sequences because its persistent, well‑balanced pipeline sustains high Tensor‑Core utilization. FP8 has higher potential but the paper’s FP8 path still pays layout and startup/coordination costs and lacks persistence and the same load‑balancing tuned for causal/small cases. Making FP8 persistent, fusing and hiding the transpose/permute work inside the pipeline, adding dynamic/smaller tile scheduling for triangular masks, and possibly deeper overlap will close the gap.

### How is backward pass adapted to the asynchronous/pipelined design, especially for dsoftmax?
Question:
The paper focuses on the forward pass algorithm and mentions the backward in a later section. For the backward, dS = dsoftmax(dP) involves rowwise reductions dependent on P, and dQ, dK, dV each involve GEMMs. How are the producer-consumer split, circular buffers, and 2-stage (or 3-stage) pipelining applied in backward so that reductions in dsoftmax are overlapped with GEMMs, while preserving numerical stability with running max/rescale if needed? Any notable differences from the forward overlap strategy?
Answer:
Short answer: the backward pass in FlashAttention-3 is handled by the same producer/consumer + circular shared-memory buffer idea as the forward pass, but the pipeline stages are rearranged so the row-wise reductions needed by $d\text{softmax}$ run concurrently with the GEMMs that compute $dV$, $dP$, $dQ$, $dK$. Concretely, the implementation recomputes score blocks ($S = Q K^T$) and softmax blocks ($P$) on the fly (or reuses the compact block log-normalizers $L$ stored in the forward), uses register/SMs buffers to hold the intermediate $P$ and partial reductions, and schedules three kinds of work in a 2- or 3-stage pipeline: (a) an SS-GEMM to produce the next $S$ block, (b) a RS/SS-GEMM to produce a $dP$ or $dV$ block, and (c) a small-width, latency-bound rowwise reduction (the $d\text{softmax}$ reduction) that computes the per-row dot product $\sum(p * dP)$ and applies the $\operatorname{diag}(p)-p p^T$ formula. By assigning these roles across warp-specialized producer and consumer warps and by using the circular SMEM buffer and register scratchpads, the reducing work is overlapped under Tensor Core GEMMs while preserving numerical stability via the same running-max / rescale mechanics used in the forward pass.

Why this is possible (intuitive): modern GPUs (Hopper) expose asynchronous TMAs and asynchronous Tensor Core GEMMs (WGMMA). The reductions in $d\text{softmax}$ are small in FLOP count but latency-bound (exponentials, multiplies, reductions), while GEMMs are very high throughput. So you hide the reductions by scheduling them when another warpgroup’s Tensor Cores are busy. The backward has more GEMMs than the forward ($dV$, $dP$, $dQ$, $dK$), so there is even more “matmul surface” to hide the reduction latency behind.

Technical sketch (how it maps to the paper’s machinery)
1. Recompute or reuse softmax blocks
   - You need $P$ (or enough information to form $d\text{softmax}$). FlashAttention-3 follows the same memory-avoiding philosophy: it does not materialize full $S$ or $P$ in global memory. Instead you either (a) recompute the $S = Q K^T$ block and run the same local softmax as in forward (using the same running-max / running-sum rescale trick, producing the same per-block log-normalizer $L_i$), or (b) use the small per-block metadata ($L_i$ and possibly compact storage) saved at forward-time to stabilize recomputation. In either case you perform the softmax computation in the same numerically-stable way as forward: keep per-row running max $m$ and running sum $\ell$, apply the rowwise subtraction of $m$ and scaling factors exactly as in the forward algorithm to avoid overflow/underflow.
2. Compute $dP$ and the rowwise reduction for $d\text{softmax}$
   - $dP = dO\, V^T$ is a GEMM across blocks (RS-GEMM / SS-GEMM depending on storage). The $d\text{softmax}$ operation per row is
     $$
     dS_{\text{row}} = (\operatorname{diag}(p) - p p^T)\, dP_{\text{row}} = p * \big(dP_{\text{row}} - \big(\sum (p * dP_{\text{row}})\big)\big)
     $$
     so you must compute the scalar $r = \sum(p * dP_{\text{row}})$ (a rowwise dot-product) and then form $p * (dP_{\text{row}} - r)$.
   - Implementation: stream a block of $P$ (recomputed from $S$) and a block of $dP$ (from the GEMM). While a GEMM is running (asynchronous WGMMA), use other warps to do the dot-product reductions over the just-produced $p$ and $dP$ data. The running-max/rescale used in forward is applied identically during recomputation: compute $p = \operatorname{softmax}(S - m)$ with the same $m$ and $\ell$/rescale chain so that the exponentials are numerically stable. Perform the rowwise inner product in FP32 accumulators (keep reductions in higher precision), then apply the formula to yield the $dS$ block.
3. Use $dS$ to form $dQ$ and $dK$ via GEMMs
   - Once a $dS$ block is available, the two GEMMs $dQ = \alpha\, dS\, K$ and $dK = \alpha\, dS^T\, Q$ are launched as WGMMA operations. These GEMMs are also asynchronous and can be overlapped with other stages.
4. Producer-consumer split, circular buffering, and pipeline stages
   - Producer warps stream $Q$, $K$, $V$, $dO$ blocks (via TMA) into stages of a circular SMEM buffer as in the forward algorithm.
   - Consumer warps cycle through a multi-stage loop. A minimal 2-stage pipeline does: (stage A) issue WGMMA to compute next $S = Q K^T$, (stage B) issue WGMMA to compute $dP = dO V^T$ for the current block, and in between wait on the small non-GEMM reduction to compute $r = \sum(p * dP)$ and finish $dS$ for the previous block. Because GEMM latency is large and asynchronous, the reduction for block $j$ can be scheduled to run while GEMM for block $j+1$ (or $j+2$) is executing, thereby overlapping the latency.
   - When register pressure allows, a 3-stage pipeline can be used: $S_{\text{next}}$ compute, $dP_{\text{current}}$ GEMM, and reduction for the previous block are all live concurrently in different register/SMEM buffers. The third stage increases overlap but costs more registers (the paper discusses this tradeoff).
5. Numerical stability and rescaling
   - Use the exact same running-max/rescale pattern used in the forward pass: when recomputing softmax on a block you maintain the running rowwise maximum $m$ and running sum $\ell$, and apply the $\exp(S - m)$ pattern and incremental rescaling (diagonal rescales of $O$ in forward; here the same per-row $m/\ell$ values or the same rescale algebra is used so that softmax and $d\text{softmax}$ are consistent).
   - Perform reductions and exponentials in FP32 (or higher accumulator precision) even when GEMMs run in FP16/FP8. The paper emphasizes that intermediate softmax state is kept in higher precision (forward kept softmax in FP32 to reduce error); the backward should do the same for the reduction to retain numerical stability. If FP8 is used for matmuls, keep the softmax accumulators and reduction scalars in FP32 and apply block quantization / incoherent processing as in forward to mitigate FP8 quantization error.
6. Practical considerations called out in the paper
   - Compiler reordering: the pseudocode assumes a particular instruction ordering; in practice you need to enforce ordering via explicit bar.syncs and by careful SASS-level control (the paper describes verifying SASS).
   - Register pressure: storing extra $S_{\text{next}}$, partial $P$, and reduction accumulators increases register usage. That constrains block sizes; the same trade-offs described for the forward 2- vs 3-stage pipeline apply.
   - Overlap is easier in backward because there are more GEMMs to hide the reductions behind (paper reports $1.5$–$1.75\times$ backward speedup).
   - FP8 specifics: if you target FP8 for GEMMs, do GEMMs in FP8 with FP32 accumulators and keep softmax reductions in FP32; ensure layout transformations (in-kernel transpose and register permutations) are done as in forward so the pipeline remains valid.

How backward differs from forward in practice
The core ideas (producer/consumer, circular SMEM buffer, running max/rescale numerics, and overlapping small-elementwise ops under big GEMMs) are the same, but the backward has two practical differences. First, the backward requires explicit rowwise inner-product reductions ($\sum p * dP$) per row; these introduce an extra reduction stage that was not required in the forward (forward only needed rowmax and rowsum of exponentials). Second, there are more GEMMs overall ($dV$, $dP$, $dQ$, $dK$), which increases both the opportunity and the complexity of overlap: you can overlap reductions under multiple GEMM streams, but you also need more register/SMEM scratch space to stage intermediate $dS/dP$ blocks. Thus the backward often benefits from a 3-stage pipeline or more aggressive warp-specialization to balance register use vs. overlap, whereas the forward could get big wins already with a 2-stage ping-pong scheduling.

Summary
In short, FlashAttention-3 implements the backward by recomputing or reusing compact per-block normalization data, streaming small softmax/reduction work into separate warp-specialized lanes and overlapped buffers, and scheduling the reduction ($d\text{softmax}$) work to execute while Tensor Cores handle adjacent GEMMs. Numerical stability is preserved by applying the same running-max/rescale strategy and by doing reductions in higher precision; the main trade-offs are register pressure and compiler ordering, which motivate the 2- vs 3-stage pipeline choices described in the paper.
