\title{Blogs about the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

# Benchmarks That Matter: FA-3 vs FA-2 vs cuDNN on H100

## Summary

FlashAttention-3 (FA-3) rethinks attention for Hopper (H100) GPUs by exploiting asynchrony (warp-specialization + overlapping) and hardware FP8. The result: a practical attention primitive that is measurably faster than previous software (FlashAttention-2 / FA-2) and competitive with vendor libraries (cuDNN) in many real scenarios.

Key empirical takeaways from the paper:
- FA-3 achieves a 1.5–2.0× speedup over FA-2 in the forward pass and about 1.5–1.75× in the backward pass.
- FP16 FA-3 reaches up to 740 TFLOPs/s on H100 (about 75% of theoretical Tensor Core throughput for the tested configuration).
- FP8 FA-3 can reach nearly 1.2 PFLOPs/s on H100 in favorable settings.
- For medium and long sequences (≥ 1k tokens) FA-3 can match or beat cuDNN’s H100-optimized attention; in some long-sequence regimes it surpasses the vendor kernel.
- Numerical fidelity: FP16 FA-3 matches FA-2 (and is better than a standard attention implementation that spills softmax to lower precision). FP8 FA-3 with block quantization and incoherent processing reduces FP8 quantization error by about 2.6× compared to per-tensor FP8 baseline.

Below I unpack what these numbers mean, where FA-3 wins (and where it doesn’t), and practical guidance on choosing FP16 vs FP8 for real workloads.

## What the TFLOPs/s and utilization numbers mean

TFLOPs/s is a high-level proxy for how well a kernel uses Tensor Core throughput. H100 exposes very high theoretical GEMM throughput; reaching a high percentage of that number indicates an implementation efficiently schedules compute and hides memory/other-latency.

- FA-2 on H100 had low Tensor Core utilization (reported ≈35%). This means lots of compute potential was idle because of sequential dependencies, memory stalls, or inability to use Hopper-specific asynchronous instructions.
- FA-3’s engineering changes (producer/consumer warp specialization, pipelining softmax under asynchronous GEMMs, and FP8-aware layout/quantization) push utilization much higher — the paper reports up to ~75% for FP16 (740 TFLOPs/s) and even higher raw TFLOPs for FP8 (close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s) because FP8 GEMM throughput is roughly 2× FP16 on Hopper.

Important nuance: TFLOPs alone don’t prove better end-to-end performance for every shape. They are meaningful when the measured FLOP count is correct and comparable across implementations (the paper uses standard FLOP accounting for attention). The paper confirms that higher TFLOPs map to reduced wall-clock time across a wide sweep of sequence lengths and head sizes.

## Where FA-3 clearly wins

- Long and medium sequences (1k tokens and up): FA-3 is consistently faster than FA-2 and in many tested shapes is as fast as or faster than cuDNN’s H100-optimized attention. The memory-efficient fused kernel design plus better Tensor Core utilization is particularly effective for large-ish sequence lengths where GEMM dominates overall cost.
- Forward and backward speedups versus FA-2: across head dims and sequence lengths the paper reports 1.5–2.0× forward improvement and about 1.5–1.75× improvement in backward — a significant engineering win for both training and large-batch inference workloads.
- FP8 throughput: when accuracy requirements and workload shape align, FA-3’s FP8 path unlocks almost double the GEMM throughput of FP16, giving raw TFLOPs in the PFLOP/s regime on H100.

## Where FA-3 ties or lags

- Small sequence lengths and causal masking: vendor kernels (cuDNN FP8 kernels in particular) are still heavily tuned and can outperform FA-3 in some small-sequence or strictly causal scenarios. The paper notes FP8 FA-3 does not always outperform cuDNN for small sequences and causal masking; part of this is that FA-3’s FP8 implementation in the paper did not yet use the same persistent-kernel/load-balancing optimizations used in the FP16 path.
- FP8 sensitivity by head dimension: the paper reports that for head dimension 64 FP8 FA-3 is ahead, while for head dimensions 128 and 256 FP8 FA-3 is at par without causal masking and behind when causal masking is applied. So the FP8 advantage is shape-dependent: smaller heads can benefit more from FP8 in the reported experiments.

## Accuracy and FP8 precautions

Accuracy is not solved by raw throughput. The paper addresses numerical error rigorously:

- FP16 FA-3 keeps critical intermediate results (softmax scaling) in FP32 as needed, so numerical error matches FA-2 and improves on a naive standard implementation that downcasts intermediates.
- FP8 naively applied (per-tensor scaling) suffers from high quantization error, especially with outlier activations common in large models. FA-3 introduces two practical mitigations:
  - Block quantization: quantize per-block (block = the tile size used by the algorithm) instead of per-tensor. This reduces dynamic range pressure inside each block.
  - Incoherent processing: multiply Q and K by a cheap random orthogonal transform (Hadamard + sign diagonals) before quantization; because the transform is orthogonal, the attention product is unchanged in exact arithmetic, but quantization errors are more evenly spread, reducing worst-case impact of outliers.
- With these mitigations, FP8 FA-3 reduces RMSE by ≈2.6× compared to a baseline per-tensor FP8 attention implementation tested in the paper. That’s a meaningful improvement, but FP8 still has higher absolute error than FP16; whether that is acceptable depends on your training/inference sensitivity.

## Practical guidance: when to pick FP16 vs FP8 (based on the paper)

- Default choice: FP16 FA-3. If you want predictable accuracy, robust numerical behavior, and large speedups over FA-2 (and often over vendor kernels for medium/long sequences), FP16 FA-3 is the safest and most practical pick. It gives large speedups while preserving numerical fidelity (softmax reduction kept in FP32).
- Choose FP8 when:
  - You need maximal throughput and your workload shape matches the FP8 sweet spot: medium/large sequence length, and head dimensions where FP8 FA-3 is competitive or ahead (e.g., headdir size 64 favored in the paper; other shapes may vary).
  - You can tolerate some quantization noise or you have validated that block quantization + incoherent processing yields acceptable accuracy for your model. The paper’s techniques reduce FP8 error significantly versus naive FP8.
  - You are running non-causal attention or your implementation includes persistent kernel optimizations for FP8 (the paper notes the FP8 kernel was not yet persistent/fully load-balanced, which affected small-sequence and causal-case performance).
- Avoid FP8 if:
  - You are sensitive to even modest numerical differences (e.g., certain training regimes where small numerical differences amplify).
  - Your typical sequences are short or predominantly causal and you rely on minimal latency rather than peak throughput. In those cases vendor kernels or FP16 FA-3 may be better.
- Always validate: before switching model training/inference to FP8 at scale, run an empirical fidelity check on your task. The paper’s block quantization and incoherent transforms are promising, but their effectiveness depends on per-model activation statistics.

## Real-workload expectations

If your real workload is long-context inference (documents, long dialogues, high-resolution modality tokens) or training with long sequences, FA-3 should give you immediate runtime benefits over FA-2 and in many cases improve on cuDNN’s runtime in the medium/long sequence regime. Expect:
- 1.5–2× forward speedups over FA-2; similar scaled wins in backward.
- FP16 performance that is robust and often reaches ~70–75% of the H100 Tensor Core FLOP roof for the tested shapes.
- If you enable FP8 and apply block quantization + incoherent processing, you can get near-doubling of GEMM throughput, but verify numerical requirements and test causal/small-sequence cases carefully.

## Final notes

FA-3 is a systems-level advance — it combines algorithmic changes (pipelined/overlapped softmax + GEMM) with careful hardware-aware engineering (warp specialization, TMA/WGMMA use, FP8 layout handling) and numerical techniques for low-precision. The paper demonstrates that these combined moves are necessary to extract the performance Hopper hardware can provide, and that reasonable FP8 accuracy is achievable with block-level and incoherent quantization tricks.

If you operate on H100-class hardware and your workloads include medium/long sequences, FP16 FA-3 is an excellent first drop-in. Consider FP8 for throughput-hungry, validated workloads where you can tolerate a little error or have applied FA-3’s FP8 mitigations and additional kernel optimizations.
