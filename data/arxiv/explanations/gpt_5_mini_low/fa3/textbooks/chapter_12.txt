\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

Chapter 12: Empirical Results: Performance and Accuracy

# Throughput: FP16 and FP8 peak performance
FlashAttention-3 (FA-3) demonstrates substantial throughput improvements on the NVIDIA H100 SXM5 GPU by exploiting asynchrony and low-precision Tensor Core execution. Measured peak forward-pass throughput for FA-3 in FP16 reaches up to 740 TFLOPs/s, which the authors report as approximately 75% of the theoretical Tensor Core FP16 peak on that device. When targeting FP8 Tensor Cores, FA-3 attains throughputs approaching 1.2 PFLOPs/s. These measured numbers summarize the central hardware-efficiency gains: FP16 FA-3 substantially raises Tensor Core utilization relative to prior implementations, while FP8 FA-3 leverages the doubled FP8 Tensor Core throughput to approach petaflop-scale sustained performance.

Two points are important for interpreting these throughput figures. First, reported TFLOPs/s are computed using the problem’s floating-point operation count (the authors use the formula given in their benchmarking setup for forward and backward FLOPs). Second, the high utilization in FP16 (75%) and the nearly-petaflop FP8 peak are the result of multiple algorithmic and implementation techniques combined in FA-3: warp-specialization (producer/consumer partitioning), pipelined overlap of GEMM and softmax, efficient use of asynchronous WGMMA/TMA, and FP8-specific layout/transformation handling that enables legal, high-throughput FP8 WGMMA calls.

# Forward and backward speedups versus FlashAttention-2 and standard attention
FA-3 yields consistent and large speedups compared to both FlashAttention-2 (FA-2) and “standard” attention implementations that materialize intermediate matrices to DRAM.

For the forward pass in FP16, FA-3 achieves a 1.5–2.0× speedup over FA-2 across the evaluated settings. The authors report that for certain long-sequence settings FA-3 reaches the 740 TFLOPs/s peak mentioned above, while FA-2 attains much lower utilization (e.g., prior work measured FA-2 to be around 35% utilization on H100). Against a baseline standard attention implementation (which materializes the full score and softmax matrices in HBM), FA-3 is even more dramatic: the paper reports FA-3 can be up to 3–16× faster depending on sequence length and problem size.

For the backward pass, FA-3 also improves performance: the backward pass speedup over FA-2 is reported as roughly 1.5–1.75×. The authors explain that the backward pass contains more matmuls (and recomputation in their benchmarks), and FA-3’s improvements in GEMM utilization and overlap similarly pay off there, yielding comparable relative gains.

Those speedups are the result of FA-3’s combination of techniques: asynchronous producer-consumer warp-specialization to keep TMA and WGMMA busy, ping-pong and intra-warpgroup pipelining to hide low-throughput softmax work underneath GEMMs, and, in the FP8 variant, layout and register transformations that allow back-to-back FP8 WGMMA usage.

# Comparisons with vendor libraries (cuDNN) across head dimensions and masking
The paper compares FA-3 to a vendor-optimized implementation (cuDNN) as well as FA-2 implementations including a Triton variant that uses H100-specific instructions. The main empirical patterns reported:

- For medium to long sequence lengths (roughly 1k tokens and above), FA-3 matches or surpasses the performance of the vendor’s cuDNN implementation in many evaluated configurations. This is noteworthy because cuDNN contains closed-source, device-specific optimizations targeting H100.
- The relative advantage of FA-3 depends on head dimension and causal masking. For large sequence lengths, FA-3 in FP16 outperforms the cuDNN kernel. For FP8 FA-3, the behavior is mixed: for head dimension 64 FA-3 FP8 is ahead of cuDNN, while for head dimensions 128 and 256 it is comparable for non-causal cases and behind cuDNN when causal masking is applied. The authors note that part of this behavior for FP8 arises because their FP8 kernel lacks the persistent-kernel and load-balancing strategies that their FP16 implementation uses; thus FP8 can be comparatively weaker for smaller sequences and in causal settings where kernel persistence helps.

These comparisons emphasize that FA-3 narrows and often closes the gap with vendor libraries (and in some regimes exceeds them), while also providing a general, open-source implementation that benefits from the hardware-focused algorithmic choices described in the paper.

# Ablations: warp-specialization and 2-stage GEMM–softmax pipelining
To isolate the contributions of FA-3’s algorithmic innovations, the authors performed ablations that turn off individual components and measure performance changes on a representative configuration: batch = 4, sequence length 8448, 16 heads, head dimension 128. Two ablated factors of particular interest are (1) warp-specialization (producer/consumer partitioning to exploit asynchronous TMA and WGMMA) and (2) the 2-stage pipelined overlap that interleaves GEMMs and softmax inside the consumer warpgroup.

The reported ablation results show that enabling warp-specialization and the 2-stage pipelining together yields large performance gains: the baseline (no pipelining / no warp-specialization) in that setting runs around 570 TFLOPs, whereas with the full FA-3 combination the measured performance increases to about 661 TFLOPs. Thus the combined asynchronous scheduling techniques contribute a sizeable portion of the overall speedup (the concrete example in the paper shows improvement from 570 to 661 TFLOPs in that configuration). The ablation supports the claim that overlapping low-throughput softmax operations (exponential/sum) with high-throughput Tensor Core GEMMs and separating producer/consumer roles into specialized warpgroups are both important for achieving high Tensor Core utilization.

The ablation discussion also highlights practical trade-offs the authors faced: (a) compiler reordering can interfere with intended instruction-level overlap and requires careful control and inspection of generated assembly, (b) register pressure increases when adding pipelining stages because intermediate buffers such as a preserved \mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}} must be held live in registers, and (c) deeper pipelining (e.g., a 3-stage pipeline) can further increase overlap but also exacerbates register-pressure trade-offs and tile-size choices.

# RMSE evaluation versus FP64 reference with synthetic outliers
Accuracy is a central concern when moving to lower-precision formats such as FP8. The authors evaluate numerical error of FA-3 and baselines against an FP64 reference implementation under a synthetic data distribution designed to mimic rare outlier activations observed in large models. Each entry of \mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, and \mathbf{V} is drawn from

\[
\mathcal{N}(0,1) + \mathcal{N}(0,100)\cdot \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.001),
\]

i.e., typical entries are standard normal but with 0.1% probability an additional high-variance (std 10) term is added to create rare large-magnitude outliers.

The paper reports root-mean-square error (RMSE) against the FP64 reference for several implementations and precisions:

- In FP16, both FA-2 and FA-3 achieve about 1.7× lower RMSE than a standard attention implementation that materializes intermediates in HBM. The improvement stems from keeping intermediate softmax-related results in higher precision (FP32 accumulators / intermediate reductions) inside the fused FA kernels.
- For FP8, a baseline attention implementation that uses per-tensor quantization is substantially less accurate. FA-3 introduces two techniques to reduce FP8 quantization error: block quantization (one scale per block) and incoherent processing (random orthogonalization before quantization, implemented efficiently as Hadamard- and sign-matrix products fused with prior operations). With those techniques, FA-3 in FP8 is reported to be approximately 2.6× more accurate (lower RMSE) than the baseline FP8 attention that uses per-tensor scaling.

Thus, FA-3’s FP8-oriented accuracy techniques materially reduce the adverse impact of low mantissa width and magnitudes outliers, bringing FP8 attention closer to acceptable numerical fidelity for workloads with outliers.

# When FP8 leads or lags: dependence on head dimension and causal masking
The empirical behavior of FA-3’s FP8 implementation varies with head dimension and whether causal masking is applied. The authors summarize the observed patterns:

- For head dimension 64, FA-3 in FP8 tends to be ahead of cuDNN’s implementation (i.e., faster) in the forward pass for large sequences.
- For head dimensions 128 and 256, FP8 FA-3 is competitive (on par) with cuDNN for non-causal attention on large sequences, but it lags behind cuDNN when causal masking is applied.
- The paper attributes some of the FP8 shortfalls in small/causal regimes to the lack of persistent-kernel and load-balancing optimizations in the authors’ FP8 kernel (their FP16 kernel has a persistent kernel and load-balancing strategy). In small or causal workloads, kernel launch overheads and less effective overlap can make FP8 FA-3 less advantageous even if raw Tensor Core throughput is higher.

In summary, FP8 FA-3 achieves impressive asymptotic throughput on long sequences and small-to-moderate head sizes, and its accuracy is materially improved over naive per-tensor FP8 attention through block quantization and incoherent processing. That said, for some head sizes and masked (causal) workloads—especially those with short sequences or where persistent kernels matter—FP8 FA-3 may be at best competitive or sometimes behind highly tuned vendor kernels.

# Key empirical takeaways
Putting the results together, the empirical study in the paper supports these central conclusions:

- FA-3 substantially raises effective Tensor Core utilization for attention on Hopper H100 GPUs. FP16 FA-3 reaches up to 740 TFLOPs/s (∼75% utilization), while FP8 FA-3 can approach 1.2 PFLOPs/s in favorable regimes.
- Compared to FA-2, FA-3 provides large speedups: roughly 1.5–2.0× in the forward pass and 1.5–1.75× in the backward pass, and compared to standard attention implementations the wins can be much larger (3–16× depending on size and masking).
- FA-3 is often competitive with and sometimes surpasses vendor libraries (cuDNN) for medium-to-long sequences; however, for FP8 its advantage depends on head dimension and masking, with smaller head dims favoring FA-3.
- The ablations show that warp-specialization and the WGMMA–softmax pipelining are substantive contributors to the observed throughput improvements, though they come with practical trade-offs (compiler ordering, register pressure).
- On numerical accuracy, FA-3 preserves or improves accuracy in FP16 relative to FA-2 (owing to fused, higher-precision intermediate reductions) and—crucially—implements block quantization plus incoherent processing to reduce FP8 error by about 2.6× compared to a per-tensor FP8 baseline under an outlier-prone data distribution.

These empirical results together demonstrate that careful algorithm design that incorporates hardware asynchrony and low-precision constraints can deliver both large practical speedups and manageable numerical accuracy for attention, especially in the long-context regimes that motivate these optimizations.
