\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Does NF4 rescale by standard deviation or by absolute max during quantization?
Question:
I'm reading the QLoRA paper and I'm confused about the normalization step for NF4. In the NF4 paragraph, they say weights are transformed into the range $[-1,1]$ and mention matching the standard deviation of the data type, but the concrete procedure for block-wise quantization earlier uses absolute max rescaling. For NF4 specifically, do they normalize each block by $\text{absmax}$ (as in Eq. 1), or by an estimate of $\sigma$ to match the $N(0,1)$ quantiles? If it is $\text{absmax}$ per block for step (3), how does that remain "information-theoretically optimal" for a normal distribution whose tails vary block-to-block?
Answer:
Short answer: NF4 uses the same block-wise absolute-max rescaling as the rest of the paper ($( \text{Eq.\ 1} )$). In the NF4 description the authors phrase this as “match the standard deviation of the data type,” but concretely step (3) is an absolute-max normalization of each block into the fixed NF$k$ range (they normalize the tensor into $[-1,1]$ per block and then apply the NF4 quantization).

Why the two descriptions aren’t contradictory: NF4 is derived by computing an information‑theoretic optimal $k$-bit code for a standard normal, i.e. you precompute the quantile bin centers $q_i$ from $N(0,1)$. Those $q_i$ are then linearly mapped into the range $[-1,1]$ to form the NF$k$ codebook. When you quantize an actual weight block you must bring that block into the NF$k$ range; the paper does that with the usual blockwise absolute‑max scale $c$ ($( \text{Eq.\ 1} )$). If the block is $X_{\text{block}}$, you compute $c = \max(|X_{\text{block}}|)$ and do $\mathrm{round}(\text{scale\_factor} \cdot X_{\text{block}})$ where the $\text{scale\_factor}$ maps block values into $[-1,1]$. The paper points out that this per‑block absmax rescaling is equivalent to "rescaling the block standard deviation to match the standard deviation of the $k$-bit data type" in expectation: for a block drawn from $N(0,\sigma^2)$, multiplying by $1/\text{absmax}$ is equivalent to dividing by a scale that is (randomly) related to $\sigma$, so after scaling the block roughly matches the NF$k$ distribution used to choose the quantile bins.

Intuition about optimality and block‑to‑block tail variation: the NF4 codebook is information‑theoretically optimal under the assumption that values are zero‑mean normal up to a single scale parameter. If every block were exactly $N(0,\sigma^2)$ with the same $\sigma$, you could use a single global linear scale chosen from $\sigma$ and the NF4 bins would match exactly. In practice blocks have different $\sigma$ and different extreme values (outliers). The practical compromise the paper makes is (a) use blockwise scaling (absmax per block) so each block gets its own linear normalization, and (b) use a reasonably small blocksize (they use 64) to limit how much tail/outlier heterogeneity is pooled into a single scale. That combination gives a fast, simple implementation that approximates the theoretical assumption (normal up to scale) well enough to yield the empirical benefits they report. The asymmetric construction (ensuring an exact discrete zero) is an extra practical tweak they describe.

So: NF4 = (precomputed quantile-based codebook for $N(0,1)$ mapped to $[-1,1]$) + (per-block absolute-max linear normalization of the weights into that range) — the “standard deviation matching” is an interpretive view of that linear normalization, not a separate per‑block sigma estimation step.

### How exactly are the NF4 codebook values computed and why is it ‘information-theoretically optimal’?
Question:
The paper defines NF4 by taking mid-quantiles of $N(0,1)$: $q_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(Q_X(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1}) + Q_X(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1})\big)$ and then normalizing to $[-1,1]$. 1) Why take midpoints between adjacent quantiles rather than the quantile boundaries themselves? 2) In what precise sense is this "information-theoretically optimal" for zero-mean Gaussians—does it minimize expected quantization error under $N(0,1)$, or maximize entropy of bin occupancy, or both? 3) How does the asymmetric construction to include an exact zero (splitting positive/negative bins) affect optimality guarantees?
Answer:
Short answer: NF4 takes midpoints of adjacent standard-normal quantiles because those midpoints are the natural reconstruction levels (centroids) for quantile/equiprobable bins and therefore minimize squared reconstruction error for that binning. Calling NF4 “information‑theoretically optimal for normal weights” refers to two related facts: using quantiles makes the discrete symbols equiprobable (maximizing the entropy of the symbol distribution for a fixed number of levels), and using the bin centroids (midpoints, or more generally the conditional means) minimizes expected squared error under the assumed distribution. The asymmetric tweak that forces an exact zero trades away perfect symmetry/equiprobability for an exact zero codepoint; this slightly perturbs the strict optimality guarantees but in practice has negligible effect and is important for exactly representing true zeros (padding, sparse activations).

Longer explanation with the technical intuition and how it matches the paper

1) Why midpoints (average of adjacent quantiles) rather than boundaries?
The paper constructs the $k$-bit codebook from the theoretical $N(0,1)$ quantiles. The quantiles (the boundaries) partition the real line into $2^k$ equiprobable bins. For a given binning, the optimal reconstruction value for each bin under mean squared error (MSE) is the conditional mean of the distribution inside that bin (the centroid). For narrow bins of a smooth distribution the centroid is well-approximated by the midpoint between the bin boundaries. The paper uses
$$q_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(Q_X\bigl(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1}\bigr) + Q_X\bigl(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1}\bigr)\bigr)$$
which is exactly the midpoint of the $i$-th equiprobable interval of $N(0,1)$. Using midpoints (or centroids) is what gives you small reconstruction error; using boundaries as reconstruction values would be a poor choice because boundaries are the thresholds not the representative values. So midpoints are the natural reconstruction levels for quantile-based (equiprobable) quantization and are the reason NF4 performs better than using plain 4-bit floats or integers.

2) What does “information‑theoretically optimal for zero‑mean Gaussians” precisely mean?
There are two closely related senses the paper leans on:

- Equiprobable bins maximize the discrete-entropy of the quantized symbol distribution. If you split the continuous $N(0,1)$ mass into $2^k$ bins each with probability $1/2^k$, then the resulting discrete symbol has maximum entropy $\log(2^k)=k$ bits (i.e., the most efficient use of the available symbol set). This is the sense of “information‑theoretically optimal” regarding symbol occupancy: no level is wasted due to lopsided bin populations.

- Given that binning, choosing the reconstruction value for each bin as the centroid (conditional mean) minimizes the expected squared error (the usual distortion measure). Quantile binning together with centroid reconstruction is a standard optimal (or near‑optimal) design in scalar quantization (a special case of the Lloyd–Max framework) when you want to control both occupancy and MSE for a known distribution.

So NF4 is “optimal” in the paper’s statement because (a) the bins are chosen to be equiprobable for $N(0,1)$ (maximizing entropy / equally using levels) and (b) the reconstruction points are the midpoints (centroids) that minimize MSE for those bins. Put differently: for the model assumption “weights $\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$” after scaling, NF$k$ minimizes expected reconstruction error among quantizers that use those equiprobable bins and uses all available symbol resolution efficiently.

Note: exact formal optimality in quantization theory (e.g., the global Lloyd–Max optimum) couples both bin boundaries and reconstruction points and often requires iterative optimization; for a known smooth distribution, the equiprobable partition with bin centroids is a near-optimal, analytically simple choice and has the information/entropy property described. The paper exploits the practical and theoretical advantages of that choice for normally distributed weights.

3) How does the asymmetric construction to include an exact zero affect optimality?
A perfectly symmetric equiprobable $k$-bit quantizer for a symmetric distribution like $N(0,1)$ would place an identical bin straddling zero, but that bin’s centroid would generally not be exactly zero. The paper wants an exact representable zero (a discrete code that maps exactly to real $0$) because zero appears frequently (padding, sparse values) and having an exact zero avoids any quantization error for those exact zeros.

To achieve that, they build an asymmetric codebook: split the negative side into $2^{k-1}$ bins and the positive side into $2^{k-1}+1$ bins (or vice versa), compute the midpoints for both sides, then unify and remove the duplicate zero so that one code exactly equals zero. This makes one side have one more/one fewer bin so that zero becomes an exact centroid/codepoint.

The consequence is that bins are no longer perfectly equiprobable; one or two bins have slightly different probability mass. That breaks the strict form of the equiprobability/maximum-entropy guarantee and means the MSE-optimality is slightly perturbed. In practice the effect is tiny: for modest $k$ (e.g., $k=4$) and the smooth normal density, redistributing one bin across the sign boundary only slightly changes per-bin probabilities and centroids, so expected distortion and symbol-entropy remain essentially as good as the symmetric case, while gaining the practical benefit of exact zero representation. The paper explicitly notes this tradeoff and uses the asymmetric scheme because representing zero exactly is valuable in practice (and empirical evaluations show NF4 still outperforms FP4/Int4).

Practical remarks linking to the paper
The paper also normalizes the codebook to $[-1,1]$ and applies blockwise absolute‑max scaling so that each weight block’s standard deviation matches the NF$k$ scale; that ensures the NF$k$ codepoints are the right absolute magnitudes for the particular block. The combination of theoretical quantiles (for $N(0,1)$), centroids (midpoints), block rescaling, and the asymmetric zero point is why NF4 yields lower quantization error empirically and why the authors call it “information‑theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights” in the operational sense described above.

In short: midpoints = representative/centroid values that minimize MSE for equiprobable bins; equiprobable bins = maximal symbol entropy (efficient use of $k$ bits); the zero-fixing asymmetry slightly breaks perfect optimality but is a tiny practical concession that gives the useful property of an exact zero codepoint.

### What does “Double Quantization” quantize, and is it different from quantizing weights twice?
Question:
Section Double Quantization says they quantize the quantization constants $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ into $c_2^{\text{FP8}}$ with a second level constant $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$. I first thought this meant weights themselves get quantized twice. Can someone confirm the exact pipeline? As I understand it: weights $\mathbf{W}$ are quantized into $\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}$ using per-block scales $c_2$; then $c_2$ values are themselves block-quantized to FP8 with their own per-block scale $c_1$. At runtime, you dequantize $c_2$ via $c_1$, then dequantize $\mathbf{W}$ via the recovered $c_2$. Is that correct? Are there any numerical stability issues from dequantizing a dequantized scale?
Answer:
Short answer: Double Quantization does NOT quantize the weights twice. The pipeline quantizes the weights once (to NF4) using per-block FP32 scales $c_2$, then quantizes those scale values themselves ($c_2^{\mathrm{FP8}}$) into an 8-bit representation ($c_2^{\mathrm{FP8}}$) with a second-level FP32 scale $c_1$. At runtime you first dequantize the stored $c_2^{\mathrm{FP8}}$ back to FP32 using $c_1$, then dequantize the NF4 weight blocks using the recovered $c_2$ to get the BF16 weights for computation. The paper shows this is numerically safe in practice and causes no measurable performance drop.

Explanation and details tied to the paper
The paper describes three layers of values and how they are stored:

1) The weight blocks: $\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{NF4}}$ (stored in NF4, 4-bit). Each block is quantized by dividing the original FP32 block by a per-block quantization constant $c_2^{\mathrm{FP32}}$ and rounding into the NF4 bin values.

2) The first-level quantization constants: $c_2^{\mathrm{FP32}}$. There is one $c_2$ per weight block (blocksize 64 in the paper’s setup). Storing all of these as FP32 is relatively expensive (about $32/64 = 0.5$ bits/parameter on average), so the paper applies the second quantization to these $c_2$ values.

3) The second-level quantization of scales: $c_2^{\mathrm{FP8}}$ (8-bit floats) plus a second-level global/per-block (depending on choice) FP32 scale $c_1^{\mathrm{FP32}}$ used to dequantize the $c_2$ values. In the paper they use blocksize 256 for the $c_2$ quantization and symmetric quantization after subtracting the mean (because $c_2$ are positive, they center before quantizing).

Runtime dequantization (as given in the paper)
You perform $\text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\mathrm{FP32}}, c_2^{k\text{-bit}}, W^{k\text{-bit}})$ as

- recovered_c2 = dequant($c_1^{\mathrm{FP32}}$, $c_2^{k\text{-bit}}$) — i.e., convert the stored $c_2^{\mathrm{FP8}}$ back to FP32 using $c_1$,
- $W^{\mathrm{BF16}}$ = dequant(recovered_c2, $\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{NF4}}$) — i.e., convert the NF4 weight bins back to BF16 using the recovered per-block scale.

So the weights themselves are stored only once (in NF4). The “double” refers to quantizing the scale constants ($c_2$) a second time to save memory.

Why this is numerically okay (intuitive + technical)
Intuitively, the $c_2$ values are far fewer and have much lower dynamic range/entropy than raw weights. They are just positive scale factors (one per block) and tend to vary smoothly across blocks. Quantizing those few numbers to FP8 (with centering) loses very little information relative to quantizing the whole weight tensor. The paper reports using FP8 for $c_2$ with blocksize 256 and states that “no performance degradation is observed” from this second quantization.

Technically, the design choices protect numerical stability:

- $c_1$ (the second-level scale) is stored in FP32 (paper explicitly uses $c_1^{\mathrm{FP32}}$) so the mapping from stored $c_2^{\mathrm{FP8}}$ back to FP32 uses a high-precision multiplier, avoiding catastrophic loss when recovering the per-block scale.
- They subtract the mean from $c_2$ before quantization to center values and use symmetric quantization, which reduces range and better uses FP8 dynamic range.
- They choose relatively large blocksize (256) for $c_2$ quantization so each $c_1$ covers many $c_2$ entries, limiting overhead but keeping $c_2$ variation modest within that block.
- In practice NF4 + double quantization matched 16-bit baselines (paper’s empirical results), indicating no meaningful instability introduced by double-dequantizing.

Small caveats / potential failure modes
While the paper reports no degradation, theoretically double dequantization could amplify relative errors in extreme cases: if a recovered $c_2$ is very small (close to zero) and its FP8 quantization error is a significant fraction of its value, dividing weights by this noisy scale during dequantization could inflate relative error for those blocks. The mitigation in the paper is: use FP32 for $c_1$, center $c_2$ prior to FP8, and choose block sizes that keep $c_2$ variance moderate. Empirically these choices were sufficient — the authors report that double quantization reduces the quantization-constant memory ($0.5$ bits/param $\to$ $\approx 0.127$ bits/param) with no observed accuracy loss.

Conclusion
Your interpretation is correct: weights are quantized once (to NF4) with per-block scales $c_2$, then the $c_2$ values are quantized (to FP8) with a second-level scale $c_1$. At runtime you dequantize $c_2$ using $c_1$ and then dequantize the weights using the recovered $c_2$. The paper shows this double-dequantize procedure is numerically stable in practice and does not degrade performance when using the stated choices (NF4 for weights, FP8 for $c_2$, subtract-mean centering, blocksize 64 for weights and 256 for $c_2$, and keeping $c_1$ in FP32).

### Deriving the memory savings of Double Quantization: where does the 0.373 bits/parameter come from?
Question:
The paper claims that with blocksize 64 for weights and 256 for the $c_2$ scales, the memory for scales drops from $\tfrac{32}{64}=0.5$ bits/parameter to $\tfrac{8}{64} + \tfrac{32}{64\cdot256}=0.127$ bits/parameter, a 0.373 bpp reduction. Could someone walk through the calculation assumptions? In particular: 1) Are we assuming 32-bit storage for $c_1$ and 8-bit for quantized $c_2$? 2) Why is there no extra overhead for per-block offsets (they subtract mean before quantizing $c_2$)? 3) Is metadata (like block headers) ignored, or amortized to negligible cost?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper assumes the usual first-level quantization stores one 32-bit FP scale per weight-block of size $64$, then double quantization stores the per-block scales (the $c_2$ values) as 8-bit values while keeping one 32-bit “meta”-scale $c_1$ per group of $256$ of those $c_2$'s. With those choices the arithmetic is

- first-level (single quantization) scale cost = $32$ bits per block of $64$ weights = $32/64 = 0.5$ bits/parameter,
- after double quantization the cost = $(8\ \text{bits per } c_2)/64 + (32\ \text{bits per } c_1)/(64\cdot 256) = 8/64 + 32/(64\cdot 256) \approx 0.125 + 0.00195 \approx 0.127$ bits/parameter,

so the saving is $0.5 - 0.127 \approx 0.373$ bits/parameter.

Below I walk through the assumptions and why per-block offsets / headers are not counted explicitly.

1) Bit-width assumptions ($c_1$ and $c_2$)
The paper explicitly describes the two-level scheme: the original weight tensor $W$ is quantized in blocks of size $B = 64$ with a per-block scale $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$. That means one 32-bit float scale per $64$ parameters, hence $32/64$ bits per parameter. Double Quantization then treats those $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ values as the “data” to be quantized again. The paper says they use 8-bit floats for $c_2$ (they write “We use 8-bit Floats ... for the second quantization”) and they use a blocksize of $256$ for the second quantization. The second-level ($c_1^{\text{FP32}}$) is stored at $32$ bits per group of $256$ $c_2$ values. Concretely:

- store each $c_2$ as $8$ bits; each $c_2$ still corresponds to $64$ weights, so contribution is $8/64$ bits per parameter.
- every group of $256$ $c_2$ values has one $32$-bit $c_1$; $256$ $c_2$'s cover $256\cdot 64$ weights, so the contribution per weight is $32/(256\cdot 64) = 32/(64\cdot 256)$ bits.

So the calculation is exactly the one shown in the paper:
$$
\frac{8}{64} + \frac{32}{64\cdot 256} = 0.125 + 0.00195\ldots \approx 0.127\ \text{bits/parameter}.
$$

Thus the paper is assuming $c_2$ stored in 8-bit and $c_1$ stored in 32-bit FP. The weights themselves remain in 4-bit NF4 storage (this is separate).

2) Why no extra per-block offsets for $c_2$ (mean subtraction)
The paper says “Since the $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ are positive, we subtract the mean from $c_2$ before quantization to center the values around zero and make use of symmetric quantization.” That implies the double-quantization pipeline stores whatever global/group-level additive/scale terms are needed at the $c_1$ level rather than adding a new per-$c_2$ metadata field.

Operationally you can implement this so that the stored (quantized) $c_2$ values are zero-mean within each second-level group (the $256$ $c_2$'s), and the group-level $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$ stores the group shift/scale needed when reconstructing the original $c_2$'s. In other words, the second-level FP32 constants (the $c_1$) implicitly carry the small extra information (mean / re-centering) for the whole group of $256$ $c_2$'s, so you do not need an extra $32$-bit offset per original $64$-weight block. That is why no additional per-block offset term is added in the simple accounting above.

3) Metadata / headers — ignored or amortized
Yes: the paper treats small auxiliary bookkeeping (block headers, any tiny flags, or alignment padding) as negligible and amortized over huge numbers of parameters. The per-parameter accounting shown is purely for the dominant storage terms: quantized weight bits and the quantization-constant storage. For very large models (millions to tens of billions of parameters), a few bytes of extra per-block metadata would contribute extremely tiny extra bits/parameter compared to the computed $0.127$ bpp. For example, even if you added one $32$-bit extra header per block of $64$ weights, that would cost another $32/64 = 0.5$ bits/parameter (which would be material), but the paper’s scheme does not add such headers per weight-block — the only extra $32$-bit fields are the $c_1$'s per $256$ $c_2$'s (already included). Typical implementation choices (packing, group header once per group, reusing $c_1$ for mean, etc.) make the remaining metadata negligible and are therefore omitted from the back-of-envelope numbers.

Summary / Intuition
Think of it hierarchically: originally you had one $32$-bit scale for every $64$ weights ($0.5$ bpp). Double Quantization compresses that layer of scales by representing many of those scale values (the $c_2$'s) with cheap $8$-bit entries, and only keeping a single $32$-bit “anchor” ($c_1$) for a group of $256$ of them. So the dominant cost becomes the $8$-bit-per-$c_2$ term amortized over $64$ weights ($8/64 = 0.125$ bpp) plus the tiny amortized $c_1$ cost ($32/(64\cdot 256) \approx 0.002$ bpp). That yields $\sim 0.127$ bpp, saving $\sim 0.373$ bpp versus the original $0.5$ bpp. The paper ignores tiny additional headers/flags because they are negligible at the scale of billions of parameters; any required re-centering/mean information is folded into the group-level $c_1$ rather than stored per original block.

### Why choose blocksize 64 for weights and 256 for $c_2$ scales in QLoRA?
Question:
Block-wise quantization trades off precision and scale-overhead. QLoRA uses blocksize 64 for $\mathbf{W}$ and 256 for the scale quantization $c_2$. How sensitive are results to these choices? Is 64 the smallest block size that avoids large outlier-induced distortion under NF4, or is it an empirical sweet spot? Are there results or heuristics linking model width, activation statistics, or outlier prevalence to an optimal blocksize?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper’s choices (blocksize $64$ for the $4$-bit NF4 weight storage and blocksize $256$ for the second-level quantized scales $c_2$) are pragmatic, empirically driven trade‑offs: a relatively small block for the weights preserves quantization fidelity in the presence of outliers, while a much larger block for the stored scales greatly reduces overhead because those scale vectors are much smoother and tolerate coarser grouping. The authors do not claim these are theoretically optimal or the absolute minima; rather they report that $B=64$ for $\mathbf{W}$ gives “higher quantization precision” in practice and that using FP8 with blocksize $256$ for $c_2$ “causes no performance degradation” while saving memory.

Why this makes sense (intuitive + technical):
NF4 is designed assuming weights are approximately zero‑mean normal after rescaling, but real pretrained LLM weight blocks still contain occasional large values (outliers) and nonuniform variance across blocks. Block‑wise quantization mitigates the “outlier wrecks the whole tensor” problem by giving each block its own quantization constant $c$. Smaller blocks reduce the chance that a single outlier forces a very large $c$ and thus crushes the effective resolution used for most values in that block. So choosing a small-ish blocksize for $\mathbf{W}$ directly reduces distortion from outliers and yields more accurate dequantized weights when you only have $4$ bits.

On the other hand, the $c_2$ values (the per‑weight‑block quantization constants produced in the first step) form a much smoother, lower‑dynamic‑range vector than the raw weights. In Double Quantization QLoRA treats those $c_2$ as the inputs to a second quantization pass; because $c_2$ are positive and slowly varying across many adjacent blocks, they can be grouped into larger blocks (e.g. $256$) and quantized in FP8 without empirical loss. That is exactly the paper’s point: using $B=64$ for $\mathbf{W}$ costs about $32/64=0.5$ bits/parameter for 32‑bit constants, while double quantizing to FP8 with blocksize $256$ reduces that overhead dramatically to about $8/64 + 32/(64\cdot256)=0.127$ bits/parameter with no observed accuracy drop.

What the paper does and does not show about sensitivity:
The paper gives evidence that NF4 + double quantization “recovers 16‑bit performance” and states the chosen blocksizes ($64$ for $\mathbf{W}$, $256$ for $c_2$) are used “for higher quantization precision” and “to conserve memory.” It also references prior work (e.g., Dettmers et al.) that found small blocks are required for precise $4$-bit quantization. However, the paper does not present a full ablation sweep of many block sizes or a theoretical derivation that $64$ is the minimal size that avoids outlier‑induced distortion. In short: the values are empirical sweet spots consistent with prior observations, not claimed provably optimal.

Practical heuristics you can take from the paper and related reasoning:
- If your weight blocks show frequent large outliers or heavy tails, use smaller blocksize for $\mathbf{W}$ (commonly in the range $32$–$128$). Smaller blocks reduce per‑block variance and protect resolution at the cost of more quantization constants and therefore more memory overhead.  
- The per‑block memory overhead scales like $ \text{bits\_per\_constant} / \text{blocksize}$, so the larger the model (more parameters), the more important it is to increase blocksize for constants or to double‑quantize them to avoid paying large overhead. QLoRA’s double quantization is exactly the mechanism to allow a small blocksize for accuracy while keeping constant overhead low.  
- For the second‑level quantization of scales ($c_2$), use a larger blocksize because $c_2$ vectors are smoother and have lower entropy; FP8 plus $B=256$ is a reasonable default (as the paper reports no degradation). If you see large variance in $c_2$ across nearby blocks, reduce this blocksize, but that is uncommon.  
- Relate blocksize to matrix geometry: blocksize is applied on flattened weight tensors in the paper (flattening contiguous elements). If a layer is very narrow (small number of elements along the flattened axis), very small blocks may be impossible or inefficient; if the layer is wide, you can afford smaller blocks because constants are amortized over many params. In other words, the optimal blocksize depends on per‑layer size and on how concentrated the outliers are (per‑row, per‑column, or spread).

Recommended experiment (replicable, simple): sweep a small grid such as $B_W\in\{32,64,128\}$ for $\mathbf{W}$ and $B_{c2}\in\{64,128,256,512\}$ for the second quantization of $c_2$, measure perplexity / downstream task (MMLU / Vicuna) and memory. The paper’s claim is that $B_W=64$ and $B_{c2}=256$ hit a good point on that trade‑off curve: good fidelity and much lower memory.

Summary: The choices in QLoRA are empirically driven sweet spots: use small blocks for weights to limit outlier distortion (the paper uses $B=64$) and large blocks for the scale constants because those scale vectors are smoother and tolerate coarser grouping (the paper uses $B=256$ for $c_2$ with FP8). The paper demonstrates these settings give high accuracy with much lower memory, but it does not prove these are the unique optimal values — they are practical defaults that you should validate for your model and data by a small ablation sweep.

### How do gradients flow through quantized weights if we never compute $\partial E / \partial \mathbf{W}$?
Question:
The paper says only LoRA adapter gradients $\partial E/\partial \mathbf{L}_i$ are needed and not $\partial E/\partial \mathbf{W}$ for the 4-bit base weights. But they also note that computing $\partial E/\partial \mathbf{L}_i$ entails computing $\partial \mathbf{X}/\partial \mathbf{W}$ with dequantized $\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}$. Can someone outline the backward pass math? Do we treat $\mathbf{W}$ as a constant in the update sense (no optimizer state), but still use it in Jacobian-vector products to propagate gradients to $\mathbf{L}_i$? Is any straight-through estimator (STE) used for the quantization nonlinearity, or is quantization only in storage and all computed tensors are differentiable BF16?
Answer:
Short answer: you do not need an STE. The frozen 4-bit weights are stored quantized but are always dequantized to a differentiable BF16 tensor for forward and backward computation. During backprop you treat the dequantized tensor as a fixed constant (no optimizer/update for those stored codes), but you still use it in the usual Jacobian-vector products (e.g. compute $\frac{dE}{dX}$ and then $\frac{dE}{d\text{LoRA}}$ via chain rule). Only the LoRA parameters receive gradient and optimizer state. The integer rounding that created the stored 4-bit codes is not part of the autograd path—quantization is a storage-time lossy transform; dequantization produces a continuous BF16 tensor used for all differentiable ops.

More detail / math (intuitive + technical)
Write the forward for a single linear layer with one LoRA adapter exactly as in the paper:
$Y = X W_{\text{deq}} + s X L_1 L_2$,
where $W_{\text{deq}} = \text{doubleDequant}(c_1, c_2, W_q)$ is the dequantized BF16 weight used for computation, and $L_1, L_2$ are BF16 LoRA matrices. $W_q$ and quantization constants are stored (4-bit / FP8) and dequantized on the fly to BF16.

Let $G = \partial E/\partial Y$ be the upstream gradient from the loss $E$. The gradients you actually compute and apply are the standard matrix calculus results for the LoRA term $A = s X L_1 L_2$:

- For $L_2$ (shape $r\times o$):
  $\displaystyle \partial E/\partial L_2 = (s X L_1)^{T} G$
  (equivalently, if $M = s X L_1$ with shape $b\times r$, then $\partial E/\partial L_2 = M^{T} G$, summed/averaged over batch as usual).

- For $L_1$ (shape $h\times r$):
  $\displaystyle \partial E/\partial L_1 = s X^{T} (G L_2^{T})$
  (here $G L_2^{T}$ has shape $b\times r$, then multiply by $X^{T}$ to get $h\times r$).

These are identical to ordinary LoRA gradients if $W$ were full-precision. Note that these expressions require $X$ (the layer input) and $G$; $X$ itself is produced by preceding layers that used $W_{\text{deq}}$.

Where $W_{\text{deq}}$ participates in backprop is through the standard Jacobian-vector products with respect to inputs/outputs, not as an optimizable parameter. For example, the gradient that flows into the previous layer input $X_{\text{prev}}$ is computed through the frozen dequantized weight exactly as in dense backprop:

$\displaystyle \partial E/\partial X_{\text{prev}} \ (\text{from the base linear}) = G_{\text{base}}\, W_{\text{deq}}^{T}$,

where $G_{\text{base}}$ is the gradient w.r.t. the output of the base projection (if that projection used $W_{\text{deq}}$). These are computed using $W_{\text{deq}}$ in BF16. No gradient is accumulated into the stored 4-bit representation because we do not call the optimizer on $W_q$; we do not compute or keep $\partial E/\partial W_q$ for parameter updates.

Why no STE is needed
An STE is used when you want to pretend a non-differentiable or discrete forward op (e.g., rounding) is differentiable and propagate gradients through the discrete variable to upstream parameters (i.e., you want to update the quantized/rounded variable or its inputs). QLoRA does not try to update the stored 4-bit codes; the rounding/quantization that produced $W_q$ is only a storage-time operation. At runtime QLoRA deterministically dequantizes $W_q$ into $W_{\text{deq}}$ (a floating BF16 tensor). All computations and derivatives used by autograd are standard differentiable operations on BF16 tensors. Therefore there is no need to approximate gradients through the rounding operator and no STE is used.

Put another way: quantization is only in storage; dequantization produces a continuous tensor that participates in gradient algebra. Gradients are propagated through $W_{\text{deq}}$ as multipliers in Jacobian-vector products, but no gradient-based update is applied to $W_q$ (or to $W_{\text{deq}}$). Only the LoRA parameters receive and are applied gradients.

Practical consequence
- Memory/optimizer state: you avoid optimizer states for the base weights (they are frozen). You still need to materialize $W_{\text{deq}}$ (BF16) for forward/backward uses; QLoRA minimizes that cost by on-the-fly dequantization + memory tricks (double quantization, paging).
- Numeric precision: dequantization to BF16 preserves sufficient precision for the backprop math used to compute the LoRA gradients; the success of QLoRA empirically shows this is enough to recover 16-bit finetuning performance.
- No biased gradient estimator: since no gradient is backpropagated through the quantization rounding, there is no STE bias to reason about for base-weight updates—there simply are no base-weight updates.

Summary
You compute gradients for LoRA with ordinary chain rule formulas that use dequantized BF16 weights as fixed multipliers in the Jacobian-vector products. $W$ is treated as constant for optimization (no optimizer state), but it still participates in differentiable computations so gradients flow around it to the LoRA parameters. Quantization is a storage-time lossy step; the runtime tensors are continuous BF16 and no STE is needed.

### Is there a mismatch between the NF4 ‘optimal for Gaussian’ claim and using absmax normalization per block?
Question:
NF4’s codebook is built for $N(0,1)$, but blocks are normalized by absolute max rather than by variance. For heavy-tailed or skewed blocks, $\text{absmax}$ can be dominated by outliers, leaving most mass compressed near zero. Wouldn’t per-block standard-deviation scaling be more aligned with the Gaussian-optimized codebook? Is there empirical evidence that absmax performs better in practice for pretrained LLM weights with near-Gaussian but not exact normal distributions?
Answer:
Short answer: not really — there is no practical mismatch in QLoRA between building NF4 for a standard normal and using per-block absmax scaling. The paper’s design and experiments show absmax per block is a pragmatic normalization that, together with small block sizes and the NF4 construction, aligns well with pretrained LLM weight statistics and gives better empirical results than other 4-bit formats.

Why this is reasonable (intuitive + technical): NF4 is derived by computing $k$-bit quantiles for a standard normal $N(0,1)$ and then mapping those quantile values into the target numeric range (step (1)–(2) in the paper). To quantize an actual weight block we need to map that block into the same fixed range (conventionally $[-1,1]$) so the NF4 codebook’s quantiles apply. Block-wise absolute-max normalization does exactly that: it rescales the block so its maximum magnitude hits the endpoint of the target range. In distributional terms this is equivalent to fixing the block’s scale (standard deviation up to a constant) so that the block’s empirical distribution is approximately the same shape as the reference $N(0,1)$ up to scale.

The concern you raise — that an outlier will dominate absmax and compress most values near zero so the NF4 codebook (optimized for the normal) is a mismatch — is valid in principle. But QLoRA mitigates this in practice by using small block sizes (they use blocksize $64$ for weights). With smaller blocks the probability that a single extreme outlier dominates most of the block is much lower, so the absmax is a reasonable proxy for the block’s scale. Small-block absmax keeps the mapping of typical weights into the interior of the NF4 quantile bins where the NF4 design is optimal (equal-mass bins for a normal). In other words, NF4 assumes zero-centered normality up to a scalar; absmax enforces that scalar cheaply.

Why not use per-block standard deviation instead? Scaling by sample standard deviation would in fact align more directly with the intended normalization of a normal distribution (it explicitly rescales to unit variance). But estimating variance is more expensive (requires one extra pass to compute mean/variance per block, though that is a small constant extra work) and is itself sensitive to outliers (variance is even more influenced by large magnitudes than absmax). Robust alternatives (MAD, trimmed std) are possible but would add complexity. The paper chose the conventional and very cheap absmax because it is standard in blockwise quantization and pairs well with small blocks and NF4’s design.

Empirical evidence in the paper: the authors explicitly state (and provide an appendix) that pretrained weights are close to zero-centered normal (see Appendix~\ref{app:normality}). They report systematic experiments across architectures and scales ($125\text{M}$–$65\text{B}$) comparing NF4 (with block-wise absmax normalization) to FP4 and Int4; NF4 consistently yields better perplexity and downstream zero-shot/finetuning performance (Figures/Tables cited in Section “4-bit NormalFloat Quantization” and the experiments summarized in Section “QLoRA vs. Standard Finetuning”). These empirical gains show that the combination of NF4 codebook + blockwise absmax scaling works well on real pretrained LLM weights despite non-idealities (heavy tails, small skew). They also use small blocks ($64$) and double quantization of scales to control memory while preserving accuracy.

Practical caveats and alternatives: if you had blocks that are extremely heavy-tailed or strongly skewed (e.g., a block dominated by a single large weight), absmax will compress the bulk and quantization error for the bulk will increase. In such pathological blocks, using robust scale estimators (e.g., clipping outliers, using trimmed-mean/std, or MAD-based scaling) or switching to a different blocking strategy would help. The paper does not explore those alternatives in depth, but the consistent empirical wins for NF4 with absmax across many models suggest such pathological blocks are rare enough in pretrained weights to not hurt end performance.

Conclusion: absmax per block is a practical, cheap way to match block scale to the NF4 codebook; small block sizes and the near-normality of pretrained weights make this a good fit in practice. The paper’s experiments (Appendix~\ref{app:normality}, NF4 vs FP4/Int4 comparisons, and downstream finetuning results) provide empirical evidence that absmax normalization works well with NF4 on real LLM weights. If you have a special model or suspect many extreme outliers, testing robust scaling or per-block variance normalization is reasonable, but for standard pretrained LLMs the absmax + NF4 recipe in QLoRA is empirically validated.

### What exactly is stored in memory during QLoRA training, and where are the savings coming from?
Question:
In the Memory Requirement section, the paper says the 4-bit base model is about 5,048 MB for LLaMA-7B and that LoRA gradients can dominate memory without gradient checkpointing. Could someone provide a precise breakdown for a single transformer layer during training with QLoRA? Specifically: 1) storage for quantized weights $\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}$, 2) storage for quantization constants with double quantization ($c_1$, $c_2$), 3) dequantized temporary BF16 weights during forward/backward, 4) activations and activation gradients with gradient checkpointing, 5) optimizer states for LoRA (paged) vs. full-model? Which of these scale with sequence length vs. parameter count?
Answer:
Short answer: QLoRA stores the base model weights compressed on‑device as $4$‑bit $\mathrm{NF4}$ plus small per‑block quantization constants (with double quantization), and it dequantizes those to a $\mathrm{BF16}$ buffer on the fly for compute. During training you also store (a) activations (and activation gradients), (b) the small LoRA parameters and their optimizer state, and (c) temporary dequantized $\mathrm{BF16}$ weight tensors needed for forward/backward. The big memory savings come from (1) replacing full‑precision storage of the entire base model with $4$‑bit storage + tiny quant constants, (2) only keeping optimizer state for the much smaller LoRA adapters (not the full model), and (3) paging optimizer state to CPU and using gradient checkpointing so activations, not parameter state, dominate GPU peak. Below I give a concise per‑transformer‑layer breakdown, with formulas showing what scales with sequence length versus parameter count.

Notation: let the layer be a linear projection $W$ with shape $h\times o$ (so it has $P = h\cdot o$ parameters). Let blocksize for $W$ quantization be $B$ (paper uses $B = 64$). Let the LoRA rank be $r$ so LoRA adds $L_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{h\times r}$ and $L_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times o}$. Let batch size be $b$ and sequence length be $L$ (tokens). $\mathrm{BF16}$ is $16$ bits, $\mathrm{NF4}$ is $4$ bits, $\mathrm{FP32}$ is $32$ bits, $\mathrm{FP8}$ used for second quantization of constants.

1) Storage for quantized weights $W^{\mathrm{NF4}}$ (persistent on GPU)
You store the base weight values in $4$ bits each. Memory = $P\cdot 4\ \text{bits} = P\cdot 0.5\ \text{bytes}$. This scales with parameter count $P$ (not with sequence length). Example: a $7\text{B}$ model total weights $\sim 7\times 10^{9}$ parameters; per layer it is proportional to $P$.

2) Storage for quantization constants with double quantization ($c_2, c_1$)
With blockwise quantization you need one quantization constant per block of size $B$. If there are $n = P / B$ blocks, the original single quantization would store $c_2^{\mathrm{FP32}}$ per block ($32$ bits each) giving $32\cdot n$ bits $= (32/B)$ bits per parameter $= 32/B\ \text{bits/param}$. The paper points out that with $B=64$ this is $32/64 = 0.5\ \text{bits/param}$. Double Quantization treats the $c_2$ values as a tensor and quantizes them to an $8$‑bit format ($\mathrm{FP8}$) with a second‑level $c_1^{\mathrm{FP32}}$ used to dequantize. With the paper's choices ($W$ blocksize $64$, $c_2$ blocksize $256$) the amortized cost becomes approximately $8/64 + 32/(64\cdot 256) = 0.127$ bits per parameter (paper’s calculation), i.e. a reduction of $\sim 0.373$ bits/param. So the constants cost scales with parameter count (because number of blocks $\propto P$), but it is much smaller than storing full $\mathrm{FP32}$ per parameter. Note both $c_2^{k\text{-bit}}$ and the small $c_1^{\mathrm{FP32}}$ are stored; $c_1$ is per (larger) block of $c_2$, so extremely small overhead.

3) Dequantized temporary $\mathrm{BF16}$ weights during forward/backward
For compute the layer dequantizes $W^{\mathrm{NF4}}$ to $\mathrm{BF16}$ (the computation data type). The dequantized $\mathrm{BF16}$ copy size is $P\cdot 16\ \text{bits} = P\cdot 2\ \text{bytes}$. This is a temporary/computation buffer used to run matmuls during forward and to compute $\partial X/\partial W$ during backward (the paper states they dequantize into $\mathrm{BF16}$ to compute derivatives even though they do not update $W$). Whether you need a full separate $\mathrm{BF16}$ copy simultaneously depends on implementation: typically a dequantized tile or the whole layer is dequantized at once and held in $\mathrm{BF16}$ for the forward+backward of that chunk; thus the GPU peak may briefly include this $\mathrm{BF16}$ copy. This term scales with parameter count $P$, not with sequence length $L$. Because $\mathrm{BF16}$ is $4\times$ larger than $\mathrm{NF4}$ storage, dequantization is the single largest per‑layer transient memory cost if you dequantize whole tensors at once.

4) Activations and activation gradients with gradient checkpointing
Activations (hidden states) for this layer have shape $(b, L, h)$ (or $(b\cdot L, h)$ if flattened). Stored in $\mathrm{BF16}$ they cost $b\cdot L\cdot h\cdot 16\ \text{bits}$. Activation gradients produced during backward have similar scale. These terms scale with sequence length $L$ and batch $b$ (and with hidden size $h$, which ties to parameter counts but are not the same as $P$). The paper emphasizes activations dominate memory in finetuning and that gradient checkpointing reduces stored activations by recomputing forward parts as needed: with checkpointing you store fewer intermediate activations and recompute during backward, trading GPU compute for much lower memory. The paper gives an example: for a $7\text{B}$ LLaMA model with batch size $1$ and typical LoRA setup, LoRA input gradients were $567\ \text{MB}$ without checkpointing and average $18\ \text{MB}$ per sequence with checkpointing — showing activation/activation‑gradient terms depend strongly on $L$ and checkpoint strategy. So activation memory $\propto b\cdot L$ (sequence‑length dependent), whereas most parameter storage is sequence‑length independent.

5) Optimizer states for LoRA (paged) vs. full‑model optimizer states
If you were to full‑finetune the entire model you would need optimizer state (e.g., Adam has two moment accumulators) for all $P$ parameters, typically stored in $16$ or $32$‑bit (two or more tensors of size $P$). That would scale as $O(P)$ and be very large. QLoRA only trains the LoRA adapter parameters (size $P_{\mathrm{LoRA}} = h\cdot r + r\cdot o \approx r\cdot(h+o)$), which is tiny relative to $P$. So optimizer state stored on GPU is only for the LoRA params (and possibly small extra state for other trainable modules). The paper uses Paged Optimizers: optimizer state can be allocated in unified memory and paged to CPU when not needed, avoiding GPU OOM spikes. Thus optimizer state for LoRA still scales with LoRA parameter count, not with full $P$, and can be paged off GPU to reduce GPU footprint further. In short, optimizer state cost with QLoRA is $O(P_{\mathrm{LoRA}})$ on GPU (and $O(P_{\mathrm{LoRA}})$ overall if paged to CPU), whereas full finetuning would be $O(P)$ and dominate memory.

Additional important points and interactions
- Which quantities scale with sequence length $L$: activations and activation gradients ($b\cdot L\cdot h$), and the temporary activation‑related gradients used to compute LoRA gradients (so LoRA gradient memory depends on activations too). Also some per‑token caches depending on implementation (attention caches for generation), but those are separate from training when you train with causal LM cross‑entropy.
- Which quantities scale with parameter count $P$: stored $4$‑bit $W^{\mathrm{NF4}}$ ($P\cdot 4\ \text{bits}$), quantization constants and their second quantization ($\approx P\cdot 0.127\ \text{bits}$ after DQ for the paper’s choices), temporary dequantized $\mathrm{BF16}$ copy ($P\cdot 16\ \text{bits}$ if dequantized fully at once), and full‑model optimizer states (if doing full finetuning).
- LoRA gradients memory depends on activations: computing gradients for LoRA requires inner products of per‑token inputs $X$ (size $b\cdot L\cdot h$) and output gradients, so LoRA gradient buffers and input gradients scale with $b\cdot L$ and $r$; the paper’s example shows LoRA input gradients dominated memory unless checkpointing was used.
- Savings summary: primary savings come from storing the huge base model at $4$ bits instead of $\mathrm{BF16}/\mathrm{FP32}$ (roughly $4\times$–$8\times$ reduction vs $\mathrm{BF16}/\mathrm{FP32}$ depending on base dtype), plus Double Quantization reduces the quantization‑constant overhead, and by only optimizing LoRA adapters you avoid having $O(P)$ optimizer state. The remaining dominant memory cost becomes activations (sequence dependent), which QLoRA mitigates by gradient checkpointing and Paged Optimizers to keep optimizer state off‑GPU when possible.

Concrete per‑layer formulas (compact):
- NF4 storage (persistent): $S_W = P\cdot 4\ \text{bits}$.
- Quant constants (after DQ, amortized): $S_c \approx P\cdot 0.127\ \text{bits}$ (paper’s numbers for $B=64$ and chosen second level).
- Dequantized compute weights (temporary $\mathrm{BF16}$): $S_{\mathrm{deq}} = P\cdot 16\ \text{bits}$ (may be done per‑chunk).
- Activations stored ($\mathrm{BF16}$) during forward: $S_{\mathrm{act}} = b\cdot L\cdot h\cdot 16\ \text{bits}$ (and activation gradients similar scale).
- LoRA params ($\mathrm{BF16}$): $S_{\mathrm{LoRA}} = (h\cdot r + r\cdot o)\cdot 16\ \text{bits}$ and LoRA optimizer states (e.g., $2\times$ moments) $\approx 2\cdot S_{\mathrm{LoRA}}$ (or more depending on optimizer), which can be paged.
- LoRA gradient/input gradient peak: scales $\propto b\cdot L\cdot h\cdot r$ (paper reported e.g. $567\ \text{MB}$ for $7\text{B}$ without checkpointing vs $18\ \text{MB}$ with checkpointing).

Practical implications
Implementations exploit the fact that you can dequantize per‑block or per‑tile so you often do not need to have the entire $P\cdot 16$ bits resident at once; instead you dequantize a chunk, multiply, and discard, further reducing peak memory. The paper’s CUDA kernels and the use of paging make the transient dequantized cost manageable. But conceptually, the dequantized $\mathrm{BF16}$ buffer is the compute representation and is the reason you cannot entirely avoid higher precision during training.

Conclusion
So, for a single transformer linear layer you keep a tiny $\mathrm{NF4}$ persistent store ($P\cdot 4\ \text{bits}$) plus tiny per‑block quantization constants (very small amortized bits/param after double quantization), you transiently dequantize to $\mathrm{BF16}$ for compute ($P\cdot 16\ \text{bits}$ if you materialize whole tensor, or less if tiled), you keep activations/activation gradients which scale with batch·seq_len, and you only maintain optimizer/gradient state for the small LoRA adapters (paged to CPU if needed). The memory savings come mainly from compressing the base model to $4$ bits and from avoiding full‑model optimizer states; the remaining peak is dominated by activations unless checkpointing and paging are used.

### Why do default LoRA placements (Q and V only) fail to match 16-bit full finetuning?
Question:
The paper finds that applying LoRA only to the attention Q and V projections underperforms, and that placing adapters in all linear layers is critical to match full finetuning. Is there an explanation for why limiting adaptation to Q/V is insufficient for instruction tuning? Do certain components (like output projections, feed-forward layers, or key/value) carry task-specific adjustments that cannot be captured by modifying attention projections alone? Any ablations quantifying which additional layers contribute most to the gap closing?
Answer:
Short answer: because instruction tuning changes more than just the attention “query” and “value” subspaces. The Q/V-only LoRA setup (the common default) can’t express many of the task-specific parameter changes that the rest of transformer layers (output projections, feed‑forward layers, and other linear maps) must perform during instruction finetuning. The QLoRA paper shows empirically that the single strongest factor for recovering $16$-bit full finetuning performance is how many adapters you place: adding LoRA to every linear layer of each transformer block is required to match full finetuning, while tuning other LoRA hyperparameters (e.g., rank $r$) matters little.

Why intuitively Q/V-only is insufficient
The usual Q/V LoRA idea rests on the intuition that changing attention patterns (what to attend to and how to use attended vectors) is the main lever for task adaptation. That is often true for some tasks, but instruction following is broader: it requires changes to how hidden states are transformed after attention (the output projection of the attention head), how information is mixed and re‑weighted in the feed‑forward networks (FFNs), and how those transformed hidden states are mapped to logits or downstream layers. Concretely:

• Attention is not only “query” and “value.” The output of attention combines the values via the attention weights and then applies an output linear projection; adapting only Q and V can change what gets attended to and what content gets retrieved, but it cannot change how that retrieved content is re‑projected back into the residual stream or how it interacts with subsequent FFNs. Those projection/FFN transforms are often where task‑specific post‑processing happens (formatting, style, constraint enforcement, etc.).  
• FFNs implement powerful elementwise nonlinear remapping and channel mixing; many instruction‑tuning behaviors (e.g., reformatting answers, adding persona, verbosity) can be realized as modest shifts in FFN behavior. A low‑rank change in the attention Q/V subspace cannot reproduce arbitrary needed FFN adjustments.  
• The final mapping toward logits (or intermediate output projections) can require shifts that are orthogonal to what attention changes alone can do. For example, to prefer a particular answer phrasing or to suppress certain tokens, you may need to alter the final linear mappings rather than only changing attention patterns.

What the paper shows empirically
The QLoRA paper explicitly reports that applying LoRA only to Q and V did not replicate full $16$-bit finetuning performance on their instruction tuning setups (they show this for LLaMA on Alpaca in their Figure referenced in the text). Their main ablation result is that the number/placement of adapters is the dominant LoRA hyperparameter: expanding LoRA to every linear layer in the transformer block (i.e., all projections and FFN linear layers) recovers the $16$-bit baseline, whereas changing the adapter rank $r$ or other LoRA knobs has little effect. They therefore recommend—and use—adapters in all linear layers to eliminate the accuracy gap. The paper does not present a fine‑grained per‑layer ranked list (e.g., “FFN contributes $40\%$ of the gap, attention output $30\%$…”), but the clear claim and experimental finding is that broad coverage (all linear layers) is the key to matching full finetuning.

Practical implication
Because LoRA adapters are small relative to activation memory (and QLoRA’s quantization makes the base model storage cheap), it’s affordable to place adapters widely. The paper emphasizes that most finetuning memory is in activations/gradients, not in LoRA parameters, so you can add adapters across all linear layers without hitting a large memory penalty. Their recommendation—borne out by experiments—is to place LoRA in every linear projection (queries, keys, values, output projections, and the FFN input/output linear layers) when doing instruction finetuning if your goal is to match full‑model finetuning.

Summary
Limiting LoRA to only Q and V constrains the adapter’s functional family: it can reshape attention inputs and retrieved content but cannot alter the many subsequent linear/nonlinear transforms that instruction tuning needs. The QLoRA paper’s ablations identify adapter coverage (i.e., placing adapters in all linear layers) as the critical factor to close the gap to $16$-bit full finetuning; they do not find adapter rank to be the limiting factor, and they do not present a detailed per‑layer importance ranking beyond the “all layers” vs “Q/V only” comparison.

### Does QLoRA dequantize weights on-the-fly for every forward pass and what is the runtime cost?
Question:
Implementation-wise, QLoRA stores weights in NF4 and dequantizes to BF16 for matrix multiplications. Is dequantization performed on-the-fly every forward/backward pass, or are there caches at the block level? How much overhead does double dequantization (first recovering $c_2$ from $c_1$, then weights from $c_2$) add relative to 16-bit inference/training? The paper says there is no runtime degradation vs 16-bit full finetuning—do they have microbenchmarks showing dequant cost is negligible compared to GEMMs at large batch/sequence sizes?
Answer:
Short answer: QLoRA dequantizes on-the-fly when a weight tensor is used (both forward and backward), at block granularity, and the authors report that in their experiments the dequantization cost is negligible compared to the $16$-bit GEMM cost. The paper does not publish a standalone microbenchmark that isolates the double‑dequant cost, but it gives enough information to understand why the overhead is small and how it scales.

What the paper says (implementation-level)
The paper explicitly states that QLoRA has one storage data type (typically $4$-bit $NF4$) and one computation data type ($BF16$). “Whenever a QLoRA weight tensor is used, we dequantize the tensor to BFloat16, and then perform a matrix multiplication in $16$-bit.” They define the operation as

$Y^{BF16} = X^{BF16}\ \text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{FP32}, c_2^{k\text{-}bit}, W^{NF4}) + X^{BF16} L_1^{BF16} L_2^{BF16},$

with doubleDequant doing two dequant stages to recover the BF16 weights from the stored NF4 weights and quantized quantization constants. The quantization is block-wise (they use blocksize $64$ for $W$ and $256$ for $c_2$), so dequantization is naturally performed per block.

Is dequantization done per forward/backward pass or cached?
The paper describes dequantization happening “whenever a QLoRA weight tensor is used” and does not describe or claim any persistent full-$BF16$ cache of reconstructed weights on GPU across layers or iterations. The quantization format is block-wise, and the runtime view is that stored tensors are $NF4$ + quantization constants; those are dequantized to $BF16$ for the compute. So dequantization is performed on-the-fly at use time (per forward and per backward) at block granularity. They do not report an extra caching layer that keeps a full $BF16$ copy permanently on the GPU (that would defeat the memory savings).

Why this is not prohibitive (intuition + technical reason)
Dequantization is essentially an $O(N)$ linear pass over the stored blocks: for each block you (1) recover $c_2$ from its quantized representation (the “quantized quantization constants”), (2) use $c_2$ (and optionally $c_1$) to rescale the $4$-bit entries into $BF16$ values. The heavy linear algebra work (the GEMM) is much more expensive in compute and memory traffic than the arithmetic of rescaling a block. Concretely:

- Dequant cost per element is a few scalar ops (recover scale(s) and one multiply), so the work is memory-bandwidth / element dominated and low arithmetic intensity.
- A GEMM of shape $(\text{batch}\times \text{seq},\ \text{hidden}) \times (\text{hidden},\ \text{out})$ reuses the same weight block many times across rows of $X$. At large batch or long sequence length the cost of the GEMM grows with the number of rows (amortizing the one-time per-block dequant work across many input activations). Thus the relative overhead of dequantization becomes small when $\text{batch}\times\text{sequence}$ is large.

The paper makes this same point qualitatively and uses $BF16$ GEMMs after dequant: “In practice, this means whenever a QLoRA weight tensor is used, we dequantize the tensor to BFloat16, and then perform a matrix multiplication in $16$-bit.” They conclude that they reduce memory a lot “without degrading the runtime or predictive performance compared to a $16$-bit fully finetuned baseline.” That is an empirical claim about end‑to‑end runtime, implying dequant cost didn’t create a measurable slow-down in their finetuning experiments.

Double dequantization cost
Double quantization means there are two dequant steps: first recover $c_2$ from $c_1$ (the quantized quantization constants), then use $c_2$ to dequant the $4$-bit $W$. This adds a tiny extra per‑block cost to reconstruct the scale(s) (one extra read and a small number of ops per block) before the per‑element rescale. Because the second step (the $BF16$ GEMM) dominates overall cost for typical transformer matmuls, the extra cost from the second dequant stage is again small in practice.

What the paper does and does not provide on microbenchmarks
The authors claim “QLoRA reduces the average memory requirements … without degrading the runtime or predictive performance compared to a $16$-bit fully finetuned baseline.” They also state they release CUDA kernels for $4$-bit training. However, they do not present a dedicated microbenchmark in the paper that isolates:

- pure dequant time (doubleDequant only),
- GEMM time versus (dequant + GEMM) for different batch/seq sizes,
- per-layer caching vs no-caching comparisons.

They do report that paged optimizers (a separate feature) “with a batch size of $16$, paged optimizers provide the same training speed as regular optimizers” for a $65$B model on a $48$GB GPU—this is an explicit runtime comparison related to optimizer paging, but it is not a dequant microbenchmark.

Practical takeaway and how to validate locally
From the paper you should assume dequantization is performed on-the-fly at block granularity and is implemented in optimized CUDA kernels that fuse dequant + GEMM where sensible (they open‑source CUDA kernels). The dequant overhead is small in their reported experiments because GEMMs dominate at realistic batch and sequence lengths. If you want to quantify this yourself, measure two timings on your hardware: (1) time for (doubleDequant + GEMM) per layer, and (2) time for GEMM with a pre‑dequantized $BF16$ weight (i.e., keep a $BF16$ cache) for the same shapes. The difference isolates the dequant overhead; vary $\text{batch}\times\text{sequence}$ to see how the amortization behaves.

Summary
QLoRA dequantizes on-the-fly (per use) at block level and does a two-stage dequant when using double quantization. The double dequant adds only a small extra per-block overhead; in the authors’ experiments that overhead is negligible compared to the $BF16$ GEMMs at typical transformer batch/sequence sizes, so they report no runtime degradation vs $16$-bit finetuning. The paper does not include a separated microbenchmark isolating dequant cost, so if you need precise numbers for your hardware/shape you should benchmark (doubleDequant+GEMM) versus GEMM-with-cached-$BF16$.

### What failure mode do Paged Optimizers address, and when would paging slow training?
Question:
Paged Optimizers rely on CUDA unified memory to page optimizer states between GPU and CPU RAM when long sequences cause transient spikes. 1) Which tensors are paged (optimizer states for LoRA only, or also intermediate grads)? 2) What pattern of sequence lengths or batch accumulation causes spikes large enough to need paging? 3) Is there a threshold of host-GPU bandwidth or page fault rate beyond which training slows significantly? Any guidance on how to monitor paging events and tune batch/sequence length to avoid stalls?
Answer:
Short answer: In QLoRA the “paged optimizers” mechanism is intentionally applied to the optimizer state tensors (the per-parameter moment / accumulator buffers that Adam/AdamW or RMSProp keep) and not to the forward activations or intermediate grads. Paging is only needed for transient memory spikes (primarily caused by unusually long sequences or by effective large batches from accumulation), and whether paging noticeably slows training depends on how often pages must be moved and how much host$\leftrightarrow$GPU bandwidth/latency those moves consume. The QLoRA paper deliberately pages optimizer state to host memory via CUDA Unified Memory so that rare spikes do not OOM the GPU; the authors report that when spikes are rare the training speed with paged optimizers can be the same as with regular (all-on-GPU) optimizers, but they do not provide hard thresholds — large or frequent page faults will of course stall the GPU.

Details and intuition (grounded in the paper)

Which tensors are paged?
The paper states that Paged Optimizers “allocate paged memory for the optimizer states which are then automatically evicted to CPU RAM when the GPU runs out-of-memory and paged back into GPU memory when the memory is needed in the optimizer update step.” In other words, the intended and documented use in QLoRA is to place the optimizer states (the per-parameter moment / second-moment / step buffers for the adapters) in unified memory so they can live on host RAM when not needed on the device. The forward activations, dequantized weights (used during the forward/backward), and activation/temporary gradients remain on the GPU during the forward/backward pass. The backward still needs to dequantize weights to BF16 to compute the gradients that reach the LoRA adapters, so those computation buffers must be on-device while doing the passes; they are not what QLoRA is placing in pageable storage.

What causes spikes that trigger paging?
The paper links paging to “memory spikes that occur when processing a mini-batch with a long sequence length” and to the extra memory pressure created by activation gradients (even with gradient checkpointing). Intuitively, spikes come from rare inputs whose sequence length (or tokenized-turns-in-prompt) is much larger than the normal training examples. Two common practical patterns that create spikes are: (1) variable-length batches that occasionally include one or a few very long sequences, and (2) larger effective batch-size produced by longer gradient-accumulation (or simultaneous increases in batch size and sequence length). Gradient checkpointing reduces steady activation storage, but it increases temporary recomputation during backward which still transiently needs buffers; when those transient buffers push the required memory beyond GPU capacity, unified memory will evict optimizer state pages to host RAM. The paper emphasizes that such long-sequence batches are rare in their runs, so paging happens infrequently.

When (and why) will paging slow training?
Paging is effectively migrating pages between host and device at runtime. If page faults (migrations) are rare and happen outside critical hot loops (e.g., between batches, or small transfers), the wall-clock overhead can be negligible — the paper reports they measured for 65B on 48GB and with batch size 16 they “provide the same training speed as regular optimizers.” However, if migrations happen frequently or in the middle of latency-sensitive operations (large pages moved during backward/optimizer step), each migration stalls the GPU until the page transfer completes, and training slows proportionally to the transferred bytes divided by host$\leftrightarrow$GPU bandwidth plus migration overhead/latency. Practical factors that make paging harmful are: frequent long examples so you page in/out many pages each step, a low-bandwidth link (PCIe vs NVLink), or very large optimizer-state footprint per page so each page transfer is large.

There is no single numeric threshold in the paper (they explicitly say they do not provide hard measurements because paging only occurs rarely), but the rule of thumb is: if the rate of migrated bytes per second approaches a nontrivial fraction of the GPU host link bandwidth (and particularly if migrations happen synchronously on the critical path), you will see measurable slowdown.

How to monitor paging and detect problematic page faults
Because the paper relies on CUDA Unified Memory, use the standard NVIDIA tooling to inspect memory and migration patterns. Practical suggestions:

- Watch GPU memory usage and OOM events with nvidia-smi to see when allocation pressure peaks. If GPU utilization drops and memory is close to capacity, paging is likely happening.
- Profile with NVIDIA Nsight Systems (nsys) or the older nvprof to capture Unified Memory events / migrations. Nsight Systems shows “cudaMemcpy” and unified memory page migration events and their timing; CUPTI-based tracing will show page fault and migration calls.
- Use CUDA Unified Memory counters via CUPTI or Nsight to get counts/bytes of migrations (look for “Unified Memory” or “UM” events). On Linux you may also trace relevant driver logs if available.
- At the application level, instrument host$\leftrightarrow$device copy time around optimizer steps and the batch step. If optimizer update times suddenly spike in wall-clock correlated with long sequences, that indicates paging overhead.
- Monitor PCIe / NVLink link utilization (some advanced tools and vendor counters show link throughput) — sustained high host$\leftrightarrow$GPU traffic during training is a warning sign.

How to tune batch / sequence length to avoid stalls
Because the paper’s Paged Optimizers are intended to handle only occasional spikes, the most effective practical mitigations are to make memory demand more uniform and to reduce the frequency/size of required page transfers.

1) Bucket or sort inputs by length. Grouping similarly long sequences into the same batch (length bucketing) reduces variance and avoids single-sample long-sequence spikes that overflow memory.

2) Cap max sequence length in a preprocessing step (truncate or discard very long examples) or construct batches with an explicit max_length so that worst-case allocation is controlled.

3) Use smaller per-device batch size (or increase gradient-accumulation steps) so that one long example does not combine with a big batch to overflow memory. If you do use accumulation, be mindful that accumulation increases the total memory life-time of activations and can interact with checkpointing.

4) Keep optimizer state minimal for the parts that live on the GPU. QLoRA already reduces the optimizer footprint by only optimizing LoRA parameters (their state is much smaller than full-model optimizer state), but you can further reduce optimizer state precision or use single-moment optimizers when acceptable.

5) If your hardware supports faster host$\leftrightarrow$GPU interconnects (NVLink / NVLink-C2C vs PCIe), place heavy-workload machines where that bandwidth is available; paging will be cheaper there.

6) If paging is happening at the optimizer-update time only, try to schedule page-ins before the critical operation (prefetch): CUDA provides advice APIs (cudaMemPrefetchAsync) in some environments — prefetching optimizer pages to device before the update step can reduce synchronous page faults. (Be careful: prefetch itself consumes bandwidth; it helps when you can prefetch during idle time.)

Monitoring / tuning checklist (short)
- Use nvidia-smi to watch mem usage, and nsight (nsys) to capture Unified Memory events.
- If you see frequent UM migrations or spikes in optimizer-update time correlated with long examples, apply bucketing/truncation or reduce batch size.
- If transfers are rare, accept the small overhead; if transfers are frequent, reduce the frequency/size through the steps above or use hardware with higher host$\leftrightarrow$GPU bandwidth.

Conclusion
Paged Optimizers in QLoRA are intentionally limited to paging optimizer-state tensors to host memory to avoid OOMs caused by rare long-sequence spikes. When page faults are infrequent the overhead can be negligible (the paper reports parity in speed for one tested regime), but frequent or large synchronous page migrations will stall the GPU and slow training — the exact threshold depends on the host$\leftrightarrow$GPU bandwidth and the migration rate. Practical mitigation is to reduce variance in sequence length (bucketing/truncation), control batch/accumulation sizing, and monitor migrations with Nsight/other CUDA profiling tools so you can detect and eliminate problematic paging patterns before they dominate runtime.

### How does NF4 handle exact zeros and does the asymmetric binning change reconstruction bias?
Question:
To get an exact zero, the paper proposes asymmetric NF4: $2^{k-1}$ negative bins and $2^{k-1}+1$ positive bins, then removing one duplicate zero. How does this affect the mean of the reconstructed values when the true distribution is symmetric around zero? Is there a slight positive bias due to the extra positive bin density near zero, or does the removal of one zero value ensure symmetry in practice? Any quantitative comparison of reconstruction bias vs. symmetric quantizers that include a zero code explicitly?
Answer:
Short answer: The NF4 construction in the paper does not introduce a systematic positive bias for a true zero-centered symmetric distribution. By building equal-probability bins on the negative and positive sides and then removing one duplicated zero code, the quantizer preserves symmetry in expectation; any remaining bias is only from finite-sample/blockwise effects (estimation noise, block rescaling), and the paper’s empirical results indicate those effects are negligible.

Why, intuitively and technically: NF4 is derived from quantile (equal-population) binning of a standard normal. If you were to compute the quantiles of a symmetric distribution like $N(0,1)$, the negative quantiles are the negatives of the positive quantiles: $Q_X(p) = -Q_X(1-p)$. The authors form $2^{k-1}$ negative bins and $2^{k-1}+1$ positive bins so that there is an explicit code value exactly at zero (the positive and negative constructions would both produce a zero midpoint). They then “unify these sets of $q_i$ and remove one of the two zeros.” After this removal, every positive representative value has a matching negative representative (same magnitude, opposite sign), and zero remains exactly representable. For an ideal infinite-sample, perfectly-normal, block-rescaled tensor this implies the mapping $x \to \text{quantized-code} \to \text{dequantized value}$ is antisymmetric: $\mathrm{dequant}(-x) = -\mathrm{dequant}(x)$. Therefore the expected reconstruction error over a symmetric distribution has zero mean, i.e., no systematic positive bias.

Where small bias could arise in practice: the symmetry argument relies on three idealizations: (1) the underlying distribution is exactly symmetric and matches the theoretical normal used to compute the quantiles; (2) the blockwise scaling/rescaling (absmax or std matching) is exact and identical for corresponding positive/negative magnitudes; (3) quantile values are computed without sampling/estimation noise. In real tensors you get finite-block sizes, empirical deviations from normality, and approximate quantile estimation or finite-sample noise in the block statistics. Those practical effects can induce tiny residual biases (and/or small differences in reconstruction MSE between positive and negative small magnitudes), but these are implementation/estimation errors, not a fundamental consequence of the asymmetric-bin construction.

What the paper reports: The QLoRA paper explicitly motivates the asymmetric binning only to guarantee an exact zero code while keeping the information-theoretic equal-probability property for normal weights. It does not report a numerical comparison of reconstruction mean-bias between NF4 and other symmetric quantizers that also reserve an explicit zero code, nor does it report a measured systematic positive bias. Instead, the paper shows that NF4 yields better empirical downstream performance (perplexity / zero-shot tasks; and in QLoRA finetuning it recovers 16-bit performance), which implies any reconstruction bias introduced by the data type is too small to harm model performance in their experiments.

If you want a quantitative test (recommended): compute, per block, the mean of $\bigl(\mathrm{dequant}(x) - x\bigr)$ for samples drawn from a symmetric distribution (or for actual weight blocks), then aggregate across blocks. Also compare NF4 to a symmetric $k$-bit quantizer that explicitly includes a zero code (e.g., reserve one code for zero and split remaining codes symmetrically). In practice you should find mean bias near zero and reconstruction MSE differences dominated by blocksize and rescaling choices rather than the $+1$ positive bin trick itself.

Conclusion: Removing one duplicate zero in NF4 enforces an exact zero code while keeping quantile symmetry in value magnitudes, so there is no built-in positive bias for symmetric distributions; any tiny bias observed would come from finite-sample/block-rescaling effects, and the paper’s empirical results show these effects are negligible for model quality.

In the QLoRA definition and summary, the paper alternates between “BFloat16” and “BrainFloat” (e.g., “16-bit BrainFloat”). Is this just a typo or colloquial term for the standard BF16 format? There is no separate ‘BrainFloat’ format that differs from BF16 in this context, right?

### How do they ensure that 4-bit QLoRA ‘recovers’ 16-bit performance without overfitting to the finetune data?
Question:
The results show NF4 QLoRA matches 16-bit full finetuning and 16-bit LoRA on GLUE, TK-Instruct, and MMLU in their setups. Could this parity be partly due to overfitting to the finetuning distribution that is then measured on similar tasks? Did the authors evaluate cross-task generalization where the test distribution is notably different from the finetune data? Any signs that 4-bit models are less robust out-of-domain than 16-bit, even if in-domain metrics match?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not claim a formal proof against overfitting; instead it supports “recovery” of $16$-bit performance by empirical replication of $16$-bit baselines across multiple architectures, tasks, model scales and datasets. That evidence makes overfitting to one finetune→test pairing unlikely, but the authors acknowledge and discuss limitations: they did not run a targeted out‑of‑distribution (OOD) robustness battery, so they cannot rule out subtle OOD fragilities of $4$-bit QLoRA.

Why the authors believe $4$-bit QLoRA truly recovers $16$-bit performance
The core claim ($4$-bit NF4 QLoRA matches $16$-bit full finetuning / $16$-bit LoRA) is supported by multiple, independent experiments rather than a single in‑domain comparison. They evaluate on encoder, encoder–decoder and decoder‑only architectures and on several benchmarks: GLUE (RoBERTa), Super‑NaturalInstructions / TK‑Instruct (T5), and $5$-shot MMLU after finetuning LLaMA on instruction datasets. They run these across model sizes ($125\text{M}$ up to $65\text{B}$ for some experiments) and compare against both full $16$-bit finetuning and $16$-bit LoRA baselines. Across those setups the NF4 + double quantization + LoRA adapters achieved parity with $16$-bit baselines in the reported metrics. That consistency across datasets, tasks, architectures and scales is what the paper uses as evidence that the information lost by aggressive storage quantization is effectively recovered by adapter finetuning and that this is not just an artifact of one specific finetune→test pairing.

What they did to limit trivial explanations like data leakage or narrow overfitting
The paper explicitly discusses dataset suitability and performs checks for direct leakage in the most consequential result: Guanaco (QLoRA on OASST1) vs. Vicuna prompts. They ran fuzzy string matching between OASST1 (finetune) and the Vicuna evaluation prompts and did not find overlapping prompts. They also emphasize that different finetuning datasets produce different downstream behavior (e.g., FLAN v2 finetuning helps MMLU but hurts Vicuna), which argues that gains are dataset‑dependent in an interpretable way rather than being an artifact of overfitting to a single shared distribution.

What they did not do (and what they say in the paper)
The authors acknowledge evaluation limits. They did not perform an explicit, systematic OOD robustness suite (e.g., BigBench, HELM, RAFT, or adversarial OOD stress tests) comparing $4$-bit QLoRA and $16$-bit finetuned models. They also did not do controlled cross‑task generalization experiments that deliberately finetune on one distribution and test on a substantially different hold‑out distribution to measure robustness gaps. In the Limitations section they explicitly state they did not evaluate other major benchmarks and that “it appears that the performance of these benchmarks likely depends on how similar the finetuning data is to the benchmark dataset.” They therefore stop short of claiming that $4$-bit QLoRA preserves any and all generalization properties of $16$-bit models.

So: is parity partly due to overfitting?
Given the range of experiments and architectures, the parity is unlikely to be explained solely by overfitting to a narrow finetune/test overlap. However, because the paper did not run dedicated OOD generalization or stress tests, it cannot rule out more subtle distributional brittleness differences between $4$-bit and $16$-bit systems. The authors themselves frame dataset suitability as a key factor and call for more diverse benchmarks and robustness evaluations.

Any signs that $4$-bit models are less robust OOD?
The paper reports no empirical signal that NF4 QLoRA is less robust on the benchmarks they ran: NF4 QLoRA matched or exceeded $16$-bit LoRA on GLUE, TK‑Instruct and MMLU and produced state‑of‑the‑art chatbot results under their evaluation protocols. They also report qualitative failure modes of the Guanaco chatbot (e.g., math, hallucination, refusal inconsistency), but those are normal for large models and not shown to be worse than $16$-bit counterparts. In short: no evidence in the paper of worse OOD robustness, but also no targeted evidence to confirm equivalence OOD.

What would close the gap (recommended tests the paper did not perform)
To be confident that $4$-bit QLoRA has the same OOD/generalization properties as $16$-bit finetuning you would want to run experiments the paper did not: (1) train on one large instruction distribution and evaluate on deliberately different instruction/test corpora (cross‑dataset generalization); (2) evaluate on held‑out benchmarks that probe different capabilities (BigBench, HELM, RAFT, adversarial benchmarks, factuality/hallucination suites); (3) adversarial stress tests that probe sensitivity to prompt perturbations and backdoor‑style triggers; (4) calibration and uncertainty comparisons (are logits / confidence estimates preserved after $4$-bit QLoRA?). The paper’s authors explicitly call out these kinds of future work.

Bottom line
The paper supports its recovery claim with broad in‑domain empirical replication across tasks, architectures and scales and with checks against trivial leakage, which makes simple overfitting an unlikely explanation. However, the authors did not run a dedicated OOD/generalization robustness study, and they acknowledge this limitation. Therefore, you should view the results as strong evidence that QLoRA recovers $16$-bit performance for the tested benchmarks and finetuning setups, but not as a definitive statement that $4$-bit QLoRA preserves every aspect of $16$-bit generalization in all out‑of‑distribution settings.

### Why does NF4 outperform FP4 and Int4 empirically if all are 4-bit quantizers?
Question:
The paper reports NF4 significantly outperforming FP4 and Int4 across multiple models and tasks. Intuitively, I would expect FP4 to be more flexible due to exponent/mantissa, and Int4 to be simple but robust with per-block scaling. What property of the NF4 codebook gives it a consistent advantage? Is it primarily the equal-probability binning under Gaussian assumptions (reducing overload and granular distortion), or the inclusion of an exact zero, or interactions with dequantization in BF16 GEMMs?
Answer:
Short answer: NF4’s consistent win comes mainly from matching the codebook to the empirical distribution of pretrained weights (equal-probability / quantile-based bins for a Gaussian), which minimizes both overload (clipping) and granular (rounding) error for normally-distributed, $0$-centered weights. The exact zero point and the blockwise rescaling used in QLoRA are important secondary contributors; the BF16 dequant/compute path simply benefits from the lower reconstruction error NF4 produces (there is no mysterious numeric interaction beyond better reconstructed weights).

Why that matters (intuitive + technical):
Pretrained transformer weights are roughly $0$-centered and close to normal (the paper documents this and uses it as the justification for NF4). With only $16$ representable values available in $4$ bits, how you place those $16$ reconstruction levels makes a huge difference. There are two dominant error sources in quantization: overload (values outside the representable range get clipped) and granular distortion (values inside the range get rounded to the nearest level). NF4 addresses both in a way that is optimal for Gaussian-like data.

- Equal-probability (quantile) bins for a normal: NF4 constructs its $16$ code values from quantiles of $N(0,1)$ so that, after the usual block-wise rescaling, each quantization bin is expected to contain about the same number of weights. That is information-theoretically optimal for that distribution: it minimizes expected distortion given the assumed pdf. Compared to linear Int4 bins, which put too many levels out in the tails (wasting resolution) or FP4 which has awkward spacing for small magnitudes, NF4 concentrates representational resolution where most weights actually lie (near zero). This reduces mean squared reconstruction error of each block after dequantization, which directly improves the accuracy of subsequent BF16 GEMM operations.

- Reduced overload/clipping: Because NF4’s codebook is matched to the Gaussian shape, the effective dynamic range after block rescaling better balances covering outliers versus providing fine granularity near zero. Int4 with a linear mapping can either clip more outliers if you compress the range, or if you expand range you get very coarse resolution at small magnitudes (large granular error). NF4 mitigates that trade-off by non-uniform bin placement.

- Exact zero point: The paper explicitly modifies the NF construction to include an exact zero code value (an asymmetric construction so one bin is exactly $0$). This matters in practice because many weights and many tensor entries (padding, masked tokens, sparse activations) are exactly zero or very close, and having an exact zero eliminates quantization error on those entries. That both reduces reconstruction error and provides better numeric stability for gradients flowing through the frozen quantized weights to the LoRA adapters.

- FP4 vs NF4: A $4$-bit floating format has an exponent/mantissa structure that gives a certain dynamic range and non-uniform spacing, but its spacing is fixed by the float encoding and is not matched to the Gaussian pdf of pretrained weights. FP4 can give reasonable wide-range coverage, but its distribution of representable values often leaves poor resolution in the dense “mass” around zero. Hence FP4 is more flexible in principle (dynamic range), but less efficient for the specific distribution of network weights, so it gives higher reconstruction error and worse downstream performance in practice.

- Interaction with BF16 GEMMs / dequantization: QLoRA stores weights in NF4, then dequantizes to BF16 and runs the matmul in BF16. The important part is that dequantization is a linear reconstruction of the stored code indices into BF16. The better NF4 reconstructs the true FP32 weight values (lower MSE), the closer the BF16 inputs to the GEMM are to the original full-precision model, hence better forward/backward signals for LoRA. There’s no special magic in BF16 that favors NF4 beyond it receiving a more accurate reconstructed tensor; NF4 simply produces lower quantization distortion so the BF16 computation sees higher-fidelity weights.

Putting it together: NF4 is designed to be (near-)optimal under the empirical assumption that pretrained weights are zero-mean normal after block-wise rescaling. That design choice (quantile-based codebook + explicit zero) concentrates the limited $16$ representable levels where they reduce expected error the most. The end result reported in the paper is lower post-quantization perplexity / better zero-shot and fine-tuned performance compared to FP4 and Int4. Double Quantization and block-wise scaling then preserve those benefits with modest memory overhead.

So: the primary cause is the codebook being matched to the weight distribution (equal-probability bins under a Gaussian model). The exact-zero design is a useful additional feature. The BF16 dequant path benefits because it operates on a more accurate reconstruction; there’s no separate exotic interaction required.

### How do LoRA rank and scaling hyperparameters interact with placing adapters in every layer?
Question:
The paper states that the projection rank $r$ did not matter much, while putting LoRA in more places did. Is there a known regime where increasing $r$ within a layer materially helps once adapters are in every linear submodule? Conversely, can we reduce $r$ and still match 16-bit if we keep full coverage across layers? Any guidance on the trade-off curve between number of adapter locations and per-adapter rank to keep memory roughly constant?
Answer:
Short answer: The paper’s experiments show that covering more linear submodules with LoRA matters far more than increasing the per-adapter rank $r$. In practice you should first expand adapter coverage (put LoRA in every linear projection in each transformer block) and only increase $r$ if you still need capacity for a specific task or you can afford the extra memory. If you must hold total memory roughly constant, trade-offs are simple and roughly linear: doubling the number of adapter locations allows halving $r$ (and vice‑versa) because adapter parameter count scales linearly with both.

Why the paper reaches this conclusion (intuitive + technical)
The QLoRA paper reports that using LoRA only on the usual Q/V projections failed to recover full $16$-bit finetuning performance for large models, whereas adding adapters to every linear layer recovered performance. The intuitive reason is that different submodules (attention projections, feed‑forward projections, output projections, etc.) each provide distinct degrees of freedom the model uses to adapt; spreading small low‑rank updates across those different functional places is more effective than concentrating capacity in only a few places. Technically, a LoRA adapter for a linear map $XW$ uses an additive term $s X L_1 L_2$ where $L_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{h\times r},\ L_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times o}$. The total extra parameters are proportional to $r$ times $(h+o)$ per adapter. So adding many adapters increases representational reach across the network, while increasing $r$ increases per‑location expressivity but cannot reassign capacity to locations where the model needs it.

When increasing $r$ still helps
There are regimes where raising $r$ inside each adapter can still be useful:

- If coverage is already full (adapters in all linear submodules) and the task requires richer per‑layer transformations (e.g., large domain shift, heavy structural changes, or tasks that need many new degrees of freedom), increasing $r$ can help. The paper’s core point is that for the instruction‑finetuning tasks they tried, coverage mattered more and modest $r$ values were enough to match $16$-bit.
- If you are operating under a strict limit on the number of adapter locations (for implementation or inference reasons) then increasing $r$ at those few locations is the only way to add capacity — then larger $r$ will matter.
- For much smaller models or very small layers (where activation gradient memory is small), the tradeoffs can change and per‑adapter $r$ could be more beneficial.

Can you reduce $r$ and still match $16$-bit if you keep full coverage?
Yes — that is exactly what the paper found in their experiments. Because the bulk of finetuning memory is activation gradients and not the LoRA parameter storage, you can afford many adapter locations with small $r$ and still hit the same memory envelope. In the QLoRA experiments, modest ranks (often small $r$) with adapters in every linear module recovered $16$-bit finetuning performance. The implication is that you often can reduce $r$ and keep full coverage without degrading performance for typical instruction‑tuning tasks.

Simple tradeoff bookkeeping (how to keep memory roughly constant)
Adapter parameter count scales approximately as $(\text{num\_locations}) \times r \times \text{hidden\_size}$ (ignoring small constants and output dims). So if you want to keep total adapter params approximately constant, you can set

$r_{\text{new}} = r_{\text{old}} \times \left(\dfrac{\text{num\_old\_locations}}{\text{num\_new\_locations}}\right).$

Example: if you ran with LoRA on only Q and V ( $\text{num\_old\_locations} = 2$ per block) with $r=32$, and now want adapters on every linear (say $\text{num\_new\_locations} = 8$ per block), then $r_{\text{new}} \approx 32 \times (2/8) = 8$ to keep the adapter parameter footprint similar. This linear relationship is the practical tradeoff the paper suggests: increase number of adapter sites first, then increase $r$ if needed.

Practical tuning guidance derived from QLoRA
1. Prioritize coverage: add adapters to all linear submodules in each transformer block before increasing $r$. The paper shows this is the most important hyperparameter for matching full finetuning.
2. Use modest $r$ values by default (e.g., $r \in \{4, 8, 16, 32\}$ depending on hidden size). In the authors’ experiments, increasing $r$ beyond typical small values had little effect once coverage was full.
3. Tune the scalar $s$ (the LoRA scaling) along with $r$. If you must reduce $r$, sometimes increasing $s$ can partially compensate (it scales the adapter contribution), so try $s$ in a small sweep (e.g., $0.1, 0.3, 1.0$) while keeping $r$ small.
4. If memory is the constraint, remember that LoRA parameter storage is small relative to activation gradients (the paper quantifies this). That means you can usually afford full coverage without blowing your memory budget; so don’t trim locations prematurely.
5. Only increase $r$ if (a) coverage is already full, (b) the task still underfits (validation loss or generation quality degrades), or (c) you cannot put adapters in more locations for some reason. Otherwise concentrate on coverage and simple tuning of learning rate and $s$.

Broader connection
This behavior mirrors a general ML principle: it is often better to distribute small amounts of adaptable capacity across many places in a deep model (so the network can reconfigure different computations) than to concentrate capacity in a few locations. QLoRA shows this concretely for LoRA on quantized large models: breadth (coverage) wins over depth (per‑adapter rank) for the instruction‑tuning workloads they studied.

### What is the precise Elo setup and how sensitive are rankings to sampling and K?
Question:
For chatbot evaluation, they compute Elo by sampling labeled pairwise comparisons 10,000 times and using $K=32$. 1) What is the exact update formula they use for Elo after each comparison (win/loss/tie handling)? 2) How sensitive are system-level Elo scores to the random sampling order, choice of $K$, and tie policy? 3) Could a subset of strong-vs-weak matches inflate a model’s Elo if not balanced across opponent strengths? Are there published confidence intervals or bootstrapped CIs per model?
Answer:
Short answer: they use the standard Elo update, handle ties as a half-win, and mitigate sampling/order sensitivity by repeating the tournament many times ($10{,}000$ random-seed draws) with $K=32$. Rankings are somewhat sensitive to sampling order, $K$ and tie policy (especially for closely matched systems or small/imbalanced match sets), and yes — an over-representation of strong-vs-weak matches can inflate a model’s Elo if not balanced. The paper addresses this sensitivity by resampling the labeled pairwise comparisons $10{,}000$ times and reporting that confidence intervals are wide for some comparisons; they do not publish a formal analytic CI per model but the repeated sampling gives an empirical distribution you can use as a bootstrap CI.

Details and grounding in the paper

1) Exact Elo update formula they use
The paper uses the canonical Elo update. For two players (models) A and B with current ratings $R_A$ and $R_B$, compute the expected score for A as
$E_A = \dfrac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_B - R_A)/400}}$.
After a match, let $S_A$ be the actual score for A: $1$ for a win, $0$ for a loss, and $0.5$ for a tie (the paper explicitly reduces comparisons to a three-class labeling problem that accounts for ties). Then the update is
$R_{A,\text{new}} = R_A + K \cdot (S_A - E_A)$,
and symmetrically for B (with $S_B = 1 - S_A$, or $S_B = 0.5$ for a tie). The paper states they start ratings at $1000$ and use $K = 32$.

This is consistent with the text in the Evaluation / Elo Rating paragraphs: they state "We start with a score of 1,000 and use K = 32" and describe tournament-style pairwise comparisons aggregated into Elo scores.

2) How sensitive are the system-level Elo scores to sampling order, K, and tie policy?
Intuition: Elo is an online updating procedure; the order of matches and the sequence of surprising vs. expected outcomes influence intermediate updates, and for a finite set of matches (not the infinite i.i.d. regime) this can produce variability in final ratings. Choosing $K$ changes how much each match moves ratings: larger $K$ amplifies variability (bigger jumps per upset) while smaller $K$ dampens it (more stable but slower to reflect signal). Tie policy (treating a tie as $0.5$ vs e.g. discarding ties or assigning different values) shifts expected score contributions and therefore affects final values.

What the paper actually does and observes: the authors explicitly note a significant ordering effect in GPT-4 scoring and, more generally, they control for ordering effects by randomly sampling the set of labeled pairwise comparisons and repeating the Elo aggregation $10{,}000$ times with different random seeds. They write that Table results have "very wide confidence intervals, with many models overlapping in performance," and that they "repeat this procedure $10{,}000$ times with different random seeds to control for ordering effects." Thus they treat sampling/order as a meaningful source of uncertainty and use resampling to produce an empirical distribution over Elo scores. They also report rank correlations between human and GPT-4 derived Elo (Kendall Tau and Spearman), and Fleiss kappa at sample level — all indicating moderate/noisy agreement across raters and evaluation pipelines.

Practical implications: for this paper’s data and setup, system-level Elos are somewhat sensitive: close models show overlapping score distributions and the reported uncertainty is non-negligible. Repeating the tournament many times (their $10{,}000$ resamples) is what they use to quantify and reduce the impact of sampling/order randomness.

3) Can a subset of strong-vs-weak matches inflate a model’s Elo if not balanced across opponents? Are there published CIs / bootstrapped CIs per model?
Yes, imbalance in which matchups are included can bias Elo. If a model is evaluated disproportionately on weak opponents (or on the opponent pairs that it happens to beat frequently), it will accrue more wins than if evaluated on a balanced set of opponents; Elo will then rise accordingly because Elo aggregates outcomes against whatever opponents are presented. The paper guards against this by performing comparisons on "all permutations of pairs of systems on both the Vicuna and OA benchmarks" for the head-to-head comparisons and by randomly sampling labeled comparisons for Elo aggregation. Sampling from the full set of labeled comparisons (and repeating many times) helps ensure the empirical distribution reflects the true variety of matchups rather than one particular subset that over- or under-represents weak/strong opponents.

About confidence intervals / published uncertainty: the paper does not present analytic confidence intervals per model based on a closed-form Elo variance, but it does provide empirical uncertainty via the repeated-sampling procedure. They explicitly say the Vicuna table has "very wide confidence intervals" and that they repeat Elo computation $10{,}000$ times with different random seeds. That repeated-sampling produces an empirical distribution from which one can extract bootstrapped confidence intervals for each model’s Elo; the paper uses this approach (though it doesn't present every per-model CI in the body, they refer to overlapping intervals and uncertainty). So, while the authors do not frame them as "95% bootstrap CIs" in a single table, the $10{,}000$ resamples are exactly the mechanism to produce such empirical CIs.

Recommendations if you want to reproduce / make results robust
- Use the standard Elo update above and treat ties as $S=0.5$, as the paper does. Start at $1000$ and set $K$ consistent with the paper ($32$) if you want comparable magnitudes.
- To quantify uncertainty, resample your labeled comparisons (or permute match order) many times (the paper uses $10{,}000$) and report percentile intervals (e.g., $2.5\%\text{--}97.5\%$) for each model’s Elo and for rank positions.
- Test sensitivity to $K$ by re-running the resampling for a few $K$ values (e.g., $8$, $16$, $32$, $64$). Lower $K$ reduces volatility; higher $K$ enlarges differences but also increases sensitivity to single upsets.
- Ensure match coverage is balanced across opponents (or sample uniformly from all pairwise labeled comparisons). Avoid reporting Elo from a small skewed subset of matches (e.g., mostly strong-vs-weak) because that can inflate or deflate ratings.
- If you suspect ties are ambiguous, try both tie=$0.5$ and tie-discarded variants during resampling to see how much it changes rankings.

Summary
The paper uses the standard Elo update $R_{\text{new}} = R + K\cdot(S - E)$ with $S = 1/0/0.5$ for win/loss/tie, $K = 32$, initial rating $1000$, and obtains empirical uncertainty by repeating Elo aggregation $10{,}000$ times with different random seeds. They observe that ordering / sampling causes nontrivial variance (wide intervals) and moderate disagreements between human and GPT-4 judgments. An unbalanced matchup set can indeed bias Elo, and the authors mitigate this by sampling across the full set of pairwise comparisons and resampling many times rather than relying on a single deterministic ordering.

### Is there any interaction between NF4 quantization and attention key/value cache precision at inference?
Question:
During generation, attention KV caches can dominate memory and may be stored in lower precision. QLoRA focuses on training-time memory and quantized weights, but what precision is used for KV caches in their inference experiments for Guanaco models? If KV caches use FP16/BF16, could some of the observed parity with 16-bit finetuning be sensitive to inference-time cache precision rather than just weight quantization?
Answer:
Short answer: The paper does not explicitly state “KV caches were stored in $X$ precision” for the Guanaco inference runs, but the text makes it clear that QLoRA uses a $4$‑bit storage format for model weights and dequantizes to a $16$‑bit computation type ($\mathrm{BF16}$ / BrainFloat16) when performing forward/backward passes. From that description the natural interpretation is that during generation the model runs with $16$‑bit computation and therefore the attention keys/values (the KV cache used during autoregressive decoding) are held in the $16$‑bit computation type ($\mathrm{BF16}$/$\mathrm{FP16}$), not in $4$‑bit storage.

Why I conclude that and what it implies: QLoRA’s core idea is “$4$‑bit storage, $16$‑bit compute”: the frozen base weights are stored in $NF4$ on disk/GPU memory and are double‑dequantized into the $16$‑bit computation dtype when used (see the paper’s statements that dequantization is to BFloat16 and matrix multiplies are done in $16$‑bit). KV caching is part of the runtime activations produced by those dequantized computations. Activations and caches are therefore naturally in the computation precision ($\mathrm{BF16}$/$\mathrm{FP16}$) unless you explicitly quantize them; the paper introduces Double Quantization for weight storage and Paged Optimizers for optimizer state, but it does not propose quantizing activations/KV caches for inference. So the practical inference setup used for Guanaco evaluations is best read as using $16$‑bit for compute and thus for KV caches.

Could the observed parity with $16$‑bit finetuning be an artifact of using $16$‑bit KV caches? In principle yes, but it is unlikely to be the whole story. Intuitively, the KV cache stores intermediate attention projections (activations), not learned model parameters. If you run two models with different trained weights but both execute with the same numeric compute precision for activations/caches, then any performance parity you see is primarily reflecting the effective model parameters (weights + adapters + how they interact with the input), not the cache precision. In other words, using $\mathrm{BF16}$/$\mathrm{FP16}$ compute for both the QLoRA‑trained $NF4$ model (dequantized to $\mathrm{BF16}$ at runtime) and a native $16$‑bit finetuned model gives them the same numeric format for activations, so cache precision is shared across conditions and cannot by itself explain a difference in model behavior between the two methods.

However, it is also true that lowering the precision of KV caches can influence generation quality. Attention is sensitive to numerical noise in activations: quantizing KV caches to very low precision (e.g., $8/4$ bits) can change dot‑product values, attention weights, and thus generations. Therefore an experiment that compared a $4$‑bit QLoRA model running with $\mathrm{BF16}$ activations to a $16$‑bit fully‑finetuned model running with lower‑precision activations could produce misleading conclusions. The important control is to keep inference compute precision (activations / KV cache) the same across methods when comparing finetuning approaches.

What I recommend if you want a definitive answer experimentally: (1) Confirm the runtime dtype used by your inference engine for Guanaco (most forks use $\mathrm{BF16}$/$\mathrm{FP16}$ dequantized matmuls). (2) Run a small ablation: compare generations and evaluation metrics for (a) QLoRA $NF4$ dequantized → $\mathrm{BF16}$ compute (standard), (b) same QLoRA model but quantize the KV cache to $\mathrm{FP16}$, $\mathrm{FP8}$ or $\mathrm{Int8}$ during decoding, and (c) the $16$‑bit finetuned baseline under identical cache quantization settings. If parity disappears when you quantize caches only for one condition, that would show cache precision contributes. If parity persists across cache precisions, that strengthens the claim that finetuning on quantized weights ($NF4$ + LoRA) genuinely recovers $16$‑bit behavior.

Bottom line: the paper implies (and standard practice supports) that KV caches were kept at the $16$‑bit computation precision ($\mathrm{BF16}$/$\mathrm{FP16}$). That shared inference precision makes it unlikely that cache precision alone explains the parity with $16$‑bit finetuning, though cache precision can affect generation and is worth controlling for in ablations if you need to be certain.

### How is bias handled: are bias terms stored/updated in 16-bit or quantized with NF4?
Question:
The paper focuses on quantizing weight matrices and adding LoRA adapters. What about bias vectors in linear layers and layernorm parameters? Are they kept in BF16/FP16 and updated, frozen, or quantized to NF4? If biases remain in higher precision while weights are NF4, could that explain part of the performance parity with 16-bit full finetuning?
Answer:
Short answer: QLoRA quantizes the pretrained linear weight matrices to $\text{NF4}$, but it does not quantize or update the pretrained bias vectors or LayerNorm parameters — those remain in the higher-precision computation format ($\text{BF16}/\text{FP16}$) as part of the frozen base model. During finetuning only the LoRA adapter parameters (stored/updated in $16$-bit) receive gradients and change.

Why this follows from the paper: the paper repeatedly describes QLoRA as “backpropagat[ing] gradients through a frozen, $4$-bit quantized pretrained language model into Low Rank Adapters (LoRA).” In the formal layer equation the authors show the forward pass as
$$
\mathbf{Y}^{\text{BF16}} = \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\ \text{doubleDequant}(c_1,c_2,\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}})\ +\ \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_1^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_2^{\text{BF16}},
$$
and they explicitly note “for parameter updates only the gradient with respect to the error for the adapters weights … are needed, and not for $4$-bit weights $\partial E/\partial\mathbf{W}$.” The base model is described as frozen (no weight updates), and the authors emphasize that the storage datatype is low-precision ($\text{NF4}$) while the computation datatype is $\text{BF16}$ (dequantize-to-$\text{BF16}$ for forward/backward). That design implies biases and LayerNorm parameters, being part of the pretrained (frozen) model parameters, are stored/handled as part of the frozen model and are not the trainable LoRA parameters. The paper does not describe a separate quantization of biases or LayerNorm gains/offsets to $\text{NF4}$, nor does it describe updating them during QLoRA finetuning.

Intuition and consequence: leaving small but important components (biases, LayerNorm scale/shift) in $16$-bit and frozen while quantizing the large weight matrices to $\text{NF4}$ is a natural engineering choice. Biases and LayerNorm parameters are few in number and often sensitive to low-precision noise; keeping them in higher precision avoids some quantization-induced errors while saving the bulk of memory by quantizing the giant weight tensors. Because the base model (including its biases/LayerNorms) remains frozen, any remaining performance parity with 16-bit full finetuning comes from two sources: (1) LoRA adapters, which are trained in $16$-bit and can compensate for quantization error by modifying the effective linear transforms, and (2) the fact that critical small parameters (biases / LayerNorm) remain higher precision and hence do not introduce additional numerical degradation. The paper’s claim that they “only compute weight gradients for the LoRA parameters” supports this interpretation.

If you need to be absolutely certain for a particular codebase or release, check the implementation (the authors released code/kernels): in common QLoRA/bitsandbytes implementations the large linear weights are stored in $4$-bit $\text{NF4}$ with dequantization to $\text{BF16}$ at runtime, while biases and LayerNorm parameters remain in $16$-bit and are not altered by QLoRA unless you explicitly choose a PEFT variant that updates them (e.g., BitFit).

### Could 3-bit base weights with LoRA finetuning match 16-bit, or does NF4 rely on a 4-bit floor?
Question:
The authors note as future work whether more aggressive quantization (e.g., 3-bit GPTQ + LoRA) might still recover 16-bit finetuning performance. Given the NF4 construction depends on $2^k$ quantiles, what pitfalls arise at $k=3$? Would outliers and block absmax scaling make 3-bit bins too coarse even with LoRA adaptation? Any preliminary results on perplexity or zero-shot accuracy drops when going from 4-bit to 3-bit with adapter finetuning?
Answer:
Short answer: The paper does not provide any empirical 3-bit results, and NF4 as presented relies on a 4-bit design choice for both information-theoretic reasons and practical behavior. Moving to $k = 3$ ($2^3 = 8$ quantization bins) introduces concrete pitfalls that make a straight NF3 + LoRA recipe unlikely to match 16-bit finetuning without additional algorithmic changes (e.g., better handling of outliers, learned codebooks, or per-row/group GPTQ-style calibration).

Why NF4 is not trivially generalizable to 3 bits
The NormalFloat construction in the paper is built around estimating the quantiles of a zero-mean normal and packing those values into the available discrete levels so that each quantization bin has roughly equal probability mass. That is attractive for 4 bits because you have $2^k = 16$ levels (actually arranged to give a symmetric/asymmetric zero-preserving layout), which is enough resolution to approximate a Gaussian tail shape reasonably well after blockwise absmax scaling.

Dropping to $k = 3$ gives only $2^3 = 8$ representable levels. Intuitively and technically this creates two related problems:

1. Much coarser bins. With only 8 bins the quantization error per weight increases substantially. For normally distributed weights the quantile-based levels will still be the information-theoretically optimal choice for that assumed distribution, but the absolute discretization error grows as you reduce $k$. That larger per-weight error can change activations and gradients in ways that a low-rank adapter (LoRA) cannot fully compensate for.

2. Outliers and block absmax scaling become more damaging. QLoRA/NF4 uses block-wise scaling (absmax per block) to avoid single huge outliers from collapsing the dynamic range of the entire tensor. But when each block is quantized into only 8 bins, an outlier inside the block will drive the scale up and push many "normal" values into just a couple of adjacent bins, making most representable levels unused and increasing quantization noise for the majority of entries. The paper already motivates small block sizes for 4-bit to mitigate outliers; with 3-bit you either need even smaller blocks (raising metadata overhead and possibly hurting throughput) or better outlier handling.

Why LoRA may not fully rescue 3-bit quantization
LoRA finetunes a low-rank additive correction on top of frozen base weights. The paper shows that for 4-bit NF4, LoRA adapters can recover 16-bit finetuning performance because the quantization distortion is small enough and structurally manageable by the adapters (and because NF4 + double quantization + block sizing are tuned for that balance). With coarser 3-bit base weights:

- The adapter has fundamentally limited capacity: LoRA adjusts a low-rank subspace of the layer’s mapping. Large, high-rank errors induced by very coarse quantization (especially those varying across rows/cols due to outliers) are not guaranteed to be representable by the adapter subspace. In short, LoRA can compensate for some systematic, low-rank residuals, but not arbitrary high-rank noise introduced by aggressive quantization.

- Gradient flow and stability: QLoRA computes gradients through the dequantization of the frozen quantized weights. If the dequantization errors become large or discontinuous (more common with fewer bins), the backward path through the frozen weights becomes less stable and the computed adapter gradients may be noisy or biased, making finetuning less effective.

What the paper actually says (and what it leaves open)
The paper explicitly designs and evaluates NF4 ($k = 4$) and shows empirically that NF4 + double quantization + LoRA recovers 16-bit performance across many model sizes. It also notes in Limitations that they did not evaluate more aggressive bit levels like 3-bit and conjectures that “3-bit GPTQ quantization of the base model with LoRA might also yield 16-bit full finetuning performance after finetuning,” but they leave it as future work. So the paper gives no preliminary perplexity/zero-shot accuracy numbers for $3\to4$-bit comparisons; there are no 3-bit results in the paper.

How one might make 3-bit feasible (but not with vanilla NFk alone)
If you want to push to 3 bits and still hope to match 16-bit after adapter finetuning, you will likely need some or all of the following beyond straight NF3 + LoRA:

- Better outlier handling: per-channel/per-row learned scales, clipping strategies, or mixed schemes that keep some values at higher precision (the paper cites works that manage outliers for INT8/INT4 inference).

- Improved quantization methods such as GPTQ-style post-hoc rounding / Hessian-aware quantization or K-means/codebook quantization that reduce reconstruction error for critical weight directions. Those methods can compress to 3 bits while preserving important directions better than simple uniform/quantile levels.

- Smaller block size or hybrid blocks: reducing block size reduces outlier influence but raises metadata cost; the paper uses double quantization to partly control metadata overhead for 4-bit, but with 3-bit the tradeoffs are tighter.

- Possibly training (or finetuning) the quantization parameters: learning per-block codebooks or scales during finetuning (rather than keeping them fixed) could let the system adapt the quantization to the task, but that departs from the QLoRA frozen-weight design and costs memory/complexity.

Practical takeaway
The conceptual point from the paper is: 4-bit NF4 is an information-theoretically sensible and empirically validated floor that, combined with LoRA and the other engineering tricks, recovers 16-bit finetuning performance. Moving to 3 bits is not ruled out, but it is not a drop-in change to their NFk recipe. You should expect coarser bins, stronger outlier effects, and potential adapter capacity or gradient-stability limits. To attempt 3-bit you’ll likely need more sophisticated quantization (GPTQ/codebooks), different block/scale choices, or to allow some learnable/convertible parts of the base weights (instead of freezing everything). The paper explicitly lists 3-bit as future work and provides no preliminary PPL or zero-shot accuracy numbers for 3-bit experiments.

If you want to try it experimentally, a cautious plan is: start with a GPTQ 3-bit post-hoc quantization (which focuses on minimizing reconstruction error for important directions) on the base model, keep blocksize small, test LoRA finetuning on a small scale model first, and compare PPL / zero-shot metrics to the 4-bit NF4+LoRA baseline. That will tell you empirically whether adapter finetuning can bridge the extra discretization gap in your setup.

### Do the per-block scales $c_2$ use absolute max or some robust statistic, and how are they quantized?
Question:
For the first-level quantization of $\mathbf{W}$ into NF4, the paper mentions rescaling by absolute max per block, yielding scales $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$. Before double quantization, do they apply any clipping or robust estimation (like percentile-based scaling) to reduce sensitivity to single outliers? When quantizing $c_2$ to FP8 using blocks of 256, do they use absolute max again or a different procedure due to $c_2$ being strictly positive? They mention subtracting the mean to center around zero—does this imply a symmetric FP8 representation, and does mean subtraction introduce bias on dequant?
Answer:
Short answer: For the first-level quantization of the weight blocks the paper uses the per-block absolute maximum (absmax) to form the scale constants $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ (no percentile clipping or other robust estimator is described). Those $c_2$ values are then double-quantized in blocks of $256$ to an $8$-bit float representation after subtracting the block mean to center the (strictly positive) $c_2$ values. The mean subtraction is undone at dequantization, so it does not introduce a permanent bias if implemented correctly.

Explanation and how this follows from the paper
The paper describes two stages:

1) First-level (weight) quantization. Weight tensor blocks (they use blocksize $64$ for $\mathbf{W}$) are independently scaled by their absolute maximum and quantized into NF4. This is the standard "absmax per block" rescaling described in the Background and in the NF4 procedure. The paper does not describe any percentile-based clipping or robust-statistic replacement for the absmax, so you should assume absmax is used (and therefore the per-block scale $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ is the block absmax-derived constant).

2) Double quantization of the scales. The per-block scales $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ (positive numbers) are treated as the inputs to a second quantization step to save memory. The paper explicitly states they use $8$-bit floats with a blocksize of $256$ for this second quantization and that, because the $c_2$ are positive, they subtract the mean from $c_2$ before quantizing to “center the values around zero and make use of symmetric quantization.” In other words, they shift the $c_2$ block by its mean so the centered values are roughly zero-mean, quantize those centered values using a symmetric FP8 scheme, and store whatever ancillary scalar(s) are needed (the paper calls the remaining second-level constants $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$).

Why the mean subtraction and symmetric quantization?
The motivation given is practical: symmetric quantization around zero is more efficient (and simpler) for many quantizers. Since $c_2$ are strictly positive, directly applying a symmetric quantizer would waste half the representational range on negative numbers. Subtracting the mean recenters the block so the symmetric FP8 can be used more effectively. Using a coarser blocksize ($256$) for $c_2$ is chosen because the scales are less sensitive to fine-grained per-weight differences and no performance degradation was observed.

Does mean subtraction introduce bias on dequantization?
Not if the pipeline is implemented properly. The paper’s doubleDequant operation (see equations (6) and (7)) describes a two-step dequant: first dequantize the quantized $c_2$ back to (centered) FP32 using the stored second-level constants, then dequantize the weights using those recovered $c_2$. The mean that was subtracted before FP8 quantization must be added back when reconstructing the FP32 $c_2$. If you fail to add the mean back you will get biased (too small / shifted) scales and therefore biased weight reconstructions, but the paper’s description implies the mean is inverted as part of double dequantization. So there is no permanent bias introduced by mean-subtraction as long as the inverse operation (add mean back) is performed.

Additional intuitions and trade-offs
Using absmax per block for the weights is simple and fast but sensitive to single outliers; that sensitivity is why the paper uses relatively small weight blocks ($64$) and blocks the quantization so outliers are contained locally. Double-quantizing the scales with a larger block ($256$) is a memory/time trade-off: you save memory by compressing many small scales together, and subtracting the mean allows symmetric FP8 to represent the centered magnitudes efficiently. The overall double-quantization design is presented in the paper as reducing memory (they show the bit-per-parameter arithmetic) while not degrading performance empirically.

Bottom line: first-level weight scales use per-block absmax (no robust percentile clipping described); second-level quantization of those positive scales is done after subtracting the block mean and using symmetric FP8 quantization in blocks of $256$. The mean must be added back during dequantization, so no dequant bias is introduced when implemented correctly.
