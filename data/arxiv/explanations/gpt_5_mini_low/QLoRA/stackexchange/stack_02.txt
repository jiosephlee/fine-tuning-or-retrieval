\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### How exactly are the NF4 codebook values computed and why is it ‘information-theoretically optimal’?
Question:
The paper defines NF4 by taking mid-quantiles of $N(0,1)$: $q_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(Q_X(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1}) + Q_X(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1})\big)$ and then normalizing to $[-1,1]$. 1) Why take midpoints between adjacent quantiles rather than the quantile boundaries themselves? 2) In what precise sense is this "information-theoretically optimal" for zero-mean Gaussians—does it minimize expected quantization error under $N(0,1)$, or maximize entropy of bin occupancy, or both? 3) How does the asymmetric construction to include an exact zero (splitting positive/negative bins) affect optimality guarantees?
Answer:
Short answer: NF4 takes midpoints of adjacent standard-normal quantiles because those midpoints are the natural reconstruction levels (centroids) for quantile/equiprobable bins and therefore minimize squared reconstruction error for that binning. Calling NF4 “information‑theoretically optimal for normal weights” refers to two related facts: using quantiles makes the discrete symbols equiprobable (maximizing the entropy of the symbol distribution for a fixed number of levels), and using the bin centroids (midpoints, or more generally the conditional means) minimizes expected squared error under the assumed distribution. The asymmetric tweak that forces an exact zero trades away perfect symmetry/equiprobability for an exact zero codepoint; this slightly perturbs the strict optimality guarantees but in practice has negligible effect and is important for exactly representing true zeros (padding, sparse activations).

Longer explanation with the technical intuition and how it matches the paper

1) Why midpoints (average of adjacent quantiles) rather than boundaries?
The paper constructs the $k$-bit codebook from the theoretical $N(0,1)$ quantiles. The quantiles (the boundaries) partition the real line into $2^k$ equiprobable bins. For a given binning, the optimal reconstruction value for each bin under mean squared error (MSE) is the conditional mean of the distribution inside that bin (the centroid). For narrow bins of a smooth distribution the centroid is well-approximated by the midpoint between the bin boundaries. The paper uses
$$q_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(Q_X\bigl(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1}\bigr) + Q_X\bigl(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1}\bigr)\bigr)$$
which is exactly the midpoint of the $i$-th equiprobable interval of $N(0,1)$. Using midpoints (or centroids) is what gives you small reconstruction error; using boundaries as reconstruction values would be a poor choice because boundaries are the thresholds not the representative values. So midpoints are the natural reconstruction levels for quantile-based (equiprobable) quantization and are the reason NF4 performs better than using plain 4-bit floats or integers.

2) What does “information‑theoretically optimal for zero‑mean Gaussians” precisely mean?
There are two closely related senses the paper leans on:

- Equiprobable bins maximize the discrete-entropy of the quantized symbol distribution. If you split the continuous $N(0,1)$ mass into $2^k$ bins each with probability $1/2^k$, then the resulting discrete symbol has maximum entropy $\log(2^k)=k$ bits (i.e., the most efficient use of the available symbol set). This is the sense of “information‑theoretically optimal” regarding symbol occupancy: no level is wasted due to lopsided bin populations.

- Given that binning, choosing the reconstruction value for each bin as the centroid (conditional mean) minimizes the expected squared error (the usual distortion measure). Quantile binning together with centroid reconstruction is a standard optimal (or near‑optimal) design in scalar quantization (a special case of the Lloyd–Max framework) when you want to control both occupancy and MSE for a known distribution.

So NF4 is “optimal” in the paper’s statement because (a) the bins are chosen to be equiprobable for $N(0,1)$ (maximizing entropy / equally using levels) and (b) the reconstruction points are the midpoints (centroids) that minimize MSE for those bins. Put differently: for the model assumption “weights $\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$” after scaling, NF$k$ minimizes expected reconstruction error among quantizers that use those equiprobable bins and uses all available symbol resolution efficiently.

Note: exact formal optimality in quantization theory (e.g., the global Lloyd–Max optimum) couples both bin boundaries and reconstruction points and often requires iterative optimization; for a known smooth distribution, the equiprobable partition with bin centroids is a near-optimal, analytically simple choice and has the information/entropy property described. The paper exploits the practical and theoretical advantages of that choice for normally distributed weights.

3) How does the asymmetric construction to include an exact zero affect optimality?
A perfectly symmetric equiprobable $k$-bit quantizer for a symmetric distribution like $N(0,1)$ would place an identical bin straddling zero, but that bin’s centroid would generally not be exactly zero. The paper wants an exact representable zero (a discrete code that maps exactly to real $0$) because zero appears frequently (padding, sparse values) and having an exact zero avoids any quantization error for those exact zeros.

To achieve that, they build an asymmetric codebook: split the negative side into $2^{k-1}$ bins and the positive side into $2^{k-1}+1$ bins (or vice versa), compute the midpoints for both sides, then unify and remove the duplicate zero so that one code exactly equals zero. This makes one side have one more/one fewer bin so that zero becomes an exact centroid/codepoint.

The consequence is that bins are no longer perfectly equiprobable; one or two bins have slightly different probability mass. That breaks the strict form of the equiprobability/maximum-entropy guarantee and means the MSE-optimality is slightly perturbed. In practice the effect is tiny: for modest $k$ (e.g., $k=4$) and the smooth normal density, redistributing one bin across the sign boundary only slightly changes per-bin probabilities and centroids, so expected distortion and symbol-entropy remain essentially as good as the symmetric case, while gaining the practical benefit of exact zero representation. The paper explicitly notes this tradeoff and uses the asymmetric scheme because representing zero exactly is valuable in practice (and empirical evaluations show NF4 still outperforms FP4/Int4).

Practical remarks linking to the paper
The paper also normalizes the codebook to $[-1,1]$ and applies blockwise absolute‑max scaling so that each weight block’s standard deviation matches the NF$k$ scale; that ensures the NF$k$ codepoints are the right absolute magnitudes for the particular block. The combination of theoretical quantiles (for $N(0,1)$), centroids (midpoints), block rescaling, and the asymmetric zero point is why NF4 yields lower quantization error empirically and why the authors call it “information‑theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights” in the operational sense described above.

In short: midpoints = representative/centroid values that minimize MSE for equiprobable bins; equiprobable bins = maximal symbol entropy (efficient use of $k$ bits); the zero-fixing asymmetry slightly breaks perfect optimality but is a tiny practical concession that gives the useful property of an exact zero codepoint.
