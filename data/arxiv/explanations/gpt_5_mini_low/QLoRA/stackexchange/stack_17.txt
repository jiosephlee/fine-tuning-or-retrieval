\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### What is the precise Elo setup and how sensitive are rankings to sampling and K?
Question:
For chatbot evaluation, they compute Elo by sampling labeled pairwise comparisons 10,000 times and using $K=32$. 1) What is the exact update formula they use for Elo after each comparison (win/loss/tie handling)? 2) How sensitive are system-level Elo scores to the random sampling order, choice of $K$, and tie policy? 3) Could a subset of strong-vs-weak matches inflate a model’s Elo if not balanced across opponent strengths? Are there published confidence intervals or bootstrapped CIs per model?
Answer:
Short answer: they use the standard Elo update, handle ties as a half-win, and mitigate sampling/order sensitivity by repeating the tournament many times ($10{,}000$ random-seed draws) with $K=32$. Rankings are somewhat sensitive to sampling order, $K$ and tie policy (especially for closely matched systems or small/imbalanced match sets), and yes — an over-representation of strong-vs-weak matches can inflate a model’s Elo if not balanced. The paper addresses this sensitivity by resampling the labeled pairwise comparisons $10{,}000$ times and reporting that confidence intervals are wide for some comparisons; they do not publish a formal analytic CI per model but the repeated sampling gives an empirical distribution you can use as a bootstrap CI.

Details and grounding in the paper

1) Exact Elo update formula they use
The paper uses the canonical Elo update. For two players (models) A and B with current ratings $R_A$ and $R_B$, compute the expected score for A as
$E_A = \dfrac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_B - R_A)/400}}$.
After a match, let $S_A$ be the actual score for A: $1$ for a win, $0$ for a loss, and $0.5$ for a tie (the paper explicitly reduces comparisons to a three-class labeling problem that accounts for ties). Then the update is
$R_{A,\text{new}} = R_A + K \cdot (S_A - E_A)$,
and symmetrically for B (with $S_B = 1 - S_A$, or $S_B = 0.5$ for a tie). The paper states they start ratings at $1000$ and use $K = 32$.

This is consistent with the text in the Evaluation / Elo Rating paragraphs: they state "We start with a score of 1,000 and use K = 32" and describe tournament-style pairwise comparisons aggregated into Elo scores.

2) How sensitive are the system-level Elo scores to sampling order, K, and tie policy?
Intuition: Elo is an online updating procedure; the order of matches and the sequence of surprising vs. expected outcomes influence intermediate updates, and for a finite set of matches (not the infinite i.i.d. regime) this can produce variability in final ratings. Choosing $K$ changes how much each match moves ratings: larger $K$ amplifies variability (bigger jumps per upset) while smaller $K$ dampens it (more stable but slower to reflect signal). Tie policy (treating a tie as $0.5$ vs e.g. discarding ties or assigning different values) shifts expected score contributions and therefore affects final values.

What the paper actually does and observes: the authors explicitly note a significant ordering effect in GPT-4 scoring and, more generally, they control for ordering effects by randomly sampling the set of labeled pairwise comparisons and repeating the Elo aggregation $10{,}000$ times with different random seeds. They write that Table results have "very wide confidence intervals, with many models overlapping in performance," and that they "repeat this procedure $10{,}000$ times with different random seeds to control for ordering effects." Thus they treat sampling/order as a meaningful source of uncertainty and use resampling to produce an empirical distribution over Elo scores. They also report rank correlations between human and GPT-4 derived Elo (Kendall Tau and Spearman), and Fleiss kappa at sample level — all indicating moderate/noisy agreement across raters and evaluation pipelines.

Practical implications: for this paper’s data and setup, system-level Elos are somewhat sensitive: close models show overlapping score distributions and the reported uncertainty is non-negligible. Repeating the tournament many times (their $10{,}000$ resamples) is what they use to quantify and reduce the impact of sampling/order randomness.

3) Can a subset of strong-vs-weak matches inflate a model’s Elo if not balanced across opponents? Are there published CIs / bootstrapped CIs per model?
Yes, imbalance in which matchups are included can bias Elo. If a model is evaluated disproportionately on weak opponents (or on the opponent pairs that it happens to beat frequently), it will accrue more wins than if evaluated on a balanced set of opponents; Elo will then rise accordingly because Elo aggregates outcomes against whatever opponents are presented. The paper guards against this by performing comparisons on "all permutations of pairs of systems on both the Vicuna and OA benchmarks" for the head-to-head comparisons and by randomly sampling labeled comparisons for Elo aggregation. Sampling from the full set of labeled comparisons (and repeating many times) helps ensure the empirical distribution reflects the true variety of matchups rather than one particular subset that over- or under-represents weak/strong opponents.

About confidence intervals / published uncertainty: the paper does not present analytic confidence intervals per model based on a closed-form Elo variance, but it does provide empirical uncertainty via the repeated-sampling procedure. They explicitly say the Vicuna table has "very wide confidence intervals" and that they repeat Elo computation $10{,}000$ times with different random seeds. That repeated-sampling produces an empirical distribution from which one can extract bootstrapped confidence intervals for each model’s Elo; the paper uses this approach (though it doesn't present every per-model CI in the body, they refer to overlapping intervals and uncertainty). So, while the authors do not frame them as "95% bootstrap CIs" in a single table, the $10{,}000$ resamples are exactly the mechanism to produce such empirical CIs.

Recommendations if you want to reproduce / make results robust
- Use the standard Elo update above and treat ties as $S=0.5$, as the paper does. Start at $1000$ and set $K$ consistent with the paper ($32$) if you want comparable magnitudes.
- To quantify uncertainty, resample your labeled comparisons (or permute match order) many times (the paper uses $10{,}000$) and report percentile intervals (e.g., $2.5\%\text{--}97.5\%$) for each model’s Elo and for rank positions.
- Test sensitivity to $K$ by re-running the resampling for a few $K$ values (e.g., $8$, $16$, $32$, $64$). Lower $K$ reduces volatility; higher $K$ enlarges differences but also increases sensitivity to single upsets.
- Ensure match coverage is balanced across opponents (or sample uniformly from all pairwise labeled comparisons). Avoid reporting Elo from a small skewed subset of matches (e.g., mostly strong-vs-weak) because that can inflate or deflate ratings.
- If you suspect ties are ambiguous, try both tie=$0.5$ and tie-discarded variants during resampling to see how much it changes rankings.

Summary
The paper uses the standard Elo update $R_{\text{new}} = R + K\cdot(S - E)$ with $S = 1/0/0.5$ for win/loss/tie, $K = 32$, initial rating $1000$, and obtains empirical uncertainty by repeating Elo aggregation $10{,}000$ times with different random seeds. They observe that ordering / sampling causes nontrivial variance (wide intervals) and moderate disagreements between human and GPT-4 judgments. An unbalanced matchup set can indeed bias Elo, and the authors mitigate this by sampling across the full set of pairwise comparisons and resampling many times rather than relying on a single deterministic ordering.
