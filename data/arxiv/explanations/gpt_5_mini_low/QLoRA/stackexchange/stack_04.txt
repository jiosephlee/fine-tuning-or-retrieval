\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Deriving the memory savings of Double Quantization: where does the 0.373 bits/parameter come from?
Question:
The paper claims that with blocksize 64 for weights and 256 for the $c_2$ scales, the memory for scales drops from $\tfrac{32}{64}=0.5$ bits/parameter to $\tfrac{8}{64} + \tfrac{32}{64\cdot256}=0.127$ bits/parameter, a 0.373 bpp reduction. Could someone walk through the calculation assumptions? In particular: 1) Are we assuming 32-bit storage for $c_1$ and 8-bit for quantized $c_2$? 2) Why is there no extra overhead for per-block offsets (they subtract mean before quantizing $c_2$)? 3) Is metadata (like block headers) ignored, or amortized to negligible cost?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper assumes the usual first-level quantization stores one 32-bit FP scale per weight-block of size $64$, then double quantization stores the per-block scales (the $c_2$ values) as 8-bit values while keeping one 32-bit “meta”-scale $c_1$ per group of $256$ of those $c_2$'s. With those choices the arithmetic is

- first-level (single quantization) scale cost = $32$ bits per block of $64$ weights = $32/64 = 0.5$ bits/parameter,
- after double quantization the cost = $(8\ \text{bits per } c_2)/64 + (32\ \text{bits per } c_1)/(64\cdot 256) = 8/64 + 32/(64\cdot 256) \approx 0.125 + 0.00195 \approx 0.127$ bits/parameter,

so the saving is $0.5 - 0.127 \approx 0.373$ bits/parameter.

Below I walk through the assumptions and why per-block offsets / headers are not counted explicitly.

1) Bit-width assumptions ($c_1$ and $c_2$)
The paper explicitly describes the two-level scheme: the original weight tensor $W$ is quantized in blocks of size $B = 64$ with a per-block scale $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$. That means one 32-bit float scale per $64$ parameters, hence $32/64$ bits per parameter. Double Quantization then treats those $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ values as the “data” to be quantized again. The paper says they use 8-bit floats for $c_2$ (they write “We use 8-bit Floats ... for the second quantization”) and they use a blocksize of $256$ for the second quantization. The second-level ($c_1^{\text{FP32}}$) is stored at $32$ bits per group of $256$ $c_2$ values. Concretely:

- store each $c_2$ as $8$ bits; each $c_2$ still corresponds to $64$ weights, so contribution is $8/64$ bits per parameter.
- every group of $256$ $c_2$ values has one $32$-bit $c_1$; $256$ $c_2$'s cover $256\cdot 64$ weights, so the contribution per weight is $32/(256\cdot 64) = 32/(64\cdot 256)$ bits.

So the calculation is exactly the one shown in the paper:
$$
\frac{8}{64} + \frac{32}{64\cdot 256} = 0.125 + 0.00195\ldots \approx 0.127\ \text{bits/parameter}.
$$

Thus the paper is assuming $c_2$ stored in 8-bit and $c_1$ stored in 32-bit FP. The weights themselves remain in 4-bit NF4 storage (this is separate).

2) Why no extra per-block offsets for $c_2$ (mean subtraction)
The paper says “Since the $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ are positive, we subtract the mean from $c_2$ before quantization to center the values around zero and make use of symmetric quantization.” That implies the double-quantization pipeline stores whatever global/group-level additive/scale terms are needed at the $c_1$ level rather than adding a new per-$c_2$ metadata field.

Operationally you can implement this so that the stored (quantized) $c_2$ values are zero-mean within each second-level group (the $256$ $c_2$'s), and the group-level $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$ stores the group shift/scale needed when reconstructing the original $c_2$'s. In other words, the second-level FP32 constants (the $c_1$) implicitly carry the small extra information (mean / re-centering) for the whole group of $256$ $c_2$'s, so you do not need an extra $32$-bit offset per original $64$-weight block. That is why no additional per-block offset term is added in the simple accounting above.

3) Metadata / headers — ignored or amortized
Yes: the paper treats small auxiliary bookkeeping (block headers, any tiny flags, or alignment padding) as negligible and amortized over huge numbers of parameters. The per-parameter accounting shown is purely for the dominant storage terms: quantized weight bits and the quantization-constant storage. For very large models (millions to tens of billions of parameters), a few bytes of extra per-block metadata would contribute extremely tiny extra bits/parameter compared to the computed $0.127$ bpp. For example, even if you added one $32$-bit extra header per block of $64$ weights, that would cost another $32/64 = 0.5$ bits/parameter (which would be material), but the paper’s scheme does not add such headers per weight-block — the only extra $32$-bit fields are the $c_1$'s per $256$ $c_2$'s (already included). Typical implementation choices (packing, group header once per group, reusing $c_1$ for mean, etc.) make the remaining metadata negligible and are therefore omitted from the back-of-envelope numbers.

Summary / Intuition
Think of it hierarchically: originally you had one $32$-bit scale for every $64$ weights ($0.5$ bpp). Double Quantization compresses that layer of scales by representing many of those scale values (the $c_2$'s) with cheap $8$-bit entries, and only keeping a single $32$-bit “anchor” ($c_1$) for a group of $256$ of them. So the dominant cost becomes the $8$-bit-per-$c_2$ term amortized over $64$ weights ($8/64 = 0.125$ bpp) plus the tiny amortized $c_1$ cost ($32/(64\cdot 256) \approx 0.002$ bpp). That yields $\sim 0.127$ bpp, saving $\sim 0.373$ bpp versus the original $0.5$ bpp. The paper ignores tiny additional headers/flags because they are negligible at the scale of billions of parameters; any required re-centering/mean information is folded into the group-level $c_1$ rather than stored per original block.
