\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Why does NF4 outperform FP4 and Int4 empirically if all are 4-bit quantizers?
Question:
The paper reports NF4 significantly outperforming FP4 and Int4 across multiple models and tasks. Intuitively, I would expect FP4 to be more flexible due to exponent/mantissa, and Int4 to be simple but robust with per-block scaling. What property of the NF4 codebook gives it a consistent advantage? Is it primarily the equal-probability binning under Gaussian assumptions (reducing overload and granular distortion), or the inclusion of an exact zero, or interactions with dequantization in BF16 GEMMs?
Answer:
Short answer: NF4’s consistent win comes mainly from matching the codebook to the empirical distribution of pretrained weights (equal-probability / quantile-based bins for a Gaussian), which minimizes both overload (clipping) and granular (rounding) error for normally-distributed, $0$-centered weights. The exact zero point and the blockwise rescaling used in QLoRA are important secondary contributors; the BF16 dequant/compute path simply benefits from the lower reconstruction error NF4 produces (there is no mysterious numeric interaction beyond better reconstructed weights).

Why that matters (intuitive + technical):
Pretrained transformer weights are roughly $0$-centered and close to normal (the paper documents this and uses it as the justification for NF4). With only $16$ representable values available in $4$ bits, how you place those $16$ reconstruction levels makes a huge difference. There are two dominant error sources in quantization: overload (values outside the representable range get clipped) and granular distortion (values inside the range get rounded to the nearest level). NF4 addresses both in a way that is optimal for Gaussian-like data.

- Equal-probability (quantile) bins for a normal: NF4 constructs its $16$ code values from quantiles of $N(0,1)$ so that, after the usual block-wise rescaling, each quantization bin is expected to contain about the same number of weights. That is information-theoretically optimal for that distribution: it minimizes expected distortion given the assumed pdf. Compared to linear Int4 bins, which put too many levels out in the tails (wasting resolution) or FP4 which has awkward spacing for small magnitudes, NF4 concentrates representational resolution where most weights actually lie (near zero). This reduces mean squared reconstruction error of each block after dequantization, which directly improves the accuracy of subsequent BF16 GEMM operations.

- Reduced overload/clipping: Because NF4’s codebook is matched to the Gaussian shape, the effective dynamic range after block rescaling better balances covering outliers versus providing fine granularity near zero. Int4 with a linear mapping can either clip more outliers if you compress the range, or if you expand range you get very coarse resolution at small magnitudes (large granular error). NF4 mitigates that trade-off by non-uniform bin placement.

- Exact zero point: The paper explicitly modifies the NF construction to include an exact zero code value (an asymmetric construction so one bin is exactly $0$). This matters in practice because many weights and many tensor entries (padding, masked tokens, sparse activations) are exactly zero or very close, and having an exact zero eliminates quantization error on those entries. That both reduces reconstruction error and provides better numeric stability for gradients flowing through the frozen quantized weights to the LoRA adapters.

- FP4 vs NF4: A $4$-bit floating format has an exponent/mantissa structure that gives a certain dynamic range and non-uniform spacing, but its spacing is fixed by the float encoding and is not matched to the Gaussian pdf of pretrained weights. FP4 can give reasonable wide-range coverage, but its distribution of representable values often leaves poor resolution in the dense “mass” around zero. Hence FP4 is more flexible in principle (dynamic range), but less efficient for the specific distribution of network weights, so it gives higher reconstruction error and worse downstream performance in practice.

- Interaction with BF16 GEMMs / dequantization: QLoRA stores weights in NF4, then dequantizes to BF16 and runs the matmul in BF16. The important part is that dequantization is a linear reconstruction of the stored code indices into BF16. The better NF4 reconstructs the true FP32 weight values (lower MSE), the closer the BF16 inputs to the GEMM are to the original full-precision model, hence better forward/backward signals for LoRA. There’s no special magic in BF16 that favors NF4 beyond it receiving a more accurate reconstructed tensor; NF4 simply produces lower quantization distortion so the BF16 computation sees higher-fidelity weights.

Putting it together: NF4 is designed to be (near-)optimal under the empirical assumption that pretrained weights are zero-mean normal after block-wise rescaling. That design choice (quantile-based codebook + explicit zero) concentrates the limited $16$ representable levels where they reduce expected error the most. The end result reported in the paper is lower post-quantization perplexity / better zero-shot and fine-tuned performance compared to FP4 and Int4. Double Quantization and block-wise scaling then preserve those benefits with modest memory overhead.

So: the primary cause is the codebook being matched to the weight distribution (equal-probability bins under a Gaussian model). The exact-zero design is a useful additional feature. The BF16 dequant path benefits because it operates on a more accurate reconstruction; there’s no separate exotic interaction required.
