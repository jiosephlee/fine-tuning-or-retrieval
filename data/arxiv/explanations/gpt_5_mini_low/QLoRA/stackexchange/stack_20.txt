\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Could 3-bit base weights with LoRA finetuning match 16-bit, or does NF4 rely on a 4-bit floor?
Question:
The authors note as future work whether more aggressive quantization (e.g., 3-bit GPTQ + LoRA) might still recover 16-bit finetuning performance. Given the NF4 construction depends on $2^k$ quantiles, what pitfalls arise at $k=3$? Would outliers and block absmax scaling make 3-bit bins too coarse even with LoRA adaptation? Any preliminary results on perplexity or zero-shot accuracy drops when going from 4-bit to 3-bit with adapter finetuning?
Answer:
Short answer: The paper does not provide any empirical 3-bit results, and NF4 as presented relies on a 4-bit design choice for both information-theoretic reasons and practical behavior. Moving to $k = 3$ ($2^3 = 8$ quantization bins) introduces concrete pitfalls that make a straight NF3 + LoRA recipe unlikely to match 16-bit finetuning without additional algorithmic changes (e.g., better handling of outliers, learned codebooks, or per-row/group GPTQ-style calibration).

Why NF4 is not trivially generalizable to 3 bits
The NormalFloat construction in the paper is built around estimating the quantiles of a zero-mean normal and packing those values into the available discrete levels so that each quantization bin has roughly equal probability mass. That is attractive for 4 bits because you have $2^k = 16$ levels (actually arranged to give a symmetric/asymmetric zero-preserving layout), which is enough resolution to approximate a Gaussian tail shape reasonably well after blockwise absmax scaling.

Dropping to $k = 3$ gives only $2^3 = 8$ representable levels. Intuitively and technically this creates two related problems:

1. Much coarser bins. With only 8 bins the quantization error per weight increases substantially. For normally distributed weights the quantile-based levels will still be the information-theoretically optimal choice for that assumed distribution, but the absolute discretization error grows as you reduce $k$. That larger per-weight error can change activations and gradients in ways that a low-rank adapter (LoRA) cannot fully compensate for.

2. Outliers and block absmax scaling become more damaging. QLoRA/NF4 uses block-wise scaling (absmax per block) to avoid single huge outliers from collapsing the dynamic range of the entire tensor. But when each block is quantized into only 8 bins, an outlier inside the block will drive the scale up and push many "normal" values into just a couple of adjacent bins, making most representable levels unused and increasing quantization noise for the majority of entries. The paper already motivates small block sizes for 4-bit to mitigate outliers; with 3-bit you either need even smaller blocks (raising metadata overhead and possibly hurting throughput) or better outlier handling.

Why LoRA may not fully rescue 3-bit quantization
LoRA finetunes a low-rank additive correction on top of frozen base weights. The paper shows that for 4-bit NF4, LoRA adapters can recover 16-bit finetuning performance because the quantization distortion is small enough and structurally manageable by the adapters (and because NF4 + double quantization + block sizing are tuned for that balance). With coarser 3-bit base weights:

- The adapter has fundamentally limited capacity: LoRA adjusts a low-rank subspace of the layer’s mapping. Large, high-rank errors induced by very coarse quantization (especially those varying across rows/cols due to outliers) are not guaranteed to be representable by the adapter subspace. In short, LoRA can compensate for some systematic, low-rank residuals, but not arbitrary high-rank noise introduced by aggressive quantization.

- Gradient flow and stability: QLoRA computes gradients through the dequantization of the frozen quantized weights. If the dequantization errors become large or discontinuous (more common with fewer bins), the backward path through the frozen weights becomes less stable and the computed adapter gradients may be noisy or biased, making finetuning less effective.

What the paper actually says (and what it leaves open)
The paper explicitly designs and evaluates NF4 ($k = 4$) and shows empirically that NF4 + double quantization + LoRA recovers 16-bit performance across many model sizes. It also notes in Limitations that they did not evaluate more aggressive bit levels like 3-bit and conjectures that “3-bit GPTQ quantization of the base model with LoRA might also yield 16-bit full finetuning performance after finetuning,” but they leave it as future work. So the paper gives no preliminary perplexity/zero-shot accuracy numbers for $3\to4$-bit comparisons; there are no 3-bit results in the paper.

How one might make 3-bit feasible (but not with vanilla NFk alone)
If you want to push to 3 bits and still hope to match 16-bit after adapter finetuning, you will likely need some or all of the following beyond straight NF3 + LoRA:

- Better outlier handling: per-channel/per-row learned scales, clipping strategies, or mixed schemes that keep some values at higher precision (the paper cites works that manage outliers for INT8/INT4 inference).

- Improved quantization methods such as GPTQ-style post-hoc rounding / Hessian-aware quantization or K-means/codebook quantization that reduce reconstruction error for critical weight directions. Those methods can compress to 3 bits while preserving important directions better than simple uniform/quantile levels.

- Smaller block size or hybrid blocks: reducing block size reduces outlier influence but raises metadata cost; the paper uses double quantization to partly control metadata overhead for 4-bit, but with 3-bit the tradeoffs are tighter.

- Possibly training (or finetuning) the quantization parameters: learning per-block codebooks or scales during finetuning (rather than keeping them fixed) could let the system adapt the quantization to the task, but that departs from the QLoRA frozen-weight design and costs memory/complexity.

Practical takeaway
The conceptual point from the paper is: 4-bit NF4 is an information-theoretically sensible and empirically validated floor that, combined with LoRA and the other engineering tricks, recovers 16-bit finetuning performance. Moving to 3 bits is not ruled out, but it is not a drop-in change to their NFk recipe. You should expect coarser bins, stronger outlier effects, and potential adapter capacity or gradient-stability limits. To attempt 3-bit you’ll likely need more sophisticated quantization (GPTQ/codebooks), different block/scale choices, or to allow some learnable/convertible parts of the base weights (instead of freezing everything). The paper explicitly lists 3-bit as future work and provides no preliminary PPL or zero-shot accuracy numbers for 3-bit experiments.

If you want to try it experimentally, a cautious plan is: start with a GPTQ 3-bit post-hoc quantization (which focuses on minimizing reconstruction error for important directions) on the base model, keep blocksize small, test LoRA finetuning on a small scale model first, and compare PPL / zero-shot metrics to the 4-bit NF4+LoRA baseline. That will tell you empirically whether adapter finetuning can bridge the extra discretization gap in your setup.
