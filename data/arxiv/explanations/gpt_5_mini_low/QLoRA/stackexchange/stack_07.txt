\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Is there a mismatch between the NF4 ‘optimal for Gaussian’ claim and using absmax normalization per block?
Question:
NF4’s codebook is built for $N(0,1)$, but blocks are normalized by absolute max rather than by variance. For heavy-tailed or skewed blocks, $\text{absmax}$ can be dominated by outliers, leaving most mass compressed near zero. Wouldn’t per-block standard-deviation scaling be more aligned with the Gaussian-optimized codebook? Is there empirical evidence that absmax performs better in practice for pretrained LLM weights with near-Gaussian but not exact normal distributions?
Answer:
Short answer: not really — there is no practical mismatch in QLoRA between building NF4 for a standard normal and using per-block absmax scaling. The paper’s design and experiments show absmax per block is a pragmatic normalization that, together with small block sizes and the NF4 construction, aligns well with pretrained LLM weight statistics and gives better empirical results than other 4-bit formats.

Why this is reasonable (intuitive + technical): NF4 is derived by computing $k$-bit quantiles for a standard normal $N(0,1)$ and then mapping those quantile values into the target numeric range (step (1)–(2) in the paper). To quantize an actual weight block we need to map that block into the same fixed range (conventionally $[-1,1]$) so the NF4 codebook’s quantiles apply. Block-wise absolute-max normalization does exactly that: it rescales the block so its maximum magnitude hits the endpoint of the target range. In distributional terms this is equivalent to fixing the block’s scale (standard deviation up to a constant) so that the block’s empirical distribution is approximately the same shape as the reference $N(0,1)$ up to scale.

The concern you raise — that an outlier will dominate absmax and compress most values near zero so the NF4 codebook (optimized for the normal) is a mismatch — is valid in principle. But QLoRA mitigates this in practice by using small block sizes (they use blocksize $64$ for weights). With smaller blocks the probability that a single extreme outlier dominates most of the block is much lower, so the absmax is a reasonable proxy for the block’s scale. Small-block absmax keeps the mapping of typical weights into the interior of the NF4 quantile bins where the NF4 design is optimal (equal-mass bins for a normal). In other words, NF4 assumes zero-centered normality up to a scalar; absmax enforces that scalar cheaply.

Why not use per-block standard deviation instead? Scaling by sample standard deviation would in fact align more directly with the intended normalization of a normal distribution (it explicitly rescales to unit variance). But estimating variance is more expensive (requires one extra pass to compute mean/variance per block, though that is a small constant extra work) and is itself sensitive to outliers (variance is even more influenced by large magnitudes than absmax). Robust alternatives (MAD, trimmed std) are possible but would add complexity. The paper chose the conventional and very cheap absmax because it is standard in blockwise quantization and pairs well with small blocks and NF4’s design.

Empirical evidence in the paper: the authors explicitly state (and provide an appendix) that pretrained weights are close to zero-centered normal (see Appendix~\ref{app:normality}). They report systematic experiments across architectures and scales ($125\text{M}$–$65\text{B}$) comparing NF4 (with block-wise absmax normalization) to FP4 and Int4; NF4 consistently yields better perplexity and downstream zero-shot/finetuning performance (Figures/Tables cited in Section “4-bit NormalFloat Quantization” and the experiments summarized in Section “QLoRA vs. Standard Finetuning”). These empirical gains show that the combination of NF4 codebook + blockwise absmax scaling works well on real pretrained LLM weights despite non-idealities (heavy tails, small skew). They also use small blocks ($64$) and double quantization of scales to control memory while preserving accuracy.

Practical caveats and alternatives: if you had blocks that are extremely heavy-tailed or strongly skewed (e.g., a block dominated by a single large weight), absmax will compress the bulk and quantization error for the bulk will increase. In such pathological blocks, using robust scale estimators (e.g., clipping outliers, using trimmed-mean/std, or MAD-based scaling) or switching to a different blocking strategy would help. The paper does not explore those alternatives in depth, but the consistent empirical wins for NF4 with absmax across many models suggest such pathological blocks are rare enough in pretrained weights to not hurt end performance.

Conclusion: absmax per block is a practical, cheap way to match block scale to the NF4 codebook; small block sizes and the near-normality of pretrained weights make this a good fit in practice. The paper’s experiments (Appendix~\ref{app:normality}, NF4 vs FP4/Int4 comparisons, and downstream finetuning results) provide empirical evidence that absmax normalization works well with NF4 on real LLM weights. If you have a special model or suspect many extreme outliers, testing robust scaling or per-block variance normalization is reasonable, but for standard pretrained LLMs the absmax + NF4 recipe in QLoRA is empirically validated.
