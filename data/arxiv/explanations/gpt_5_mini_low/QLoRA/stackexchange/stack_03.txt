\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### What does “Double Quantization” quantize, and is it different from quantizing weights twice?
Question:
Section Double Quantization says they quantize the quantization constants $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ into $c_2^{\text{FP8}}$ with a second level constant $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$. I first thought this meant weights themselves get quantized twice. Can someone confirm the exact pipeline? As I understand it: weights $\mathbf{W}$ are quantized into $\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}$ using per-block scales $c_2$; then $c_2$ values are themselves block-quantized to FP8 with their own per-block scale $c_1$. At runtime, you dequantize $c_2$ via $c_1$, then dequantize $\mathbf{W}$ via the recovered $c_2$. Is that correct? Are there any numerical stability issues from dequantizing a dequantized scale?
Answer:
Short answer: Double Quantization does NOT quantize the weights twice. The pipeline quantizes the weights once (to NF4) using per-block FP32 scales $c_2$, then quantizes those scale values themselves ($c_2^{\mathrm{FP8}}$) into an 8-bit representation ($c_2^{\mathrm{FP8}}$) with a second-level FP32 scale $c_1$. At runtime you first dequantize the stored $c_2^{\mathrm{FP8}}$ back to FP32 using $c_1$, then dequantize the NF4 weight blocks using the recovered $c_2$ to get the BF16 weights for computation. The paper shows this is numerically safe in practice and causes no measurable performance drop.

Explanation and details tied to the paper
The paper describes three layers of values and how they are stored:

1) The weight blocks: $\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{NF4}}$ (stored in NF4, 4-bit). Each block is quantized by dividing the original FP32 block by a per-block quantization constant $c_2^{\mathrm{FP32}}$ and rounding into the NF4 bin values.

2) The first-level quantization constants: $c_2^{\mathrm{FP32}}$. There is one $c_2$ per weight block (blocksize 64 in the paper’s setup). Storing all of these as FP32 is relatively expensive (about $32/64 = 0.5$ bits/parameter on average), so the paper applies the second quantization to these $c_2$ values.

3) The second-level quantization of scales: $c_2^{\mathrm{FP8}}$ (8-bit floats) plus a second-level global/per-block (depending on choice) FP32 scale $c_1^{\mathrm{FP32}}$ used to dequantize the $c_2$ values. In the paper they use blocksize 256 for the $c_2$ quantization and symmetric quantization after subtracting the mean (because $c_2$ are positive, they center before quantizing).

Runtime dequantization (as given in the paper)
You perform $\text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\mathrm{FP32}}, c_2^{k\text{-bit}}, W^{k\text{-bit}})$ as

- recovered_c2 = dequant($c_1^{\mathrm{FP32}}$, $c_2^{k\text{-bit}}$) — i.e., convert the stored $c_2^{\mathrm{FP8}}$ back to FP32 using $c_1$,
- $W^{\mathrm{BF16}}$ = dequant(recovered_c2, $\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{NF4}}$) — i.e., convert the NF4 weight bins back to BF16 using the recovered per-block scale.

So the weights themselves are stored only once (in NF4). The “double” refers to quantizing the scale constants ($c_2$) a second time to save memory.

Why this is numerically okay (intuitive + technical)
Intuitively, the $c_2$ values are far fewer and have much lower dynamic range/entropy than raw weights. They are just positive scale factors (one per block) and tend to vary smoothly across blocks. Quantizing those few numbers to FP8 (with centering) loses very little information relative to quantizing the whole weight tensor. The paper reports using FP8 for $c_2$ with blocksize 256 and states that “no performance degradation is observed” from this second quantization.

Technically, the design choices protect numerical stability:

- $c_1$ (the second-level scale) is stored in FP32 (paper explicitly uses $c_1^{\mathrm{FP32}}$) so the mapping from stored $c_2^{\mathrm{FP8}}$ back to FP32 uses a high-precision multiplier, avoiding catastrophic loss when recovering the per-block scale.
- They subtract the mean from $c_2$ before quantization to center values and use symmetric quantization, which reduces range and better uses FP8 dynamic range.
- They choose relatively large blocksize (256) for $c_2$ quantization so each $c_1$ covers many $c_2$ entries, limiting overhead but keeping $c_2$ variation modest within that block.
- In practice NF4 + double quantization matched 16-bit baselines (paper’s empirical results), indicating no meaningful instability introduced by double-dequantizing.

Small caveats / potential failure modes
While the paper reports no degradation, theoretically double dequantization could amplify relative errors in extreme cases: if a recovered $c_2$ is very small (close to zero) and its FP8 quantization error is a significant fraction of its value, dividing weights by this noisy scale during dequantization could inflate relative error for those blocks. The mitigation in the paper is: use FP32 for $c_1$, center $c_2$ prior to FP8, and choose block sizes that keep $c_2$ variance moderate. Empirically these choices were sufficient — the authors report that double quantization reduces the quantization-constant memory ($0.5$ bits/param $\to$ $\approx 0.127$ bits/param) with no observed accuracy loss.

Conclusion
Your interpretation is correct: weights are quantized once (to NF4) with per-block scales $c_2$, then the $c_2$ values are quantized (to FP8) with a second-level scale $c_1$. At runtime you dequantize $c_2$ using $c_1$ and then dequantize the weights using the recovered $c_2$. The paper shows this double-dequantize procedure is numerically stable in practice and does not degrade performance when using the stated choices (NF4 for weights, FP8 for $c_2$, subtract-mean centering, blocksize 64 for weights and 256 for $c_2$, and keeping $c_1$ in FP32).
