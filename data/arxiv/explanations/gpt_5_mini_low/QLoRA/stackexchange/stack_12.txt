\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### How does NF4 handle exact zeros and does the asymmetric binning change reconstruction bias?
Question:
To get an exact zero, the paper proposes asymmetric NF4: $2^{k-1}$ negative bins and $2^{k-1}+1$ positive bins, then removing one duplicate zero. How does this affect the mean of the reconstructed values when the true distribution is symmetric around zero? Is there a slight positive bias due to the extra positive bin density near zero, or does the removal of one zero value ensure symmetry in practice? Any quantitative comparison of reconstruction bias vs. symmetric quantizers that include a zero code explicitly?
Answer:
Short answer: The NF4 construction in the paper does not introduce a systematic positive bias for a true zero-centered symmetric distribution. By building equal-probability bins on the negative and positive sides and then removing one duplicated zero code, the quantizer preserves symmetry in expectation; any remaining bias is only from finite-sample/blockwise effects (estimation noise, block rescaling), and the paper’s empirical results indicate those effects are negligible.

Why, intuitively and technically: NF4 is derived from quantile (equal-population) binning of a standard normal. If you were to compute the quantiles of a symmetric distribution like $N(0,1)$, the negative quantiles are the negatives of the positive quantiles: $Q_X(p) = -Q_X(1-p)$. The authors form $2^{k-1}$ negative bins and $2^{k-1}+1$ positive bins so that there is an explicit code value exactly at zero (the positive and negative constructions would both produce a zero midpoint). They then “unify these sets of $q_i$ and remove one of the two zeros.” After this removal, every positive representative value has a matching negative representative (same magnitude, opposite sign), and zero remains exactly representable. For an ideal infinite-sample, perfectly-normal, block-rescaled tensor this implies the mapping $x \to \text{quantized-code} \to \text{dequantized value}$ is antisymmetric: $\mathrm{dequant}(-x) = -\mathrm{dequant}(x)$. Therefore the expected reconstruction error over a symmetric distribution has zero mean, i.e., no systematic positive bias.

Where small bias could arise in practice: the symmetry argument relies on three idealizations: (1) the underlying distribution is exactly symmetric and matches the theoretical normal used to compute the quantiles; (2) the blockwise scaling/rescaling (absmax or std matching) is exact and identical for corresponding positive/negative magnitudes; (3) quantile values are computed without sampling/estimation noise. In real tensors you get finite-block sizes, empirical deviations from normality, and approximate quantile estimation or finite-sample noise in the block statistics. Those practical effects can induce tiny residual biases (and/or small differences in reconstruction MSE between positive and negative small magnitudes), but these are implementation/estimation errors, not a fundamental consequence of the asymmetric-bin construction.

What the paper reports: The QLoRA paper explicitly motivates the asymmetric binning only to guarantee an exact zero code while keeping the information-theoretic equal-probability property for normal weights. It does not report a numerical comparison of reconstruction mean-bias between NF4 and other symmetric quantizers that also reserve an explicit zero code, nor does it report a measured systematic positive bias. Instead, the paper shows that NF4 yields better empirical downstream performance (perplexity / zero-shot tasks; and in QLoRA finetuning it recovers 16-bit performance), which implies any reconstruction bias introduced by the data type is too small to harm model performance in their experiments.

If you want a quantitative test (recommended): compute, per block, the mean of $\bigl(\mathrm{dequant}(x) - x\bigr)$ for samples drawn from a symmetric distribution (or for actual weight blocks), then aggregate across blocks. Also compare NF4 to a symmetric $k$-bit quantizer that explicitly includes a zero code (e.g., reserve one code for zero and split remaining codes symmetrically). In practice you should find mean bias near zero and reconstruction MSE differences dominated by blocksize and rescaling choices rather than the $+1$ positive bin trick itself.

Conclusion: Removing one duplicate zero in NF4 enforces an exact zero code while keeping quantile symmetry in value magnitudes, so there is no built-in positive bias for symmetric distributions; any tiny bias observed would come from finite-sample/block-rescaling effects, and the paper’s empirical results show these effects are negligible for model quality.
