\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Why do default LoRA placements (Q and V only) fail to match 16-bit full finetuning?
Question:
The paper finds that applying LoRA only to the attention Q and V projections underperforms, and that placing adapters in all linear layers is critical to match full finetuning. Is there an explanation for why limiting adaptation to Q/V is insufficient for instruction tuning? Do certain components (like output projections, feed-forward layers, or key/value) carry task-specific adjustments that cannot be captured by modifying attention projections alone? Any ablations quantifying which additional layers contribute most to the gap closing?
Answer:
Short answer: because instruction tuning changes more than just the attention “query” and “value” subspaces. The Q/V-only LoRA setup (the common default) can’t express many of the task-specific parameter changes that the rest of transformer layers (output projections, feed‑forward layers, and other linear maps) must perform during instruction finetuning. The QLoRA paper shows empirically that the single strongest factor for recovering $16$-bit full finetuning performance is how many adapters you place: adding LoRA to every linear layer of each transformer block is required to match full finetuning, while tuning other LoRA hyperparameters (e.g., rank $r$) matters little.

Why intuitively Q/V-only is insufficient
The usual Q/V LoRA idea rests on the intuition that changing attention patterns (what to attend to and how to use attended vectors) is the main lever for task adaptation. That is often true for some tasks, but instruction following is broader: it requires changes to how hidden states are transformed after attention (the output projection of the attention head), how information is mixed and re‑weighted in the feed‑forward networks (FFNs), and how those transformed hidden states are mapped to logits or downstream layers. Concretely:

• Attention is not only “query” and “value.” The output of attention combines the values via the attention weights and then applies an output linear projection; adapting only Q and V can change what gets attended to and what content gets retrieved, but it cannot change how that retrieved content is re‑projected back into the residual stream or how it interacts with subsequent FFNs. Those projection/FFN transforms are often where task‑specific post‑processing happens (formatting, style, constraint enforcement, etc.).  
• FFNs implement powerful elementwise nonlinear remapping and channel mixing; many instruction‑tuning behaviors (e.g., reformatting answers, adding persona, verbosity) can be realized as modest shifts in FFN behavior. A low‑rank change in the attention Q/V subspace cannot reproduce arbitrary needed FFN adjustments.  
• The final mapping toward logits (or intermediate output projections) can require shifts that are orthogonal to what attention changes alone can do. For example, to prefer a particular answer phrasing or to suppress certain tokens, you may need to alter the final linear mappings rather than only changing attention patterns.

What the paper shows empirically
The QLoRA paper explicitly reports that applying LoRA only to Q and V did not replicate full $16$-bit finetuning performance on their instruction tuning setups (they show this for LLaMA on Alpaca in their Figure referenced in the text). Their main ablation result is that the number/placement of adapters is the dominant LoRA hyperparameter: expanding LoRA to every linear layer in the transformer block (i.e., all projections and FFN linear layers) recovers the $16$-bit baseline, whereas changing the adapter rank $r$ or other LoRA knobs has little effect. They therefore recommend—and use—adapters in all linear layers to eliminate the accuracy gap. The paper does not present a fine‑grained per‑layer ranked list (e.g., “FFN contributes $40\%$ of the gap, attention output $30\%$…”), but the clear claim and experimental finding is that broad coverage (all linear layers) is the key to matching full finetuning.

Practical implication
Because LoRA adapters are small relative to activation memory (and QLoRA’s quantization makes the base model storage cheap), it’s affordable to place adapters widely. The paper emphasizes that most finetuning memory is in activations/gradients, not in LoRA parameters, so you can add adapters across all linear layers without hitting a large memory penalty. Their recommendation—borne out by experiments—is to place LoRA in every linear projection (queries, keys, values, output projections, and the FFN input/output linear layers) when doing instruction finetuning if your goal is to match full‑model finetuning.

Summary
Limiting LoRA to only Q and V constrains the adapter’s functional family: it can reshape attention inputs and retrieved content but cannot alter the many subsequent linear/nonlinear transforms that instruction tuning needs. The QLoRA paper’s ablations identify adapter coverage (i.e., placing adapters in all linear layers) as the critical factor to close the gap to $16$-bit full finetuning; they do not find adapter rank to be the limiting factor, and they do not present a detailed per‑layer importance ranking beyond the “all layers” vs “Q/V only” comparison.
