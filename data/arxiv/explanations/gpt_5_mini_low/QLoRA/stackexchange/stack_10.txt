\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Does QLoRA dequantize weights on-the-fly for every forward pass and what is the runtime cost?
Question:
Implementation-wise, QLoRA stores weights in NF4 and dequantizes to BF16 for matrix multiplications. Is dequantization performed on-the-fly every forward/backward pass, or are there caches at the block level? How much overhead does double dequantization (first recovering $c_2$ from $c_1$, then weights from $c_2$) add relative to 16-bit inference/training? The paper says there is no runtime degradation vs 16-bit full finetuning—do they have microbenchmarks showing dequant cost is negligible compared to GEMMs at large batch/sequence sizes?
Answer:
Short answer: QLoRA dequantizes on-the-fly when a weight tensor is used (both forward and backward), at block granularity, and the authors report that in their experiments the dequantization cost is negligible compared to the $16$-bit GEMM cost. The paper does not publish a standalone microbenchmark that isolates the double‑dequant cost, but it gives enough information to understand why the overhead is small and how it scales.

What the paper says (implementation-level)
The paper explicitly states that QLoRA has one storage data type (typically $4$-bit $NF4$) and one computation data type ($BF16$). “Whenever a QLoRA weight tensor is used, we dequantize the tensor to BFloat16, and then perform a matrix multiplication in $16$-bit.” They define the operation as

$Y^{BF16} = X^{BF16}\ \text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{FP32}, c_2^{k\text{-}bit}, W^{NF4}) + X^{BF16} L_1^{BF16} L_2^{BF16},$

with doubleDequant doing two dequant stages to recover the BF16 weights from the stored NF4 weights and quantized quantization constants. The quantization is block-wise (they use blocksize $64$ for $W$ and $256$ for $c_2$), so dequantization is naturally performed per block.

Is dequantization done per forward/backward pass or cached?
The paper describes dequantization happening “whenever a QLoRA weight tensor is used” and does not describe or claim any persistent full-$BF16$ cache of reconstructed weights on GPU across layers or iterations. The quantization format is block-wise, and the runtime view is that stored tensors are $NF4$ + quantization constants; those are dequantized to $BF16$ for the compute. So dequantization is performed on-the-fly at use time (per forward and per backward) at block granularity. They do not report an extra caching layer that keeps a full $BF16$ copy permanently on the GPU (that would defeat the memory savings).

Why this is not prohibitive (intuition + technical reason)
Dequantization is essentially an $O(N)$ linear pass over the stored blocks: for each block you (1) recover $c_2$ from its quantized representation (the “quantized quantization constants”), (2) use $c_2$ (and optionally $c_1$) to rescale the $4$-bit entries into $BF16$ values. The heavy linear algebra work (the GEMM) is much more expensive in compute and memory traffic than the arithmetic of rescaling a block. Concretely:

- Dequant cost per element is a few scalar ops (recover scale(s) and one multiply), so the work is memory-bandwidth / element dominated and low arithmetic intensity.
- A GEMM of shape $(\text{batch}\times \text{seq},\ \text{hidden}) \times (\text{hidden},\ \text{out})$ reuses the same weight block many times across rows of $X$. At large batch or long sequence length the cost of the GEMM grows with the number of rows (amortizing the one-time per-block dequant work across many input activations). Thus the relative overhead of dequantization becomes small when $\text{batch}\times\text{sequence}$ is large.

The paper makes this same point qualitatively and uses $BF16$ GEMMs after dequant: “In practice, this means whenever a QLoRA weight tensor is used, we dequantize the tensor to BFloat16, and then perform a matrix multiplication in $16$-bit.” They conclude that they reduce memory a lot “without degrading the runtime or predictive performance compared to a $16$-bit fully finetuned baseline.” That is an empirical claim about end‑to‑end runtime, implying dequant cost didn’t create a measurable slow-down in their finetuning experiments.

Double dequantization cost
Double quantization means there are two dequant steps: first recover $c_2$ from $c_1$ (the quantized quantization constants), then use $c_2$ to dequant the $4$-bit $W$. This adds a tiny extra per‑block cost to reconstruct the scale(s) (one extra read and a small number of ops per block) before the per‑element rescale. Because the second step (the $BF16$ GEMM) dominates overall cost for typical transformer matmuls, the extra cost from the second dequant stage is again small in practice.

What the paper does and does not provide on microbenchmarks
The authors claim “QLoRA reduces the average memory requirements … without degrading the runtime or predictive performance compared to a $16$-bit fully finetuned baseline.” They also state they release CUDA kernels for $4$-bit training. However, they do not present a dedicated microbenchmark in the paper that isolates:

- pure dequant time (doubleDequant only),
- GEMM time versus (dequant + GEMM) for different batch/seq sizes,
- per-layer caching vs no-caching comparisons.

They do report that paged optimizers (a separate feature) “with a batch size of $16$, paged optimizers provide the same training speed as regular optimizers” for a $65$B model on a $48$GB GPU—this is an explicit runtime comparison related to optimizer paging, but it is not a dequant microbenchmark.

Practical takeaway and how to validate locally
From the paper you should assume dequantization is performed on-the-fly at block granularity and is implemented in optimized CUDA kernels that fuse dequant + GEMM where sensible (they open‑source CUDA kernels). The dequant overhead is small in their reported experiments because GEMMs dominate at realistic batch and sequence lengths. If you want to quantify this yourself, measure two timings on your hardware: (1) time for (doubleDequant + GEMM) per layer, and (2) time for GEMM with a pre‑dequantized $BF16$ weight (i.e., keep a $BF16$ cache) for the same shapes. The difference isolates the dequant overhead; vary $\text{batch}\times\text{sequence}$ to see how the amortization behaves.

Summary
QLoRA dequantizes on-the-fly (per use) at block level and does a two-stage dequant when using double quantization. The double dequant adds only a small extra per-block overhead; in the authors’ experiments that overhead is negligible compared to the $BF16$ GEMMs at typical transformer batch/sequence sizes, so they report no runtime degradation vs $16$-bit finetuning. The paper does not include a separated microbenchmark isolating dequant cost, so if you need precise numbers for your hardware/shape you should benchmark (doubleDequant+GEMM) versus GEMM-with-cached-$BF16$.
