\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Does NF4 rescale by standard deviation or by absolute max during quantization?
Question:
I'm reading the QLoRA paper and I'm confused about the normalization step for NF4. In the NF4 paragraph, they say weights are transformed into the range $[-1,1]$ and mention matching the standard deviation of the data type, but the concrete procedure for block-wise quantization earlier uses absolute max rescaling. For NF4 specifically, do they normalize each block by $\text{absmax}$ (as in Eq. 1), or by an estimate of $\sigma$ to match the $N(0,1)$ quantiles? If it is $\text{absmax}$ per block for step (3), how does that remain "information-theoretically optimal" for a normal distribution whose tails vary block-to-block?
Answer:
Short answer: NF4 uses the same block-wise absolute-max rescaling as the rest of the paper ($( \text{Eq.\ 1} )$). In the NF4 description the authors phrase this as “match the standard deviation of the data type,” but concretely step (3) is an absolute-max normalization of each block into the fixed NF$k$ range (they normalize the tensor into $[-1,1]$ per block and then apply the NF4 quantization).

Why the two descriptions aren’t contradictory: NF4 is derived by computing an information‑theoretic optimal $k$-bit code for a standard normal, i.e. you precompute the quantile bin centers $q_i$ from $N(0,1)$. Those $q_i$ are then linearly mapped into the range $[-1,1]$ to form the NF$k$ codebook. When you quantize an actual weight block you must bring that block into the NF$k$ range; the paper does that with the usual blockwise absolute‑max scale $c$ ($( \text{Eq.\ 1} )$). If the block is $X_{\text{block}}$, you compute $c = \max(|X_{\text{block}}|)$ and do $\mathrm{round}(\text{scale\_factor} \cdot X_{\text{block}})$ where the $\text{scale\_factor}$ maps block values into $[-1,1]$. The paper points out that this per‑block absmax rescaling is equivalent to "rescaling the block standard deviation to match the standard deviation of the $k$-bit data type" in expectation: for a block drawn from $N(0,\sigma^2)$, multiplying by $1/\text{absmax}$ is equivalent to dividing by a scale that is (randomly) related to $\sigma$, so after scaling the block roughly matches the NF$k$ distribution used to choose the quantile bins.

Intuition about optimality and block‑to‑block tail variation: the NF4 codebook is information‑theoretically optimal under the assumption that values are zero‑mean normal up to a single scale parameter. If every block were exactly $N(0,\sigma^2)$ with the same $\sigma$, you could use a single global linear scale chosen from $\sigma$ and the NF4 bins would match exactly. In practice blocks have different $\sigma$ and different extreme values (outliers). The practical compromise the paper makes is (a) use blockwise scaling (absmax per block) so each block gets its own linear normalization, and (b) use a reasonably small blocksize (they use 64) to limit how much tail/outlier heterogeneity is pooled into a single scale. That combination gives a fast, simple implementation that approximates the theoretical assumption (normal up to scale) well enough to yield the empirical benefits they report. The asymmetric construction (ensuring an exact discrete zero) is an extra practical tweak they describe.

So: NF4 = (precomputed quantile-based codebook for $N(0,1)$ mapped to $[-1,1]$) + (per-block absolute-max linear normalization of the weights into that range) — the “standard deviation matching” is an interpretive view of that linear normalization, not a separate per‑block sigma estimation step.
