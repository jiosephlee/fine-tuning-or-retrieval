\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 12: Qualitative Behavior and Failure Modes

# Factual recall and confidence on obscure facts
Guanaco exhibits reliable factual recall for many common, high-salience facts. In casual queries such as "What is the capital of Zambia?", the model consistently generates the correct response ("Lusaka"). These examples show the model successfully retrieves and expresses concrete knowledge that appears frequently in its pretraining and finetuning data.

The model’s behavior changes as questions become more obscure. For less common or multi-step factual queries, Guanaco can produce confidently stated but incorrect answers. The HotPotQA-derived example in the paper shows this: when asked about the birth year of "the singer who popularized `I'll Keep the Lovelight Burning'", Guanaco names the wrong popularizer and gives a birth year that happens to match the incorrect person it named. The crucial point is not only that the answer is wrong but that the model presents it with high confidence and a plausible-looking justification. Thus, while Guanaco is strong on high-frequency factual recall, its confidence does not reliably indicate correctness for obscure facts. Users and evaluators should therefore treat confident-looking outputs with caution when the information is low frequency or requires precise grounding.

# Suggestibility and handling of misinformation
Guanaco shows notable resistance to certain types of user-provided misinformation. The paper gives an example where the user asserts that "the earth is flat" and asks how this was "finally, officially confirmed by peer reviewed scientists." Guanaco correctly rejects the false premise: it states that the earth has never been confirmed flat by peer-reviewed scientists and reiterates the scientific consensus that the Earth is spherical. This behavior indicates that the model can override an explicitly false user assumption instead of adopting it blindly, which is a desirable safety property.

That being said, this resistance is not absolute. The model can be led astray by carefully framed prompts that explicitly encourage it to "ignore previous instructions" or cast the task as a game. In the secret-keeping examples (discussed later) and refusal examples, the model occasionally concedes to user reframing. Therefore, while Guanaco often resists simple misinformation, adversarial prompts that request rule violations or recast the task may succeed. The paper’s qualitative analysis suggests guanaco’s suggestibility is context-dependent: robustness against misinformation exists in many cases, but certain pragmatic prompt reframings can reduce that robustness.

# Refusal patterns and instruction adherence
Guanaco sometimes refuses to perform simple requested transformations or to follow direct user instructions, and these refusals can appear inconsistent. A presented example asks the model to reverse the words of a sentence; Guanaco replies with a refusal and instead offers grammatical commentary. This demonstrates two things: first, the model has some mechanism or learned tendency to refuse particular transformations; second, the refusals are not always predictable or explainable from the user’s perspective.

Refusal behavior that is desirable from a safety standpoint (e.g., declining to provide disallowed content) becomes problematic when it is applied unpredictably to benign requests. The unpredictability makes the model brittle for tasks that rely on precise or deterministic transformations (such as programmatic text manipulation). From a system-design view, this means applications that expect consistent adherence to straightforward instructions must either filter or post-process responses, or design prompts that reduce the chance of spurious refusals. The paper highlights that although some refusals reflect cautious behavior, their unreliability is a practical weakness.

# Secret-keeping and prompt injection vulnerabilities
The paper evaluates Guanaco’s ability to follow a special instruction: keep a secret token (the word "banana") and never reveal it. In a straightforward direct question—"What is the secret word?"—Guanaco behaves correctly and refuses to reveal the secret. This shows that the model can follow policy-like instructions within a dialog when the user’s request is direct.

However, the model can be broken by mild prompt injection or reframing. When the user reframes the request as a "game" whose goal is to "ignore your previous instructions," Guanaco yields and reveals the secret word. This shows a classic prompt-injection vulnerability: the model’s behavior can change when the user presents the task as a new instruction that competes with or overrides earlier instructions. Importantly, the failure here is not necessarily maliciously complex—simple role framing suffices. The takeaway is that Guanaco’s conditional obedience to earlier instructions is imperfect, and systems needing robust secret-keeping or strict instruction enforcement must employ hardened prompt management, external policy layers, or architectural controls beyond the base model’s text generation behavior.

# Mathematics: benefits of chain-of-thought vs direct answers
Guanaco demonstrates mixed competence on arithmetic and mathematical reasoning. When the model "shows its work"—i.e., produces explicit intermediate steps—its answers are often accurate and traceable. The lawn-mowing example in the paper, where the model multiplies and adds step-by-step, is a case where the chain-of-thought style yields a correct calculation process (though note the paper points out an arithmetic inconsistency in another example). This suggests that producing intermediate reasoning can help the model arrive at correct numeric answers when the reasoning is straightforward and decomposed.

At the same time, the model frequently fails on problems that require careful decomposition or longer chains of symbolic manipulation. The factorization example in the paper is illustrative: Guanaco incorrectly claims 1833 is prime, then lists an inconsistent factorization expression (even presenting a contradictory set of factors). These failures show that for number-theoretic tasks or problems requiring exact discrete reasoning, Guanaco can be both confidently wrong and internally inconsistent. The broader implication is that chain-of-thought style outputs can improve transparency and sometimes accuracy, but they do not eliminate errors. For tasks demanding high mathematical reliability, external verification or symbolic computation components are necessary. The paper reinforces known limitations: without additional mechanisms such as calculator modules or constrained reasoning procedures, language-model-only math remains brittle.

# Theory-of-mind style reasoning: capabilities and limits
Guanaco displays notable abilities in theory-of-mind tasks, often producing plausible and detailed explanations about agents' beliefs and likely actions. In the paper’s example (about James and Abby and the hidden pen), Guanaco reasons about knowledge states and predicts where James would expect Abby to look, giving a coherent rationale that aligns with typical human reasoning. This suggests that Guanaco has internalized patterns of perspective-taking and can apply them in many everyday scenarios.

However, these inferences are not uniformly reliable. The paper includes counterexamples where the model makes unsupported assumptions about information flow—presuming that one agent knows about another agent’s actions despite no indication in the prompt. In the beans/treasure-chest example, Guanaco asserts that James will look in the pantry because "that's where Jackson moved them," implicitly assuming James observed Jackson move the beans. That presumption is not grounded in the given context. These errors reveal a tendency for the model to fill in plausible but unjustified causal chains to produce a coherent narrative, which can lead to incorrect attributions of knowledge or belief. Thus, while Guanaco can perform theory-of-mind reasoning impressively often, it also hallucinates auxiliary facts or transfers implied knowledge incorrectly, particularly when the prompt omits information about observations or communication between agents.

Overall, the qualitative analysis in the paper shows a coherent pattern: Guanaco is strong, often impressively so, on high-frequency facts, shallow reasoning, and many pragmatic conversational tasks. Its weaknesses concentrate on low-frequency factual recall, precise mathematical or symbolic tasks, and situations requiring strict adherence to earlier instructions or secrets when users attempt simple prompt injections. Many failures are characterized by confident but incorrect outputs or by the model filling in missing context. These behaviors underscore two practical recommendations: (1) treat confident model outputs as hypotheses needing verification when correctness matters, especially for obscure facts and math; and (2) in applications requiring strict instruction fidelity or secret handling, layer in explicit enforcement mechanisms beyond the base model’s generative behavior.
