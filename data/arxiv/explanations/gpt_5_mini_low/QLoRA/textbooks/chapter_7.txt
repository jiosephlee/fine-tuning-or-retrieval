\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 7: Experimental Design and Baselines

# Architectures and scales: RoBERTa, T5, LLaMA (125M to 65B)
The experiments in the QLoRA paper span three major model families and a very wide range of parameter scales to demonstrate that the method is broadly applicable. The three model families used are RoBERTa (encoder-only), T5 (encoder-decoder), and LLaMA (decoder-only). Model sizes range from small research-scale models (roughly 125M parameters) up to the largest publicly available LLaMA models used in the work (65B parameters). This breadth allows the authors to validate both (a) that QLoRA recovers 16-bit finetuning quality on small and medium models where full 16-bit finetuning is tractable, and (b) that QLoRA enables finetuning of very large models (33B and 65B) on single GPUs where conventional full-precision finetuning would be infeasible.

For the smallest models (125M to a few billion parameters), the authors directly compare QLoRA to both full 16-bit fine-tuning and 16-bit LoRA adapter finetuning. For larger models (7B up to 65B), the focus shifts to comparing QLoRA (using 4-bit storage) with 16-bit LoRA baselines or other publicly released instruction-tuned models, since full 16-bit full-model finetuning at those scales would require server-class multi-GPU memory budgets and is not available as a practical baseline in the experiments.

Where relevant, the paper examines multiple checkpoints across the families (for example, LLaMA models at 7B, 13B, 33B, and 65B) to assess how QLoRA’s memory savings and fidelity scale with model size. The intention is to establish that the same quantization + adapter approach applies across encoder, encoder-decoder, and decoder-only architectures.

# Tasks: GLUE, Super-NaturalInstructions, 5-shot MMLU, instruction tuning
The experimental design uses a set of canonical academic tasks and practical instruction-tuning setups to validate QLoRA across both established benchmarks and applied instruction following.

For standard supervised-finetuning tasks, the authors evaluate on GLUE using a RoBERTa-large backbone. GLUE provides a well-understood diagnostic suite for transfer learning and is suitable for small-to-medium model comparisons where full finetuning is feasible.

For instruction-following and multitask transfer, the Super-NaturalInstructions (abbreviated as TKInstruct in the paper) setup with T5 models is used. Super-NaturalInstructions contains a wide variety of instruction-style input-output pairs and tests how well finetuning teaches a model to follow diverse instructions.

To test few-shot general language understanding and to measure performance at scale, the authors evaluate on the Massively Multitask Language Understanding benchmark (MMLU). They use the 5-shot evaluation protocol to compare models after instruction finetuning. MMLU is treated as a multiple-choice accuracy test across 57 tasks, offering a standard way to measure knowledge and reasoning ability across many domains.

Finally, the paper performs extensive instruction-tuning experiments. These include finetuning on eight diverse instruction datasets (e.g., OASST1, HH-RLHF, Alpaca, FLAN v2, Chip2, Longform, Self-Instruct, Unnatural-Instructions). Instruction tuning evaluations are both quantitative (MMLU, Vicuna prompts, OASST1 validation) and qualitative. For chatbot-style behavior, the authors evaluate generative outputs using curated prompt sets (Vicuna prompts and OASST1 user messages) and human/GPT-4 pairwise comparison tournaments to measure dialogue quality.

Across these tasks the experimental aim is twofold: to determine whether QLoRA recovers full precision performance on standard benchmarks, and to study practical instruction-following performance (including chatbots) at scales that are otherwise difficult to explore.

# Data types compared: BF16/FP16, 8-bit, FP4, NF4 (with/without DQ)
A central axis of the experiments contrasts storage and computation data types. The main data types considered are:

- 16-bit BrainFloat (BF16, used as the computation data type in QLoRA; the paper also references FP16 baselines where applicable).
- 8-bit quantized representations (used for some adapter baselines and in double-quantization of quantization constants).
- 4-bit floating/integer types tested in prior quantization work (FP4, Int4).
- 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4), the new k-bit NormalFloat data type introduced in the paper, designed to be information-theoretically optimal for zero-mean normally distributed weights.

The experimental comparisons are designed to show two things: first, that NF4 is empirically superior to other 4-bit data types (FP4 and Int4) for post-quantization accuracy and perplexity across multiple pretrained checkpoints; and second, that QLoRA using NF4 (often combined with Double Quantization) recovers the performance of BF16 baselines after adapter finetuning.

Double Quantization (DQ) is explicitly evaluated as a memory-reduction technique: the per-block quantization constants produced by the first quantization stage (stored as 32-bit floats) are themselves quantized (to 8-bit floats in the paper) with a larger blocksize. In practice the paper uses NF4 for the stored weights and FP8 for the second-level quantization constants, with blocksize choices tuned for precision and memory (blocksize 64 for weight blocks, blocksize 256 for quantized constants). The comparisons include NF4 with and without Double Quantization to demonstrate the memory reduction advantages of DQ without detectable performance loss.

# Hyperparameter searches for fair full-finetuning and LoRA baselines
To ensure a fair comparison, the authors take pains to tune hyperparameters for both full-model finetuning baselines and LoRA adapter baselines. Two important experimental observations shape the hyperparameter strategy:

1. Default LoRA placements and sizes are insufficient to match full finetuning performance for large base models. The paper finds that applying LoRA only to the query and value attention projection matrices, a common prior practice, does not replicate full finetuning quality. Instead, to match full finetuning they apply adapters more broadly—LoRA at all linear layers throughout the transformer blocks. The number of adapters (i.e., how many layers are instrumented with LoRA) is a critical hyperparameter; expanding adapters to more layers substantially improves performance, whereas the projection rank \(r\) is observed to be less sensitive within reasonable ranges.

2. Default full-finetuning hyperparameters are under-tuned. For robust 16-bit baselines, the authors perform searches over learning rates and batch sizes to locate strong operating points. Concretely, for the 7B LLaMA experiments on Alpaca, they search learning rates in the range \(1\times 10^{-6}\) to \(5\times 10^{-5}\) and batch sizes between 8 and 128. These ranges are used to identify strong full-finetuning baselines to compare against both 16-bit and 4-bit adapter finetuning methods.

For scaling to larger models, the authors reuse hyperparameters found at smaller scales with only modest adjustments: for the 33B and 65B LLaMA runs they halve the learning rate and double the batch size compared to 7B settings. They report that learning rate and batch size required the most tuning across scales, while other LoRA hyperparameters generalized well.

By documenting and searching over these hyperparameters, the experiments aim to avoid attributing wins to implementation details or poor baselines. The finding that LoRA must be applied more widely than commonly practiced is an important experimental control that allows QLoRA to be judged against well-tuned alternatives.

# Why paged optimizers are not benchmarked in isolation
Paged Optimizers are a practical engineering component used to prevent out-of-memory (OOM) spikes during training when activation gradient checkpointing and long sequences temporarily exceed GPU memory. The technique leverages NVIDIA Unified Memory so that optimizer states can be allocated in paged memory and spilled to host RAM when the GPU runs out of room; they are faultlessly paged back when needed for the optimizer update.

The paper does not present isolated benchmarks for Paged Optimizers for two reasons stated by the authors. First, the paging behavior only triggers under infrequent specific circumstances (e.g., processing a minibatch with a long sequence length that creates a transient spike). Second, in their runtime analysis for 65B models on a 48GB GPU with batch size 16 they report that paged optimizers provide the same end-to-end training speed as regular optimizers; however this effect depends on the workload and paging frequency. Because the impact is sporadic and highly dependent on dataset sequence-length distributions and checkpointing patterns, the authors chose not to present a comprehensive standalone benchmark of paged optimizers. Instead, paged optimizers are included as an enabling engineering ingredient: they make 33B and 65B single-GPU QLoRA finetuning feasible and robust, but their exact runtime tradeoffs are context dependent and left to future work to characterize in detail.

# Evaluation metrics and reporting conventions
The paper uses a mix of established task metrics and careful conventions for reporting generative/chatbot evaluations. The primary evaluation metrics and conventions are:

- MMLU 5-shot accuracy: For knowledge and reasoning evaluation, models are measured on the standard 5-shot multiple-choice MMLU benchmark. Accuracy is reported as the percentage of correct choices aggregated across task subsets.

- GLUE and Super-NaturalInstructions metrics: For supervised finetuning experiments on GLUE and Super-NaturalInstructions, the standard task-specific metrics are used (e.g., GLUE scores aggregated per dataset conventions).

- Perplexity and zero-shot task performance after post-quantization: When evaluating quantization methods themselves, the authors measure post-quantization perplexity and zero-shot accuracy on language modeling and classification-style tasks across different checkpoints and sizes.

- Vicuna and OA generative benchmarks: For chatbot-style generative evaluations the paper uses two curated prompt sets: the 80 Vicuna prompts and the roughly 953 user queries derived from the OASST1 validation set (including previous conversation context when appropriate). Generation uses nucleus sampling with \(p=0.9\) and temperature \(=0.7\) in all comparisons to standardize stochasticity.

- GPT-4 automated scores relative to ChatGPT: Following the protocol from Vicuna, GPT-4 is used to assign a score out of 10 to both ChatGPT (as a reference) and a model under test for a given prompt + response pair. The model’s score is reported as a percentage of ChatGPT’s score. To address a discovered ordering bias in GPT-4 (a tendency to give higher scores to the response shown earlier), the paper reports the mean score across both ordering permutations of the two responses.

- Pairwise comparisons and three-way labeling: For head-to-head system comparisons, the authors reduce evaluation to a three-class choice (model A wins / model B wins / tie). GPT-4 is prompted to pick the better response or declare a tie. Human annotators are also used for pairwise judgments following the same scheme. This simplifies aggregation into tournament-style rankings.

- Elo rating aggregation: Pairwise judgments (from humans and from GPT-4) are aggregated into Elo scores. The Elo system is initialized with a score of 1000, with update parameter \(K=32\). To mitigate sampling and ordering noise, the authors repeat the Elo computation 10,000 times with different random seeds and report aggregated Elo rankings. Elo scores translate into expected win rates between systems; this relative ranking is preferred by the authors to absolute rubric-based scores because it avoids some of the grounding ambiguities of numeric scales.

- Inter-annotator agreement reporting: To communicate reliability, the paper reports measures of agreement such as Fleiss’ \(\kappa\) and reports correlations between human and GPT-4 system-level rankings (Kendall’s \(\tau\) and Spearman \(r\)). For example, they find moderate system-level agreement between humans and GPT-4 (\(\tau=0.43\), \(r=0.55\)), but weaker sample-level agreement (Fleiss’ \(\kappa=0.25\)), indicating the evaluations are useful but imperfect and that model-based evaluation has biases to be considered.

- Additional qualitative analysis: Beyond scalar metrics, the authors present qualitative failure and success cases (e.g., factual errors, refusal behavior, math mistakes) to contextualize strengths and weaknesses that scalar metrics do not capture.

These conventions collectively aim to provide both rigorous task-based comparisons where ground truth exists (MMLU, GLUE) and realistic generative comparisons where human preferences matter (Vicuna, OA), while controlling for ordering biases and aggregating pairwise judgments in a statistically robust manner via Elo.

# Summary of experimental design choices
The experimental design intentionally spans architectures, model scales, tasks, and quantization choices to test the two central claims: (1) NF4-based 4-bit storage combined with adapter finetuning recovers 16-bit finetuning performance, and (2) QLoRA enables practical, high-fidelity finetuning of very large models (33B–65B) on single GPUs.

Key practical choices that underpin reproducibility are: using BF16 as the computation dtype in QLoRA, NF4 as the 4-bit storage dtype (with blocksize 64 for weights), Double Quantization of quantization constants (FP8 with blocksize 256), broadly-applied LoRA adapters (adapters at essentially all linear layers), explicit hyperparameter searches for 16-bit baselines (learning rates \(1\times10^{-6}\) to \(5\times10^{-5}\), batch sizes 8–128), and careful evaluation protocols (nucleus sampling with \(p=0.9\), temperature 0.7; averaging GPT-4 scores across prompt order; aggregating pairwise judgments via Elo with repeated sampling). Paged Optimizers are integrated as an enabling engineering mechanism but are not isolatedly benchmarked because their performance effects are workload-dependent and intermittent.

Taken together, these experimental design choices and baselines aim to ensure that improvements attributed to QLoRA arise from its quantization + adapter methodology, rather than from under-tuned baselines, inconsistent evaluation, or dataset selection.
