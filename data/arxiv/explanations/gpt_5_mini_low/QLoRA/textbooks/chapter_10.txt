\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 10: Evaluating LLMs as Chatbots: Protocols and Pitfalls

# MMLU 5-shot: scope and limitations
The Massively Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) benchmark is a multi-domain multiple-choice evaluation designed to probe a language model's knowledge across a broad range of subjects (e.g., mathematics, history, law, computer science). In the QLoRA study it is used as a complementary, well-established measure of language understanding after instruction finetuning. The typical evaluation mode reported in the paper is 5-shot accuracy: for each MMLU question, the model is provided with five in-context examples (shots) followed by the test question, and the model's chosen answer is compared to the ground truth to compute accuracy. Aggregating over the full suite of tasks yields a single scalar 5-shot accuracy score that summarizes performance on MMLU.

MMLU is useful because it covers a wide range of domain knowledge and because its multiple-choice format makes automated scoring straightforward. However, the paper emphasizes limitations of relying solely on MMLU when the research goal is to evaluate chatbot behavior. First, strong MMLU performance does not imply strong generative or conversational performance: QLoRA experiments show that finetuning data choice matters, and a model that does well on MMLU (for example, when finetuned on FLAN v2) can perform worse on human-style chatbot benchmarks like Vicuna or OA. Second, MMLU evaluates a specific type of knowledge retrieval and reasoning in a multiple-choice format; it does not capture multi-turn conversational dynamics, response quality, refusal behavior, hallucination tendencies, or alignment with instruction-following preferences. Thus MMLU should be seen as a complementary, task-specific probe rather than a comprehensive measure of chatbot quality.

Because MMLU is sensitivity-limited to the similarity between finetuning data and the benchmark distribution, the paper uses it primarily to measure knowledge retention and domain-general understanding after instruction finetuning, while reserving separate generative evaluations for conversational capabilities. In summary, MMLU 5-shot provides a reproducible quantitative measure of knowledge-like abilities, but evaluating chatbots requires additional generative and human-centered evaluations to capture interactional qualities and instruction-following behavior.

# Generative benchmarks: Vicuna prompts and OASST1 validation (OA)
To evaluate generative conversational quality, the paper uses two curated prompt collections: the Vicuna prompts and the Open Assistant (OASST1) validation queries. These serve different but related evaluation purposes.

The Vicuna prompt set is a small hand-curated collection of 80 prompts spanning diverse categories. It was originally used in prior work for relative scoring against ChatGPT. In QLoRA the Vicuna prompts are used without modification to produce model responses under a fixed decoding policy (nucleus sampling with $p=0.9$ and temperature $0.7$). The small size and curation make Vicuna convenient for comparative evaluation across many models, but its limited scale creates large uncertainty in absolute comparisons: the paper reports wide confidence intervals for relative scores and notes that many models overlap in performance. Thus Vicuna is useful for compact comparisons but not as a definitive large-sample benchmark.

The OASST1 validation dataset (called OA in the paper) is a much larger, multilingual collection of crowd-sourced multi-turn dialogs from the Open Assistant project. For OA evaluation the authors select all user messages in the validation split as queries, and include the conversation history (previous turns) as part of the prompt. This produces 953 unique user queries which provide a broader, multi-turn test set more representative of realistic assistant interactions. OA is particularly useful when evaluating models finetuned on OASST1 variants because it overlaps in style and domain, but the paper also checks for prompt overlap and finds no direct prompt leakage after fuzzy matching.

Using both datasets provides a balance: Vicuna for standard comparative reporting and OA for a larger, multi-turn evaluation that better stresses conversation handling. However, the paper cautions that the two benchmarks favor different models depending on their finetuning data; e.g., Vicuna tends to favor some open-source models while OA may favor ChatGPT, illustrating that dataset selection strongly influences apparent ranking.

# Automated evaluation with GPT-4: scoring vs pairwise comparisons
The paper uses GPT-4 as an automated judge in two complementary evaluation modes: absolute scoring against a reference (ChatGPT) and pairwise head-to-head comparisons.

In the scoring procedure adapted from prior work, GPT-4 is presented with a query and two responses (ChatGPT's and the target model's). It is prompted to assign a numerical score out of ten to each response and to provide an explanation. The model-level performance is then reported as the target model's mean score expressed as a percentage of ChatGPT's mean score; this produces a relative score that can be above or below 100\% depending on absolute scores. The authors observed an important artifact: a significant ordering effect where GPT-4 tends to assign higher scores to the response appearing earlier in the prompt. To mitigate this bias they report mean scores computed over both presentation orders. This mode provides a scalar relative measure but is sensitive to ordering biases and to GPT-4's internal preferences.

To avoid some problems of absolute scoring and to better align with tournament-style evaluation, the authors also use GPT-4 to perform pairwise comparisons. In this setup, GPT-4 is shown a prompt and two responses (from two systems) and asked to pick the better response or declare a tie, providing a brief rationale. These three-way outcomes (win/tie/loss) are then used as match results for Elo rating. Pairwise comparisons reduce dependence on absolute score calibration and are more naturally aggregated via tournament algorithms. The paper uses GPT-4 comparisons across all permutations of model pairs on both Vicuna and OA benchmarks.

Overall, GPT-4 judgments are a practical, cheaper alternative to large-scale human annotation and correlate moderately with human rankings at the system level, but they exhibit biases (ordering effects, self-preference) and sample-level disagreements with humans. The paper treats GPT-4 as a useful but imperfect automated evaluator and recommends steps (e.g., randomizing order and averaging) to mitigate known artifacts.

# Human evaluation on AMT: replication of both protocols
To validate GPT-4-based evaluations and to provide a human-ground-truth signal, the study runs human annotation on Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT) using protocols that mirror the automated evaluations. Two separate human annotation tasks are described.

First, humans compare a model's response against ChatGPT using the same scoring style: annotators score each response (or compare them directly) and provide judgments that can be converted into relative performance. The paper reports using two annotators per example for the ChatGPT comparison task. Second, for pairwise comparisons among multiple systems (the tournament setup), the authors acquire three human judgments per pairwise comparison. These replicate the GPT-4 pairwise protocol but provide a human consensus that can be used to compute Elo ratings.

Human evaluation serves two purposes in the paper: (1) to calibrate and validate GPT-4 automated comparisons, and (2) to compute Elo ratings grounded in human preference data. The paper reports moderate agreement among human annotators (system-level Fleiss $\kappa=0.42$ and example-level Fleiss $\kappa$ values that vary depending on the task), indicating that even human judgments contain subjectivity and noise. These results motivate using multiple annotators and aggregation methods (majority vote or Elo aggregation) rather than single annotations.

Overall, performing human evaluation on AMT in parallel to GPT-4 evaluations allows the authors to characterize where automated judgments match human preferences and where they diverge, providing a richer understanding of model quality and the reliability of automated proxies.

# Elo tournaments: setup, repeated sampling, and interpretation
The paper adopts tournament-style evaluation to aggregate pairwise judgments into a global ranking using the Elo rating system. Elo is a well-known method that converts match outcomes into skill ratings where the expected score versus an opponent can be mapped to an Elo difference. The key steps in the paper's Elo setup are as follows.

Matches: Each match is a pairwise comparison on a single prompt between two models. The outcome is win/loss/tie, determined either by human annotators (majority vote) or by GPT-4. Aggregation: For Elo computation the authors start all models at a base rating of 1000 and use a K-factor of 32. The Elo update rule adjusts ratings after each match proportional to the difference between the observed outcome and the expected outcome. Repeated sampling: To reduce sensitivity to match order and sampling variability, the authors repeat the Elo aggregation procedure 10,000 times with different random seeds and random match orderings. This produces more stable mean Elo estimates and helps account for stochasticity in sampling comparisons from the larger set of pairwise judgments.

Interpretation: Elo ratings provide an interpretable scale of relative performance: differences in Elo map to expected win probabilities (e.g., an Elo difference corresponds to a specific expected win rate). The paper reports Elo rankings for models on Vicuna and OA benchmarks separately, using both human and GPT-4 match outcomes. The Elo-based ranking reduces dependence on absolute scales and helps compare many systems in a single aggregated metric.

Caveats: Elo assumes transitivity and consistent pairwise evaluation behavior, assumptions that are imperfect for subjective language tasks. The repeated-sampling protocol helps mitigate variability, but Elo should be interpreted as a relative ranking modality rather than a definitive measure of absolute quality. Because pairwise judgments themselves can be noisy or biased (see next section), Elo rankings reflect the aggregation of those judgments and are sensitive to their properties.

# Observed biases and order effects; agreement metrics and caveats
The paper documents several empirical phenomena that affect both automated and human evaluations and that must be accounted for when interpreting results.

Order effects: When using GPT-4 as the rater, there is a significant ordering bias: GPT-4 tends to give higher scores to the response appearing earlier in the prompt. The authors control for this by averaging scores across both presentation orders; failing to randomize and average can produce systematic advantages for one model. Humans also can be influenced by presentation order, though the paper highlights GPT-4's pronounced ordering effect specifically.

Self-preference and scale calibration: In comparisons, GPT-4 sometimes assigns systematically higher scores to its own outputs relative to human ratings. For example, GPT-4's Elo for its own outputs can be substantially higher than the human-derived Elo, indicating a self-favoring tendency or differences in calibration between human and model evaluators. This affects absolute score interpretation: automated scores may overestimate the quality of some systems compared to humans.

Agreement metrics: The study computes several inter-annotator and inter-evaluator agreement statistics to quantify consistency. System-level Kendall Tau ($\tau=0.43$) and Spearman rank correlation ($r=0.55$) between GPT-4 and human Elo rankings indicate moderate agreement, but not full concordance. Example-level agreement between GPT-4 and human majority votes is weaker (Fleiss $\kappa=0.25$), signaling that while high-level rankings align somewhat, per-example judgments frequently differ. Human annotator agreement is moderate (reported Fleiss $\kappa=0.42$ in some tasks), showing substantial subjectivity in the task itself.

Other biases and limitations: The paper notes that benchmark choice interacts with finetuning data. A dataset that closely matches a finetuning distribution (e.g., FLAN v2 for MMLU) will favor models tuned on that dataset, while a chat-focused dataset (e.g., OASST1) will favor models trained on conversational data. This dataset-model interaction undermines claims of universal superiority and highlights that benchmark selection effectively steers evaluation outcomes.

Implications: Because both GPT-4 and humans show disagreement and biases, the authors recommend a mixed evaluation strategy: use MMLU for knowledge probing, use curated generative benchmarks (Vicuna and OA) for conversational testing, obtain both GPT-4 automated judgments and human annotations (with randomization to control order effects), and aggregate pairwise comparisons via Elo with repeated sampling. This multipronged approach reduces dependence on any single noisy or biased instrument and provides a more robust picture of chatbot performance. Nonetheless, all aggregated metrics should be interpreted with the caveat that subjective preferences, dataset suitability, and evaluator biases materially influence results.
