\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 11: Guanaco: Results and Analysis

# Vicuna relative scores: Guanaco 65B at 99.3% of ChatGPT
The Guanaco family of models, produced by applying QLoRA finetuning to large LLaMA base models, achieves competitive results on the Vicuna benchmark when measured relative to ChatGPT. The single best model in our suite, Guanaco 65B, attains 99.3% of ChatGPT’s performance on the Vicuna prompts under the automated relative-scoring protocol used in the study. This relative-percentage metric is computed by asking GPT-4 to score ChatGPT’s response and a model’s response on a 0–10 scale for each prompt, then expressing the model’s total score as a percentage of ChatGPT’s total score. Guanaco 65B’s score near 100% indicates that, by this automated metric, its responses are very close to ChatGPT’s outputs on the Vicuna prompt set.

Two practical caveats accompany this headline number. First, the paper reports wide confidence intervals on absolute and relative Vicuna scores; many models overlap within those intervals, so small percentage differences should be interpreted cautiously. Second, the authors observed ordering effects in GPT-4 scoring (a bias toward the response appearing earlier in the prompt); to mitigate this they recommend averaging over both presentation orders. Because of these concerns about absolute-scale variability and order bias, the paper argues for complementary, pairwise evaluation methods (Elo-style tournament comparisons) as a more robust means to compare chatbots.

# Memory/runtime benefits: 33B at ~21GB vs 13B full models
A key practical advantage of QLoRA-finetuned Guanaco models is the reduced memory footprint during inference and the dramatically reduced training resource requirements compared to full 16-bit finetuning. The paper highlights that a Guanaco 33B model stored and served at 4-bit precision requires roughly 21 GB of memory, whereas some previously released full-precision 13B models require more memory (an example reported is a 26 GB Alpaca model). This represents a meaningful win for deployment and for researchers: larger models (more parameters) but quantized (4-bit) can be more memory-efficient than smaller full-precision models, enabling higher-capacity models to run on the same or less memory.

On the training side, QLoRA reduces the memory required to finetune very large models so that a 65B-parameter model can be finetuned on a single 48 GB GPU (using 4-bit storage, Double Quantization, NF4, LoRA adapters and paged optimizers). The paper reports that Guanaco 33B can be trained in under 12 hours on a single 24 GB consumer GPU and Guanaco 65B in roughly 24 hours on a single professional GPU—timelines that are orders of magnitude cheaper than full 16-bit fine-tuning of such models and that enable much broader experimentation and iteration.

# Elo rankings: Guanaco 33B/65B vs GPT-4, ChatGPT, Vicuna, OA
To compare chatbots more robustly, the authors run pairwise head-to-head comparisons and aggregate match outcomes using Elo ratings. Matches are produced by sampling prompts and comparing model responses pairwise; winners are judged by either human annotators or GPT-4. Elo provides a relative ranking that is less sensitive to the grounding problems of absolute rating scales.

Key findings reported in the study include:
- Guanaco 33B and Guanaco 65B are among the top open-source models and outperform previously released open models on the Vicuna and OA benchmarks, ranking below GPT-4 but competitive with ChatGPT. In human-annotator based Elo comparisons, Guanaco 33B and 65B show strong placements—only GPT-4 is clearly above them in the reported tournament outcomes.
- When comparing against GPT-4 directly in human-annotated pairwise comparisons, Guanaco 33B and 65B have an expected win probability of about 30% versus GPT-4. That is, in head-to-head human judgments, these Guanaco models win roughly 30% of matches against GPT-4, indicating meaningful but not parity-level competitiveness.
- The authors observe a systematic discrepancy between GPT-4 and human annotators in some settings. For example, GPT-4 gives a higher self-consistency boost to its own outputs: GPT-4’s Elo on the evaluated set is reported as 1348 while human annotators place GPT-4’s Elo at 1176, a substantial gap that implies GPT-4 favors its own outputs when serving as the judge.
- System-level correlation statistics reported in the paper show partial agreement between GPT-4 and human judgments: Kendall’s Tau of \tau = 0.43 and Spearman rank correlation r = 0.55 across systems. At the example level, agreement between GPT-4 and the human majority vote is weaker (Fleiss’ \kappa = 0.25), indicating the two judges sometimes disagree on individual prompt outcomes even if system-level rankings are moderately aligned.

Because of these differences, the paper emphasizes that combining human and automated judgments and reporting Elo rankings reduces reliance on absolute scores and offers a more stable way to compare systems.

# Benchmark orthogonality: MMLU vs chatbot quality
The study highlights a crucial empirical observation: performance on widely used academic benchmarks (e.g., MMLU) does not necessarily predict chatbot quality as evaluated in conversational benchmarks (Vicuna, OA). In particular, models fine-tuned or evaluated on datasets similar to MMLU can achieve high MMLU scores but perform poorly on the Vicuna chatbot benchmark; conversely, models tuned on chat-style data can do well on Vicuna but not necessarily on MMLU.

Concretely, the authors find that finetuning on FLAN v2 leads to particularly strong MMLU performance but relatively weak Vicuna chatbot performance. By contrast, finetuning on chat-oriented datasets like OASST1 yields stronger performance on conversational benchmarks while not necessarily maximizing MMLU scores. This indicates an orthogonality between (a) Massive Multitask Language Understanding style multiple-choice benchmarks and (b) open-ended conversational quality: the finetuning data distribution matters a great deal for which downstream metrics improve.

This observation has two implications. First, the choice of finetuning data must be aligned with the intended downstream application (classroom/exam-style reasoning versus conversational assistant behavior). Second, benchmark designers and researchers should be careful about using a single benchmark (or small set of benchmarks) as a proxy for general model quality; different benchmarks probe different capabilities and success on one does not guarantee success on another.

# Implications of dataset choice (e.g., FLAN v2 vs OASST1) on outcomes
The paper provides an explicit comparison of several instruction-following datasets and reports that dataset quality and suitability often trump sheer dataset size. Two illustrative examples are emphasized:

- OASST1 (Open Assistant): a relatively small, high-quality, crowd-sourced dataset (on the order of ~9k samples in certain uses described) that produced the best-performing Guanaco models on conversational benchmarks. The top Guanaco models finetuned on variants of OASST1 become state-of-the-art among open-source chatbots in the authors’ evaluations.
- FLAN v2: a much larger dataset when subsampled for some experiments (e.g., the paper mentions a 450k-sample subsample), which yields strong performance on MMLU but comparatively poor conversational performance on Vicuna/OA.

The central message is that a compact, high-quality dataset that matches the target task distribution (instructions and chat transcripts for a conversational assistant) can outperform much larger but less well-aligned corpora. The paper also stresses that how conversations are represented and which portions are used for finetuning (e.g., whether only responses or the entire turn history is used) affects outcomes, so preprocessing choices also matter.

# Open-source training data vs proprietary data considerations
The study explicitly notes that Guanaco’s top-performing model (Guanaco 65B) was trained on OASST1, a dataset whose collection guidelines explicitly forbid use of outputs from GPT-family models. Thus, the best Guanaco model in this study is notable because it is not trained on proprietary model outputs. The authors contrast this with other high-performing models trained on proprietary or mixed data sources and make two related points:

1. Accessibility and reproducibility: Because Guanaco uses non-proprietary training data (at least for its top model), it illustrates that state-of-the-art conversational performance can be achieved without access to proprietary teacher-model distillations. This is important for reproducibility and for enabling independent researchers to study and iterate on instruction finetuning.

2. Performance gaps remain for purely open-source data in some comparisons: the next-best performing model that is trained solely on open-source data in the authors’ comparisons was an Anthropic HH-RLHF-derived model, but it scored substantially lower (roughly 30 percentage points lower on Vicuna relative scoring) than Guanaco 65B. This suggests that both dataset curation and the training procedure interact strongly with model scale and architecture to determine final performance.

The authors emphasize that QLoRA makes it feasible for smaller teams and research groups to train competitive chatbots on open data: by drastically reducing memory and compute costs, more groups can experiment with different datasets, carefully curate open-source corpora, and probe how dataset choice affects behavior—without relying on proprietary distillation data.

# Synthesis and practical takeaways
Guanaco, produced with QLoRA finetuning, demonstrates that 4-bit quantized finetuning with LoRA adapters can yield models that are competitive with large proprietary chatbots on conversational benchmarks, while requiring far less memory and compute to train and to serve. The experimental results point to several practical recommendations:

- Use task-matched, high-quality instruction/chat data where possible. A smaller but well-aligned dataset (e.g., OASST1 for chat) can outperform larger but less relevant corpora.
- Prefer pairwise, Elo-style evaluations for chatbot comparisons, and complement automated judges (GPT-4) with human annotations because automated judges show ordering biases and can disagree with humans on specific examples.
- Leverage QLoRA to explore larger model sizes at lower precision: increasing parameter count while lowering storage precision can yield better practical performance for a given resource envelope than smaller full-precision models.
- For open science, carefully curated open datasets plus efficient finetuning methods like QLoRA enable creation of competitive chatbots without proprietary training data, increasing transparency and reproducibility.

In short, Guanaco provides a strong empirical demonstration that resource-efficient finetuning can produce state-of-the-art open chatbot models when combined with careful dataset selection and robust evaluation methodologies.
