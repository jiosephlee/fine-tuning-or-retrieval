\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 9: Instruction Tuning at Scale with QLoRA

# Datasets: OASST1, HH-RLHF, Alpaca, Self-instruct, Unnatural Instructions, FLAN v2, Chip2, Longform

A central part of instruction tuning experiments with QLoRA is the choice of datasets used to teach a pretrained LLM how to follow human-style instructions. In the QLoRA study the authors assemble eight diverse instruction-following datasets that capture different collection modalities, licenses, sizes, and intended behaviors. These datasets are: OASST1, HH-RLHF, Alpaca, Self-instruct, Unnatural Instructions, FLAN v2, Chip2, and Longform.

OASST1 is a crowd-sourced multi-turn dialogue collection intended for assistant-style behavior and was curated to be usable for RLHF training. HH-RLHF is another human-judged dataset that arises from human preference data (the Anthropic-style helpfulness/harmfulness collections). Alpaca, Self-instruct, and Unnatural Instructions are examples of model-distillation and synthetic instruction collections where outputs are generated or refined using another model (often an instruction-tuned model). FLAN v2 is an aggregated instruction-tuning corpus designed to cover many task formats and is relatively large. Chip2 (also referred to as OIG/Chip2 in the paper) and Longform are hybrid or specialized collections: Chip2 is a large corpus aggregation with permissive licensing goals while Longform focuses on longer-form generation tasks.

These datasets differ not only in size but in the nature of their instructions: some are single-turn question→answer pairs, others are multi-turn dialogues, some are distilled from model outputs (which may encode model biases), and some consist of human-created preference data. The QLoRA experiments intentionally include all of these varieties to study how dataset type and quality influence final chatbot behavior when applied at large scales enabled by 4-bit finetuning.

# Supervised cross-entropy finetuning only (no RLHF)

A deliberate design choice in the QLoRA experiments is to use supervised learning with cross-entropy loss exclusively during instruction finetuning, rather than incorporating reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) or other preference optimization procedures. For all finetuning runs reported in the paper (including those on OASST1 and HH-RLHF), the training objective is the standard teacher-forcing cross-entropy on token sequences: the model is trained to maximize the likelihood of the human-provided target continuation given the instruction prompt.

There are two practical motivations for this choice. First, using supervised cross-entropy simplifies the experimental setting and isolates the question of whether quantized 4-bit finetuning with LoRA adapters can recover or exceed the behavior produced by full-precision finetuning. Second, it allows a controlled comparison across datasets without introducing the additional complexity and instability of RLHF pipelines. The authors point out that, despite relying only on supervised learning, their best QLoRA-trained models (the Guanaco family) achieve competitive chatbot performance: e.g., Guanaco-65B obtains near parity with ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark.

This supervised-only setup also highlights that high quality instruction data alone, when applied with effective low-precision finetuning, can produce strong conversational agents. It leaves open the question—explicitly noted by the authors—of how RLHF might further change model behavior and whether QLoRA would retain its efficiency advantages in that setting.

# Labeling choices for multi-response datasets

Several instruction datasets in the study contain multiple candidate responses or multi-turn trees of responses (for example, OASST1 and HH-RLHF can include several alternative replies and branching conversations). When training with cross-entropy loss, the choice of which target to present as the supervised label matters because the model will learn to reproduce the supplied response pattern.

The QLoRA work adopts a pragmatic policy for such datasets: when multiple responses are available, they select the "top" response at every level of the conversation tree and train on the full selected conversation, including instructions. Concretely, this means for a multi-turn conversation where a set of candidate responses or continuations exists, the authors choose the single best response per turn (as determined by dataset structure or provided rankings) and concatenate those turns into a single supervised example. This preserves the multi-turn dialogue structure and provides the model with coherent assistant behavior across the conversation, rather than training on multiple alternative one-turn continuations that could introduce conflicting supervision.

For datasets where the distinction between instruction and response is clear, the authors finetune only on the response text in their primary ablations. This reduces confusion in supervision where instructions should remain as prompt context rather than be memorized as part of the target. These labeling choices are important because they directly influence the style and tendencies the model learns: training on top responses and entire dialogue trees biases the model to follow the most preferred assistant pattern consistently and to produce multi-turn behavior coherent with human demonstrations.

# Hyperparameter transfer across 7B → 33B → 65B (learning rate and batch size scaling)

A key practical advantage of QLoRA is that it enables experiments across much larger model scales on single GPUs. That capability allows the authors to investigate how finetuning hyperparameters transfer across model sizes. Their central observation is that most LoRA hyperparameters (for example, number of adapters per layer and adapter rank) and many training settings found to work well at the 7B scale generalize to 33B and 65B models; only two training hyperparameters required adjustment as scale increases: learning rate and batch size.

Specifically, the authors describe a simple scaling rule in their experiments: when moving from 7B to 33B and 65B LLaMA models they halve the learning rate while doubling the batch size. This adjustment is motivated by the practicalities of stable optimization at larger scales and is the only systematic change required to obtain good performance across scales in their runs. All other hyperparameters tuned at 7B—such as number of LoRA adapters per layer and number of training epochs—generalized to 33B and 65B without retuning.

This hyperparameter transferability is important because it reduces the compute and researcher effort required to scale up: one can find a robust configuration at the 7B scale and apply straightforward scale-aware adjustments for learning rate and batch size when training much larger models under QLoRA. It also enables efficient exploration of dataset and modeling choices at scales that were previously infeasible to run on single consumer-class GPUs.

# Baselines: Vicuna, Open Assistant, GPT-4, GPT-3.5-turbo, Bard

To contextualize the effectiveness of QLoRA-trained models, the authors compare their models to a range of both open and closed-source baselines. Relevant baselines in the study include:

- Vicuna: an open-source chatbot that distills behavior from ShareGPT conversations; often referenced for high-quality open-source instruction tuning at 13B scale.
- Open Assistant: an LLaMA 33B model finetuned with RLHF on the OASST1 dataset; used as an open-source point of comparison for RLHF-style assistant behavior.
- GPT-4: the strongest commercial comparison in the paper, serving as an upper reference for chatbot performance in several evaluations.
- GPT-3.5-turbo (ChatGPT): the original ChatGPT model used as a reference scoring target in the Vicuna-style automated ratings; many evaluations are reported relative to ChatGPT’s score.
- Bard: another commercial comparison included in pairwise and tournament evaluations.

Evaluations use a mixture of automatic model-based judgment (notably GPT-4 rating outputs) and human annotation (via Amazon Mechanical Turk). The authors report multiple metrics: percentage of ChatGPT score on the Vicuna prompt set, and Elo ratings from pairwise comparisons aggregated across many matches. These diverse baselines allow both direct comparisons to prior open-source instruction models and to state-of-the-art proprietary systems.

# Key findings: data quality > dataset size; task–dataset suitability matters

The QLoRA experiments reveal two crucial and interrelated findings about instruction tuning at scale.

First, data quality matters more than dataset size. The study shows that tuning on a small, high-quality dataset can outperform tuning on a much larger but lower-quality dataset. A striking example in the paper is that a relatively small, well-curated dataset such as OASST1 (roughly 9k high-quality samples in the authors’ usage) produced superior chatbot behavior compared to a much larger FLAN v2 subset (hundreds of thousands of samples) for conversational benchmarks. This indicates that quantity alone is not sufficient for instruction-following generalization; the match between dataset style and the target assistant behavior—and the overall quality and realism of responses in the dataset—play a larger role.

Second, task–dataset suitability matters and benchmark performance is not universally predictive. The authors find partial orthogonality between MMLU (a knowledge and reasoning benchmark) performance and chatbot conversational quality as measured on Vicuna / OA benchmarks. For example, models finetuned on FLAN v2 achieve strong MMLU scores but perform comparatively worse on conversational benchmarks like Vicuna, whereas models finetuned on OASST1 or similar chat-focused datasets shine on Vicuna but may not be the best on MMLU. This demonstrates that being "good" on one benchmark does not imply being good on another; the finetuning data must be appropriate for the desired downstream behavior.

These findings have practical implications. If the goal is to build a chat assistant, prioritizing in-distribution high-quality conversational data (even if smaller) is more effective than scaling up large multi-task datasets indiscriminately. Conversely, if the target is standard academic benchmarks of knowledge or reasoning, broader or differently constructed instruction data may be more suitable.

The authors also highlight methodological consequences for evaluation: common chatbot benchmarks can be noisy and insufficiently specific about what constitutes good performance. They therefore recommend pairwise comparisons and Elo-style aggregation to better capture relative system strengths. In addition, they show that model-based evaluations (GPT-4 judging competitions) broadly agree with human judgments at the system level but still exhibit bias (e.g., ordering effects and occasional disagreement), underscoring that careful evaluation design remains essential.

# Closing perspective: what QLoRA enables for instruction tuning

Taken together, the dataset design choices, supervised training strategy, label selection for multi-response examples, scale-aware hyperparameter rules, and clear baselines provide a concrete protocol for instruction tuning with QLoRA. The combination of efficient 4-bit storage (NF4 plus double quantization), paged optimizers to avoid memory spikes, and LoRA adapters makes it feasible to experiment at 33B and 65B scales on single GPUs. That capability, in turn, lets researchers test the empirical claims summarized above: that small, high-quality conversational datasets can outperform much larger multi-task corpora for chat behavior; that supervised cross-entropy finetuning is sufficient to produce competitive assistant behavior when paired with appropriate data; and that careful labeling choices for multi-response datasets materially affect the assistant persona and reliability the model learns.

By lowering the computational barrier to scale, QLoRA shifts the research focus from "can we afford to run this experiment?" to "which data and labeling choices produce the behaviors we want?" — and the experiments in the paper show those data and labeling choices matter more than raw dataset size when the goal is instruction-following chat behavior.
