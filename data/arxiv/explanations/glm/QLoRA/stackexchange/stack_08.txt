\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Why does the NF4 quantile formula average two adjacent quantile values?
Question:
The paper defines the NF4 quantization values as:
$$q_i = \frac{1}{2}\left( Q_X\left(\frac{i}{2^k+1}\right) + Q_X\left(\frac{i+1}{2^k+1}\right)\right)$$
where $Q_X(\cdot)$ is the quantile function of $N(0,1)$. I understand that $Q_X(p)$ gives the value such that $P(X \leq x) = p$ for a standard normal. But why is each quantization level defined as the average of two adjacent quantile evaluations? What is the intuition behind this averaging — is it trying to place each quantization level at the center of the bin rather than at the edge? And why use $2^k+1$ quantile points to define $2^k$ levels?
Answer:
The averaging in the NF4 formula serves to place each quantization level at the **midpoint of its bin** in value space, and the use of $2^k+1$ quantile evaluations is what makes this midpoint computation possible while avoiding infinite values.

To see why, consider what happens if you try to define $2^k$ quantization levels for a normal distribution directly. The "obvious" approach would be to evaluate $Q_X$ at $2^k$ equally spaced probabilities: $\frac{1}{2^k}, \frac{2}{2^k}, \ldots, \frac{2^k}{2^k}$. But $Q_X(1) = +\infty$ for a normal distribution, so this fails. You could instead use $\frac{1}{2^k+1}, \frac{2}{2^k+1}, \ldots, \frac{2^k}{2^k+1}$, which gives $2^k$ finite values. However, each of these values sits at the **boundary** between two probability bins, not at the center of one. Using a boundary value as the representative for a bin means that values on one side of the bin are systematically over-quantized and values on the other side are systematically under-quantized.

The paper's formula solves both problems at once. By evaluating $Q_X$ at $2^k+1$ points — $\frac{0}{2^k+1}, \frac{1}{2^k+1}, \ldots, \frac{2^k}{2^k+1}$ — it creates $2^k$ intervals, each of width $\frac{1}{2^k+1}$ in probability space. The endpoints $0$ and $1$ are avoided (since $Q_X(0) = -\infty$ and $Q_X(1) = +\infty$), so all evaluated quantiles are finite. Then, for each interval, the representative value is taken as the average of the quantile values at the two endpoints:

$$q_i = \frac{1}{2}\left( Q_X\left(\frac{i}{2^k+1}\right) + Q_X\left(\frac{i+1}{2^k+1}\right)\right)$$

This is precisely the **midpoint** of bin $i$ in value space. Intuitively, if a bin spans from quantile boundary $Q_X\left(\frac{i}{2^k+1}\right)$ to $Q_X\left(\frac{i+1}{2^k+1}\right)$, then the midpoint is the natural representative — it is the value closest, on average, to all the real values that fall within that bin. This is analogous to how $k$-means clustering assigns each cluster the mean of its members: the center of a bin minimizes the expected quantization error for values assigned to that bin.

The paper notes that quantile quantization is "information-theoretically optimal" because it ensures each bin receives an equal number of values from the input distribution (equal probability mass per bin). The averaging step complements this by ensuring the representative value within each equal-probability bin is optimally placed. Together, these two properties — equal probability mass and centered representatives — minimize the expected quantization error, which is what makes NF4 effective for the approximately normal weight distributions found in pretrained neural networks.

It is worth noting that the total probability mass covered by the $2^k$ bins is $\frac{2^k}{2^k+1}$, slightly less than $1$. The small uncovered tails at each end contain roughly $\frac{1}{2(2^k+1)}$ probability mass each. For $k=4$ (NF4), this means each bin has width $\frac{1}{17} \approx 0.059$ in probability space, and the uncovered tails total about $5.9\%$ of the distribution. After rescaling weights to $[-1, 1]$, these tails correspond to the most extreme outlier values, which the paper handles through block-wise quantization with per-block scaling constants rather than through the data type itself.
