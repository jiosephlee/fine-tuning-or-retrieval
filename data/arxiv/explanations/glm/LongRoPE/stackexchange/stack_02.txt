\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### Why does LongRoPE use evolutionary search instead of gradient-based optimization for finding rescale factors?
Question:
The LongRoPE paper formulates the problem of finding optimal RoPE rescale factors $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i, \hat{n})$ as an optimization problem (Eq. 3) that minimizes perplexity. However, they then solve this using an evolutionary search algorithm rather than gradient descent. Why can't we just use gradient-based optimization here? The perplexity loss $\mathcal{L}$ seems differentiable with respect to the rescale factors $\lambda_i$ since they appear inside the cosine and sine functions in RoPE. Is there a specific reason the search space is non-differentiable, or is the evolutionary search chosen for other reasons?
Answer:
The key reason LongRoPE uses evolutionary search rather than gradient-based optimization lies in the **practical structure of the optimization problem**, not in the mathematical differentiability of the loss itself.

While it is true that the rescale factors $\lambda_i$ appear inside differentiable cosine and sine functions, there are several fundamental obstacles to using gradient descent here:

**1. The search space is explicitly discretized.** The paper defines $\lambda_i$ on a grid from $1.0$ to $s \times 1.25$ with step size $0.01$, and $\hat{n}$ is drawn from a discrete set $\{0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 64, 128, 256\}$. The indicator function $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i, \hat{n})$ that switches between extrapolation and interpolation based on $\hat{n}$ is inherently non-differentiable with respect to $\hat{n}$. This discrete structure is central to the problem formulation, making it a combinatorial search problem rather than a continuous optimization one.

**2. Perplexity evaluation requires a full forward pass through the LLM.** Computing the gradient of $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to $\lambda_i$ would require backpropagation through the entire LLM (all attention layers, all positions), which is far more expensive than the forward-only perplexity evaluation used in evolutionary search. The paper notes that even just computing perplexity is "time-consuming" when $L'$ is large. Gradient computation would multiply this cost significantly, especially for target lengths like $256\text{k}$ or $2048\text{k}$.

**3. The optimization landscape is highly non-convex.** The rescale factors interact in complex ways through the transformer architecture, and the relationship between $\lambda_i$ values and perplexity is unlikely to be smooth or convex. Evolutionary search can explore the global space more effectively by maintaining a diverse population, while gradient descent would be prone to getting stuck in local minima.

**4. The monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i \leq \lambda_{i+1}$ is naturally handled by evolutionary search.** The paper enforces this constraint (motivated by NTK theory) by simply rejecting individuals that violate it during mutation and crossover. In gradient-based optimization, this would require projected gradient descent or Lagrangian methods, adding complexity.

**5. Evolutionary search leverages prior knowledge effectively.** The paper seeds the initial population with known good solutions from PI, NTK, and YaRN, then mutates them. This warm-starting approach is natural in evolutionary search but harder to replicate as initialization in gradient descent, where the discrete structure of the problem would still be an obstacle.

In summary, the problem is framed as a combinatorial search over a discretized space with a non-differentiable component ($\hat{n}$), expensive evaluation, and structural constraints. Evolutionary search is well-suited to all of these characteristics, while gradient-based optimization would require continuous relaxations, expensive backward passes through the LLM, and additional machinery to handle constraints—all while offering no guarantee of finding better solutions in this non-convex landscape.
