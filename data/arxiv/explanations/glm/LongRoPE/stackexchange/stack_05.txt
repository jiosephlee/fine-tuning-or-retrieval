\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### What does 'non-uniformity in token positions' mean and why do initial tokens need less interpolation?
Question:
The LongRoPE paper identifies two forms of non-uniformities: varying RoPE dimensions and token positions. I understand the dimensional non-uniformity (different RoPE dimensions have different frequencies and need different interpolation). But I'm confused about the token position non-uniformity. The paper says 'RoPE for the initial tokens in the input sequence should be extrapolated with less interpolation' because 'they receive large attention scores, making them crucial to attention layers.' Why do initial tokens receive large attention scores, and why does this mean their RoPE should not be interpolated? The paper references StreamingLLM and LM-Infinite but I'm not familiar with those works. Can someone explain the intuition behind keeping initial tokens' RoPE unchanged?
Answer:
The "non-uniformity in token positions" refers to the LongRoPE paper's key finding that not all token positions should be treated equally when applying positional interpolation. Specifically, the initial $\hat{n}$ tokens in a sequence should retain their original RoPE encoding (i.e., be extrapolated) rather than having their rotation angles compressed (interpolated).

To understand why, we need to look at the phenomenon the paper references from StreamingLLM and LM-Infinite. These works observed that in transformer attention, the initial tokens—particularly the very first ones—act as "attention sinks." In standard softmax attention, scores are normalized to sum to 1. When later tokens attend backward across a long context, they often lack strong semantic connections to most earlier positions. Rather than distributing attention uniformly, the model tends to dump excess attention onto the first few tokens. This means initial tokens receive disproportionately large attention scores, making them functionally critical anchors for the attention mechanism.

The paper's insight is that if these initial tokens are so important to attention computation, then changing their positional encodings through interpolation could be harmful. During pre-training, the model learned specific attention patterns tied to the exact RoPE angles of these early positions. Positional interpolation compresses position indices by a rescale factor $\lambda_i$, so a token at position $n$ would have its rotation angle changed from $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$ to $\frac{n}{\lambda_i \beta^i}$. For the initial tokens, this compression alters the precise positional relationships the model was trained on, potentially disrupting the attention sink behavior.

The paper formalizes this in its formulation: for positions $n < \hat{n}$, the rescale factor $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i, \hat{n}) = 1$ (no interpolation, original RoPE preserved), while for positions $n \geq \hat{n}$, $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i, \hat{n}) = \frac{1}{\lambda_i}$ (interpolation applied). The intuition is that the model can better tolerate positional compression for the vast majority of tokens in the middle and end of the sequence, but the few initial tokens serve as structural pillars of the attention pattern and should keep their original positional information intact.

The paper validates this empirically in Table 2 (referenced as `tbl:tokenposition`), showing that retaining the first $\hat{n}$ tokens without interpolation improves perplexity, and that the optimal $\hat{n}$ depends on the target extension length. However, the ablation study in the experiments section notes that this token-position non-uniformity becomes less impactful at extreme extensions like 2048k, suggesting the benefit diminishes when sequences become extraordinarily long.
