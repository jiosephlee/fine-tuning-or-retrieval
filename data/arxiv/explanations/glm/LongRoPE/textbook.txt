\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

# Introduction to Context Window Extension in LLMs

Large Language Models (LLMs) such as LLaMA2 and Mistral have achieved remarkable success across a wide range of tasks, yet they often operate under a fundamental constraint: a limited context window. For example, LLaMA2 has a pre-trained context window of only 4096 tokens. When an input sequence exceeds this limit, the model encounters token positions it has never seen during training, and its performance degrades accordingly. This limitation poses significant challenges in important scenarios that demand long-context understanding. In-context learning, where a model is prompted with numerous examples to guide its behavior, naturally requires a large context window to accommodate all the demonstrations. Similarly, LLM agents that iteratively refine their outputs or interact with complex environments accumulate long histories of tokens that quickly surpass the model's context capacity. Extending the context window is therefore a highly desirable goal, as it unlocks the ability to process longer documents, maintain richer conversational memory, and perform more sophisticated reasoning over extended inputs.

## The Importance of Large Context Windows in LLMs

The context window of an LLM defines the maximum number of tokens the model can attend to in a single forward pass. Within this window, every token can directly interact with every other token through the self-attention mechanism, allowing the model to capture dependencies regardless of their distance. When the input exceeds the context window, however, the model has no mechanism to incorporate the excess tokens without external strategies such as truncation, retrieval, or sliding-window attention, all of which sacrifice information or introduce architectural complexity.

A large context window is particularly valuable because it enables the model to perform tasks that are fundamentally impossible with short contexts. Consider document-level summarization of an entire book, where the model must integrate information distributed across hundreds of thousands of tokens. Or consider few-shot in-context learning, where providing dozens or hundreds of high-quality examples in the prompt can dramatically improve task performance. Each example consumes tokens, and with a 4096-token limit, only a handful of examples can fit. With a 2-million-token window, the same model could be exposed to thousands of examples simultaneously, dramatically expanding its capabilities. LLM agents that operate over multiple turns of interaction, reading and writing code, executing tools, and accumulating observations, similarly benefit from larger context windows because they can retain the full history of their actions and observations without losing early context.

Beyond these application-driven motivations, a large context window also has implications for the model's internal representations. The rotary position embeddings (RoPE) used in modern LLMs encode positional information through rotation angles applied to each dimension of the embedding. The model learns to interpret these angles during pre-training, and positions beyond the training range produce rotation angles that fall outside the distribution the model has learned. This out-of-distribution issue is a core reason why simply feeding longer sequences into a pre-trained model does not work—the model has never learned to interpret the positional signals associated with those new positions. Extending the context window, therefore, is not merely a matter of allowing longer inputs but of ensuring the model can meaningfully process and attend to tokens at positions it was never trained to handle.

## Major Obstacles to Extending Context Windows Beyond 128k Tokens

While recent work has successfully extended pre-trained LLM context windows to approximately 128k tokens through fine-tuning on longer texts, pushing beyond this limit introduces three major obstacles that the paper identifies and addresses.

The first obstacle is the introduction of catastrophic values by new, untrained position indices. When the context window is extended from 4k to over 1000k tokens, more than 90% of the positions in the extended window are new positions that the model has never encountered during pre-training. These new positions produce rotation angles in RoPE that fall outside the distribution the model has learned, leading to out-of-distribution issues. As a result, fine-tuning becomes difficult to converge—the model must simultaneously learn to interpret a vast range of new positional signals while maintaining its existing language understanding capabilities. The sheer proportion of new positions makes this an especially daunting challenge, as the model is overwhelmed by unfamiliar positional encodings that disrupt its attention patterns.

The second obstacle is the scarcity of long text data and the computational expense of training on such data. Fine-tuning to extend the context window typically requires texts of the corresponding target length. However, high-quality texts exceeding one million tokens are extremely limited in current datasets. Even when such texts are available, training on them is prohibitively expensive. The attention mechanism in Transformers has quadratic complexity with respect to sequence length, meaning that processing a 2048k-token sequence requires vastly more computation than processing a 4k-token sequence. The GPU resources and training hours needed to fine-tune on sequences of this length scale dramatically, making direct fine-tuning at the target length practically infeasible for most research groups.

The third obstacle is the dispersal of attention across numerous token positions when the context window becomes extremely long. As the model's attention is spread thinly across millions of token positions, the attention scores for any individual token become diluted. This dispersal degrades the model's performance, particularly on shorter contexts within the original window. The phenomenon occurs because positional interpolation—used to map new positions into the model's trained range—compresses the position embeddings. With a 512$\times$ extension ratio, positions within the original 4k context window become particularly crowded, forcing the model to distinguish between tokens whose positional encodings are nearly identical. This crowding negatively affects the model's ability to differentiate closely positioned tokens and degrades its overall language modeling performance on the original short context window.

## Overview of Existing Context Window Extension Approaches

Several approaches have been proposed to address the challenge of context window extension, each with its own strengths and limitations. The primary technique for mitigating the out-of-distribution problem caused by new positions is to interpolate the RoPE positional embedding, which downscales new position indices to fit within the pre-trained range. Among these methods, Position Interpolation (PI) is the most straightforward. PI linearly reduces the rotation angles of all positions by the extension ratio $s$, where $s = L'/L$ is the ratio of the extended context length $L'$ to the original length $L$. While simple, this uniform linear scaling makes the position information very "crowded," as all positions are compressed into the original range by the same factor. The model struggles to distinguish closely positioned tokens, and PI tends to underperform at high extension ratios, necessitating fine-tuning to achieve acceptable results.

NTK-based methods take a different approach by drawing on Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) theory. Rather than applying uniform interpolation across all RoPE dimensions, NTK distributes the interpolation pressure unevenly: lower dimensions (which correspond to higher frequencies) are scaled less, while higher dimensions (which correspond to lower frequencies) are scaled more. This results in a combination of positional interpolation and extrapolation, where the rescale factor for the $i$-th dimension is $\lambda = s^i$. The improved dynamic NTK variant adjusts the extension ratio at each position based on the current sequence length. Unlike PI, which requires fine-tuning, NTK-aware methods can extend context windows without fine-tuning, but they typically achieve a maximum extension ratio of about 4$\times$ before performance degrades.

YaRN represents a significant advancement by categorizing RoPE dimensions into three frequency-based groups, each treated with a different interpolation strategy. High-frequency dimensions undergo extrapolation (with $\lambda = 1$, meaning no scaling is applied), low-frequency dimensions use linear interpolation (as in PI), and the dimensions in between employ the NTK approach. The key innovation of YaRN lies in recognizing that different frequency bands within the positional embedding serve different roles and should be handled differently. However, the grouping of RoPE dimensions into these three categories depends on human-led empirical experiments, which may result in sub-optimal performance for new LLMs whose architectural details differ from those for which YaRN was originally designed.

Beyond positional embedding interpolation, other approaches include retrieval-based methods that use external memory modules to store and fetch long past context, and attention-based methods that manipulate attention mechanisms with novel masks to extend the effective input context without modifying the context window length. Fine-tuning based approaches such as Code LLaMA and ScaledRoPE choose a very large base value for RoPE and fine-tune on the target length. However, the paper notes that positional embedding exhibits a complex non-uniform information entropy within the Transformer architecture, and existing approaches do not effectively leverage this non-uniformity, leading to information loss that limits the achievable context window size.

## Introduction to LongRoPE and Its Three Key Innovations

LongRoPE is introduced as a method that, for the first time, extends the context window of pre-trained LLMs to 2048k tokens—an unprecedented achievement—while requiring at most 1,000 fine-tuning steps at training lengths within 256k, and while maintaining performance on the original short context window. The method is built on three key innovations that work together to overcome the obstacles described above.

The first innovation is the identification and exploitation of two forms of non-uniformities in positional interpolation through an efficient evolutionary search. The paper reveals empirically that effective positional interpolation should consider two distinct forms of non-uniformity: one across varying RoPE dimensions and another across token positions. Lower RoPE dimensions and initial starting token positions benefit from less interpolation, but the optimal solutions depend on the target extended length. By capturing these non-uniformities, LongRoPE effectively retains information in the original RoPE, particularly in key dimensions and at critical token positions, minimizing the information loss caused by positional interpolation. This provides a better initialization for fine-tuning and enables an 8$\times$ context window extension even in non-fine-tuning scenarios, a significant improvement over the 4$\times$ maximum achieved by prior NTK-based methods. The search algorithm identifies specialized rescale factors for each RoPE dimension based on token positions, using an evolutionary search with two optimization techniques—optimized initial population generation and a monotonically non-decreasing constraint—to navigate the exponentially large search space efficiently.

The second innovation is a progressive extension strategy that avoids the need for direct fine-tuning on extremely long texts. Rather than attempting to search and fine-tune directly at 2048k—which would require prohibitively expensive computational resources and face convergence difficulties due to the large extension ratio—LongRoPE proceeds in stages. It first searches for and fine-tunes a 256k-length LLM, using a two-phase fine-tuning schedule: 400 steps with RoPE rescale factors for 128k, followed by 600 additional steps with rescale factors for 256k on the resulting checkpoint. Then, leveraging the fact that the non-uniform positional interpolation can achieve an 8$\times$ extension without fine-tuning, LongRoPE conducts a second search for new RoPE rescale factors on the fine-tuned 256k LLM. This secondary interpolation ultimately achieves the 2048k context window with a final extension ratio of 512$\times$, without requiring any further fine-tuning.

The third innovation addresses the performance degradation on the original, shorter context window that occurs after extending to 2048k. As mentioned, positional interpolation with a 512$\times$ extension ratio causes positions within the original 4k context window to become particularly crowded, degrading the model's short-context performance. To mitigate this, LongRoPE performs an additional evolutionary search on the extended LLM to readjust the RoPE rescale factors specifically for short context lengths such as 4k and 8k. Because less positional interpolation is needed for shorter lengths, the maximum allowable searched $\lambda$ is reduced to encourage less interpolation. During inference, if the sequence length falls below 8k, the LLM dynamically updates its RoPE with these specially searched rescale factors, recovering its original short-context performance while retaining the ability to handle extremely long sequences when needed.

Together, these three innovations enable LongRoPE to achieve what prior methods could not: a context window of over 2 million tokens with minimal fine-tuning, while preserving the model's capabilities on shorter inputs. The method retains the original LLM architecture with only minor modifications to the positional embedding, allowing it to reuse most pre-existing optimizations. The paper demonstrates the effectiveness of LongRoPE through extensive experiments on LLaMA2 and Mistral, showing that models extended via LongRoPE maintain low perplexity from 4k to 2048k evaluation lengths, achieve over 90% passkey retrieval accuracy across this entire range, and deliver comparable accuracy on standard benchmarks designed within the 4096-token context window.

# Foundations of Positional Embeddings and Interpolation

## Mathematical Formulation of Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE)

Transformer models require explicit positional information to represent the order of input tokens. Without such information, the self-attention mechanism—which lies at the heart of the Transformer architecture—would be entirely permutation-invariant, treating a sequence of tokens as an unordered bag. Position embeddings solve this problem by encoding each token's position within the sequence, allowing the model to distinguish, for example, the first word of a sentence from the last. Among the various positional encoding schemes developed over the years, Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) has emerged as one of the most widely adopted methods in modern large language models (LLMs), including LLaMA2 and Mistral.

RoPE encodes positional information by rotating pairs of dimensions in the query and key vectors through angles that depend on the token's position index. For a token at position index $n$, its corresponding RoPE encoding can be simplified as a vector of cosine and sine values:

$$\left[ \cos(n\theta_0), \sin(n\theta_0), \cos(n\theta_1), \cdot\cdot\cdot, \cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}), \sin(n\theta_{d/2-1}) \right]$$

Here, $d$ is the embedding dimension, and $n\theta_i$ is the rotary angle of the token at position $n$. The term $\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}$ represents the rotation frequencies for the $i$-th dimension pair, where $i$ ranges from $0$ to $d/2 - 1$. The default base value of $\theta$ is $10000$, a choice that ensures a wide range of frequencies across the dimension pairs. Lower values of $i$ correspond to higher rotation frequencies, meaning that the angle $n\theta_i$ changes rapidly as the position $n$ increases. Conversely, higher values of $i$ correspond to lower frequencies, where the angle changes more slowly with position. This frequency spectrum is critical to how RoPE functions: the high-frequency dimensions capture fine-grained positional distinctions between nearby tokens, while the low-frequency dimensions encode coarser positional information about tokens that are far apart in the sequence.

The elegance of RoPE lies in the fact that the relative positional information between two tokens emerges naturally from the dot product of their rotary-encoded query and key vectors. When the model computes attention scores, the relative angle between two positions—determined by the difference in their position indices—is implicitly captured by the trigonometric properties of the rotation. This means that RoPE does not need to explicitly store or compute relative positions; they arise as a consequence of the rotation operation itself. This property makes RoPE particularly well-suited for extension, since adjusting the rotation angles can, in principle, change the effective positional range of the model without altering its fundamental architecture.

Understanding the frequency structure of RoPE is essential for the remainder of this chapter. The fact that different dimension pairs operate at different frequencies means that a uniform adjustment to all dimensions—as some interpolation methods propose—may not be optimal. As we will see, the non-uniform distribution of frequencies across dimensions creates opportunities for more sophisticated interpolation strategies that can preserve critical positional information while extending the context window.

## Context Window Extension Ratio and the Concept of Positional Interpolation

Large language models are typically pre-trained with a fixed context window size. For example, LLaMA2 is pre-trained with a 4096-token context window limit. Beyond this window, the model encounters position indices it has never seen during training, leading to out-of-distribution issues and degraded performance. This is a significant limitation, as many important applications—such as in-context learning with numerous examples and LLM agents that process long interaction histories—benefit from much larger context windows. The challenge, then, is to extend the effective context window of a pre-trained LLM without requiring prohibitively expensive retraining from scratch.

To formalize this extension, we define the context window extension ratio $s$ as the ratio of the desired extended context length $L'$ to the original pre-trained length $L$:

$$s = \frac{L'}{L}$$

For instance, extending a model from a 4k context window to a 128k context window corresponds to an extension ratio of $s = 32$. The extension ratio quantifies how far beyond the pre-trained range the model must operate, and it directly determines the magnitude of the positional adjustments required.

The core idea behind positional interpolation is to downscale the rotation frequencies $\theta_i$ in RoPE so that the new, larger range of position indices maps back into the range of angles that the model was originally trained on. If the original model was trained on positions $0$ through $L-1$, the rotary angles it encountered ranged from $0$ to roughly $(L-1) \cdot \theta_i$ for each dimension $i$. When we extend to $L'$ positions, the angles would naively range from $0$ to $(L'-1) \cdot \theta_i$, which extends well beyond the trained range. Positional interpolation addresses this by applying a rescale factor that compresses the new range back into the original range.

To unify the various positional interpolation methods, the paper introduces a general formulation. Let $\beta = \theta^{2/d}$, and let $\lambda$ denote the actual rescale factor, which is related to $s$ but whose precise form varies across methods. The unified interpolated RoPE encoding becomes:

$$\left[\cos\left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^0}\right), \sin\left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^0}\right), \cos\left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^1}\right), \cdot\cdot\cdot, \sin\left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^{d/2-1}}\right)\right]$$

In this formulation, the rescale factor $\lambda$ determines how much the rotation angles are compressed. Different interpolation methods differ in how they set $\lambda$—whether it is a single constant applied uniformly to all dimensions, a function of the dimension index $i$, or a piecewise function that groups dimensions by frequency. The key insight is that the choice of $\lambda$ profoundly affects how well the model generalizes to longer sequences. A poor choice can lead to information loss, where critical positional distinctions encoded in the original RoPE are degraded or destroyed. As we will see in the following sections, the search for the optimal $\lambda$ is central to the development of methods like PI, NTK, YaRN, and ultimately LongRoPE.

It is worth noting that extending the context window involves more than just adjusting positional embeddings. The paper identifies three major obstacles to extending context windows beyond the pre-trained limit. First, untrained new position indices introduce many catastrophic values, leading to out-of-distribution issues that make fine-tuning difficult to converge—particularly when an extension from 4k to over 1000k introduces more than 90\% new positions. Second, fine-tuning usually requires texts of corresponding lengths, but long texts exceeding 1000k tokens are scarce in current datasets, and training on such texts is computationally expensive. Third, when extending to extremely long context windows, attention becomes dispersed as it is spread thinly across numerous token positions, degrading performance on the original short context. Positional interpolation addresses the first obstacle by mapping new positions back into the trained range, but the way this mapping is done determines how much information is preserved and how well the model performs.

## Linear Position Interpolation (PI) and Its Limitations with Crowded Positions

Linear Position Interpolation (PI) is the most straightforward approach to extending the context window via positional interpolation. The core idea is elegantly simple: for a target extension ratio $s$, linearly reduce the rotation angles of all positions by the factor $\lambda = s$ across all RoPE dimensions. In other words, every position index $n$ in the extended sequence is effectively mapped to the position $n/s$ in the original coordinate system. This ensures that the maximum position index in the extended sequence, $L' - 1$, maps to approximately $L - 1$ in the original range, keeping all rotary angles within the distribution the model was trained on.

The mathematical effect of PI is to uniformly compress the positional information across all dimensions. Since $\lambda$ is set to the constant $s$ regardless of the dimension index $i$, every RoPE dimension pair experiences the same degree of compression. This uniformity is appealing in its simplicity, but it introduces a significant drawback: the position information becomes very "crowded." Consider what happens when $s = 32$: tokens that were originally separated by one position in the pre-trained model are now separated by only $1/32$ of the original angular distance. This means that the model must distinguish between tokens whose rotary angles differ by a much smaller amount than it was trained to discriminate.

This crowding effect is particularly problematic because the model's ability to distinguish closely positioned tokens depends on the angular resolution of the rotary encoding. When positions are compressed uniformly, the angular differences between adjacent tokens shrink, and the model loses the ability to resolve fine-grained positional distinctions. This is why PI tends to underperform at high extension ratios. While a $2\times$ or $4\times$ extension may produce acceptable results, the crowding becomes increasingly severe as $s$ grows, leading to rapid degradation in language modeling performance. The paper notes that PI necessitates fine-tuning to achieve reasonable performance, as the compressed positional embeddings are sufficiently different from the original distribution that the model cannot adapt without additional training.

Another way to understand the limitation of PI is to consider the information content of each RoPE dimension. As discussed earlier, different dimension pairs encode positional information at different frequencies. The high-frequency dimensions, which are responsible for distinguishing nearby tokens, are the most affected by uniform compression. When these dimensions are compressed by the same factor as the low-frequency dimensions, the fine-grained positional information they carry is disproportionately degraded. This observation motivates the search for non-uniform interpolation strategies that can allocate the compression pressure more intelligently across dimensions.

Despite its limitations, PI remains an important baseline method. Its conceptual clarity and ease of implementation make it a natural starting point for understanding positional interpolation, and more sophisticated methods can be viewed as attempts to address its specific shortcomings. The paper uses PI as one of three methods (alongside NTK and YaRN) to initialize the population in its evolutionary search algorithm, recognizing that while PI may not be optimal, it captures a meaningful region of the solution space that more advanced methods build upon.

## NTK-based Interpolation and Its Distribution of Interpolation Pressure Across Dimensions

The NTK-based interpolation approach addresses the crowding problem of PI by taking an information-encoding perspective on RoPE. Rather than applying a uniform compression factor across all dimensions, NTK-aware methods distribute the interpolation pressure unevenly across RoPE dimensions, recognizing that different dimensions serve different roles in encoding positional information. The method draws on the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) theory, which provides a framework for understanding how neural networks encode information across different frequency components.

The key insight of NTK-based interpolation is that lower RoPE dimensions—which operate at higher frequencies and encode fine-grained positional distinctions—should be scaled less, while higher dimensions—which operate at lower frequencies and encode coarser positional information—should be scaled more. In the unified formulation, this corresponds to setting $\lambda = s^i$, where $i$ is the dimension index. As a result, the rescale factor grows with the dimension index: the lowest dimensions (small $i$) experience minimal compression, while the highest dimensions (large $i$) experience compression comparable to or exceeding that of PI.

This non-uniform distribution has two important consequences. First, the high-frequency dimensions that are most critical for distinguishing nearby tokens are largely preserved, since their rotation angles are barely modified. This mitigates the crowding problem that plagues PI, allowing the model to maintain its ability to resolve fine-grained positional differences even at extended context lengths. Second, the low-frequency dimensions, which are less sensitive to fine-grained distinctions, absorb most of the compression. This is acceptable because these dimensions primarily encode coarse positional information, and the model can tolerate more compression in this regime without significant degradation.

The NTK approach results in both positional interpolation and extrapolation occurring simultaneously across different dimensions. For the lowest dimensions, the rescale factor $\lambda = s^i$ approaches 1 when $i$ is near 0, meaning that these dimensions effectively undergo extrapolation—the model uses the original, uncompressed RoPE angles even for position indices beyond the pre-trained range. For higher dimensions, the rescale factor grows toward $s$ or beyond, meaning that these dimensions undergo interpolation similar to PI. This mixed strategy is fundamentally different from PI's uniform approach and represents a significant conceptual advance.

An improvement over the original NTK method, known as dynamic NTK, adjusts the extension ratio at each position based on the current sequence length. This dynamic adjustment allows the model to adapt its interpolation strategy on the fly, applying more conservative interpolation for shorter sequences and more aggressive interpolation for longer sequences. This is particularly useful in scenarios where the model encounters inputs of varying lengths, as it avoids over-compressing positional information for sequences that do not require the full extended context window.

A notable advantage of NTK-aware methods is that they can extend context windows in non-fine-tuning scenarios. Unlike PI, which typically requires fine-tuning to achieve acceptable performance, NTK-based methods can produce reasonable results by simply modifying the RoPE rescale factors without any additional training. However, the paper notes that this capability is usually limited to a maximum extension ratio of approximately $4\times$. Beyond this ratio, the extrapolation in high-frequency dimensions begins to introduce out-of-distribution angles that the model has never encountered, leading to performance degradation. This limitation motivates the search for more sophisticated interpolation strategies that can push beyond the $4\times$ barrier without fine-tuning—a goal that LongRoPE achieves by more fully exploiting the non-uniformities in positional interpolation.

## YaRN's Frequency-based Grouping Approach and Its Reliance on Empirical Rules

YaRN represents a further refinement of positional interpolation by introducing a more structured approach to non-uniform interpolation. Rather than applying a continuous formula like NTK's $\lambda = s^i$, YaRN categorizes RoPE dimensions into three frequency-based groups, each of which receives a different interpolation strategy. This grouping reflects the observation that the frequency spectrum of RoPE dimensions is not monolithic—different regions of the spectrum serve qualitatively different roles in positional encoding, and each region may benefit from a tailored interpolation approach.

The first group consists of high-frequency dimensions, which are the lowest-indexed dimension pairs in RoPE. For these dimensions, YaRN applies extrapolation, setting $\lambda = 1$. This means that the rotation angles for these dimensions are left completely unmodified—the model uses the original, uncompressed RoPE angles for all position indices, even those beyond the pre-trained range. The rationale is that these high-frequency dimensions are the most sensitive to compression and the most critical for fine-grained positional distinctions. By leaving them unmodified, YaRN preserves the model's ability to distinguish nearby tokens, avoiding the crowding problem of PI.

The second group consists of low-frequency dimensions, which are the highest-indexed dimension pairs. For these dimensions, YaRN applies linear interpolation, equivalent to PI's uniform compression with $\lambda = s$. The rationale is that these dimensions encode coarse positional information and are relatively insensitive to fine-grained distinctions. Compressing them uniformly is acceptable because the model can tolerate the resulting crowding in these dimensions without significant performance loss.

The third group consists of the dimensions that fall in-between the high and low frequency groups. For these intermediate dimensions, YaRN employs the NTK-based interpolation strategy, applying the $\lambda = s^i$ formula. This provides a smooth transition between the extrapolation of the high-frequency group and the linear interpolation of the low-frequency group, avoiding abrupt discontinuities in the rescale factors across adjacent dimensions.

The key innovation of YaRN lies in its recognition that a one-size-fits-all approach to interpolation—whether uniform like PI or smoothly varying like NTK—is suboptimal, and that different frequency regimes demand qualitatively different treatment. By dividing the frequency spectrum into three groups and applying distinct strategies to each, YaRN achieves better performance than either PI or NTK in many settings.

However, YaRN has a notable limitation: the grouping of RoPE dimensions into the three frequency-based categories currently depends on human-led empirical experiments. The boundaries between the high, intermediate, and low frequency groups are not determined by a principled optimization process but rather by trial-and-error experimentation conducted by the method's designers. This has two important consequences. First, the empirically determined boundaries may be sub-optimal for new LLMs that differ in architecture, embedding dimension, or training distribution from the models on which YaRN was developed. What works well for one model may not transfer perfectly to another. Second, the reliance on human-designed rules means that YaRN cannot automatically adapt to new models or new target extension ratios. Each new setting may require fresh empirical exploration to determine the optimal group boundaries, a process that is labor-intensive and may not converge to the best solution.

The paper provides evidence of this sub-optimality. In experiments on LLaMA2-7B, YaRN underperforms compared to both PI and NTK on the PG19 dataset in non-fine-tuning scenarios. Specifically, YaRN's perplexity spikes after 7k tokens in an 8k context size, indicating that it does not successfully reach the target context window length for non-fine-tuned LLMs. This finding suggests that the empirical grouping used by YaRN, while effective in some settings, does not fully exploit the non-uniformities present in RoPE's positional encoding.

This limitation of YaRN—its reliance on fixed, human-designed rules for dimension grouping—is one of the key motivations behind LongRoPE. Rather than relying on empirical rules to determine how different dimensions should be interpolated, LongRoPE uses an evolutionary search algorithm to discover the optimal rescale factors for each dimension individually, without imposing a predetermined grouping structure. As the paper demonstrates, this approach can identify non-uniform interpolations that significantly outperform YaRN, PI, and NTK, by more effectively preserving the critical information encoded in RoPE's positional embeddings. The search-based approach also generalizes naturally to new models and new target extension ratios, since the optimization process adapts to the specific characteristics of each setting.

In summary, the progression from PI to NTK to YaRN represents a gradual recognition of the importance of non-uniformity in positional interpolation. PI applies uniform compression, NTK introduces a smooth non-uniformity based on frequency, and YaRN refines this into a grouped strategy. Each step addresses limitations of the previous approach, but each also introduces new dependencies on human-designed rules or assumptions. The foundational understanding of these methods—and their respective strengths and limitations—provides the necessary context for appreciating how LongRoPE's search-based approach can more fully exploit the non-uniformities in RoPE to achieve context window extensions far beyond what these earlier methods can accomplish.

# Discovering Non-Uniformities in Positional Interpolation

The extension of large language model context windows has been a persistent challenge, with existing methods encountering limitations that prevent scaling beyond approximately 128k tokens. A central obstacle lies in how positional embeddings—specifically Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE)—are interpolated when extending beyond the pre-trained context window. This chapter examines the empirical analysis conducted by the authors of LongRoPE, who systematically investigated why existing positional interpolation methods fall short and discovered two critical forms of non-uniformity that, when properly exploited, dramatically improve context extension performance. Their findings not only expose sub-optimalities in prior approaches but also reveal a path toward extending context windows to over two million tokens.

# Non-Uniformity Across RoPE Dimensions and the Limitations of Current Methods

To understand the non-uniformity across RoPE dimensions, we must first recall the structure of RoPE encoding. For a token at position index $n$, RoPE generates a sequence of cosine and sine values based on rotation frequencies $\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}$, where $d$ is the embedding dimension and $\theta$ defaults to a base value of 10000. The full encoding takes the form $[cos(n\theta_0), sin(n\theta_0), cos(n\theta_1), \cdot\cdot\cdot, cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}), sin(n\theta_{d/2-1})]$. Each dimension $i$ corresponds to a different frequency, with lower dimensions having higher frequencies and higher dimensions having lower frequencies. This frequency structure is fundamental to understanding why uniform interpolation is sub-optimal.

When extending the context window from an original length $L$ to an extended length $L'$, the extension ratio is defined as $s = \frac{L'}{L}$. Existing positional interpolation methods all attempt to rescale the rotation frequencies $\theta_i$ based on $s$, but they differ in how they distribute the interpolation pressure across RoPE dimensions. The authors unify these methods through a common formulation, where the rescaled RoPE takes the form $[cos(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^0}), sin(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^0}), cos(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^1}), \cdot\cdot\cdot, sin(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^{d/2-1}})]$, with $\beta = \theta^{2/d}$ and $\lambda$ representing the rescale factor.

Position Interpolation (PI) applies the simplest approach: it linearly reduces all rotation angles by the same factor $\lambda = s$ across all RoPE dimensions. While straightforward, this uniform scaling makes position information "crowded," meaning the model struggles to distinguish between tokens at closely positioned indices. This crowding effect becomes increasingly problematic at higher extension ratios, where the positional information from the original context is compressed into a much smaller region. The model's ability to differentiate between adjacent tokens degrades because the angular differences between neighboring positions shrink uniformly.

NTK-based methods attempt to address this crowding by distributing the interpolation pressure unequally across dimensions. Drawing on Neural Tangent Kernel theory, NTK scales lower (high-frequency) dimensions less and higher (low-frequency) dimensions more, resulting in $\lambda = s^i$. This approach acknowledges that not all dimensions should be treated identically, but it still relies on a fixed formulaic relationship between the extension ratio and the dimension index. The improved dynamic NTK variant adjusts the extension ratio at each position based on the current sequence length, adding some adaptivity, but the underlying interpolation pattern remains formulaically determined.

YaRN introduces a more sophisticated approach by dividing RoPE dimensions into three frequency-based groups, each receiving a different interpolation strategy. High-frequency dimensions undergo extrapolation ($\lambda = 1$, meaning no interpolation), low-frequency dimensions use linear interpolation (PI-style), and the dimensions in between employ the NTK approach. While this grouping represents a step toward recognizing dimensional non-uniformity, the boundaries between groups and the strategies assigned to each group depend on human-led empirical experiments. This manual design process may result in sub-optimal performance, particularly when applied to new LLMs with different architectures or embedding dimensions than those for which YaRN was originally tuned.

The authors hypothesized that the positional embedding exhibits what they term "complex non-uniform information entropy" within the Transformer architecture, and that this subtlety is not effectively captured by any of the existing methods. To test this hypothesis, they employed an evolutionary search algorithm to discover better non-uniform positional interpolations for LLaMA2-7B. The search was guided by perplexity measurements on five random samples from the PG19 validation set, and it sought the optimal rescale factor $\lambda$ for each RoPE dimension independently.

The results of this search revealed a striking finding: the optimal rescale factors $\lambda$ across RoPE dimensions are substantially non-uniform, deviating significantly from the fixed scale $s$ used in PI, the formulaic calculation of NTK, or the group-wise assignment of YaRN. When the authors compared the perplexity of LLaMA2-7B under different methods on both PG19 and Proof-pile test sets—without any fine-tuning—their searched solution showed significant improvements over all existing approaches. Notably, YaRN actually underperformed compared to both PI and NTK on PG19, because it failed to reach the target context window length for non-fine-tuned LLMs. For example, YaRN's perplexity spiked after 7k tokens in an 8k context size, indicating that its human-designed grouping was not appropriate for this particular model and extension scenario.

The non-uniform factors discovered through search significantly improved LLaMA2's language modeling performance for both 8k and 16k context windows without fine-tuning. The key insight is that by allowing each RoPE dimension to have its own independently optimized rescale factor, the resulting positional embedding can more effectively preserve the original RoPE information. This is particularly important for key dimensions that carry critical positional information. By reducing the interpolation-induced information loss in these dimensions, the model maintains a better ability to distinguish closely positioned tokens, which is essential for coherent language modeling over extended contexts.

The practical implication of this finding is profound: the search space for optimal positional interpolation is much richer than what existing methods explore. A $4\times$ extension, for instance, leads to $400^{128/2} \times 14 = 4 \times 10^{167}$ possible combinations of rescale factors across dimensions and token position thresholds. Existing methods each explore a single point or a narrow region within this vast space, while the evolutionary search can navigate it more effectively to find solutions that preserve the information most critical to the model's performance.

# The Importance of Extrapolating Initial Token Positions with Less Interpolation

The second form of non-uniformity discovered by the authors relates not to which RoPE dimensions are interpolated, but to which token positions receive interpolation. This finding stems from the observation that not all token positions are equally important to the model's attention mechanisms. The authors hypothesized that the initial tokens in an input sequence—those at the beginning of the context—receive disproportionately large attention scores and are therefore crucial to the functioning of attention layers. This phenomenon has been observed in prior work such as Streaming LLM and LM-Infinite, where initial tokens serve as "attention sinks" that stabilize the model's behavior over long sequences.

Based on this hypothesis, the authors proposed that the RoPE encoding for the initial $\hat{n}$ tokens in an input sequence should undergo less interpolation than subsequent tokens. In other words, for these early positions, the model should rely more on extrapolation—using the original, unmodified RoPE embedding—rather than compressing the positional information through interpolation. The intuition is that since these initial positions are so important for attention, distorting their positional information through interpolation could be more harmful than for later positions that receive less attention.

To verify this hypothesis, the authors conducted a systematic experiment. They extended LLaMA2-7B's context window to 8k and 16k using both PI and NTK, while varying the number of initial tokens $\hat{n}$ that were kept without interpolation. The values of $\hat{n}$ ranged from 0 (which reverts to the original PI or NTK with all positions interpolated) to 256. For positions $n < \hat{n}$, the original RoPE rotary angle $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$ was used without any rescaling. For positions $n \geq \hat{n}$, the rescale factor was applied as usual.

The results confirmed the hypothesis with two key observations. First, retaining the starting tokens without position interpolation indeed improved performance. Even keeping as few as 2 tokens without interpolation yielded measurable improvements over the baseline where all tokens were interpolated. This validates the intuition that the initial positions carry unique importance in the attention mechanism and that preserving their original positional encoding is beneficial.

Second, the optimal number of starting tokens $\hat{n}$ depends on the target extension length. Different extension ratios require different numbers of preserved initial tokens to achieve the best perplexity. This means that the non-uniformity across token positions is not a fixed property but rather interacts with the degree of extension being attempted. For shorter extensions, a smaller $\hat{n}$ might suffice, while longer extensions may benefit from preserving more initial tokens. This dependency on the target length further underscores the limitations of fixed, rule-based approaches to positional interpolation and motivates the use of a search algorithm that can discover the optimal $\hat{n}$ for each specific extension scenario.

The practical implementation of this non-uniformity involves introducing an indicator function $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i, \hat{n})$ that applies different treatment based on position. For positions $n < \hat{n}$, the indicator returns 1, meaning the original rotary angle $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$ is used without modification. For positions $n \geq \hat{n}$, the indicator returns $\frac{1}{\lambda_i}$, applying the searched rescale factor. This design elegantly separates the positional space into two regions: an initial region where extrapolation is preferred and a subsequent region where interpolation is applied. The threshold $\hat{n}$ and the per-dimension rescale factors $\lambda_i$ are both determined through the evolutionary search, allowing the method to jointly optimize both forms of non-uniformity.

# How Non-Uniform Positional Interpolation Benefits Both Fine-Tuning and Non-Fine-Tuning Settings

While the benefits of non-uniform positional interpolation were first demonstrated in non-fine-tuning scenarios—at 8k and 16k context windows—the authors recognized that longer extensions would inevitably require fine-tuning. The question then became whether the advantages of non-uniform interpolation persist when fine-tuning is involved, and whether the searched rescale factors provide a better starting point for the fine-tuning process.

To investigate this, the authors fine-tuned LLaMA2-7B using their searched RoPE for a 64k context window. The comparison with PI and YaRN, both before and after fine-tuning, revealed that the non-uniform positional interpolation method significantly outperformed both baselines. This improvement was consistent across the two evaluation phases, demonstrating that the benefits of non-uniform interpolation are not limited to the pre-fine-tuning regime.

The mechanism behind this improvement lies in how non-uniform interpolation affects the initialization for fine-tuning. When fine-tuning begins, the model's positional embeddings are already in a modified state due to the interpolation applied. If this interpolation introduces excessive information loss—as is the case with PI's uniform compression—the fine-tuning process must work harder to recover the lost information and adapt to the new positional encoding. In contrast, by preserving key dimensions and critical initial token positions, the non-uniform interpolation minimizes the initial information loss. This means the model starts fine-tuning from a state that is closer to a valid configuration, making it easier for the optimization process to converge.

The authors observed that this better initialization is particularly important when the extension ratio is large. Extending from 4k to over 1000k introduces more than 90% new positions that the model has never been trained on. These new positions introduce many "catastrophic values" that push the model into out-of-distribution states, making fine-tuning difficult to converge. By using non-uniform interpolation to carefully manage how these new positions are encoded, the method reduces the severity of this out-of-distribution problem and provides a smoother path for the model to adapt to the extended context window.

In non-fine-tuning settings, the benefits are even more dramatic. The authors demonstrated that their evolutionary search can extend LLaMA2's context window from 4k to 32k—an $8\times$ extension—without any fine-tuning. In contrast, existing methods such as PI, NTK, and YaRN cause perplexity to spike after only a $2\times$ extension in non-fine-tuned scenarios. This $8\times$ extension capability becomes a critical building block in the progressive extension strategy that ultimately reaches 2048k tokens. By first fine-tuning to 256k and then applying a second search for an $8\times$ non-fine-tuned extension, the method achieves 2048k without requiring prohibitively expensive direct fine-tuning on million-token-length texts.

The dual benefit of non-uniform interpolation—providing both a better initialization for fine-tuning and enabling larger extensions without fine-tuning—creates a synergistic effect. The fine-tuning phase benefits from starting at a better point, requiring fewer training steps to achieve convergence (the authors report only 1k fine-tuning steps). The post-fine-tuning extension then leverages the $8\times$ non-fine-tuning capability to reach extreme context lengths that would otherwise be infeasible. This combination is what makes the 2048k context window achievable with reasonable computational resources.

# Summary of Findings: Utilizing Non-Uniformities to Improve LLM Context Extension Performance

The empirical analysis conducted by the authors culminates in a clear and actionable set of findings that fundamentally reshape our understanding of positional interpolation for context window extension. The central discovery is that two forms of non-uniformity—across RoPE dimensions and across token positions—play critical roles in determining the effectiveness of context extension, and that existing methods fail to fully exploit either form.

The first non-uniformity, across RoPE dimensions, reflects the fact that different dimensions of the positional encoding carry different types of information and have different sensitivities to interpolation. Lower dimensions with higher frequencies require less interpolation to preserve their information, while higher dimensions with lower frequencies can tolerate more interpolation. However, the optimal rescale factor for each dimension is not determined by a simple formula but depends on the specific model and target extension length. The evolutionary search is able to discover these optimal factors by directly optimizing perplexity, leading to significant improvements over the fixed strategies of PI, NTK, and YaRN.

The second non-uniformity, across token positions, reflects the observation that initial tokens in a sequence receive disproportionate attention and should therefore retain their original positional encoding through extrapolation rather than interpolation. The optimal number of initial tokens to preserve, $\hat{n}$, also depends on the target extension length, further emphasizing the need for a search-based approach rather than a fixed rule.

When both non-uniformities are exploited together, the resulting positional interpolation minimizes information loss in the most critical parts of the RoPE encoding. This has two major consequences. First, it provides a better initialization for fine-tuning, making the optimization process more efficient and more likely to converge to a good solution. Second, it enables context window extension without fine-tuning at ratios up to $8\times$, far exceeding the approximately $2\times$ to $4\times$ limits of existing methods.

These findings directly motivate the design of LongRoPE, which formalizes the search for non-uniform rescale factors as an optimization problem, uses an efficient evolutionary algorithm to navigate the exponentially large search space, and applies the results in a progressive extension strategy. The search space includes a specialized rescale factor $\lambda_i$ for each RoPE dimension, ranging from 1.0 (direct extrapolation) to $s \times 1.25$ (more interpolation than PI), along with the token position threshold $\hat{n}$ selected from a set of empirically chosen values. The evolutionary search is guided by perplexity on validation samples and incorporates optimizations such as seeding the initial population with known good solutions (PI, NTK, YaRN) and enforcing a monotonically non-decreasing constraint on rescale factors across dimensions based on NTK theory.

The ablation studies confirm that both forms of non-uniformity contribute to performance. Non-uniformity in RoPE dimensions significantly reduces perplexity compared to PI's linear interpolation. Non-uniformity in token positions further improves performance at moderate extension lengths (16k and 32k), though the benefit diminishes at extreme lengths (2048k), possibly because preserving only initial tokens becomes less useful when the context is so long that the initial tokens are a negligible fraction of the total. This observation is identified as an area for future work.

In summary, the authors' empirical analysis reveals that the path to extending LLM context windows lies not in more complex fixed formulas or larger fine-tuning budgets, but in recognizing and exploiting the inherent non-uniformities in how positional information is distributed across dimensions and positions. By doing so through an efficient search algorithm, they achieve context windows of unprecedented length while maintaining the model's original capabilities at shorter contexts.

# The LongRoPE Search Algorithm

## Problem Formulation for Multidimensional Non-uniform Position Interpolation

The central insight driving LongRoPE is that positional interpolation in Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) exhibits complex non-uniformities that existing methods fail to fully exploit. While methods such as Position Interpolation (PI), NTK, and YaRN have made strides in extending LLM context windows, they rely on either uniform scaling across all dimensions or coarse, rule-based groupings that do not capture the full spectrum of non-uniformity present in the Transformer architecture. To systematically harness these non-uniformities, LongRoPE frames the problem of finding optimal rescale factors as a formal search problem over a vast solution space.

The formulation begins with the RoPE encoding itself. For a token at position index $n$, its RoPE encoding can be written as a sequence of cosine and sine values computed from rotary angles:

$$\left[ \cos(n\theta_0), \sin(n\theta_0), \cos(n\theta_1), \cdots, \cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}), \sin(n\theta_{d/2-1}) \right]$$

where $d$ is the embedding dimension, $n\theta_i$ is the rotary angle of the token at position $n$, and $\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}$ represents the rotation frequencies. The default base value of $\theta$ is 10000. When extending the context window from an original length $L$ to a target length $L'$, the extension ratio is defined as $s = \frac{L'}{L}$. Existing positional interpolation methods downscale the rotation frequencies $\theta_i$ based on this ratio $s$, and these methods can be unified under a single framework where $\beta = \theta^{2/d}$ and $\lambda$ denotes the actual rescale factor:

$$\left[\cos\left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^0}\right), \sin\left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^0}\right), \cos\left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^1}\right), \cdots, \sin\left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^{d/2-1}}\right)\right]$$

In PI, $\lambda = s$ uniformly across all dimensions. In NTK, $\lambda = s^i$, distributing interpolation pressure non-uniformly across dimensions. YaRN groups dimensions into three frequency-based categories, each receiving a different interpolation strategy. However, as the paper's empirical analysis reveals, the optimal interpolation is far more nuanced than any of these rule-based approaches can capture.

To account for the two discovered forms of non-uniformity—varying RoPE dimensions and varying token positions—LongRoPE introduces a set of rescale factors $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i, \hat{n})$ that operate on both dimensions and positions. The formal optimization problem is defined as follows. Given a LLM targeting a context window size of $L'$ and lengthy input documents $\mathbf{X}$, where each $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbf{X}$ surpasses $L'$ in token length, and denoting the original rotary angle of the $i$-th dimension in RoPE at token position $n$ as $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$, the objective is:

$$\underset{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbf{X}; \, |\mathbf{x}| \geq L'}{\operatorname{arg\,min}} \, \mathcal{L}\left(\text{LLM}(\text{RoPE}, \mathbf{X})\right)$$

where the rescaled RoPE at token position $n$ is:

$$\text{RoPE}_{(n)} = \left[\cdots, \cos\left(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i, \hat{n}) \times \frac{n}{\beta^i}\right), \sin\left(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i, \hat{n}) \times \frac{n}{\beta^i}\right), \cdots\right]$$

and the rescale function $\mathbb{I}$ is defined piecewise:

$$\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i, \hat{n}) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } n < \hat{n} \\ \frac{1}{\lambda_i} & \text{if } n \geq \hat{n} \end{cases}$$

Here, $\hat{\lambda}_i$ is the rescale factor for the $i$-th RoPE dimension and $\hat{n}$ is the token position threshold. For the initial $\hat{n} - 1$ token positions, the rescale factor does not take effect, meaning the original RoPE rotary angle $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$ is preserved without any interpolation. For tokens at positions $n \geq \hat{n}$, the rescale factor $\frac{1}{\lambda_i}$ is applied, downscaling the rotary angle for that dimension. The goal is to find the optimal set of rescale factors $(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_0, \hat{n}), \mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_1, \hat{n}), \ldots)$ across all $d$ RoPE dimensions such that the target LLM, equipped with the rescaled RoPE, achieves the minimum next-token prediction loss $\mathcal{L}$ (i.e., the perplexity) on input samples of length $L'$.

This formulation is significant because it generalizes all prior positional interpolation methods. When $\hat{n} = 0$ and all $\lambda_i = s$, it reduces to PI. When $\hat{n} = 0$ and $\lambda_i = s^i$, it corresponds to NTK. The formulation also subsumes YaRN's group-wise strategy as a special case. By casting the problem as a search over a continuous space of rescale factors and a discrete space of position thresholds, LongRoPE opens the door to discovering interpolations that are strictly more expressive than any hand-designed rule.

## Designing the Search Space for Rescale Factors and Token Position Thresholds

The effectiveness of any search algorithm depends critically on how the search space is defined. LongRoPE designs a search space that is both expressive enough to capture the two forms of non-uniformity and structured enough to permit efficient exploration. The search space is defined over two types of variables: the per-dimension rescale factors $\lambda_i$ and the token position threshold $\hat{n}$.

For the rescale factors $\lambda_i$, the search is conducted independently for each of the $d/2$ RoPE dimensions. The range for each $\lambda_i$ extends from a minimum value of 1.0, which corresponds to direct extrapolation (no interpolation at all, meaning the original rotary angles are preserved), to a maximum value of $s \times 1.25$, which allows for more aggressive interpolation than even PI (which uses $\lambda = s$). The step size for the search is 0.01, providing fine-grained resolution over the possible rescale values. The decision to allow $\lambda_i$ to exceed $s$ reflects the insight that some dimensions may benefit from more interpolation than PI applies, while others may need less, and the optimal balance varies with the target extension ratio.

The inclusion of $\lambda_i = 1.0$ as the minimum is particularly important. A rescale factor of 1.0 means that the corresponding RoPE dimension is extrapolated—its rotary angles remain unchanged from the pre-trained model. This is valuable for high-frequency dimensions that encode fine-grained positional information and would suffer from the crowding effect of interpolation. By allowing the search to choose extrapolation for select dimensions while interpolating others, the search space encompasses hybrid strategies that no single rule-based method can express.

For the token position threshold $\hat{n}$, the search space is discrete and consists of the values $\{0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 64, 128, 256\}$. When $\hat{n} = 0$, all token positions use the searched rescale factors, meaning there is no positional non-uniformity and only dimensional non-uniformity is exploited. When $\hat{n} > 0$, the first $\hat{n}$ tokens in the input sequence are exempt from interpolation entirely—their rotary angles are computed using the original, unmodified RoPE. This design is motivated by the empirical finding that initial tokens receive disproportionately large attention scores and are therefore critical to the model's attention mechanism, as observed in Streaming LLM and LM-Infinite. By preserving the original positional encoding for these initial tokens, the model retains the attention patterns it learned during pre-training for the most influential positions.

The rationale for the specific set of $\hat{n}$ values is that the optimal number of initial tokens to preserve depends on the target extension length. For shorter extensions, a small $\hat{n}$ may suffice, while for longer extensions, more tokens may need to be preserved. The discrete set includes both small values (0, 1, 2, 4) for fine-grained control and larger values (64, 128, 256) for scenarios where more tokens benefit from extrapolation. The empirical study showed that the optimal $\hat{n}$ varies with the target extension length, making it essential to include a range of values in the search space.

To simplify the search, the algorithm searches for $\lambda_i$ and $\hat{n}$ directly, rather than searching for the composite function $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i, \hat{n})$. The relationship $\hat{\lambda}_i = 1/\lambda_i$ is used to convert between the two representations. This decoupling makes the search more tractable and interpretable, as the dimensional and positional non-uniformities can be optimized somewhat independently.

The sheer size of this search space is staggering. For a modest extension ratio of $s = 4\times$, the number of possible configurations is $400^{128/2} \times 14 = 4 \times 10^{167}$. This figure arises because each of the 64 RoPE dimensions has approximately 400 possible values for $\lambda_i$ (from 1.0 to $4 \times 1.25 = 5.0$ with step size 0.01), and there are 14 possible values for $\hat{n}$. For larger extension ratios, the search space grows exponentially. This combinatorial explosion makes exhaustive search infeasible and motivates the use of an evolutionary search algorithm with specialized optimization techniques to navigate the space efficiently.

## Evolution-based Search Methodology for Identifying Optimal RoPE Rescale Factors

Given the enormous search space, LongRoPE employs an evolution-based search algorithm to discover optimal rescale factors. Evolutionary search is a metaheuristic inspired by biological evolution, in which a population of candidate solutions undergoes iterative improvement through selection, mutation, and crossover operations. The algorithm is well-suited to this problem because it can explore a large, non-convex search space without requiring gradient information, and it can be guided by the perplexity of the LLM as a fitness metric.

The algorithm operates as follows. It takes as input the target LLM, input samples $\mathbf{X}$, a population size $P$, a mutation size $N_1$, a crossover size $N_2$, a maximum number of iterations $\mathcal{T}$, and a mutation probability $p$. The algorithm maintains a population of candidate rescale factor configurations, where each individual in the population is a complete set of $\lambda_i$ values for all RoPE dimensions along with a choice of $\hat{n}$. The fitness of each individual is measured by applying its rescale factors to the target LLM and computing the perplexity on the input samples $\mathbf{X}$.

The search proceeds iteratively. First, an initial population $\text{P}_0$ is generated using the optimized initialization procedure. Then, for each iteration $i$ from 1 to $\mathcal{T}$, the perplexity of each individual in the current population $\text{P}_{i-1}$ is computed. The top-$k$ individuals with the lowest perplexity are maintained as parents for the next generation. New candidate solutions are generated through two mechanisms: mutation, which perturbs the rescale factors of selected parents with probability $p$, and crossover, which combines rescale factors from pairs of parents. Both mutation and crossover are subject to the monotonically non-decreasing constraint, ensuring that generated solutions are viable. The new population $\text{P}_i$ is formed by combining the mutated individuals, the crossover individuals, and the retained top-$k$ individuals. After all iterations are complete, the individual with the lowest perplexity across all evaluated configurations is returned as the optimal solution.

The specific hyperparameters used in the experiments provide insight into the practical scale of the search. For target context window sizes within 256k, the algorithm uses a population size $P = 64$, mutation and crossover sizes $N_1 = N_2 = 16$, a mutation probability $p = 0.3$, and a maximum of $\mathcal{T} = 40$ iterations. The top-32 individuals are selected as parents in each iteration. Perplexity is computed using 5 random samples from the PG19 validation set, with a minimum length requirement matching the target context length. For target window sizes exceeding 512k, the population, mutation, and crossover sizes are halved to reduce computational cost, and perplexity is measured on 3 random samples from the Pile-Books3 validation set. This reduction is necessary because evaluating perplexity on extremely long sequences is time-consuming, and the search must remain tractable.

The choice of perplexity as the fitness function is deliberate. Perplexity directly measures the LLM's ability to predict the next token in a sequence, which is the fundamental capability that context window extension seeks to preserve. A lower perplexity on long documents indicates that the model can effectively leverage extended context to make better predictions. By using perplexity on documents that exceed the target context length, the search ensures that the discovered rescale factors genuinely enable the model to process long sequences, rather than merely fitting to a specific evaluation metric.

The evolutionary search is particularly appropriate for this problem because the relationship between rescale factors and perplexity is highly non-linear and non-convex. Small changes in a single dimension's rescale factor can interact with changes in other dimensions in complex ways, and the optimal configuration may lie far from any of the existing rule-based solutions. The evolutionary approach, with its combination of exploitation (through top-$k$ selection) and exploration (through mutation and crossover), is designed to navigate such landscapes effectively.

## Optimized Initial Population Generation Using PI, NTK, and YaRN

A critical factor in the efficiency of evolutionary search is the quality of the initial population. If the initial population is generated purely at random, many individuals may correspond to poor positional interpolation strategies, wasting valuable perplexity computations on unpromising candidates. Since each perplexity evaluation requires running the LLM on long sequences—a computationally expensive operation—reducing the number of wasted evaluations is essential for practical applicability.

LongRoPE addresses this by seeding the initial population with known good solutions. Specifically, the RoPE rescale factor configurations corresponding to three existing methods—PI, NTK, and YaRN—are included as individuals in the initial population. These three methods represent the state of the art in positional interpolation and each embodies a different philosophy: PI uses uniform linear interpolation, NTK applies frequency-aware non-uniform scaling based on Neural Tangent Kernel theory, and YaRN combines extrapolation, NTK, and linear interpolation across three frequency-based dimension groups. By including all three, the initial population spans a diverse range of interpolation strategies, providing the evolutionary search with multiple high-quality starting points.

For the remaining $P - 3$ individuals in the initial population, the algorithm generates them by randomly mutating the three seed configurations with probability $p$. This mutation-based generation ensures that the rest of the initial population is concentrated in the neighborhood of known good solutions, rather than being scattered uniformly across the entire search space. The mutation probability $p = 0.3$ means that, on average, 30% of the rescale factors in each generated individual differ from their parent seed, providing a balance between proximity to known solutions and exploration of new configurations.

This initialization strategy is analogous to warm-starting an optimization algorithm. Instead of beginning the search from scratch, the algorithm begins from the best that current methods have to offer and then iteratively improves upon them. This is particularly important for large extension ratios, where the search space is vast and random initialization would likely produce configurations with extremely high perplexity, providing little signal to guide the evolutionary process.

The inclusion of all three methods—PI, NTK, and YaRN—also ensures diversity in the initial gene pool. Since these methods represent qualitatively different approaches to positional interpolation, their rescale factor configurations occupy different regions of the search space. Mutation and crossover operations on these diverse seeds can produce offspring that combine the strengths of multiple approaches, potentially discovering hybrid strategies that outperform any single method. For example, crossover between an NTK individual and a YaRN individual might produce a configuration that retains NTK's smooth frequency-based scaling in some dimensions while adopting YaRN's aggressive extrapolation in others.

## Monotonically Non-decreasing Constraint to Prune Poor Solutions and Reduce Search Costs

Even with optimized initialization, the evolutionary search can generate candidate solutions that are unlikely to perform well. In a search space as large as this one, naive mutation and crossover can produce configurations that are fundamentally unsound from the perspective of positional encoding theory. Evaluating the perplexity of such configurations wastes computational resources, particularly when the target context length $L'$ is large and each perplexity computation requires processing long sequences through the LLM.

To address this, LongRoPE imposes a monotonically non-decreasing constraint on the rescale factors: $\lambda_i \leq \lambda_{i+1}$ for all consecutive RoPE dimensions $i$. Only candidate configurations that satisfy this constraint are actually evaluated by the LLM for perplexity. Configurations that violate the constraint are pruned before the expensive perplexity computation, significantly reducing the search cost.

The theoretical basis for this constraint comes from the NTK theory, which relates the frequency of RoPE dimensions to their interpolation requirements. In RoPE, lower dimensions (smaller $i$) correspond to higher frequencies, meaning their rotary angles change more rapidly with position. These high-frequency dimensions encode fine-grained positional information and are more sensitive to interpolation, which compresses the angular differences between nearby positions. Higher dimensions (larger $i$) correspond to lower frequencies, encoding coarser positional information that is more amenable to interpolation. The NTK theory therefore suggests that lower dimensions should receive less interpolation (smaller $\lambda_i$) and higher dimensions should receive more interpolation (larger $\lambda_i$), which is precisely what the monotonically non-decreasing constraint enforces.

This constraint serves as a domain-specific prior that dramatically reduces the effective search space. Without the constraint, the number of valid configurations for each dimension's $\lambda_i$ is independent, leading to the exponential growth described earlier. With the constraint, the values of $\lambda_i$ are partially ordered, meaning that once $\lambda_i$ is chosen, $\lambda_{i+1}$ is restricted to values greater than or equal to $\lambda_i$. This ordering eliminates a vast number of configurations that would assign more interpolation to high-frequency dimensions and less to low-frequency dimensions—a pattern that contradicts the theoretical understanding of how RoPE dimensions should be scaled.

The practical impact of this constraint is substantial. By filtering out configurations that violate the monotonicity prior before they are evaluated, the algorithm avoids spending computational resources on solutions that are theoretically unlikely to perform well. This is especially important for large target context lengths, where each perplexity evaluation may require processing sequences of hundreds of thousands or even millions of tokens through the LLM. The time required for a single such evaluation can be significant, making the pruning of poor candidates essential for maintaining reasonable search times.

The constraint is applied both to mutated individuals and to crossover individuals. When a mutation operation produces a configuration that violates $\lambda_i \leq \lambda_{i+1}$, the configuration is either discarded or repaired before evaluation. Similarly, crossover operations that combine rescale factors from two parents are checked for monotonicity, and only offspring that satisfy the constraint are evaluated. This ensures that every perplexity computation performed during the search is on a theoretically sound candidate, maximizing the information gained per unit of computational cost.

It is worth noting that the constraint does not eliminate all sub-optimal solutions—it only eliminates those that are sub-optimal in a specific, theory-guided way. There may still be configurations that satisfy the monotonicity constraint but perform poorly for other reasons. The evolutionary search, guided by perplexity, is responsible for navigating among these remaining candidates to find the best configuration. The constraint and the evolutionary search are thus complementary: the constraint reduces the search space based on theoretical knowledge, while the evolutionary search explores the remaining space based on empirical performance.

## Achieving 8x Context Window Extension Without Fine-tuning

One of the most striking capabilities of the LongRoPE search algorithm is its ability to extend an LLM's context window by up to 8 times without any fine-tuning. This is a significant advancement over existing methods, which typically require fine-tuning to achieve even moderate extensions. PI, for instance, necessitates fine-tuning for any extension beyond the pre-trained context window, and NTK-aware methods, while capable of non-fine-tuning extension, are usually limited to a maximum extension ratio of approximately 4 times. YaRN, despite its sophisticated grouping strategy, has been observed to underperform even PI and NTK on certain datasets when applied without fine-tuning, as its perplexity spikes after the target context window length for non-fine-tuned LLMs.

The key to LongRoPE's superior non-fine-tuning performance lies in the quality of the rescale factors discovered by the evolutionary search. By fully exploiting both forms of non-uniformity—dimensional and positional—the search identifies configurations that minimize the information loss caused by positional interpolation. The resulting positional embedding effectively preserves the original RoPE, especially in key dimensions and at critical token positions. This preservation is crucial because the pre-trained LLM has already learned to interpret specific rotary angle patterns; by minimizing disruption to these patterns, the model can generalize to longer sequences without requiring additional training to adapt to the modified positional encoding.

The empirical evidence for this capability is compelling. When applied to LLaMA2, which has an original context window of 4k tokens, LongRoPE's evolutionary search successfully extends the context window to 32k without any fine-tuning. This represents an 8-fold extension. In contrast, existing methods such as PI, NTK, and YaRN cause the perplexity to spike after only a 2-fold extension, indicating that the model's language modeling ability degrades rapidly beyond a modest increase in context length. The searched rescale factors enable the model to maintain low, stable perplexity across the entire 32k window, demonstrating that the model can effectively leverage the extended context to make accurate next-token predictions.

This 8-fold non-fine-tuning extension capability plays a pivotal role in LongRoPE's progressive extension strategy. The strategy for achieving a 2048k context window relies on first fine-tuning the model to 256k and then applying a second LongRoPE search to extend from 256k to 2048k without additional fine-tuning. The second extension represents an 8-fold increase (from 256k to 2048k), which is precisely within the range that the search algorithm can achieve without fine-tuning. This alignment between the search algorithm's non-fine-tuning extension capability and the progressive extension strategy's requirements is not coincidental—it is a direct consequence of the search's ability to discover rescale factors that minimize information loss, enabling the fine-tuned model to generalize to much longer contexts than it was trained on.

The ability to achieve substantial extension without fine-tuning has important practical implications. Fine-tuning on long texts requires both the availability of sufficiently long training data and substantial computational resources, including GPU time and memory. For context lengths exceeding 128k, suitable training data is scarce, and the computational cost of training on such long sequences is prohibitive. By enabling extension through search alone, LongRoPE provides a lightweight mechanism for pushing context boundaries that complements, rather than replaces, fine-tuning. Fine-tuning can be applied at moderate lengths (e.g., 256k) where data and resources are more readily available, and the search algorithm can then extend the fine-tuned model to extreme lengths (e.g., 2048k) without additional training.

In summary, the 8-fold non-fine-tuning extension is a direct consequence of the evolutionary search's ability to discover non-uniform rescale factors that preserve the informational content of the original RoPE. By considering both dimensional and positional non-uniformities and by efficiently navigating the vast search space through optimized initialization and the monotonicity constraint, LongRoPE identifies positional interpolation strategies that are fundamentally more effective than those designed by human intuition. This capability is a cornerstone of the method, enabling both standalone context extension and the progressive extension strategy that ultimately achieves the unprecedented 2048k context window.

# Progressive Extension to 2048K Tokens

The quest to extend large language model context windows to millions of tokens confronts a formidable wall of practical and theoretical obstacles. While modest extensions to around 128k tokens have been achieved through direct fine-tuning on longer texts, pushing beyond this frontier to 2048k tokens requires a fundamentally different strategy. This chapter details the progressive extension strategy employed by LongRoPE, a method that achieves an unprecedented 2048k context window through a sequence of carefully orchestrated steps, each building upon the last. We also examine the critical issue of recovering performance on shorter context windows after such extreme extension, a problem that arises as an inevitable consequence of aggressive positional interpolation.

# Challenges of Direct Fine-Tuning for 2048K Context Lengths

To appreciate the necessity of a progressive approach, one must first understand why direct fine-tuning to 2048k tokens is impractical. The challenges are multifaceted, spanning computational, data, and modeling dimensions.

The first and most immediate obstacle is the sheer computational cost. Fine-tuning a pre-trained LLM on sequences of 2048k tokens demands enormous GPU resources and extensive training hours. Even at the intermediate target of 256k tokens, the paper reports requiring 16 A100 GPUs for LLaMA2 fine-tuning. Scaling this to 2048k tokens would require resources that are, for most practitioners, prohibitively expensive. The computational complexity of the attention mechanism grows quadratically with sequence length, meaning that a sequence of 2048k tokens is approximately 64 times more expensive to process per token than one of 256k tokens. This makes direct fine-tuning at the target length largely infeasible.

The second challenge is the scarcity of training data. LongRoPE targets a context window of 2048k tokens, yet current datasets contain very few texts that exceed even 1000k tokens in length. The paper notes that long texts in current datasets, particularly those exceeding 1000k tokens, are limited. Without sufficient training data at the target length, fine-tuning cannot provide meaningful gradients to teach the model how to handle such extreme positions. This data scarcity problem is not merely a matter of collecting more text; genuinely long, coherent documents are rare, and synthetic data generation at such scales introduces its own complications.

The third challenge relates to the difficulty of convergence during fine-tuning under large extension ratios. When extending from a 4k context window to 2048k, the extension ratio is $512\times$, meaning that more than 90\% of the position indices in the extended window are new positions that the pre-trained model has never encountered. These untrained position indices introduce catastrophic values that lead to out-of-distribution issues, making fine-tuning difficult to converge. The paper explicitly states that, based on their experiences, it is challenging to well fine-tune the LLMs under a large extension ratio. The model's attention becomes dispersed as it is spread thinly across numerous token positions, further degrading the learning signal.

A fourth challenge emerges after extension: performance degradation on the original short context window. When positional interpolation is applied with a $512\times$ extension ratio, positions within the original 4k context window become particularly crowded. The interpolation forces position embeddings in higher dimensions to reside in a much narrower region than they were originally trained for, negatively affecting the language model's performance on tasks that operate within the original context window. This is a known issue of positional interpolation that becomes especially severe at extreme extension ratios.

Given these challenges, it becomes clear that a naive approach of searching for RoPE rescale factors at 2048k and directly fine-tuning is doomed to fail. Instead, LongRoPE adopts a progressive strategy that decomposes the problem into manageable stages, leveraging the method's unique capability to achieve $8\times$ extension without fine-tuning.

# Step 1: Extending Pre-Trained LLM to 256k with LongRoPE Search

The first stage of the progressive extension strategy involves using LongRoPE's evolutionary search algorithm to find optimal RoPE rescale factors for intermediate context window sizes of 128k and 256k tokens. This stage is critical because it establishes the positional embedding configuration that will serve as the foundation for subsequent fine-tuning.

The search is conducted on the pre-trained LLM, targeting context window sizes of 128k and 256k, corresponding to extension ratios of $32\times$ and $64\times$ respectively (from the original 4k context window of LLaMA2). The search algorithm explores the vast space of possible rescale factors $\lambda_i$ for each RoPE dimension $i$, along with the token position threshold $\hat{n}$ that controls how many initial tokens are exempt from interpolation. Each $\lambda_i$ is allowed to range from a minimum of 1.0 (representing direct extrapolation, meaning no interpolation is applied to that dimension) to a maximum of $s \times 1.25$ (representing slightly more aggressive interpolation than standard position interpolation), with a step size of 0.01, where $s$ is the target extension ratio. The token position threshold $\hat{n}$ is drawn from the set $\{0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 64, 128, 256\}$.

The evolutionary search employs an optimized initial population that includes the rescale factors corresponding to three existing methods—PI, NTK, and YaRN—as starting individuals, along with random mutations of these factors. The search is guided by perplexity measurements on validation samples from the PG19 dataset, using 5 random samples for target windows within 256k. The monotonically non-decreasing constraint $\lambda_i \leq \lambda_{i+1}$ is imposed to reduce the search space, grounded in the NTK theory's insight that lower dimensions with higher frequency require less interpolation while higher dimensions with lower frequency can tolerate more.

The search is conducted for both 128k and 256k targets because the fine-tuning strategy in the next step uses a two-phase approach that first leverages the 128k rescale factors and then transitions to the 256k factors. Having both sets of rescale factors ready allows for a smooth, staged fine-tuning process. The 128k search has an extension ratio of $32\times$, while the 256k search has an extension ratio of $64\times$, and the non-uniform rescale factors discovered by the search at each target length are specifically optimized for that length, capturing the dimension-specific and position-specific non-uniformities that minimize information loss in the positional embedding.

# Step 2: Efficient Fine-Tuning to the 256k Context Window

Once the optimal RoPE rescale factors for 128k and 256k have been identified, the next stage is to fine-tune the pre-trained LLM to achieve a functional 256k context window. The fine-tuning process is carefully designed to be efficient, requiring only 1,000 fine-tuning steps in total, which is remarkably few given the magnitude of the extension.

The fine-tuning is divided into two phases. In the first phase, the pre-trained LLaMA2 model is fine-tuned for 400 steps using the RoPE rescale factors searched for the 128k context window. The training data comes from the RedPajama dataset, chunked into 128k segments with beginning-of-sequence (BOS) and end-of-sequence (EOS) tokens bookending each segment. A learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-5}$ with linear decay is used, along with a global batch size of 32. This phase is trained on 8 A100 GPUs using a distributed training system.

In the second phase, the RoPE rescale factors are replaced with those searched for the 256k context window on the checkpoint from the first phase, and an additional 600 steps of fine-tuning are conducted. This phase requires 16 A100 GPUs due to the longer sequence lengths. The total of 1,000 fine-tuning steps (400 + 600) is significantly more efficient than attempting to directly fine-tune to 256k from the pre-trained model, which the paper notes would be more challenging and less effective.

This two-phase approach works because the 128k fine-tuning phase serves as a warm-up that helps the model gradually adapt to longer context windows. Starting with the 128k rescale factors, which correspond to a $32\times$ extension, allows the model to learn how to handle moderately extended positions before being confronted with the full $64\times$ extension of 256k. The transition from 128k to 256k rescale factors mid-training provides a smoother optimization trajectory than jumping directly to the $64\times$ extension, mirroring the progressive philosophy of the overall strategy.

For Mistral-7B, the fine-tuning follows a different configuration inspired by YaRN's settings. A constant learning rate of $1 \times 10^{-6}$ and a global batch size of 64 are used. Both the 128k and 256k models are fine-tuned for 400 steps on the Together Computer's Long-Data Collections using a 16k sequence length, trained on 4 A100 GPUs. Notably, the Mistral fine-tuning uses a training sequence length of 16k, which is shorter than the target context window, and this choice has implications for the model's ability to further extend its context window, as discussed in the results section.

The efficiency of this fine-tuning approach is a key advantage. By requiring only 1,000 steps at a maximum training length of 256k, the method avoids the need for direct fine-tuning on 2048k-length sequences, which would be computationally prohibitive and data-limited. The fine-tuned 256k model serves as the launchpad for the final extension to 2048k.

# Step 3: Secondary Search on the Fine-Tuned LLM to Achieve 2048K Context Without Further Fine-Tuning

The third and most innovative stage of the progressive extension strategy leverages LongRoPE's capability to achieve $8\times$ extension without fine-tuning. Having fine-tuned the LLM to a 256k context window, the method performs a secondary evolutionary search on the fine-tuned model to find new RoPE rescale factors that extend the context window from 256k to 2048k, an additional $8\times$ extension, without any further fine-tuning.

This stage is possible because LongRoPE's non-uniform positional interpolation has been demonstrated to achieve up to $8\times$ extension in non-fine-tuning scenarios. When applied to the original pre-trained LLM, this capability extends the context window from 4k to 32k without fine-tuning. The key insight is that this same capability applies to fine-tuned extended LLMs as well. The fine-tuned 256k model has already learned to handle positions up to 256k, and the secondary search finds rescale factors that interpolate the 2048k positions into a range that the fine-tuned model can effectively process.

The secondary search is conducted with adjusted parameters to account for the larger target context window. For target windows over 512k, the population size, mutation size, and crossover size are halved compared to the search settings used for windows within 256k. Specifically, while the within-256k search uses a population size of $P = 64$, mutation and crossover sizes of $N_1 = N_2 = 16$, a mutation probability of $p = 0.3$, and a maximum of $\mathcal{T} = 40$ iterations, the search for windows over 512k reduces these sizes to maintain computational efficiency. Perplexity for the secondary search is measured on 3 random samples from the Pile-Books3 validation set, which contains texts long enough to evaluate at the 2048k scale.

The resulting extension ratio from the original 4k context window is $512\times$, achieved through a composition of two stages: a $64\times$ extension via fine-tuning to 256k, followed by an $8\times$ extension via the secondary search without fine-tuning. This composition is what makes the 2048k context window tractable; it decomposes an infeasible $512\times$ direct extension into a feasible $64\times$ fine-tuning extension and an $8\times$ search-only extension.

The paper presents two variants of the 2048k model: LongRoPE-2048k (ft=128k) and LongRoPE-2048k (ft=256k), corresponding to models fine-tuned to 128k and 256k respectively before the secondary search. For LLaMA2, the variant fine-tuned at 256k performs better at 2048k because the secondary extension ratio is smaller ($8\times$ from 256k to 2048k) compared to the variant fine-tuned at 128k ($16\times$ from 128k to 2048k). A smaller secondary extension ratio means less aggressive interpolation is required, preserving more of the original positional information and resulting in better performance. For Mistral, however, the variant fine-tuned at 128k performs better, a result the paper attributes to the use of 16k training sequence length during Mistral's fine-tuning, which affects the model's ability to further extend its context window after fine-tuning.

The ablation study confirms the effectiveness of this secondary search. When extending the fine-tuned LLaMA2-256k model to 512k, 1024k, and 2048k using PI and YaRN instead of LongRoPE's non-uniform search, the perplexity under PI and YaRN quickly increases with the extension ratio. In contrast, LongRoPE's non-uniform positional interpolation sustains a consistent level of perplexity, demonstrating that the search-based approach to finding dimension-specific and position-specific rescale factors is essential for maintaining performance at extreme context lengths.

# Shorter Context Window Recovery to Mitigate Performance Degradation on Original 4k/8k Lengths

A critical and often overlooked consequence of extreme context window extension is the degradation of performance on the original, shorter context window. After extending to 2048k, the model's performance on tasks that operate within the original 4k or 8k context window can drop significantly. This degradation occurs because positional interpolation with a $512\times$ extension ratio forces the position embeddings within the original context window into a much narrower region than they were originally trained for, effectively crowding the positions and making it harder for the model to distinguish between closely positioned tokens.

The paper explains this phenomenon as a known issue of positional interpolation. When the rescale factors are set to accommodate 2048k positions, the positions within the original 4k window become particularly crowded because the interpolation compresses the entire 2048k range into the model's pre-trained positional space. This crowding is especially problematic for higher RoPE dimensions, which encode coarser positional information and are more sensitive to the compression. The result is that the model's language modeling ability on short sequences degrades, which is problematic because many practical tasks, including standard benchmarks, operate within the original 4k context window.

To mitigate this issue, LongRoPE introduces a shorter context window recovery mechanism. The key idea is to perform an additional evolutionary search on the extended LLM to adjust the RoPE rescale factors specifically for short context lengths, such as 4k and 8k. The search algorithm is the same evolutionary search used for the main extension, but with a crucial modification: the maximum allowed searched $\lambda$ values are reduced. This reduction encourages less positional interpolation at shorter lengths, which is appropriate because shorter sequences require far less compression of position indices than the full 2048k extension would impose.

The recovery search produces a separate set of RoPE rescale factors optimized for short context lengths. During inference, the LLM dynamically adjusts which set of rescale factors to use based on the actual sequence length. When the input sequence length is less than 8k, the model switches to the short-context rescale factors, which apply less interpolation and thus preserve the original positional embedding structure that the pre-trained model learned. For longer sequences, the 2048k rescale factors are used to enable the extended context window.

The ablation study demonstrates the effectiveness of this recovery mechanism. Comparing the perplexity of LongRoPE-LLaMA2-2048k on the Proof-pile dataset at 4k and 8k lengths, with and without the recovery search, the results show a significant performance improvement at short context lengths when the recovery factors are applied. The average LLM benchmark accuracy also improves, confirming that the recovery mechanism restores the model's ability to perform well on standard tasks designed for the original context window.

This dynamic switching mechanism is a practical and elegant solution to the tension between long and short context performance. Rather than forcing a single set of rescale factors to serve both extreme lengths, the model maintains two sets of factors and selects the appropriate one based on the input. This approach allows LongRoPE-2048k models to achieve comparable results on standard benchmarks within the original 4096 context window, including tasks such as ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, MMLU, and TruthfulQA, while simultaneously enabling the model to process sequences up to 2048k tokens. The paper reports that LongRoPE-2048k models even outperform the original Mistral on TruthfulQA by $+0.5\%$, demonstrating that the recovery mechanism effectively preserves, and in some cases enhances, short-context capabilities.

In summary, the progressive extension strategy of LongRoPE addresses the multifaceted challenges of extending LLM context windows to 2048k tokens through a carefully designed sequence of steps. The strategy begins with an evolutionary search for RoPE rescale factors at intermediate lengths (128k and 256k), proceeds with efficient two-phase fine-tuning to 256k, and culminates in a secondary search that leverages the $8\times$ non-fine-tuning extension capability to reach 2048k. The shorter context window recovery mechanism ensures that the extreme extension does not come at the cost of performance on the original context window, making the resulting models practical for both long-context and short-context applications.

# Experimental Evaluation and Results

# Evaluation Setup: Models, Fine-Tuning, and Baselines

The experimental validation of LongRoPE is designed to rigorously assess whether the method can truly extend the context window of pre-trained large language models (LLMs) to an unprecedented 2048k tokens while preserving performance on shorter sequences. To this end, the authors apply LongRoPE to two well-known open-source LLMs: LLaMA2-7B and Mistral-7B. These models are chosen because they both rely on Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE), which is the positional encoding scheme that LongRoPE modifies. By testing on two different architectures, the authors can demonstrate that LongRoPE is not specific to a single model family but is broadly applicable to any RoPE-based LLM. Both models originally have relatively short context windows — LLaMA2 has a 4096-token limit, and Mistral also operates within a comparable range — making them ideal candidates for testing dramatic context window extensions.

The fine-tuning procedure differs slightly between the two models, reflecting their distinct training characteristics and the authors' desire to build upon established practices in the literature. For LLaMA2, the fine-tuning uses a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-5}$ with linear decay and a global batch size of 32. The training data comes from the RedPajama dataset, which is chunked into 128k-token segments, each bookended with beginning-of-sequence (BOS) and end-of-sequence (EOS) tokens. The fine-tuning is performed in two stages: first, 400 steps are completed to reach a 128k context window, and then, starting from the checkpoint produced after those 400 steps, an additional 600 steps are conducted with the RoPE rescale factors updated to target 256k. This two-stage approach proves more effective than directly fine-tuning to 256k in one shot. The 128k training requires 8 A100 GPUs using a distributed training system, while the 256k stage requires 16 A100 GPUs, reflecting the increased memory and computational demands of longer sequences.

For Mistral, the authors follow the fine-tuning setup established by YaRN, a prior context extension method. Specifically, a constant learning rate of $1 \times 10^{-6}$ and a global batch size of 64 are used. Both the 128k and 256k models are trained for 400 steps on the Together Computer's Long-Data Collections using a 16k sequence length. This setup uses 4 A100 GPUs. The choice to use a 16k training length — significantly shorter than the target context windows — is inherited from YaRN's protocol and has important implications for the results, as we will discuss later in the chapter.

The search for optimal RoPE rescale factors is a critical component of LongRoPE, and its configuration is tailored to the target context window size. For target window sizes up to 256k, the evolutionary search uses a population size $P = 64$, a mutation size $N_1 = 16$, a crossover size $N_2 = 16$, a mutation probability $p = 0.3$, and a maximum of $\mathcal{T} = 40$ iterations. In each iteration, the top-32 individuals (those with the lowest perplexity) are selected as parents for mutation and crossover. The perplexity that guides the search is calculated using 5 random samples from the PG19 validation set, with each sample required to be at least as long as the target context length. For context windows exceeding 512k, the population, mutation, and crossover sizes are halved to manage computational costs, and perplexity is measured on 3 random samples from the Pile-Books3 validation set instead.

The baselines against which LongRoPE is compared represent the state of the art in context window extension. Because reaching 2048k requires fine-tuned models with intermediate context windows, the authors produce two variants: LongRoPE-2048k (ft=128k), which is fine-tuned to 128k before the second search extends it to 2048k, and LongRoPE-2048k (ft=256k), which is fine-tuned to 256k before the second search. These are compared against several open-sourced LLMs that have been fine-tuned after applying other positional interpolation methods, including PI (Position Interpolation), NTK, and YaRN. The specific baselines include Together-32k, Code LLaMA, LongLoRA-full-FT-100k, YaRN-LLaMA, and YaRN-Mistral. This diverse set of baselines ensures that LongRoPE is evaluated against the best available alternatives across different extension ratios and model families.

# Performance on Long Sequence Language Modeling Within 256k Evaluation Lengths

The first major set of results evaluates how well LongRoPE-extended models perform on long sequence language modeling tasks within a 256k evaluation length. This evaluation serves as a foundational test: if a model's context window has been genuinely extended, it should be able to leverage longer context to produce better predictions, which manifests as decreasing perplexity as the evaluation length increases. Perplexity, for those unfamiliar, is a standard measure of language model quality that quantifies how well a model predicts the next token in a sequence; lower perplexity indicates better predictive performance. The authors use a sliding window of 256 tokens for evaluation, which means perplexity is computed over windows of 256 tokens that slide across the document, allowing assessment at various context lengths.

Two datasets are used to demonstrate the generalizability of the results: Proof-pile and PG19. Proof-pile is a dataset of mathematical and scientific texts, while PG19 is a collection of long literary texts from Project Gutenberg books. For PG19, the entire test split of 100 documents is used. For Proof-pile, the authors follow YaRN's protocol and randomly select 10 samples, each with at least 128k tokens in length. The use of two distinct datasets — one mathematical and one literary — helps ensure that any observed improvements are not artifacts of a particular domain.

The results on both datasets reveal two key observations. First, the LongRoPE-extended models show an overall decreasing perplexity trend from 4k to 256k evaluation lengths. This is a crucial finding because it directly demonstrates that the models can effectively utilize longer context to improve their language modeling performance. A model whose context window has not been genuinely extended would show perplexity that plateaus or increases beyond its original training length, as it would encounter out-of-distribution position indices. The decreasing perplexity trend confirms that LongRoPE successfully extends the model's effective context window well beyond its original 4k limit.

Second, even with a context window that is 16 times longer than the baselines — a condition that is typically challenging for maintaining performance at shorter lengths — the LongRoPE-2048k models outperform state-of-the-art baselines within the 256k context length. This is a particularly notable result because one might expect that a model optimized for extremely long contexts (2048k) would sacrifice performance at shorter lengths. The fact that LongRoPE-2048k models maintain superiority even at shorter lengths speaks to the effectiveness of the non-uniform positional interpolation, which preserves key information in the original RoPE embeddings rather than uniformly compressing all position information. The comparison with YaRN is especially instructive: YaRN, which also uses non-uniform interpolation but relies on human-designed grouping rules, underperforms relative to PI and NTK on PG19 in non-fine-tuned settings because it does not reach the target context window length. For example, YaRN's perplexity spikes after 7k in an 8k context size, indicating that its fixed grouping strategy is sub-optimal. LongRoPE's evolutionary search, by contrast, discovers rescale factors that are specifically optimized for each model and target length, avoiding such failure modes.

# Perplexity Evaluation on Extremely Long Sequences Beyond 2000k Tokens

While the 256k evaluation demonstrates LongRoPE's effectiveness at moderately long lengths, the true test of the method is whether it can maintain language modeling quality at extremely long context lengths — specifically, beyond 2048k tokens. This is the regime that no prior method has successfully achieved, and it presents unique challenges including attention dispersion (where attention is spread thinly across millions of token positions) and the scarcity of training data at such lengths.

To evaluate performance on extremely long documents, the authors use the Books3 dataset, which is derived from a collection of full-length books. For evaluation efficiency, 20 books are randomly selected, each exceeding 2048k tokens in length. The sliding window for evaluation is set to 256k, meaning perplexity is computed over 256k-token windows that slide across each book. This setup allows the authors to measure perplexity at various points within extremely long documents and observe how the models perform as the context length approaches and exceeds 2 million tokens.

The results on Books3 reveal that LongRoPE successfully extends both LLaMA2-7B and Mistral-7B's context windows to 2048k while achieving perplexity that is comparable to or superior to baselines within shorter lengths of 8k to 128k. This is a remarkable achievement: the LongRoPE models not only handle extremely long contexts but also remain competitive at shorter lengths, demonstrating the versatility of the approach. However, notable performance differences emerge between the 2048k LLaMA2 and Mistral models.

For LLaMA2, the performance aligns with expectations: perplexity decreases gracefully with longer contexts, with only marginal increases at 1024k and 2048k. This graceful degradation is important because it indicates that the model's language modeling quality does not catastrophically fail even at extreme lengths. Furthermore, LongRoPE-2048k performs better when fine-tuned at 256k rather than 128k. This is because the secondary extension ratio — the factor by which the context window is extended from the fine-tuned length to 2048k — is smaller when starting from 256k (i.e., $2048/256 = 8\times$) compared to starting from 128k (i.e., $2048/128 = 16\times$). A smaller secondary extension ratio means less aggressive interpolation is needed, which better preserves the positional information and results in lower perplexity.

For Mistral, the situation is more complex. Mistral outperforms baselines at shorter lengths, but its perplexity exceeds 7 beyond 256k, indicating some degradation at the longest lengths. Additionally, Mistral performs better at a fine-tuning window size of 128k rather than 256k, which is the opposite of LLaMA2's pattern. The authors attribute this to the fine-tuning protocol: for Mistral's 128k and 256k fine-tuning, the YaRN setting is followed, which uses a 16k training length. This shorter training length affects Mistral's ability to further extend its context window after fine-tuning, because the model has not been exposed to sufficiently long sequences during fine-tuning to develop robust representations for long-range dependencies. This finding underscores the importance of fine-tuning configuration: the training length, learning rate, and number of steps all interact with the positional interpolation strategy to determine the final model's performance at extreme context lengths.

# Passkey Retrieval Accuracy Across Millions of Tokens

While perplexity is a valuable metric for assessing language modeling quality, it does not directly measure a model's ability to perform specific tasks that require retrieving information from long contexts. To address this, the authors evaluate LongRoPE on a synthetic passkey retrieval task, which provides a more direct measure of the effective context window in generative tasks.

In the passkey retrieval task, a model is given a long document containing a sea of irrelevant text, within which a random five-digit passkey is hidden at some position. The model is then asked to retrieve the passkey. This task is specifically designed to test whether the model can attend to and recall information from arbitrary positions within a very long context. If a model's effective context window is genuinely 2048k tokens, it should be able to retrieve a passkey regardless of where it is placed within that window.

The evaluation procedure involves 10 iterations of the passkey retrieval task, with the passkey placed at a random location uniformly distributed across the evaluation context length. This random placement is important because it ensures that the model must be able to attend to any position within the context, not just positions near the beginning or end. The use of 10 iterations provides a reasonable estimate of the retrieval accuracy while keeping the evaluation computationally feasible.

The results of the passkey retrieval experiment are striking. Existing models — those extended using PI, NTK, or YaRN — show retrieval accuracy that rapidly drops to 0 beyond 128k. This means that these models, despite being fine-tuned for longer contexts, cannot reliably retrieve information from positions beyond approximately 128k tokens. This limitation reflects the fundamental constraints of existing positional interpolation methods: they either introduce too much information loss through uniform interpolation (as in PI) or rely on sub-optimal human-designed rules for non-uniform interpolation (as in YaRN).

In contrast, LongRoPE-LLaMA2-2048k (ft=256k) maintains a retrieval accuracy of $\geq 90\%$ from 4k all the way to 2048k. This means that even when the passkey is hidden among over 2 million tokens of irrelevant text, the model can still find it with high reliability. This is a direct demonstration that the effective context window of the model has been genuinely extended to 2048k tokens, not just that the model produces low perplexity at that length. The retrieval task requires the model to form precise attention to specific positions within the context, which is only possible if the positional embeddings at those positions are meaningful and distinguishable.

LongRoPE-Mistral-2048k (ft=128k) achieves 100% accuracy up to 1800k, dropping to 60% at 2048k. This slight degradation at the extreme end aligns with the perplexity results on Books3, where Mistral's perplexity was observed to increase beyond 256k. The 60% accuracy at 2048k, while not perfect, still represents a substantial achievement: the model can retrieve a passkey from 2 million tokens of text more than half the time, far exceeding any existing method. The difference between LLaMA2 and Mistral at 2048k again reflects the different fine-tuning configurations, with LLaMA2 benefiting from the larger fine-tuning length of 256k, which results in a smaller secondary extension ratio.

The passkey retrieval results are perhaps the most compelling evidence of LongRoPE's effectiveness because they directly measure the model's functional ability to use information from extremely long contexts. A model might achieve low perplexity on long documents simply by learning broad statistical patterns, without truly attending to specific positions. The passkey task eliminates this possibility by requiring precise retrieval from specific positions, providing a clean test of the effective context window.

# Performance on Standard LLM Benchmarks Within the Original 4096 Context Window

A critical concern when extending an LLM's context window is whether the extension degrades performance on the original, shorter context window for which the model was initially trained. This concern is well-founded: positional interpolation methods compress position embeddings into a narrower range, which can make positions within the original context window crowded and degrade the model's ability to distinguish between closely positioned tokens. With a 512$\times$ extension ratio (from 4k to 2048k), this crowding effect becomes particularly severe, as positions that were originally spread across the full range of RoPE angles are compressed into a tiny fraction of that range.

To assess whether LongRoPE suffers from this degradation, the authors evaluate the LongRoPE-2048k models on the Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard, which comprises standard LLM benchmarks designed for evaluation within short context windows. These benchmarks include 25-shot ARC-Challenge (a science question-answering task), 10-shot HellaSwag (a commonsense reasoning task), 5-shot MMLU (a multitask language understanding benchmark), and 0-shot TruthfulQA (a benchmark measuring whether a model avoids generating false statements). These benchmarks are evaluated in zero-shot and few-shot settings, meaning the models receive either no examples or a small number of examples in their prompts, all of which fit well within the original 4096-token context window.

The results demonstrate that the LongRoPE-2048k models achieve comparable results on these standard benchmarks, matching the performance of models that have not been extended. Remarkably, LongRoPE-Mistral-2048k even outperforms the original Mistral on TruthfulQA by $+0.5\%$, suggesting that the non-uniform positional interpolation, rather than degrading the model, may in some cases slightly improve its ability to generate truthful responses. This is an encouraging finding that speaks to the preservation of key information in the RoPE embeddings: by using evolutionary search to find rescale factors that preserve important dimensions and token positions, LongRoPE avoids the information loss that plagues uniform interpolation methods.

For LongRoPE-LLaMA2-2048k, the model fine-tuned at 256k shows slightly more performance degradation than the one fine-tuned at 128k, but the degradation remains within reasonable ranges for most tasks. This slight degradation is consistent with the intuition that a larger fine-tuning length introduces more interpolation, which can affect the model's behavior at short lengths. However, the authors' short context window recovery strategy — which involves performing an additional evolutionary search to adjust RoPE rescale factors specifically for 4k and 8k lengths — helps mitigate this degradation. During inference, if the sequence length is less than 8k, the model dynamically switches to these specially searched rescale factors, which encourage less positional interpolation and thus reduce the crowding effect.

The effectiveness of this recovery strategy is confirmed through ablation experiments. The authors compare the perplexity of LongRoPE-LLaMA2-2048k on Proof-pile at 4k and 8k lengths, along with the average LLM benchmark accuracy, both with and without the short context recovery. The results show a significant performance improvement at short context lengths when the recovery strategy is applied. This demonstrates that the performance degradation at short lengths is not an inherent limitation of context window extension but rather a consequence of using a single set of rescale factors across all lengths. By dynamically adjusting the rescale factors based on the actual sequence length, LongRoPE achieves the best of both worlds: excellent performance at extremely long lengths and maintained performance at short lengths.

In summary, the experimental evaluation comprehensively demonstrates LongRoPE's effectiveness across multiple dimensions. The perplexity results on PG19, Proof-pile, and Books3 show that the method extends the effective context window from 4k to 2048k while maintaining language modeling quality. The passkey retrieval results provide direct evidence that the extended context window is functionally useful, enabling precise information retrieval from millions of tokens. And the standard benchmark results confirm that this massive context extension does not come at the cost of performance on shorter, more common tasks. Together, these results establish LongRoPE as a significant advance in the field of context window extension for large language models.

# Ablation Studies and Related Works

In this chapter, we turn our attention to two critical aspects of the LongRoPE method: ablation studies that dissect and validate the contribution of each individual component, and a discussion of related works that situates LongRoPE within the broader landscape of context window extension techniques. Ablation studies are a cornerstone of rigorous machine learning research, as they allow us to attribute performance gains to specific design choices and rule out the possibility that improvements are merely artifacts of combined modifications. By systematically removing or substituting components, we can build a clear causal picture of what drives the method's effectiveness. Following this internal validation, we contextualize LongRoPE against alternative paradigms—retrieval-based, attention-based, and fine-tuning-based approaches—to understand its unique advantages and limitations relative to the field.

#

### Effectiveness of the Second Positional Interpolation in the Progressive Extension Strategy

One of the most distinctive features of LongRoPE is its progressive extension strategy, which avoids the need to directly fine-tune on extremely long texts (e.g., 2048k tokens) by instead fine-tuning on a more manageable 256k length and then applying a second round of non-uniform positional interpolation. A natural question arises: is this second positional interpolation actually necessary, or could a simpler method such as standard Position Interpolation (PI) or YaRN achieve the same result when applied on top of the fine-tuned 256k model? To answer this, the authors conduct a carefully controlled ablation experiment using their fine-tuned LLaMA2-256k model as the starting point. From this 256k-capable model, they extend the context window to 512k, 1024k, and 2048k using three different approaches: LongRoPE's evolutionary search-based non-uniform positional interpolation, PI, and YaRN.

The results are striking and underscore the importance of using the non-uniform search for the second interpolation step. When LongRoPE's search algorithm is used, the perplexity remains at a consistent, low level across all three extended lengths (512k, 1024k, and 2048k). This stability indicates that the model continues to effectively model language even as the context window grows to extreme lengths. In contrast, when PI is used for the second interpolation, perplexity increases rapidly and dramatically as the extension ratio grows from $2\times$ (256k to 512k) to $8\times$ (256k to 2048k). The same degradation, though somewhat less severe, is observed with YaRN.

The intuition behind this result ties directly back to the core motivation of LongRoPE. PI applies a uniform linear scaling across all RoPE dimensions, which means it does not account for the fact that different dimensions encode positional information at different frequencies. When the extension ratio is large—as is the case when going from 256k to 2048k, an $8\times$ increase—this uniform scaling causes severe information loss in the dimensions that are most critical for distinguishing nearby token positions. The result is that the model struggles to differentiate tokens that are close together, leading to higher perplexity. YaRN improves upon PI by grouping dimensions into frequency bands and applying different strategies to each band, but its grouping relies on human-designed rules that may not be optimal for the specific model and extension ratio at hand. LongRoPE's evolutionary search, by contrast, discovers dimension-specific rescale factors that are tailored to the model and the target length, preserving the most important positional information and thus maintaining low perplexity even at extreme extension ratios.

This ablation also validates a key architectural decision in LongRoPE: the observation that the non-uniform positional interpolation is effective not only on the original pre-trained LLM but also on an already fine-tuned extended LLM. This dual applicability is what makes the progressive strategy viable. If the search only worked well on pre-trained models, the second interpolation step would fail, and the method would be unable to reach 2048k without prohibitively expensive fine-tuning on million-token texts. The fact that the search generalizes to fine-tuned models demonstrates a form of robustness: the non-uniformities in RoPE that the search exploits are intrinsic to the positional encoding scheme and persist regardless of whether the model has been fine-tuned for longer contexts.

#

### Effectiveness of Shorter Context Length Recovery on Standard Benchmarks

A well-known side effect of extending an LLM's context window through positional interpolation is that performance on the original, shorter context window tends to degrade. This occurs because interpolation compresses the positional embeddings into a narrower range. When the extension ratio is large—such as the $512\times$ ratio involved in going from 4k to 2048k—the positions within the original 4k context window become extremely crowded. In effect, tokens that were originally spread across a wide range of positional encodings are now squeezed into a tiny fraction of that range, making it harder for the model to distinguish between them. This degradation is particularly problematic because many real-world tasks, including standard LLM benchmarks, operate within the original 4k or 8k context window.

To address this issue, LongRoPE introduces a shorter context window recovery mechanism. The idea is elegantly simple: after extending the model to 2048k, the authors perform an additional evolutionary search on the extended LLM to find a separate set of RoPE rescale factors optimized specifically for short context lengths (4k and 8k). Crucially, this search is constrained differently from the search used for long contexts. The maximum allowable rescale factor $\lambda$ is reduced, which encourages less interpolation—i.e., the positional embeddings for short contexts are kept closer to their original, un-interpolated form. During inference, the LLM dynamically switches between the long-context and short-context RoPE rescale factors based on the actual sequence length. If the input sequence is shorter than 8k tokens, the model uses the short-context factors; otherwise, it uses the long-context factors.

The ablation results demonstrate the effectiveness of this recovery mechanism. The authors compare the perplexity of LongRoPE-LLaMA2-2048k on the Proof-pile dataset at 4k and 8k lengths, both with and without the recovery adjustment. They also report the average accuracy on standard LLM benchmarks. The results show a significant improvement in perplexity at short context lengths when the recovery mechanism is applied, confirming that the readjusted RoPE factors successfully counteract the crowding effect caused by the extreme extension ratio.

This finding has important practical implications. In real-world deployment, an LLM will encounter inputs of varying lengths—some short, some extremely long. A method that extends the context window at the cost of destroying performance on short inputs would be of limited utility. The recovery mechanism ensures that LongRoPE-2048k models remain competent across the full spectrum of input lengths, from 4k to 2048k, without requiring separate models for different length regimes. The dynamic switching of RoPE factors during inference is a lightweight operation that adds negligible computational overhead, making this approach practical for production systems.

The broader lesson here is that context window extension should not be treated as a one-way operation. Just as we scale up the context window, we must also be prepared to scale back down and recover the model's original capabilities. The asymmetry between the long-context and short-context RoPE factors reflects a fundamental insight: the optimal positional encoding strategy depends on the length of the input, and a single set of factors cannot simultaneously serve both extremes.

### Ablation Analysis on the Two Forms of Non-uniformities (RoPE Dimensions vs. Token Positions)

A central claim of LongRoPE is that effective positional interpolation must account for two distinct forms of non-uniformity: (1) variation across RoPE dimensions, and (2) variation across token positions, specifically the observation that initial tokens benefit from less interpolation. To validate this claim, the authors design a two-part ablation study that isolates the contribution of each non-uniformity.

In the first experiment, they extend LLaMA2-7B from its original 4k context window to 16k and 32k using three different approaches: standard PI (which applies uniform linear interpolation and thus ignores both non-uniformities), searching for RoPE dimension non-uniformity only (optimizing $\hat{\lambda}_i$ for each dimension while setting $\hat{n} = 0$, meaning all token positions are interpolated), and searching for both non-uniformities simultaneously (optimizing both $\hat{\lambda}_i$ and $\hat{n}$). The perplexity is evaluated without any fine-tuning, providing a clean measure of how well each interpolation strategy preserves the model's language modeling ability.

The results reveal that non-uniformity in RoPE dimensions is the dominant contributor to performance improvement. Searching for dimension-specific rescale factors alone yields a substantial reduction in perplexity compared to PI's uniform linear interpolation. This confirms the core hypothesis that different RoPE dimensions encode positional information at different frequencies and that a one-size-fits-all scaling factor inevitably over-interpolates some dimensions and under-interpolates others. By allowing each dimension to have its own rescale factor, the search can preserve the critical high-frequency dimensions that are essential for distinguishing nearby tokens while applying more aggressive interpolation to low-frequency dimensions that can tolerate it.

Adding the token position non-uniformity (i.e., searching for $\hat{n}$ in addition to $\hat{\lambda}_i$) provides a further, though smaller, improvement at the 16k and 32k lengths. This validates the finding that initial tokens—which receive large attention scores and are therefore disproportionately important to the attention mechanism—should be extrapolated rather than interpolated. By keeping the first $\hat{n}$ tokens at their original RoPE positions, the model retains the positional information that matters most for its attention computation, leading to better performance.

In the second experiment, the authors extend their fine-tuned 256k-length LLaMA2 to 2048k using the same three approaches. Here, the picture changes somewhat. The dimension non-uniformity continues to provide a significant improvement over PI, reaffirming its importance regardless of the extension ratio. However, the token position non-uniformity does not show the same benefit at 2048k. The authors hypothesize that at extremely long lengths, preserving only the initial tokens without interpolation becomes less useful. The intuition is that when the context window spans over 2 million tokens, the relative importance of any single token position—even an initial one—is diluted across the vast sequence. The attention mechanism, faced with an enormous number of positions, distributes its focus more evenly, and the special status of initial tokens diminishes.

This finding is itself an important scientific contribution. It suggests that the two non-uniformities are not equally important at all scales. The dimension non-uniformity is a fundamental property of RoPE that persists across all extension ratios, because it is rooted in the mathematical structure of the encoding—different dimensions rotate at different frequencies, and this is true regardless of context length. The token position non-uniformity, on the other hand, is an empirical observation tied to the attention pattern of the model, and this pattern may shift as the context window grows to extreme lengths. The authors note that the ineffectiveness of token position non-uniformity at 2048k is left as an open question for future work, suggesting that a more nuanced treatment of position-dependent interpolation at extreme lengths may yield further improvements.

For students, this ablation study serves as a model example of how to rigorously evaluate the contribution of individual design choices in a machine learning method. Rather than simply reporting the final performance of the complete system, the authors dissect the method into its constituent parts and evaluate each in isolation. This approach not only validates the method but also builds understanding of why it works, which is essential for future research and for applying the method to new models and tasks.

### Related Works: Retrieval-Based Approaches and External Memory

To fully appreciate the contributions of LongRoPE, it is important to understand the alternative paradigms that have been pursued for handling long contexts in LLMs. One prominent category of approaches is retrieval-based methods, which take a fundamentally different philosophical stance from positional interpolation. Rather than attempting to extend the model's internal context window, retrieval-based methods keep the context window fixed at its original size and instead augment the model with an external memory module that stores long past context. At inference time, a retrieval module fetches relevant documents or passages from this external memory and feeds them into the model's existing context window.

This approach has been explored in several works. For example, LongLLaMA and similar architectures incorporate memory mechanisms that allow the model to access information from beyond its native context window. Another line of work uses retrieval-augmented generation, where an external retriever identifies relevant text segments from a large corpus and injects them into the prompt. The Retrieval-Enhanced Transformer (RETRO) and its successors use a memory store of text chunks that are retrieved based on similarity to the current context, effectively giving the model access to information far beyond what its context window could hold.

The key architectural characteristic of retrieval-based approaches is that they typically require explicit modifications to the LLM architecture. The model must be equipped with mechanisms for querying the external memory, integrating retrieved information with the current context, and managing the storage and retrieval pipeline. These modifications can be substantial, involving changes to the attention mechanism, the addition of cross-attention layers between the model and the memory, and the training of a separate retriever model.

LongRoPE stands in contrast to this paradigm. Its modifications are confined to the positional embedding layer, which is a relatively small and modular component of the Transformer architecture. The model's attention mechanism, feed-forward layers, and overall structure remain unchanged. This lightweight approach has several practical advantages. First, it means that LongRoPE can be applied to any LLM that uses RoPE positional encoding, without requiring architectural redesign. Second, it preserves compatibility with the extensive ecosystem of optimizations that have been developed for standard Transformer models, including efficient attention implementations, quantization, and hardware-specific kernels. Third, the method is simpler to implement and debug, as it involves only a change to the positional encoding parameters rather than a fundamental restructuring of the model.

Moreover, retrieval-based methods are inherently limited to tasks that can be decomposed into a retrieval step followed by a generation step. They excel at tasks like question answering over long documents, where the relevant information can be identified and fetched. However, they are less suited to tasks that require the model to maintain a coherent understanding of the entire long context, such as long document summarization, few-shot learning with many examples, or tasks that require reasoning across distant parts of the input. LongRoPE, by extending the model's native context window, enables the model to attend to all tokens in the input simultaneously, supporting a broader range of long-context tasks.

It is also worth noting that retrieval-based approaches and LongRoPE are not mutually exclusive. In principle, a model with an extended context window could also be equipped with a retrieval mechanism, combining the benefits of both approaches. The retrieval module could fetch relevant passages from an external corpus, while the extended context window allows the model to process those passages alongside the original input with full attention.

### Related Works: Attention-Based Context Window Extensions and Fine-Tuning Optimizations

Beyond retrieval-based methods, two other categories of related work merit discussion: attention-based context window extensions and fine-tuning-based approaches. Each addresses the challenge of long contexts from a different angle, and understanding their strengths and limitations helps clarify the unique position of LongRoPE in the landscape.

Attention-based context window extensions tackle the problem from the perspective of the attention mechanism itself. The core challenge in extending the context window is that as the number of tokens grows, the attention computation becomes increasingly expensive—both in terms of memory and computation—and the attention scores become dispersed, spread thinly across a large number of positions. Several works propose novel attention masks and mechanisms to address this issue. Streaming LLM, for example, identifies a set of "attention sinks"—initial tokens that receive disproportionately high attention scores—and constructs an attention pattern that retains these sinks alongside a sliding window of recent tokens. This allows the model to generate text that extends far beyond its training context window without retraining. LM-Infinite employs a similar idea, using a specialized attention mask that limits the model's attention to a subset of positions, thereby preventing the attention explosion that would otherwise occur with very long inputs. Parallel window attention divides the input into windows and processes each window with local attention, reducing the computational burden while maintaining some ability to model long-range dependencies.

These attention-based approaches are complementary to positional interpolation methods like LongRoPE. They address a different aspect of the problem: while positional interpolation ensures that the model can correctly interpret the positions of tokens beyond its training range, attention-based methods ensure that the model's attention computation remains tractable and effective at long lengths. In principle, the two approaches could be combined—a model with LongRoPE's non-uniform positional interpolation could also use a streaming or windowed attention mask to further improve efficiency at extreme lengths. The authors of LongRoPE explicitly acknowledge this complementarity, suggesting that their method and attention-based extensions serve different purposes and could be integrated in future work.

The third category of related work encompasses fine-tuning-based approaches, which focus on the question of how to effectively fine-tune pre-trained LLMs with modified positional embeddings to achieve longer context windows. Several works in this category choose a very large base value for RoPE (the parameter $\theta$ in the RoPE formulation, which defaults to 10000) and then fine-tune the model on texts of the target length. Code LLaMA, for instance, uses a larger base value and fine-tunes on long code documents. LLaMA2 Long and ScaledRoPE follow a similar strategy, adjusting the RoPE base and fine-tuning on extended sequences. The intuition behind increasing the base value is that it stretches the positional encoding space, allowing the model to represent a wider range of positions before encountering the out-of-distribution problem. However, this approach requires fine-tuning on texts of the target length, which becomes prohibitively expensive for very long contexts. It also lacks the flexibility of LongRoPE, which can target various lengths through its search algorithm without modifying the RoPE base.

More recently, as the community has pushed toward context windows exceeding 128k tokens, the computational cost of fine-tuning has become a major bottleneck. Training on sequences of this length requires enormous GPU resources and extensive training time. To mitigate this overhead, methods like LongLoRA and PoSE have been proposed. LongLoRA uses a combination of LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) and shifted sparse attention to reduce the memory and computation required for fine-tuning on long sequences. PoSE (Positional Skip-wise Training) trains the model on shorter segments while simulating longer positions through position manipulation, avoiding the need to process full-length sequences during training.

LongRoPE is orthogonal to these efficient fine-tuning methods. While LongRoPE's search algorithm provides an optimal initialization for fine-tuning by minimizing the information loss from positional interpolation, the actual fine-tuning process can leverage any of these efficient training techniques. For example, one could use LongRoPE to find the optimal rescale factors for a 256k context window, then use LongLoRA to efficiently fine-tune the model on 256k-length texts. The combination would benefit from both the improved initialization (leading to faster convergence and better final performance) and the reduced training cost (from the efficient fine-tuning method). This orthogonality is a significant advantage, as it means LongRoPE can be integrated into existing training pipelines without conflict, and future advances in efficient fine-tuning can directly benefit LongRoPE-based models.

In summary, the landscape of context window extension methods is diverse, with each category addressing a different aspect of the challenge. Retrieval-based methods handle long context by externalizing memory, at the cost of architectural complexity. Attention-based methods make long-context computation tractable by modifying the attention pattern, complementing but not replacing positional interpolation. Fine-tuning-based methods with large base values provide a straightforward but inflexible and expensive approach to extending the context window. Efficient fine-tuning methods reduce the cost of training on long sequences. LongRoPE distinguishes itself through its lightweight, search-based approach to positional interpolation, which provides a flexible and effective solution that can be combined with other methods to achieve context windows of unprecedented length while preserving the model's original architecture and capabilities.
