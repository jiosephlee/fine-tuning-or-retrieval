# Introduction to LLM-Based Tabular Learning

## The Role of LLMs in Reasoning and Tabular Learning

Large Language Models, or LLMs, have emerged as powerful tools capable of generating suitable responses for previously unseen tasks without requiring task-specific fine-tuning. Trained on extensive text corpora spanning diverse domains, these models possess rich prior knowledge that can substitute for expert domain knowledge in many areas, from genomics to healthcare to finance. This remarkable ability stems from their training on massive amounts of text data, which allows them to capture patterns, relationships, and facts about the world that prove valuable when applied to new problems. A key mechanism through which LLMs demonstrate this capability is in-context learning, whereby a task description and a few examples are incorporated into the input prompt. The LLM then understands the context of the task and "attends" to important features and examples, producing outputs that reflect both its pre-trained knowledge and the specific information provided in the prompt.

Tabular learning, which involves making predictions from structured data organized in rows and columns, is vital for many real-world applications. Consider, for example, predicting whether a patient will develop a particular disease based on clinical measurements, or determining whether a loan applicant will default based on financial history. These tasks traditionally require labeled training data to train machine learning models. However, in many real-world scenarios, obtaining large quantities of labeled data is expensive or impractical. This is where LLMs offer a unique advantage: their prior knowledge can supplement the limited labeled data available. For instance, in disease prediction tasks, an LLM's pre-trained knowledge might include the understanding that older individuals face an increased risk of certain diseases, or that particular biomarkers are associated with specific conditions. This knowledge, encoded during pre-training on medical literature and electronic health records, can be leveraged even when only a handful of labeled examples are available.

The application of LLMs to tabular learning typically involves serializing the tabular data into text format. Each sample, with its feature values, is converted into a natural language description, and together with feature names, feature descriptions, and a task description, is fed into the LLM's input prompt. The LLM then infers the relationships between the task and the features to produce a prediction. This approach has proven effective, particularly in low-shot regimes where conventional methods struggle due to insufficient training data. Prior knowledge provided by LLMs has outperformed conventional tabular learning baselines in these settings. The LLM serves as both a repository of domain knowledge and a reasoning engine, capable of drawing connections between features and outcomes that might not be immediately apparent from a small number of training examples alone.

## Current Limitations: End-to-End Prediction Costs, Fine-Tuning Requirements, and Prompt Size Constraints

Despite the demonstrated effectiveness of LLM-based tabular learning methods, several significant limitations constrain their real-world applicability. The first major limitation is the computational cost associated with end-to-end predictions. In current approaches, at least one LLM inference is required per sample at prediction time. This means that for every new data point that needs to be classified, the entire serialized sample must be passed through the LLM, which is computationally expensive and introduces significant latency. In industrial settings where predictions must be made for large volumes of data points in real time or near-real time, this per-sample inference cost becomes prohibitive. Unlike conventional machine learning models that can make predictions via simple matrix operations once trained, these LLM-based methods require the full computational overhead of a large neural network forward pass for each individual prediction.

A second major limitation concerns fine-tuning requirements. Most LLM-based tabular learning methods achieve high accuracy only when the LLM is fine-tuned on the specific task using parameter-efficient techniques such as LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) or IA3 (Injection of Adapted features). While these techniques are more efficient than full fine-tuning, they still require access to the model's internal parameters and training infrastructure. This requirement becomes increasingly problematic given the current trend in the LLM ecosystem, where recently-proposed top-performance LLMs only permit limited access via inference APIs. For example, models like GPT-4 and PaLM do not provide users with access to their training codebases or tuning APIs, making it impossible to apply fine-tuning-based methods to these state-of-the-art models. Practitioners are thus faced with a difficult choice: either use older, less capable models that support fine-tuning, or use state-of-the-art models only through their inference APIs, forgoing the performance gains that fine-tuning provides.

The third limitation involves prompt size constraints. LLMs have a finite context window, meaning there is a limit to the amount of text that can be included in a single input prompt. When the number of features in tabular data grows, the serialized text length becomes long, potentially exceeding the allowable input prompt size. This is particularly problematic for datasets with a large number of attributes; for instance, the Communities dataset and the Myocardial dataset include over 100 attributes each. Even when the prompt does not strictly exceed the context window, research has shown that LLMs experience a decline in performance when prompts become lengthy. This phenomenon, sometimes referred to as "lost in the middle," means that information presented in the middle of a long prompt may not be effectively utilized by the model. For tabular data with many features, some approaches simply turn infeasible, while others suffer degraded performance. These three limitations—computational cost of per-sample inference, dependence on fine-tuning, and vulnerability to prompt size constraints—pose fundamental challenges to the real-world application of LLM-based tabular learning models and motivate the search for alternative paradigms.

## The FeatLLM Paradigm: LLMs as Feature Engineers

The FeatLLM framework introduces a paradigm shift in how LLMs are utilized for tabular learning. Rather than treating the LLM as an end-to-end predictor that processes each sample individually, FeatLLM assigns the LLM the role of a feature engineer. The core insight is that the LLM's prior knowledge and reasoning capabilities can be more efficiently utilized by having the model generate criteria, or rules, that determine which feature conditions are associated with each class, rather than having it directly predict the class for each sample. For example, in a disease prediction task, instead of asking the LLM whether each individual patient has the disease, FeatLLM asks the LLM to infer and generate rules that determine which feature conditions result in identifying the disease—such as "age greater than 60 AND blood pressure above 140." These rules are then used to create new features that replace the existing ones in solving the prediction task.

The FeatLLM framework structures the LLM's rule-extraction process into two sub-tasks, designed to mimic how an expert human might approach a tabular learning problem. The first sub-task involves understanding the problem and inferring the relationship between features and the target variable, relying on the LLM's general knowledge and common sense without reference to specific data examples. This step encourages the LLM to fully extract its prior knowledge about the problem domain. The second sub-task uses the knowledge from the first step along with a few-shot example demonstrations to derive decisive rules that distinguish between classes. This two-step reasoning process, inspired by chain-of-thought approaches, helps prevent the model from identifying spurious correlations in irrelevant columns and focuses attention on more significant features. The LLM is instructed to generate a fixed number of rules (specifically, 10) per class, which balances the need for sufficient coverage against the risk of creating an excessively large prompt or overfitting in the low-shot regime.

Once the rules are generated, they must be transformed into usable features. Since the rules are expressed in natural language, FeatLLM employs the LLM itself to parse them into executable program code. The function name, input and output descriptions, and the inferred rules are included in a prompt, and the LLM generates Python code that can be executed to transform the data. Each sample is then represented by a binary feature vector for each class, where each binary value indicates whether the sample satisfies the corresponding rule for that class (1 if satisfied, 0 if not). This transformation shifts the LLM's contribution from prediction to feature generation, enabling a simpler downstream model to handle the actual classification. The structured reasoning approach, combined with the rule-based feature representation, effectively filters out uninformative features and captures the LLM's domain knowledge in a compact, interpretable form.

## Key Benefits of FeatLLM: Low Inference Cost, API-Level Access, and Prompt Size Mitigation

The FeatLLM framework offers several key advantages over existing LLM-based tabular learning approaches, addressing each of the limitations identified earlier. The first benefit is low inference cost. Since the LLM is used only during the feature engineering stage—generating rules and parsing them into code—the LLM is not involved in the inference process at all. Once the rules are extracted and the downstream model is trained, predictions for new samples involve only checking which rules each sample satisfies and applying the trained model. Specifically, FeatLLM uses a simple linear model without bias, where the class likelihood is computed as a dot product between non-negative trainable weights and the binary feature vector, followed by a softmax function to obtain class probabilities. The probability for each class $\mathbf{p}^i$ for sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ is computed as:

$$\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,$$

$$\mathbf{p}^i = \text{Softmax}([\text{logit}_{1}^i, ..., \text{logit}_{c}^i]).$$

Here, $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0, 1\}^R$ is the binary feature vector for class $k$ with $R$ rules per class, $\mathbf{w}_k$ are the trainable weights, and $c$ is the number of classes. The weights are projected into a positive subspace to ensure that the LLM-generated features do not reduce the likelihood of a class during learning. This simple model makes predictions computationally inexpensive, comparable to conventional machine learning methods, and dramatically faster than approaches that require a full LLM forward pass per sample. Experimental results confirm that FeatLLM's inference time is comparable to conventional methods, while other LLM-based approaches exhibit much higher inference times.

The second benefit is that FeatLLM requires only API-level access to LLMs. Because the LLM is never fine-tuned—the rules are extracted through in-context learning, where few-shot example demonstrations are added to the prompt without any parameter updates—FeatLLM can leverage any LLM that provides an inference API. This is a significant advantage over methods that require fine-tuning, as it makes FeatLLM compatible with the most capable and recently-proposed LLMs, including those that do not offer training codebases or tuning APIs. The training process for FeatLLM involves only 30 API queries, which does not impose a significant budget burden and can be parallelized. This makes the framework feasible for practitioners who rely on commercial LLM services and do not have the resources or access to fine-tune large models.

The third benefit is the mitigation of prompt size constraints. FeatLLM employs ensemble methods, specifically feature bagging, to handle datasets with large numbers of features or samples. In each trial, a subset of features and instances is selected, reducing the serialized text length to fit within the LLM's context window. The entire rule-extraction and model-training process is repeated multiple times (the paper uses 20 ensembles by default), and predictions are made by averaging the class probabilities across all trials. To introduce randomness and diversity across trials, a sufficiently high temperature is set for LLM inference, and the order of few-shot demonstrations is randomized. This ensemble approach offers two key advantages. First, even if the LLM generates incorrect rules in some trials, correct rules from other trials can compensate, enhancing overall robustness. This aligns with the concept of LLM self-consistency, where rules commonly inferred across multiple trials are more likely to be accurate. Second, the ensemble method directly addresses the prompt size limitation: while a single rule set might not capture the relationships of all features, the combination of various weak learners created across multiple trials can nullify the impact of incomplete feature or instance coverage in individual trials. Experimental results demonstrate that FeatLLM, employing both bagging and ensemble techniques, proves effective even on datasets with numerous features, where other LLM-based approaches fail to produce predictions altogether.

# Background and Related Work

## Challenges and Strategies in Few-Shot Tabular Learning

Few-shot learning has emerged as a critical paradigm in machine learning, driven by the practical need to extract generalizable representations from data even when labeling costs are prohibitive. The advancement of few-shot learning has enabled models to learn effectively from minimal labeled samples, a capability initially demonstrated on image data and subsequently extended across various data modalities including text, audio, and, more recently, tabular data. Tabular data, which underpins countless real-world applications in domains such as healthcare, finance, and social sciences, presents unique challenges that distinguish it from other data modalities and make few-shot learning particularly difficult.

The fundamental challenge in few-shot tabular learning stems from the risk of learning spurious correlations when only a small number of labeled samples are available. With limited data, conventional supervised learning models tend to identify patterns that exist in the training samples by chance rather than capturing genuine, generalizable relationships between features and targets. This problem is exacerbated by the heterogeneous nature of tabular data, where features may be of different types—numerical, categorical, or ordinal—and may exhibit complex, non-linear interactions that are difficult to discern from few examples. Unlike image or text data, where spatial or sequential structure can be exploited by convolutional or recurrent architectures, tabular data lacks such inherent structural priors that could guide learning in low-data regimes.

To address these challenges, recent efforts have explored strategies that go beyond standard supervised learning by incorporating additional sources of information or domain-specific prior knowledge to impart appropriate inductive biases for model training. One prominent direction involves leveraging large quantities of more readily-available unlabeled data through semi-supervised learning approaches, where unlabeled samples are used alongside the few labeled examples to learn better representations. Another direction involves seeking good model initialization through task-agnostic unsupervised pre-training, where models are first trained on auxiliary objectives before being fine-tuned on the target task with limited labels. The central intuition behind all these strategies is that external knowledge—whether from unlabeled data, pre-training tasks, or domain expertise—can compensate for the scarcity of labeled samples and help the model avoid overfitting to spurious patterns.

However, the heterogeneous nature of tabular data poses a persistent obstacle: it is challenging to find universally applicable data augmentations or pre-training objectives that transfer well across diverse tabular tasks. Unlike images, where augmentations like cropping, flipping, or color jittering are broadly effective, or text, where paraphrasing and back-translation are widely applicable, tabular augmentations must respect the semantic meaning and type constraints of each column, making them task-specific and less generalizable. As a result, many of these augmentation-based strategies have not been notably effective in few-shot settings, motivating the exploration of alternative approaches such as transfer learning and language-interfaced methods that can inject richer prior knowledge into the learning process.

## Unsupervised Pre-training and Data Augmentation Approaches

Unsupervised pre-training and data augmentation have been explored as means to improve few-shot tabular learning by extracting useful representations from data without relying on labels. The core idea is to leverage the structure present in the data itself—either through unlabeled samples or through self-supervised objectives—to learn representations that generalize better when only a few labeled examples are available for the downstream task.

Several approaches have adopted semi-supervised learning frameworks that utilize large quantities of unlabeled data alongside a small number of labeled samples. For instance, SubTab employs judicious augmentation of unlabeled data to enable semi-supervised learning, seeking to extract useful signal from the unlabeled portion of the dataset to complement the limited labeled examples. Similarly, VIME follows a comparable philosophy, using unlabeled data to improve model performance in regimes where labeled data is scarce. The intuition is that even without labels, the distributional structure of the data can reveal meaningful patterns that aid generalization.

Another family of approaches relies on task-agnostic unsupervised pre-training objectives. These methods typically involve masking or corrupting part of the input data and then training a model to reconstruct the original values, thereby learning useful representations of the data distribution. TabNet, for example, employs such a reconstruction objective as part of its pre-training procedure. Similarly, MET utilizes masking-based objectives to learn representations that can later be fine-tuned for downstream few-shot tasks. The rationale is that a model trained to predict missing values must understand the relationships between features, and this understanding transfers to improved performance when labels are scarce.

Data augmentation has also been applied in the context of contrastive learning objectives for tabular data. SCARF, for instance, generates augmented views of tabular samples and applies contrastive learning to bring representations of augmentations of the same sample closer together while pushing apart representations of different samples. This approach, inspired by successful contrastive learning frameworks in other domains, aims to learn invariant representations that are robust to perturbations in the input. The general principle of contrastive learning—learning by comparing positive and negative pairs—has proven powerful in self-supervised settings across modalities.

STUNT represents another approach that leverages unlabeled data, using it to generate self-supervised signals that can guide few-shot learning. By treating the unlabeled data as a source of auxiliary training signal, STUNT seeks to improve the model's ability to generalize from the few labeled examples available. This approach, along with others that assume access to large unlabeled datasets, can provide meaningful improvements, though obtaining such large quantities of unlabeled data may be infeasible in many real-world applications.

Despite the promise of these methods, the heterogeneous nature of tabular data makes it challenging to design augmentations and pre-training objectives that are universally effective. Unlike images, where standard augmentations preserve semantic content, or text, where meaning is largely preserved under paraphrasing, perturbations to tabular data can easily produce semantically invalid samples—for example, by assigning an impossible value to a categorical feature or breaking the logical relationship between two numerical columns. Consequently, these strategies have not been notably effective in few-shot settings, as noted in the literature, motivating the exploration of transfer learning and language-based approaches that can inject richer, more structured prior knowledge.

## Transfer Learning in Tabular Data

Transfer learning has emerged as a promising strategy for few-shot tabular learning, aiming to overcome the limitations of augmentation-based approaches by transferring knowledge acquired from a broad range of tabular datasets to the target task. The central idea is that a model pre-trained on diverse tabular tasks can learn generalizable representations that facilitate zero-shot or few-shot inference on new, previously unseen tabular tasks, thereby reducing the dependence on large labeled datasets for each individual task.

TransTab exemplifies this approach by training transformer-based models that learn semantic relationships between columns through their names, rather than relying solely on the raw values in the tables. By incorporating column names as a source of semantic information, TransTab can capture the meaning of features in a way that generalizes across different tables and tasks. For instance, a column named "age" in one dataset and "years" in another may serve similar purposes, and a model that understands this semantic correspondence can transfer knowledge between them. The knowledge extracted from various tables and columns during pre-training enables the model to perform zero-shot or few-shot inference for new tabular tasks, leveraging the semantic understanding acquired during pre-training to make predictions even when labeled data for the new task is minimal.

TabPFN takes a different approach to transfer learning for tabular data. Rather than pre-training on real tabular datasets, it generates synthetic data by mixing different types of distributions to create a diverse pre-training corpus. A Bayesian neural network is then pre-trained on this synthetic data, learning a prior over tabular learning tasks that captures the diversity of possible relationships between features and targets. After pre-training, inference is performed by inputting both training and test samples of the target task into the network without any further training. The model leverages the prior knowledge gained from pre-training on diverse synthetic distributions to make predictions on the target task, effectively using the pre-trained prior as a substitute for the large labeled dataset that would otherwise be needed. This approach has been shown to surpass the performance of traditional tree-based methods and has proven particularly beneficial in few-shot settings, where the pre-trained prior can guide predictions when data is scarce.

Other transfer learning approaches for tabular data include generative models that learn to generate synthetic tabular data for pre-training and methods that seek to identify common structural patterns across diverse tabular datasets. The general principle underlying all these approaches is that knowledge acquired from one set of tasks can be transferred to improve performance on a different but related task, reducing the amount of labeled data needed for the target task. This is particularly valuable in few-shot settings, where the ability to leverage prior knowledge can mean the difference between a model that generalizes and one that overfits to the few available examples.

Despite their promise, transfer learning approaches face their own challenges. The diversity of tabular tasks in terms of feature types, target distributions, and underlying relationships means that knowledge transfer is not always straightforward. A model pre-trained on one set of tasks may not transfer well to a task with fundamentally different structure, and the synthetic data generated for pre-training may not capture the full range of patterns present in real-world tabular data. Nevertheless, these approaches represent an important step toward data-efficient tabular learning and have demonstrated that transfer learning can be a viable strategy for improving few-shot performance.

## Language-Interfaced Tabular Learning: Data Serialization and In-Context Learning

Language-interfaced tabular learning represents a paradigm shift in how prior knowledge is incorporated into tabular prediction tasks. The approach harnesses the rich prior knowledge encapsulated by Large Language Models (LLMs), which have been trained on extensive and diverse text corpora and have demonstrated the ability to generalize to unseen tasks without task-specific fine-tuning. LLMs possess broad knowledge across domains—from medical and financial concepts to social and scientific phenomena—making them a potentially valuable source of prior knowledge for tabular learning tasks where domain expertise is beneficial.

The fundamental mechanism of language-interfaced tabular learning is data serialization: tabular data is converted into natural language text that can be fed into the input prompts of LLMs. The serialization process transforms each sample from a vector of feature values into a textual description that pairs each feature name with its corresponding value, followed by the label. Specifically, the serialization follows a structured format such as "$f_1$ is $\mathbf{x}^i_1$. ... $f_d$ is $\mathbf{x}^i_d$. Answer: $\mathbf{y}^i$", where $f_j$ denotes the natural language name of the $j$-th feature, $\mathbf{x}^i_j$ is its value for the $i$-th sample, and $\mathbf{y}^i$ is the corresponding label. This serialized representation, along with feature descriptions and task descriptions, is included in the LLM's input prompt, enabling the model to infer the relationships between features and the target variable.

In-context learning is a key capability that makes language-interfaced tabular learning feasible without requiring any model training. By including a task description and a few labeled examples in the prompt, the LLM can understand the context of the task and "attend" to important features and examples, effectively performing few-shot learning through prompt engineering alone. For instance, when given a task description such as "Does this patient's myocardial infarction complications data indicate chronic heart failure? Yes or no?" along with a few example demonstrations of patients with known outcomes, the LLM can leverage both its prior knowledge about heart disease and the patterns in the provided examples to make predictions on new, unlabeled samples. This demonstrates the capability of LLMs to analyze data and act as expert feature engineers, attending to the most relevant features for the task at hand.

The prior knowledge provided by LLMs has proven effective in tabular learning, outperforming conventional tabular learning baselines in low-shot regimes. For example, LLMs can bring to bear knowledge such as the increased risk of certain diseases in older individuals, which can be beneficial for disease prediction tasks even when the training data is too sparse for conventional models to learn such relationships. By incorporating feature descriptions and task descriptions in natural language, the LLM can infer the semantic meaning of features and their relevance to the target, enabling it to make predictions that go beyond what would be possible from the few labeled examples alone.

However, current LLM-based tabular learning methods have several limitations. For end-to-end predictions, at least one LLM inference is required per sample, making the approach computationally expensive, especially when dealing with large datasets. Most methods also require fine-tuning the LLM for high accuracy, which is infeasible for LLMs that do not come with accessible training codebases or tuning APIs—a prominent concern given that recently-proposed top-performance LLMs only permit limited access via inference APIs. Additionally, most approaches struggle with lengthy prompts: when the number of features in tabular data grows to the point where the serialized text becomes long, the approach often becomes infeasible or experiences a decline in performance due to the limited context window of LLMs. These constraints pose fundamental challenges to the real-world application of LLM-based tabular learning models, motivating new approaches that can leverage LLM prior knowledge without requiring per-sample inference at test time.

## Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning Techniques

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques have become an important tool for adapting LLMs to specific tasks, including tabular learning, without the prohibitive computational cost of fine-tuning all parameters of the model. These methods enable the adaptation of large, pre-trained language models to downstream tasks by updating only a small subset of parameters, thereby reducing the computational and memory requirements while still achieving performance improvements that approach or match those of full fine-tuning.

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) is one such parameter-efficient fine-tuning technique that has been employed in the context of language-interfaced tabular learning. The core idea behind LoRA is to freeze the pre-trained model weights and inject trainable rank decomposition matrices into each layer of the transformer architecture. By learning only these low-rank updates, LoRA can adapt the model to a new task with a fraction of the trainable parameters that would be required for full fine-tuning. This makes it feasible to fine-tune large language models on tabular tasks even with limited computational resources, though it still requires access to the model's internal parameters and training infrastructure.

IA3 is another parameter-efficient fine-tuning technique that has been specifically utilized in the context of tabular learning. IA3 works by injecting trainable vectors into the attention mechanism and feed-forward layers of the transformer, scaling the keys, values, and intermediate activations. These injected vectors have a very small number of parameters compared to the full model, making the fine-tuning process highly efficient. TabLLM, for example, employs IA3 to train an LLM with few-shot samples for tabular prediction tasks, achieving strong performance by combining the prior knowledge of the pre-trained LLM with task-specific adaptation through IA3. The approach serializes tabular data into text, includes feature and task descriptions, and then fine-tunes the LLM using IA3 to adapt it to the specific tabular prediction task.

Despite the effectiveness of parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques, they introduce additional constraints on the applicability of language-interfaced tabular learning. Most critically, these techniques require access to the model's internal parameters and training code, which is often not available for state-of-the-art LLMs that are only accessible through inference APIs. Many recently proposed top-performance LLMs, such as PaLM and GPT-4, only permit limited access via inference APIs, making parameter-efficient fine-tuning infeasible for these models. This limitation restricts the applicability of approaches like TabLLM to LLMs with publicly available checkpoints, such as T0, which may not possess the same level of prior knowledge or reasoning capability as the most advanced models.

Furthermore, even when parameter-efficient fine-tuning is feasible, the resulting models still require passing each sample through the LLM at inference time, incurring significant computational cost compared to conventional machine learning approaches. The combination of training requirements and per-sample inference cost creates a barrier to the widespread adoption of these methods in real-world applications, particularly in industrial settings where inference latency and computational budget are critical concerns. These limitations highlight the need for alternative approaches that can leverage the prior knowledge of LLMs for tabular learning without requiring model fine-tuning or per-sample LLM inference, motivating frameworks like FeatLLM that use LLMs as feature engineers rather than end-to-end predictors.

The FeatLLM Framework: Methodology

# Problem formulation for tabular learning tasks

To build a robust system for few-shot tabular learning, we must first rigorously define the structure of the data and the learning problem. In the tabular learning setting addressed by the FeatLLM framework, we begin with a dataset $\mathcal{D}$ comprising $N$ labeled samples, denoted as $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}^i, \mathbf{y}^i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$. Each input sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ is a $d$-dimensional vector representing $d$ distinct input features. Crucially, because this methodology relies on large language models (LLMs), the purely numerical or categorical data must be grounded in natural language. Therefore, the dataset is accompanied by natural language feature names, denoted by the set $F = \{f_j\}_{j=1}^{d}$, along with separate textual definitions that describe what each feature represents. 

The framework assumes a classification setup, meaning the label $\mathbf{y}^i$ for any given sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ belongs to a predefined set of classes. The primary objective is to learn a mapping from the input features to the target label. However, the defining characteristic of the problem is the few-shot learning regime. In a $k$-shot learning experiment, the model does not have access to all $N$ labeled samples. Instead, only a small subset of $k$ labeled samples (where $k < N$) is randomly sampled from the dataset and provided to the model for training. This constraint mimics real-world scenarios where obtaining labeled data is expensive or difficult, requiring the model to generalize effectively from very limited supervision.

# Prompt design for rule extraction

The core innovation of the FeatLLM framework lies in treating the LLM not as an end-to-end predictor, but as an expert feature engineer. To achieve this, the framework requires a carefully constructed prompt that guides the LLM to extract "rules"—the underlying conditions associated with each answer class—from the limited data it is given. An effective prompt must structure the problem-solving process in a way that mimics how a human expert would approach a tabular learning task. 

To ensure the LLM extracts rules based on an accurate and logical reasoning path, the input prompt is divided into three main components: basic information description, reasoning instruction, and response instruction. The basic information description provides the LLM with the foundational context of the task. The reasoning instruction then dictates how the LLM should process this information to deduce relationships and rules. Finally, the response instruction enforces a strict format on the output so that the generated rules can be easily parsed and utilized by the downstream system. By structuring the prompt in this way, FeatLLM filters out uninformative features and focuses the LLM's reasoning on generating decisive rules that distinguish between classes.

# Basic information description: task, features, and serialized demonstrations

The first component of the prompt, the basic information description, equips the LLM with the essential context needed to solve the tabular learning problem. This section contains three key elements: the task description, the feature descriptions, and serialized example demonstrations. The task description is formulated as a natural language question that clearly states the prediction objective, such as "Does this patient's myocardial infarction complications data indicate chronic heart failure? Yes or no?". This phrasing aligns the tabular prediction task with the question-answering format that LLMs are highly optimized to handle.

Next, the feature descriptions provide the LLM with the meaning of each column in the tabular data. For each feature, the description indicates its value type (e.g., numerical or categorical) and optionally includes a more detailed definition. For categorical variables, examples of the possible categories are provided to ensure the LLM understands the domain of the values. Finally, the prompt includes a few example demonstrations, which are the $k$ labeled training samples serialized into text. The serialization follows a straightforward format that maps the feature names and sample values into a natural language sentence, ending with the ground-truth label. Specifically, a sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ with label $\mathbf{y}^i$ is serialized as: $f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ ... \ f_d\  \text{is}\  \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \ \text{Answer:}\  \mathbf{y}^i$. By formatting the tabular data in this manner, the LLM can seamlessly interpret the structured data as a natural language context.

# Two-step reasoning instruction: causal inference and rule deduction

The second component of the prompt is the reasoning instruction, which is designed to enhance the LLM's logical processing by breaking the inference task into two distinct steps. This structured approach begins with an introductory sentence similar to the chain-of-thought methodology, prompting the LLM to reason step-by-step. In the first step, the LLM is instructed to infer the causal relationship or tendency between the features and the task description. Importantly, the LLM is directed to perform this step without referring to the example demonstrations, relying entirely on its internal prior knowledge or common sense. This allows the LLM to fully extract its general knowledge about the problem domain, establishing a baseline understanding of which features are likely to be important before looking at the specific data.

In the second step, the LLM uses the example demonstrations alongside the knowledge derived from the first step to deduce explicit rules for each class. This two-step reasoning process is critical for preventing the model from identifying spurious correlations in irrelevant columns, as it forces the model to ground its rules in established prior knowledge before fitting the few-shot examples. To maintain a balance between underfitting (too few rules) and creating an excessively large prompt (too many rules), the LLM is instructed to extract a fixed number of rules—specifically, 10 rules—for each class. Additionally, the LLM is instructed to refer to the feature types provided in the basic information description when formulating these rules, ensuring there are no type mismatches in the generated conditions.

# Response instruction: formatting rules for automated parsing

The final component of the prompt is the response instruction, which dictates how the LLM should structure its output to facilitate automated parsing and feature generation. Because the ultimate goal is to use these rules to programmatically transform data into new binary features, the LLM's natural language output must adhere to a predictable and structured format. The prompt provides explicit guidelines on the required format for the overall response for each answer class, as well as the specific structure that each individual rule must follow.

These instructions guide the LLM to select the appropriate formatting based on the feature type (e.g., using inequalities for numerical features or exact matches for categorical features). Furthermore, the response instructions allow the LLM to combine multiple conditions into a single rule using logical operators like AND or OR when appropriate. By strictly constraining the output format, the framework ensures that the inferred rules are both human-readable and machine-parseable, bridging the gap between the LLM's natural language generation and the downstream computational requirements.

# Parsing natural language rules into executable code for feature generation

Once the LLM generates the rules in natural language, the FeatLLM framework must use these rules to create new binary features for the data. For each class, a new binary feature is created for every rule, indicating whether a given sample satisfies the conditions of that rule (represented as $1$) or not (represented as $0$). These binary features are then used to predict the likelihood that a sample belongs to a specific class. However, because the rules are generated in natural language, an automated parsing strategy is required to apply them to the tabular data.

Despite the constraints enforced by the response instructions, LLM outputs can still contain noisy syntactic variations. For instance, an LLM might substitute mathematical symbols like "$>$" or "$<$" with textual phrases like "greater than" or "less than." Building a rigid parser to handle all such variations would be complex and fragile. Instead, FeatLLM leverages the LLM itself to parse its own rules into executable program code. The LLM is presented with a new prompt containing the function name, the input and output descriptions, and the previously inferred rules. Because LLMs are trained on code corpora and excel at understanding semantic meaning despite syntactic changes, they can generate a simple Python function that accurately encodes the rules. This generated code is then executed using Python's `exec()` function to automatically perform the data conversion, transforming the original dataset into a new set of binary features indicating rule satisfaction.

# Inferring class likelihood using a non-negative weighted linear model

With the new binary features generated from the rules, the framework needs a mechanism to infer the class likelihood of each sample. A simple baseline would be to count how many rules for each class a sample satisfies. However, not all rules are equally important, so the framework must learn the significance of each rule from the few-shot training samples. To do this, FeatLLM employs a simple linear machine learning model without a bias term. Let $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0, 1\}^R$ denote the generated binary feature vector for sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ corresponding to class $k$, where $R$ is the number of rules per class and $c$ is the total number of classes. The probability for each class, $\mathbf{p}^i$, is computed using a non-negative weighted linear model followed by a softmax function.

The logit for class $k$ is calculated as $\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i$, where $\mathbf{w}_k$ represents the trainable weights that signify the importance of each feature. By projecting these weights into a positive subspace using the $\max(\cdot, 0)$ operation, the model ensures that the features generated by the LLM do not reduce the likelihood of a class during learning. The logit vector across all classes, $[\text{logit}_{1}^i, ..., \text{logit}_{c}^i]$, is then passed through the softmax function to produce valid class probabilities $\mathbf{p}^i$. The model optimizes the cross-entropy loss to learn the weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ using the available few-shot training samples, effectively calibrating the importance of each LLM-generated rule.

# Ensembling with bagging: introducing randomness and overcoming prompt size limits

To further improve robustness and prediction accuracy, FeatLLM repeatedly executes the entire rule generation and model training process to create an ensemble of inference models. Given a collection of trained weights from $T$ trials, denoted as $\{\mathbf{w}[t]\}_{t=1}^T$, the final prediction for a sample is made by averaging the class probabilities $\mathbf{p}^i$ computed with each weight set $\mathbf{w}[t]$. This ensemble approach leverages the concept of self-consistency: rules that are commonly inferred across multiple independent trials are more likely to be accurate, and correct rules from some trials can compensate for incorrect rules generated in others.

To ensure diversity across the trials and make the ensemble effective, randomness is introduced during rule generation. A sufficiently high temperature is set for the LLM inference to encourage varied outputs, and the order of the few-shot example demonstrations is randomized for each trial, as the order of examples is known to impact LLM query outcomes. Furthermore, the framework employs bagging—randomly selecting subsets of features or instances for each trial. This bagging technique serves a dual purpose. First, it provides diverse weak learners for the ensemble, similar to a random forest. Second, it directly addresses the limitation of LLM prompt sizes. When tabular data contains a large number of features or samples, serializing the entire dataset can exceed the LLM's allowable input prompt size. By using bagging to select only a subset of features and instances, the framework keeps the prompt size manageable, ensuring robust performance even on wide or lengthy tabular datasets.

# Experimental Setup and Performance Evaluation

The experimental validation of any machine learning framework is essential to demonstrate its practical utility, robustness, and advantages over existing methods. In the case of FeatLLM, the authors designed a comprehensive set of experiments spanning a diverse collection of tabular datasets, comparing against nine different baseline methods, and evaluating performance across multiple few-shot learning regimes. This chapter walks through each component of the experimental design in detail, explaining not just what was done but why each choice matters for a fair and informative evaluation.

## Dataset Overview: Real-World, Recent, and Synthetic Tabular Datasets

A critical aspect of evaluating any machine learning method is the selection of datasets. The choice of datasets determines the breadth of domains covered, the variety of data characteristics tested, and ultimately the generalizability of the conclusions drawn. The authors of FeatLLM curated 13 datasets for binary or multi-class classification tasks, deliberately chosen to span a wide range of domains and to include both well-established benchmarks and carefully constructed datasets that guard against potential confounds.

The first group of datasets consists of nine real-world tabular datasets that have been widely used in the machine learning community. The Adult dataset focuses on predicting whether an individual earns over \$50,000 annually, a classic socio-economic prediction task. The Bank dataset involves predicting whether a customer will subscribe to a term deposit, representing a marketing and customer behavior prediction problem. The Blood dataset addresses whether donors will return for subsequent donations, which is a donor retention prediction task. The Car dataset involves predicting the quality of a car, and the Communities dataset focuses on predicting the crime rate in a specified region. The Credit-g dataset is concerned with predicting whether an individual poses a good or bad credit risk. The Diabetes dataset predicts the presence of diabetes in an individual, and the Heart dataset predicts the occurrence of coronary artery disease. Finally, the Myocardial dataset predicts whether an individual suffers from chronic heart failure.

These datasets vary significantly in size and complexity. A particularly important characteristic is the number of features: datasets such as Communities and Myocardial include over 100 attributes. This is significant because when tabular data is serialized into text for LLM consumption, the resulting prompt can exceed the LLM's context window size. This poses a fundamental challenge for LLM-based methods that require passing the entire feature set through the LLM for each sample. The inclusion of these high-dimensional datasets therefore tests whether methods can handle scenarios that are realistic in industrial applications but problematic for naive LLM-based approaches.

The second group consists of two recent real-world datasets: Cultivars and NHANES. The Cultivars dataset involves predicting how high the grain yield of a soybean cultivar will be, and the NHANES dataset involves predicting a given person's age group from their record. Both of these datasets were recently donated to the UCI Machine Learning Repository after September 2023. This timing is critical because the LLM API used by FeatLLM, specifically \texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0613}, would have completed its pre-training before these datasets became publicly available. This ensures that any performance FeatLLM achieves on these datasets is not a result of the LLM having memorized the data during pre-training, but rather stems from genuine reasoning and feature engineering capabilities.

The third group consists of two synthetic datasets: Sequence-type and Solution-mix. The Sequence-type dataset involves classifying a given sequence as one of four types: arithmetic, geometric, Fibonacci, or Collatz. The Solution-mix dataset involves predicting whether the percentage concentration of a mixture from four solutions exceeds 0.5. Because these datasets are synthetically generated, they provide an absolute guarantee that the LLM could not have encountered or memorized them during pre-training. This is an important control because one of the persistent concerns with evaluating LLM-based methods is the possibility that the model has seen the test data during pre-training, which would inflate performance estimates and lead to misleading conclusions.

Together, these 13 datasets cover diverse domains including healthcare, finance, social science, agriculture, and mathematics. Each dataset provides a clear name and description for each attribute, which is essential for FeatLLM because the framework relies on natural language feature descriptions to guide the LLM's rule generation process. The combination of well-known benchmarks, recent datasets, and synthetic data provides a thorough test of FeatLLM's ability to generalize across domains and its capacity for genuine reasoning rather than memorization.

## Baseline Methods: Traditional Machine Learning, Unlabeled Data Approaches, and LLM-Based Models

To rigorously evaluate FeatLLM, the authors compared it against nine baselines organized into three categories. Each category represents a distinct approach to tabular learning, and understanding the strengths and limitations of each baseline is essential for interpreting the significance of FeatLLM's performance.

The first category consists of conventional tabular learning approaches that do not leverage any external knowledge or LLM capabilities. These include logistic regression (LogReg), XGBoost, and RandomForest. Logistic regression is a simple linear model that has been a workhorse of statistical learning for decades. XGBoost is a gradient-boosted decision tree method known for its strong performance on tabular data and its dominance in many Kaggle competitions. RandomForest is an ensemble of decision trees that provides robust performance through bagging and random feature selection. These three methods serve as fundamental baselines: if FeatLLM cannot outperform them, its practical value would be questionable. For these traditional methods, hyperparameters were determined using grid search combined with $k$-fold cross-validation, ensuring that each baseline was given a fair opportunity to perform well.

The second category includes methods that assume access to large quantities of unlabeled data. These are SCARF and STUNT. SCARF is a contrastive learning method designed for tabular data that uses data augmentation to apply contrastive learning objectives. STUNT is a more recent approach that leverages unlabeled data for semi-supervised tabular learning. The inclusion of these baselines is important because they represent alternative strategies for dealing with the few-shot learning challenge. While FeatLLM leverages the prior knowledge embedded in LLMs to compensate for limited labeled data, SCARF and STUNT instead leverage additional unlabeled data. However, as the authors note, obtaining large quantities of unlabeled datasets may be infeasible in many real-world applications, making these methods less practically applicable in certain settings. Despite this caveat, including them as baselines provides a comprehensive comparison of different strategies for addressing the data scarcity problem.

The third category consists of recent approaches that also leverage LLMs for tabular learning. These are TabPFN, in-context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM. TabPFN generates a synthetic dataset with diverse distributions to pre-train a Bayesian neural network. After pre-training, inference is performed by inputting both training and test samples of the target task without further training. The prior knowledge gained from various datasets helps it surpass the performance of traditional tree-based approaches, particularly in few-shot settings. In-context learning directly embeds few-shot training samples into the input prompt without any tuning, relying on the LLM's ability to learn from examples in context. TABLET further incorporates additional hints into the prompt through an external classifier that provides rule-sets and prototypes to enhance the quality of inference. TabLLM employs parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques such as IA3 to train the LLM with few-shot samples, adapting the model's parameters to the specific task.

The LLM-based baselines are particularly important because they represent the most direct competitors to FeatLLM. All of these methods use the LLM for end-to-end prediction, meaning that each test sample must be passed through the LLM to obtain a prediction. This contrasts with FeatLLM, which uses the LLM only during the feature engineering phase and then employs a simple downstream model for inference. The comparison with these baselines therefore tests not only predictive performance but also the efficiency of the two paradigms: LLM-as-predictor versus LLM-as-feature-engineer.

An important implementation detail is that for the in-context learning baseline, GPT-3.5 was used as the backbone, while for TabLLM, the T0 model was employed following the original setting. This difference is necessitated by the fact that TabLLM requires parameter-efficient fine-tuning, which requires access to the model's internal parameters. GPT-3.5, being accessible only through an inference API, does not permit such fine-tuning. This constraint highlights a practical limitation of fine-tuning-based approaches: they cannot be applied to state-of-the-art LLMs that only provide API-level access, a growing concern as more powerful models are released with restricted access.

## Implementation Details: Model Configurations and Hyperparameter Settings

The implementation details of FeatLLM and its baselines are crucial for reproducibility and for understanding the computational costs associated with each approach. FeatLLM was designed to use GPT-3.5 as its LLM backbone, specifically the \texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0613} model, but the framework is designed to be agnostic to the choice of LLM. This means that any sufficiently capable LLM could be substituted, and the authors provide additional results with different backbones in the appendix.

For the LLM inference during rule generation, the temperature was set to 0.5 and the top-p value was set to the default value of 1. The temperature parameter controls the randomness of the LLM's output: a temperature of 0 would make the output deterministic, while higher temperatures introduce more variability. A temperature of 0.5 strikes a balance, introducing enough randomness to generate diverse rules across different ensemble trials while maintaining coherent and sensible rule generation. This is particularly important for the ensemble approach, where diversity among the individual models contributes to robustness.

Two key hyperparameters govern FeatLLM's behavior: the number of ensembles and the number of rules per class. The number of ensembles was set to 20, meaning that the entire process of rule generation, parsing, and model training was repeated 20 times, with the final prediction obtained by averaging the class probabilities across all 20 trials. The number of rules per class was set to 10, meaning that for each class, the LLM was instructed to generate 10 rules that distinguish that class from others. The authors investigated the impact of these hyperparameters and found that performance improves as the number of ensembles increases, but beyond approximately 20, the performance gains begin to converge. For the number of rules, while there was not a statistically significant difference across different values, 10 was determined to be the most appropriate number considering the trade-off between prompt length and performance. More rules increase the input dimensionality and thus the information available, but in the low-shot regime, this can also lead to overfitting.

The downstream linear model was trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.01 for 200 epochs. The Adam optimizer is a widely used adaptive learning rate optimization algorithm that combines the benefits of momentum and adaptive learning rates. For optimal epoch selection, $k$-fold cross-validation was employed, which helps prevent overfitting by validating the model's performance on held-out folds during training.

For the baseline methods, the implementation followed established practices. Traditional machine learning methods such as logistic regression, XGBoost, and RandomForest used grid search combined with $k$-fold cross-validation to determine their hyperparameters. The value of $k$ was set to either 2 or 4, with the constraint that the training set must include at least one example of each class. This constraint is essential in few-shot settings because with very few samples, there is a risk that a random split might exclude all examples of one class from the training set, making learning impossible.

## Main Results: FeatLLM's Performance Against Baselines in Various Few-Shot Regimes

The main experimental results provide a comprehensive evaluation of FeatLLM's performance across all 13 datasets and against all nine baselines. The experiments were repeated three times with different random seeds for the selection of training and test sets, and the results are reported as the average and standard deviation of the Area Under the Curve (AUC) values. AUC is a standard metric for classification that measures the ability of a model to distinguish between classes, with values ranging from 0.5 (random guessing) to 1.0 (perfect classification).

Across the diverse set of datasets, FeatLLM consistently ranked as the top performer or secured the second place when compared with all baseline models. This consistency is a strong indicator of the framework's robustness and generalizability. On average, FeatLLM outperformed alternatives such as TabLLM and STUNT by approximately 10\%, which is a substantial margin in the context of classification performance.

Several notable patterns emerged from the results. First, STUNT and TabPFN, despite demonstrating significant performance improvements on certain datasets, did not show substantial overall enhancement over conventional machine learning models. This suggests that while leveraging unlabeled data or pre-training on synthetic data can be beneficial in specific cases, these strategies do not consistently provide the broad advantages needed for practical few-shot tabular learning.

Second, LLM-based models such as in-context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM encountered difficulties on tabular datasets with a large number of features. This is a direct consequence of the prompt size limitation: when the number of features grows, the serialized text representation of the data becomes too long to fit within the LLM's input context window. This failure mode is particularly problematic because real-world tabular datasets in domains such as healthcare and finance often contain hundreds of features, precisely the scenarios where few-shot learning is most needed due to the difficulty and cost of obtaining labeled data.

Third, FeatLLM, through its use of bagging and ensemble techniques, proved effective even on datasets with numerous features. The bagging approach allows FeatLLM to select a subset of features for each trial, keeping the prompt size manageable. The ensemble then combines the weak learners from each trial, each of which may only consider a subset of features, into a robust final predictor. This approach elegantly addresses the prompt size limitation that cripples other LLM-based methods, making FeatLLM practical for a wider range of real-world applications.

The authors also note an important practical consideration regarding the bagging and ensemble concept. While this concept could in principle be adapted to existing LLM-based approaches, such an adaptation would require doubling the number of per-sample inferences through the LLM. Since these approaches already require one LLM inference per sample at test time, adding bagging and ensembling would substantially increase the already high computational complexity, rendering them impractical. FeatLLM avoids this problem entirely because it only uses the LLM during the feature engineering phase, and the downstream model used at inference time is a simple linear model that does not require LLM queries.

## Efficiency of FeatLLM Compared to Models Requiring Larger Sample Sizes

Beyond raw predictive performance, the efficiency of FeatLLM in terms of both sample complexity and computational cost is a critical aspect of its evaluation. The authors conducted specific analyses to quantify these advantages.

In terms of sample efficiency, FeatLLM demonstrated a remarkable ability to achieve competitive performance with very few labeled samples. When using just 4 shots (i.e., 4 labeled training examples per class), FeatLLM achieved performance comparable to conventional tabular baselines that required 32 shots. This eightfold reduction in the number of labeled samples needed has profound implications for real-world applications where labeled data is expensive or difficult to obtain. In healthcare, for example, obtaining labeled data often requires expert annotation by physicians, which is both costly and time-consuming. In finance, labeled data may require waiting for outcomes to materialize, such as loan defaults or fraud confirmations. The ability to achieve strong performance with minimal labeled data therefore directly translates to faster deployment and lower costs in practical settings.

The authors also compared the training and inference times between FeatLLM and other methods using the Adult dataset with a training set of 16 shots. For hardware, one A100 GPU was used as the default, except for TabLLM which required four A100 GPUs for model parallelism due to the memory requirements of fine-tuning a large language model. The runtime comparisons revealed that FeatLLM exhibits relatively low inference time, comparable to that of conventional methods like logistic regression, XGBoost, and RandomForest. This is because at inference time, FeatLLM only requires a simple dot product operation between the binary features and the learned weights, followed by a softmax function. No LLM query is needed for any test sample.

In contrast, other LLM-based approaches such as in-context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM exhibited much higher inference times. This is expected because each of these methods requires at least one LLM inference per test sample, and LLM inference is computationally expensive even when accessed through an API. For applications that require predictions on large numbers of samples, this per-sample LLM inference cost can become prohibitive, making these methods impractical for production deployment at scale.

In terms of training time, FeatLLM was comparable with other LLM-based methods. The training process for FeatLLM requires only 30 API queries to the LLM: these queries are used for rule generation across the ensemble trials. Since each query is independent, they can be parallelized, further reducing the wall-clock training time. This is a modest computational requirement that does not impose a significant burden on the budget, especially compared to methods that require fine-tuning large language models or processing each training sample through the LLM.

The combination of low inference cost, modest training cost, API-only access requirements, and the ability to handle large feature sets positions FeatLLM as a practical and efficient solution for few-shot tabular learning. The framework achieves high prediction performance while avoiding the computational bottlenecks that limit the applicability of other LLM-based approaches, making it a superior alternative for applying LLMs to real-world tabular data in a data-efficient manner.

Analysis and Discussion

# Ablation Study: Evaluating the Impact of Weight Tuning, Ensembling, Descriptions, and Reasoning

To truly understand how a complex machine learning framework achieves its performance, researchers rely on ablation studies. An ablation study is a systematic process of removing or disabling specific components of a model to observe how their absence affects overall performance. This process helps identify which parts of the architecture are critical to its success and which are merely supplementary. In the context of FeatLLM, the ablation study examines four key components: weight tuning, ensembling, feature descriptions, and reasoning instructions. 

The first component evaluated through ablation is weight tuning, denoted as "-Tuning" in the study. In the full FeatLLM framework, after the Large Language Model (LLM) generates a set of rules and converts them into binary features, a simple linear model is trained to learn the importance, or weights, of each rule for predicting class likelihood. The ablation "-Tuning" removes this training process; instead of learning the weights, the model simply sums up the binary features to compute the logit for each class. The findings reveal that omitting weight tuning degrades performance. Interestingly, the benefit of weight tuning becomes more pronounced as the number of shots, or training samples, increases. This indicates that with more data, the model can more accurately estimate the importance of different rules, making the tuning process highly effective.

The second component is ensembling, denoted as "-Ensemble". The full FeatLLM framework repeatedly executes the rule generation and prediction process multiple times with different random subsets of features and instances—a technique known as bagging—and averages their predictions. Removing this ensemble process leads to a drop in performance across all experimental settings. The ensemble approach is crucial because it allows correct rules generated across different trials to compensate for any incorrect rules produced in a single trial, resulting in robust predictions. 

The third and fourth components are feature descriptions ("-Description") and reasoning instructions ("-Reasoning"). The feature description provides the LLM with the value type and definition of each feature in the dataset. The reasoning instruction guides the LLM through a two-step process: first inferring the causal relationship between features and the task using general prior knowledge, and then deducing rules based on that knowledge and a few examples. Ablating these components significantly degrades performance, particularly in low-shot regimes where the number of training samples is very small. This observation suggests that when data is scarce, the efficient utilization of the LLM's prior knowledge—facilitated by explicit descriptions and structured reasoning—becomes the primary driver of performance improvement.

# Training and Inference Time Comparison Across Different Methods

Beyond predictive accuracy, the computational cost of a machine learning model is a critical factor for its real-world applicability. For tabular learning models utilizing LLMs, the computational expense can be a major bottleneck. This section compares the training and inference time of FeatLLM against both conventional machine learning baselines and other LLM-based methods. The experiments for this comparison are conducted on the Adult dataset using a training set of $16$ shots, utilizing an A100 GPU (with the TabLLM baseline specifically requiring four A100 GPUs for model parallelism).

At inference time, FeatLLM demonstrates a significant advantage over other LLM-based approaches. Methods like in-context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM require at least one LLM inference per sample, meaning the LLM must process each individual data point to generate a prediction. This makes their inference times substantially higher. In contrast, FeatLLM shifts the role of the LLM from end-to-end prediction to feature engineering. Once the LLM generates the rules and the new binary features are created, the LLM is no longer needed. The final prediction is made by a simple downstream linear model, which requires minimal computational overhead. As a result, FeatLLM's inference time is remarkably low and comparable to conventional tabular methods like Logistic Regression, XGBoost, and RandomForest.

When it comes to training time, FeatLLM remains efficient and practical. Although the framework involves querying the LLM to generate rules, it only requires $30$ API queries to generate the ensemble of rule sets. This number of queries does not impose a significant burden on the budget and, importantly, these queries can be parallelized to further reduce the actual wall-clock time. Thus, FeatLLM strikes an effective balance, achieving the high performance associated with LLM-based methods while avoiding the prohibitive inference costs that typically accompany them.

# Assessing the Quality and Informativeness of Generated Features

A fundamental question about any feature engineering framework is whether the newly generated features are actually informative for the task at hand. To investigate the quality of the rule-based features produced by FeatLLM, the researchers conducted a simple yet revealing experiment. They measured the average Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC) between the newly generated features and the target labels. An AUROC of $0.5$ indicates a random, uninformative feature, while values above $0.5$ indicate that the feature carries useful predictive information. By calculating the ratio of useful features—those with an AUROC greater than $0.5$—for each dataset, they could quantify the informativeness of the rules.

The results confirm that before any tuning takes place, the majority of the rules created by FeatLLM are relevant to the target attribute. This means the LLM is successfully leveraging its prior knowledge and the few-shot examples to generate features that provide informative insights for addressing the task. However, not all generated rules will be equally useful, and some might be redundant or irrelevant. 

To handle this, the linear model's weight tuning process acts as an automatic filter. During training, the model learns the importance of each rule-based feature. Rules that have little relevance to the data are assigned low weights. By applying a threshold (set at $0.1$ based on the overall histogram of weights), the framework automatically filters out these less relevant rules. After this tuning and filtering process, the retained features represent a highly purified set of rules, ensuring that the downstream model relies only on the most informative signals provided by the LLM.

# Robustness to Spurious Correlations in Low-Shot Regimes

In low-shot learning regimes, where the number of labeled training samples is extremely small, models are highly susceptible to learning spurious correlations—accidental relationships in the data that do not reflect true underlying causal factors. To test how effectively FeatLLM filters out such noise, the researchers designed a specific experiment using the Heart dataset. They progressively attached columns from the Adult dataset—which are entirely irrelevant to the Heart task—to the Heart data and measured the performance changes of various models. To ensure a fair comparison, all models were provided with the same $8$ shots of labeled samples and did not use any unlabeled datasets.

The results show that compared to baselines, FeatLLM exhibited the least performance degradation as the number of unrelated columns increased. This robustness is a direct result of the framework's design. When FeatLLM generates rules, it does not merely look for statistical patterns in the few-shot examples; it explicitly forces the consideration of the relationship between the task and the features based on the LLM's prior knowledge. This mechanism prevents the model from generating rules based on the irrelevant, noisy columns. In fact, the researchers observed that rules based on noisy columns accounted for only $1-2\%$ of the total rules generated.

However, the study also notes a limitation: if the attached noisy columns come from a closely related domain, FeatLLM can potentially be misled. In such cases, the LLM might misunderstand the causal relationships between the task and the features, leading it to learn spurious correlations. This highlights that while prior knowledge is a powerful tool for resisting random noise, it can sometimes be a vulnerability if the external data appears topically related but is not actually causally linked.

# Hyper-Parameter Analysis: Optimizing the Number of Ensembles and Rules

The performance and computational efficiency of FeatLLM are governed by two main hyper-parameters: the number of ensembles and the number of rules extracted per class during each LLM query. Understanding the impact of these hyper-parameters is essential for optimizing the framework's performance.

The first hyper-parameter is the number of ensembles. Because FeatLLM uses bagging and averaging to combine predictions from multiple trials, increasing the number of ensembles generally improves robustness and accuracy. Experiments showed that as the number of ensembles increases, performance does indeed improve. However, this improvement is subject to diminishing returns. Beyond a certain point—specifically, around $20$ ensembles—the performance gains begin to converge, meaning additional ensembles yield negligible benefits. Thus, $20$ is identified as an effective and efficient default number of ensembles.

The second hyper-parameter is the number of rules generated per class. The researchers conducted experiments to see how varying this number affects performance. While the differences were not statistically significant across various values, they determined that $10$ rules per class is the most appropriate choice. This decision is based on a trade-off between prompt length and performance. Extracting more rules increases the dimensionality of the input data, which theoretically provides more information to the model. However, in the low-shot regime, a high number of features can easily lead to overfitting, where the model memorizes the training data rather than learning generalizable patterns. Furthermore, increasing the number of rules enlarges the length of the prompt sent to the LLM, which can be computationally and financially costly. Therefore, setting the number of rules to $10$ provides an optimal balance, capturing enough informative rules to achieve high performance without unnecessarily bloating the prompt or inducing overfitting.

# Conclusion, Broader Impact, and Future Directions

# Summary of FeatLLM's contributions to few-shot tabular learning

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into tabular learning has historically been constrained by significant computational bottlenecks and practical limitations. Traditional approaches require passing every individual sample through the LLM during inference, which becomes prohibitively expensive and slow for large datasets. Furthermore, many high-performing LLM-based methods rely on fine-tuning the model parameters, an approach that is infeasible for state-of-the-art LLMs that are only accessible via inference APIs without exposed training codebases. Finally, as the number of features in a tabular dataset grows, the serialized text length can exceed the LLM's prompt size limit, leading to infeasibility or degraded performance. The \textsf{FeatLLM} framework introduces a novel paradigm that resolves these issues by shifting the role of the LLM from an end-to-end predictor to an automated feature engineer. 

\textsf{FeatLLM} leverages the in-context learning abilities of LLMs to deduce "rules," or the specific conditions associated with each answer class, from a small number of demonstration samples. This is achieved through a carefully structured prompt that guides the LLM to first infer the causal relationships between features and the target task using its prior knowledge, and then derive decisive rules based on the few-shot examples. Because the LLM generates these rules as natural language, \textsf{FeatLLM} employs the LLM itself to parse these rules into executable program code. This code is then used to transform the original tabular data into a set of binary features indicating whether each sample adheres to the generated rules. 

Once these binary features are extracted, the LLM is no longer needed. The framework relies on a simple downstream machine learning model—specifically, a linear model without bias—to estimate class likelihoods. By projecting the weights into a positive subspace and applying a softmax function, the model ensures that the LLM-generated features do not reduce the likelihood of a class during learning. To further enhance robustness and handle excessive prompt sizes, \textsf{FeatLLM} utilizes an ensemble approach combined with feature bagging. By repeatedly generating features with randomized demonstration orders and subset selections, the framework mitigates the impact of incorrect rules generated in individual trials, leveraging the LLM's self-consistency to ensure that commonly inferred rules dominate the final prediction. Ultimately, \textsf{FeatLLM} achieves high-performance few-shot learning, significantly outperforming alternatives like TabLLM and STUNT by an average of $10\%$, while requiring only API-level access and eliminating the need for per-sample LLM inference at test time.

# Broader impact: applications in data-scarce industries like finance and healthcare

The development of \textsf{FeatLLM} carries significant implications for real-world applications, particularly in domains where data collection is inherently difficult. Standard supervised tabular learning models are heavily reliant on large quantities of labeled data to achieve acceptable performance. However, in many critical industries, labeling data requires specialized expertise, making it a costly and time-consuming process. This limitation often leads to low-shot learning regimes where the number of available labeled samples is vastly insufficient for training complex models, increasing the risk of learning spurious correlations and overfitting. 

\textsf{FeatLLM} directly addresses this challenge by supplementing limited labeled data with the extensive prior knowledge encoded within LLMs. Because LLMs are trained on massive text corpora that encompass a wide range of human knowledge, they can act as a proxy for expert domain knowledge. This capability is particularly beneficial for practitioners in industries such as finance and healthcare. In healthcare, for instance, predicting patient outcomes or disease complications often relies on complex, high-dimensional tabular data where collecting a large, labeled dataset is constrained by patient privacy regulations and the high cost of medical expert annotation. Since LLMs are pre-trained with relevant medical and financial texts, the \textsf{FeatLLM} framework can leverage this embedded knowledge to generate meaningful, task-relevant features even when only a few labeled samples are available. 

By utilizing the LLM's prior knowledge to generate robust rules for feature engineering, \textsf{FeatLLM} pushes the boundaries of data efficiency, enabling models to generalize effectively from minimal supervision. This methodology allows practitioners in data-scarce fields to build high-performing predictive models without the prohibitive costs of massive data labeling campaigns. As a result, \textsf{FeatLLM} presents a superior alternative for applying LLMs to real-world tabular data in a way that is both computationally and economically feasible, mitigating the overfitting risks that plague standard supervised learning in low-data environments.

# Ethical considerations: societal biases and misinformation in LLM prior knowledge

While the incorporation of an LLM's prior knowledge into tabular learning provides a powerful mechanism for overcoming data scarcity, it also introduces significant ethical challenges that must be carefully considered. The core advantage of \textsf{FeatLLM}—its ability to draw on the broad, pre-trained knowledge of LLMs—becomes a potential liability when the information encoded within those models contains societal biases or misinformation. Because the framework relies on the LLM to infer causal relationships and generate decisive rules for feature creation, any prejudiced or factually incorrect concepts present in the LLM's training corpus will directly influence the engineered features. 

In the context of \textsf{FeatLLM}, the LLM's prior knowledge is not merely a passive background; it actively shapes the predictive model. The rules generated by the LLM are used to transform the input data, and these transformed features can dominate the final class likelihood estimates. If the LLM's prior knowledge includes, for example, historical biases regarding credit risk in finance or demographic disparities in healthcare outcomes, \textsf{FeatLLM} may inadvertently generate rules that reinforce these inequities. Because the LLM's knowledge is utilized to such an extent that it can dominate over the observed or collected labeled data samples, the model's predictions might reflect the biases of the LLM rather than the true underlying distribution of the target task.

Therefore, a critical area for future research and widespread adoption of this framework is the rigorous analysis and mitigation of these biases. Ensuring that the rules inferred by the LLM are fair, accurate, and free from harmful stereotypes is essential before deploying \textsf{FeatLLM} in sensitive real-world applications. Understanding how the LLM's prior knowledge interacts with the few-shot examples to form these rules is crucial for maintaining the integrity and fairness of the resulting tabular learning model. 

# Future work: scaling to larger datasets, exploring diverse feature types, and enhancing interpretability

Although \textsf{FeatLLM} presents a robust solution for few-shot tabular learning, its current iteration is specifically designed to operate within the low-shot learning regime. This focus means the framework is optimized for scenarios with minimal labeled data, and its behavior in data-rich environments remains an open question. One of the primary goals for future research is to expand the capabilities of \textsf{FeatLLM} to craft new features for datasets with a much larger number of samples. Scaling the framework to handle extensive datasets will require careful consideration of how the downstream linear model scales with an increased volume of data and whether the rule-generation process remains robust when the LLM is provided with a wider variety of few-shot examples.

Beyond scaling the data size, there is significant potential in diversifying the types of features that the framework can generate. Currently, \textsf{FeatLLM} extracts rules that are parsed into binary features, indicating whether a sample satisfies a specific condition. However, the rich prior knowledge of LLMs could be utilized to explore various other feature types beyond simple rule-based binaries. For instance, LLMs could potentially generate continuous features, complex interaction terms, or hierarchical categorical embeddings that capture more nuanced relationships within the data. Expanding the framework to support a wider array of feature types could further enhance its predictive power and adaptability to different forms of tabular data.

Finally, future work will focus on bringing greater interpretability to the feature engineering process. While the rule-based approach of \textsf{FeatLLM} inherently provides some level of interpretability by explicitly mapping conditions to class likelihoods, further improvements could make the model's decision-making process entirely transparent to human practitioners. Enhancing interpretability would not only build trust in the model's predictions but also enable domain experts to understand, validate, and refine the rules generated by the LLM. This focus on interpretability, combined with expanded capabilities in data scale and feature diversity, will enable the application of \textsf{FeatLLM} across a much wider array of tasks and industries.
