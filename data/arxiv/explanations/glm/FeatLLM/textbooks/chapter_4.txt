\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 4: Experimental Setup and Performance Evaluation

The experimental validation of any machine learning framework is essential to demonstrate its practical utility, robustness, and advantages over existing methods. In the case of FeatLLM, the authors designed a comprehensive set of experiments spanning a diverse collection of tabular datasets, comparing against nine different baseline methods, and evaluating performance across multiple few-shot learning regimes. This chapter walks through each component of the experimental design in detail, explaining not just what was done but why each choice matters for a fair and informative evaluation.

## Dataset Overview: Real-World, Recent, and Synthetic Tabular Datasets

A critical aspect of evaluating any machine learning method is the selection of datasets. The choice of datasets determines the breadth of domains covered, the variety of data characteristics tested, and ultimately the generalizability of the conclusions drawn. The authors of FeatLLM curated 13 datasets for binary or multi-class classification tasks, deliberately chosen to span a wide range of domains and to include both well-established benchmarks and carefully constructed datasets that guard against potential confounds.

The first group of datasets consists of nine real-world tabular datasets that have been widely used in the machine learning community. The Adult dataset focuses on predicting whether an individual earns over \$50,000 annually, a classic socio-economic prediction task. The Bank dataset involves predicting whether a customer will subscribe to a term deposit, representing a marketing and customer behavior prediction problem. The Blood dataset addresses whether donors will return for subsequent donations, which is a donor retention prediction task. The Car dataset involves predicting the quality of a car, and the Communities dataset focuses on predicting the crime rate in a specified region. The Credit-g dataset is concerned with predicting whether an individual poses a good or bad credit risk. The Diabetes dataset predicts the presence of diabetes in an individual, and the Heart dataset predicts the occurrence of coronary artery disease. Finally, the Myocardial dataset predicts whether an individual suffers from chronic heart failure.

These datasets vary significantly in size and complexity. A particularly important characteristic is the number of features: datasets such as Communities and Myocardial include over 100 attributes. This is significant because when tabular data is serialized into text for LLM consumption, the resulting prompt can exceed the LLM's context window size. This poses a fundamental challenge for LLM-based methods that require passing the entire feature set through the LLM for each sample. The inclusion of these high-dimensional datasets therefore tests whether methods can handle scenarios that are realistic in industrial applications but problematic for naive LLM-based approaches.

The second group consists of two recent real-world datasets: Cultivars and NHANES. The Cultivars dataset involves predicting how high the grain yield of a soybean cultivar will be, and the NHANES dataset involves predicting a given person's age group from their record. Both of these datasets were recently donated to the UCI Machine Learning Repository after September 2023. This timing is critical because the LLM API used by FeatLLM, specifically \texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0613}, would have completed its pre-training before these datasets became publicly available. This ensures that any performance FeatLLM achieves on these datasets is not a result of the LLM having memorized the data during pre-training, but rather stems from genuine reasoning and feature engineering capabilities.

The third group consists of two synthetic datasets: Sequence-type and Solution-mix. The Sequence-type dataset involves classifying a given sequence as one of four types: arithmetic, geometric, Fibonacci, or Collatz. The Solution-mix dataset involves predicting whether the percentage concentration of a mixture from four solutions exceeds 0.5. Because these datasets are synthetically generated, they provide an absolute guarantee that the LLM could not have encountered or memorized them during pre-training. This is an important control because one of the persistent concerns with evaluating LLM-based methods is the possibility that the model has seen the test data during pre-training, which would inflate performance estimates and lead to misleading conclusions.

Together, these 13 datasets cover diverse domains including healthcare, finance, social science, agriculture, and mathematics. Each dataset provides a clear name and description for each attribute, which is essential for FeatLLM because the framework relies on natural language feature descriptions to guide the LLM's rule generation process. The combination of well-known benchmarks, recent datasets, and synthetic data provides a thorough test of FeatLLM's ability to generalize across domains and its capacity for genuine reasoning rather than memorization.

## Baseline Methods: Traditional Machine Learning, Unlabeled Data Approaches, and LLM-Based Models

To rigorously evaluate FeatLLM, the authors compared it against nine baselines organized into three categories. Each category represents a distinct approach to tabular learning, and understanding the strengths and limitations of each baseline is essential for interpreting the significance of FeatLLM's performance.

The first category consists of conventional tabular learning approaches that do not leverage any external knowledge or LLM capabilities. These include logistic regression (LogReg), XGBoost, and RandomForest. Logistic regression is a simple linear model that has been a workhorse of statistical learning for decades. XGBoost is a gradient-boosted decision tree method known for its strong performance on tabular data and its dominance in many Kaggle competitions. RandomForest is an ensemble of decision trees that provides robust performance through bagging and random feature selection. These three methods serve as fundamental baselines: if FeatLLM cannot outperform them, its practical value would be questionable. For these traditional methods, hyperparameters were determined using grid search combined with $k$-fold cross-validation, ensuring that each baseline was given a fair opportunity to perform well.

The second category includes methods that assume access to large quantities of unlabeled data. These are SCARF and STUNT. SCARF is a contrastive learning method designed for tabular data that uses data augmentation to apply contrastive learning objectives. STUNT is a more recent approach that leverages unlabeled data for semi-supervised tabular learning. The inclusion of these baselines is important because they represent alternative strategies for dealing with the few-shot learning challenge. While FeatLLM leverages the prior knowledge embedded in LLMs to compensate for limited labeled data, SCARF and STUNT instead leverage additional unlabeled data. However, as the authors note, obtaining large quantities of unlabeled datasets may be infeasible in many real-world applications, making these methods less practically applicable in certain settings. Despite this caveat, including them as baselines provides a comprehensive comparison of different strategies for addressing the data scarcity problem.

The third category consists of recent approaches that also leverage LLMs for tabular learning. These are TabPFN, in-context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM. TabPFN generates a synthetic dataset with diverse distributions to pre-train a Bayesian neural network. After pre-training, inference is performed by inputting both training and test samples of the target task without further training. The prior knowledge gained from various datasets helps it surpass the performance of traditional tree-based approaches, particularly in few-shot settings. In-context learning directly embeds few-shot training samples into the input prompt without any tuning, relying on the LLM's ability to learn from examples in context. TABLET further incorporates additional hints into the prompt through an external classifier that provides rule-sets and prototypes to enhance the quality of inference. TabLLM employs parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques such as IA3 to train the LLM with few-shot samples, adapting the model's parameters to the specific task.

The LLM-based baselines are particularly important because they represent the most direct competitors to FeatLLM. All of these methods use the LLM for end-to-end prediction, meaning that each test sample must be passed through the LLM to obtain a prediction. This contrasts with FeatLLM, which uses the LLM only during the feature engineering phase and then employs a simple downstream model for inference. The comparison with these baselines therefore tests not only predictive performance but also the efficiency of the two paradigms: LLM-as-predictor versus LLM-as-feature-engineer.

An important implementation detail is that for the in-context learning baseline, GPT-3.5 was used as the backbone, while for TabLLM, the T0 model was employed following the original setting. This difference is necessitated by the fact that TabLLM requires parameter-efficient fine-tuning, which requires access to the model's internal parameters. GPT-3.5, being accessible only through an inference API, does not permit such fine-tuning. This constraint highlights a practical limitation of fine-tuning-based approaches: they cannot be applied to state-of-the-art LLMs that only provide API-level access, a growing concern as more powerful models are released with restricted access.

## Implementation Details: Model Configurations and Hyperparameter Settings

The implementation details of FeatLLM and its baselines are crucial for reproducibility and for understanding the computational costs associated with each approach. FeatLLM was designed to use GPT-3.5 as its LLM backbone, specifically the \texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0613} model, but the framework is designed to be agnostic to the choice of LLM. This means that any sufficiently capable LLM could be substituted, and the authors provide additional results with different backbones in the appendix.

For the LLM inference during rule generation, the temperature was set to 0.5 and the top-p value was set to the default value of 1. The temperature parameter controls the randomness of the LLM's output: a temperature of 0 would make the output deterministic, while higher temperatures introduce more variability. A temperature of 0.5 strikes a balance, introducing enough randomness to generate diverse rules across different ensemble trials while maintaining coherent and sensible rule generation. This is particularly important for the ensemble approach, where diversity among the individual models contributes to robustness.

Two key hyperparameters govern FeatLLM's behavior: the number of ensembles and the number of rules per class. The number of ensembles was set to 20, meaning that the entire process of rule generation, parsing, and model training was repeated 20 times, with the final prediction obtained by averaging the class probabilities across all 20 trials. The number of rules per class was set to 10, meaning that for each class, the LLM was instructed to generate 10 rules that distinguish that class from others. The authors investigated the impact of these hyperparameters and found that performance improves as the number of ensembles increases, but beyond approximately 20, the performance gains begin to converge. For the number of rules, while there was not a statistically significant difference across different values, 10 was determined to be the most appropriate number considering the trade-off between prompt length and performance. More rules increase the input dimensionality and thus the information available, but in the low-shot regime, this can also lead to overfitting.

The downstream linear model was trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.01 for 200 epochs. The Adam optimizer is a widely used adaptive learning rate optimization algorithm that combines the benefits of momentum and adaptive learning rates. For optimal epoch selection, $k$-fold cross-validation was employed, which helps prevent overfitting by validating the model's performance on held-out folds during training.

For the baseline methods, the implementation followed established practices. Traditional machine learning methods such as logistic regression, XGBoost, and RandomForest used grid search combined with $k$-fold cross-validation to determine their hyperparameters. The value of $k$ was set to either 2 or 4, with the constraint that the training set must include at least one example of each class. This constraint is essential in few-shot settings because with very few samples, there is a risk that a random split might exclude all examples of one class from the training set, making learning impossible.

## Main Results: FeatLLM's Performance Against Baselines in Various Few-Shot Regimes

The main experimental results provide a comprehensive evaluation of FeatLLM's performance across all 13 datasets and against all nine baselines. The experiments were repeated three times with different random seeds for the selection of training and test sets, and the results are reported as the average and standard deviation of the Area Under the Curve (AUC) values. AUC is a standard metric for classification that measures the ability of a model to distinguish between classes, with values ranging from 0.5 (random guessing) to 1.0 (perfect classification).

Across the diverse set of datasets, FeatLLM consistently ranked as the top performer or secured the second place when compared with all baseline models. This consistency is a strong indicator of the framework's robustness and generalizability. On average, FeatLLM outperformed alternatives such as TabLLM and STUNT by approximately 10\%, which is a substantial margin in the context of classification performance.

Several notable patterns emerged from the results. First, STUNT and TabPFN, despite demonstrating significant performance improvements on certain datasets, did not show substantial overall enhancement over conventional machine learning models. This suggests that while leveraging unlabeled data or pre-training on synthetic data can be beneficial in specific cases, these strategies do not consistently provide the broad advantages needed for practical few-shot tabular learning.

Second, LLM-based models such as in-context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM encountered difficulties on tabular datasets with a large number of features. This is a direct consequence of the prompt size limitation: when the number of features grows, the serialized text representation of the data becomes too long to fit within the LLM's input context window. This failure mode is particularly problematic because real-world tabular datasets in domains such as healthcare and finance often contain hundreds of features, precisely the scenarios where few-shot learning is most needed due to the difficulty and cost of obtaining labeled data.

Third, FeatLLM, through its use of bagging and ensemble techniques, proved effective even on datasets with numerous features. The bagging approach allows FeatLLM to select a subset of features for each trial, keeping the prompt size manageable. The ensemble then combines the weak learners from each trial, each of which may only consider a subset of features, into a robust final predictor. This approach elegantly addresses the prompt size limitation that cripples other LLM-based methods, making FeatLLM practical for a wider range of real-world applications.

The authors also note an important practical consideration regarding the bagging and ensemble concept. While this concept could in principle be adapted to existing LLM-based approaches, such an adaptation would require doubling the number of per-sample inferences through the LLM. Since these approaches already require one LLM inference per sample at test time, adding bagging and ensembling would substantially increase the already high computational complexity, rendering them impractical. FeatLLM avoids this problem entirely because it only uses the LLM during the feature engineering phase, and the downstream model used at inference time is a simple linear model that does not require LLM queries.

## Efficiency of FeatLLM Compared to Models Requiring Larger Sample Sizes

Beyond raw predictive performance, the efficiency of FeatLLM in terms of both sample complexity and computational cost is a critical aspect of its evaluation. The authors conducted specific analyses to quantify these advantages.

In terms of sample efficiency, FeatLLM demonstrated a remarkable ability to achieve competitive performance with very few labeled samples. When using just 4 shots (i.e., 4 labeled training examples per class), FeatLLM achieved performance comparable to conventional tabular baselines that required 32 shots. This eightfold reduction in the number of labeled samples needed has profound implications for real-world applications where labeled data is expensive or difficult to obtain. In healthcare, for example, obtaining labeled data often requires expert annotation by physicians, which is both costly and time-consuming. In finance, labeled data may require waiting for outcomes to materialize, such as loan defaults or fraud confirmations. The ability to achieve strong performance with minimal labeled data therefore directly translates to faster deployment and lower costs in practical settings.

The authors also compared the training and inference times between FeatLLM and other methods using the Adult dataset with a training set of 16 shots. For hardware, one A100 GPU was used as the default, except for TabLLM which required four A100 GPUs for model parallelism due to the memory requirements of fine-tuning a large language model. The runtime comparisons revealed that FeatLLM exhibits relatively low inference time, comparable to that of conventional methods like logistic regression, XGBoost, and RandomForest. This is because at inference time, FeatLLM only requires a simple dot product operation between the binary features and the learned weights, followed by a softmax function. No LLM query is needed for any test sample.

In contrast, other LLM-based approaches such as in-context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM exhibited much higher inference times. This is expected because each of these methods requires at least one LLM inference per test sample, and LLM inference is computationally expensive even when accessed through an API. For applications that require predictions on large numbers of samples, this per-sample LLM inference cost can become prohibitive, making these methods impractical for production deployment at scale.

In terms of training time, FeatLLM was comparable with other LLM-based methods. The training process for FeatLLM requires only 30 API queries to the LLM: these queries are used for rule generation across the ensemble trials. Since each query is independent, they can be parallelized, further reducing the wall-clock training time. This is a modest computational requirement that does not impose a significant burden on the budget, especially compared to methods that require fine-tuning large language models or processing each training sample through the LLM.

The combination of low inference cost, modest training cost, API-only access requirements, and the ability to handle large feature sets positions FeatLLM as a practical and efficient solution for few-shot tabular learning. The framework achieves high prediction performance while avoiding the computational bottlenecks that limit the applicability of other LLM-based approaches, making it a superior alternative for applying LLMs to real-world tabular data in a data-efficient manner.
