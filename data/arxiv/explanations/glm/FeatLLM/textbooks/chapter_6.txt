Chapter 6: Conclusion, Broader Impact, and Future Directions

# Summary of FeatLLM's contributions to few-shot tabular learning

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into tabular learning has historically been constrained by significant computational bottlenecks and practical limitations. Traditional approaches require passing every individual sample through the LLM during inference, which becomes prohibitively expensive and slow for large datasets. Furthermore, many high-performing LLM-based methods rely on fine-tuning the model parameters, an approach that is infeasible for state-of-the-art LLMs that are only accessible via inference APIs without exposed training codebases. Finally, as the number of features in a tabular dataset grows, the serialized text length can exceed the LLM's prompt size limit, leading to infeasibility or degraded performance. The \textsf{FeatLLM} framework introduces a novel paradigm that resolves these issues by shifting the role of the LLM from an end-to-end predictor to an automated feature engineer. 

\textsf{FeatLLM} leverages the in-context learning abilities of LLMs to deduce "rules," or the specific conditions associated with each answer class, from a small number of demonstration samples. This is achieved through a carefully structured prompt that guides the LLM to first infer the causal relationships between features and the target task using its prior knowledge, and then derive decisive rules based on the few-shot examples. Because the LLM generates these rules as natural language, \textsf{FeatLLM} employs the LLM itself to parse these rules into executable program code. This code is then used to transform the original tabular data into a set of binary features indicating whether each sample adheres to the generated rules. 

Once these binary features are extracted, the LLM is no longer needed. The framework relies on a simple downstream machine learning model—specifically, a linear model without bias—to estimate class likelihoods. By projecting the weights into a positive subspace and applying a softmax function, the model ensures that the LLM-generated features do not reduce the likelihood of a class during learning. To further enhance robustness and handle excessive prompt sizes, \textsf{FeatLLM} utilizes an ensemble approach combined with feature bagging. By repeatedly generating features with randomized demonstration orders and subset selections, the framework mitigates the impact of incorrect rules generated in individual trials, leveraging the LLM's self-consistency to ensure that commonly inferred rules dominate the final prediction. Ultimately, \textsf{FeatLLM} achieves high-performance few-shot learning, significantly outperforming alternatives like TabLLM and STUNT by an average of $10\%$, while requiring only API-level access and eliminating the need for per-sample LLM inference at test time.

# Broader impact: applications in data-scarce industries like finance and healthcare

The development of \textsf{FeatLLM} carries significant implications for real-world applications, particularly in domains where data collection is inherently difficult. Standard supervised tabular learning models are heavily reliant on large quantities of labeled data to achieve acceptable performance. However, in many critical industries, labeling data requires specialized expertise, making it a costly and time-consuming process. This limitation often leads to low-shot learning regimes where the number of available labeled samples is vastly insufficient for training complex models, increasing the risk of learning spurious correlations and overfitting. 

\textsf{FeatLLM} directly addresses this challenge by supplementing limited labeled data with the extensive prior knowledge encoded within LLMs. Because LLMs are trained on massive text corpora that encompass a wide range of human knowledge, they can act as a proxy for expert domain knowledge. This capability is particularly beneficial for practitioners in industries such as finance and healthcare. In healthcare, for instance, predicting patient outcomes or disease complications often relies on complex, high-dimensional tabular data where collecting a large, labeled dataset is constrained by patient privacy regulations and the high cost of medical expert annotation. Since LLMs are pre-trained with relevant medical and financial texts, the \textsf{FeatLLM} framework can leverage this embedded knowledge to generate meaningful, task-relevant features even when only a few labeled samples are available. 

By utilizing the LLM's prior knowledge to generate robust rules for feature engineering, \textsf{FeatLLM} pushes the boundaries of data efficiency, enabling models to generalize effectively from minimal supervision. This methodology allows practitioners in data-scarce fields to build high-performing predictive models without the prohibitive costs of massive data labeling campaigns. As a result, \textsf{FeatLLM} presents a superior alternative for applying LLMs to real-world tabular data in a way that is both computationally and economically feasible, mitigating the overfitting risks that plague standard supervised learning in low-data environments.

# Ethical considerations: societal biases and misinformation in LLM prior knowledge

While the incorporation of an LLM's prior knowledge into tabular learning provides a powerful mechanism for overcoming data scarcity, it also introduces significant ethical challenges that must be carefully considered. The core advantage of \textsf{FeatLLM}—its ability to draw on the broad, pre-trained knowledge of LLMs—becomes a potential liability when the information encoded within those models contains societal biases or misinformation. Because the framework relies on the LLM to infer causal relationships and generate decisive rules for feature creation, any prejudiced or factually incorrect concepts present in the LLM's training corpus will directly influence the engineered features. 

In the context of \textsf{FeatLLM}, the LLM's prior knowledge is not merely a passive background; it actively shapes the predictive model. The rules generated by the LLM are used to transform the input data, and these transformed features can dominate the final class likelihood estimates. If the LLM's prior knowledge includes, for example, historical biases regarding credit risk in finance or demographic disparities in healthcare outcomes, \textsf{FeatLLM} may inadvertently generate rules that reinforce these inequities. Because the LLM's knowledge is utilized to such an extent that it can dominate over the observed or collected labeled data samples, the model's predictions might reflect the biases of the LLM rather than the true underlying distribution of the target task.

Therefore, a critical area for future research and widespread adoption of this framework is the rigorous analysis and mitigation of these biases. Ensuring that the rules inferred by the LLM are fair, accurate, and free from harmful stereotypes is essential before deploying \textsf{FeatLLM} in sensitive real-world applications. Understanding how the LLM's prior knowledge interacts with the few-shot examples to form these rules is crucial for maintaining the integrity and fairness of the resulting tabular learning model. 

# Future work: scaling to larger datasets, exploring diverse feature types, and enhancing interpretability

Although \textsf{FeatLLM} presents a robust solution for few-shot tabular learning, its current iteration is specifically designed to operate within the low-shot learning regime. This focus means the framework is optimized for scenarios with minimal labeled data, and its behavior in data-rich environments remains an open question. One of the primary goals for future research is to expand the capabilities of \textsf{FeatLLM} to craft new features for datasets with a much larger number of samples. Scaling the framework to handle extensive datasets will require careful consideration of how the downstream linear model scales with an increased volume of data and whether the rule-generation process remains robust when the LLM is provided with a wider variety of few-shot examples.

Beyond scaling the data size, there is significant potential in diversifying the types of features that the framework can generate. Currently, \textsf{FeatLLM} extracts rules that are parsed into binary features, indicating whether a sample satisfies a specific condition. However, the rich prior knowledge of LLMs could be utilized to explore various other feature types beyond simple rule-based binaries. For instance, LLMs could potentially generate continuous features, complex interaction terms, or hierarchical categorical embeddings that capture more nuanced relationships within the data. Expanding the framework to support a wider array of feature types could further enhance its predictive power and adaptability to different forms of tabular data.

Finally, future work will focus on bringing greater interpretability to the feature engineering process. While the rule-based approach of \textsf{FeatLLM} inherently provides some level of interpretability by explicitly mapping conditions to class likelihoods, further improvements could make the model's decision-making process entirely transparent to human practitioners. Enhancing interpretability would not only build trust in the model's predictions but also enable domain experts to understand, validate, and refine the rules generated by the LLM. This focus on interpretability, combined with expanded capabilities in data scale and feature diversity, will enable the application of \textsf{FeatLLM} across a much wider array of tasks and industries.
