Chapter 2: Background and Related Work

## Challenges and Strategies in Few-Shot Tabular Learning

Few-shot learning has emerged as a critical paradigm in machine learning, driven by the practical need to extract generalizable representations from data even when labeling costs are prohibitive. The advancement of few-shot learning has enabled models to learn effectively from minimal labeled samples, a capability initially demonstrated on image data and subsequently extended across various data modalities including text, audio, and, more recently, tabular data. Tabular data, which underpins countless real-world applications in domains such as healthcare, finance, and social sciences, presents unique challenges that distinguish it from other data modalities and make few-shot learning particularly difficult.

The fundamental challenge in few-shot tabular learning stems from the risk of learning spurious correlations when only a small number of labeled samples are available. With limited data, conventional supervised learning models tend to identify patterns that exist in the training samples by chance rather than capturing genuine, generalizable relationships between features and targets. This problem is exacerbated by the heterogeneous nature of tabular data, where features may be of different types—numerical, categorical, or ordinal—and may exhibit complex, non-linear interactions that are difficult to discern from few examples. Unlike image or text data, where spatial or sequential structure can be exploited by convolutional or recurrent architectures, tabular data lacks such inherent structural priors that could guide learning in low-data regimes.

To address these challenges, recent efforts have explored strategies that go beyond standard supervised learning by incorporating additional sources of information or domain-specific prior knowledge to impart appropriate inductive biases for model training. One prominent direction involves leveraging large quantities of more readily-available unlabeled data through semi-supervised learning approaches, where unlabeled samples are used alongside the few labeled examples to learn better representations. Another direction involves seeking good model initialization through task-agnostic unsupervised pre-training, where models are first trained on auxiliary objectives before being fine-tuned on the target task with limited labels. The central intuition behind all these strategies is that external knowledge—whether from unlabeled data, pre-training tasks, or domain expertise—can compensate for the scarcity of labeled samples and help the model avoid overfitting to spurious patterns.

However, the heterogeneous nature of tabular data poses a persistent obstacle: it is challenging to find universally applicable data augmentations or pre-training objectives that transfer well across diverse tabular tasks. Unlike images, where augmentations like cropping, flipping, or color jittering are broadly effective, or text, where paraphrasing and back-translation are widely applicable, tabular augmentations must respect the semantic meaning and type constraints of each column, making them task-specific and less generalizable. As a result, many of these augmentation-based strategies have not been notably effective in few-shot settings, motivating the exploration of alternative approaches such as transfer learning and language-interfaced methods that can inject richer prior knowledge into the learning process.

## Unsupervised Pre-training and Data Augmentation Approaches

Unsupervised pre-training and data augmentation have been explored as means to improve few-shot tabular learning by extracting useful representations from data without relying on labels. The core idea is to leverage the structure present in the data itself—either through unlabeled samples or through self-supervised objectives—to learn representations that generalize better when only a few labeled examples are available for the downstream task.

Several approaches have adopted semi-supervised learning frameworks that utilize large quantities of unlabeled data alongside a small number of labeled samples. For instance, SubTab employs judicious augmentation of unlabeled data to enable semi-supervised learning, seeking to extract useful signal from the unlabeled portion of the dataset to complement the limited labeled examples. Similarly, VIME follows a comparable philosophy, using unlabeled data to improve model performance in regimes where labeled data is scarce. The intuition is that even without labels, the distributional structure of the data can reveal meaningful patterns that aid generalization.

Another family of approaches relies on task-agnostic unsupervised pre-training objectives. These methods typically involve masking or corrupting part of the input data and then training a model to reconstruct the original values, thereby learning useful representations of the data distribution. TabNet, for example, employs such a reconstruction objective as part of its pre-training procedure. Similarly, MET utilizes masking-based objectives to learn representations that can later be fine-tuned for downstream few-shot tasks. The rationale is that a model trained to predict missing values must understand the relationships between features, and this understanding transfers to improved performance when labels are scarce.

Data augmentation has also been applied in the context of contrastive learning objectives for tabular data. SCARF, for instance, generates augmented views of tabular samples and applies contrastive learning to bring representations of augmentations of the same sample closer together while pushing apart representations of different samples. This approach, inspired by successful contrastive learning frameworks in other domains, aims to learn invariant representations that are robust to perturbations in the input. The general principle of contrastive learning—learning by comparing positive and negative pairs—has proven powerful in self-supervised settings across modalities.

STUNT represents another approach that leverages unlabeled data, using it to generate self-supervised signals that can guide few-shot learning. By treating the unlabeled data as a source of auxiliary training signal, STUNT seeks to improve the model's ability to generalize from the few labeled examples available. This approach, along with others that assume access to large unlabeled datasets, can provide meaningful improvements, though obtaining such large quantities of unlabeled data may be infeasible in many real-world applications.

Despite the promise of these methods, the heterogeneous nature of tabular data makes it challenging to design augmentations and pre-training objectives that are universally effective. Unlike images, where standard augmentations preserve semantic content, or text, where meaning is largely preserved under paraphrasing, perturbations to tabular data can easily produce semantically invalid samples—for example, by assigning an impossible value to a categorical feature or breaking the logical relationship between two numerical columns. Consequently, these strategies have not been notably effective in few-shot settings, as noted in the literature, motivating the exploration of transfer learning and language-based approaches that can inject richer, more structured prior knowledge.

## Transfer Learning in Tabular Data

Transfer learning has emerged as a promising strategy for few-shot tabular learning, aiming to overcome the limitations of augmentation-based approaches by transferring knowledge acquired from a broad range of tabular datasets to the target task. The central idea is that a model pre-trained on diverse tabular tasks can learn generalizable representations that facilitate zero-shot or few-shot inference on new, previously unseen tabular tasks, thereby reducing the dependence on large labeled datasets for each individual task.

TransTab exemplifies this approach by training transformer-based models that learn semantic relationships between columns through their names, rather than relying solely on the raw values in the tables. By incorporating column names as a source of semantic information, TransTab can capture the meaning of features in a way that generalizes across different tables and tasks. For instance, a column named "age" in one dataset and "years" in another may serve similar purposes, and a model that understands this semantic correspondence can transfer knowledge between them. The knowledge extracted from various tables and columns during pre-training enables the model to perform zero-shot or few-shot inference for new tabular tasks, leveraging the semantic understanding acquired during pre-training to make predictions even when labeled data for the new task is minimal.

TabPFN takes a different approach to transfer learning for tabular data. Rather than pre-training on real tabular datasets, it generates synthetic data by mixing different types of distributions to create a diverse pre-training corpus. A Bayesian neural network is then pre-trained on this synthetic data, learning a prior over tabular learning tasks that captures the diversity of possible relationships between features and targets. After pre-training, inference is performed by inputting both training and test samples of the target task into the network without any further training. The model leverages the prior knowledge gained from pre-training on diverse synthetic distributions to make predictions on the target task, effectively using the pre-trained prior as a substitute for the large labeled dataset that would otherwise be needed. This approach has been shown to surpass the performance of traditional tree-based methods and has proven particularly beneficial in few-shot settings, where the pre-trained prior can guide predictions when data is scarce.

Other transfer learning approaches for tabular data include generative models that learn to generate synthetic tabular data for pre-training and methods that seek to identify common structural patterns across diverse tabular datasets. The general principle underlying all these approaches is that knowledge acquired from one set of tasks can be transferred to improve performance on a different but related task, reducing the amount of labeled data needed for the target task. This is particularly valuable in few-shot settings, where the ability to leverage prior knowledge can mean the difference between a model that generalizes and one that overfits to the few available examples.

Despite their promise, transfer learning approaches face their own challenges. The diversity of tabular tasks in terms of feature types, target distributions, and underlying relationships means that knowledge transfer is not always straightforward. A model pre-trained on one set of tasks may not transfer well to a task with fundamentally different structure, and the synthetic data generated for pre-training may not capture the full range of patterns present in real-world tabular data. Nevertheless, these approaches represent an important step toward data-efficient tabular learning and have demonstrated that transfer learning can be a viable strategy for improving few-shot performance.

## Language-Interfaced Tabular Learning: Data Serialization and In-Context Learning

Language-interfaced tabular learning represents a paradigm shift in how prior knowledge is incorporated into tabular prediction tasks. The approach harnesses the rich prior knowledge encapsulated by Large Language Models (LLMs), which have been trained on extensive and diverse text corpora and have demonstrated the ability to generalize to unseen tasks without task-specific fine-tuning. LLMs possess broad knowledge across domains—from medical and financial concepts to social and scientific phenomena—making them a potentially valuable source of prior knowledge for tabular learning tasks where domain expertise is beneficial.

The fundamental mechanism of language-interfaced tabular learning is data serialization: tabular data is converted into natural language text that can be fed into the input prompts of LLMs. The serialization process transforms each sample from a vector of feature values into a textual description that pairs each feature name with its corresponding value, followed by the label. Specifically, the serialization follows a structured format such as "$f_1$ is $\mathbf{x}^i_1$. ... $f_d$ is $\mathbf{x}^i_d$. Answer: $\mathbf{y}^i$", where $f_j$ denotes the natural language name of the $j$-th feature, $\mathbf{x}^i_j$ is its value for the $i$-th sample, and $\mathbf{y}^i$ is the corresponding label. This serialized representation, along with feature descriptions and task descriptions, is included in the LLM's input prompt, enabling the model to infer the relationships between features and the target variable.

In-context learning is a key capability that makes language-interfaced tabular learning feasible without requiring any model training. By including a task description and a few labeled examples in the prompt, the LLM can understand the context of the task and "attend" to important features and examples, effectively performing few-shot learning through prompt engineering alone. For instance, when given a task description such as "Does this patient's myocardial infarction complications data indicate chronic heart failure? Yes or no?" along with a few example demonstrations of patients with known outcomes, the LLM can leverage both its prior knowledge about heart disease and the patterns in the provided examples to make predictions on new, unlabeled samples. This demonstrates the capability of LLMs to analyze data and act as expert feature engineers, attending to the most relevant features for the task at hand.

The prior knowledge provided by LLMs has proven effective in tabular learning, outperforming conventional tabular learning baselines in low-shot regimes. For example, LLMs can bring to bear knowledge such as the increased risk of certain diseases in older individuals, which can be beneficial for disease prediction tasks even when the training data is too sparse for conventional models to learn such relationships. By incorporating feature descriptions and task descriptions in natural language, the LLM can infer the semantic meaning of features and their relevance to the target, enabling it to make predictions that go beyond what would be possible from the few labeled examples alone.

However, current LLM-based tabular learning methods have several limitations. For end-to-end predictions, at least one LLM inference is required per sample, making the approach computationally expensive, especially when dealing with large datasets. Most methods also require fine-tuning the LLM for high accuracy, which is infeasible for LLMs that do not come with accessible training codebases or tuning APIs—a prominent concern given that recently-proposed top-performance LLMs only permit limited access via inference APIs. Additionally, most approaches struggle with lengthy prompts: when the number of features in tabular data grows to the point where the serialized text becomes long, the approach often becomes infeasible or experiences a decline in performance due to the limited context window of LLMs. These constraints pose fundamental challenges to the real-world application of LLM-based tabular learning models, motivating new approaches that can leverage LLM prior knowledge without requiring per-sample inference at test time.

## Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning Techniques

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques have become an important tool for adapting LLMs to specific tasks, including tabular learning, without the prohibitive computational cost of fine-tuning all parameters of the model. These methods enable the adaptation of large, pre-trained language models to downstream tasks by updating only a small subset of parameters, thereby reducing the computational and memory requirements while still achieving performance improvements that approach or match those of full fine-tuning.

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) is one such parameter-efficient fine-tuning technique that has been employed in the context of language-interfaced tabular learning. The core idea behind LoRA is to freeze the pre-trained model weights and inject trainable rank decomposition matrices into each layer of the transformer architecture. By learning only these low-rank updates, LoRA can adapt the model to a new task with a fraction of the trainable parameters that would be required for full fine-tuning. This makes it feasible to fine-tune large language models on tabular tasks even with limited computational resources, though it still requires access to the model's internal parameters and training infrastructure.

IA3 is another parameter-efficient fine-tuning technique that has been specifically utilized in the context of tabular learning. IA3 works by injecting trainable vectors into the attention mechanism and feed-forward layers of the transformer, scaling the keys, values, and intermediate activations. These injected vectors have a very small number of parameters compared to the full model, making the fine-tuning process highly efficient. TabLLM, for example, employs IA3 to train an LLM with few-shot samples for tabular prediction tasks, achieving strong performance by combining the prior knowledge of the pre-trained LLM with task-specific adaptation through IA3. The approach serializes tabular data into text, includes feature and task descriptions, and then fine-tunes the LLM using IA3 to adapt it to the specific tabular prediction task.

Despite the effectiveness of parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques, they introduce additional constraints on the applicability of language-interfaced tabular learning. Most critically, these techniques require access to the model's internal parameters and training code, which is often not available for state-of-the-art LLMs that are only accessible through inference APIs. Many recently proposed top-performance LLMs, such as PaLM and GPT-4, only permit limited access via inference APIs, making parameter-efficient fine-tuning infeasible for these models. This limitation restricts the applicability of approaches like TabLLM to LLMs with publicly available checkpoints, such as T0, which may not possess the same level of prior knowledge or reasoning capability as the most advanced models.

Furthermore, even when parameter-efficient fine-tuning is feasible, the resulting models still require passing each sample through the LLM at inference time, incurring significant computational cost compared to conventional machine learning approaches. The combination of training requirements and per-sample inference cost creates a barrier to the widespread adoption of these methods in real-world applications, particularly in industrial settings where inference latency and computational budget are critical concerns. These limitations highlight the need for alternative approaches that can leverage the prior knowledge of LLMs for tabular learning without requiring model fine-tuning or per-sample LLM inference, motivating frameworks like FeatLLM that use LLMs as feature engineers rather than end-to-end predictors.
