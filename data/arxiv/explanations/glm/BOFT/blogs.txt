# Adapting AI Giants: How BOFT Makes Fine-Tuning Massive Models Affordable

## The Challenge of Adapting Foundation Models

Large foundation models like ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion are incredibly powerful, but training them from scratch is prohibitively expensive. To leverage these models for specific downstream tasks, we need efficient adaptation methods. While there are various ways to adapt these models—such as adding adapter layers or using prompt tuning—model finetuning stands out because it introduces no additional inference latency. The fundamental principle behind model finetuning is to ensure that the finetuned model remains similar to the pretrained model, thereby preserving the hard-earned pretraining knowledge.

## The Bottleneck in Orthogonal Finetuning

One promising finetuning approach is Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). The core idea behind OFT is to preserve the relational information—specifically the pairwise angles between neurons—of the pretrained weight matrices. It achieves this by transforming the neurons of the same layer with a learnable orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$. This preserves the semantic knowledge from pretraining and ensures stable training.

However, OFT has a catch: a dense $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. To reduce this, OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure, breaking the matrix into smaller independent blocks. While this saves parameters, it introduces a rigid sparsity pattern. Dimensions in different blocks are transformed independently, reducing the model's expressiveness and preventing it from approximating classic linear transforms. A natural question arises: can we construct a dense orthogonal matrix without losing parameter efficiency?

## Enter BOFT: The Orthogonal Butterfly

The solution proposed in the paper is Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT). BOFT composes a dense orthogonal matrix by multiplying several sparse orthogonal matrices together: $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{B}_m \mathbf{B}_{m-1} \cdots \mathbf{B}_1$. 

To understand why this works, the authors frame the problem as an information transmission task on a grid. Generating a dense matrix is like ensuring information can flow from every node in the first level of a grid to every node in the last level. A naive dense connection requires too many edges, corresponding to a massive number of parameters. 

Here is where the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm comes to the rescue. The butterfly structure in this algorithm efficiently exchanges information with $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges. By applying butterfly factorization, BOFT generates a $d$-dimensional dense orthogonal matrix using a product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse matrices, each having $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries. This drops the required parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ down to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$, a massive reduction for high-dimensional models.

## Why BOFT Excels

BOFT introduces a generalized orthogonal finetuning framework that subsumes OFT as a special case. By using the butterfly structure, BOFT creates a smoother interpolation between the expressivity of a full orthogonal matrix and the regularity of an identity matrix. This structured approach brings several advantages:

*   **Drastic Parameter Reduction**: BOFT significantly cuts down the trainable parameters. The paper shows that BOFT with a smaller block size and more factorization steps can yield similar approximation errors to standard block-diagonal OFT but with far fewer parameters. In general, BOFT takes $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters for finetuning a linear layer of size $d \times n$.
*   **Knowledge Preservation**: Like OFT, BOFT multiplies the pretrained weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^0$ by an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$. This preserves the spectral norm of the weights, ensuring that the pretrained knowledge is largely retained and training remains stable.
*   **No Inference Latency**: Although BOFT introduces a slight training runtime overhead due to the repeated matrix multiplications, the learned orthogonal matrices can be multiplied directly into the pretrained weights after finetuning. This means there is zero additional inference latency when the model is deployed.
*   **Better Inductive Bias**: Because the butterfly structure is the backbone of many fast linear transforms like the discrete Fourier transform, it introduces a structural prior that benefits generalization and prevents overfitting.

In short, BOFT offers a principled way to adapt massive AI models. By framing dense orthogonal matrix generation as an information transmission problem and leveraging the butterfly structure, BOFT drastically cuts down the trainable parameters while maintaining the dense connections needed to preserve the model's original knowledge.



# The Butterfly Effect in AI: Borrowing Math from 1960s Algorithms for Modern Fine-Tuning

## The Heavy Cost of Adapting Giants

Foundation models like ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion are powerful, but their massive scale makes training them from scratch prohibitively expensive. As models grow to hundreds of billions of parameters, researchers must find efficient ways to adapt them to specific downstream tasks without retraining. One elegant approach is Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), which updates a pretrained model by multiplying its frozen weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. This preserves the pairwise angles between neurons, ensuring that the model's pretraining knowledge is not destroyed during the fine-tuning process.

However, OFT comes with a heavy computational price. A $d$-dimensional dense orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. To reduce this cost, the original OFT method introduced a block-diagonal structure, treating the matrix as a set of independent, smaller orthogonal matrices. While this saves parameters, it introduces an unnatural assumption: it forces the dimensions of a neuron to be divided into isolated groups based on their indices, which are transformed independently. This limits expressivity and cannot approximate classic linear transforms.

## Viewing Matrices as Information Networks

To build a dense orthogonal matrix without the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameter cost, the authors of the BOFT paper propose an "information transmission" perspective. If we decompose a dense matrix $\bm{R}$ into a product of multiple sparse matrices, such as $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$, we can view this as transmitting information across a grid-structured graph.

In this graph, the matrix $\bm{B}_i$ represents the connections between the $i$-th level of nodes and the $(i+1)$-th level. For the final matrix $\bm{R}$ to be dense, information from every node in the first level must be able to reach every node in the last level. The challenge becomes finding a sequence of sparse matrices that achieves this dense connectivity while using as few trainable parameters (or edges in the graph) as possible.

## The Cooley-Tukey Connection

This is where a 1960s breakthrough in signal processing comes to the rescue. The Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm revolutionized signal processing by efficiently computing the Discrete Fourier Transform. A key component of this algorithm is the "butterfly structure"—a network topology that allows for highly efficient information exchange between nodes.

The authors realized that the butterfly structure is exactly what is needed to solve their information transmission problem. In a butterfly network, information from different nodes can be exchanged efficiently using $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges, rather than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. By applying butterfly factorization, a $d$-dimensional dense matrix can be represented as a product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse matrices, each containing $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries.

## Enter Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)

The paper introduces Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), which applies this butterfly factorization to orthogonal finetuning. The key insight is that if each individual sparse matrix in the factorization is orthogonal, their product will also be a dense orthogonal matrix.

To achieve this, BOFT parameterizes the butterfly components using small, orthogonal block matrices (constructed via a Cayley transform). By chaining these together, BOFT creates a dense orthogonal transformation using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters—a dramatic reduction from the original OFT's $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ requirement.

BOFT also subsumes the original OFT as a special case. When the factorization depth $m = 1$, BOFT reduces to the block-diagonal OFT. By increasing $m$, we can smoothly interpolate between the regularity of an identity matrix and the expressivity of a full orthogonal matrix.

## Why Butterflies Beat Blocks

The structural prior provided by the butterfly matrix offers significant advantages over the block-diagonal approach. Because butterfly structures are the backbone of many classic fast linear transforms—like the FFT, discrete cosine transform, and Hadamard transform—BOFT can easily recover these transformations. The block-diagonal structure in original OFT cannot.

This gives BOFT a highly beneficial inductive bias. By constraining the fine-tuning to a structured subset of the orthogonal group, BOFT prevents overfitting while maintaining enough expressivity to adapt to new tasks. Empirically, the authors show that BOFT yields better parameter efficiency than OFT. For example, BOFT($9, 2$) is more expressive than the original block-diagonal OFT with block size 16, yet it uses far fewer parameters.

## A Drop-In Replacement for Modern AI

By making orthogonal finetuning parameter-efficient, BOFT transforms it from a niche technique for controlling text-to-image diffusion models into a general-purpose fine-tuning method. The authors applied BOFT to a wide range of tasks, adapting large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models. Across the board, BOFT outperformed current state-of-the-art parameter-efficient finetuning methods, validating its superior generalization and efficiency.

While BOFT introduces slightly more training runtime overhead due to the repeated matrix multiplications, the learned orthogonal matrices can be multiplied directly into the pretrained weights after training. This means BOFT introduces zero inference latency. It is a powerful reminder that sometimes the most advanced AI innovations can be found by revisiting classic algorithms from the early days of computer science.



# Networking Meets Neural Networks: Viewing Fine-Tuning as an Information Highway

Adapting massive foundation models like GPT-3 or Stable Diffusion to new tasks is a major challenge. Training them from scratch is prohibitively expensive, so we rely on fine-tuning. One powerful method is Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), which transforms a model's neurons using orthogonal matrices to preserve the pairwise angles between them. This maintains the pretraining knowledge beautifully, but there's a catch: high-dimensional orthogonal matrices require a massive number of trainable parameters. 

To solve this, a recent paper introduces Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT). The authors tackle the parameter-efficiency problem by taking a fascinating step back, viewing the construction of orthogonal matrices through the lens of an information transmission network.

## The Information Transmission Perspective

To reduce the number of trainable parameters in OFT, the authors propose composing a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse matrices. Mathematically, we want to generate a dense matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ via a factorization like $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$. 

Instead of looking at this purely through linear algebra, the authors reframe it as an information transmission problem on a grid-structured graph. Imagine a grid with $d$ rows and $m+1$ columns of nodes. 

- Each matrix $\bm{B}_i$ represents the connectivity (the directed edges) between the nodes in the $i$-th level and the $(i+1)$-th level. 
- If the element at the $(j_1, j_2)$ position of $\bm{B}_i$ is non-zero, it means there is a directed edge allowing information to flow from the $j_2$-th node in level $i$ to the $j_1$-th node in level $i+1$. 
- For the final composed matrix $\bm{R}$ to be dense, every node in the very first level must have a path to transmit information to every node in the very last level. 

This graphical perspective makes it much easier to reason about how to build our matrices. To achieve parameter efficiency, we want two things: dense connectivity (so information can flow everywhere) and the minimum number of free edges (to keep trainable parameters low).

## The Block-Diagonal Bottleneck

The original OFT attempted to save parameters by using a block-diagonal structure for the orthogonal matrix. In this setup, the matrix is divided into smaller independent blocks along the diagonal. 

Through the information transmission lens, we can instantly see the flaw in this approach. A block-diagonal structure creates isolated sub-networks. Information can flow freely within a block, but it can never cross over to a different block. Because information from the first level cannot reach all nodes in the final level, the resulting matrix is sparse. This severely limits the expressiveness of the finetuned model, preventing it from approximating classic linear transforms.

## Butterflies to the Rescue

We need a network topology that can connect every source node to every receiver node using as few edges as possible. The authors find the perfect solution in an unexpected place: the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithm.

The FFT algorithm uses a "butterfly" structure to efficiently exchange information, where a local change in the frequency domain causes a global change in the spatial domain. By applying butterfly factorization, the authors create a dense orthogonal matrix using a product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse matrices, each with $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries. 

This brings the total parameter count down from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for a dense matrix to just $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ for BOFT, all while ensuring the matrix remains completely dense and orthogonal! The resulting method, BOFT, generalizes OFT (which is just a special case of BOFT) and allows for a much smoother interpolation between expressivity and regularity.

## Why This Matters

By visualizing matrix factorization as information passing through a grid-like network, the authors make the underlying math of orthogonal finetuning intuitive and accessible. This networking perspective doesn't just simplify the concept; it actively guides the design of a better algorithm. 

BOFT’s structured approach introduces a beneficial inductive bias that prevents overfitting and perfectly preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weights. Because the learned matrices can simply be multiplied back into the pretrained weights after training, BOFT introduces zero inference latency. It's a fantastic example of how borrowing concepts from entirely different disciplines—like computer networking and signal processing—can push the boundaries of modern machine learning.



# Beyond Theory: How BOFT Outperforms LoRA in Vision, Language, and Image Generation

Training large foundation models like ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion from scratch is prohibitively expensive. As a result, researchers have developed various parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) methods to adapt these massive models to specific downstream tasks. While techniques like LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) have gained immense popularity, a new method called Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) is pushing the boundaries of parameter efficiency and generalization across vision, language, and image generation tasks.

## The Challenge of Preserving Pretrained Knowledge

When finetuning a foundation model, the goal is to adapt it to a new task without losing the vast amount of knowledge it acquired during pretraining. Most finetuning methods update the model's weights, but doing so haphazardly can destroy the pretraining knowledge. A principled approach, known as Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), tackles this by transforming the neurons of a layer with an orthogonal matrix. This preserves the pairwise angles between neurons, ensuring the semantic knowledge from pretraining remains intact.

However, OFT has a practical drawback: high dimensionality. Generating a dense $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is not very efficient. The original OFT method tried to solve this by using a block-diagonal structure, but this forced the orthogonal transformation to be applied independently in separate blocks, reducing expressiveness and introducing undesirable inductive biases.

## Enter the Butterfly Structure

To solve OFT's parameter bottleneck, the researchers behind BOFT drew inspiration from an unexpected source: the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithm. The FFT uses a specific network topology called a butterfly graph to exchange information efficiently. 

BOFT applies this butterfly structure to factorize a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse matrices, each with $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries. By ensuring each of these sparse matrices is orthogonal, BOFT constructs a dense orthogonal matrix using only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. This is a massive reduction from the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters required by a standard dense orthogonal matrix.

This approach is a generalization of OFT. Depending on the parameters, BOFT can reduce to the original OFT or the block-diagonal OFT, but by using the butterfly structure, it smoothly interpolates between expressivity (a full orthogonal matrix) and regularity (an identity matrix).

## Real-World Applications and Superior Generalization

For the first time, the researchers applied orthogonal finetuning beyond controllable text-to-image generation, putting BOFT to the test across a diverse range of domains: large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models. 

The empirical results were strong. BOFT outperformed current state-of-the-art methods by a considerable margin, validating its superior parameter-efficiency and generalization ability. But why does it perform so well in practice? The paper points to a few key reasons:

* **Spectral Properties:** Unlike LoRA, which uses an additive low-rank matrix, BOFT uses a multiplicative orthogonal matrix. This perfectly preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix, which greatly benefits training stability and generalization.
* **Structured Inductive Bias:** The butterfly factorization in BOFT introduces a structured inductive bias. Because the butterfly structure is shared by many classic fast linear transforms—like the discrete Fourier transform, discrete sine/cosine transform, and Hadamard transform—this structural prior helps prevent overfitting and benefits generalization.
* **Expressivity:** The butterfly structure allows BOFT to recover many classic linear transforms, which is impossible for the block-diagonal structure of the original OFT. The researchers found that BOFT with a smaller block size and more factorization steps is generally more parameter-efficient while maintaining excellent expressivity.

## A Step Forward for Foundation Models

BOFT represents a significant step forward in the efficient adaptation of foundation models. By framing the generation of an orthogonal matrix as an information transmission problem and solving it with the butterfly structure from FFTs, the researchers have created a method that is both theoretically sound and empirically superior. 

While there is a slight training runtime overhead due to the repeated matrix multiplication of sparse matrices, this cost disappears during inference. Once finetuning is complete, the learned orthogonal matrices can be directly multiplied into the pretrained model, resulting in zero additional inference latency. This makes BOFT a highly practical and powerful tool for adapting the next generation of massive AI models.
