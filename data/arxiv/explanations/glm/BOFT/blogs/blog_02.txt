\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

# The Butterfly Effect in AI: Borrowing Math from 1960s Algorithms for Modern Fine-Tuning

## The Heavy Cost of Adapting Giants

Foundation models like ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion are powerful, but their massive scale makes training them from scratch prohibitively expensive. As models grow to hundreds of billions of parameters, researchers must find efficient ways to adapt them to specific downstream tasks without retraining. One elegant approach is Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), which updates a pretrained model by multiplying its frozen weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. This preserves the pairwise angles between neurons, ensuring that the model's pretraining knowledge is not destroyed during the fine-tuning process.

However, OFT comes with a heavy computational price. A $d$-dimensional dense orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. To reduce this cost, the original OFT method introduced a block-diagonal structure, treating the matrix as a set of independent, smaller orthogonal matrices. While this saves parameters, it introduces an unnatural assumption: it forces the dimensions of a neuron to be divided into isolated groups based on their indices, which are transformed independently. This limits expressivity and cannot approximate classic linear transforms.

## Viewing Matrices as Information Networks

To build a dense orthogonal matrix without the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameter cost, the authors of the BOFT paper propose an "information transmission" perspective. If we decompose a dense matrix $\bm{R}$ into a product of multiple sparse matrices, such as $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$, we can view this as transmitting information across a grid-structured graph.

In this graph, the matrix $\bm{B}_i$ represents the connections between the $i$-th level of nodes and the $(i+1)$-th level. For the final matrix $\bm{R}$ to be dense, information from every node in the first level must be able to reach every node in the last level. The challenge becomes finding a sequence of sparse matrices that achieves this dense connectivity while using as few trainable parameters (or edges in the graph) as possible.

## The Cooley-Tukey Connection

This is where a 1960s breakthrough in signal processing comes to the rescue. The Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm revolutionized signal processing by efficiently computing the Discrete Fourier Transform. A key component of this algorithm is the "butterfly structure"—a network topology that allows for highly efficient information exchange between nodes.

The authors realized that the butterfly structure is exactly what is needed to solve their information transmission problem. In a butterfly network, information from different nodes can be exchanged efficiently using $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges, rather than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. By applying butterfly factorization, a $d$-dimensional dense matrix can be represented as a product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse matrices, each containing $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries.

## Enter Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)

The paper introduces Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), which applies this butterfly factorization to orthogonal finetuning. The key insight is that if each individual sparse matrix in the factorization is orthogonal, their product will also be a dense orthogonal matrix.

To achieve this, BOFT parameterizes the butterfly components using small, orthogonal block matrices (constructed via a Cayley transform). By chaining these together, BOFT creates a dense orthogonal transformation using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters—a dramatic reduction from the original OFT's $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ requirement.

BOFT also subsumes the original OFT as a special case. When the factorization depth $m = 1$, BOFT reduces to the block-diagonal OFT. By increasing $m$, we can smoothly interpolate between the regularity of an identity matrix and the expressivity of a full orthogonal matrix.

## Why Butterflies Beat Blocks

The structural prior provided by the butterfly matrix offers significant advantages over the block-diagonal approach. Because butterfly structures are the backbone of many classic fast linear transforms—like the FFT, discrete cosine transform, and Hadamard transform—BOFT can easily recover these transformations. The block-diagonal structure in original OFT cannot.

This gives BOFT a highly beneficial inductive bias. By constraining the fine-tuning to a structured subset of the orthogonal group, BOFT prevents overfitting while maintaining enough expressivity to adapt to new tasks. Empirically, the authors show that BOFT yields better parameter efficiency than OFT. For example, BOFT($9, 2$) is more expressive than the original block-diagonal OFT with block size 16, yet it uses far fewer parameters.

## A Drop-In Replacement for Modern AI

By making orthogonal finetuning parameter-efficient, BOFT transforms it from a niche technique for controlling text-to-image diffusion models into a general-purpose fine-tuning method. The authors applied BOFT to a wide range of tasks, adapting large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models. Across the board, BOFT outperformed current state-of-the-art parameter-efficient finetuning methods, validating its superior generalization and efficiency.

While BOFT introduces slightly more training runtime overhead due to the repeated matrix multiplications, the learned orthogonal matrices can be multiplied directly into the pretrained weights after training. This means BOFT introduces zero inference latency. It is a powerful reminder that sometimes the most advanced AI innovations can be found by revisiting classic algorithms from the early days of computer science.
