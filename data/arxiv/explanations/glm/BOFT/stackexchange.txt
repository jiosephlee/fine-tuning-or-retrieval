\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why does preserving pairwise angles between neurons help maintain pretraining knowledge?
Question:
I'm reading the BOFT paper and they mention that OFT (Orthogonal Finetuning) transforms neurons by an orthogonal matrix to "preserve the pairwise angles between neurons." I understand that orthogonal matrices preserve dot products mathematically, but I'm confused about why this is important for finetuning. The paper says this preserves "semantic knowledge from pretraining" but doesn't elaborate. Could someone explain the intuition behind why preserving angles between neurons (i.e., columns of the weight matrix $W^0$) helps maintain pretraining knowledge? Is there a specific property of neural network weights that makes angular relationships particularly meaningful?
Answer:
The key insight is that in a pretrained neural network, the geometric arrangement of neurons in feature space encodes semantic relationships learned during pretraining. When we view each column (neuron) of the weight matrix $W^0$ as a direction in the input feature space, the forward pass $z = (W^0)^\top x$ computes how well the input $x$ aligns with each of these directions. The pairwise angles between these directions encode how the model represents the relationships between different concepts — neurons detecting semantically related features will have smaller angles between them, while neurons detecting unrelated features will be closer to orthogonal.

When OFT applies an orthogonal matrix $R$ to transform the weights as $RW^0$, the angle between any two neurons $w_i$ and $w_j$ (columns of $W^0$) is preserved because

$$\cos\theta'_{ij} = \frac{(Rw_i)^\top (Rw_j)}{\|Rw_i\|\|Rw_j\|} = \frac{w_i^\top R^\top R w_j}{\|w_i\|\|w_j\|} = \frac{w_i^\top w_j}{\|w_i\|\|w_j\|} = \cos\theta_{ij}.$$

This means the finetuned model operates in the same representational geometry as the pretrained model — the same concepts are still related in the same way, just viewed from a rotated (or reflected) perspective. You can think of this as rotating a coordinate system without stretching any axis: the underlying space and all relationships within it remain intact. In contrast, unconstrained finetuning (as in full finetuning or LoRA's additive update $W^0 + BA$) can arbitrarily distort these angles, potentially collapsing the carefully learned representational diversity — two neurons that were positioned to detect distinct concepts might drift toward similar directions, destroying semantic distinctions the model worked hard to learn during pretraining.

The paper connects this to work on hyperspherical energy and generalization (citing Liu et al., 2018, 2021), which studies how the distribution of neurons on the unit hypersphere — characterized precisely by their pairwise angles — relates to a model's generalization ability. A well-spread, diverse arrangement of neurons on the hypersphere tends to yield better generalization, and by preserving all pairwise angles, orthogonal finetuning maintains this distribution throughout adaptation. Additionally, the paper notes that orthogonal transformations preserve the spectral norm of $W^0$ (since singular values are unchanged by left-multiplication with an orthogonal matrix), which further benefits training stability and generalization, as established in the spectral normalization literature.

The bilinear interpretation the paper provides also helps build intuition: the finetuned forward pass $z_i = w_i^\top R x$ can be read as learning a new "similarity metric" $R$ through which the input is viewed, while the neurons $w_i$ themselves retain their pretrained relationships. This is analogous to distance metric learning, where you learn a transformation of the input space without changing the fundamental structure of the reference points. The orthogonal constraint ensures that this transformation is "gentle" — it can rotate and reflect but never stretch or compress the space, keeping the pretrained knowledge geometrically intact while still allowing meaningful adaptation to downstream tasks.

### How does BOFT(1,b) mathematically reduce to block-diagonal OFT?
Question:
In the BOFT paper, they claim that when $m=1$, BOFT($1$,$b$) reduces to the block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. The forward pass is defined as $z = (R(m,b) \cdot W^0)^T x$ where $R(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)}$. When $m=1$, this becomes $R(1,b) = \tilde{B}^b_{(d,1)}$. The paper also defines $\tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)}$ as shorthand for $\tilde{B}^b(d, 2^{m-i+1})$. 

I'm struggling to trace through the notation to see how this single butterfly component equals a block-diagonal matrix with block size $b$. Can someone walk through this reduction step by step, clarifying the notation?
Answer:
# How BOFT$(1,b)$ Reduces to Block-Diagonal OFT

The key to tracing this reduction lies in a subtle notational convention and understanding how the butterfly factor's block generalization works. Let me walk through it step by step.

## The Notational Convention

The paper states: *"For convenience, we denote BOFT with $R(m,\frac{b}{2})$ as BOFT$(m,b)$, where $b\geq 2$."* This means BOFT$(1,b)$ actually uses the function $R(1, \frac{b}{2})$, not $R(1, b)$. This offset by $\frac{b}{2}$ is crucial and easy to miss.

## Tracing the Reduction

Starting from $R(m, b') = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{B}^{b'}_{(d,i)}$ with the shorthand $\tilde{B}^{b'}_{(d,i)} = \tilde{B}^{b'}(d, 2^{m-i+1})$, we substitute $m=1$ and $b' = \frac{b}{2}$:

$$R\left(1, \frac{b}{2}\right) = \tilde{B}^{\frac{b}{2}}_{(d,1)} = \tilde{B}^{\frac{b}{2}}\left(d, 2^{1-1+1}\right) = \tilde{B}^{\frac{b}{2}}(d, 2)$$

Now we need to understand what $\tilde{B}^{\frac{b}{2}}(d, 2)$ looks like. The block butterfly component $\tilde{B}^{b'}(d, k)$ is a block-diagonal matrix whose blocks are generalized butterfly factors $B^F(k)$, where each scalar entry in the original factor is replaced by a $b' \times b'$ matrix.

The original butterfly factor for $k=2$ is:

$$B^F(2) = \begin{bmatrix} d_1 & d_2 \\ d_3 & d_4 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}$$

where each $d_i$ is a scalar (since $d_i \in \mathbb{R}^{k/2} = \mathbb{R}^1$). When generalized with block parameter $b' = \frac{b}{2}$, each scalar entry becomes a $\frac{b}{2} \times \frac{b}{2}$ matrix, so the generalized factor becomes a $b \times b$ matrix:

$$B^F(2) = \begin{bmatrix} D_1 & D_2 \\ D_3 & D_4 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$$

where each $D_i \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{b}{2} \times \frac{b}{2}}$.

## The Final Structure

Since $\tilde{B}^{\frac{b}{2}}(d, 2)$ is block-diagonal with blocks of size $b \times b$, we get:

$$R\left(1, \frac{b}{2}\right) = \tilde{B}^{\frac{b}{2}}(d, 2) = \text{diag}\left(R_1, R_2, \ldots, R_{d/b}\right)$$

where each $R_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ is a $b \times b$ orthogonal matrix. The paper states that *"to guarantee the block butterfly component $\tilde{B}^b(d,2)$ to be orthogonal, we parameterize each $2b \times 2b$ block matrix to be orthogonal"* — in our case with parameter $\frac{b}{2}$, each $b \times b$ block is parameterized as orthogonal via Cayley transform.

This is precisely the block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$: $\text{diag}(R_1, R_2, \cdots, R_r)$ where $R_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ and $br = d$, exactly as defined in the original OFT.

## Intuitive Summary

The reduction works because with $m=1$, there is only a single butterfly component (no factorization into multiple sparse matrices). That single component has the smallest possible butterfly factor size $k=2$, and when generalized with block parameter $\frac{b}{2}$, the $2 \times 2$ factor with $\frac{b}{2} \times \frac{b}{2}$ matrix entries produces $b \times b$ blocks on the diagonal. The $\frac{b}{2}$ offset in the notation convention is precisely calibrated so that BOFT$(1,b)$ yields blocks of size $b$, matching block-diagonal OFT.

### What does the information transmission framework's "free edges" concept mean?
Question:
The BOFT paper introduces an "information transmission" framework where generating a dense matrix $R = B_m B_{m-1} \cdots B_1$ is viewed as transmitting information on a grid with $d \times (m+1)$ nodes. They identify two desiderata: (i) dense connectivity and (ii) minimum free edges.

I'm confused about what exactly counts as a "free edge" versus a constrained one. The paper says: "for a $d \times d$ orthogonal with $d$ non-zero entries, these entries can only be $\pm 1$" and that these should not be counted as trainable. Why can these entries only be $\pm 1$? And how does the orthogonality constraint create "additional dependency among edges" such that "one missing edge could imply another missing edge"?
Answer:
## Understanding "Free Edges" in the BOFT Information Transmission Framework

The concept of "free edges" in the BOFT paper refers to edges (i.e., non-zero matrix entries) that correspond to *continuously trainable parameters*, as opposed to edges that are structurally forced by the orthogonality constraint and whose values are fixed to $\pm 1$.

### Why a $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix with $d$ non-zero entries can only have $\pm 1$ values

The key insight is that an orthogonal matrix with exactly $d$ non-zero entries must have exactly one non-zero entry per row and per column — it is a *signed permutation matrix*. Here is why: orthogonality requires that every column has unit norm. If each column contains exactly one non-zero entry (which is forced when the total count is $d$ across a $d \times d$ matrix), then that single non-zero entry must have magnitude $1$ to satisfy $\|\text{column}_j\|_2 = 1$. The only freedom is the sign, giving values of $\pm 1$. Since sign is discrete (not a continuous parameter), the paper considers these entries as non-trainable. In the information transmission grid, these $d$ edges form a bijection between nodes in adjacent levels, which is the minimum needed for full rank — a necessary condition for orthogonality. They are "not free" because they carry no learnable continuous information; they are structurally determined.

### What makes an edge "free"

A *free edge* is any edge beyond these $d$ structurally necessary bijection edges. Free edges correspond to matrix entries that can be continuously varied (subject to orthogonality) and thus represent trainable parameters. The desideratum of "minimum free edges" asks: among all factorizations $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \cdots \bm{B}_1$ that achieve dense connectivity, which one uses the fewest such trainable entries? This is why the butterfly structure is attractive — it achieves dense connectivity with $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ total edges, of which only the "free" ones count toward the trainable parameter budget.

### How orthogonality creates dependencies among edges

Orthogonality imposes that rows and columns are orthonormal, which couples entries that would otherwise be independent. The paper illustrates this with a $2 \times 2$ orthogonal matrix. Consider:

$$\bm{B} = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$$

Orthogonality requires $a^2 + b^2 = 1$ (row 1 norm), $c^2 + d^2 = 1$ (row 2 norm), and $ac + bd = 0$ (rows orthogonal). If we set $a = 0$ (removing one edge), then $b^2 = 1$, so $b = \pm 1$. The orthogonality condition $ac + bd = 0$ becomes $bd = 0$, and since $b \neq 0$, we must have $d = 0$. Thus, removing the edge at position $(1,1)$ *forces* the removal of the edge at position $(2,2)$. The matrix collapses to a signed permutation matrix:

$$\bm{B} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \pm 1 \\ \pm 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

This is the "additional dependency among edges" the paper describes: one missing edge implies another missing edge. More generally, the orthogonality constraint means the sparsity pattern of each factor $\bm{B}_i$ cannot be chosen arbitrarily — specifying some entries as zero propagates constraints to other entries, potentially adding or removing edges to maintain orthonormality. This is why the paper notes that "for each feasible set of edge connections, the orthogonality may sometimes add or remove some edges," making the counting of free edges subtler than simply tallying non-zero entries in an unconstrained matrix.

### Summary

In short, "free edges" are the continuously trainable non-zero entries in the factorization matrices, excluding the $d$ per-level bijection edges whose values are locked to $\pm 1$ by orthogonality. The orthogonality constraint further entangles edges by creating algebraic dependencies among them, so that the sparsity pattern of an orthogonal matrix is not freely specifiable — setting one entry to zero can force others to zero (or to $\pm 1$), as the $2 \times 2$ example demonstrates. This interplay between structural necessity and algebraic dependency is what makes minimizing free edges a nontrivial design problem, one that the butterfly structure solves efficiently.

### What does Theorem 1 (Expressivity of BOFT) actually prove about expressivity?
Question:
The BOFT paper states Theorem 1: "BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size. For the butterfly matrix to approximate all orthogonal matrices of size $d$, we can multiply butterfly matrices with $B_{d-1,1}(d)B^T_{d-1,2}(d)\cdots B_{1,1}(d)B^T_{1,2}(d)$, where $B_{i,j}(d)$ are butterfly matrices."

I have several confusions:
1. What does "more expressive" mean formally? Is it saying the set of matrices representable by BOFT is a superset of those representable by OFT with the same block size?
2. The theorem mentions needing to multiply multiple butterfly matrices to approximate "all orthogonal matrices" - does this mean a single butterfly matrix is NOT fully expressive over the orthogonal group?
3. How does this relate to the generalized form $R^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)$ mentioned after the theorem?
Answer:
The key to understanding Theorem 1 is recognizing that it makes two distinct claims: a **relative** claim (BOFT vs. OFT with the same block size) and an **absolute** claim (what is needed to cover the entire orthogonal group).

**On the meaning of "more expressive":** Formally, "more expressive" means that the set of orthogonal matrices representable by BOFT with block size $b$ is a strict superset of those representable by OFT with the same block size $b$. This follows directly from the fact that $\text{BOFT}(1, b)$ reduces exactly to block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, while $\text{BOFT}(m, b)$ with $m > 1$ produces a dense orthogonal matrix through butterfly factorization. Since the block-diagonal structure (where $R = \text{diag}(R_1, \ldots, R_r)$ with each $R_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ and $br = d$) is a special case of the butterfly structure, anything OFT can represent, BOFT can also represent, plus additional dense matrices that OFT cannot. The paper's simulation results reinforce this: $\text{BOFT}(9, 2)$ achieves better approximation of a random $512 \times 512$ orthogonal matrix than $\text{BOFT}(1, 16)$ (which is OFT with block size 16), while using fewer parameters.

**On whether a single butterfly matrix covers the full orthogonal group:** No, a single butterfly matrix does not cover the entire orthogonal group. The theorem explicitly states that to approximate *all* orthogonal matrices of size $d$, one needs to multiply $2(d-1)$ butterfly matrices together in the form $B_{d-1,1}(d)B^T_{d-1,2}(d) \cdots B_{1,1}(d)B^T_{1,2}(d)$. A single butterfly matrix $B(d)$ already produces a dense orthogonal matrix (every entry is generically nonzero), but it represents a structured *subset* of the orthogonal group, not the entire group. The butterfly structure imposes a particular factorization pattern, and while this subset is much richer than the block-diagonal subset, it is still a proper subset. The paper notes that this product of multiple butterfly matrices corresponds to what is called a "kaleidoscope hierarchy" in the literature (Dao et al., 2019).

**On the generalized form $R^G$:** The generalized form $R^G(m_1, b_1, m_2, b_2, l) = R_{l,1}(m_1, b_1) R^T_{l,2}(m_2, b_2) \cdots R_{1,1}(m_1, b_1) R^T_{1,2}(m_2, b_2)$ is the direct operationalization of Theorem 1's second claim. Instead of using a single BOFT matrix $R(m, b)$, the generalized form multiplies $l$ pairs of BOFT matrices (and their transposes) together. Each $R_{i,j}(m, b)$ is itself a standard BOFT orthogonal matrix composed of butterfly components. When $m_1 = m_2 = \log d$, $b_1 = b_2 = 2$, and $l = d - 1$, this product uses $2(d-1)$ butterfly matrices and can represent the entire orthogonal group, matching the construction in Theorem 1. In this sense, $R^G$ interpolates between the standard BOFT (when $l = 1$, it reduces to a single BOFT matrix) and full expressivity over the orthogonal group (when $l = d - 1$ with appropriate $m$ and $b$).

It is worth noting, as the paper itself emphasizes, that greater expressivity is not automatically desirable for finetuning. The paper explicitly cautions that "better expressiveness does not always lead to better performance," citing full finetuning as a counterexample. The value of BOFT lies in finding a favorable trade-off between expressivity and regularity: the butterfly structure provides structured priors shared with classic transforms like the FFT and Hadamard transform, which serve as an inductive bias that helps generalization while still allowing dense transformations that OFT's block-diagonal structure cannot achieve.

### How does the butterfly structure in BOFT relate to the Cooley-Tukey FFT algorithm?
Question:
The BOFT paper draws inspiration from the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm to create butterfly structures for parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning. I understand that FFT uses a butterfly pattern to efficiently compute the DFT by recursively splitting into smaller transforms.

However, I'm struggling to see the precise mathematical connection to how BOFT factorizes orthogonal matrices. The paper defines butterfly factors $B^F(k)$ and butterfly matrices $B(d)$ recursively, but:
1. How exactly does the recursive structure of Cooley-Tukey FFT translate to the recursive definition of $B(d)$?
2. Why does this particular structure help reduce parameters from $O(d^2)$ to $O(d \log d)$ while maintaining orthogonality?
3. Is the connection mainly structural (the butterfly graph topology) or is there a deeper mathematical relationship?
Answer:
# How the Butterfly Structure in BOFT Relates to Cooley-Tukey FFT

The connection between BOFT's butterfly factorization and the Cooley-Tukey FFT is both structural and mathematical, and understanding it requires looking at how the recursive decomposition in FFT translates to matrix factorization.

## Recursive Structure: The Core Analogy

The Cooley-Tukey algorithm reduces an $N$-point DFT to two $N/2$-point DFTs by splitting the input into even- and odd-indexed elements, computing the smaller transforms, and then combining them with "twiddle factor" multiplications. This recursion produces $\log_2 N$ levels of butterfly operations, where each level mixes pairs of elements in a specific pattern.

BOFT mirrors this exactly. The butterfly matrix $B(d)$ is defined recursively as:

$$B(d) = \tilde{B}(d,d) \cdot \begin{bmatrix} B_1(d/2) & 0 \\ 0 & B_2(d/2) \end{bmatrix} = \tilde{B}(d,d)\tilde{B}(d,d/2)\cdots\tilde{B}(d,2)$$

The block-diagonal matrix $\text{diag}(B_1(d/2), B_2(d/2))$ corresponds to the two smaller $N/2$-point transforms, while the butterfly component $\tilde{B}(d,k)$ at each level plays the role of the butterfly operations that combine the sub-problem solutions. Unrolling the recursion yields $\log_2 d$ sparse matrices, exactly as the FFT recursion produces $\log_2 N$ stages of butterflies. The key insight is that the DFT matrix itself can be written as a product of these sparse butterfly matrices — this is the well-known matrix factorization of the FFT, and BOFT directly adopts this factorization structure.

## Why Parameters Drop to $O(d \log d)$ While Staying Orthogonal

A dense $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix requires $O(d^2)$ parameters (via Cayley parameterization with a skew-symmetric matrix $Q$, this is $d(d-1)/2$). The butterfly structure reduces this through two mechanisms working together.

First, sparsity at each level: each butterfly component $\tilde{B}(d,k)$ is block-diagonal with $d/k$ blocks of size $k \times k$. At the finest level ($k=2$), this means $d/2$ blocks of $2 \times 2$ orthogonal matrices, each requiring only $1$ parameter. At any level with block size $k$, there are $d/k$ blocks each needing $k(k-1)/2$ parameters, giving $O(d \cdot k)$ parameters per level.

Second, logarithmic depth: the product only needs $\log_2 d$ such sparse matrices to achieve dense connectivity. The information transmission framework in the paper explains why: after $\log_2 d$ stages of butterfly mixing, every input node has a path to every output node, producing a dense matrix.

Combining these, with $k=2$ at each of $\log_2 d$ levels: the total parameter count is $(d/2) \times \log_2 d = O(d \log d)$.

Orthogonality is preserved because the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal. Each butterfly component $\tilde{B}(d,k)$ is parameterized to be individually orthogonal (via Cayley transform on each block), so their product $R = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{B}_{(d,i)}$ is automatically orthogonal. The non-zero pattern of larger butterfly components $\tilde{B}(d,k)$ for $k > 2$ is simply a permutation of the pattern in $\tilde{B}(d,2)$, so the same orthogonality parameterization extends naturally.

## Structural, Mathematical, and Information-Theoretic Connection

The connection goes beyond surface-level topology similarity. Mathematically, the FFT is itself representable as a product of butterfly matrices — the factorization in BOFT is literally the matrix representation of the FFT's computational structure. As the paper notes, butterfly matrices can perfectly recover classic transforms including the DFT, DCT, and Hadamard transform, which is why this parameterization carries a useful inductive bias for finetuning.

At a deeper level, both FFT and BOFT solve the same information transmission problem: efficiently connecting $d$ inputs to $d$ outputs such that every input can influence every output. The FFT achieves this for frequency decomposition; BOFT achieves it for orthogonal weight transformation. The butterfly graph topology solves this with $O(d \log d)$ edges (parameters), which is provably more efficient than the $O(d^2)$ edges a single dense matrix requires. The paper formalizes this through its information transmission framework, where the desiderata are dense connectivity (every source node reaches every receiver) and minimum free edges (fewest trainable parameters). The butterfly structure from Cooley-Tukey naturally satisfies both, which is precisely why the authors drew the connection.

The trade-off BOFT introduces compared to block-diagonal OFT is also illuminated by this connection. Block-diagonal OFT corresponds to a single level of the butterfly (the case $m=1$), where information stays within blocks and never mixes across groups. Adding more butterfly levels ($m > 1$) progressively introduces cross-block mixing, interpolating between the block-diagonal structure (high regularity, low expressivity) and a full dense orthogonal matrix (low regularity, high expressivity). This interpolation is the structural analog of going from a single DFT stage to the full recursive FFT — each additional stage expands which frequency components can interact.

### Where does the formula $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ for BOFT parameter count come from?
Question:
The BOFT paper states that "BOFT($m$,$b$) takes $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters for finetuning a linear layer of size $d \times n$." I'm trying to understand where this formula comes from.

I understand the general setup:
- There are $m$ butterfly components $\tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)}$
- Each component uses Cayley parameterization with skew-symmetric matrices
- The orthogonality constraint reduces parameters

But I can't figure out:
1. Why the factor of $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)$? Is this related to the dimension of skew-symmetric matrices of size $b \times b$?
2. How does the $d$ factor appear when we have block-diagonal structures?
3. Why isn't $n$ (the output dimension) involved in the parameter count, given we're finetuning a $d \times n$ layer?

Could someone break down this calculation step by step?
Answer:
The formula $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ comes from counting the free parameters in the skew-symmetric matrices used to parameterize the orthogonal blocks across all butterfly components. Let me break this down step by step.

**The block structure.** In BOFT($m$,$b$), the notation means we use $\bm{R}(m, \frac{b}{2})$, i.e., $m$ butterfly components with block parameter $\frac{b}{2}$. Each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b/2}_{(d,i)}$ is a block-diagonal matrix of size $d \times d$. The butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(2)$, when generalized with block parameter $\frac{b}{2}$, becomes a $b \times b$ matrix (since each scalar entry becomes a $\frac{b}{2} \times \frac{b}{2}$ block, and the $2 \times 2$ structure of $\bm{B}^F(2)$ yields $2 \times \frac{b}{2} = b$ per dimension). The block-diagonal $\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b/2}(d,2)$ therefore contains $\frac{d}{b}$ such blocks, each of size $b \times b$. The paper confirms this when it states that each "$2b \times 2b$ block matrix" is parameterized as orthogonal — in BOFT($m$,$b$) the analogous blocks are $b \times b$.

**The $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)$ factor.** Each $b \times b$ block must be orthogonal, which is enforced via the Cayley parameterization $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is a $b \times b$ skew-symmetric matrix ($\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$). A $b \times b$ skew-symmetric matrix has zeros on the diagonal and is determined by its upper-triangular entries, giving $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$ free parameters. Dividing by the block size $b$ gives $\frac{b-1}{2}$, which is the per-dimension parameter cost of each orthogonal block.

**The $d$ factor.** Since there are $\frac{d}{b}$ blocks per butterfly component, each requiring $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$ parameters, the total per component is $\frac{d}{b} \times \frac{b(b-1)}{2} = \frac{(b-1)d}{2}$. This is where $d$ enters: it is the total input dimension being partitioned into $\frac{d}{b}$ blocks, and the parameter count scales linearly with $d$ regardless of block size.

**The $m$ factor.** BOFT uses $m$ butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b/2}_{(d,1)}, \ldots, \tilde{\bm{B}}^{b/2}_{(d,m)}$, each contributing $\frac{(b-1)d}{2}$ parameters. Multiplying by $m$ gives the total: $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$.

**Why $n$ does not appear.** The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ acts only on the $d$-dimensional input space. The forward pass is $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}(m,b) \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ is frozen. Since all trainable parameters reside in $\bm{R}$, which is $d \times d$, the output dimension $n$ has no bearing on the parameter count.

**Sanity checks.** For BOFT($1$,$b$), which reduces to block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, the formula gives $\frac{(b-1)d}{2}$ — exactly $\frac{d}{b}$ blocks each needing $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$ parameters. For BOFT($\log d$, $2$), it gives $\frac{d \log d}{2}$, matching the $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ claim. For BOFT($1$,$d$), which reduces to original OFT with a full orthogonal matrix, it gives $\frac{d(d-1)}{2} \approx \mathcal{O}(d^2)$, the correct parameter count for a $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix parameterized via a skew-symmetric matrix.

### Why can't conventional dropout work with BOFT's multiplicative weight update?
Question:
The BOFT paper proposes a "multiplicative dropout" specifically for BOFT, stating that "conventional Dropout naturally works for LoRA, but not for BOFT due to our multiplicative weight update."

The multiplicative dropout they propose involves: "randomly picking $p_1$ percent of the butterfly components and $p_2$ percent of the diagonal blocks in each butterfly component, and then replacing these blocks as identity matrices."

I'm confused about:
1. Why exactly does conventional dropout (zeroing out random elements) fail for multiplicative weight updates? Is there a mathematical reason?
2. Why is replacing blocks with identity matrices the appropriate analog of dropout in this context? 
3. Does this relate to the fact that identity matrices are the "neutral element" for multiplication, just as zero is for addition?
Answer:
# Why Conventional Dropout Fails for BOFT and Why Identity Replacement Works

The key insight is exactly the one you suspected in your third question: the identity matrix is the neutral (identity) element of matrix multiplication, just as zero is the neutral element of addition. The failure of conventional dropout in BOFT and the design of multiplicative dropout both follow directly from this distinction between additive and multiplicative weight updates.

## Why conventional dropout breaks BOFT

In LoRA, the finetuned weight is $W^0 + \Delta W$, where $\Delta W = BA$ is a low-rank additive update. Conventional dropout works by zeroing out random elements of $\Delta W$ (or of $B$ and $A$). This is safe because zero is the additive identity: zeroing out the update simply means "do not modify the pretrained weights along this direction," so the model falls back to $W^0$, which is exactly the behavior you want from a regularization perspective — sometimes the finetuning contribution is removed, leaving the pretrained knowledge untouched.

In BOFT, the finetuned weight is $R(m,b) \cdot W^0$, where $R(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{B}_{(d,i)}^b$ is a product of orthogonal butterfly components. This is a multiplicative update. If you apply conventional dropout by zeroing out random entries of $R$ or of the butterfly components, two problems arise.

First, zeroing out entries destroys orthogonality. The entire motivation for OFT/BOFT is that $R$ is orthogonal, which provably preserves pairwise angles between neurons and preserves the spectral norm of $W^0$. If you randomly zero out elements, the resulting matrix no longer satisfies $R^\top R = I$, so you lose both the generalization benefits and the training stability that orthogonal finetuning is designed to provide. The matrix may even become singular.

Second, zeroing out entries of $R$ does not produce a meaningful "fallback" to the pretrained model. In the additive case, dropping the update recovers $W^0$ exactly. In the multiplicative case, zeroing out part of $R$ gives a degraded, non-orthogonal transformation applied to $W^0$ — not $W^0$ itself. So the regularization interpretation of dropout ("sometimes use the pretrained model") breaks down entirely.

## Why replacing blocks with identity matrices is the correct analog

The paper notes that each butterfly component $\tilde{B}_{(d,i)}^b$ can be permuted into a $2b \times 2b$ block-diagonal orthogonal matrix. The multiplicative dropout therefore operates on two granularities: with probability $p_1$, an entire butterfly component is replaced by $\bm{I}_d$, and within each remaining component, with probability $p_2$, individual $2b \times 2b$ diagonal blocks are replaced by $\bm{I}_{2b}$.

This works because the identity matrix is the multiplicative identity: $\bm{I} \cdot M = M$ for any matrix $M$. Replacing a butterfly component (or a block within it) with $\bm{I}$ means that component contributes no transformation — information passes through unchanged, exactly as if that component had been removed from the product. This is the precise multiplicative analog of zeroing out a term in a sum.

Crucially, the identity matrix is itself orthogonal, so replacing a component or block with $\bm{I}$ preserves the orthogonality of the overall product. Since the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal, and $\bm{I}$ is orthogonal, the full matrix $R(m,b)$ remains orthogonal regardless of which components or blocks are dropped. Thus, multiplicative dropout achieves the same regularizing effect as conventional dropout — randomly removing the contribution of parts of the finetuning transformation — while preserving the structural properties that make BOFT effective.

## The broader principle

This is a general pattern in regularizing structured reparameterizations. Whenever a model expresses its update as a composition (product) of transformation matrices, the neutral element of the composition operation is the appropriate "dropout" value. For additive updates like LoRA, the neutral element is zero. For multiplicative/orthogonal updates like BOFT, the neutral element is the identity matrix. Using zero in the multiplicative case is not merely suboptimal — it is structurally wrong, because zero is not even in the orthogonal group, so it takes the model outside the hypothesis class it was designed to explore. The identity matrix, by contrast, lies at the boundary of BOFT's hypothesis class (it is the initialization point and the "no-change" transformation), making it the natural anchor for regularization.

### How does BOFT create 'smoother interpolation' between identity and orthogonal matrices?
Question:
The BOFT paper claims that "BOFT uses the butterfly structure to construct a smoother interpolation between matrices from the full orthogonal group (expressivity) and identity matrices (regularity)" and mentions an "intriguing weight interpolation" in their figures.

I'm confused about what exactly is being interpolated and how:
1. Is it the orthogonal matrix $R(m,b)$ that's being interpolated between $I$ and some target orthogonal matrix?
2. What makes this interpolation "smoother" compared to block-diagonal OFT? Is there a formal notion of smoothness being used?
3. How does the factorization $R = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)}$ enable this interpolation property?
4. The paper says this "enables us to find a smaller hypothesis class within the orthogonal group" - how does smoother interpolation relate to finding a good hypothesis class?
Answer:
## Answer

The "smoother interpolation" refers to the granularity of the expressivity-regularity trade-off, not a formal mathematical notion of smoothness (e.g., differentiability). Let me unpack this carefully.

**What is being interpolated:** The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is what gets interpolated. At one extreme, when all butterfly components are initialized as identity matrices (via zero skew-symmetric matrices in the Cayley parameterization), $\bm{R}(m,b) = \bm{I}_d$. At the other extreme, when $m = \log \frac{2d}{b}$, the product of all butterfly components yields a *dense* orthogonal matrix. The paper notes that BOFT($1,b$) reduces to block-diagonal OFT, while BOFT($\log \frac{2d}{b}, b$) produces a dense matrix. So varying $m$ from $1$ to $\log \frac{2d}{b}$ interpolates between a sparse, highly regular structure and a dense, fully expressive one.

**Why BOFT's interpolation is "smoother":** In standard OFT, the expressivity-regularity trade-off is controlled by the block size $b$. To get a dense orthogonal matrix, you must set $b = d$, jumping directly from a block-diagonal structure (with $b < d$) to a completely unconstrained full orthogonal matrix. There is no intermediate state — you either have isolated blocks operating independently, or you have a fully dense matrix with $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. BOFT, by contrast, introduces the number of factors $m$ as an additional degree of freedom. Each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is individually block-diagonal (with a permuted sparsity pattern), but multiplying successive components together progressively *mixes information across blocks*. Adding one more factor to the product is a smaller, more incremental change than jumping from block size $b$ to $d$. This gives a finer-grained spectrum of intermediate structures between the two extremes.

**How the factorization enables this:** Each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ has a block-diagonal sparsity pattern, but different components have *different* permutations of which dimensions interact. When you compute the product $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,m)} \cdots \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,1)}$, the multiplication successively fills in off-diagonal entries. With only $m=1$ factor, the product is block-diagonal — dimensions within the same block interact, but dimensions across blocks never mix. With $m=2$ factors, the second factor's permutation pattern allows some cross-block interactions. Each additional factor introduces a new permutation pattern that connects previously disconnected blocks, following the butterfly graph's information-routing structure. This is directly visible through the information transmission framework in the paper: each factor corresponds to one "level" of the butterfly graph, and information can only flow through $m$ levels. To achieve full connectivity (every source node reaching every receiver node), you need $m = \mathcal{O}(\log d)$ levels. With fewer levels, you get *partial* connectivity — some off-diagonal entries remain zero. This partial connectivity is precisely the "intermediate" point in the interpolation.

**Connection to the hypothesis class:** The set of all possible $\bm{R}(m,b)$ for fixed $m$ and $b$ constitutes a structured subset of the orthogonal group — a hypothesis class. When $m$ is small, this class is small (more regular, closer to identity, fewer effective parameters at $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$). When $m$ is large, the class grows toward the full orthogonal group (more expressive). The "smoother interpolation" means that BOFT provides many more intermediate hypothesis classes than OFT does, where the only meaningful choice is between block-diagonal (small $b$) and fully dense ($b = d$). The paper argues that the butterfly structure's hypothesis classes carry a beneficial inductive bias because they can recover classic linear transforms like the FFT, DCT, and Hadamard transform — something block-diagonal OFT cannot do at any block size. This makes the intermediate hypothesis classes not just arbitrary subsets, but *structured* ones aligned with natural linear transformations. Finding the right intermediate class (the right $m$ and $b$) lets you balance expressivity (enough capacity for the downstream task) against regularity (enough constraint to prevent overfitting), which is the core generalization challenge in finetuning that the paper emphasizes: "better expressiveness does not always lead to better performance."

### Why is it impossible for block-diagonal OFT to recover classic linear transforms?
Question:
The BOFT paper argues that "the butterfly structure can easily recover many classic linear transforms while it is impossible for the block-diagonal structure in OFT to recover any." They use this to argue that BOFT has better inductive bias.

I understand that butterfly matrices are structurally related to FFT, but:
1. Why is it mathematically impossible for block-diagonal matrices to represent transforms like FFT, DCT, or Hadamard transforms? Is it because these transforms require mixing across all dimensions, while block-diagonal only mixes within blocks?
2. Does this impossibility result stem purely from the sparsity pattern, or also from the orthogonality constraint?
3. How significant is this limitation for practical finetuning tasks - is recovering classic transforms actually relevant, or is this more of a theoretical motivation?
Answer:
The impossibility stems fundamentally from the **sparsity pattern** of the block-diagonal structure, not from the orthogonality constraint, and your intuition about mixing across dimensions is exactly right.

A block-diagonal orthogonal matrix in OFT has the form $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \cdots, \bm{R}_r)$ where each $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ is an orthogonal matrix and $b \cdot r = d$. By definition, this matrix has zeros in every off-diagonal block, meaning that output dimension $j$ in block $i$ can only depend on input dimensions within the same block $i$. There is no information flow between blocks.

Classic transforms like the FFT, DCT, and Hadamard transform are **dense** — every output dimension depends on every input dimension. For example, the Discrete Fourier Transform matrix has entries $F_{jk} = e^{-2\pi i jk/d}$, which are non-zero for all $(j,k)$ pairs. The Hadamard matrix has entries $\pm 1/\sqrt{d}$ everywhere. Since these transforms have non-zero entries in positions that are structurally forced to be zero in any block-diagonal matrix with block size $b < d$, no such block-diagonal matrix can represent them — regardless of what values the non-zero blocks take.

Importantly, this impossibility has nothing to do with the orthogonality constraint. In fact, the FFT, DCT, and Hadamard transforms are themselves orthogonal (or unitary), so orthogonality is perfectly compatible with them. The barrier is purely structural: the block-diagonal sparsity pattern prohibits cross-block mixing, while these transforms require universal mixing. If OFT sets $b = d$ (a single block), it becomes a full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix that **can** represent these transforms — but this requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, defeating the purpose of parameter efficiency. The paper notes this explicitly: "the original OFT has to use the block size $b=d$ to generate a dense orthogonal matrix, while BOFT can use any $b$ to achieve this."

The butterfly structure, by contrast, factorizes a dense matrix into $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse matrices, each with $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries. Through the recursive composition of these sparse factors, information from every source node can reach every receiver node across $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ stages. This is precisely the property that enables the Cooley-Tukey FFT algorithm: the butterfly network achieves dense connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges, and the resulting matrix product is dense. As the paper states, "the butterfly structure along with permutations can perfectly recover many classic fast linear transforms."

Regarding practical significance, recovering classic transforms is **not** literally required by downstream finetuning tasks. The paper is transparent about this — it uses the FFT/DCT/Hadamard recovery property as a **theoretical motivation** for why butterfly factorization provides a better inductive bias. The deeper argument is about the expressivity-regularity trade-off: block-diagonal OFT constrains dimensions to interact only within arbitrary index-based groups, which "makes no sense to divide the dimensions of a neuron into $r$ groups based on their indices," whereas BOFT enables mixing across all dimensions while still maintaining a structured, parameter-efficient hypothesis class. The practical benefit is that BOFT can produce dense orthogonal matrices with $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters instead of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$, and the simulation results in the paper (Figure 2) confirm that BOFT achieves better approximation of random orthogonal matrices than block-diagonal OFT at the same parameter budget. The classic-transform recovery argument serves to justify that this structural choice is not arbitrary — it aligns with a family of transforms that have proven fundamental across signal processing and linear algebra, lending it a principled inductive bias that the block-diagonal structure lacks.

### How does the Cayley parameterization ensure orthogonality in BOFT's butterfly components?
Question:
The BOFT paper uses Cayley parameterization to ensure orthogonality: $R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}$ where $Q$ is skew-symmetric ($Q = -Q^T$). They apply this to parameterize each $2b \times 2b$ block in the butterfly components.

I have several questions about this:
1. The paper mentions that for the non-zero pattern of butterfly components with $k > 2$, these are "block-wise permutations" of $\tilde{B}^b(d,2)$. How does permutation preserve the orthogonality guaranteed by Cayley parameterization?
2. For a $2b \times 2b$ block, the skew-symmetric matrix $Q$ would have $b(2b-1)$ free parameters. How does this connect to the $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)$ factor in the parameter count formula?
3. Is there a reason the Cayley parameterization is preferred over alternatives like Givens rotations or Householder reflections for this specific application?
Answer:
The three parts of your question touch in the core of how BOFT constructs dense orthogonal matrices from sparse structured factors. Let me address each in turn.

**1. How permutation preserves orthogonality**

The key insight is that permutation matrices are themselves orthogonal: for any permutation matrix $P$, we have $P^T P = I$. This means that conjugation by a permutation matrix preserves orthogonality. Specifically, if $M$ is orthogonal, then $P M P^T$ is also orthogonal, since $(PMP^T)^T(PMP^T) = PM^T P^T P M P^T = PM^T M P^T = PIP^T = I$.

The paper states that the non-zero pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d, k)$ for $k > 2$ is a "block-wise permutation" of the non-zero pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d, 2)$. Concretely, $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d, 2)$ is block-diagonal with $2b \times 2b$ orthogonal blocks, each parameterized via the Cayley transform. For larger $k$, the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ spreads its non-zero $2b \times 2b$ blocks across a wider $kb \times kb$ footprint in a pattern determined by the butterfly topology—but these blocks still tile the matrix without overlap, each row and column touching exactly one such block. There exists a block-wise permutation matrix $P$ such that $P \tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d, k) P^T$ recovers the standard block-diagonal structure of $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d, 2)$. Since the permuted version is block-diagonal with orthogonal blocks (hence orthogonal), and $P$ is orthogonal, the original $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d, k)$ is orthogonal as well. In practice, one does not explicitly apply the permutation; one simply parameterizes each $2b \times 2b$ non-zero block with the Cayley transform, and the overall orthogonality follows from the non-overlapping tiling structure.

**2. Connecting the parameter count**

The apparent mismatch arises from a notational subtlety in how BOFT relates block sizes to the $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b$ notation. The paper defines $\mathrm{BOFT}(m, b)$ as corresponding to $\bm{R}(m, \frac{b}{2})$, meaning the superscript $b$ in $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b$ is $\frac{b}{2}$, not $b$. This means each entry in the butterfly factor's $\bm{d}_i$ vectors is a $\frac{b}{2} \times \frac{b}{2}$ matrix, making each orthogonal block $b \times b$ (not $2b \times 2b$).

With this understanding, each $b \times b$ orthogonal block requires a $b \times b$ skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$, which has $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$ free parameters. The number of such blocks per butterfly component is $\frac{d}{b}$ (since each block occupies $b$ dimensions and the matrix is $d \times d$). Thus, the parameters per component are $\frac{d}{b} \cdot \frac{b(b-1)}{2} = \frac{d(b-1)}{2}$, and across $m$ components the total is $\frac{(b-1)dm}{2}$, matching the formula in the paper.

The $b(2b-1)$ figure you mentioned would apply if the blocks were genuinely $2b \times 2b$, but in $\mathrm{BOFT}(m, b)$ the blocks are $b \times b$. You can verify this with the special case the paper provides: $\mathrm{BOFT}(1, b)$ reduces to block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, where there are $\frac{d}{b}$ blocks each needing $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$ parameters, giving $\frac{d(b-1)}{2}$—which is exactly $\frac{(b-1)dm}{2}$ with $m = 1$.

**3. Why Cayley over Givens rotations or Householder reflections**

The paper does not explicitly compare these alternatives, but it notes that the Cayley parameterization is adopted following prior work on orthogonal finetuning (citing Qiu et al. and Liu et al.). Several properties make it well-suited for this application. First, identity initialization is natural and trivial: setting $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$ gives $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{0})(\bm{I} - \bm{0})^{-1} = \bm{I}$, which is essential for finetuning because it means training starts from the exact pretrained model. Second, the Cayley transform provides a smooth, diffeomorphic mapping from the vector space of skew-symmetric matrices to the orthogonal group, giving a well-behaved optimization landscape with no singularities or discontinuities at the identity. Third, it provides a compact parameterization: a single $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$-parameter skew-symmetric matrix generates a full $b \times b$ orthogonal block, which integrates cleanly into the block-diagonal structure of each butterfly component. Givens rotations would require composing $O(b^2)$ individual rotation matrices sequentially, introducing a more complex computational graph and potential gradient instability, while Householder reflections would use $b$ vectors of $b$ parameters each ($b^2$ total—nearly twice as many parameters as Cayley's $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$) and have a less smooth geometry near the identity. The Cayley parameterization thus offers the best combination of parameter efficiency, optimization smoothness, and initialization convenience for the BOFT framework.
