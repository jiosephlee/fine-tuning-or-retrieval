\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why can't conventional dropout work with BOFT's multiplicative weight update?
Question:
The BOFT paper proposes a "multiplicative dropout" specifically for BOFT, stating that "conventional Dropout naturally works for LoRA, but not for BOFT due to our multiplicative weight update."

The multiplicative dropout they propose involves: "randomly picking $p_1$ percent of the butterfly components and $p_2$ percent of the diagonal blocks in each butterfly component, and then replacing these blocks as identity matrices."

I'm confused about:
1. Why exactly does conventional dropout (zeroing out random elements) fail for multiplicative weight updates? Is there a mathematical reason?
2. Why is replacing blocks with identity matrices the appropriate analog of dropout in this context? 
3. Does this relate to the fact that identity matrices are the "neutral element" for multiplication, just as zero is for addition?
Answer:
# Why Conventional Dropout Fails for BOFT and Why Identity Replacement Works

The key insight is exactly the one you suspected in your third question: the identity matrix is the neutral (identity) element of matrix multiplication, just as zero is the neutral element of addition. The failure of conventional dropout in BOFT and the design of multiplicative dropout both follow directly from this distinction between additive and multiplicative weight updates.

## Why conventional dropout breaks BOFT

In LoRA, the finetuned weight is $W^0 + \Delta W$, where $\Delta W = BA$ is a low-rank additive update. Conventional dropout works by zeroing out random elements of $\Delta W$ (or of $B$ and $A$). This is safe because zero is the additive identity: zeroing out the update simply means "do not modify the pretrained weights along this direction," so the model falls back to $W^0$, which is exactly the behavior you want from a regularization perspective — sometimes the finetuning contribution is removed, leaving the pretrained knowledge untouched.

In BOFT, the finetuned weight is $R(m,b) \cdot W^0$, where $R(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{B}_{(d,i)}^b$ is a product of orthogonal butterfly components. This is a multiplicative update. If you apply conventional dropout by zeroing out random entries of $R$ or of the butterfly components, two problems arise.

First, zeroing out entries destroys orthogonality. The entire motivation for OFT/BOFT is that $R$ is orthogonal, which provably preserves pairwise angles between neurons and preserves the spectral norm of $W^0$. If you randomly zero out elements, the resulting matrix no longer satisfies $R^\top R = I$, so you lose both the generalization benefits and the training stability that orthogonal finetuning is designed to provide. The matrix may even become singular.

Second, zeroing out entries of $R$ does not produce a meaningful "fallback" to the pretrained model. In the additive case, dropping the update recovers $W^0$ exactly. In the multiplicative case, zeroing out part of $R$ gives a degraded, non-orthogonal transformation applied to $W^0$ — not $W^0$ itself. So the regularization interpretation of dropout ("sometimes use the pretrained model") breaks down entirely.

## Why replacing blocks with identity matrices is the correct analog

The paper notes that each butterfly component $\tilde{B}_{(d,i)}^b$ can be permuted into a $2b \times 2b$ block-diagonal orthogonal matrix. The multiplicative dropout therefore operates on two granularities: with probability $p_1$, an entire butterfly component is replaced by $\bm{I}_d$, and within each remaining component, with probability $p_2$, individual $2b \times 2b$ diagonal blocks are replaced by $\bm{I}_{2b}$.

This works because the identity matrix is the multiplicative identity: $\bm{I} \cdot M = M$ for any matrix $M$. Replacing a butterfly component (or a block within it) with $\bm{I}$ means that component contributes no transformation — information passes through unchanged, exactly as if that component had been removed from the product. This is the precise multiplicative analog of zeroing out a term in a sum.

Crucially, the identity matrix is itself orthogonal, so replacing a component or block with $\bm{I}$ preserves the orthogonality of the overall product. Since the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal, and $\bm{I}$ is orthogonal, the full matrix $R(m,b)$ remains orthogonal regardless of which components or blocks are dropped. Thus, multiplicative dropout achieves the same regularizing effect as conventional dropout — randomly removing the contribution of parts of the finetuning transformation — while preserving the structural properties that make BOFT effective.

## The broader principle

This is a general pattern in regularizing structured reparameterizations. Whenever a model expresses its update as a composition (product) of transformation matrices, the neutral element of the composition operation is the appropriate "dropout" value. For additive updates like LoRA, the neutral element is zero. For multiplicative/orthogonal updates like BOFT, the neutral element is the identity matrix. Using zero in the multiplicative case is not merely suboptimal — it is structurally wrong, because zero is not even in the orthogonal group, so it takes the model outside the hypothesis class it was designed to explore. The identity matrix, by contrast, lies at the boundary of BOFT's hypothesis class (it is the initialization point and the "no-change" transformation), making it the natural anchor for regularization.
