\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does the Cayley parameterization ensure orthogonality in BOFT's butterfly components?
Question:
The BOFT paper uses Cayley parameterization to ensure orthogonality: $R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}$ where $Q$ is skew-symmetric ($Q = -Q^T$). They apply this to parameterize each $2b \times 2b$ block in the butterfly components.

I have several questions about this:
1. The paper mentions that for the non-zero pattern of butterfly components with $k > 2$, these are "block-wise permutations" of $\tilde{B}^b(d,2)$. How does permutation preserve the orthogonality guaranteed by Cayley parameterization?
2. For a $2b \times 2b$ block, the skew-symmetric matrix $Q$ would have $b(2b-1)$ free parameters. How does this connect to the $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)$ factor in the parameter count formula?
3. Is there a reason the Cayley parameterization is preferred over alternatives like Givens rotations or Householder reflections for this specific application?
Answer:
The three parts of your question touch in the core of how BOFT constructs dense orthogonal matrices from sparse structured factors. Let me address each in turn.

**1. How permutation preserves orthogonality**

The key insight is that permutation matrices are themselves orthogonal: for any permutation matrix $P$, we have $P^T P = I$. This means that conjugation by a permutation matrix preserves orthogonality. Specifically, if $M$ is orthogonal, then $P M P^T$ is also orthogonal, since $(PMP^T)^T(PMP^T) = PM^T P^T P M P^T = PM^T M P^T = PIP^T = I$.

The paper states that the non-zero pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d, k)$ for $k > 2$ is a "block-wise permutation" of the non-zero pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d, 2)$. Concretely, $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d, 2)$ is block-diagonal with $2b \times 2b$ orthogonal blocks, each parameterized via the Cayley transform. For larger $k$, the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ spreads its non-zero $2b \times 2b$ blocks across a wider $kb \times kb$ footprint in a pattern determined by the butterfly topology—but these blocks still tile the matrix without overlap, each row and column touching exactly one such block. There exists a block-wise permutation matrix $P$ such that $P \tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d, k) P^T$ recovers the standard block-diagonal structure of $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d, 2)$. Since the permuted version is block-diagonal with orthogonal blocks (hence orthogonal), and $P$ is orthogonal, the original $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d, k)$ is orthogonal as well. In practice, one does not explicitly apply the permutation; one simply parameterizes each $2b \times 2b$ non-zero block with the Cayley transform, and the overall orthogonality follows from the non-overlapping tiling structure.

**2. Connecting the parameter count**

The apparent mismatch arises from a notational subtlety in how BOFT relates block sizes to the $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b$ notation. The paper defines $\mathrm{BOFT}(m, b)$ as corresponding to $\bm{R}(m, \frac{b}{2})$, meaning the superscript $b$ in $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b$ is $\frac{b}{2}$, not $b$. This means each entry in the butterfly factor's $\bm{d}_i$ vectors is a $\frac{b}{2} \times \frac{b}{2}$ matrix, making each orthogonal block $b \times b$ (not $2b \times 2b$).

With this understanding, each $b \times b$ orthogonal block requires a $b \times b$ skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$, which has $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$ free parameters. The number of such blocks per butterfly component is $\frac{d}{b}$ (since each block occupies $b$ dimensions and the matrix is $d \times d$). Thus, the parameters per component are $\frac{d}{b} \cdot \frac{b(b-1)}{2} = \frac{d(b-1)}{2}$, and across $m$ components the total is $\frac{(b-1)dm}{2}$, matching the formula in the paper.

The $b(2b-1)$ figure you mentioned would apply if the blocks were genuinely $2b \times 2b$, but in $\mathrm{BOFT}(m, b)$ the blocks are $b \times b$. You can verify this with the special case the paper provides: $\mathrm{BOFT}(1, b)$ reduces to block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, where there are $\frac{d}{b}$ blocks each needing $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$ parameters, giving $\frac{d(b-1)}{2}$—which is exactly $\frac{(b-1)dm}{2}$ with $m = 1$.

**3. Why Cayley over Givens rotations or Householder reflections**

The paper does not explicitly compare these alternatives, but it notes that the Cayley parameterization is adopted following prior work on orthogonal finetuning (citing Qiu et al. and Liu et al.). Several properties make it well-suited for this application. First, identity initialization is natural and trivial: setting $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$ gives $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{0})(\bm{I} - \bm{0})^{-1} = \bm{I}$, which is essential for finetuning because it means training starts from the exact pretrained model. Second, the Cayley transform provides a smooth, diffeomorphic mapping from the vector space of skew-symmetric matrices to the orthogonal group, giving a well-behaved optimization landscape with no singularities or discontinuities at the identity. Third, it provides a compact parameterization: a single $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$-parameter skew-symmetric matrix generates a full $b \times b$ orthogonal block, which integrates cleanly into the block-diagonal structure of each butterfly component. Givens rotations would require composing $O(b^2)$ individual rotation matrices sequentially, introducing a more complex computational graph and potential gradient instability, while Householder reflections would use $b$ vectors of $b$ parameters each ($b^2$ total—nearly twice as many parameters as Cayley's $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$) and have a less smooth geometry near the identity. The Cayley parameterization thus offers the best combination of parameter efficiency, optimization smoothness, and initialization convenience for the BOFT framework.
