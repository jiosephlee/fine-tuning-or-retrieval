\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### What does Theorem 1 (Expressivity of BOFT) actually prove about expressivity?
Question:
The BOFT paper states Theorem 1: "BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size. For the butterfly matrix to approximate all orthogonal matrices of size $d$, we can multiply butterfly matrices with $B_{d-1,1}(d)B^T_{d-1,2}(d)\cdots B_{1,1}(d)B^T_{1,2}(d)$, where $B_{i,j}(d)$ are butterfly matrices."

I have several confusions:
1. What does "more expressive" mean formally? Is it saying the set of matrices representable by BOFT is a superset of those representable by OFT with the same block size?
2. The theorem mentions needing to multiply multiple butterfly matrices to approximate "all orthogonal matrices" - does this mean a single butterfly matrix is NOT fully expressive over the orthogonal group?
3. How does this relate to the generalized form $R^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)$ mentioned after the theorem?
Answer:
The key to understanding Theorem 1 is recognizing that it makes two distinct claims: a **relative** claim (BOFT vs. OFT with the same block size) and an **absolute** claim (what is needed to cover the entire orthogonal group).

**On the meaning of "more expressive":** Formally, "more expressive" means that the set of orthogonal matrices representable by BOFT with block size $b$ is a strict superset of those representable by OFT with the same block size $b$. This follows directly from the fact that $\text{BOFT}(1, b)$ reduces exactly to block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, while $\text{BOFT}(m, b)$ with $m > 1$ produces a dense orthogonal matrix through butterfly factorization. Since the block-diagonal structure (where $R = \text{diag}(R_1, \ldots, R_r)$ with each $R_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ and $br = d$) is a special case of the butterfly structure, anything OFT can represent, BOFT can also represent, plus additional dense matrices that OFT cannot. The paper's simulation results reinforce this: $\text{BOFT}(9, 2)$ achieves better approximation of a random $512 \times 512$ orthogonal matrix than $\text{BOFT}(1, 16)$ (which is OFT with block size 16), while using fewer parameters.

**On whether a single butterfly matrix covers the full orthogonal group:** No, a single butterfly matrix does not cover the entire orthogonal group. The theorem explicitly states that to approximate *all* orthogonal matrices of size $d$, one needs to multiply $2(d-1)$ butterfly matrices together in the form $B_{d-1,1}(d)B^T_{d-1,2}(d) \cdots B_{1,1}(d)B^T_{1,2}(d)$. A single butterfly matrix $B(d)$ already produces a dense orthogonal matrix (every entry is generically nonzero), but it represents a structured *subset* of the orthogonal group, not the entire group. The butterfly structure imposes a particular factorization pattern, and while this subset is much richer than the block-diagonal subset, it is still a proper subset. The paper notes that this product of multiple butterfly matrices corresponds to what is called a "kaleidoscope hierarchy" in the literature (Dao et al., 2019).

**On the generalized form $R^G$:** The generalized form $R^G(m_1, b_1, m_2, b_2, l) = R_{l,1}(m_1, b_1) R^T_{l,2}(m_2, b_2) \cdots R_{1,1}(m_1, b_1) R^T_{1,2}(m_2, b_2)$ is the direct operationalization of Theorem 1's second claim. Instead of using a single BOFT matrix $R(m, b)$, the generalized form multiplies $l$ pairs of BOFT matrices (and their transposes) together. Each $R_{i,j}(m, b)$ is itself a standard BOFT orthogonal matrix composed of butterfly components. When $m_1 = m_2 = \log d$, $b_1 = b_2 = 2$, and $l = d - 1$, this product uses $2(d-1)$ butterfly matrices and can represent the entire orthogonal group, matching the construction in Theorem 1. In this sense, $R^G$ interpolates between the standard BOFT (when $l = 1$, it reduces to a single BOFT matrix) and full expressivity over the orthogonal group (when $l = d - 1$ with appropriate $m$ and $b$).

It is worth noting, as the paper itself emphasizes, that greater expressivity is not automatically desirable for finetuning. The paper explicitly cautions that "better expressiveness does not always lead to better performance," citing full finetuning as a counterexample. The value of BOFT lies in finding a favorable trade-off between expressivity and regularity: the butterfly structure provides structured priors shared with classic transforms like the FFT and Hadamard transform, which serve as an inductive bias that helps generalization while still allowing dense transformations that OFT's block-diagonal structure cannot achieve.
