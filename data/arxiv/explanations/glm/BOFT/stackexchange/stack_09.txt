\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does the butterfly structure in BOFT relate to the Cooley-Tukey FFT algorithm?
Question:
The BOFT paper draws inspiration from the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm to create butterfly structures for parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning. I understand that FFT uses a butterfly pattern to efficiently compute the DFT by recursively splitting into smaller transforms.

However, I'm struggling to see the precise mathematical connection to how BOFT factorizes orthogonal matrices. The paper defines butterfly factors $B^F(k)$ and butterfly matrices $B(d)$ recursively, but:
1. How exactly does the recursive structure of Cooley-Tukey FFT translate to the recursive definition of $B(d)$?
2. Why does this particular structure help reduce parameters from $O(d^2)$ to $O(d \log d)$ while maintaining orthogonality?
3. Is the connection mainly structural (the butterfly graph topology) or is there a deeper mathematical relationship?
Answer:
# How the Butterfly Structure in BOFT Relates to Cooley-Tukey FFT

The connection between BOFT's butterfly factorization and the Cooley-Tukey FFT is both structural and mathematical, and understanding it requires looking at how the recursive decomposition in FFT translates to matrix factorization.

## Recursive Structure: The Core Analogy

The Cooley-Tukey algorithm reduces an $N$-point DFT to two $N/2$-point DFTs by splitting the input into even- and odd-indexed elements, computing the smaller transforms, and then combining them with "twiddle factor" multiplications. This recursion produces $\log_2 N$ levels of butterfly operations, where each level mixes pairs of elements in a specific pattern.

BOFT mirrors this exactly. The butterfly matrix $B(d)$ is defined recursively as:

$$B(d) = \tilde{B}(d,d) \cdot \begin{bmatrix} B_1(d/2) & 0 \\ 0 & B_2(d/2) \end{bmatrix} = \tilde{B}(d,d)\tilde{B}(d,d/2)\cdots\tilde{B}(d,2)$$

The block-diagonal matrix $\text{diag}(B_1(d/2), B_2(d/2))$ corresponds to the two smaller $N/2$-point transforms, while the butterfly component $\tilde{B}(d,k)$ at each level plays the role of the butterfly operations that combine the sub-problem solutions. Unrolling the recursion yields $\log_2 d$ sparse matrices, exactly as the FFT recursion produces $\log_2 N$ stages of butterflies. The key insight is that the DFT matrix itself can be written as a product of these sparse butterfly matrices — this is the well-known matrix factorization of the FFT, and BOFT directly adopts this factorization structure.

## Why Parameters Drop to $O(d \log d)$ While Staying Orthogonal

A dense $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix requires $O(d^2)$ parameters (via Cayley parameterization with a skew-symmetric matrix $Q$, this is $d(d-1)/2$). The butterfly structure reduces this through two mechanisms working together.

First, sparsity at each level: each butterfly component $\tilde{B}(d,k)$ is block-diagonal with $d/k$ blocks of size $k \times k$. At the finest level ($k=2$), this means $d/2$ blocks of $2 \times 2$ orthogonal matrices, each requiring only $1$ parameter. At any level with block size $k$, there are $d/k$ blocks each needing $k(k-1)/2$ parameters, giving $O(d \cdot k)$ parameters per level.

Second, logarithmic depth: the product only needs $\log_2 d$ such sparse matrices to achieve dense connectivity. The information transmission framework in the paper explains why: after $\log_2 d$ stages of butterfly mixing, every input node has a path to every output node, producing a dense matrix.

Combining these, with $k=2$ at each of $\log_2 d$ levels: the total parameter count is $(d/2) \times \log_2 d = O(d \log d)$.

Orthogonality is preserved because the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal. Each butterfly component $\tilde{B}(d,k)$ is parameterized to be individually orthogonal (via Cayley transform on each block), so their product $R = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{B}_{(d,i)}$ is automatically orthogonal. The non-zero pattern of larger butterfly components $\tilde{B}(d,k)$ for $k > 2$ is simply a permutation of the pattern in $\tilde{B}(d,2)$, so the same orthogonality parameterization extends naturally.

## Structural, Mathematical, and Information-Theoretic Connection

The connection goes beyond surface-level topology similarity. Mathematically, the FFT is itself representable as a product of butterfly matrices — the factorization in BOFT is literally the matrix representation of the FFT's computational structure. As the paper notes, butterfly matrices can perfectly recover classic transforms including the DFT, DCT, and Hadamard transform, which is why this parameterization carries a useful inductive bias for finetuning.

At a deeper level, both FFT and BOFT solve the same information transmission problem: efficiently connecting $d$ inputs to $d$ outputs such that every input can influence every output. The FFT achieves this for frequency decomposition; BOFT achieves it for orthogonal weight transformation. The butterfly graph topology solves this with $O(d \log d)$ edges (parameters), which is provably more efficient than the $O(d^2)$ edges a single dense matrix requires. The paper formalizes this through its information transmission framework, where the desiderata are dense connectivity (every source node reaches every receiver) and minimum free edges (fewest trainable parameters). The butterfly structure from Cooley-Tukey naturally satisfies both, which is precisely why the authors drew the connection.

The trade-off BOFT introduces compared to block-diagonal OFT is also illuminated by this connection. Block-diagonal OFT corresponds to a single level of the butterfly (the case $m=1$), where information stays within blocks and never mixes across groups. Adding more butterfly levels ($m > 1$) progressively introduces cross-block mixing, interpolating between the block-diagonal structure (high regularity, low expressivity) and a full dense orthogonal matrix (low regularity, high expressivity). This interpolation is the structural analog of going from a single DFT stage to the full recursive FFT — each additional stage expands which frequency components can interact.
