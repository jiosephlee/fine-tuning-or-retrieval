\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does BOFT create 'smoother interpolation' between identity and orthogonal matrices?
Question:
The BOFT paper claims that "BOFT uses the butterfly structure to construct a smoother interpolation between matrices from the full orthogonal group (expressivity) and identity matrices (regularity)" and mentions an "intriguing weight interpolation" in their figures.

I'm confused about what exactly is being interpolated and how:
1. Is it the orthogonal matrix $R(m,b)$ that's being interpolated between $I$ and some target orthogonal matrix?
2. What makes this interpolation "smoother" compared to block-diagonal OFT? Is there a formal notion of smoothness being used?
3. How does the factorization $R = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)}$ enable this interpolation property?
4. The paper says this "enables us to find a smaller hypothesis class within the orthogonal group" - how does smoother interpolation relate to finding a good hypothesis class?
Answer:
## Answer

The "smoother interpolation" refers to the granularity of the expressivity-regularity trade-off, not a formal mathematical notion of smoothness (e.g., differentiability). Let me unpack this carefully.

**What is being interpolated:** The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is what gets interpolated. At one extreme, when all butterfly components are initialized as identity matrices (via zero skew-symmetric matrices in the Cayley parameterization), $\bm{R}(m,b) = \bm{I}_d$. At the other extreme, when $m = \log \frac{2d}{b}$, the product of all butterfly components yields a *dense* orthogonal matrix. The paper notes that BOFT($1,b$) reduces to block-diagonal OFT, while BOFT($\log \frac{2d}{b}, b$) produces a dense matrix. So varying $m$ from $1$ to $\log \frac{2d}{b}$ interpolates between a sparse, highly regular structure and a dense, fully expressive one.

**Why BOFT's interpolation is "smoother":** In standard OFT, the expressivity-regularity trade-off is controlled by the block size $b$. To get a dense orthogonal matrix, you must set $b = d$, jumping directly from a block-diagonal structure (with $b < d$) to a completely unconstrained full orthogonal matrix. There is no intermediate state — you either have isolated blocks operating independently, or you have a fully dense matrix with $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. BOFT, by contrast, introduces the number of factors $m$ as an additional degree of freedom. Each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is individually block-diagonal (with a permuted sparsity pattern), but multiplying successive components together progressively *mixes information across blocks*. Adding one more factor to the product is a smaller, more incremental change than jumping from block size $b$ to $d$. This gives a finer-grained spectrum of intermediate structures between the two extremes.

**How the factorization enables this:** Each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ has a block-diagonal sparsity pattern, but different components have *different* permutations of which dimensions interact. When you compute the product $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,m)} \cdots \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,1)}$, the multiplication successively fills in off-diagonal entries. With only $m=1$ factor, the product is block-diagonal — dimensions within the same block interact, but dimensions across blocks never mix. With $m=2$ factors, the second factor's permutation pattern allows some cross-block interactions. Each additional factor introduces a new permutation pattern that connects previously disconnected blocks, following the butterfly graph's information-routing structure. This is directly visible through the information transmission framework in the paper: each factor corresponds to one "level" of the butterfly graph, and information can only flow through $m$ levels. To achieve full connectivity (every source node reaching every receiver node), you need $m = \mathcal{O}(\log d)$ levels. With fewer levels, you get *partial* connectivity — some off-diagonal entries remain zero. This partial connectivity is precisely the "intermediate" point in the interpolation.

**Connection to the hypothesis class:** The set of all possible $\bm{R}(m,b)$ for fixed $m$ and $b$ constitutes a structured subset of the orthogonal group — a hypothesis class. When $m$ is small, this class is small (more regular, closer to identity, fewer effective parameters at $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$). When $m$ is large, the class grows toward the full orthogonal group (more expressive). The "smoother interpolation" means that BOFT provides many more intermediate hypothesis classes than OFT does, where the only meaningful choice is between block-diagonal (small $b$) and fully dense ($b = d$). The paper argues that the butterfly structure's hypothesis classes carry a beneficial inductive bias because they can recover classic linear transforms like the FFT, DCT, and Hadamard transform — something block-diagonal OFT cannot do at any block size. This makes the intermediate hypothesis classes not just arbitrary subsets, but *structured* ones aligned with natural linear transformations. Finding the right intermediate class (the right $m$ and $b$) lets you balance expressivity (enough capacity for the downstream task) against regularity (enough constraint to prevent overfitting), which is the core generalization challenge in finetuning that the paper emphasizes: "better expressiveness does not always lead to better performance."
