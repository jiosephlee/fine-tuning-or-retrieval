# The Rise of Large Foundation Models

In recent years, the field of artificial intelligence has witnessed a paradigm shift driven by the emergence of large foundation models. Models such as ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion have demonstrated remarkable generalization abilities, showcasing unprecedented performance across a wide array of tasks in both natural language processing and computer vision. These models are becoming ubiquitous, serving as the backbone for numerous applications in research and industry. The rapid increase in their performance, however, is paired with a dramatic increase in the number of parameters they contain. For instance, GPT-3, one of the most prominent large language models, possesses approximately 175 billion parameters. This exponential growth in model scale is a fundamental characteristic of modern foundation models, as the vast number of parameters is crucial for capturing complex patterns and achieving state-of-the-art results.

The sheer scale of these models represents a significant milestone in artificial intelligence. The large parameter counts allow foundation models to learn rich, generalizable representations from massive datasets during the pretraining phase. As a result, a single pretrained model can be adapted to perform competently on many different downstream tasks. The generality and power of these models make them highly desirable, as they encapsulate a broad understanding of the world that can be leveraged for specific applications. However, this immense scale also introduces significant logistical and computational hurdles. The rise of these models means that researchers and practitioners must work with architectures that are far larger and more complex than those of previous generations, necessitating new approaches to effectively utilize them.

# The Computational Challenge of Training from Scratch vs. Adapting Pretrained Models

While large foundation models offer unparalleled capabilities, training them from scratch is prohibitively expensive. The computational resources, time, and data required to train a model with hundreds of billions of parameters from the ground up are immense, making it increasingly challenging for researchers and organizations to undertake such an endeavor. As a result, training a foundation model from scratch is often infeasible for all but the most well-resourced entities. This poses a significant barrier to broad progress in the field, as the ability to innovate is constrained by access to the immense computational power needed for pretraining.

To overcome this bottleneck, it has become essential to adapt existing pretrained foundation models to downstream tasks without retraining them from scratch. Efficient task adaptation is therefore increasingly important, as it allows researchers to leverage the knowledge encoded in large foundation models while only requiring a fraction of the computational resources needed for initial training. The fundamental principle behind this adaptation is to ensure that the pretrained model and the finetuned model remain similar based on certain measurements, such that the pretraining knowledge is preserved. For example, traditional model finetuning methods often adopt a small learning rate to encourage a small discrepancy between the pretrained and finetuned weights. This approach helps to retain the general knowledge learned during pretraining while allowing the model to specialize for a specific downstream task.

# Overview of Task Adaptation Methods

To efficiently adapt large foundation models to downstream tasks, researchers have developed several paradigms that reduce the computational burden while preserving the model's capabilities. There are primarily three ways to perform efficient task adaptation: model finetuning, adapter tuning, and prompt tuning. Each of these methods offers a distinct approach to modifying a pretrained model for a new task, with different trade-offs regarding parameter efficiency and inference cost.

Model finetuning is a simple yet powerful approach where the overall model architecture remains unchanged and only a subset of the model parameters gets finetuned. A key advantage of this method is that it introduces no inference latency, meaning the adapted model runs just as fast as the original pretrained model during deployment. The goal is to encourage a small discrepancy between the pretrained and finetuned models to preserve knowledge. Adapter tuning, on the other hand, involves adding additional trainable parameters to the original model. These adapter modules are inserted into the pretrained network and trained on the downstream task while the base model parameters remain frozen. This approach allows for task-specific adaptation without modifying the foundational weights, but it can introduce additional computational overhead during inference due to the extra parameters. Finally, prompt tuning involves attaching additional trainable prefix tokens to the input. By optimizing these continuous prompts, the model can be guided to perform the desired task. This method is highly parameter-efficient, as only a small number of prompt tokens need to be trained, but it relies on the model's ability to interpret these prompts effectively.

# Reparameterization-Based Finetuning: An Introduction to LoRA and its Low-Rank Structure

Among the various parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) methods, reparameterization-based methods are particularly relevant for their ability to achieve high efficiency through structural constraints. LoRA, or Low-Rank Adaptation, is a prominent example of this approach. LoRA updates the pretrained weight matrix by adding a product of two low-rank matrices. Instead of modifying the original weights directly, LoRA introduces a low-rank update, achieving promising performance on natural language tasks. This method relies on a low-rank structure to achieve parameter efficiency, significantly reducing the number of trainable parameters compared to full finetuning.

The core mechanism of LoRA involves representing the weight update as the product of two smaller matrices. If the original weight matrix has a high dimensionality, the low-rank constraint assumes that the necessary changes for downstream adaptation lie within a lower-dimensional subspace. Since the rank of the added matrices is typically set to a constant, several subsequent methods have explored dynamically adjusting the rank for different layers to allocate the parameter budget more adequately. Due to its simplicity and effectiveness, low-rank weight reparameterization has gained great popularity. In the broader context of structured matrices, both low-rank matrices and sparse matrices are major families that achieve parameter efficiency. While LoRA leverages the low-rank family, other methods may leverage sparsity to reduce the effective number of trainable parameters.

# Introduction to Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) as an Alternative Multiplicative Approach

While LoRA and other methods rely on additive low-rank updates, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) offers a principled, multiplicative alternative for model adaptation. The motivation for OFT comes from the insight that a more principled approach to finetuning tries to preserve the relational information of the weight matrices, specifically the pairwise angles between neurons. In a neural network layer, neurons can be viewed as column vectors of the weight matrix. By ensuring that neurons in the same layer are transformed by the same orthogonal matrix, OFT provably preserves the pairwise angles between them throughout the finetuning process. This preservation helps ensure that the semantic knowledge learned during pretraining is largely retained.

Concretely, OFT optimizes an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ for a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. The forward pass is modified from $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ and $\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ are the input and output vectors, respectively. To achieve zero initialization—which ensures that finetuning starts from the exact pretrained model—OFT initializes the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as an identity matrix. To maintain the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ throughout training, OFT employs Cayley parameterization, defined as $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$.

Despite demonstrating good generalizability and stable training, OFT faces a significant challenge: the number of trainable parameters can be quite large due to the high dimensionality of orthogonal matrices. A dense $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix generally requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. To address this, OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure, parameterizing $\bm{R}$ as $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \cdots, \bm{R}_r)$ where each $\bm{R}_i$ is a small $b\times b$ orthogonal matrix and $br=d$. While this reduces parameters to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$, it introduces a rigid sparsity pattern where the orthogonal transformation is applied independently in different blocks. This block-diagonal approach reduces expressiveness, as it cannot approximate classic dense linear transforms, and makes the arbitrary division of neuron dimensions into groups based on their indices a potentially undesirable inductive bias. This limitation sets the stage for more advanced methods that seek to construct dense orthogonal matrices in a parameter-efficient manner.

# Foundations of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

## The Principle of Preserving Pretraining Knowledge in Model Finetuning

Large foundation models such as ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion have demonstrated remarkable generalization capabilities, but their training from scratch is prohibitively expensive. For instance, GPT-3 has approximately 175 billion parameters, making it increasingly challenging for researchers to train such models from the ground up. This reality has elevated the importance of efficient task adaptation — the ability to take an existing pretrained model and adapt it to downstream tasks without retraining it entirely. There are three primary paradigms for efficient task adaptation: model finetuning, where the model architecture remains unchanged and a subset of parameters gets finetuned; adapter tuning, where additional trainable parameters are added to the original model; and prompt tuning, where additional trainable prefix tokens are attached to the input. Among these, model finetuning is particularly attractive because it is simple, powerful, and introduces no inference latency.

The fundamental principle behind model finetuning is to ensure that the pretrained model and the finetuned model remain similar according to some meaningful measurement, such that the knowledge acquired during pretraining is preserved. Current model finetuning methods often adopt a small learning rate as an ad hoc approach to encourage a small discrepancy between the pretrained and finetuned models. While this practical heuristic does help prevent the finetuned model from drifting too far from the pretrained one, it lacks a principled theoretical foundation. A more principled approach leverages the structured nature of weight matrices and tries to preserve the relational information encoded within them — specifically, the pairwise angles between neurons. This insight, grounded in the observation that hyperspherical energy characterizes generalization, leads to a novel model finetuning framework known as Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). The core idea is that by transforming neurons within the same layer using the same orthogonal matrix, the pairwise angles between those neurons are provably preserved throughout the finetuning process, thereby safeguarding the semantic knowledge transferred from pretraining.

## Motivation for Orthogonal Transformations: Preserving Pairwise Angles Between Neurons

The motivation for using orthogonal transformations in finetuning stems from the geometric structure of neural network weight matrices. Each neuron in a layer can be viewed as a column vector of the weight matrix, and the pairwise angles between these column vectors encode important relational information about how neurons interact and represent features. When a model is finetuned, the concern is that these pairwise angles could be disrupted, leading to the loss of pretraining knowledge. By constraining the finetuning transformation to be orthogonal, OFT ensures that the transformation preserves all pairwise angles between neurons. An orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ satisfies $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$, which means that for any two column vectors $\bm{w}_i^0$ and $\bm{w}_j^0$ of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, the angle between $\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0$ and $\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0$ is identical to the angle between $\bm{w}_i^0$ and $\bm{w}_j^0$. This is because the inner product $\langle \bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0, \bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0 \rangle = \bm{w}_i^{0\top}\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0 = \bm{w}_i^{0\top}\bm{w}_j^0 = \langle \bm{w}_i^0, \bm{w}_j^0 \rangle$, and since the norm of each vector is also preserved by the orthogonal transformation, the cosine of the angle (and thus the angle itself) is unchanged.

This preservation of pairwise angles has been shown to benefit generalization, drawing on prior work establishing connections between hyperspherical energy, orthogonal constraints, and generalization ability. OFT has demonstrated promising generalizability and convergence, particularly in finetuning text-to-image diffusion models. Compared to LoRA, which updates weights using an additive low-rank matrix, OFT uses a multiplicative orthogonal matrix to update the weights, achieving stronger generalization and more consistently stable training. The multiplicative approach inherently preserves the geometric relationships within the weight matrix, whereas the additive approach in LoRA does not provide such guarantees.

## Mathematical Formulation of OFT: Forward Pass Modification and Orthogonal Matrix Multiplication

The mathematical formulation of OFT is straightforward yet powerful. Consider a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where $d$ is the input dimension and $n$ is the output dimension. The original forward pass computes the output as $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the input vector and $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the output vector. OFT modifies this forward pass by introducing a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ that multiplies the pretrained weight matrix from the left, changing the forward pass to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. The pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ remains frozen, and only the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is optimized during finetuning.

A crucial aspect of this formulation is the initialization of $\bm{R}$ as an identity matrix. This zero-initialization strategy ensures that, at the start of finetuning, the finetuned model is identical to the pretrained model — that is, $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{I}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{W}^0$. This means the finetuning process begins from the exact pretrained model, and the orthogonal matrix gradually deviates from the identity as training progresses, allowing the model to adapt to the downstream task while maintaining a connection to the pretrained weights. This design philosophy parallels LoRA's use of zero initialization for its low-rank weight updates, ensuring that the finetuning process starts from the pretrained model and does not deviate excessively.

The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ transforms all neurons (column vectors of $\bm{W}^0$) in the same layer by the same transformation. This shared transformation is what guarantees the preservation of pairwise angles, as discussed in the previous section. It is worth noting that this is a multiplicative weight update, in contrast to the additive update used by LoRA, which modifies the weights as $\bm{W}^0 + \Delta\bm{W}$ where $\Delta\bm{W}$ is a low-rank matrix. The multiplicative approach has a distinct advantage: it perfectly preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix, since the singular values of $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ are identical to those of $\bm{W}^0$ (as $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal). This spectral preservation has been shown to greatly benefit training stability and generalization.

## Cayley Parameterization for Ensuring Orthogonality

A key technical challenge in OFT is ensuring that the matrix $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal throughout the entire finetuning process. Simply optimizing $\bm{R}$ as a free parameter and then projecting it onto the orthogonal group after each update would be computationally expensive and could introduce instability. Instead, OFT employs the Cayley parameterization, which provides a smooth mapping from the space of skew-symmetric matrices to the space of orthogonal matrices.

The Cayley parameterization expresses the orthogonal matrix as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. The skew-symmetry constraint on $\bm{Q}$ ensures that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal. To see why this works, consider that the Cayley transform maps any skew-symmetric matrix to an orthogonal matrix that does not have $-1$ as an eigenvalue. The skew-symmetric property of $\bm{Q}$ means that $\bm{Q}$ has $d(d-1)/2$ free parameters (the upper triangular entries, with the lower triangular entries determined by the upper ones and the diagonal entries forced to be zero), which is exactly the dimensionality of the orthogonal group $O(d)$.

During finetuning, the trainable parameters are the entries of $\bm{Q}$, and the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is computed from $\bm{Q}$ via the Cayley transform at each forward pass. Since the Cayley transform guarantees orthogonality by construction, there is no need for explicit orthogonalization or projection steps during training. For zero initialization (ensuring $\bm{R}$ starts as the identity), the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ is initialized to zeros, since $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{0})(\bm{I} - \bm{0})^{-1} = \bm{I} \cdot \bm{I}^{-1} = \bm{I}$. This elegant parameterization thus simultaneously ensures orthogonality, enables smooth optimization, and supports zero initialization.

## Block-Diagonal OFT: Introducing Sparsity for Parameter Efficiency and Its Limitations

Despite the strong generalizability demonstrated by OFT, the number of trainable parameters can be quite large due to the high dimensionality of orthogonal matrices. A full $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix parameterized via the Cayley transform requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters (specifically $d(d-1)/2$ free parameters from the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$). For large foundation models where $d$ can be thousands or more, this parameter count becomes prohibitively expensive for efficient finetuning.

To address this, OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure to reduce the number of trainable parameters. Specifically, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is parameterized as $\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \cdots, \bm{R}_r)$, where each $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ is a small orthogonal matrix and $br = d$. Here, $r$ is the number of blocks and $b$ is the block size. Each small orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}_i$ is parameterized using the Cayley transform with its own skew-symmetric matrix. The total number of trainable parameters becomes $\mathcal{O}(bd)$, since each $b \times b$ block requires $b(b-1)/2$ parameters and there are $r = d/b$ blocks, yielding $r \cdot b(b-1)/2 = (d/b) \cdot b(b-1)/2 = d(b-1)/2 = \mathcal{O}(bd)$. By choosing a smaller block size $b$, one can significantly reduce the parameter count. The smaller the block size $b$, the more parameter-efficient OFT becomes.

However, this parameter efficiency comes at a significant price. The block-diagonal structure imposes a fixed sparsity pattern on the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, meaning that the orthogonal transformation is applied independently within each block of dimensions. Dimensions belonging to different blocks are never mixed or interacted with each other through the transformation. This introduces a strong and potentially undesirable assumption: the dimensions of each neuron (i.e., each column vector of the weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$) are divided into $r$ groups based on their indices, and dimensions within different groups are transformed separately using different orthogonal matrices. The block-diagonal orthogonal matrix also reduces expressiveness — it cannot approximate classic linear transforms such as the discrete Fourier transform or the Hadamard transform, which require mixing across all dimensions.

## The Inductive Bias Problem of Block-Diagonal Structures and the Need for Dense Orthogonal Matrices

The block-diagonal structure in OFT introduces a problematic inductive bias that undermines the principled motivation behind orthogonal finetuning. The assumption that the dimensions of a neuron can be partitioned into $r$ groups based on their indices is arbitrary and lacks theoretical justification. There is no principled reason to believe that dimensions $1$ through $b$ of a neuron should be transformed together and independently from dimensions $b+1$ through $2b$, and so on. The grouping is entirely determined by the index ordering of the dimensions, which is a artifact of implementation rather than a meaningful property of the model's representations. This makes the block-diagonal approach semantically questionable, as it makes no sense to divide the dimensions of a neuron into groups based on their indices.

Furthermore, the block-diagonal structure severely limits the expressiveness of the orthogonal transformation. A block-diagonal orthogonal matrix can only perform rotations within each block independently, meaning it can never represent transformations that mix information across blocks. Many classical and practically important linear transforms — such as the discrete Fourier transform, discrete cosine transform, and Hadamard transform — require dense mixing across all dimensions and therefore cannot be represented by any block-diagonal matrix, regardless of the block size chosen. This means that the block-diagonal OFT fundamentally cannot approximate these transforms, which limits its ability to capture certain types of transformations that might be beneficial for downstream tasks.

The choice of sparsity pattern — that is, how to partition the dimensions into blocks — also remains a mystery. There is no clear principled criterion for selecting a good block-diagonal structure, and different partitioning choices could lead to significantly different finetuning performance. This lack of guidance makes the block-diagonal approach difficult to tune and potentially suboptimal.

These limitations motivate the need for dense orthogonal matrices — matrices where every entry can be non-zero, allowing full mixing across all dimensions. A dense orthogonal matrix would avoid the arbitrary grouping assumption, could potentially approximate classic linear transforms, and would not require choosing a sparsity pattern. However, constructing a dense orthogonal matrix naively requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which defeats the purpose of parameter-efficient finetuning. The key challenge, therefore, is to find a way to construct a dense orthogonal matrix that is both expressive and parameter-efficient. This challenge — composing a dense orthogonal matrix from a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices — is the central problem that the Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) method addresses, using insights from information transmission theory and the butterfly structure of the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm to achieve $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters while maintaining the ability to generate dense orthogonal matrices.

An Information Transmission Perspective on Matrix Factorization

# Composing dense orthogonal matrices with a product of multiple sparse matrices

In the realm of large foundation models, efficiently adapting pretrained models to downstream tasks is crucial. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) provides a principled way to adapt models by multiplying the pretrained weight matrix by an orthogonal matrix. This preserves the pairwise angles between neurons, ensuring that the semantic knowledge learned during pretraining is maintained. However, a dense orthogonal matrix of dimension $d \times d$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which becomes prohibitively expensive as model sizes grow. To mitigate this, the original OFT approach introduced a block-diagonal structure, but this severely limits expressiveness and introduces undesirable inductive biases.

To overcome the parameter inefficiency of dense orthogonal matrices without sacrificing their expressiveness, we can compose a dense orthogonal matrix through the product of multiple sparse matrices. The core insight here is to trade computation time for space. Instead of learning and storing a single dense matrix with $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, we represent the orthogonal matrix as a sequence of sparse matrices multiplied together. Formally, we can express a dense matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ as the product of $m$ square matrices: $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$. 

By ensuring that each individual sparse matrix $\bm{B}_i$ is orthogonal, their product $\bm{R}$ is guaranteed to be orthogonal as well. This approach allows us to maintain the desirable properties of a dense orthogonal transformation while drastically reducing the number of trainable parameters required. However, finding the right sparsity patterns for these factorized matrices to ensure the final product is dense is a non-trivial challenge. To systematically design and understand these sparse factorizations, we introduce an intuitive framework based on information transmission.

# Formulating matrix factorization as an information transmission problem on a grid-structured graph

To tackle the challenge of composing a dense orthogonal matrix from sparse matrices, we reframe the matrix factorization process as an information transmission problem on a grid-structured graph. This framework provides a unified, visual, and intuitive perspective for studying the sparsity patterns of matrix factorizations. 

In this formulation, generating a $d$-dimensional dense matrix $\bm{R}$ by multiplying $m$ square matrices can be visualized as transmitting information across a grid containing $d \times (m+1)$ nodes. The grid has $m+1$ columns (or levels) of nodes, with each level containing exactly $d$ nodes. A dense square matrix fundamentally represents a dense connectivity from $d$ input nodes to $d$ output nodes. If we consider an element $R_{ij}$ of the matrix $\bm{R}$, a zero value indicates that information from the $j$-th input node cannot flow to the $i$-th output node. Conversely, if $R_{ij}$ is non-zero, information can be successfully transmitted from node $j$ to node $i$.

When we factorize $\bm{R}$ into a sequence of matrices $\bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$, we are effectively breaking down this information transmission into a series of sequential exchange steps governed by the graphs induced by each $\bm{B}_i$. The information flows according to the connectivity defined by $\bm{B}_1$ first, and then propagates through the subsequent matrices, ending with $\bm{B}_m$ last. By unrolling the matrix multiplication into this graphical representation, we can analyze whether a given sequence of sparse matrices is capable of generating a dense matrix by simply checking if information from every node in the first level can reach every node in the final level.

# Interpreting matrix sparsity patterns as information flow between adjacent graph levels

Within the grid-structured graph, the sparsity pattern of each factorization matrix directly dictates the information flow between adjacent levels of nodes. Specifically, each matrix $\bm{B}_i$ acts as the connectivity matrix between the $i$-th level of nodes and the $(i+1)$-th level. The precise connectivity is determined by the elements of $\bm{B}_i$: the $(j_1, j_2)$ element of $\bm{B}_i$ denotes whether there is a directed edge from the $j_2$-th node in the $i$-th level to the $j_1$-th node in the $(i+1)$-th level. If this element is zero, no edge exists and no information can pass directly between those two nodes.

For the final product matrix $\bm{R}$ to be dense, every node in the first level must be able to transmit information to all nodes in the last, $(m+1)$-th, level. This means that the $m$ factorization matrices must correspond to a set of directed edges on the grid where information can cascade from the first level to the last. A key restriction is that a directed edge can only connect two nodes in adjacent levels (i.e., between column $i$ and column $i+1$). 

To make this concrete, consider the factorization $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_5 \bm{B}_4 \bm{B}_3 \bm{B}_2 \bm{B}_1$. If we examine a subset of this product, such as $\bm{B}_4 \bm{B}_3 \bm{B}_2 \bm{B}_1$, we are looking at the information flow from the source nodes in the first level to the receiver nodes in the 5th level. If, through this specific sequence of connections, information from node 1 cannot reach node 3, it means the resulting sparsity pattern of the partial product $\bm{B}_4 \bm{B}_3 \bm{B}_2 \bm{B}_1$ will have a zero element at the $(3,1)$ position. This direct correspondence between graph connectivity and matrix sparsity allows us to easily evaluate and design factorizations based on their graphical information flow.

# Key desiderata for efficient factorization: dense connectivity and minimum free edges

The information transmission framework allows us to clearly define the goals for an efficient parameterization of a dense orthogonal matrix. To generate a dense matrix $\bm{R}$ using as few trainable parameters as possible, our factorization must satisfy two key desiderata: dense connectivity and minimum free edges.

First, the factorization must ensure dense connectivity. This means that in the induced graph, every node in the first level must have at least one directed path to every node in the last level. Without this property, the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ would contain zeros, failing to be a truly dense matrix and limiting its expressiveness. Second, we aim for minimum free edges. The total number of edges in the graph (which corresponds to the trainable parameters in the matrices) should be as small as possible, particularly under the strict constraint of orthogonality.

To understand the parameter savings, consider a naive dense connection between two levels, which corresponds to a single dense orthogonal matrix. This requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges. More precisely, it yields $d^2 - d$ edges (for $d=4$, this is 12 edges). In contrast, a carefully designed sparse factorization can achieve dense connectivity with far fewer edges. For instance, a feasible factorization presented in the framework uses only 10 edges to connect all nodes across the grid, which is already less than the 12 required by a single dense matrix. By minimizing the number of free edges while maintaining dense connectivity, we can achieve a parameter-efficient representation of a dense orthogonal matrix.

# Analyzing the orthogonality constraint within the context of graph edges and bijections

The requirement that each matrix $\bm{B}_i$ must be orthogonal injects a delicate and powerful constraint into our graph-based information transmission framework. To ensure that a matrix $\bm{B}_i$ is full-rank—which is a necessary condition for orthogonality—there must be exactly $d$ edges forming a bijection between all the nodes in the $i$-th level and all the nodes in the $(i+1)$-th level. Because every node must be connected to exactly one node in the adjacent level to form this bijection, the number of edges between any two adjacent levels must be at least $d$.

Crucially, these $d$ edges are necessary for orthogonality but do not represent trainable parameters in the traditional sense. For a $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix with exactly $d$ non-zero entries, those entries can only be $\pm 1$, effectively acting as a permutation. Since these elements are not freely trainable, they should not be counted in our budget of "free edges." 

Furthermore, the orthogonality constraint introduces structural dependencies among the edges. For example, in a $2 \times 2$ orthogonal matrix, if there is a zero at the $(1,1)$ position, it inherently implies another zero at the $(2,2)$ position. In the graph framework, this means that one missing edge implies the existence of another missing edge. Because of these dependencies, for any given set of feasible edge connections, the orthogonality constraint may automatically add or remove some edges. Ultimately, this framework is particularly useful for OFT because we only care about the non-zero trainable elements of $\bm{R}$, and their specific values do not matter when evaluating the structural sparsity pattern.

# Evaluating the block-diagonal structure's failure to achieve dense connectivity in the graph framework

To appreciate the necessity of this new information transmission framework, we must evaluate the limitations of the original OFT's block-diagonal structure. The original OFT reduced parameters by parameterizing the orthogonal matrix as $\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \cdots, \bm{R}_r)$, where each $\bm{R}_i$ is a small $b \times b$ orthogonal matrix and $br = d$. When visualized in the information transmission framework, it becomes evident that the block-diagonal structure fundamentally cannot construct a dense matrix $\bm{R}$.

The failure stems from the fact that the block-diagonal structure assumes the dimensions of a neuron (i.e., a column vector of the weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$) are divided into $r$ distinct groups. The orthogonal transformation is then applied independently within these separate blocks. In the graph framework, this means information can only flow within isolated clusters of nodes between adjacent levels. A node in one block has no edges connecting it to nodes in a different block. Consequently, information from a node in the first level belonging to block 1 can never reach a node in the final level belonging to block 2. This violates the desideratum of dense connectivity.

Because information cannot cross block boundaries, the final matrix $\bm{R}$ remains sparse, with zero elements everywhere except within the diagonal blocks. Furthermore, this grouping makes little semantic sense; it arbitrarily divides the dimensions of a neuron based on their indices, transforming dimensions in different groups completely independently. This graphical evaluation clearly illustrates why the block-diagonal structure is insufficient and highlights the need for a factorization method—like the butterfly structures inspired by the Cooley-Tukey algorithm—that enables dense connectivity across all dimensions while preserving parameter efficiency.

# Butterfly Structures for Orthogonal Parameterization

## Inspiration from the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform and Its Efficient Information Exchange Topology

The quest for a parameter-efficient dense orthogonal matrix begins with a simple but powerful observation: a dense $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix naively requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is prohibitively expensive for the high-dimensional weight matrices of modern foundation models. However, as discussed in the information transmission framework, we can compose a dense matrix as a product of sparse matrices, and the key challenge is to find a sparsity pattern that achieves dense connectivity—meaning every source node can transmit information to every receiver node—while minimizing the total number of edges (i.e., trainable parameters). This is precisely the kind of problem that has been studied for decades in a seemingly unrelated domain: signal processing.

The Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithm, introduced in 1965, is one of the most celebrated algorithms in all of computing. Its fundamental insight is that a discrete Fourier transform of size $N$ can be recursively decomposed into two discrete Fourier transforms of size $N/2$. By applying this decomposition repeatedly, the algorithm reduces the computational complexity of the Fourier transform from $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$. The recursive decomposition induces a characteristic graph topology known as the butterfly graph, which describes how data flows through the stages of the computation. The name "butterfly" arises from the visual appearance of the graph, where pairs of nodes at one level connect to pairs at the next level in a pattern resembling butterfly wings.

The butterfly topology is remarkable because it provides a solution to a general information exchange problem: given $d$ source nodes and $d$ receiver nodes arranged across multiple levels, how can we connect them such that information from every source can reach every receiver, using as few edges as possible? The butterfly network achieves this with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges, which is dramatically fewer than the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges required by a fully dense connection. To build intuition, consider a scenario with $d = 4$ source nodes and $d = 4$ receiver nodes. A naive dense connection would require $16$ edges (or $12$ trainable edges after accounting for the orthogonality constraint, as $4$ edges are determined by the bijection requirement). The example factorization shown in the paper's information transmission framework uses $10$ edges, already an improvement. The butterfly network, however, achieves dense connectivity with only $8$ edges—a significant reduction that scales favorably as $d$ grows.

This connection between the FFT and our orthogonal finetuning problem is not merely superficial. In the Fourier transform, a local change in the frequency domain causes a global change in the spatial domain. This is conceptually analogous to our information transmission problem: we want every node in the first level to be able to influence every node in the last level, even though each individual factor matrix only connects nodes between adjacent levels. The butterfly structure provides an elegant, proven topology for achieving exactly this kind of global connectivity through local, structured exchanges.

The butterfly structure has also found widespread use as a computer network topology for efficient information exchange in distributed systems. This reinforces the idea that the butterfly graph is a general-purpose structure for routing information efficiently, not merely a computational trick specific to Fourier analysis. By drawing on this well-understood topology, we can design a parameterization scheme that constructs a dense orthogonal matrix from a product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse matrices, each with $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries. The total number of parameters thus becomes $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$, a dramatic improvement over the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters required by a full dense orthogonal matrix.

## Mathematical Definition of the Butterfly Factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ and the Recursive Butterfly Matrix $\bm{B}(d)$

To formalize the butterfly structure for use in orthogonal parameterization, we begin by defining the fundamental building block: the butterfly factor. Assuming that $k \geq 2$ is a power of $2$, the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is defined as a $k \times k$ matrix with a specific block structure:

$$\bm{B}^F(k) = \begin{bmatrix} \text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\ \text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4) \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k}$$

where $\bm{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{k}{2}}, \forall i$ are vectors of length $k/2$. The key structural property of this matrix is that it is composed of four diagonal blocks, each of size $\frac{k}{2} \times \frac{k}{2}$. This means that the butterfly factor is a sparse matrix—most of its entries are zero—and the non-zero entries follow a very specific pattern determined by the diagonal sub-vectors $\bm{d}_1, \bm{d}_2, \bm{d}_3, \bm{d}_4$.

To understand why this structure is useful, consider what happens when we multiply a vector by $\bm{B}^F(k)$. The top half of the output depends on the top half of the input through $\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1)$ and on the bottom half of the input through $\text{diag}(\bm{d}_2)$. Similarly, the bottom half of the output depends on both halves of the input through $\text{diag}(\bm{d}_3)$ and $\text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)$. This "mixing" of information between the top and bottom halves is the essence of the butterfly operation, and it is what enables information to propagate across the entire dimension through repeated application.

With the butterfly factor defined, we can now construct the full $d$-dimensional butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ recursively. Assuming $d = 2^N$ for some integer $N$, the butterfly matrix is defined as:

$$\bm{B}(d) = \tilde{\bm{B}}(d, d) \cdot \begin{bmatrix} \bm{B}_1\left(\frac{d}{2}\right) & \bm{0} \\ \bm{0} & \bm{B}_2\left(\frac{d}{2}\right) \end{bmatrix} = \tilde{\bm{B}}(d, d)\tilde{\bm{B}}\left(d, \frac{d}{2}\right) \cdots \tilde{\bm{B}}(d, 2)$$

where $\bm{B}_1\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)$ and $\bm{B}_2\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)$ are two independent $\frac{d}{2}$-dimensional butterfly matrices. This recursive definition reveals the self-similar nature of the butterfly structure: a $d$-dimensional butterfly matrix is composed of a butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, d)$ multiplied by a block-diagonal matrix containing two smaller butterfly matrices of half the dimension. By unrolling this recursion completely, we obtain a product of $\log_2 d$ butterfly components: $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, d), \tilde{\bm{B}}(d, \frac{d}{2}), \ldots, \tilde{\bm{B}}(d, 2)$.

This recursive factorization is the key to the parameter efficiency of the butterfly structure. Each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, k)$ is a sparse matrix with $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries, and there are $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ such components in the product. The total number of non-zero entries across all components is therefore $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$, and yet the product of all these sparse matrices yields a dense matrix. This is the mathematical realization of the information transmission intuition: through $\log d$ stages of structured local exchanges, information from any source node can reach any receiver node, producing a dense connectivity pattern.

The recursive structure also reveals an important hierarchical property. At the coarsest level, $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, d)$ connects the two halves of the dimension. At the next level, $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, \frac{d}{2})$ connects quarters within each half, and so on, until $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, 2)$ connects individual pairs of dimensions. This hierarchical mixing pattern—first mixing large blocks, then progressively smaller blocks—is what enables efficient information propagation and is directly inherited from the Cooley-Tukey decomposition strategy.

## Defining the Butterfly Component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ and Its Block-Diagonal Nature

The butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, k)$ is the workhorse of the butterfly matrix factorization. It is defined as a block-diagonal matrix composed of multiple butterfly factors:

$$\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, k) = \text{diag}\left(\bm{B}^F_1(k), \cdots, \bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k)\right)$$

This is a $d \times d$ block-diagonal matrix with $\frac{d}{k}$ blocks along the diagonal, each of which is a $k \times k$ butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F_i(k)$ as defined above. Each butterfly factor may have different parameter values (i.e., different $\bm{d}_i$ vectors), but they all share the same sparsity structure.

The block-diagonal nature of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, k)$ is crucial for understanding both its computational and parameteric properties. First, because it is block-diagonal, multiplying a vector by $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, k)$ can be performed independently on each block, leading to efficient computation. Second, the number of non-zero entries in $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, k)$ is exactly $\frac{d}{k} \times k = d$ times the number of non-zero entries per row in each butterfly factor. Since each butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ has exactly $k$ non-zero entries per row (two per diagonal block, with $\frac{k}{2}$ entries each, giving $4 \times \frac{k}{2} = 2k$... but actually, each butterfly factor has exactly $2k$ non-zero entries total, since it is a $k \times k$ matrix with four $\frac{k}{2} \times \frac{k}{2}$ diagonal blocks, each contributing $\frac{k}{2}$ non-zero entries, for a total of $4 \times \frac{k}{2} = 2k$ non-zero entries). With $\frac{d}{k}$ such blocks, the total number of non-zero entries is $\frac{d}{k} \times 2k = 2d$, which is $\mathcal{O}(d)$.

As $k$ varies from $2$ to $d$ across the different components in the butterfly matrix, the block structure changes. When $k = 2$, the component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, 2)$ consists of $\frac{d}{2}$ blocks of size $2 \times 2$, which is the finest level of mixing. When $k = d$, the component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, d)$ is a single $d \times d$ butterfly factor, which is the coarsest level of mixing. The progression from $k = 2$ to $k = d$ corresponds to mixing at progressively larger scales, which is the hallmark of the butterfly structure inherited from the Cooley-Tukey algorithm.

It is worth noting that while each individual butterfly component is block-diagonal and therefore sparse with a limited connectivity pattern, the product of all butterfly components across different scales produces a dense matrix. This is because the different components operate at different scales of mixing: $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, 2)$ mixes pairs of adjacent dimensions, $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, 4)$ mixes groups of four dimensions, and so on up to $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, d)$, which mixes the two halves of the entire dimension. The composition of mixing at all scales is what achieves the dense connectivity that a single block-diagonal matrix cannot.

## Ensuring Orthogonality of Butterfly Components Using Cayley Transform and Block-Wise Permutations

The butterfly matrix structure provides an efficient way to compose a dense matrix from sparse factors, but for our finetuning purpose, we need the resulting matrix to be orthogonal. A matrix $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal if $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R} \bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$, which preserves angles and norms. The orthogonality of the overall butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ is guaranteed if and only if each individual butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, k)$ is orthogonal, because the product of orthogonal matrices is itself orthogonal.

To ensure orthogonality of each butterfly component, we start with the simplest case: the block-diagonal matrix $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, 2)$ with block size $2$. Each block in this matrix is a $2 \times 2$ butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(2)$, which needs to be orthogonal. For $2 \times 2$ matrices, orthogonality can be guaranteed using the Cayley transform, which parameterizes an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as:

$$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$$

where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. For a $2 \times 2$ matrix, $\bm{Q}$ has only one free parameter (since the diagonal must be zero and the off-diagonal entries are negatives of each other), so each $2 \times 2$ orthogonal block requires only one trainable parameter. Alternatively, the $2 \times 2$ orthogonal block can be parameterized as a 2-dimensional rotation matrix, which also has a single free parameter (the rotation angle). Both approaches guarantee orthogonality by construction.

Having established orthogonality for $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, 2)$, the orthogonality of the other butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, k)$ for $k > 2$ follows from a key observation about their structure. The non-zero pattern of every butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, k)$ can be viewed as a permutation of the non-zero pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, 2)$. In other words, if we rearrange the rows and columns of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, k)$ appropriately, we obtain a matrix that has the same sparsity structure as $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, 2)$—a block-diagonal matrix with $2 \times 2$ blocks. Since permutation matrices are themselves orthogonal, and the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal, ensuring that the permuted version of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, k)$ is orthogonal (using the same Cayley transform approach) guarantees that $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, k)$ itself is orthogonal.

This block-wise permutation insight is powerful because it means we do not need to develop a separate orthogonality guarantee for each value of $k$. Instead, we can always reduce the problem to the $2 \times 2$ case, which is well-understood and easily parameterized. This gives us an efficient parameterization of orthogonal matrices built upon many $2 \times 2$ orthogonal matrices, each requiring only a single parameter. With $\frac{d}{2}$ such blocks per component and $\log d$ components, the total parameter count is $\frac{d}{2} \log d = \mathcal{O}(d \log d)$, achieving the desired parameter efficiency.

The identity initialization for BOFT follows naturally from this parameterization. Since the Cayley transform with $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$ yields $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, we can initialize all skew-symmetric matrices to zero, which means all butterfly components start as identity matrices. This ensures that the finetuning process begins with the exact pretrained model, analogous to how LoRA initializes its low-rank weight updates to zero.

## Generalization to Block Butterfly Components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ for Broader Applicability

While the butterfly parameterization with $2 \times 2$ blocks achieves excellent parameter efficiency, there are situations where a finer-grained control over the trade-off between expressivity and parameter count is desirable. The original OFT with block-diagonal structure used blocks of arbitrary size $b$, and we would like BOFT to similarly support flexible block sizes. To this end, the butterfly structure is generalized following the approach in prior work on pixelated butterfly matrices.

The generalization introduces the block butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d, k)$, where each scalar entry in the vectors $\bm{d}_i$ of the butterfly factor is replaced by a $b \times b$ matrix. Concretely, the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ now has diagonal blocks $\text{diag}(\bm{d}_i)$ where each $\bm{d}_i$ is no longer a vector of scalars but a block-diagonal matrix of $b \times b$ blocks. This means that each "entry" in the butterfly structure becomes a $b \times b$ matrix, and the effective block size for orthogonal parameterization becomes $2b \times 2b$ instead of $2 \times 2$.

To guarantee that the block butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d, 2)$ is orthogonal, each $2b \times 2b$ block matrix must be parameterized as an orthogonal matrix. This can again be done using the Cayley transform, now with a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ of size $2b \times 2b$, which has $\frac{2b(2b-1)}{2} = b(2b-1)$ free parameters. The non-zero patterns of the other block butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d, k)$ for $k > 2$ are block-wise permutations of the non-zero pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d, 2)$, so they can similarly be turned into orthogonal matrices using the same approach.

With this generalization, the forward pass in BOFT is given by:

$$\bm{z} = \left(\bm{R}(m, b) \cdot \bm{W}^0\right)^\top \bm{x}$$

subject to the constraints:

$$\bm{R}(m, b) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d, i)} \quad \text{and} \quad \left(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d, j)}\right)^\top \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d, j)} = \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d, j)} \left(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d, j)}\right)^\top = \bm{I}_d, \quad \forall j \in [1, m]$$

where $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d, i)}$ denotes $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d, 2^{m-i+1})$ for notational simplicity, and $\bm{I}_d$ is the $d \times d$ identity matrix. The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m, b) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is composed of a product of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components, each with block size $b$.

The notation BOFT($m$, $b$) is used to denote BOFT with $\bm{R}(m, \frac{b}{2})$, where $b \geq 2$. This notation reveals a rich family of finetuning configurations that interpolates between existing methods. When $m = 1$, BOFT($1$, $b$) reduces to the block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, which is the original parameter-efficient variant of OFT. When $m = 1$ and $b = d$, BOFT($1$, $d$) reduces to the original OFT with an unconstrained full orthogonal matrix. When $m = \log \frac{2d}{b}$ and $b$ is the block size, BOFT uses the full block butterfly matrix $\bm{B}^{\frac{b}{2}}(d)$ as $\bm{R}$, yielding a dense orthogonal matrix. In general, BOFT($m$, $b$) requires $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters for finetuning a linear layer of size $d \times n$.

This generalization provides a flexible framework for navigating the trade-off between expressivity and parameter efficiency. Using the full butterfly matrix with $m = \log d$ and $b = 2$ gives BOFT $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters, while the original OFT with a full dense orthogonal matrix uses $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, and block-diagonal OFT with block number $r$ uses $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ parameters. The critical advantage of BOFT is that it can generate a dense orthogonal matrix with any block size $b$, whereas the original OFT must use $b = d$ to achieve density, requiring the full $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameter budget. This flexibility makes BOFT a strictly more general framework that subsumes OFT as a special case while opening up a much richer space of parameter-efficient orthogonal parameterizations.

# The Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) Method

## Formulating the BOFT Forward Pass with the Product of Multiple Orthogonal Butterfly Components

The core of the Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) method lies in its reformulation of the forward pass during finetuning. Recall that in standard Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ is transformed by a single learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, modifying the forward pass from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. BOFT retains this multiplicative structure but replaces the monolithic orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ with a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices, each derived from the butterfly factorization.

Concretely, the orthogonal matrix in BOFT is constructed as $\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is an orthogonal butterfly component. The forward pass in BOFT is therefore given by $\bm{z} = \big(\bm{R}(m,b) \cdot \bm{W}^0\big)^\top \bm{x}$, subject to the constraint that each butterfly component satisfies $\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)} = \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)} \big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top = \bm{I}_d$ for all $j \in [1, m]$. This constraint ensures that the product of these components remains orthogonal, preserving the key property of OFT that pairwise angles between neurons are maintained throughout finetuning.

To understand how each butterfly component is constructed, we begin with the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$, defined for $k \geq 2$ (a power of 2) as a $k \times k$ matrix of the form $\begin{bmatrix} \text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\ \text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4) \end{bmatrix}$, where each $\bm{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}$. The butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is then defined recursively: $\bm{B}(d) = \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d) \cdot \begin{bmatrix} \bm{B}_1(d/2) & \bm{0} \\ \bm{0} & \bm{B}_2(d/2) \end{bmatrix} = \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d/2) \cdots \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$, where $\bm{B}_1(d/2)$ and $\bm{B}_2(d/2)$ are themselves butterfly matrices of half the dimension. The butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ is a block-diagonal matrix of size $d \times d$ with block size $k$, composed of butterfly factors: $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k) = \text{diag}(\bm{B}^F_1(k), \cdots, \bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k))$.

To ensure orthogonality of each butterfly component, BOFT generalizes the butterfly structure by introducing the block butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$, where each scalar entry in the vectors $\bm{d}_i$ becomes a $b \times b$ matrix. For the base component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$, orthogonality is guaranteed by parameterizing each $2b \times 2b$ block matrix as orthogonal using the Cayley transform: $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix ($\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$). The non-zero pattern of every other butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ for $k > 2$ can be viewed as a permutation of the non-zero pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$, and therefore all butterfly components can similarly be parameterized as orthogonal matrices. This construction yields an efficient parameterization of a potentially dense orthogonal matrix built from many small $2b \times 2b$ orthogonal building blocks.

The notation $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is used as shorthand for $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d, 2^{m-i+1})$, indexing the butterfly components in the product from $i = 1$ to $m$. This recursive, factorized construction is what allows BOFT to generate a dense orthogonal matrix — one that can mix information across all $d$ dimensions — while using far fewer trainable parameters than a fully dense orthogonal matrix would require.

## BOFT($m$,$b$) Notation and Its Relationship to the Block Size $b$ and Number of Components $m$

The notation BOFT($m$,$b$) provides a compact way to specify the configuration of the butterfly factorization used in finetuning. Here, $m$ denotes the number of orthogonal butterfly components in the product that constitutes the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$, and $b$ controls the block size within each butterfly component. More precisely, the paper defines BOFT with $\bm{R}(m, \frac{b}{2})$ as BOFT($m$,$b$), where $b \geq 2$. This means that the parameter $b$ in the BOFT notation corresponds to twice the block size used in the underlying block butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$.

The relationship between $m$ and $b$ is central to understanding the expressivity and parameter efficiency trade-offs in BOFT. The block size $b$ determines the granularity of each orthogonal building block: larger values of $b$ mean that each $2b \times 2b$ block in the butterfly component can represent a richer set of orthogonal transformations, but at the cost of more parameters per block. The number of components $m$ determines how many layers of sparse orthogonal transformations are composed: more components allow information to propagate more broadly across dimensions, increasing the density and expressivity of the final orthogonal matrix.

When $m$ is set to its maximum value of $\log \frac{2d}{b}$ (assuming $d$ is a power of 2), BOFT uses the full block butterfly matrix $\bm{B}^{b/2}(d)$ as $\bm{R}$, which yields a dense orthogonal matrix. This is the setting in which the product of butterfly components achieves full dense connectivity — every dimension in the input can influence every dimension in the output through the chain of sparse matrix multiplications. With fewer components (smaller $m$), the resulting matrix has a more restricted sparsity pattern, and the transformation is less expressive but more parameter-efficient.

The interplay between $m$ and $b$ also governs where BOFT falls on the spectrum between expressivity and regularity. The butterfly structure provides a smoother interpolation between the full orthogonal group (maximum expressivity, achieved when $m$ and $b$ are large) and the identity matrix (maximum regularity, achieved at initialization). This is in contrast to the block-diagonal structure of the original OFT, which provides a coarser trade-off: one either uses a single large block (full expressivity but many parameters) or many small blocks (fewer parameters but restricted expressivity). The butterfly structure's ability to achieve dense connectivity with multiple sparse factors is what makes this smoother interpolation possible.

## How BOFT Subsumes OFT as a Special Case

One of the elegant properties of BOFT is that it naturally subsumes the original OFT as a special case, providing a unified orthogonal finetuning framework. This subsumption occurs through specific choices of the parameters $m$ and $b$.

When $m = 1$, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ reduces to a single butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$, which is a block-diagonal matrix composed of $2b \times 2b$ orthogonal blocks. This is exactly the block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, where the orthogonal transformation is applied independently within each block and dimensions in different blocks do not interact. Thus, BOFT($1$,$b$) is equivalent to block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$.

Furthermore, when $m = 1$ and $b = d$, the single butterfly component becomes a full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix without any block-diagonal restriction. This corresponds to the original OFT with an unconstrained full orthogonal matrix, where every dimension can interact with every other dimension. Thus, BOFT($1$,$d$) recovers the original OFT in its most expressive form.

At the other extreme, when $m = \log \frac{2d}{b}$ and $b$ is kept small (e.g., $b = 2$), the product of many sparse butterfly components yields a dense orthogonal matrix — something that block-diagonal OFT can only achieve by setting $b = d$, which requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. BOFT achieves this same dense connectivity with far fewer parameters by distributing the expressivity across multiple sparse factors rather than concentrating it in a single dense matrix.

This subsumption relationship highlights that BOFT is not merely an alternative to OFT but a generalization. The block-diagonal structure of OFT represents one point in the BOFT parameter space (specifically, the point where $m = 1$), while the butterfly structure opens up a richer family of orthogonal parameterizations that interpolate between the block-diagonal extreme and the fully dense extreme. The ability to control this interpolation through $m$ and $b$ is what gives BOFT its flexibility in balancing parameter efficiency against expressivity.

## Parameter Efficiency Analysis: Achieving $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ Parameters Compared to OFT's $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$

The parameter efficiency of BOFT is one of its most compelling advantages over the original OFT. To understand this, we must carefully account for the number of effective trainable parameters in each method.

The original OFT, when using a full dense orthogonal matrix of size $d \times d$, requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. This is because a $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix has $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ degrees of freedom (the dimension of the orthogonal group $O(d)$), which grows quadratically with $d$. For large foundation models where $d$ can be in the hundreds or thousands, this quadratic scaling makes full OFT impractical.

The block-diagonal OFT addresses this by partitioning the $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix into $r$ blocks of size $b \times b$ (where $br = d$), reducing the parameter count to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ — linear in $d$ for fixed $b$. However, as discussed earlier, this comes at the cost of expressivity: the block-diagonal structure cannot produce a dense orthogonal matrix unless $b = d$, which brings us back to $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters.

BOFT breaks this impudence by composing a dense orthogonal matrix from a product of $m$ sparse orthogonal butterfly components. The parameter count for BOFT($m$,$b$) is $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters for finetuning a linear layer of size $d \times n$. This formula arises because each butterfly component contains $d/b$ blocks of size $2b \times 2b$, and each such block, being orthogonal, has $b(2b-1)$ trainable parameters (from the skew-symmetric matrix in the Cayley parameterization, which has $\frac{2b(2b-1)}{2} = b(2b-1)$ free parameters). However, the effective number of trainable parameters per block is $\frac{1}{2}(b-1) \cdot 2b \cdot \frac{d}{2b} = \frac{(b-1)d}{2}$ per component, and with $m$ components, this gives $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$.

When we use the full butterfly matrix — that is, $m = \log d$ and $b = 2$ — the parameter count becomes $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$, which is a dramatic reduction from the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters required by the original OFT with a dense orthogonal matrix. This logarithmic factor is the key insight: by trading computation time (performing $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ matrix multiplications instead of one) for space (fewer trainable parameters), BOFT achieves parameter efficiency while maintaining the ability to produce a dense orthogonal transformation.

To put this in concrete terms, consider a layer with $d = 512$. The original OFT with a full orthogonal matrix would require on the order of $512^2 / 2 \approx 131{,}000$ parameters. BOFT with $m = \log_2 512 = 9$ and $b = 2$ would require approximately $\frac{1}{2}(2-1)(512)(9) = 2{,}304$ parameters — roughly a 57x reduction. This substantial parameter savings is what makes BOFT practical for finetuning large foundation models, where multiple layers each require their own orthogonal transformation.

The paper also provides empirical evidence of this parameter efficiency through a simulation approximating a random $512 \times 512$ dense orthogonal matrix. The results show that BOFT with smaller $b$ and larger $m$ is generally more parameter-efficient. For example, BOFT($9$,$2$) achieves better expressiveness than BOFT($1$,$16$) (block-diagonal OFT with block size 16) while using far fewer parameters. Similarly, BOFT($6$,$4$) uses much fewer parameters than BOFT($2$,$16$) while achieving a similar approximation error. These findings confirm that the butterfly structure represents a more structured and parameter-efficient subset of the orthogonal group compared to the block-diagonal structure.

## Identity Initialization for Butterfly Components to Start with the Exact Pretrained Model

A critical design principle in parameter-efficient finetuning is that the finetuning process should begin from the exact pretrained model, ensuring that the initial state of the finetuned model is identical to the pretrained model. This principle prevents catastrophic forgetting of pretraining knowledge and provides a stable starting point for optimization. In LoRA, this is achieved through zero initialization of the low-rank weight matrices, so that the initial weight update is zero and the model behaves exactly as the pretrained model at the start of finetuning.

BOFT achieves the same principle through identity initialization of all butterfly components. Since the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is a product of butterfly components, initializing each component as an identity matrix ensures that the product $\bm{R}(m,b) = \bm{I}_d$ at initialization. Consequently, the forward pass at initialization becomes $\bm{z} = (\bm{I}_d \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, which is exactly the forward pass of the pretrained model.

The identity initialization is implemented through the Cayley parameterization. Recall that each orthogonal block in the butterfly components is parameterized as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix. When $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$ (the zero matrix), the Cayley transform yields $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{0})(\bm{I} - \bm{0})^{-1} = \bm{I} \cdot \bm{I}^{-1} = \bm{I}$. Therefore, by initializing all skew-symmetric matrices $\bm{Q}$ in every butterfly component to zeros, every block becomes an identity matrix, every butterfly component becomes an identity matrix, and the overall orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ becomes the identity.

This initialization strategy is important for several reasons. First, it ensures that the finetuning process starts from a point where the model's behavior is unchanged from the pretrained model, which is essential for stable optimization. Second, because the orthogonal matrix starts as the identity and is constrained to remain orthogonal throughout training, the finetuned model can never deviate too far from the pretrained model in terms of the geometric structure of its weight matrices — the pairwise angles between neurons are preserved by the orthogonal transformation, and the transformation itself starts from the identity. Third, the identity initialization provides a natural regularization effect: the model must "learn" to move away from the identity, and the structure of the butterfly factorization constrains how it can do so, which helps prevent overfitting.

## Multiplicative Dropout: Adapting Dropout for Multiplicative Weight Updates by Replacing Blocks with Identity Matrices

Dropout is a widely used regularization technique in deep learning that prevents overfitting by randomly setting a fraction of activations or weights to zero during training. LoRA, for instance, employs a conventional dropout layer on the low-rank weight update to prevent overfitting. However, conventional dropout is designed for additive weight updates and does not naturally extend to the multiplicative weight updates used in BOFT.

The challenge arises because BOFT updates the pretrained weight matrix multiplicatively: $\bm{W} = \bm{R}(m,b) \cdot \bm{W}^0$. Simply zeroing out elements of $\bm{R}(m,b)$ would violate the orthogonality constraint, potentially destabilizing training and destroying the geometric properties that BOFT is designed to preserve. A different dropout mechanism is needed that is compatible with the multiplicative structure and the orthogonality of the butterfly components.

BOFT addresses this by introducing a multiplicative dropout mechanism that leverages the structure of the butterfly factorization. The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is composed of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components, and each butterfly component is a block-diagonal matrix that can be permuted into $2b \times 2b$ block-diagonal orthogonal matrices. The multiplicative dropout operates by randomly selecting $p_1$ percent of the butterfly components and, within each selected component, randomly selecting $p_2$ percent of the diagonal blocks. These selected blocks are then replaced with identity matrices.

Replacing a block with an identity matrix is equivalent to "dropping out" the orthogonal transformation in that block — the transformation becomes the identity, which means no transformation is applied. This is the multiplicative analog of zeroing out a weight in additive methods: in additive methods, zeroing a weight means no contribution is added; in multiplicative dropout, replacing with identity means no transformation is applied. Because the identity matrix is itself orthogonal, this replacement preserves the orthogonality of the overall butterfly component and, consequently, of the full orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$.

The two-level dropout — at both the component level ($p_1$) and the block level ($p_2$) — provides fine-grained control over the regularization strength. Dropping out entire butterfly components ($p_1$) reduces the depth of the information transmission path, effectively simplifying the transformation during training. Dropping out individual blocks within components ($p_2$) further reduces the complexity of each layer's transformation. Together, these mechanisms encourage the model to learn robust transformations that do not rely on any single butterfly component or block, improving generalization.

This multiplicative dropout is a natural fit for the BOFT framework because it respects the structural properties of the butterfly factorization. It does not break orthogonality, it operates on the same building blocks that define the parameterization, and it provides the same regularization benefits as conventional dropout does for additive methods. By randomly "bypassing" parts of the orthogonal transformation during training, the model is encouraged to distribute its learned transformations across multiple components and blocks, rather than overfitting to a specific configuration of the butterfly structure.

# Theoretical Insights and Properties of BOFT

## Simulating the Expressivity of BOFT vs. OFT in Approximating Random Dense Orthogonal Matrices

A central question in parameter-efficient finetuning is how well a given parameterization can approximate the transformations that a full, unconstrained model could express. In the context of orthogonal finetuning, this question translates to: how well can a structured orthogonal matrix --- whether constructed via block-diagonal decomposition (as in standard OFT) or via butterfly factorization (as in BOFT) --- approximate an arbitrary dense orthogonal matrix drawn uniformly at random from the orthogonal group? This question is practically important because the answer tells us how much expressivity we sacrifice in exchange for parameter efficiency, and it provides a principled basis for choosing between different structured parameterizations.

To investigate this, the authors of BOFT conduct a controlled simulation study. They generate random dense orthogonal matrices of size $512 \times 512$ using a standard method for uniformly sampling from the orthogonal group. For each such random target matrix, they then attempt to approximate it using both BOFT and OFT with various configurations, measuring the approximation error as a function of the number of effective trainable parameters used. The results are averaged over 10 random seeds to ensure statistical reliability.

The key finding from this simulation is that BOFT generally yields better parameter efficiency than OFT. Concretely, the approximation error of BOFT decreases more rapidly as a function of the number of parameters than does the approximation error of OFT. The authors provide specific examples to illustrate this point. For instance, the expressiveness of BOFT$(9, 2)$ --- which uses $m = 9$ butterfly components each with block size $b = 2$ --- is better than that of BOFT$(1, 16)$, which corresponds to the original block-diagonal OFT with block size $b = 16$, despite BOFT$(9, 2)$ using significantly fewer parameters. This is a striking result: by decomposing the orthogonal matrix into a product of many sparse factors rather than a single block-diagonal matrix, BOFT achieves greater expressivity per parameter.

The simulation also reveals an important structural insight about the role of the block size $b$ and the number of factors $m$. BOFT with smaller block size $b$ and correspondingly larger number of factors $m$ is generally more parameter-efficient. For example, BOFT$(6, 4)$ uses much fewer parameters but yields a similar approximation error to BOFT$(2, 16)$. This suggests that the depth of the factorization --- that is, the number of sparse matrices multiplied together --- plays a more important role in expressivity than the width of each individual factor, which is controlled by the block size. Intuitively, this makes sense: each additional factor in the product introduces new degrees of freedom that allow the composed matrix to capture more complex transformations, while increasing the block size merely enlarges the local transformation within each block without enabling information exchange between blocks.

It is worth emphasizing what the leftmost point on each curve in the simulation represents. For each fixed block size $b$, the leftmost point corresponds to BOFT$(1, b)$, which is precisely the original block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. As one moves rightward along the curve (increasing $m$ while keeping $b$ fixed), one is adding more butterfly factors and thus increasing the number of parameters, but also increasing expressivity. The fact that curves for smaller block sizes start with higher approximation error (since BOFT$(1, b)$ with small $b$ is very restrictive) but then drop steeply as $m$ increases shows that the butterfly structure provides a smooth and efficient pathway from low expressivity to high expressivity, something that the block-diagonal structure cannot achieve without jumping to the extreme of a full dense orthogonal matrix (i.e., $b = d$, which requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters).

This simulation thus provides the first concrete evidence that the butterfly factorization in BOFT represents a more structured subset of the orthogonal group compared to the block-diagonal structure, and that this structured subset is more expressive per parameter. The intuition is that the butterfly structure allows information to flow between all dimensions through the successive sparse matrix multiplications, whereas the block-diagonal structure eternally confines information to within-block interactions, no matter how the block size is chosen.

## Theorem 1: Proof that BOFT is More Expressive than OFT with the Same Block Size

The simulation results described above provide empirical evidence that BOFT is more parameter-efficient than OFT. However, the authors also provide a formal theoretical result that establishes a strict expressivity advantage of BOFT over OFT under certain conditions. This result is stated as Theorem 1 in the paper.

The theorem asserts that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size. To understand what this means precisely, we need to clarify the notion of expressivity being used here. A parameterization is said to be more expressive than another if the set of matrices that the first parameterization can represent is a strict superset of the set of matrices that the second can represent (or, in a weaker sense, if the first can approximate a broader class of target matrices). In this context, the relevant comparison is between the set of orthogonal matrices that BOFT$(m, b)$ can represent and the set that OFT with block size $b$ (i.e., BOFT$(1, b)$) can represent, for the same block size $b$.

The key observation underlying the theorem is that the block-diagonal structure used in OFT is a special case of the butterfly structure. Specifically, when $m = 1$, BOFT$(1, b)$ reduces exactly to the block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. As $m$ increases, BOFT$(m, b)$ introduces additional butterfly factors that allow interactions between blocks, thereby expanding the set of representable orthogonal matrices beyond what the block-diagonal structure alone can achieve. Therefore, for any fixed block size $b$, increasing $m$ from 1 to any larger value strictly enlarges the hypothesis space, making BOFT strictly more expressive than OFT.

The theorem goes further to address the question of whether the butterfly matrix can approximate all orthogonal matrices of size $d$. The authors show that the butterfly matrix alone cannot represent the entire orthogonal group, but it can approximate all orthogonal matrices of size $d$ if one multiplies together sufficiently many butterfly matrices and their transposes. Specifically, the theorem states that one can approximate all orthogonal matrices of size $d$ by computing the product $\bm{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{d-1,2}(d)\cdots\bm{B}_{1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{1,2}(d)$, where each $\bm{B}_{i,j}(d)$ is a butterfly matrix. This product involves $d - 1$ pairs of butterfly matrices and their transposes, and it can represent the entire orthogonal group.

The significance of this result is twofold. First, it provides a formal guarantee that the butterfly parameterization is not a dead-end in terms of expressivity: by composing enough butterfly matrices, one can in principle approximate any orthogonal transformation. Second, it shows that the butterfly structure is a fundamentally richer parameterization than the block-diagonal structure, since the latter can never approximate certain dense orthogonal matrices regardless of how many copies are composed, due to its fixed sparsity pattern.

It is important to note, as the authors do, that better expressiveness does not always lead to better performance in finetuning. Full finetuning, despite its universal expressiveness (it can represent any weight matrix), often yields unsatisfactory performance due to overfitting, especially when the downstream task has limited data. The key to successful finetuning is finding the right trade-off between expressivity and regularity --- that is, between the ability to adapt to the downstream task and the ability to preserve the knowledge from pretraining. BOFT's advantage is not simply that it is more expressive than OFT, but that it provides a smoother and more controllable interpolation between the two extremes of full expressivity (the full orthogonal group) and full regularity (the identity matrix, which corresponds to no finetuning at all).

## Kaleidoscope Hierarchy: Generalizing BOFT to Represent the Entire Orthogonal Group

Theorem 1 suggests a natural generalization of BOFT that can represent the entire orthogonal group. This generalization is based on composing multiple BOFT matrices and their transposes in an alternating fashion, a construction that the authors connect to the concept of a kaleidoscope hierarchy.

The generalized orthogonal matrix is defined as $\bm{R}^G(m_1, b_1, m_2, b_2, l) = \bm{R}_{l,1}(m_1, b_1)\bm{R}_{l,2}^T(m_2, b_2)\cdots\bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1, b_1)\bm{R}_{1,2}^T(m_2, b_2)$, where $\bm{R}_{i,j}^T(m, b)$ denotes the transpose of the orthogonal matrix used in BOFT with parameters $m$ and $b$, and $l$ is the number of pairs in the composition. Each $\bm{R}_{i,j}(m, b)$ is an independent BOFT matrix with its own set of trainable parameters.

The critical insight is that when $m_1 = m_2 = \log d$, $b_1 = b_2 = 2$, and $l = d - 1$, the generalized matrix $\bm{R}^G(m_1, b_1, m_2, b_2, l)$ can represent the entire orthogonal group of size $d$. This means that, in principle, the kaleidoscope hierarchy provides a parameterization that is as expressive as a full, unconstrained orthogonal matrix, while still being built from structured, sparse components.

The name "kaleidoscope hierarchy" comes from prior work by Dao et al., who studied the use of such alternating compositions of butterfly matrices and their transposes to parameterize broad classes of linear transforms. The kaleidoscope pattern --- in which the structure of each factor mirrors and complements the structure of the next --- resembles the reflective symmetry of a kaleidoscope, hence the name. In the context of BOFT, the kaleidoscope hierarchy provides a principled way to scale up expressivity: each additional pair of BOFT matrices in the composition expands the set of representable transformations, and with enough pairs, the entire orthogonal group is covered.

However, the authors are careful to note that achieving full expressivity is not the goal of parameter-efficient finetuning. The kaleidoscope hierarchy is primarily of theoretical interest, as it demonstrates that the butterfly parameterization is not fundamentally limited in expressivity. In practice, one would typically use a single BOFT matrix with a moderate number of factors (far fewer than the $d - 1$ pairs needed for full expressivity), since the goal is to find a sweet spot between expressivity and regularity that generalizes well to the downstream task. The kaleidoscope hierarchy simply assures us that, if needed, we can always increase expressivity by adding more factors, without changing the fundamental structure of the parameterization.

This is in stark contrast to the block-diagonal OFT, where the only way to increase expressivity is to increase the block size $b$, which rapidly increases the parameter count (from $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ as $b$ goes from a small constant to $d$) and which cannot achieve dense connectivity unless $b = d$. The kaleidoscope hierarchy, by enabling expressivity to be scaled up through the addition of structured factors rather than through the enlargement of unstructured blocks, provides a more graceful and parameter-efficient pathway to full expressivity.

## Spectral Properties: Preservation of the Spectral Norm of Pretrained Weights for Training Stability

One of the most important theoretical advantages of orthogonal finetuning, shared by both OFT and BOFT, is its favorable spectral properties. The spectral norm (also known as the largest singular value or the operator norm) of a weight matrix is a fundamental quantity that controls the Lipschitz constant of the corresponding linear layer. A smaller spectral norm implies that the layer is more contractive, which in turn leads to more stable training and better generalization, as has been established in prior work on spectral normalization and related techniques.

To understand why orthogonal finetuning preserves the spectral norm, consider the singular value decomposition (SVD) of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$: $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, where $\bm{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and $\bm{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ are orthogonal matrices, and $\bm{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ is a diagonal matrix of singular values. The spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$ is simply the largest entry of $\bm{\Sigma}$, which we denote as $\sigma_{\max}(\bm{W}^0)$.

In both OFT and BOFT, the finetuned weight matrix is obtained by left-multiplying $\bm{W}^0$ by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, yielding $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. Since $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, the product $\bm{R}\bm{U}$ is also orthogonal (the product of two orthogonal matrices is orthogonal). Therefore, the SVD of $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ is $(\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, which has exactly the same singular value matrix $\bm{\Sigma}$ as $\bm{W}^0$. In particular, the largest singular value --- the spectral norm --- is unchanged: $\sigma_{\max}(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0) = \sigma_{\max}(\bm{W}^0)$.

This is a remarkable property: no matter what orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ is applied during finetuning, the spectral norm of the weight matrix remains exactly the same as in the pretrained model. This stands in sharp contrast to other finetuning methods such as LoRA, where the weight update is additive ($\bm{W}^0 + \Delta\bm{W}$) and can potentially increase the spectral norm, leading to instability. In LoRA, the spectral norm of the finetuned weights can grow unboundedly (depending on the magnitude of the low-rank update), which may require additional regularization such as spectral normalization to control.

The preservation of the spectral norm has been shown in prior work to greatly benefit both training stability and generalization. Intuitively, by keeping the spectral norm fixed, orthogonal finetuning ensures that the finetuned model does not become more sensitive to small perturbations in the input than the pretrained model. This prevents the explosion of gradients and activations that can occur when the spectral norm grows, and it also limits the capacity of the model to overfit to the downstream task, since the model's Lipschitz constant --- which bounds how much the output can change for a given change in the input --- remains the same as in the pretrained model.

It is worth noting that BOFT inherits this spectral preservation property from OFT, since the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m, b)$ in BOFT is composed of a product of orthogonal butterfly components, and the product of orthogonal matrices is itself orthogonal. Therefore, regardless of the choice of $m$ and $b$, the spectral norm of the finetuned weights is always exactly preserved. This means that even as we vary the number of butterfly factors and the block size to trade off expressivity and parameter efficiency, we never sacrifice the spectral stability of the finetuning process.

## Orthogonal Finetuning as Learning a Bilinear Similarity Matrix with Strong Regularity

An insightful way to understand orthogonal finetuning --- and BOFT in particular --- is to view it through the lens of bilinear similarity learning. This perspective not only provides a deeper conceptual understanding of what orthogonal finetuning is doing, but also connects it to a rich body of literature in machine learning and mathematics.

Consider the forward pass of a linear layer with orthogonal finetuning. The output for the $i$-th neuron is $z_i = \bm{w}^0_i{}^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{x} = \bm{w}^0_i{}^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{w}^0_i$ is the $i$-th column of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ (i.e., the $i$-th neuron), $\bm{R}$ is the learnable orthogonal matrix, and $\bm{x}$ is the input. Equivalently, we can write this as $z_i = \bm{w}^0_i{}^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{x}$, which can be interpreted as computing a bilinear form between the neuron $\bm{w}^0_i$ and the input $\bm{x}$, mediated by the matrix $\bm{R}^\top$. More precisely, the expression $\bm{w}^0_i{}^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{x}$ is a bilinear similarity: it measures the "similarity" between $\bm{w}^0_i$ and $\bm{x}$ under the transformation $\bm{R}^\top$.

This formulation reveals that orthogonal finetuning is fundamentally about learning a bilinear similarity matrix $\bm{R}$ that modulates the interaction between pretrained neurons and inputs. The key constraint that distinguishes orthogonal finetuning from general bilinear similarity learning is that $\bm{R}$ must be orthogonal, which imposes a strong form of regularity on the learned similarity. Orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ means that $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$, which implies that the transformation $\bm{R}$ preserves distances and angles in the input space. In other words, $\bm{R}$ is an isometry: it does not stretch or compress any direction, and it does not change the angle between any pair of vectors. This is a much stronger constraint than, say, the low-rank constraint in LoRA, which merely limits the rank of the weight update but does not impose any geometric preservation guarantee.

The connection to distance metric learning is particularly illuminating. In distance metric learning, one learns a matrix $\bm{M}$ such that the distance $d(\bm{x}, \bm{y}) = (\bm{x} - \bm{y})^\top \bm{M} (\bm{x} - \bm{y})$ reflects the semantic similarity between inputs $\bm{x}$ and $\bm{y}$. Orthogonal finetuning can be seen as a special case of distance metric learning where the learned matrix $\bm{M} = \bm{R}^\top \bm{R}$ is constrained to be the identity (since $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal), meaning that the metric itself is preserved, but the representation of the neurons is rotated. This is a subtle but important distinction: instead of learning a new metric (which could distort the geometry of the input space), orthogonal finetuning learns a new "viewpoint" (a rotation) from which the pretrained neurons are evaluated. The regularity imposed by orthogonality ensures that this new viewpoint does not introduce any distortion.

The bilinear form perspective also connects to classical results in linear algebra. A bilinear form $\bm{u}^\top \bm{M} \bm{v}$ is a generalization of the inner product $\bm{u}^\top \bm{v}$ to allow for a "weighting" matrix $\bm{M}$. When $\bm{M}$ is orthogonal, the bilinear form reduces to evaluating the inner product after applying an isometric transformation to one of the arguments. This means that orthogonal finetuning is learning the optimal isometric transformation that aligns the pretrained neurons with the downstream task, without introducing any anisotropic scaling or shearing.

In the context of BOFT, the bilinear similarity matrix $\bm{R}$ is further structured through butterfly factorization. This adds another layer of regularity on top of orthogonality: not only is $\bm{R}$ constrained to be orthogonal, but it is also constrained to be decomposable into a product of sparse, structured factors. This hierarchical regularity --- orthogonality at the global level and sparsity at the factor level --- is what gives BOFT its favorable generalization properties. The structured prior on $\bm{R}$ biases the learned similarity toward transformations that have a known, interpretable structure (such as Fourier-like or Hadamard-like transforms), rather than arbitrary rotations.

## Inductive Bias and Generalization: The Implicit Benefits of Structured Classic Linear Transforms

The concept of inductive bias --- the set of assumptions that a learning algorithm makes about the structure of the problem --- is central to understanding why some finetuning methods generalize better than others. In this section, we explore the inductive bias introduced by the butterfly structure in BOFT and argue that this bias is beneficial for generalization.

The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m, b)$ in BOFT does not represent the entire orthogonal group; rather, it represents a structured subset determined by the butterfly factorization. This structured subset is much smaller than the full orthogonal group (which has $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ degrees of freedom), and it is also structured differently from the block-diagonal subset used in OFT. The choice of this subset constitutes an inductive bias: by restricting the hypothesis class to matrices that can be decomposed into butterfly factors, BOFT implicitly assumes that the optimal orthogonal transformation for the downstream task has a butterfly-like structure.

The question is whether this inductive bias is helpful or harmful. The authors argue that it is helpful, for several reasons. First, the butterfly structure has a shared structured pattern with many classic linear transforms that are known to be useful in signal processing and mathematics. Specifically, the discrete Fourier transform (DFT), the discrete cosine transform (DCT), the discrete sine transform (DST), and the Hadamard transform can all be perfectly represented (up to permutation) by butterfly matrices. These transforms have been extensively studied and are known to capture fundamental patterns in data --- periodicity (DFT), energy compaction (DCT), symmetry (DST), and orthogonal spreading (Hadamard). By parameterizing the orthogonal transformation with butterfly factors, BOFT implicitly biases the finetuning process toward transformations that are related to these classic, well-understood transforms.

This is in contrast to the block-diagonal structure in OFT, which cannot represent any of these classic transforms (unless the block size is equal to the full dimension $d$, in which case it reduces to an unconstrained orthogonal matrix). The block-diagonal structure assumes that the dimensions of the neuron can be partitioned into independent groups, which is an arbitrary and often unjustified assumption. The butterfly structure, on the other hand, assumes that the transformation can be decomposed into a hierarchy of local, structured interactions, which is a much more natural and well-motivated assumption.

Second, the sparse matrix factorization in BOFT may also bring some implicit inductive bias, beyond the explicit structure of the butterfly factors. Prior work has shown that sparse matrix factorization can lead to implicit regularization during optimization, biasing the learned solutions toward simpler, more generalizable patterns. This is related to the broader phenomenon of implicit regularization in deep learning, where the optimization algorithm (typically stochastic gradient descent) implicitly favors solutions with certain properties (such as low norm or low rank) even when these properties are not explicitly enforced. In the case of BOFT, the sparse factorization may implicitly favor orthogonal transformations that have a simpler, more structured decomposition, which could contribute to better generalization.

Third, the butterfly structure provides a smoother interpolation between expressivity and regularity than the block-diagonal structure. In OFT, the only knob for controlling the expressivity-regularity trade-off is the block size $b$: when $b$ is small, the transformation is highly regular but not very expressive; when $b$ is large, the transformation is more expressive but less regular. There is no smooth interpolation between these extremes --- one must choose a single block size, and the resulting transformation is either block-diagonal (for $b < d$) or fully dense (for $b = d$). In BOFT, there are two knobs: the block size $b$ and the number of factors $m$. By varying both, one can smoothly interpolate between a highly regular transformation (small $b$, small $m$) and a highly expressive transformation (small $b$, large $m$), without ever needing to resort to an unstructured dense matrix. This smooth interpolation allows for a more fine-grained control of the bias-variance trade-off, which is crucial for achieving good generalization on downstream tasks with varying amounts of data.

Finally, the authors note that the structured inductive bias of BOFT is particularly well-suited for finetuning large foundation models, which are typically pretrained on diverse, large-scale datasets and therefore have already learned rich, structured representations. The butterfly structure, by biasing the finetuning transformation toward classic linear transforms, respects and builds upon the structure already present in the pretrained weights, rather than overwriting it with an arbitrary rotation. This is consistent with the general philosophy of parameter-efficient finetuning: the goal is not to learn a completely new model from scratch, but to make a small, structured adjustment to the pretrained model that adapts it to the downstream task while preserving as much of the pretraining knowledge as possible.

# Empirical Evaluation and Applications

The theoretical foundations of Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) presented in earlier chapters establish a principled framework for parameter-efficient finetuning, but the ultimate test of any finetuning method lies in its empirical performance across diverse tasks and model architectures. This chapter examines the extensive empirical study conducted to validate BOFT as a generic model finetuning method. The evaluation spans multiple modalities—computer vision, natural language processing, and text-to-image generation—and encompasses a variety of downstream tasks that stress-test the method's parameter efficiency, generalization ability, and training stability. By applying BOFT to large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models, the empirical study demonstrates that BOFT consistently outperforms current state-of-the-art methods by a considerable margin, validating its superiority as a generic model finetuning approach.

The empirical evaluation is motivated by several key questions that arise naturally from the theoretical development of BOFT. First, does the butterfly factorization, which trades computational depth for parameter savings, actually translate to better performance in practice compared to the block-diagonal structure of standard OFT? Second, can BOFT, which uses a multiplicative orthogonal update, compete with or surpass the widely adopted additive low-rank update of LoRA across diverse tasks? Third, does the structured inductive bias introduced by the butterfly topology genuinely benefit generalization, as hypothesized from its connection to classic fast linear transforms? The experiments are designed to answer these questions comprehensively.

## Applying BOFT to Large Vision Transformers (ViTs) for Computer Vision Tasks

Large vision transformers have become a dominant architecture in computer vision, demonstrating remarkable performance on tasks ranging from image classification to semantic segmentation. However, the scale of these models—often comprising hundreds of millions or even billions of parameters—makes full finetuning prohibitively expensive in terms of both computation and storage. Parameter-efficient finetuning methods are therefore essential for adapting pretrained vision transformers to downstream vision tasks, and this setting provides a rigorous testbed for comparing BOFT against alternative approaches.

The application of BOFT to vision transformers follows the general finetuning paradigm established in the method's formulation. For each linear layer in the vision transformer with a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, BOFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m, b) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ that is composed as a product of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components, each parameterized with block size $b$. The forward pass is modified from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}(m, b) \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ remain frozen and only the parameters of $\bm{R}(m, b)$ are updated during training. The total number of effective trainable parameters introduced per layer is $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$, which for the full butterfly case ($m = \log d$, $b = 2$) yields $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters—a dramatic reduction from the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters required by a full dense orthogonal matrix.

A critical consideration when applying BOFT to vision transformers is the choice of the hyperparameters $m$ and $b$, which control the trade-off between expressivity and parameter efficiency. The expressivity analysis discussed later in this chapter demonstrates that increasing $m$ (the number of butterfly components) while decreasing $b$ (the block size) generally yields better parameter efficiency. This means that for vision tasks, one can achieve a dense orthogonal transformation with far fewer parameters than would be required by standard OFT with a full orthogonal matrix, while maintaining or even improving the quality of the transformation.

The identity initialization strategy employed by BOFT is particularly well-suited for vision transformer finetuning. By initializing all butterfly components as identity matrices—which corresponds to initializing the skew-symmetric matrices in the Cayley parameterization to zeros—the finetuning process begins with the exact pretrained model. This ensures that the initial forward pass is identical to that of the pretrained model, preventing any catastrophic forgetting of pretrained knowledge at the start of training. As training progresses, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m, b)$ gradually deviates from the identity, allowing the model to adapt to the downstream task while preserving the pairwise angles between neurons that encode the semantic knowledge from pretraining.

The multiplicative Dropout mechanism proposed for BOFT also plays an important role in vision tasks. Unlike conventional Dropout, which randomly zeroes out individual weights, the multiplicative Dropout for BOFT operates at the level of butterfly components and their diagonal blocks. Specifically, it randomly selects $p_1$ percent of the butterfly components and $p_2$ percent of the diagonal blocks within each selected component, replacing them with identity matrices. This form of regularization is tailored to the structure of BOFT and helps prevent overfitting, which is especially important when the downstream vision dataset is small relative to the model capacity.

## Adapting Large Language Models (LLMs) for Natural Language Processing Tasks

Large language models such as GPT-3, with its approximately 175 billion parameters, represent some of the most powerful yet most resource-intensive models in modern machine learning. The prohibitive cost of training these models from scratch necessitates efficient adaptation strategies for downstream NLP tasks. The application of BOFT to large language models extends the orthogonal finetuning paradigm to the language domain, demonstrating that the principled preservation of neuron relationships through orthogonal transformations is beneficial beyond the vision and generative modeling contexts where it was originally applied.

The finetuning of LLMs with BOFT follows the same multiplicative weight update framework as in the vision case. For each linear layer in the transformer-based language model, the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ is frozen, and an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m, b)$ is learned to transform the neurons. The forward pass becomes $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}(m, b) \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where the orthogonal matrix is parameterized as a product of butterfly components. This multiplicative approach contrasts with LoRA, which adapts the weight matrix additively as $\bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$ where $\bm{A}$ and $\bm{B}$ are low-rank matrices.

The distinction between multiplicative and additive updates has important implications for language model finetuning. The orthogonal multiplicative update preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix, a property that has been shown to benefit training stability and generalization. Specifically, if the pretrained weight matrix has a singular value decomposition $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, then the finetuned weight matrix becomes $\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, where the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ does not alter the singular values in $\bm{\Sigma}$. This preservation of the spectral structure is particularly valuable for language models, where the pretrained spectral properties encode important information about the frequency and scale of different features in the language representation.

The parameter efficiency of BOFT is especially advantageous for LLMs, where even a small percentage reduction in trainable parameters translates to enormous absolute savings. With the full butterfly parameterization ($m = \log d$, $b = 2$), BOFT requires only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters per layer, compared to $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for a full orthogonal matrix or $\mathcal{O}(rd)$ for LoRA with rank $r$. For the very large hidden dimensions common in modern LLMs (e.g., $d = 12288$ for GPT-3's largest layers), this logarithmic factor represents a reduction from approximately 150 million parameters to approximately 160 thousand parameters per layer for a single orthogonal transformation.

The connection between BOFT and bilinear similarity learning, as discussed in the theoretical analysis, provides additional insight into why the method is effective for language tasks. BOFT can be written as learning a bilinear similarity $\bm{w}^0_i^\top \bm{R} \bm{x}$, where $\bm{w}^0_i$ is the $i$-th neuron (column vector) of the weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ and $\bm{x}$ is the input. This perspective reveals that BOFT is effectively learning a bilinear similarity matrix $\bm{R}$ with strong regularity constraints (orthogonality), which connects it to distance metric learning. In the context of language models, this means that BOFT is learning to reposition the neurons in the representation space while preserving their relative geometry, allowing the model to adapt to the statistical structure of the downstream language task without destroying the pretrained linguistic knowledge.

## Finetuning Text-to-Image Diffusion Models for Controllable Generation

Text-to-image diffusion models, such as Stable Diffusion, represent a class of generative models that have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in synthesizing high-quality images from textual descriptions. The original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) method was first developed and validated in the context of finetuning text-to-image diffusion models for controllable generation, making this application a natural starting point for evaluating the improvements offered by BOFT.

In the controllable generation setting, the goal is to adapt a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model so that it can generate images conditioned on additional control signals—such as depth maps, edge maps, or segmentation masks—while preserving the model's ability to generate high-quality, diverse images. This task is particularly challenging because it requires the finetuned model to learn new conditional dependencies without forgetting the rich generative capabilities acquired during pretraining. The preservation of pairwise angles between neurons, which is the core principle behind orthogonal finetuning, is directly relevant to this challenge because it ensures that the semantic relationships encoded in the pretrained model's neurons are maintained throughout the finetuning process.

BOFT improves upon standard OFT in this setting by enabling the use of dense orthogonal matrices with far fewer parameters. The original OFT, when using a block-diagonal structure, must choose a block size $b$ that balances parameter efficiency against expressivity. Smaller block sizes are more parameter-efficient but introduce undesirable inductive biases—specifically, the assumption that the dimensions of each neuron can be meaningfully divided into independent groups based on their indices, which makes little sense in practice. BOFT resolves this tension by using the butterfly structure to compose a dense orthogonal matrix from multiple sparse orthogonal factors, allowing every dimension of a neuron to interact with every other dimension through the chain of butterfly components, while still maintaining parameter efficiency.

The weight interpolation property of BOFT, which arises from the factorized structure of the orthogonal matrix, provides an intriguing advantage for diffusion model finetuning. Because the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m, b)$ is a product of $m$ butterfly components, each initialized as an identity matrix, the finetuning process can be viewed as progressively perturbing the identity transformation through a sequence of structured, sparse operations. This factorized structure allows for smooth interpolation between the pretrained model (corresponding to $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$) and the fully finetuned model, which can be useful for applications that require fine-grained control over the degree of adaptation.

The training stability benefits of orthogonal finetuning are particularly valuable for diffusion models, which are known to be sensitive to weight perturbations during finetuning. The spectral norm preservation property ensures that the scale of the weight matrices remains bounded throughout training, preventing the exploding or vanishing gradient problems that can arise when the spectral properties of the weights are altered. This stability allows for more aggressive learning rates and faster convergence compared to methods that do not preserve the spectral structure, such as LoRA.

## Comparative Performance Analysis of BOFT Against LoRA and Standard OFT

A central aspect of the empirical evaluation is the comparative analysis of BOFT against its two most natural baselines: LoRA, the widely adopted low-rank adaptation method, and standard OFT, the block-diagonal orthogonal finetuning method that BOFT generalizes. Understanding the relative strengths and weaknesses of these methods requires examining their structural differences, their parameter efficiency, and their empirical performance across diverse tasks.

The fundamental distinction between these methods lies in how they modify the pretrained weight matrix. LoRA employs an additive update: $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$, where $\bm{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $\bm{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ are low-rank matrices with rank $r \ll \min(d, n)$. This additive update introduces $\mathcal{O}(r(d + n))$ trainable parameters and can be viewed as perturbing the pretrained weights within a low-dimensional subspace. Standard OFT, in contrast, employs a multiplicative update: $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0$, where $\bm{R}$ is a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix with $r$ blocks of size $b \times b$ (such that $br = d$). This introduces $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ parameters when using $r = d/b$ blocks, and the multiplicative update preserves the pairwise angles between neurons. BOFT generalizes OFT by replacing the block-diagonal structure with a butterfly-factorized dense orthogonal matrix, introducing $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ parameters where $m$ is the number of butterfly components.

The relationship between BOFT and standard OFT is characterized by a clean nesting structure. BOFT($1$, $b$) reduces to block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, where only a single butterfly component is used, corresponding to a block-diagonal matrix with $2b \times 2b$ blocks. BOFT($1$, $d$) reduces to the original OFT with an unconstrained full orthogonal matrix of size $d \times d$. BOFT($\log \frac{2d}{b}$, $b$) uses the full block butterfly matrix, yielding a dense orthogonal matrix. This nesting means that BOFT subsumes OFT as a special case and provides a continuous spectrum of parameter-efficiency trade-offs between the two extremes of OFT.

The expressivity comparison between BOFT and OFT is informed by the simulation study in which both methods are used to approximate a random $512 \times 512$ dense orthogonal matrix. The results, averaged over 10 random seeds, reveal several important findings. First, BOFT generally yields better parameter efficiency than OFT: for the same number of effective trainable parameters, BOFT achieves a lower approximation error. Second, BOFT with smaller block size $b$ and larger number of components $m$ tends to be more parameter-efficient. For example, the expressiveness of BOFT($9$, $2$) is better than that of BOFT($1$, $16$) despite using far fewer parameters. Similarly, BOFT($6$, $4$) uses much fewer parameters but achieves a similar approximation error to BOFT($2$, $16$). These findings confirm that the butterfly structure provides a more efficient way to parameterize dense orthogonal matrices compared to the block-diagonal structure.

The theoretical expressivity result formalizes this advantage: BOFT is provably more expressive than OFT with the same block size. Specifically, the butterfly matrix can perfectly recover many classic fast linear transforms—including the discrete Fourier transform and the Hadamard transform—which is impossible for the block-diagonal structure. Furthermore, by composing multiple butterfly matrices and their transposes in a kaleidoscope hierarchy, $\bm{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{d-1,2}(d)\cdots\bm{B}_{1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{1,2}(d)$, one can represent the entire orthogonal group of size $d$. This means that while a single butterfly matrix represents a structured subset of the orthogonal group, the composition of multiple butterfly matrices can achieve universal expressivity.

The comparison with LoRA reveals a different set of trade-offs. While LoRA's additive low-rank update is conceptually simple and computationally efficient, it does not preserve the spectral properties of the pretrained weights. The singular values of $\bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$ can differ arbitrarily from those of $\bm{W}^0$, depending on the rank and magnitude of the update. This can lead to training instability and degraded generalization, particularly when the pretrained model has carefully tuned spectral properties. BOFT, by contrast, perfectly preserves the singular values of the pretrained weight matrix, as the orthogonal multiplicative update $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ only rotates the left singular vectors without altering the singular value matrix $\bm{\Sigma}$. This spectral preservation has been shown to greatly benefit both training stability and generalization, giving BOFT a principled advantage over LoRA in settings where the pretrained model's spectral structure is important.

Both low-rank matrices (used by LoRA) and sparse matrices (used by BOFT) are major families of structured matrices that achieve parameter efficiency, but they introduce different inductive biases. The low-rank structure assumes that the weight update lies in a low-dimensional subspace, which is effective when the task adaptation requires changes along a few principal directions. The butterfly sparse structure, on the other hand, assumes that the weight transformation can be decomposed into a sequence of local, structured operations that collectively achieve a global transformation—a assumption that is shared by many classic linear transforms and is therefore well-aligned with the structure of natural data transformations.

## Evaluating Parameter-Efficiency, Generalization Ability, and Training Stability Across Diverse Downstream Tasks

The ultimate validation of any finetuning method comes from its performance across a diverse set of downstream tasks, evaluated on multiple dimensions: parameter efficiency (how few trainable parameters are needed to achieve good performance), generalization ability (how well the finetuned model performs on unseen data), and training stability (how reliably the method converges without requiring extensive hyperparameter tuning).

Parameter efficiency is quantified by the number of effective trainable parameters introduced by each method. For BOFT($m$, $b$), this count is $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ per layer, which for the full butterfly case ($m = \log d$, $b = 2$) becomes approximately $d \log d$. This is a significant reduction from the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters required by the original OFT with a full dense orthogonal matrix and is competitive with or superior to LoRA's $\mathcal{O}(r(d+n))$ parameters when $r$ is chosen to achieve comparable performance. The empirical results demonstrate that BOFT consistently achieves strong performance with fewer parameters than both LoRA and standard OFT, confirming that the butterfly factorization provides a more efficient parameterization of the orthogonal group.

The generalization ability of BOFT is supported by several theoretical and empirical considerations. The structured inductive bias introduced by the butterfly factorization is beneficial for generalization because it shares structural patterns with many classic linear transforms, including the discrete Fourier transform, discrete sine/cosine transform, and Hadamard transform. This means that BOFT naturally biases the learned transformation toward these well-studied mathematical structures, which are known to capture important patterns in natural data. Additionally, the sparse matrix factorization in BOFT may introduce implicit regularization effects, similar to how sparsity has been shown to benefit generalization in other contexts. The empirical study confirms that BOFT achieves superior generalization compared to both LoRA and standard OFT across the diverse tasks evaluated, including vision, language, and generative modeling tasks.

Training stability is a critical practical consideration, and BOFT benefits from several properties that promote stable training. The preservation of the spectral norm of the pretrained weights ensures that the scale of the weight matrices remains bounded, preventing the instabilities that can arise from uncontrolled changes to the weight magnitudes. The identity initialization ensures that training starts from the exact pretrained model, avoiding the initial instability that can occur when the finetuning parameters are randomly initialized. The Cayley parameterization, which ensures that the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal throughout training by construction (rather than relying on soft regularization), provides a hard constraint that prevents the transformation from deviating too far from the identity in ways that would compromise the pretrained knowledge. The empirical results show that BOFT achieves consistently stable training across all evaluated tasks and model architectures, without the need for extensive hyperparameter search or learning rate scheduling that is often required for less constrained methods.

One practical consideration in the evaluation is the training runtime overhead of BOFT. Because the final orthogonal matrix in BOFT is the product of $m$ orthogonal matrices, the forward and backward passes require $m$ matrix multiplications rather than a single multiplication as in standard OFT. This introduces a slightly larger training runtime overhead compared to OFT. However, this overhead is limited to the training phase: after finetuning is complete, the BOFT-learned orthogonal matrices can be directly multiplied into the pretrained model weights, yielding a single weight matrix $\bm{R}(m, b) \cdot \bm{W}^0$ that replaces the original $\bm{W}^0$. This means that BOFT introduces no additional inference latency—the finetuned model has exactly the same architecture and computational cost as the pretrained model, which is a significant advantage over adapter-based methods that add extra parameters and computation to the model.

The empirical study also evaluates the robustness of BOFT to the choice of hyperparameters $m$ and $b$. The expressivity analysis suggests that BOFT with smaller $b$ and larger $m$ is generally more parameter-efficient, and this is confirmed in the downstream task evaluations. The flexibility of the BOFT framework allows practitioners to choose different points on the parameter-efficiency spectrum depending on the constraints of their application: BOFT($1$, $b$) provides the simplest and most parameter-efficient option (equivalent to block-diagonal OFT), while BOFT($\log \frac{2d}{b}$, $b$) provides the full butterfly matrix with dense connectivity. The empirical results demonstrate that intermediate choices, such as BOFT($m$, $b$) with $m$ between 2 and $\log \frac{2d}{b}$, often provide the best trade-off between parameter efficiency and task performance.

In summary, the extensive empirical study validates BOFT as a powerful and generic model finetuning method. By applying BOFT to large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models across diverse downstream tasks, the study demonstrates that BOFT consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods by a considerable margin. The butterfly factorization enables parameter-efficient dense orthogonal transformations that preserve the semantic knowledge from pretraining while providing sufficient expressivity for task adaptation. The structured inductive bias of the butterfly topology, grounded in its connection to classic fast linear transforms, provides a principled prior that benefits generalization. The spectral preservation property ensures training stability across tasks and architectures. Together, these properties make BOFT a compelling choice for parameter-efficient finetuning of large foundation models across modalities.

# Limitations and Future Directions

## Training Runtime Overhead

A central limitation of Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) lies in its training runtime overhead. The fundamental mechanism by which BOFT achieves parameter efficiency is through matrix factorization: rather than storing and learning a single dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, which would require $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, BOFT composes $\bm{R}$ as a product of $m$ sparse orthogonal matrices, specifically $\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, where each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is a block-diagonal matrix of sparse orthogonal factors. While this factorization dramatically reduces the number of trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ when using the full butterfly structure, it introduces a computational trade-off: the matrix multiplication must be performed repeatedly in each training iteration.

The paper explicitly acknowledges this trade between computation time and space. During the forward pass, the input vector $\bm{x}$ is transformed via $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}(m,b) \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where computing $\bm{R}(m,b)$ requires sequentially multiplying $m$ sparse matrices together. Each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ has $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries, and there are $m$ such components, so the total computational cost of forming or applying the product is $\mathcal{O}(dm)$ per layer per forward pass. When $m = \log \frac{2d}{b}$, this becomes $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$. During backpropagation, gradients must flow through each of these multiplicative factors, further increasing the computational burden relative to the original OFT, which applies a single (albeit potentially dense) orthogonal matrix.

Importantly, the paper notes that this runtime overhead is "slightly larger than OFT," suggesting that the additional cost is manageable in practice but nonetheless represents a limitation that distinguishes BOFT from methods like LoRA, which do not require such repeated matrix multiplications. The paper identifies improving BOFT's training runtime as an open problem, one that remains unresolved within the scope of the current work. This overhead is the price paid for the parameter efficiency and the ability to generate dense orthogonal transformations using sparse factors, and any future work seeking to deploy BOFT in highly resource-constrained settings would need to address this computational cost.

## Zero Inference Latency

Despite the training runtime overhead, one of the most appealing properties of BOFT is that it introduces zero inference latency. This is a direct consequence of the multiplicative nature of the weight update in orthogonal finetuning. During finetuning, the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ is frozen, and the model learns the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ such that the effective weight becomes $\bm{R}(m,b) \cdot \bm{W}^0$. After the finetuning process is complete, the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ can be directly multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ to obtain a single updated weight matrix $\bm{W}^{\text{finetuned}} = \bm{R}(m,b) \cdot \bm{W}^0$.

Once this multiplication is performed, the resulting matrix $\bm{W}^{\text{finetuned}}$ is a standard dense matrix of the same dimensions as the original pretrained weight matrix. At inference time, the forward pass reverts to the standard linear computation $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^{\text{finetuned}})^\top \bm{x}$, which is identical in computational cost to the original pretrained model. There is no need to compute the product of $m$ sparse butterfly components at inference time, because all of that structure has been collapsed into the single merged weight matrix. This is a significant practical advantage, as it means that deploying a BOFT-finetuned model incurs no additional computational overhead compared to deploying the original pretrained model.

This property is shared with other model finetuning approaches, such as LoRA, where the low-rank update can similarly be merged back into the pretrained weights after training. The paper highlights this as a distinguishing feature of model finetuning methods more broadly compared to adapter tuning and prompt tuning, the latter of which introduce additional trainable parameters or tokens that persist during inference and can introduce latency. For BOFT specifically, the zero inference latency property ensures that the parameter efficiency gains during training do not come at the cost of a slower model at deployment time, making it practical for real-world applications where inference speed is critical.

## Open Questions Regarding the Optimality of Butterfly Networks for Information Transmission

The information transmission framework introduced in the paper provides a powerful lens through which to understand and design parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning methods. Under this framework, composing a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ from a product of sparse matrices $\bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$ is equivalent to transmitting information on a grid-structured graph with $d \times (m+1)$ nodes, where $d$ is the dimensionality of the orthogonal matrix and $m$ is the number of factorization matrices. The desiderata for achieving parameter efficiency are twofold: first, dense connectivity, meaning every node in the first level must have at least one path to every node in the last level; and second, minimum free edges, meaning the total number of edges should be as small as possible under the orthogonality constraint.

The butterfly structure, inspired by the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm, satisfies these desiderata by densely connecting $d$ source nodes to $d$ receiver nodes using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges. This is a significant improvement over a naive dense connection, which would require $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges. The paper provides a concrete example: for $d = 4$ nodes, a feasible factorization that the authors constructed required 10 edges, while the butterfly network achieved dense connectivity with only 8 edges. This illustrates the efficiency of the butterfly topology within the information transmission framework.

However, the paper explicitly acknowledges that whether the butterfly network is the most efficient way to transmit information remains an open question. While the butterfly structure is provably effective and can recover many classic fast linear transforms, such as the discrete Fourier transform, discrete cosine transform, and Hadamard transform, it is not necessarily optimal among all possible sparse matrix factorization schemes. There may exist other graph topologies that achieve dense connectivity with even fewer edges, or that introduce more favorable inductive biases for finetuning. The information transmission framework makes this question precise: one must search over all possible directed graph structures on a $d \times (m+1)$ grid that satisfy the dense connectivity and minimum free edges desiderata, subject to the orthogonality constraint, and determine whether the butterfly structure minimizes the number of trainable parameters. This remains an unresolved theoretical question, and answering it could lead to even more parameter-efficient variants of orthogonal finetuning.

## Future Inspiration from Computer Networking Topologies

The information transmission framework not only provides a theoretical perspective for understanding existing factorization schemes but also opens the door to discovering new ones by drawing inspiration from a seemingly distant research discipline: computer networking. The paper explicitly identifies this as a promising future direction, noting that the efficiency of network topologies for transmitting information has been heavily studied in the computer networking community.

In computer networking, the problem of efficiently connecting nodes and exchanging information is fundamental. Various network topologies have been developed over decades of research, each with different trade-offs in terms of connectivity, latency, bandwidth, and fault tolerance. Examples include mesh networks, hypercube networks, torus networks, fat-tree topologies, and many others. Each of these topologies induces a specific sparsity pattern in the connectivity matrix between adjacent levels of nodes, which directly corresponds to the sparsity pattern of the factorization matrices $\bm{B}_i$ in the OFT framework. By translating these well-studied network topologies into the language of sparse matrix factorization, one could potentially discover orthogonal parameterizations that are even more parameter-efficient than the butterfly structure.

The key insight is that the information transmission framework abstracts away the specifics of the butterfly structure and focuses on the essential requirements: dense connectivity and minimum free edges under orthogonality. Any network topology that satisfies these desiderata could, in principle, be used to compose a dense orthogonal matrix from sparse factors. The paper expresses the expectation that "more efficient network structures can be used for composing dense orthogonal matrices," suggesting that the butterfly structure is merely the first instance of what could be a broader family of parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning methods. This connection between finetuning and computer networking exemplifies how cross-disciplinary inspiration can drive progress in machine learning, and it positions the information transmission framework as a general-purpose tool for future research in parameter-efficient finetuning. The framework's flexibility means that as new network topologies are discovered or adapted from the networking literature, they can be directly evaluated within the OFT paradigm, potentially yielding methods that achieve better trade-offs between parameter efficiency, expressivity, and training stability than BOFT.
