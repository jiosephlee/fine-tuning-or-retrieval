Chapter 1: The Rise of Large Foundation Models

In recent years, the field of artificial intelligence has witnessed a paradigm shift driven by the emergence of large foundation models. Models such as ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion have demonstrated remarkable generalization abilities, showcasing unprecedented performance across a wide array of tasks in both natural language processing and computer vision. These models are becoming ubiquitous, serving as the backbone for numerous applications in research and industry. The rapid increase in their performance, however, is paired with a dramatic increase in the number of parameters they contain. For instance, GPT-3, one of the most prominent large language models, possesses approximately 175 billion parameters. This exponential growth in model scale is a fundamental characteristic of modern foundation models, as the vast number of parameters is crucial for capturing complex patterns and achieving state-of-the-art results.

The sheer scale of these models represents a significant milestone in artificial intelligence. The large parameter counts allow foundation models to learn rich, generalizable representations from massive datasets during the pretraining phase. As a result, a single pretrained model can be adapted to perform competently on many different downstream tasks. The generality and power of these models make them highly desirable, as they encapsulate a broad understanding of the world that can be leveraged for specific applications. However, this immense scale also introduces significant logistical and computational hurdles. The rise of these models means that researchers and practitioners must work with architectures that are far larger and more complex than those of previous generations, necessitating new approaches to effectively utilize them.

# The Computational Challenge of Training from Scratch vs. Adapting Pretrained Models

While large foundation models offer unparalleled capabilities, training them from scratch is prohibitively expensive. The computational resources, time, and data required to train a model with hundreds of billions of parameters from the ground up are immense, making it increasingly challenging for researchers and organizations to undertake such an endeavor. As a result, training a foundation model from scratch is often infeasible for all but the most well-resourced entities. This poses a significant barrier to broad progress in the field, as the ability to innovate is constrained by access to the immense computational power needed for pretraining.

To overcome this bottleneck, it has become essential to adapt existing pretrained foundation models to downstream tasks without retraining them from scratch. Efficient task adaptation is therefore increasingly important, as it allows researchers to leverage the knowledge encoded in large foundation models while only requiring a fraction of the computational resources needed for initial training. The fundamental principle behind this adaptation is to ensure that the pretrained model and the finetuned model remain similar based on certain measurements, such that the pretraining knowledge is preserved. For example, traditional model finetuning methods often adopt a small learning rate to encourage a small discrepancy between the pretrained and finetuned weights. This approach helps to retain the general knowledge learned during pretraining while allowing the model to specialize for a specific downstream task.

# Overview of Task Adaptation Methods

To efficiently adapt large foundation models to downstream tasks, researchers have developed several paradigms that reduce the computational burden while preserving the model's capabilities. There are primarily three ways to perform efficient task adaptation: model finetuning, adapter tuning, and prompt tuning. Each of these methods offers a distinct approach to modifying a pretrained model for a new task, with different trade-offs regarding parameter efficiency and inference cost.

Model finetuning is a simple yet powerful approach where the overall model architecture remains unchanged and only a subset of the model parameters gets finetuned. A key advantage of this method is that it introduces no inference latency, meaning the adapted model runs just as fast as the original pretrained model during deployment. The goal is to encourage a small discrepancy between the pretrained and finetuned models to preserve knowledge. Adapter tuning, on the other hand, involves adding additional trainable parameters to the original model. These adapter modules are inserted into the pretrained network and trained on the downstream task while the base model parameters remain frozen. This approach allows for task-specific adaptation without modifying the foundational weights, but it can introduce additional computational overhead during inference due to the extra parameters. Finally, prompt tuning involves attaching additional trainable prefix tokens to the input. By optimizing these continuous prompts, the model can be guided to perform the desired task. This method is highly parameter-efficient, as only a small number of prompt tokens need to be trained, but it relies on the model's ability to interpret these prompts effectively.

# Reparameterization-Based Finetuning: An Introduction to LoRA and its Low-Rank Structure

Among the various parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) methods, reparameterization-based methods are particularly relevant for their ability to achieve high efficiency through structural constraints. LoRA, or Low-Rank Adaptation, is a prominent example of this approach. LoRA updates the pretrained weight matrix by adding a product of two low-rank matrices. Instead of modifying the original weights directly, LoRA introduces a low-rank update, achieving promising performance on natural language tasks. This method relies on a low-rank structure to achieve parameter efficiency, significantly reducing the number of trainable parameters compared to full finetuning.

The core mechanism of LoRA involves representing the weight update as the product of two smaller matrices. If the original weight matrix has a high dimensionality, the low-rank constraint assumes that the necessary changes for downstream adaptation lie within a lower-dimensional subspace. Since the rank of the added matrices is typically set to a constant, several subsequent methods have explored dynamically adjusting the rank for different layers to allocate the parameter budget more adequately. Due to its simplicity and effectiveness, low-rank weight reparameterization has gained great popularity. In the broader context of structured matrices, both low-rank matrices and sparse matrices are major families that achieve parameter efficiency. While LoRA leverages the low-rank family, other methods may leverage sparsity to reduce the effective number of trainable parameters.

# Introduction to Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) as an Alternative Multiplicative Approach

While LoRA and other methods rely on additive low-rank updates, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) offers a principled, multiplicative alternative for model adaptation. The motivation for OFT comes from the insight that a more principled approach to finetuning tries to preserve the relational information of the weight matrices, specifically the pairwise angles between neurons. In a neural network layer, neurons can be viewed as column vectors of the weight matrix. By ensuring that neurons in the same layer are transformed by the same orthogonal matrix, OFT provably preserves the pairwise angles between them throughout the finetuning process. This preservation helps ensure that the semantic knowledge learned during pretraining is largely retained.

Concretely, OFT optimizes an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ for a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. The forward pass is modified from $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ and $\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ are the input and output vectors, respectively. To achieve zero initialization—which ensures that finetuning starts from the exact pretrained model—OFT initializes the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as an identity matrix. To maintain the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ throughout training, OFT employs Cayley parameterization, defined as $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$.

Despite demonstrating good generalizability and stable training, OFT faces a significant challenge: the number of trainable parameters can be quite large due to the high dimensionality of orthogonal matrices. A dense $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix generally requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. To address this, OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure, parameterizing $\bm{R}$ as $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \cdots, \bm{R}_r)$ where each $\bm{R}_i$ is a small $b\times b$ orthogonal matrix and $br=d$. While this reduces parameters to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$, it introduces a rigid sparsity pattern where the orthogonal transformation is applied independently in different blocks. This block-diagonal approach reduces expressiveness, as it cannot approximate classic dense linear transforms, and makes the arbitrary division of neuron dimensions into groups based on their indices a potentially undesirable inductive bias. This limitation sets the stage for more advanced methods that seek to construct dense orthogonal matrices in a parameter-efficient manner.
