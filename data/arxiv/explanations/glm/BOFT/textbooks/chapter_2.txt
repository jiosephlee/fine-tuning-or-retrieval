Chapter 2: Foundations of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

## The Principle of Preserving Pretraining Knowledge in Model Finetuning

Large foundation models such as ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion have demonstrated remarkable generalization capabilities, but their training from scratch is prohibitively expensive. For instance, GPT-3 has approximately 175 billion parameters, making it increasingly challenging for researchers to train such models from the ground up. This reality has elevated the importance of efficient task adaptation — the ability to take an existing pretrained model and adapt it to downstream tasks without retraining it entirely. There are three primary paradigms for efficient task adaptation: model finetuning, where the model architecture remains unchanged and a subset of parameters gets finetuned; adapter tuning, where additional trainable parameters are added to the original model; and prompt tuning, where additional trainable prefix tokens are attached to the input. Among these, model finetuning is particularly attractive because it is simple, powerful, and introduces no inference latency.

The fundamental principle behind model finetuning is to ensure that the pretrained model and the finetuned model remain similar according to some meaningful measurement, such that the knowledge acquired during pretraining is preserved. Current model finetuning methods often adopt a small learning rate as an ad hoc approach to encourage a small discrepancy between the pretrained and finetuned models. While this practical heuristic does help prevent the finetuned model from drifting too far from the pretrained one, it lacks a principled theoretical foundation. A more principled approach leverages the structured nature of weight matrices and tries to preserve the relational information encoded within them — specifically, the pairwise angles between neurons. This insight, grounded in the observation that hyperspherical energy characterizes generalization, leads to a novel model finetuning framework known as Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). The core idea is that by transforming neurons within the same layer using the same orthogonal matrix, the pairwise angles between those neurons are provably preserved throughout the finetuning process, thereby safeguarding the semantic knowledge transferred from pretraining.

## Motivation for Orthogonal Transformations: Preserving Pairwise Angles Between Neurons

The motivation for using orthogonal transformations in finetuning stems from the geometric structure of neural network weight matrices. Each neuron in a layer can be viewed as a column vector of the weight matrix, and the pairwise angles between these column vectors encode important relational information about how neurons interact and represent features. When a model is finetuned, the concern is that these pairwise angles could be disrupted, leading to the loss of pretraining knowledge. By constraining the finetuning transformation to be orthogonal, OFT ensures that the transformation preserves all pairwise angles between neurons. An orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ satisfies $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$, which means that for any two column vectors $\bm{w}_i^0$ and $\bm{w}_j^0$ of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, the angle between $\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0$ and $\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0$ is identical to the angle between $\bm{w}_i^0$ and $\bm{w}_j^0$. This is because the inner product $\langle \bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0, \bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0 \rangle = \bm{w}_i^{0\top}\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0 = \bm{w}_i^{0\top}\bm{w}_j^0 = \langle \bm{w}_i^0, \bm{w}_j^0 \rangle$, and since the norm of each vector is also preserved by the orthogonal transformation, the cosine of the angle (and thus the angle itself) is unchanged.

This preservation of pairwise angles has been shown to benefit generalization, drawing on prior work establishing connections between hyperspherical energy, orthogonal constraints, and generalization ability. OFT has demonstrated promising generalizability and convergence, particularly in finetuning text-to-image diffusion models. Compared to LoRA, which updates weights using an additive low-rank matrix, OFT uses a multiplicative orthogonal matrix to update the weights, achieving stronger generalization and more consistently stable training. The multiplicative approach inherently preserves the geometric relationships within the weight matrix, whereas the additive approach in LoRA does not provide such guarantees.

## Mathematical Formulation of OFT: Forward Pass Modification and Orthogonal Matrix Multiplication

The mathematical formulation of OFT is straightforward yet powerful. Consider a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where $d$ is the input dimension and $n$ is the output dimension. The original forward pass computes the output as $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the input vector and $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the output vector. OFT modifies this forward pass by introducing a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ that multiplies the pretrained weight matrix from the left, changing the forward pass to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. The pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ remains frozen, and only the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is optimized during finetuning.

A crucial aspect of this formulation is the initialization of $\bm{R}$ as an identity matrix. This zero-initialization strategy ensures that, at the start of finetuning, the finetuned model is identical to the pretrained model — that is, $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{I}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{W}^0$. This means the finetuning process begins from the exact pretrained model, and the orthogonal matrix gradually deviates from the identity as training progresses, allowing the model to adapt to the downstream task while maintaining a connection to the pretrained weights. This design philosophy parallels LoRA's use of zero initialization for its low-rank weight updates, ensuring that the finetuning process starts from the pretrained model and does not deviate excessively.

The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ transforms all neurons (column vectors of $\bm{W}^0$) in the same layer by the same transformation. This shared transformation is what guarantees the preservation of pairwise angles, as discussed in the previous section. It is worth noting that this is a multiplicative weight update, in contrast to the additive update used by LoRA, which modifies the weights as $\bm{W}^0 + \Delta\bm{W}$ where $\Delta\bm{W}$ is a low-rank matrix. The multiplicative approach has a distinct advantage: it perfectly preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix, since the singular values of $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ are identical to those of $\bm{W}^0$ (as $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal). This spectral preservation has been shown to greatly benefit training stability and generalization.

## Cayley Parameterization for Ensuring Orthogonality

A key technical challenge in OFT is ensuring that the matrix $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal throughout the entire finetuning process. Simply optimizing $\bm{R}$ as a free parameter and then projecting it onto the orthogonal group after each update would be computationally expensive and could introduce instability. Instead, OFT employs the Cayley parameterization, which provides a smooth mapping from the space of skew-symmetric matrices to the space of orthogonal matrices.

The Cayley parameterization expresses the orthogonal matrix as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. The skew-symmetry constraint on $\bm{Q}$ ensures that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal. To see why this works, consider that the Cayley transform maps any skew-symmetric matrix to an orthogonal matrix that does not have $-1$ as an eigenvalue. The skew-symmetric property of $\bm{Q}$ means that $\bm{Q}$ has $d(d-1)/2$ free parameters (the upper triangular entries, with the lower triangular entries determined by the upper ones and the diagonal entries forced to be zero), which is exactly the dimensionality of the orthogonal group $O(d)$.

During finetuning, the trainable parameters are the entries of $\bm{Q}$, and the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is computed from $\bm{Q}$ via the Cayley transform at each forward pass. Since the Cayley transform guarantees orthogonality by construction, there is no need for explicit orthogonalization or projection steps during training. For zero initialization (ensuring $\bm{R}$ starts as the identity), the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ is initialized to zeros, since $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{0})(\bm{I} - \bm{0})^{-1} = \bm{I} \cdot \bm{I}^{-1} = \bm{I}$. This elegant parameterization thus simultaneously ensures orthogonality, enables smooth optimization, and supports zero initialization.

## Block-Diagonal OFT: Introducing Sparsity for Parameter Efficiency and Its Limitations

Despite the strong generalizability demonstrated by OFT, the number of trainable parameters can be quite large due to the high dimensionality of orthogonal matrices. A full $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix parameterized via the Cayley transform requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters (specifically $d(d-1)/2$ free parameters from the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$). For large foundation models where $d$ can be thousands or more, this parameter count becomes prohibitively expensive for efficient finetuning.

To address this, OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure to reduce the number of trainable parameters. Specifically, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is parameterized as $\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \cdots, \bm{R}_r)$, where each $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ is a small orthogonal matrix and $br = d$. Here, $r$ is the number of blocks and $b$ is the block size. Each small orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}_i$ is parameterized using the Cayley transform with its own skew-symmetric matrix. The total number of trainable parameters becomes $\mathcal{O}(bd)$, since each $b \times b$ block requires $b(b-1)/2$ parameters and there are $r = d/b$ blocks, yielding $r \cdot b(b-1)/2 = (d/b) \cdot b(b-1)/2 = d(b-1)/2 = \mathcal{O}(bd)$. By choosing a smaller block size $b$, one can significantly reduce the parameter count. The smaller the block size $b$, the more parameter-efficient OFT becomes.

However, this parameter efficiency comes at a significant price. The block-diagonal structure imposes a fixed sparsity pattern on the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, meaning that the orthogonal transformation is applied independently within each block of dimensions. Dimensions belonging to different blocks are never mixed or interacted with each other through the transformation. This introduces a strong and potentially undesirable assumption: the dimensions of each neuron (i.e., each column vector of the weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$) are divided into $r$ groups based on their indices, and dimensions within different groups are transformed separately using different orthogonal matrices. The block-diagonal orthogonal matrix also reduces expressiveness — it cannot approximate classic linear transforms such as the discrete Fourier transform or the Hadamard transform, which require mixing across all dimensions.

## The Inductive Bias Problem of Block-Diagonal Structures and the Need for Dense Orthogonal Matrices

The block-diagonal structure in OFT introduces a problematic inductive bias that undermines the principled motivation behind orthogonal finetuning. The assumption that the dimensions of a neuron can be partitioned into $r$ groups based on their indices is arbitrary and lacks theoretical justification. There is no principled reason to believe that dimensions $1$ through $b$ of a neuron should be transformed together and independently from dimensions $b+1$ through $2b$, and so on. The grouping is entirely determined by the index ordering of the dimensions, which is a artifact of implementation rather than a meaningful property of the model's representations. This makes the block-diagonal approach semantically questionable, as it makes no sense to divide the dimensions of a neuron into groups based on their indices.

Furthermore, the block-diagonal structure severely limits the expressiveness of the orthogonal transformation. A block-diagonal orthogonal matrix can only perform rotations within each block independently, meaning it can never represent transformations that mix information across blocks. Many classical and practically important linear transforms — such as the discrete Fourier transform, discrete cosine transform, and Hadamard transform — require dense mixing across all dimensions and therefore cannot be represented by any block-diagonal matrix, regardless of the block size chosen. This means that the block-diagonal OFT fundamentally cannot approximate these transforms, which limits its ability to capture certain types of transformations that might be beneficial for downstream tasks.

The choice of sparsity pattern — that is, how to partition the dimensions into blocks — also remains a mystery. There is no clear principled criterion for selecting a good block-diagonal structure, and different partitioning choices could lead to significantly different finetuning performance. This lack of guidance makes the block-diagonal approach difficult to tune and potentially suboptimal.

These limitations motivate the need for dense orthogonal matrices — matrices where every entry can be non-zero, allowing full mixing across all dimensions. A dense orthogonal matrix would avoid the arbitrary grouping assumption, could potentially approximate classic linear transforms, and would not require choosing a sparsity pattern. However, constructing a dense orthogonal matrix naively requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which defeats the purpose of parameter-efficient finetuning. The key challenge, therefore, is to find a way to construct a dense orthogonal matrix that is both expressive and parameter-efficient. This challenge — composing a dense orthogonal matrix from a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices — is the central problem that the Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) method addresses, using insights from information transmission theory and the butterfly structure of the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm to achieve $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters while maintaining the ability to generate dense orthogonal matrices.
