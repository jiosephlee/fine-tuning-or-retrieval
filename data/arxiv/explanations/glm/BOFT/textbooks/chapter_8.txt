\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 8: Limitations and Future Directions

## Training Runtime Overhead

A central limitation of Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) lies in its training runtime overhead. The fundamental mechanism by which BOFT achieves parameter efficiency is through matrix factorization: rather than storing and learning a single dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, which would require $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, BOFT composes $\bm{R}$ as a product of $m$ sparse orthogonal matrices, specifically $\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, where each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is a block-diagonal matrix of sparse orthogonal factors. While this factorization dramatically reduces the number of trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ when using the full butterfly structure, it introduces a computational trade-off: the matrix multiplication must be performed repeatedly in each training iteration.

The paper explicitly acknowledges this trade between computation time and space. During the forward pass, the input vector $\bm{x}$ is transformed via $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}(m,b) \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where computing $\bm{R}(m,b)$ requires sequentially multiplying $m$ sparse matrices together. Each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ has $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries, and there are $m$ such components, so the total computational cost of forming or applying the product is $\mathcal{O}(dm)$ per layer per forward pass. When $m = \log \frac{2d}{b}$, this becomes $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$. During backpropagation, gradients must flow through each of these multiplicative factors, further increasing the computational burden relative to the original OFT, which applies a single (albeit potentially dense) orthogonal matrix.

Importantly, the paper notes that this runtime overhead is "slightly larger than OFT," suggesting that the additional cost is manageable in practice but nonetheless represents a limitation that distinguishes BOFT from methods like LoRA, which do not require such repeated matrix multiplications. The paper identifies improving BOFT's training runtime as an open problem, one that remains unresolved within the scope of the current work. This overhead is the price paid for the parameter efficiency and the ability to generate dense orthogonal transformations using sparse factors, and any future work seeking to deploy BOFT in highly resource-constrained settings would need to address this computational cost.

## Zero Inference Latency

Despite the training runtime overhead, one of the most appealing properties of BOFT is that it introduces zero inference latency. This is a direct consequence of the multiplicative nature of the weight update in orthogonal finetuning. During finetuning, the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ is frozen, and the model learns the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ such that the effective weight becomes $\bm{R}(m,b) \cdot \bm{W}^0$. After the finetuning process is complete, the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ can be directly multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ to obtain a single updated weight matrix $\bm{W}^{\text{finetuned}} = \bm{R}(m,b) \cdot \bm{W}^0$.

Once this multiplication is performed, the resulting matrix $\bm{W}^{\text{finetuned}}$ is a standard dense matrix of the same dimensions as the original pretrained weight matrix. At inference time, the forward pass reverts to the standard linear computation $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^{\text{finetuned}})^\top \bm{x}$, which is identical in computational cost to the original pretrained model. There is no need to compute the product of $m$ sparse butterfly components at inference time, because all of that structure has been collapsed into the single merged weight matrix. This is a significant practical advantage, as it means that deploying a BOFT-finetuned model incurs no additional computational overhead compared to deploying the original pretrained model.

This property is shared with other model finetuning approaches, such as LoRA, where the low-rank update can similarly be merged back into the pretrained weights after training. The paper highlights this as a distinguishing feature of model finetuning methods more broadly compared to adapter tuning and prompt tuning, the latter of which introduce additional trainable parameters or tokens that persist during inference and can introduce latency. For BOFT specifically, the zero inference latency property ensures that the parameter efficiency gains during training do not come at the cost of a slower model at deployment time, making it practical for real-world applications where inference speed is critical.

## Open Questions Regarding the Optimality of Butterfly Networks for Information Transmission

The information transmission framework introduced in the paper provides a powerful lens through which to understand and design parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning methods. Under this framework, composing a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ from a product of sparse matrices $\bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$ is equivalent to transmitting information on a grid-structured graph with $d \times (m+1)$ nodes, where $d$ is the dimensionality of the orthogonal matrix and $m$ is the number of factorization matrices. The desiderata for achieving parameter efficiency are twofold: first, dense connectivity, meaning every node in the first level must have at least one path to every node in the last level; and second, minimum free edges, meaning the total number of edges should be as small as possible under the orthogonality constraint.

The butterfly structure, inspired by the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm, satisfies these desiderata by densely connecting $d$ source nodes to $d$ receiver nodes using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges. This is a significant improvement over a naive dense connection, which would require $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges. The paper provides a concrete example: for $d = 4$ nodes, a feasible factorization that the authors constructed required 10 edges, while the butterfly network achieved dense connectivity with only 8 edges. This illustrates the efficiency of the butterfly topology within the information transmission framework.

However, the paper explicitly acknowledges that whether the butterfly network is the most efficient way to transmit information remains an open question. While the butterfly structure is provably effective and can recover many classic fast linear transforms, such as the discrete Fourier transform, discrete cosine transform, and Hadamard transform, it is not necessarily optimal among all possible sparse matrix factorization schemes. There may exist other graph topologies that achieve dense connectivity with even fewer edges, or that introduce more favorable inductive biases for finetuning. The information transmission framework makes this question precise: one must search over all possible directed graph structures on a $d \times (m+1)$ grid that satisfy the dense connectivity and minimum free edges desiderata, subject to the orthogonality constraint, and determine whether the butterfly structure minimizes the number of trainable parameters. This remains an unresolved theoretical question, and answering it could lead to even more parameter-efficient variants of orthogonal finetuning.

## Future Inspiration from Computer Networking Topologies

The information transmission framework not only provides a theoretical perspective for understanding existing factorization schemes but also opens the door to discovering new ones by drawing inspiration from a seemingly distant research discipline: computer networking. The paper explicitly identifies this as a promising future direction, noting that the efficiency of network topologies for transmitting information has been heavily studied in the computer networking community.

In computer networking, the problem of efficiently connecting nodes and exchanging information is fundamental. Various network topologies have been developed over decades of research, each with different trade-offs in terms of connectivity, latency, bandwidth, and fault tolerance. Examples include mesh networks, hypercube networks, torus networks, fat-tree topologies, and many others. Each of these topologies induces a specific sparsity pattern in the connectivity matrix between adjacent levels of nodes, which directly corresponds to the sparsity pattern of the factorization matrices $\bm{B}_i$ in the OFT framework. By translating these well-studied network topologies into the language of sparse matrix factorization, one could potentially discover orthogonal parameterizations that are even more parameter-efficient than the butterfly structure.

The key insight is that the information transmission framework abstracts away the specifics of the butterfly structure and focuses on the essential requirements: dense connectivity and minimum free edges under orthogonality. Any network topology that satisfies these desiderata could, in principle, be used to compose a dense orthogonal matrix from sparse factors. The paper expresses the expectation that "more efficient network structures can be used for composing dense orthogonal matrices," suggesting that the butterfly structure is merely the first instance of what could be a broader family of parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning methods. This connection between finetuning and computer networking exemplifies how cross-disciplinary inspiration can drive progress in machine learning, and it positions the information transmission framework as a general-purpose tool for future research in parameter-efficient finetuning. The framework's flexibility means that as new network topologies are discovered or adapted from the networking literature, they can be directly evaluated within the OFT paradigm, potentially yielding methods that achieve better trade-offs between parameter efficiency, expressivity, and training stability than BOFT.
