\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 7: Empirical Evaluation and Applications

The theoretical foundations of Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) presented in earlier chapters establish a principled framework for parameter-efficient finetuning, but the ultimate test of any finetuning method lies in its empirical performance across diverse tasks and model architectures. This chapter examines the extensive empirical study conducted to validate BOFT as a generic model finetuning method. The evaluation spans multiple modalities—computer vision, natural language processing, and text-to-image generation—and encompasses a variety of downstream tasks that stress-test the method's parameter efficiency, generalization ability, and training stability. By applying BOFT to large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models, the empirical study demonstrates that BOFT consistently outperforms current state-of-the-art methods by a considerable margin, validating its superiority as a generic model finetuning approach.

The empirical evaluation is motivated by several key questions that arise naturally from the theoretical development of BOFT. First, does the butterfly factorization, which trades computational depth for parameter savings, actually translate to better performance in practice compared to the block-diagonal structure of standard OFT? Second, can BOFT, which uses a multiplicative orthogonal update, compete with or surpass the widely adopted additive low-rank update of LoRA across diverse tasks? Third, does the structured inductive bias introduced by the butterfly topology genuinely benefit generalization, as hypothesized from its connection to classic fast linear transforms? The experiments are designed to answer these questions comprehensively.

## Applying BOFT to Large Vision Transformers (ViTs) for Computer Vision Tasks

Large vision transformers have become a dominant architecture in computer vision, demonstrating remarkable performance on tasks ranging from image classification to semantic segmentation. However, the scale of these models—often comprising hundreds of millions or even billions of parameters—makes full finetuning prohibitively expensive in terms of both computation and storage. Parameter-efficient finetuning methods are therefore essential for adapting pretrained vision transformers to downstream vision tasks, and this setting provides a rigorous testbed for comparing BOFT against alternative approaches.

The application of BOFT to vision transformers follows the general finetuning paradigm established in the method's formulation. For each linear layer in the vision transformer with a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, BOFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m, b) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ that is composed as a product of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components, each parameterized with block size $b$. The forward pass is modified from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}(m, b) \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ remain frozen and only the parameters of $\bm{R}(m, b)$ are updated during training. The total number of effective trainable parameters introduced per layer is $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$, which for the full butterfly case ($m = \log d$, $b = 2$) yields $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters—a dramatic reduction from the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters required by a full dense orthogonal matrix.

A critical consideration when applying BOFT to vision transformers is the choice of the hyperparameters $m$ and $b$, which control the trade-off between expressivity and parameter efficiency. The expressivity analysis discussed later in this chapter demonstrates that increasing $m$ (the number of butterfly components) while decreasing $b$ (the block size) generally yields better parameter efficiency. This means that for vision tasks, one can achieve a dense orthogonal transformation with far fewer parameters than would be required by standard OFT with a full orthogonal matrix, while maintaining or even improving the quality of the transformation.

The identity initialization strategy employed by BOFT is particularly well-suited for vision transformer finetuning. By initializing all butterfly components as identity matrices—which corresponds to initializing the skew-symmetric matrices in the Cayley parameterization to zeros—the finetuning process begins with the exact pretrained model. This ensures that the initial forward pass is identical to that of the pretrained model, preventing any catastrophic forgetting of pretrained knowledge at the start of training. As training progresses, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m, b)$ gradually deviates from the identity, allowing the model to adapt to the downstream task while preserving the pairwise angles between neurons that encode the semantic knowledge from pretraining.

The multiplicative Dropout mechanism proposed for BOFT also plays an important role in vision tasks. Unlike conventional Dropout, which randomly zeroes out individual weights, the multiplicative Dropout for BOFT operates at the level of butterfly components and their diagonal blocks. Specifically, it randomly selects $p_1$ percent of the butterfly components and $p_2$ percent of the diagonal blocks within each selected component, replacing them with identity matrices. This form of regularization is tailored to the structure of BOFT and helps prevent overfitting, which is especially important when the downstream vision dataset is small relative to the model capacity.

## Adapting Large Language Models (LLMs) for Natural Language Processing Tasks

Large language models such as GPT-3, with its approximately 175 billion parameters, represent some of the most powerful yet most resource-intensive models in modern machine learning. The prohibitive cost of training these models from scratch necessitates efficient adaptation strategies for downstream NLP tasks. The application of BOFT to large language models extends the orthogonal finetuning paradigm to the language domain, demonstrating that the principled preservation of neuron relationships through orthogonal transformations is beneficial beyond the vision and generative modeling contexts where it was originally applied.

The finetuning of LLMs with BOFT follows the same multiplicative weight update framework as in the vision case. For each linear layer in the transformer-based language model, the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ is frozen, and an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m, b)$ is learned to transform the neurons. The forward pass becomes $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}(m, b) \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where the orthogonal matrix is parameterized as a product of butterfly components. This multiplicative approach contrasts with LoRA, which adapts the weight matrix additively as $\bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$ where $\bm{A}$ and $\bm{B}$ are low-rank matrices.

The distinction between multiplicative and additive updates has important implications for language model finetuning. The orthogonal multiplicative update preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix, a property that has been shown to benefit training stability and generalization. Specifically, if the pretrained weight matrix has a singular value decomposition $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, then the finetuned weight matrix becomes $\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, where the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ does not alter the singular values in $\bm{\Sigma}$. This preservation of the spectral structure is particularly valuable for language models, where the pretrained spectral properties encode important information about the frequency and scale of different features in the language representation.

The parameter efficiency of BOFT is especially advantageous for LLMs, where even a small percentage reduction in trainable parameters translates to enormous absolute savings. With the full butterfly parameterization ($m = \log d$, $b = 2$), BOFT requires only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters per layer, compared to $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for a full orthogonal matrix or $\mathcal{O}(rd)$ for LoRA with rank $r$. For the very large hidden dimensions common in modern LLMs (e.g., $d = 12288$ for GPT-3's largest layers), this logarithmic factor represents a reduction from approximately 150 million parameters to approximately 160 thousand parameters per layer for a single orthogonal transformation.

The connection between BOFT and bilinear similarity learning, as discussed in the theoretical analysis, provides additional insight into why the method is effective for language tasks. BOFT can be written as learning a bilinear similarity $\bm{w}^0_i^\top \bm{R} \bm{x}$, where $\bm{w}^0_i$ is the $i$-th neuron (column vector) of the weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ and $\bm{x}$ is the input. This perspective reveals that BOFT is effectively learning a bilinear similarity matrix $\bm{R}$ with strong regularity constraints (orthogonality), which connects it to distance metric learning. In the context of language models, this means that BOFT is learning to reposition the neurons in the representation space while preserving their relative geometry, allowing the model to adapt to the statistical structure of the downstream language task without destroying the pretrained linguistic knowledge.

## Finetuning Text-to-Image Diffusion Models for Controllable Generation

Text-to-image diffusion models, such as Stable Diffusion, represent a class of generative models that have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in synthesizing high-quality images from textual descriptions. The original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) method was first developed and validated in the context of finetuning text-to-image diffusion models for controllable generation, making this application a natural starting point for evaluating the improvements offered by BOFT.

In the controllable generation setting, the goal is to adapt a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model so that it can generate images conditioned on additional control signals—such as depth maps, edge maps, or segmentation masks—while preserving the model's ability to generate high-quality, diverse images. This task is particularly challenging because it requires the finetuned model to learn new conditional dependencies without forgetting the rich generative capabilities acquired during pretraining. The preservation of pairwise angles between neurons, which is the core principle behind orthogonal finetuning, is directly relevant to this challenge because it ensures that the semantic relationships encoded in the pretrained model's neurons are maintained throughout the finetuning process.

BOFT improves upon standard OFT in this setting by enabling the use of dense orthogonal matrices with far fewer parameters. The original OFT, when using a block-diagonal structure, must choose a block size $b$ that balances parameter efficiency against expressivity. Smaller block sizes are more parameter-efficient but introduce undesirable inductive biases—specifically, the assumption that the dimensions of each neuron can be meaningfully divided into independent groups based on their indices, which makes little sense in practice. BOFT resolves this tension by using the butterfly structure to compose a dense orthogonal matrix from multiple sparse orthogonal factors, allowing every dimension of a neuron to interact with every other dimension through the chain of butterfly components, while still maintaining parameter efficiency.

The weight interpolation property of BOFT, which arises from the factorized structure of the orthogonal matrix, provides an intriguing advantage for diffusion model finetuning. Because the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m, b)$ is a product of $m$ butterfly components, each initialized as an identity matrix, the finetuning process can be viewed as progressively perturbing the identity transformation through a sequence of structured, sparse operations. This factorized structure allows for smooth interpolation between the pretrained model (corresponding to $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$) and the fully finetuned model, which can be useful for applications that require fine-grained control over the degree of adaptation.

The training stability benefits of orthogonal finetuning are particularly valuable for diffusion models, which are known to be sensitive to weight perturbations during finetuning. The spectral norm preservation property ensures that the scale of the weight matrices remains bounded throughout training, preventing the exploding or vanishing gradient problems that can arise when the spectral properties of the weights are altered. This stability allows for more aggressive learning rates and faster convergence compared to methods that do not preserve the spectral structure, such as LoRA.

## Comparative Performance Analysis of BOFT Against LoRA and Standard OFT

A central aspect of the empirical evaluation is the comparative analysis of BOFT against its two most natural baselines: LoRA, the widely adopted low-rank adaptation method, and standard OFT, the block-diagonal orthogonal finetuning method that BOFT generalizes. Understanding the relative strengths and weaknesses of these methods requires examining their structural differences, their parameter efficiency, and their empirical performance across diverse tasks.

The fundamental distinction between these methods lies in how they modify the pretrained weight matrix. LoRA employs an additive update: $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$, where $\bm{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $\bm{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ are low-rank matrices with rank $r \ll \min(d, n)$. This additive update introduces $\mathcal{O}(r(d + n))$ trainable parameters and can be viewed as perturbing the pretrained weights within a low-dimensional subspace. Standard OFT, in contrast, employs a multiplicative update: $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0$, where $\bm{R}$ is a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix with $r$ blocks of size $b \times b$ (such that $br = d$). This introduces $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ parameters when using $r = d/b$ blocks, and the multiplicative update preserves the pairwise angles between neurons. BOFT generalizes OFT by replacing the block-diagonal structure with a butterfly-factorized dense orthogonal matrix, introducing $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ parameters where $m$ is the number of butterfly components.

The relationship between BOFT and standard OFT is characterized by a clean nesting structure. BOFT($1$, $b$) reduces to block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, where only a single butterfly component is used, corresponding to a block-diagonal matrix with $2b \times 2b$ blocks. BOFT($1$, $d$) reduces to the original OFT with an unconstrained full orthogonal matrix of size $d \times d$. BOFT($\log \frac{2d}{b}$, $b$) uses the full block butterfly matrix, yielding a dense orthogonal matrix. This nesting means that BOFT subsumes OFT as a special case and provides a continuous spectrum of parameter-efficiency trade-offs between the two extremes of OFT.

The expressivity comparison between BOFT and OFT is informed by the simulation study in which both methods are used to approximate a random $512 \times 512$ dense orthogonal matrix. The results, averaged over 10 random seeds, reveal several important findings. First, BOFT generally yields better parameter efficiency than OFT: for the same number of effective trainable parameters, BOFT achieves a lower approximation error. Second, BOFT with smaller block size $b$ and larger number of components $m$ tends to be more parameter-efficient. For example, the expressiveness of BOFT($9$, $2$) is better than that of BOFT($1$, $16$) despite using far fewer parameters. Similarly, BOFT($6$, $4$) uses much fewer parameters but achieves a similar approximation error to BOFT($2$, $16$). These findings confirm that the butterfly structure provides a more efficient way to parameterize dense orthogonal matrices compared to the block-diagonal structure.

The theoretical expressivity result formalizes this advantage: BOFT is provably more expressive than OFT with the same block size. Specifically, the butterfly matrix can perfectly recover many classic fast linear transforms—including the discrete Fourier transform and the Hadamard transform—which is impossible for the block-diagonal structure. Furthermore, by composing multiple butterfly matrices and their transposes in a kaleidoscope hierarchy, $\bm{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{d-1,2}(d)\cdots\bm{B}_{1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{1,2}(d)$, one can represent the entire orthogonal group of size $d$. This means that while a single butterfly matrix represents a structured subset of the orthogonal group, the composition of multiple butterfly matrices can achieve universal expressivity.

The comparison with LoRA reveals a different set of trade-offs. While LoRA's additive low-rank update is conceptually simple and computationally efficient, it does not preserve the spectral properties of the pretrained weights. The singular values of $\bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$ can differ arbitrarily from those of $\bm{W}^0$, depending on the rank and magnitude of the update. This can lead to training instability and degraded generalization, particularly when the pretrained model has carefully tuned spectral properties. BOFT, by contrast, perfectly preserves the singular values of the pretrained weight matrix, as the orthogonal multiplicative update $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ only rotates the left singular vectors without altering the singular value matrix $\bm{\Sigma}$. This spectral preservation has been shown to greatly benefit both training stability and generalization, giving BOFT a principled advantage over LoRA in settings where the pretrained model's spectral structure is important.

Both low-rank matrices (used by LoRA) and sparse matrices (used by BOFT) are major families of structured matrices that achieve parameter efficiency, but they introduce different inductive biases. The low-rank structure assumes that the weight update lies in a low-dimensional subspace, which is effective when the task adaptation requires changes along a few principal directions. The butterfly sparse structure, on the other hand, assumes that the weight transformation can be decomposed into a sequence of local, structured operations that collectively achieve a global transformation—a assumption that is shared by many classic linear transforms and is therefore well-aligned with the structure of natural data transformations.

## Evaluating Parameter-Efficiency, Generalization Ability, and Training Stability Across Diverse Downstream Tasks

The ultimate validation of any finetuning method comes from its performance across a diverse set of downstream tasks, evaluated on multiple dimensions: parameter efficiency (how few trainable parameters are needed to achieve good performance), generalization ability (how well the finetuned model performs on unseen data), and training stability (how reliably the method converges without requiring extensive hyperparameter tuning).

Parameter efficiency is quantified by the number of effective trainable parameters introduced by each method. For BOFT($m$, $b$), this count is $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ per layer, which for the full butterfly case ($m = \log d$, $b = 2$) becomes approximately $d \log d$. This is a significant reduction from the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters required by the original OFT with a full dense orthogonal matrix and is competitive with or superior to LoRA's $\mathcal{O}(r(d+n))$ parameters when $r$ is chosen to achieve comparable performance. The empirical results demonstrate that BOFT consistently achieves strong performance with fewer parameters than both LoRA and standard OFT, confirming that the butterfly factorization provides a more efficient parameterization of the orthogonal group.

The generalization ability of BOFT is supported by several theoretical and empirical considerations. The structured inductive bias introduced by the butterfly factorization is beneficial for generalization because it shares structural patterns with many classic linear transforms, including the discrete Fourier transform, discrete sine/cosine transform, and Hadamard transform. This means that BOFT naturally biases the learned transformation toward these well-studied mathematical structures, which are known to capture important patterns in natural data. Additionally, the sparse matrix factorization in BOFT may introduce implicit regularization effects, similar to how sparsity has been shown to benefit generalization in other contexts. The empirical study confirms that BOFT achieves superior generalization compared to both LoRA and standard OFT across the diverse tasks evaluated, including vision, language, and generative modeling tasks.

Training stability is a critical practical consideration, and BOFT benefits from several properties that promote stable training. The preservation of the spectral norm of the pretrained weights ensures that the scale of the weight matrices remains bounded, preventing the instabilities that can arise from uncontrolled changes to the weight magnitudes. The identity initialization ensures that training starts from the exact pretrained model, avoiding the initial instability that can occur when the finetuning parameters are randomly initialized. The Cayley parameterization, which ensures that the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal throughout training by construction (rather than relying on soft regularization), provides a hard constraint that prevents the transformation from deviating too far from the identity in ways that would compromise the pretrained knowledge. The empirical results show that BOFT achieves consistently stable training across all evaluated tasks and model architectures, without the need for extensive hyperparameter search or learning rate scheduling that is often required for less constrained methods.

One practical consideration in the evaluation is the training runtime overhead of BOFT. Because the final orthogonal matrix in BOFT is the product of $m$ orthogonal matrices, the forward and backward passes require $m$ matrix multiplications rather than a single multiplication as in standard OFT. This introduces a slightly larger training runtime overhead compared to OFT. However, this overhead is limited to the training phase: after finetuning is complete, the BOFT-learned orthogonal matrices can be directly multiplied into the pretrained model weights, yielding a single weight matrix $\bm{R}(m, b) \cdot \bm{W}^0$ that replaces the original $\bm{W}^0$. This means that BOFT introduces no additional inference latency—the finetuned model has exactly the same architecture and computational cost as the pretrained model, which is a significant advantage over adapter-based methods that add extra parameters and computation to the model.

The empirical study also evaluates the robustness of BOFT to the choice of hyperparameters $m$ and $b$. The expressivity analysis suggests that BOFT with smaller $b$ and larger $m$ is generally more parameter-efficient, and this is confirmed in the downstream task evaluations. The flexibility of the BOFT framework allows practitioners to choose different points on the parameter-efficiency spectrum depending on the constraints of their application: BOFT($1$, $b$) provides the simplest and most parameter-efficient option (equivalent to block-diagonal OFT), while BOFT($\log \frac{2d}{b}$, $b$) provides the full butterfly matrix with dense connectivity. The empirical results demonstrate that intermediate choices, such as BOFT($m$, $b$) with $m$ between 2 and $\log \frac{2d}{b}$, often provide the best trade-off between parameter efficiency and task performance.

In summary, the extensive empirical study validates BOFT as a powerful and generic model finetuning method. By applying BOFT to large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models across diverse downstream tasks, the study demonstrates that BOFT consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods by a considerable margin. The butterfly factorization enables parameter-efficient dense orthogonal transformations that preserve the semantic knowledge from pretraining while providing sufficient expressivity for task adaptation. The structured inductive bias of the butterfly topology, grounded in its connection to classic fast linear transforms, provides a principled prior that benefits generalization. The spectral preservation property ensures training stability across tasks and architectures. Together, these properties make BOFT a compelling choice for parameter-efficient finetuning of large foundation models across modalities.
