\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why is the model called "1.58 bits" when weights can take 3 values {-1, 0, 1}?
Question:
The paper introduces "BitNet b1.58" where every parameter is ternary, taking values in \{-1, 0, 1\}. The name suggests 1.58 bits per weight. I understand ternary values mean 3 possible states, and $\log_2(3) \approx 1.585$, so I assume this is where 1.58 comes from. But I'm confused: if each weight is stored as a ternary value, doesn't that still require 2 bits in practice (since you can't store 1.58 bits in hardware)? Is the 1.58 just a theoretical information-theoretic measure, or is there an actual encoding scheme that achieves this? And if it's theoretical, why not just call it a "ternary" or "2-bit" model?
Answer:
The "$1.58$ bits" in BitNet b$1.58$ is indeed an information-theoretic measure, not a claim about how the weights are literally stored in current hardware. As you correctly noted, it comes from $\log_2(3) \approx 1.585$, which is the minimum number of bits needed to encode one of three equally likely states. The naming convention follows from the original BitNet, which used binary weights $\{-1, 1\}$ and was called a "$1$-bit" model. When the authors added the value $0$ to get $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the information content per weight went from $\log_2(2) = 1$ bit to $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$ bits, hence "b$1.58$."

You are correct that on standard hardware, storing a single ternary value naively requires $2$ bits, since hardware addresses memory in byte-aligned chunks and you cannot store a fractional bit in isolation. The paper itself acknowledges this: they report latency and memory using a **$2$-bit kernel** and note there is "still room for optimization to further reduce the cost." So in their current implementation, each weight does occupy $2$ bits.

However, the $1.58$-bit figure is not purely academic. It reflects the **entropy** of the weight distribution—the actual information each weight carries. In practice, you can approach this theoretical limit by **packing** multiple ternary values together. For example, $5$ ternary values have $3^5 = 243$ possible combinations, which fit into $8$ bits (since $2^8 = 256$), yielding $8/5 = 1.6$ bits per weight. With larger block sizes, you can get arbitrarily close to $\log_2(3)$. This is why the authors argue the model "opens the door for designing specific hardware optimized for $1$-bit LLMs"—custom hardware could implement such packing schemes natively.

As for why not call it "ternary" or "$2$-bit": "ternary" would be descriptively accurate but would break continuity with the original "$1$-bit BitNet" naming and would not convey the efficiency story the paper is telling. "$2$-bit" would be misleading because $2$ bits can represent $4$ states, suggesting the model has more representational capacity per weight than it actually does. The name "$1.58$ bits" precisely communicates the information content while placing the model on the same "bits per weight" scale used throughout the $1$-bit LLM literature, making it easy to compare with the original $1$-bit BitNet and potential future variants.

### How can matrix multiplication use only integer addition and no multiplication?
Question:
The paper claims that BitNet b1.58's matrix multiplication "only involves integer addition" and "requires almost no multiplication operations." I understand that if weights are in \{-1, 0, 1\}, then multiplying an activation by a weight is trivial: the result is either the activation, its negation, or zero. But the activations themselves are 8-bit integers, not ternary. So during matrix multiplication $\widetilde{W}x$, are we computing something like: for each row of $\widetilde{W}$, we sum the activations (or their negations) where the corresponding weight is +1 or -1, skipping where it's 0? Is this what they mean by "only addition"? And is this truly multiplication-free, or are there still some multiplications happening elsewhere?
Answer:
# How BitNet b1.58 Eliminates Multiplication in Matrix Multiplication

Yes, your understanding is essentially correct. The key insight is that when weights are constrained to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the matrix multiplication $\widetilde{W}x$ reduces to a sequence of additions and negations.

## The Mechanism

For a single row of $\widetilde{W}$, computing the dot product with the activation vector $x$ (where $x$ contains INT8 values) works as follows:

- Where $\widetilde{W}_{ij} = 1$, you add $x_j$ to the accumulator.
- Where $\widetilde{W}_{ij} = -1$, you add $-x_j$ (i.e., subtract $x_j$).
- Where $\widetilde{W}_{ij} = 0$, you skip that element entirely.

So instead of computing $mn$ floating-point multiply-accumulate operations, you compute $mn$ integer additions (some of which are subtractions). At the hardware level, negation is trivial — it's implemented via two's complement, which itself reduces to addition with a bit flip. This is why the paper states the matrix multiplication "only involves integer addition."

## Why "Almost No Multiplication"?

The paper uses the phrase "almost no multiplication" rather than "no multiplication" because the linear layers (which constitute the bulk of LLM computation) are multiplication-free, but other components are not. Specifically:

- **Embeddings remain full precision** (FP16/BF16), as the paper notes that "the embedding remains full precision and its memory proportion is smaller for larger models."
- **RMSNorm, SwiGLU, and rotary embeddings** (all part of the LLaMA-alike architecture) involve operations that may require multiplication.
- **Attention computation** (softmax, scaling by $1/\sqrt{d}$, etc.) still involves floating-point operations.

The paper's energy analysis confirms this: "The majority of BitNet b1.58 is INT8 addition calculation," implying the majority — but not 100% — of the computation is addition-based.

## The Quantization of Activations

You correctly noted that activations are 8-bit integers, not ternary. The paper describes that activations are "scaled to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token" using symmetric quantization (no zero-point). This keeps activations as INT8 values, which is important because the energy savings come not just from eliminating multiplications but also from using cheaper integer addition operations instead of floating-point ones. The paper cites that this saves "71.4 times arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips."

## Summary

Your interpretation is exactly right: the ternary weights transform each multiply-accumulate into an add-or-skip (or subtract-and-add) operation. The phrase "almost no multiplication" acknowledges that while the dominant cost — the linear layers — is now multiplication-free, auxiliary operations outside the BitLinear layers still require some multiplications.

### Why does adding 0 to the weight set enable "feature filtering"?
Question:
The paper states that including 0 in the model weights (going from the original BitNet's \{-1, 1\} to \{-1, 0, 1\}) provides "explicit support for feature filtering, which can significantly improve the performance of 1-bit LLMs." I understand that a weight of 0 means a particular feature has no contribution to the output. But isn't a near-zero weight in a continuous-valued model effectively doing the same thing? Why is having an explicit 0 value qualitatively different from having a small floating-point weight? Is the claim that the ternary representation creates a form of structured sparsity that helps the model learn to ignore irrelevant features more effectively? Could someone elaborate on the mechanism by which this "feature filtering" improves performance?
Answer:
# Answer

The key to understanding this lies in the quantization function itself and what information is preserved versus destroyed when moving from continuous to discrete weights.

In the original BitNet with weights constrained to $\{-1, 1\}$, every single weight is forced to contribute either positively or negatively to the output. There is no "off" state. When the underlying continuous weight $W_{ij}$ is near zero—meaning the model has learned that this particular connection is unimportant—the binary quantization function has no choice but to round it to either $+1$ or $-1$. This injects noise: a feature the model wants to ignore gets a full-magnitude positive or negative contribution. Every weight participates in every computation, whether the model wants it to or not.

In contrast, the ternary quantization in BitNet b1.58 uses:

$$\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$$

where $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$ is the average absolute magnitude. After scaling by $\gamma$, any weight whose normalized value falls in the range $(-0.5, 0.5)$ rounds to exactly $0$. This creates a "dead zone" around zero where features are filtered out entirely—they contribute nothing to the output and, importantly, can be skipped during computation.

The qualitative difference from a small FP16 weight is twofold. First, in a continuous model, a weight of $0.001$ still participates in every matrix multiplication with its full floating-point overhead; the feature is attenuated but not absent. The ternary $0$ is a hard, discrete "off" switch that creates genuine structured sparsity. Second, and more critically for 1-bit models specifically: in the binary $\{-1, 1\}$ regime, the model *cannot express* "this feature is irrelevant" at all—the quantization destroys that information by forcing every weight to $\pm 1$. Adding $0$ back recovers this essential capability that was lost when moving from continuous to binary weights. It gives the ternary model a way to represent the full spectrum of "positive," "negative," and "irrelevant," whereas the binary model could only represent the first two.

The performance improvement comes from this regularization effect. The rounding-to-zero mechanism acts as a form of learned sparsity: connections that are weak relative to the average magnitude get pruned during training, reducing noise from unimportant features. The model can then allocate its limited representational capacity (just three values per weight) toward the connections that matter most. This is particularly valuable for 1-bit LLMs, where the extreme compression makes every weight's contribution disproportionately impactful—a single spurious $\pm 1$ weight contributes as much signal as a genuinely important one, so the ability to silence irrelevant connections is far more critical than in FP16 models where weak weights naturally have small magnitudes.

### Is BitNet b1.58 a form of post-training quantization, and if not, why does it matter?
Question:
The paper distinguishes BitNet b1.58 from post-training quantization methods like GPTQ and AWQ, stating that BitNet is "trained from scratch" with 1.58-bit weights. I'm confused about why training from scratch with quantized weights is better than quantizing after training. Intuitively, I would think a model trained with full precision would learn better representations, and then you could compress those representations afterward. Why does training with ternary weights from the start avoid the "sub-optimal" nature of post-training quantization that the paper mentions? Does the model learn fundamentally different representations when constrained to \{-1, 0, 1\} from the beginning?
Answer:
The key distinction is that post-training quantization (PTQ) applies quantization as an afterthought to a model that was optimized under completely different assumptions, whereas BitNet b1.58 bakes the quantization constraint directly into the training objective. This matters because the optimization process can *adapt* to the constraint, rather than having errors imposed on it retroactively.

When you train a model in FP16 and then quantize afterward using methods like GPTQ or AWQ, the full-precision model has learned a weight distribution that was never optimized for the target bit width. The quantization function then maps these weights to low-bit values, introducing errors that the model has no opportunity to correct. PTQ methods attempt to compensate for this — GPTQ uses second-order information to minimize the quantization error, for instance — but they are fundamentally working within a model whose internal representations were shaped by full-precision arithmetic. At aggressive bit widths like $1.58$ bits, these errors become large enough that post-hoc compensation is insufficient, which is why the paper calls PTQ "sub-optimal."

BitNet b1.58, by contrast, applies the `absmean` quantization function during the forward pass of training itself. The full-precision weights are quantized to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ on-the-fly using $\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$, and the model sees only these ternary weights during the forward computation. Gradients flow back through this quantization operation (using a straight-through estimator, as in the original BitNet), allowing the optimizer to adjust the underlying full-precision weights so that they produce *good* ternary values after quantization. In other words, the model learns a weight distribution that is explicitly aligned with the quantization function, rather than one that merely happens to be quantizable with some residual error.

The user's intuition that a full-precision model would learn "better representations" is reasonable in isolation, but it misses a crucial point: the quality of representations only matters insofar as they survive the deployment format. A full-precision model may learn richer representations, but if those representations degrade significantly under aggressive quantization, the deployed model is worse than one that learned slightly less rich representations natively within the ternary constraint. The paper's results support this — BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 LLaMA at 3B and above, demonstrating that the model can learn sufficiently expressive representations even with ternary weights, provided it is trained to do so from the start.

The model does appear to learn fundamentally different representations. The paper notes that the inclusion of $0$ — absent in the original $1$-bit BitNet — enables "explicit support for feature filtering," meaning the model can learn to route information by setting weights to zero. This is a structurally different representational strategy from a full-precision model, where near-zero weights carry gradient information and contribute marginally to computations. In BitNet b1.58, a zero weight is a hard on/off decision, and the model learns to use this discreteness productively during training.

The broader principle here is well-established in the quantization literature: quantization-aware training (QAT) generally outperforms PTQ, and the gap widens at lower bit widths. BitNet b1.58 can be understood as taking QAT to its logical extreme — rather than simulating quantization during training and dequantizing for computation, the model computes natively in the quantized space. The paper's claim of a "new scaling law" reflects the fact that the optimal trade-off between model size, training tokens, and precision is different when you commit to low-bit weights from the outset, rather than treating quantization as a compression step applied to an already-trained model.

### Why are activations 8-bit if this is a "1-bit" LLM?
Question:
The paper describes BitNet b1.58 as having "1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations." I'm confused about the asymmetry here. The title and framing emphasize the 1.58-bit nature, but the activations seem to be quantized to 8 bits, which is much higher. Why can't the activations also be ternary? Is there a fundamental reason why weights can be aggressively quantized but activations cannot? Does the 8-bit activation quantization limit the efficiency gains, or is the weight quantization the dominant factor in the memory and energy savings reported?
Answer:
The asymmetry between 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations in BitNet b1.58 is a deliberate design choice rooted in how information flows through neural networks and where the computational bottlenecks actually lie.

The key insight is that weights and activations play fundamentally different roles. Weights are static parameters learned during training; they store the model's knowledge. Activations are dynamic signals computed at runtime; they carry the actual information being processed for each specific input. Because weights are fixed after training, they can be aggressively quantized with minimal loss, especially when the model is trained from scratch with quantization in mind—as BitNet b1.58 is. The ternary constraint $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ is baked into the training process itself, so the model learns to represent its knowledge using only these three values. The inclusion of $0$ specifically enables "feature filtering," where certain connections can be effectively pruned, improving modeling capacity over the original binary BitNet.

Activations, however, are recomputed for every forward pass and must represent continuous, input-dependent values. If you quantize activations too aggressively, you lose information that the model cannot recover, because the activations encode the specific context and content of the input sequence. The paper recognizes this: it mentions that further compression of activations "to 4 bits or even lower" is left as future work, suggesting the authors view 8 bits as a safe current operating point rather than a fundamental limit.

The efficiency gains come primarily from two sources, and the weight quantization is indeed the dominant factor. First, the memory footprint reduction—$3.55\times$ less GPU memory at 3B scale—comes largely from storing weights as 1.58 bits instead of 16 bits, since weights constitute the vast majority of model parameters. This also addresses the expensive DRAM-to-SRAM transfer bottleneck the paper highlights. Second, the energy savings arise because with ternary weights, matrix multiplication reduces to integer addition: multiplying by $-1$, $0$, or $1$ requires only negation, zeroing, or pass-through, eliminating floating-point multiplication entirely. The paper reports $71.4\times$ energy savings for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips, with the computation being dominated by INT8 addition rather than FP16 multiply-add operations.

So the 8-bit activations do not substantially limit the efficiency gains because the dominant costs—memory storage and bandwidth for weights, and the elimination of multiplications—are addressed by the weight quantization alone. The activations only need to be precise enough to preserve the signal flowing through the network, and 8 bits provides sufficient headroom while still halving the activation memory compared to FP16 (which the paper notes doubles the achievable context length for KV caches). The architecture essentially trades maximal weight precision for maximal computational efficiency, while keeping activations at a precision that does not bottleneck signal quality.

### Why does BitNet b1.58 only match FP16 LLaMA starting at 3B parameters?
Question:
The results show that BitNet b1.58 matches the perplexity and zero-shot performance of FP16 LLaMA only starting from a 3B model size. For smaller models, there appears to be a performance gap. I'm wondering what causes this threshold. Is there something fundamental about model capacity that makes ternary weights insufficient for small models but adequate for larger ones? The paper mentions a "new scaling law" — does this imply that 1.58-bit models scale more favorably than FP16 models as size increases? If so, what is the theoretical explanation for why having fewer bits per weight hurts less at larger scales?
Answer:
The key to understanding this threshold lies in the relationship between model over-parameterization and the information loss from quantization. When you reduce each weight from 16 bits to 1.58 bits (ternary values $\{-1, 0, 1\}$), you lose roughly $10\times$ the information per parameter. Whether this loss matters depends critically on how much "slack" or redundancy the model has.

**The redundancy argument** is central here. Small models are more tightly parameterized—each weight carries a larger share of the model's total information, and there is less redundancy to compensate for quantization error. When you have only hundreds of millions of parameters, forcing each into one of three values degrades a significant fraction of the information the network needs. In contrast, large models are heavily over-parameterized: many weights are somewhat redundant, and the network can route around the constraints of ternary values by adjusting other weights during training. The extra capacity allows the model to find good solutions within the restricted weight space.

**The scaling law perspective** explains why the gap narrows and eventually closes. Standard neural scaling laws describe loss as decreasing roughly as a power law with model size, i.e., $L(N) \propto N^{-\alpha}$. The paper's claim of a "new scaling law" suggests that 1.58-bit models follow a scaling curve that is steeper—meaning their performance improves faster as a function of size—converging with the FP16 curve at around 3B parameters. Intuitively, the degradation from quantization grows much slower than the benefit of adding more parameters. The quantization penalty is roughly proportional to the number of parameters (you lose information per weight), but the modeling benefit of scale grows as a power law. At some crossover point, the power-law improvement from additional capacity overtakes the linear penalty from reduced precision, and that crossover empirically lands near 3B for this architecture.

**The role of ternary values** (including $0$) also matters. The paper notes that the inclusion of $0$ enables "explicit support for feature filtering," introducing a form of sparsity. In larger models, the ability to selectively zero out connections becomes increasingly useful—larger networks benefit more from structured sparsity because they have more features to selectively route through. This may contribute to the steeper scaling of the 1.58-bit curve: the ternary representation isn't just a lossy compression but also an inductive bias that becomes more advantageous at scale.

It's worth noting that this is not unique to BitNet—similar phenomena appear in post-training quantization literature, where smaller models suffer larger accuracy drops from quantization. The difference is that BitNet trains from scratch with ternary constraints, which mitigates but does not eliminate the small-model penalty. The fundamental tradeoff between precision and parameter count remains, and 3B appears to be the empirical point where the scaling advantage of additional parameters fully compensates for the per-weight information loss.

### How does the absmean quantization function work during backpropagation?
Question:
The paper defines the absmean quantization function as $\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1)$ where $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|$. I understand the forward pass: you scale the weights by their mean absolute value, round to the nearest integer in \{-1, 0, 1\}, and clip. But I'm confused about how gradients flow through this during backpropagation. The round function is non-differentiable, and clipping has zero gradient outside the range. Does the paper use a straight-through estimator (STE) like in the original BitNet? If so, how does this interact with the absmean scaling factor $\gamma$, which itself depends on the weights? Does $\gamma$ get treated as a constant during backprop, or are gradients computed through it as well?
Answer:
The paper itself doesn't go into detail on the backward pass, but it explicitly states that the quantization implementation follows BitNet ("The quantization function for activations follows the same implementation in BitNet"). BitNet uses the Straight-Through Estimator (STE), and BitNet b1.58 inherits this approach for its weight quantization.

Here's how it works conceptually. During the forward pass, the full-precision weights $W$ are quantized to ternary values $\widetilde{W}$ via the absmean function. During the backward pass, the STE simply bypasses the non-differentiable $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ operation by treating its derivative as the identity (within the clip range). That is, the gradient of the quantized output with respect to its input is approximated as:

$$\frac{\partial \widetilde{W}}{\partial W} \approx \frac{1}{\gamma} \cdot \mathbf{1}\left[\left|\frac{W}{\gamma}\right| \leq 1\right]$$

So gradients flow through as if the rounding never happened, with the clip contributing a zero gradient for values outside $[-1, 1]$ after scaling (this is standard for STE with clipping). The chain rule then gives:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W} \approx \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \widetilde{W}} \cdot \frac{1}{\gamma}$$

for weights within the clip range.

Regarding your question about $\gamma$: it is treated as a constant during backpropagation. That is, $\gamma$ is detached from the computation graph. There are several reasons for this. First, $\gamma$ depends on $W$ through $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|$, and computing gradients through it would require differentiating through the absolute value and the sum over all matrix entries, which would add significant complexity without clear benefit. Second, $\gamma$ serves as a normalization factor—it rescales the weight distribution so that rounding produces a sensible spread across $\{-1, 0, +1\}$—rather than as a learnable parameter. Third, this is consistent with standard practice in quantization-aware training, where scaling factors are typically detached (this is also how BitNet itself handles it, and BitNet b1.58 explicitly reuses BitNet's implementation).

Intuitively, you can think of the forward pass as "snap the weights to the nearest ternary value, using the mean absolute magnitude as the scale," and the backward pass as "pretend the snapping didn't happen and just let gradients flow through, modulated by $1/\gamma$." The $1/\gamma$ factor arises because the forward pass divides by $\gamma$ before rounding, so the STE effectively inverts that scaling when propagating gradients back to the full-precision $W$. The full-precision weights $W$ are what actually get updated by the optimizer; the ternary $\widetilde{W}$ is recomputed from $W$ at each forward pass.

This design is part of a broader pattern in quantization-aware training (also seen in BinaryConnect, the original BitNet, and other low-bit networks): the model maintains full-precision "master" weights for gradient-based learning, but uses quantized weights for the actual forward computation. The STE is what makes this feasible despite the non-differentiability of the quantization step.

### What does "zero-point quantization" mean and why was it removed for activations?
Question:
The paper states that for activation quantization, unlike the original BitNet, they "do not scale the activations before the non-linear functions to the range $[0, Q_b]$. Instead, the activations are all scaled to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token to get rid of the zero-point quantization." I'm not familiar with zero-point quantization. What is it, and why does scaling to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ instead of $[0, Q_b]$ eliminate it? The paper says this is "more convenient and simple for both implementation and system-level optimization, while introduces negligible effects to the performance." Could someone explain the trade-off here and why this change was made?
Answer:
In quantization, a "zero-point" is an integer offset used in **asymmetric quantization** to handle value ranges that are not centered at $0$. When you quantize a tensor whose range is $[a, b]$ (with $a \neq -b$) to $n$-bit integers $[0, 2^n - 1]$, the mapping cannot be a simple scale factor, because real-valued $0$ does not coincide with integer-valued $0$. You therefore need both a scale $s$ and a zero-point $z$:

$$q = \mathrm{round}(x / s) + z, \qquad \hat{x} = s \cdot (q - z),$$

where $s = (b - a)/(2^n - 1)$ and $z = \mathrm{round}(-a / s)$. The zero-point $z$ records where the real number $0$ lands in the integer grid, so that the original $0$ can be recovered exactly after dequantization. This is important because activations often pass through non-linearities like ReLU, which produce strictly non-negative outputs in $[0, Q_b]$, and the original BitNet scaled activations into exactly that range before such non-linearities. Asymmetric quantization (with a non-zero zero-point) was therefore natural there.

The catch is that the zero-point makes every downstream matrix multiplication more expensive. With asymmetric quantization, computing $Y = W X$ in quantized form becomes

$$Y = s_w s_x \, (W_q - z_w)(X_q - z_x),$$

which expands into terms involving $z_w$ and $z_x$ that must be subtracted and accumulated separately. On hardware this means extra addition/subtraction logic, extra memory traffic to carry the per-tensor (or per-token) zero-points, and more complex kernel implementations — exactly the kinds of "system-level optimization" headaches the paper wants to avoid.

In BitNet b1.58, the authors instead scale activations to the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token. Because the range is symmetric around $0$, the zero-point is simply $0$ and can be dropped entirely. The quantized matmul reduces to

$$Y = s_w s_x \, W_q X_q,$$

with no offset correction terms. This is what the paper means by "get rid of the zero-point quantization."

The reason this works particularly well for BitNet b1.58 is architectural: the model uses LLaMA-style components, including RMSNorm and SwiGLU, rather than the ReLU-based design of the original BitNet. RMSNorm outputs are not restricted to be positive, and SwiGLU (which multiplies a SiLU-gated branch by another linear projection) also produces signed values. So activations naturally live on both sides of $0$, and a symmetric range is a good fit rather than a wasteful one. Per-token scaling further adapts the range to each token's actual magnitude, so very little representational capacity is wasted on the "wrong" side of $0$.

The trade-off, then, is essentially dynamic range efficiency versus implementation simplicity. With a strictly non-negative activation distribution (as after a plain ReLU), symmetric quantization to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ would waste roughly half of the integer codes on values that never occur, which is exactly why asymmetric quantization with a zero-point was introduced historically. For BitNet b1.58, where activations are signed and where the dominant cost is now system-level efficiency (kernel simplicity, hardware-friendliness, low latency) rather than squeezing out the last bit of representational precision, dropping the zero-point is a clear win. The authors report that the impact on perplexity and downstream task performance is negligible, consistent with the fact that the model is trained from scratch with quantization-aware training, so the weights learn to compensate for any small loss of activation resolution.

In short: the zero-point is the offset you need when your quantization range is not centered at $0$; switching to a symmetric $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ range makes that offset $0$, simplifying the math and the kernels at the cost of a small (here, negligible) loss of quantization efficiency for asymmetric distributions.

### Are the claimed efficiency equivalences (e.g., 13B BitNet vs 3B FP16) based on real measurements or extrapolation?
Question:
The paper claims equivalences like "13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient, in terms of latency, memory usage and energy consumption, than 3B FP16 LLM" and similar statements for other size pairs. I'm trying to understand whether these are direct measurements or extrapolations. The paper mentions evaluating models at 7B, 13B, and 70B sizes and reporting trends in Figure 1 and 2. Were these specific cross-size comparisons (e.g., 13B ternary vs 3B FP16) actually measured, or are they inferred from the trend lines? Also, does the memory comparison account for the embedding layer, which the paper notes "remains full precision"?
Answer:
# Answer

The short answer is that these equivalences appear to be **inferences from trend lines** rather than direct cross-size measurements, and the paper gives you enough information to tell which is which.

## Which sizes were actually measured?

The paper states that models were evaluated at specific sizes: BitNet b1.58 at 3B, 3.9B, 7B, 13B, and 70B, and FP16 LLaMA at 3B, 7B, 13B, and 70B. Latency and memory were **directly measured** using the FasterTransformer codebase with a 2-bit kernel integrated from Ladder. So for sizes like 13B BitNet vs. 3B FP16, or 70B BitNet vs. 13B FP16, both endpoints were indeed measured.

However, the paper frames the equivalences as being "based on the results in Figure 1 and 2," which it describes as illustrating **trends** of latency, memory, and energy. This phrasing, combined with the fact that the paper highlights how speed-up ratios *change* with model size, suggests the specific cross-size equivalences are derived from interpolating or reading off these trend curves rather than being presented as standalone direct comparisons. Notably, the 30B BitNet vs. 7B FP16 equivalence involves a model size (30B) that was **not** among the evaluated sizes, so that particular claim must be an interpolation from the trend lines.

## Energy is explicitly an estimate

For energy, the paper uses the word "estimate" directly: "We also **estimate** the arithmetic operations energy consumption." It relies on an energy model from cited references (~\cite{energycost, pokebnn}) for arithmetic operation costs on 7nm chips rather than wall-clock power measurement. So the energy component of the equivalences is a modeled estimate, not a direct measurement, even where the corresponding model sizes were evaluated.

## Does the memory comparison include the embedding layer?

Yes, the embedding layer is included in the memory figures, and the paper explicitly accounts for it. In the "Memory and Latency" paragraph, the paper states: "the embedding remains full precision and its memory proportion is smaller for larger models." This is offered as an *explanation* for why the memory savings ratio increases with model size. The intuition is straightforward: the embedding parameters are full-precision (FP16) and scale with vocabulary size, while the bulk of the model (the linear layers) is ternary. As models grow larger, the ternary linear layers dominate the parameter count, so the fixed-cost full-precision embeddings become a smaller fraction of total memory. This means the *relative* memory advantage of BitNet b1.58 improves as model size increases. The paper is transparent that embeddings are not quantized and that this affects the scaling trend—it is not hidden in the measurements.

## Summary

The latency and memory numbers at specific model sizes (7B, 13B, 70B) are real measurements. The specific size-pair equivalences listed (e.g., 13B ternary vs. 3B FP16) appear to be derived from the trend lines in Figures 1 and 2, with at least one pair (30B vs. 7B) necessarily being an interpolation since 30B was not measured. The energy figures are modeled estimates based on published per-operation energy costs, not direct measurements. The memory comparison does account for the full-precision embedding layer, and the paper uses it to explain the scaling trend in memory savings.

### Why does BitNet b1.58 use LLaMA-alike components, and does this affect the fairness of comparison?
Question:
The paper mentions that BitNet b1.58 adopts LLaMA-alike components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding, no biases) and compares against a "reproduced FP16 LLaMA LLM." I'm wondering: was the original BitNet architecture different from LLaMA, and if so, does adopting LLaMA components conflate the architectural improvements with the quantization benefits? If the FP16 baseline is also LLaMA-based, then the comparison seems fair for isolating the effect of 1.58-bit quantization. But is there any possibility that some of the performance gains come from the architectural choices rather than the ternary weights? Also, is the "reproduced FP16 LLaMA" a well-validated baseline, or could there be training inefficiencies in the reproduction that make it an unfairly weak baseline?
Answer:
The short answer is that the comparison is designed to isolate the effect of ternary quantization, and both the original BitNet and the FP16 baseline share the same LLaMA-alike architecture, so the architectural variable is controlled for.

Regarding whether the original BitNet architecture differed from LLaMA, the paper describes BitNet as "a Transformer that replaces nn.Linear with BitLinear." The original BitNet did not necessarily use LLaMA-specific components like RMSNorm, SwiGLU, or rotary embeddings. In BitNet b1.58, the authors explicitly adopted these components, stating their motivation is to "embrace the open-source community" so that the model "can be integrated into the popular open-source software (e.g., Huggingface, vLLM, and llama.cpp) with minimal efforts." This is an engineering and adoption decision, not an attempt to gain performance through architectural changes.

On the question of fairness, the key point is that the FP16 baseline is also a LLaMA-alike model. The paper says it compared BitNet b1.58 "to our reproduced FP16 LLaMA LLM in various sizes" and that "to ensure a fair comparison, we pre-trained the models on the RedPajama dataset for 100 billion tokens." Since both models use the same architecture (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, no biases) and the same training data and token budget, the only systematic difference is whether the linear layers use ternary weights via BitLinear or standard FP16 weights. This means the architectural improvements you mention cannot be confounded with the quantization benefits, because both sides of the comparison share them equally.

Your concern about whether the reproduced FP16 LLaMA is a well-validated baseline is reasonable and is the one place where some skepticism is warranted. The paper does not provide a direct comparison between its reproduced FP16 LLaMA and the officially released LLaMA models from Meta, so there is no explicit calibration showing that the reproduction matches the performance of the original. However, there are a few points that partially address this concern. First, the comparison is internal and controlled—both models are trained from scratch under identical conditions (same data, same token count, same model sizes), so even if the absolute performance of the baseline is somewhat below what Meta achieved with the official LLaMA, the relative comparison between FP16 and $1.58$-bit remains informative about the cost of quantization. Second, the paper also includes a comparison against StableLM-3B at 2T tokens, which is a state-of-the-art independently developed model, and BitNet b1.58 outperforms it on all evaluated end tasks. This provides some external validation that the BitNet b1.58 results are competitive with real-world baselines, not just with a potentially weak internal reproduction.

In summary, the architectural choices are held constant across both the ternary and FP16 models, so the comparison does isolate the effect of $1.58$-bit quantization. The main residual uncertainty is whether the reproduced FP16 LLaMA baseline is as strong as it could be, but the internal consistency of the experimental setup and the external comparison to StableLM-3B provide reasonable evidence that the conclusions are not artifacts of a weak baseline.
