\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why does BitNet b1.58 only match FP16 LLaMA starting at 3B parameters?
Question:
The results show that BitNet b1.58 matches the perplexity and zero-shot performance of FP16 LLaMA only starting from a 3B model size. For smaller models, there appears to be a performance gap. I'm wondering what causes this threshold. Is there something fundamental about model capacity that makes ternary weights insufficient for small models but adequate for larger ones? The paper mentions a "new scaling law" — does this imply that 1.58-bit models scale more favorably than FP16 models as size increases? If so, what is the theoretical explanation for why having fewer bits per weight hurts less at larger scales?
Answer:
The key to understanding this threshold lies in the relationship between model over-parameterization and the information loss from quantization. When you reduce each weight from 16 bits to 1.58 bits (ternary values $\{-1, 0, 1\}$), you lose roughly $10\times$ the information per parameter. Whether this loss matters depends critically on how much "slack" or redundancy the model has.

**The redundancy argument** is central here. Small models are more tightly parameterized—each weight carries a larger share of the model's total information, and there is less redundancy to compensate for quantization error. When you have only hundreds of millions of parameters, forcing each into one of three values degrades a significant fraction of the information the network needs. In contrast, large models are heavily over-parameterized: many weights are somewhat redundant, and the network can route around the constraints of ternary values by adjusting other weights during training. The extra capacity allows the model to find good solutions within the restricted weight space.

**The scaling law perspective** explains why the gap narrows and eventually closes. Standard neural scaling laws describe loss as decreasing roughly as a power law with model size, i.e., $L(N) \propto N^{-\alpha}$. The paper's claim of a "new scaling law" suggests that 1.58-bit models follow a scaling curve that is steeper—meaning their performance improves faster as a function of size—converging with the FP16 curve at around 3B parameters. Intuitively, the degradation from quantization grows much slower than the benefit of adding more parameters. The quantization penalty is roughly proportional to the number of parameters (you lose information per weight), but the modeling benefit of scale grows as a power law. At some crossover point, the power-law improvement from additional capacity overtakes the linear penalty from reduced precision, and that crossover empirically lands near 3B for this architecture.

**The role of ternary values** (including $0$) also matters. The paper notes that the inclusion of $0$ enables "explicit support for feature filtering," introducing a form of sparsity. In larger models, the ability to selectively zero out connections becomes increasingly useful—larger networks benefit more from structured sparsity because they have more features to selectively route through. This may contribute to the steeper scaling of the 1.58-bit curve: the ternary representation isn't just a lossy compression but also an inductive bias that becomes more advantageous at scale.

It's worth noting that this is not unique to BitNet—similar phenomena appear in post-training quantization literature, where smaller models suffer larger accuracy drops from quantization. The difference is that BitNet trains from scratch with ternary constraints, which mitigates but does not eliminate the small-model penalty. The fundamental tradeoff between precision and parameter count remains, and 3B appears to be the empirical point where the scaling advantage of additional parameters fully compensates for the per-weight information loss.
