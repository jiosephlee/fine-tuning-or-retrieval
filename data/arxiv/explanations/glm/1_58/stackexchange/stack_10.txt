\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why does BitNet b1.58 use LLaMA-alike components, and does this affect the fairness of comparison?
Question:
The paper mentions that BitNet b1.58 adopts LLaMA-alike components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding, no biases) and compares against a "reproduced FP16 LLaMA LLM." I'm wondering: was the original BitNet architecture different from LLaMA, and if so, does adopting LLaMA components conflate the architectural improvements with the quantization benefits? If the FP16 baseline is also LLaMA-based, then the comparison seems fair for isolating the effect of 1.58-bit quantization. But is there any possibility that some of the performance gains come from the architectural choices rather than the ternary weights? Also, is the "reproduced FP16 LLaMA" a well-validated baseline, or could there be training inefficiencies in the reproduction that make it an unfairly weak baseline?
Answer:
The short answer is that the comparison is designed to isolate the effect of ternary quantization, and both the original BitNet and the FP16 baseline share the same LLaMA-alike architecture, so the architectural variable is controlled for.

Regarding whether the original BitNet architecture differed from LLaMA, the paper describes BitNet as "a Transformer that replaces nn.Linear with BitLinear." The original BitNet did not necessarily use LLaMA-specific components like RMSNorm, SwiGLU, or rotary embeddings. In BitNet b1.58, the authors explicitly adopted these components, stating their motivation is to "embrace the open-source community" so that the model "can be integrated into the popular open-source software (e.g., Huggingface, vLLM, and llama.cpp) with minimal efforts." This is an engineering and adoption decision, not an attempt to gain performance through architectural changes.

On the question of fairness, the key point is that the FP16 baseline is also a LLaMA-alike model. The paper says it compared BitNet b1.58 "to our reproduced FP16 LLaMA LLM in various sizes" and that "to ensure a fair comparison, we pre-trained the models on the RedPajama dataset for 100 billion tokens." Since both models use the same architecture (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, no biases) and the same training data and token budget, the only systematic difference is whether the linear layers use ternary weights via BitLinear or standard FP16 weights. This means the architectural improvements you mention cannot be confounded with the quantization benefits, because both sides of the comparison share them equally.

Your concern about whether the reproduced FP16 LLaMA is a well-validated baseline is reasonable and is the one place where some skepticism is warranted. The paper does not provide a direct comparison between its reproduced FP16 LLaMA and the officially released LLaMA models from Meta, so there is no explicit calibration showing that the reproduction matches the performance of the original. However, there are a few points that partially address this concern. First, the comparison is internal and controlled—both models are trained from scratch under identical conditions (same data, same token count, same model sizes), so even if the absolute performance of the baseline is somewhat below what Meta achieved with the official LLaMA, the relative comparison between FP16 and $1.58$-bit remains informative about the cost of quantization. Second, the paper also includes a comparison against StableLM-3B at 2T tokens, which is a state-of-the-art independently developed model, and BitNet b1.58 outperforms it on all evaluated end tasks. This provides some external validation that the BitNet b1.58 results are competitive with real-world baselines, not just with a potentially weak internal reproduction.

In summary, the architectural choices are held constant across both the ternary and FP16 models, so the comparison does isolate the effect of $1.58$-bit quantization. The main residual uncertainty is whether the reproduced FP16 LLaMA baseline is as strong as it could be, but the internal consistency of the experimental setup and the external comparison to StableLM-3B provide reasonable evidence that the conclusions are not artifacts of a weak baseline.
