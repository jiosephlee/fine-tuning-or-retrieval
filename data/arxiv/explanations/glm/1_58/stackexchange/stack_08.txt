\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### What does "zero-point quantization" mean and why was it removed for activations?
Question:
The paper states that for activation quantization, unlike the original BitNet, they "do not scale the activations before the non-linear functions to the range $[0, Q_b]$. Instead, the activations are all scaled to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token to get rid of the zero-point quantization." I'm not familiar with zero-point quantization. What is it, and why does scaling to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ instead of $[0, Q_b]$ eliminate it? The paper says this is "more convenient and simple for both implementation and system-level optimization, while introduces negligible effects to the performance." Could someone explain the trade-off here and why this change was made?
Answer:
In quantization, a "zero-point" is an integer offset used in **asymmetric quantization** to handle value ranges that are not centered at $0$. When you quantize a tensor whose range is $[a, b]$ (with $a \neq -b$) to $n$-bit integers $[0, 2^n - 1]$, the mapping cannot be a simple scale factor, because real-valued $0$ does not coincide with integer-valued $0$. You therefore need both a scale $s$ and a zero-point $z$:

$$q = \mathrm{round}(x / s) + z, \qquad \hat{x} = s \cdot (q - z),$$

where $s = (b - a)/(2^n - 1)$ and $z = \mathrm{round}(-a / s)$. The zero-point $z$ records where the real number $0$ lands in the integer grid, so that the original $0$ can be recovered exactly after dequantization. This is important because activations often pass through non-linearities like ReLU, which produce strictly non-negative outputs in $[0, Q_b]$, and the original BitNet scaled activations into exactly that range before such non-linearities. Asymmetric quantization (with a non-zero zero-point) was therefore natural there.

The catch is that the zero-point makes every downstream matrix multiplication more expensive. With asymmetric quantization, computing $Y = W X$ in quantized form becomes

$$Y = s_w s_x \, (W_q - z_w)(X_q - z_x),$$

which expands into terms involving $z_w$ and $z_x$ that must be subtracted and accumulated separately. On hardware this means extra addition/subtraction logic, extra memory traffic to carry the per-tensor (or per-token) zero-points, and more complex kernel implementations — exactly the kinds of "system-level optimization" headaches the paper wants to avoid.

In BitNet b1.58, the authors instead scale activations to the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token. Because the range is symmetric around $0$, the zero-point is simply $0$ and can be dropped entirely. The quantized matmul reduces to

$$Y = s_w s_x \, W_q X_q,$$

with no offset correction terms. This is what the paper means by "get rid of the zero-point quantization."

The reason this works particularly well for BitNet b1.58 is architectural: the model uses LLaMA-style components, including RMSNorm and SwiGLU, rather than the ReLU-based design of the original BitNet. RMSNorm outputs are not restricted to be positive, and SwiGLU (which multiplies a SiLU-gated branch by another linear projection) also produces signed values. So activations naturally live on both sides of $0$, and a symmetric range is a good fit rather than a wasteful one. Per-token scaling further adapts the range to each token's actual magnitude, so very little representational capacity is wasted on the "wrong" side of $0$.

The trade-off, then, is essentially dynamic range efficiency versus implementation simplicity. With a strictly non-negative activation distribution (as after a plain ReLU), symmetric quantization to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ would waste roughly half of the integer codes on values that never occur, which is exactly why asymmetric quantization with a zero-point was introduced historically. For BitNet b1.58, where activations are signed and where the dominant cost is now system-level efficiency (kernel simplicity, hardware-friendliness, low latency) rather than squeezing out the last bit of representational precision, dropping the zero-point is a clear win. The authors report that the impact on perplexity and downstream task performance is negligible, consistent with the fact that the model is trained from scratch with quantization-aware training, so the weights learn to compensate for any small loss of activation resolution.

In short: the zero-point is the offset you need when your quantization range is not centered at $0$; switching to a symmetric $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ range makes that offset $0$, simplifying the math and the kernels at the cost of a small (here, negligible) loss of quantization efficiency for asymmetric distributions.
