\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Is BitNet b1.58 a form of post-training quantization, and if not, why does it matter?
Question:
The paper distinguishes BitNet b1.58 from post-training quantization methods like GPTQ and AWQ, stating that BitNet is "trained from scratch" with 1.58-bit weights. I'm confused about why training from scratch with quantized weights is better than quantizing after training. Intuitively, I would think a model trained with full precision would learn better representations, and then you could compress those representations afterward. Why does training with ternary weights from the start avoid the "sub-optimal" nature of post-training quantization that the paper mentions? Does the model learn fundamentally different representations when constrained to \{-1, 0, 1\} from the beginning?
Answer:
The key distinction is that post-training quantization (PTQ) applies quantization as an afterthought to a model that was optimized under completely different assumptions, whereas BitNet b1.58 bakes the quantization constraint directly into the training objective. This matters because the optimization process can *adapt* to the constraint, rather than having errors imposed on it retroactively.

When you train a model in FP16 and then quantize afterward using methods like GPTQ or AWQ, the full-precision model has learned a weight distribution that was never optimized for the target bit width. The quantization function then maps these weights to low-bit values, introducing errors that the model has no opportunity to correct. PTQ methods attempt to compensate for this — GPTQ uses second-order information to minimize the quantization error, for instance — but they are fundamentally working within a model whose internal representations were shaped by full-precision arithmetic. At aggressive bit widths like $1.58$ bits, these errors become large enough that post-hoc compensation is insufficient, which is why the paper calls PTQ "sub-optimal."

BitNet b1.58, by contrast, applies the `absmean` quantization function during the forward pass of training itself. The full-precision weights are quantized to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ on-the-fly using $\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$, and the model sees only these ternary weights during the forward computation. Gradients flow back through this quantization operation (using a straight-through estimator, as in the original BitNet), allowing the optimizer to adjust the underlying full-precision weights so that they produce *good* ternary values after quantization. In other words, the model learns a weight distribution that is explicitly aligned with the quantization function, rather than one that merely happens to be quantizable with some residual error.

The user's intuition that a full-precision model would learn "better representations" is reasonable in isolation, but it misses a crucial point: the quality of representations only matters insofar as they survive the deployment format. A full-precision model may learn richer representations, but if those representations degrade significantly under aggressive quantization, the deployed model is worse than one that learned slightly less rich representations natively within the ternary constraint. The paper's results support this — BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 LLaMA at 3B and above, demonstrating that the model can learn sufficiently expressive representations even with ternary weights, provided it is trained to do so from the start.

The model does appear to learn fundamentally different representations. The paper notes that the inclusion of $0$ — absent in the original $1$-bit BitNet — enables "explicit support for feature filtering," meaning the model can learn to route information by setting weights to zero. This is a structurally different representational strategy from a full-precision model, where near-zero weights carry gradient information and contribute marginally to computations. In BitNet b1.58, a zero weight is a hard on/off decision, and the model learns to use this discreteness productively during training.

The broader principle here is well-established in the quantization literature: quantization-aware training (QAT) generally outperforms PTQ, and the gap widens at lower bit widths. BitNet b1.58 can be understood as taking QAT to its logical extreme — rather than simulating quantization during training and dequantizing for computation, the model computes natively in the quantized space. The paper's claim of a "new scaling law" reflects the fact that the optimal trade-off between model size, training tokens, and precision is different when you commit to low-bit weights from the outset, rather than treating quantization as a compression step applied to an already-trained model.
