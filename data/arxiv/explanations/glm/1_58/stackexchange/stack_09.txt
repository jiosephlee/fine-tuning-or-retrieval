\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Are the claimed efficiency equivalences (e.g., 13B BitNet vs 3B FP16) based on real measurements or extrapolation?
Question:
The paper claims equivalences like "13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient, in terms of latency, memory usage and energy consumption, than 3B FP16 LLM" and similar statements for other size pairs. I'm trying to understand whether these are direct measurements or extrapolations. The paper mentions evaluating models at 7B, 13B, and 70B sizes and reporting trends in Figure 1 and 2. Were these specific cross-size comparisons (e.g., 13B ternary vs 3B FP16) actually measured, or are they inferred from the trend lines? Also, does the memory comparison account for the embedding layer, which the paper notes "remains full precision"?
Answer:
# Answer

The short answer is that these equivalences appear to be **inferences from trend lines** rather than direct cross-size measurements, and the paper gives you enough information to tell which is which.

## Which sizes were actually measured?

The paper states that models were evaluated at specific sizes: BitNet b1.58 at 3B, 3.9B, 7B, 13B, and 70B, and FP16 LLaMA at 3B, 7B, 13B, and 70B. Latency and memory were **directly measured** using the FasterTransformer codebase with a 2-bit kernel integrated from Ladder. So for sizes like 13B BitNet vs. 3B FP16, or 70B BitNet vs. 13B FP16, both endpoints were indeed measured.

However, the paper frames the equivalences as being "based on the results in Figure 1 and 2," which it describes as illustrating **trends** of latency, memory, and energy. This phrasing, combined with the fact that the paper highlights how speed-up ratios *change* with model size, suggests the specific cross-size equivalences are derived from interpolating or reading off these trend curves rather than being presented as standalone direct comparisons. Notably, the 30B BitNet vs. 7B FP16 equivalence involves a model size (30B) that was **not** among the evaluated sizes, so that particular claim must be an interpolation from the trend lines.

## Energy is explicitly an estimate

For energy, the paper uses the word "estimate" directly: "We also **estimate** the arithmetic operations energy consumption." It relies on an energy model from cited references (~\cite{energycost, pokebnn}) for arithmetic operation costs on 7nm chips rather than wall-clock power measurement. So the energy component of the equivalences is a modeled estimate, not a direct measurement, even where the corresponding model sizes were evaluated.

## Does the memory comparison include the embedding layer?

Yes, the embedding layer is included in the memory figures, and the paper explicitly accounts for it. In the "Memory and Latency" paragraph, the paper states: "the embedding remains full precision and its memory proportion is smaller for larger models." This is offered as an *explanation* for why the memory savings ratio increases with model size. The intuition is straightforward: the embedding parameters are full-precision (FP16) and scale with vocabulary size, while the bulk of the model (the linear layers) is ternary. As models grow larger, the ternary linear layers dominate the parameter count, so the fixed-cost full-precision embeddings become a smaller fraction of total memory. This means the *relative* memory advantage of BitNet b1.58 improves as model size increases. The paper is transparent that embeddings are not quantized and that this affects the scaling trend—it is not hidden in the measurements.

## Summary

The latency and memory numbers at specific model sizes (7B, 13B, 70B) are real measurements. The specific size-pair equivalences listed (e.g., 13B ternary vs. 3B FP16) appear to be derived from the trend lines in Figures 1 and 2, with at least one pair (30B vs. 7B) necessarily being an interpolation since 30B was not measured. The energy figures are modeled estimates based on published per-operation energy costs, not direct measurements. The memory comparison does account for the full-precision embedding layer, and the paper uses it to explain the scaling trend in memory savings.
