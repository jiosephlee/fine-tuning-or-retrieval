# Beyond Zeros and Ones: Understanding the 1.58-Bit LLM Revolution

Large language models keep getting bigger, but our hardware budgets don't. While the industry has been squeezing models into 4-bit quantization schemes as a post-training afterthought, a new paper from Microsoft Research asks a more radical question: what if we train models from scratch to operate on just three values?

The answer is BitNet b1.58, and it might change how we think about LLM computation entirely.

## What Does "1.58 Bits" Actually Mean?

When we say a model uses 1-bit weights, we typically mean each parameter is either 0 or 1. BitNet b1.58 goes a step further by making every weight ternary: each parameter takes one of three values: $\{-1, 0, 1\}$.

Here's where the 1.58 comes from. In information theory, the number of bits needed to represent a value with $N$ possible states is $\log_2(N)$. For ternary values where $N = 3$:

$$\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$$

So "1.58 bits" is simply the information-theoretic cost of storing one of three possible states. It's a clever naming convention that signals this model sits between traditional 1-bit binary systems and 2-bit systems, while taking advantage of the additional expressiveness that a third value provides.

## Why Add Zero to Binary Weights?

The original BitNet used binary weights of $\{-1, 1\}$. BitNet b1.58 introduces 0 as a third option, and this isn't just a minor tweak. The inclusion of 0 provides explicit support for feature filtering. When a weight is 0, that connection is effectively pruned, allowing the model to selectively ignore certain features rather than always applying either a positive or negative influence.

This matters for performance. The paper shows that BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision (FP16) Transformer LLMs in both perplexity and downstream task performance starting at around 3B parameters, using the same training token budget. The original binary BitNet couldn't quite close that gap.

## The Math: From Multiplication to Addition

To understand why 1.58-bit models are interesting from a hardware perspective, we need to look at what actually happens during matrix multiplication in a standard Transformer.

In a conventional FP16 LLM, the bulk of computation comes from floating-point addition and multiplication. Matrix multiplication between weights $W$ and activations $x$ involves computing $Wx$, which requires multiplying pairs of 16-bit floats and adding the results.

BitNet b1.58 changes this fundamentally. Since weights are constrained to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, multiplying by a weight becomes trivial:

- Multiply by $1$: keep the value as-is
- Multiply by $-1$: negate the value
- Multiply by $0$: zero it out

No floating-point multiplication is needed for the weight-activation products. The entire matrix multiplication reduces to integer addition operations on the 8-bit activations. As the paper notes, this is a new computation paradigm where "almost no multiplication operations" are required.

## How Weights Become Ternary

BitNet b1.58 uses an `absmean` quantization function to convert trained weights into ternary values during training. The process works as follows.

First, compute the average absolute value of the weight matrix:

$$\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$$

Then scale and round each weight to the nearest integer in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$:

$$\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$$

where $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ clamps values to the range $[-1, 1]$ and rounds to the nearest integer. This means weights near zero become 0, weights significantly positive become 1, and weights significantly negative become $-1$.

The activations are quantized to 8-bit integers using per-token scaling to the range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, without zero-point quantization. This simplifies implementation while having negligible impact on performance.

## The Numbers: Speed, Memory, and Energy

The paper provides concrete comparisons between BitNet b1.58 and a reproduced FP16 LLaMA model, both trained on 100 billion tokens from the RedPajama dataset.

At 3B parameters, BitNet b1.58 matches LLaMA's perplexity while being 2.71× faster and using 3.55× less GPU memory. At 3.9B parameters, BitNet b1.58 actually outperforms LLaMA 3B on end tasks while still being faster and more memory-efficient.

The advantages scale with model size. At 70B parameters, BitNet b1.58 is 4.1× faster than the FP16 baseline. On two 80GB A100 GPUs, the 70B BitNet b1.58 supports 11× the batch size of LLaMA 70B, delivering 8.9× higher throughput.

Energy consumption tells an even more dramatic story. Since matrix multiplication in BitNet b1.58 consists primarily of INT8 addition rather than FP16 addition and multiplication, the paper estimates 71.4× lower arithmetic operation energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips.

## A New Scaling Law

One of the most interesting findings is that 1.58-bit models redefine the cost-performance tradeoff. The paper establishes these equivalences:

- A 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 3B FP16 LLM
- A 30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 7B FP16 LLM  
- A 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 13B FP16 LLM

In other words, you can run a model 4× larger in 1.58-bit precision while still being cheaper to serve than a smaller FP16 model. This opens up a different scaling strategy where model capacity can grow without proportionally growing inference cost.

## LLaMA Compatibility

An important practical detail: BitNet b1.58 adopts LLaMA-like architectural components, including RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and no biases. This means it can integrate with popular open-source tools like Hugging Face, vLLM, and llama.cpp with minimal effort. The researchers clearly designed this with the existing ecosystem in mind.

## Why This Matters

Post-training quantization to 4-bit has become standard practice, but it's fundamentally a compromise. You train in full precision, then compress afterward, accepting some accuracy loss. BitNet b1.58 takes a different approach: train with quantization baked in from the start, so the model learns to work within the ternary constraint.

The results suggest this approach works. When trained on 2 trillion tokens following the StableLM-3B recipe, BitNet b1.58 outperforms the state-of-the-art open-source 3B model on all evaluated tasks. The ternary constraint doesn't seem to limit generalization, at least not at this scale.

The paper also hints at future directions that could further amplify these gains. Mixture-of-Experts models, which are currently limited by memory and inter-chip communication overhead, could benefit enormously from reduced parameter size. Long-context applications, currently bottlenecked by KV cache memory, could effectively double their context length thanks to 8-bit activations. And edge devices, constrained by memory and compute, become viable targets for models that need neither multiplication nor high-precision storage.

Perhaps most provocatively, the authors call for new hardware designed specifically for 1-bit LLMs. Current GPUs and accelerators are optimized for floating-point operations. If the future of LLMs involves mostly integer addition over ternary weights, the optimal hardware looks quite different from what we have today.

Whether or not 1.58-bit models become the dominant paradigm, BitNet b1.58 makes a compelling case that the era of treating quantization as an afterthought may be coming to an end.



# Smaller, Faster, Just as Smart: How BitNet b1.58 Matches Full-Precision AI

As large language models (LLMs) continue to grow in size, their appetite for memory, compute, and energy has become a major bottleneck. Traditional models rely on 16-bit floating-point weights (FP16 or BF16), meaning a massive amount of the computation and memory transfer is spent on floating-point addition and multiplication. But what if we could drastically cut that down without losing any of the model's smarts?

Enter BitNet b1.58, a new 1-bit LLM variant where every single parameter is constrained to just three values: -1, 0, and 1. In the binary system, this ternary setup translates to 1.58 bits. The most exciting part? It matches the performance of full-precision FP16 models like LLaMA, starting at just 3 billion parameters, while being significantly faster and cheaper to run.

## The 1.58-Bit Innovation: Why Zero Matters

The original BitNet architecture introduced 1-bit weights, but BitNet b1.58 makes a crucial modification by adding the value 0 to the mix. While moving from binary $\{-1, 1\}$ to ternary $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ might seem like a small step, it has a profound impact on the model's capabilities.

The inclusion of 0 provides explicit support for feature filtering. When a weight is 0, the model effectively ignores that particular feature, simplifying the computation and improving the model's predictive strength. This gives BitNet b1.58 a stronger modeling capacity compared to the original 1-bit BitNet, allowing it to match full-precision baselines in both perplexity and end-task performance.

## Inside the Quantization Process

To achieve these ternary weights, BitNet b1.58 uses an \emph{absmean} quantization function. The process works by first scaling the weight matrix by its average absolute value. Then, each value is rounded to the nearest integer among the set $\{-1, 0, +1\}$.

Mathematically, the quantized weight $\widetilde{W}$ is computed as:

$$
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)
$$

Here, the $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ function is defined as:

$$
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x)))
$$

And the scaling factor $\gamma$ is the average of the absolute values of the weights:

$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|
$$

For activations, the model uses an 8-bit quantization approach. Instead of scaling activations to a range of $[0, Q_b]$ before non-linear functions, BitNet b1.58 scales them to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token. This removes the need for zero-point quantization, making the implementation simpler and more efficient with negligible impact on performance.

## A LLaMA-like Backbone for Easy Adoption

To make the model easy to integrate into the open-source ecosystem, the authors designed BitNet b1.58 to closely resemble the popular LLaMA architecture. It replaces standard linear layers with \emph{BitLinear} layers and adopts several key LLaMA components:

- RMSNorm for normalization
- SwiGLU for the activation function
- Rotary embeddings
- Removal of all biases

This thoughtful design choice means BitNet b1.58 can be integrated into popular open-source software like Huggingface, vLLM, and llama.cpp with minimal effort.

## Proving It: Benchmark Results

The paper provides compelling evidence that 1.58-bit models can stand toe-to-toe with their FP16 counterparts. The researchers compared BitNet b1.58 against a reproduced FP16 LLaMA LLM, pre-training both on the RedPajama dataset for 100 billion tokens.

The results are striking. BitNet b1.58 matches the full-precision LLaMA LLM in terms of perplexity starting at a 3B model size. When scaled slightly to 3.9B parameters, BitNet b1.58 actually outperforms the LLaMA LLM 3B model in zero-shot accuracy across a range of language tasks, including ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, and BoolQ. Crucially, it achieves this while being 2.4 times faster and consuming 3.32 times less memory.

The efficiency gap only widens as the models scale up. At 70B parameters, BitNet b1.58 is 4.1 times faster than the FP16 LLaMA baseline. Because the model only uses integer addition for matrix multiplication—eliminating costly floating-point operations—it saves massive amounts of energy. The authors estimate that BitNet b1.58 reduces arithmetic operation energy consumption by a factor of 71.4 on 7nm chips.

## A New Scaling Law

BitNet b1.58 proves that we don't need to rely on heavy floating-point computations to achieve state-of-the-art LLM performance. By embracing 1.58-bit ternary weights and clever feature filtering, we can build models that are just as smart but dramatically faster, smaller, and more energy-efficient. This presents a new scaling law for LLMs, one where efficiency and performance grow hand-in-hand, opening the door for a new generation of hardware specifically optimized for 1-bit computation.



# Slashing the AI Energy Bill: The Massive Efficiency Gains of 1.58-Bit Models

As large language models grow ever larger, their environmental and economic costs have become impossible to ignore. Training and deploying these models requires massive amounts of memory, bandwidth, and energy—primarily driven by the floating-point multiplication operations at the heart of matrix multiplication. But what if we could dramatically cut these costs without sacrificing performance?

A recent paper introducing **BitNet b1.58** demonstrates that we can. By constraining every model weight to just three values—$\{-1, 0, 1\}$—the authors achieve performance matching full-precision (FP16) models while slashing memory usage, latency, and energy consumption by orders of magnitude. Let's break down what makes this approach so efficient and what it means for sustainable AI deployment.

## The Core Idea: Ternary Weights

Traditional LLMs use 16-bit floating-point values (FP16 or BF16) for every parameter. BitNet b1.58 takes a radically different approach: every weight is constrained to one of three values: $-1$, $0$, or $+1$. In the binary system, this requires $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$ bits per weight—hence the name.

The quantization uses an **absmean** function that scales the weight matrix by its average absolute value, then rounds each value to the nearest integer in $\{-1, 0, +1\}$:

$$\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$$

where $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$ and $\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x)))$.

The inclusion of $0$ is a key innovation over the original 1-bit BitNet. This explicit support for feature filtering significantly improves modeling capability, enabling the ternary model to match FP16 performance.

## Why It's So Much More Efficient

The efficiency gains stem from a fundamental shift in computation. In standard LLMs, matrix multiplication involves expensive floating-point addition *and* multiplication. With ternary weights, matrix multiplication reduces to **integer addition only**—multiplying by $-1$, $0$, or $+1$ requires no multiplication operation at all.

This translates into massive savings across multiple dimensions:

### Memory: 3.55x Reduction

At the 3B parameter scale, BitNet b1.58 uses **3.55 times less GPU memory** than an equivalent FP16 LLaMA model. The memory advantage grows with model size because the embedding layers (which remain full precision) represent a smaller proportion of the total parameters in larger models.

### Latency: Up to 4.1x Faster

The speed improvements are equally dramatic:

- **3B model**: 2.71x faster than FP16 baseline
- **3.9B model**: 2.4x faster (while outperforming LLaMA 3B)
- **70B model**: 4.1x faster than FP16 baseline

The acceleration increases with model size because the cost of linear layers grows with scale, and these are precisely the operations optimized by the 1.58-bit approach.

### Energy: 71.4x Less for Arithmetic Operations

Perhaps the most striking result is energy consumption. On 7nm chips, BitNet b1.58 saves **71.4 times** the arithmetic operations energy for matrix multiplication compared to FP16 LLaMA. This is because the bulk of computation shifts from FP16 addition and multiplication to INT8 addition only.

### Throughput: 8.9x Higher

For 70B parameter models running on two 80GB A100 GPUs, BitNet b1.58 supports up to **11 times the batch size** of FP16 LLaMA, resulting in **8.9x higher throughput**.

## The New Scaling Law

These efficiency gains enable a new scaling paradigm. Based on the paper's results, here's the equivalence between 1.58-bit and 16-bit models:

- A **13B BitNet b1.58** is more efficient than a 3B FP16 LLM
- A **30B BitNet b1.58** is more efficient than a 7B FP16 LLM  
- A **70B BitNet b1.58** is more efficient than a 13B FP16 LLM

In other words, you can deploy a model 4-5x larger in parameter count while still using less memory, latency, and energy than the smaller FP16 alternative.

## Matching Full Precision Performance

The critical question: does all this efficiency come at the cost of capability? The evidence says no.

BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 LLaMA in perplexity starting at 3B parameters, trained on 100 billion tokens. On zero-shot accuracy across benchmarks including ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, and BoolQ, the performance gap narrows as model size increases—with BitNet b1.58 matching FP16 baselines from 3B onward.

When trained on 2T tokens following the StableLM-3B recipe, BitNet b1.58 **outperforms** the state-of-the-art open-source 3B model on all evaluated end tasks, demonstrating strong generalization capabilities.

## Architecture: LLaMA-Compatible Design

Importantly, BitNet b1.58 doesn't require a completely new architecture. It adopts LLaMA-alike components to ensure compatibility with the open-source ecosystem:

- **RMSNorm** for normalization
- **SwiGLU** activation
- **Rotary embeddings** for positional encoding
- **No biases** in the model

This means BitNet b1.58 can integrate into popular frameworks like Hugging Face, vLLM, and llama.cpp with minimal effort—a crucial consideration for practical adoption.

## What This Means for Deployment

The implications extend far beyond raw numbers:

**Edge and Mobile**: The reduced memory and energy requirements make it feasible to run capable LLMs on devices previously too constrained to support them. The approach is particularly CPU-friendly, which is significant given that CPUs dominate edge and mobile computing.

**Long Sequences**: By reducing activations from 16 bits to 8 bits, BitNet b1.58 can double the context length for the same resources. Future work could compress activations further to 4 bits or lower.

**Mixture-of-Experts**: The high memory consumption and inter-chip communication overhead that limit MoE deployment can be substantially reduced with 1.58-bit weights, potentially allowing entire models to fit on a single chip.

## Looking Forward

The paper's authors call for a new generation of hardware specifically optimized for 1-bit LLMs. Just as companies like Groq have built specialized LPUs for LLM inference, future hardware could be designed around the integer-addition paradigm that BitNet enables.

The current results were measured using a 2-bit kernel, meaning there's still room for optimization. Purpose-built hardware could push these efficiency gains even further.

## The Bottom Line

BitNet b1.58 demonstrates that we don't need to choose between capability and sustainability. By rethinking the fundamental representation of model weights, we can achieve full-precision performance at a fraction of the cost—less memory, faster inference, and dramatically lower energy consumption.

For developers and enterprises deploying LLMs at scale, this isn't just an incremental improvement. It's a step change in efficiency that could reshape how we think about the economics and environmental impact of AI.



# From Data Centers to Your Pocket: The Future of 1-Bit Hardware and Edge AI

For years, the rapid growth of Large Language Models (LLMs) has been synonymous with massive data centers, sky-high energy bills, and memory bottlenecks. Running a state-of-the-art model typically requires high-precision floating-point math (16-bit values) that demands specialized, power-hungry hardware. But what if we could drastically shrink the mathematical footprint of these models without sacrificing performance? 

A recent paper titled "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits" introduces a new architecture called **BitNet b1.58** that does exactly this. By constraining every single weight in the model to just three values—$\{-1, 0, 1\}$—the authors demonstrate that we can match the performance of full-precision models while unlocking massive gains in speed, memory, and energy efficiency. This shift doesn't just optimize existing data centers; it paves the way for powerful LLMs to run natively on edge and mobile devices, and it sparks a call to action for a new generation of specialized hardware.

## The Math Behind the Shrink: 1.58 Bits

Standard LLMs use 16-bit floating-point numbers (FP16 or BF16) to represent their parameters. The bulk of the computational cost in these models comes from matrix multiplication, which requires complex floating-point addition and multiplication. 

BitNet b1.58 takes a radically different approach. It forces every parameter to be ternary, taking on a value of $-1$, $0$, or $1$. In the binary system, it takes $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$ bits to represent these three possible states, hence the name "1.58-bit." 

By constraining the weights to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the heavy lifting of matrix multiplication is transformed. Multiplying by $1$ means keeping the value as is, multiplying by $-1$ simply flips the sign, and multiplying by $0$ drops the calculation entirely. Instead of power-hungry floating-point multiplications, the model only needs to perform integer additions. According to the paper, this shift saves 71.4 times the arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips compared to FP16 baselines.

## Unchaining LLMs from the Data Center

The implications of this efficiency are profound. The paper highlights that 1.58-bit LLMs dramatically reduce both memory footprint and latency. In their experiments, a 70B parameter BitNet b1.58 model was 4.1 times faster and supported 8.9 times the throughput of a standard FP16 LLaMA equivalent. The scaling laws they uncovered show that a 13B BitNet b1.58 model is actually more efficient in latency, memory, and energy than a 3B FP16 model.

These metrics aren't just about making servers run faster; they are the key to moving LLMs out of the cloud and into our pockets. The authors explicitly note that edge and mobile devices are heavily constrained by memory and computational power. However, the reduced memory and energy demands of 1.58-bit LLMs make them highly suitable for these environments. 

Furthermore, BitNet b1.58 uses 8-bit activations instead of the standard 16-bit, effectively halving the memory consumed by the KV cache. This allows the context length to double given the same resources—a critical advantage for edge devices with limited memory. Because 1.58-bit models rely primarily on integer addition rather than complex floating-point operations, they are inherently more friendly to the CPU processors that power our phones and edge devices.

## A Call to Action for Next-Generation Hardware

While BitNet b1.58 can be integrated into existing open-source software stacks with minimal effort, its true potential lies in future hardware. The paper emphasizes that the current paradigm of floating-point computation is suboptimal for 1-bit models.

Recent initiatives like Groq's Language Processing Units (LPUs) have demonstrated the potential of building specialized hardware for LLMs. The authors of the paper take this a step further, issuing a clear call to action for the tech industry: design and build new hardware systems optimized specifically for 1-bit LLMs. 

A chip designed from the ground up to handle ternary weights and integer addition could unlock unprecedented performance. The paper notes that 1-bit models could eventually allow entire massive models to be placed on a single chip, eliminating the high overhead of transferring activations across networks and reducing the number of devices required for complex architectures like Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models.

## The Road Ahead

BitNet b1.58 proves that we do not need to choose between model performance and computational efficiency. By redefining the fundamental math of large language models, the authors have established a new scaling law. As the industry answers the call to build 1-bit optimized hardware, we are likely to see a wave of powerful, native AI applications running directly on our phones and edge devices, untethered from the data center.
