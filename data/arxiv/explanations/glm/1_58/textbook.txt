# Introduction to 1-bit LLMs and Motivation

## The Scaling Challenge in Large Language Models

In recent years, the field of artificial intelligence has witnessed a rapid and transformative growth in the size and capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs). These models, built primarily on the Transformer architecture, have demonstrated remarkable performance across a wide range of natural language processing tasks, from text generation and translation to question answering and code completion. However, this impressive trajectory of scaling has come at a steep cost. As LLMs have grown from hundreds of millions to hundreds of billions of parameters, they have introduced significant challenges for deployment and raised serious concerns about their environmental and economic impact due to high energy consumption.

The core of the challenge lies in the fundamental nature of how these models are represented and computed. Vanilla LLMs operate using 16-bit floating-point values, denoted as FP16 or BF16, meaning that every single parameter in the model is stored as a 16-bit number. Since the bulk of any LLM's computation consists of matrix multiplication operations, the major computational cost arises from the floating-point addition and multiplication operations that these matrix multiplications require. Floating-point multiplication is an especially expensive operation, consuming significantly more energy and requiring more complex circuitry than simpler arithmetic operations. As models scale to billions and trillions of parameters, the sheer volume of these floating-point operations creates a computational bottleneck that limits how fast and how efficiently these models can run.

This scaling challenge is not merely an engineering inconvenience; it has profound implications for the accessibility and sustainability of AI technology. Training and deploying large models requires massive clusters of specialized hardware, such as GPUs or TPUs, which consume enormous amounts of electricity. The environmental footprint of training a single large LLM has been compared to that of several cars over their entire lifetimes. Furthermore, the economic cost of deploying such models — whether in cloud environments or on edge devices — can be prohibitive, limiting the democratization of AI technology to a small number of well-resourced organizations. These challenges motivate the search for fundamentally new approaches to representing and computing LLMs that can maintain their impressive performance while dramatically reducing their computational, memory, and energy costs.

## Limitations of Post-Training Quantization

One widely adopted approach to address the computational and memory challenges of LLMs is post-training quantization. This technique involves taking a model that has already been trained at full precision (i.e., FP16 or BF16) and reducing the precision of its weights and activations after the fact, creating what is known as a low-bit model for inference. The trend in the field has been to progressively move from 16-bit representations to lower bit-widths, such as 4-bit variants, in an effort to reduce memory usage and accelerate computation.

While post-training quantization is widely used in industry LLMs and does provide meaningful reductions in memory and computational requirements, it is fundamentally sub-optimal. The key issue is that the model was originally trained under the assumption that its weights and activations would be represented at full precision. When the precision is reduced after training, the model must contend with quantization errors — the discrepancies between the original full-precision values and their quantized approximations — that it was never trained to handle. These errors accumulate across the many layers and operations of the model, leading to degradation in performance. The model has not learned to be robust to the information loss that quantization introduces, and as a result, the quality of the model's outputs suffers, particularly as the bit-width is reduced to very low levels.

This limitation suggests that simply applying quantization after training is a compromise rather than a solution. A more principled approach would be to design models that are natively trained at low precision from the very beginning, allowing the model to learn and adapt to the constraints of low-bit representation during the training process itself. This is the key insight that motivates the development of 1-bit model architectures, which we will explore in subsequent sections.

## The Memory Bandwidth Bottleneck: DRAM to SRAM Transfer

Beyond the computational cost of floating-point arithmetic, there is another critical bottleneck in LLM inference that is often overlooked: the cost of transferring model parameters from memory to the processing units that perform the computation. During inference, model parameters must be transferred from DRAM (Dynamic Random Access Memory), where they are stored, to the memory of an on-chip accelerator such as SRAM (Static Random Access Memory), where the actual computation takes place. This transfer process can be extremely expensive in terms of both time and energy.

The reason this transfer is so costly lies in the architecture of modern computing systems. DRAM is relatively inexpensive and can store large amounts of data, but it is physically separated from the processing units and requires significant time and energy to access. SRAM, on the other hand, is much faster and more energy-efficient to access but is significantly more expensive to manufacture and thus typically available in much smaller capacities. There have been attempts to enlarge SRAM to improve throughput by reducing the frequency of DRAM-to-SRAM transfers, but this approach introduces significantly higher hardware costs, making it an uneconomical solution for large-scale LLM deployment.

This memory bandwidth bottleneck is particularly problematic for LLMs because of their enormous parameter counts. Every time a forward pass is performed, billions of parameters must be loaded from DRAM to the on-chip memory. The time spent loading these weights can dominate the total inference time, especially for large models, creating a situation where the processing units are starved for data and spend much of their time idle, waiting for the next batch of weights to arrive.

Compared to full-precision models, 1-bit LLMs have a much lower memory footprint from both a capacity and bandwidth standpoint. Since each parameter is represented by only 1 or 1.58 bits instead of 16 bits, the total amount of data that must be transferred from DRAM to SRAM is reduced by roughly an order of magnitude. This dramatic reduction in memory traffic can significantly reduce the cost and time of loading weights, leading to faster and more efficient inference. This advantage becomes increasingly significant as models scale to larger sizes, where the memory bandwidth bottleneck becomes even more pronounced.

## The Emergence of 1-bit Model Architectures (BitNet)

Given the limitations of post-training quantization and the persistent challenges of computational and memory costs, recent work on 1-bit model architectures, such as BitNet, presents a promising and fundamentally different direction for reducing the cost of LLMs while maintaining their performance. The core idea behind BitNet is to train models natively with binary weights — that is, each weight in the model takes on one of two values, typically $+1$ or $-1$ — rather than training at full precision and then quantizing.

The implications of this approach are profound. In vanilla LLMs, where weights are 16-bit floating-point values, the matrix multiplication operations that form the backbone of the model's computation require floating-point addition and multiplication. Floating-point multiplication, in particular, is a complex and energy-intensive operation that involves aligning exponents, multiplying mantissas, and normalizing the result. In contrast, when weights are constrained to $+1$ or $-1$, the matrix multiplication simplifies dramatically. Multiplying any activation value by $+1$ leaves it unchanged, and multiplying by $-1$ simply negates it. As a result, the matrix multiplication in BitNet only involves integer addition operations, completely eliminating the need for expensive multiplication. This saves orders of magnitude in energy cost for LLMs.

This energy saving has a cascading effect on performance. The fundamental limit to compute performance in many chips is power — the amount of heat that can be dissipated determines how fast the chip can operate. By dramatically reducing the energy required for each operation, BitNet effectively allows chips to operate at higher clock speeds or process more data in parallel within the same power budget. In other words, the energy savings can be translated directly into faster computation, making BitNet not just more efficient but also potentially faster than its full-precision counterparts.

BitNet represents a new computation paradigm for LLMs, one that is fundamentally aligned with the constraints and opportunities of low-bit representation. By designing the model architecture and training process around the assumption of binary weights, rather than treating low-bit representation as an afterthought, BitNet opens the door to a new generation of LLMs that are both high-performance and highly efficient.

## From BitNet to BitNet b1.58: Introducing Ternary Weights {-1, 0, 1}

While the original BitNet demonstrated the potential of 1-bit model architectures, it also revealed opportunities for improvement. In this work, the authors introduce BitNet b1.58, a significant variant of BitNet in which every single parameter (or weight) of the LLM is ternary, taking on values from the set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. This seemingly small addition of the value $0$ to the original binary set $\{-1, 1\}$ has profound implications for the model's capabilities and performance.

The inclusion of $0$ as a possible weight value provides a crucial advantage: explicit support for feature filtering. In a neural network, each weight determines the strength of the connection between two neurons. A weight of $+1$ indicates a strong positive connection, $-1$ indicates a strong negative connection, and $0$ indicates no connection at all. In the original BitNet, every connection was either positive or negative — there was no way for the model to "turn off" a connection entirely. By introducing the value $0$, BitNet b1.58 gains the ability to selectively filter features, effectively pruning unimportant connections during the training process itself. This significantly improves the modeling capability of the network, allowing it to learn more nuanced and efficient representations.

The quantization function used in BitNet b1.58 is based on an \emph{absmean} approach. Specifically, the weight matrix $W$ is first scaled by its average absolute value $\gamma$, which is computed as:

$$\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$$

where $n$ and $m$ are the dimensions of the weight matrix. Each weight is then divided by $\gamma$ (plus a small constant $\epsilon$ for numerical stability) and rounded to the nearest integer, clipped to the range $[-1, 1]$:

$$\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$$

where the $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ function is defined as:

$$\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\left(a, \min\left(b, \mathrm{round}(x)\right)\right)$$

This quantization process maps the continuous learned weights to the discrete set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ in a principled manner, preserving the relative magnitudes and signs of the original weights as much as possible.

For activations, BitNet b1.58 follows the same quantization approach as the original BitNet, with one modification: activations are scaled to the range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token, rather than being scaled to $[0, Q_b]$ before non-linear functions. This change eliminates the need for zero-point quantization, which simplifies both implementation and system-level optimization, while introducing negligible effects on performance.

BitNet b1.58 also adopts components from the LLaMA architecture, which has become the de facto backbone for open-source LLMs. Specifically, it uses RMSNorm for normalization, SwiGLU as the activation function, rotary embedding for positional encoding, and removes all biases from the model. This design choice ensures that BitNet b1.58 can be integrated into popular open-source software frameworks with minimal effort, facilitating adoption and further development by the research community.

Importantly, BitNet b1.58 retains all the benefits of the original 1-bit BitNet, including its new computation paradigm that requires almost no multiplication operations for matrix multiplication. The addition of the $0$ weight value does not change this fundamental advantage — multiplying any activation by $0$ simply produces $0$, which can be handled trivially in hardware. At the same time, BitNet b1.58 is much more efficient than FP16 LLM baselines in terms of memory consumption, throughput, and latency. The authors' experiments demonstrate that BitNet b1.58 can match full precision (FP16) baselines in terms of both perplexity and end-task performance, starting from a 3B model size, when using the same configuration (e.g., model size, training tokens, etc.). This is a remarkable result, showing that the explicit feature filtering enabled by ternary weights closes the performance gap that existed with binary weights.

## The Mathematical Significance of 1.58 Bits in the Binary System

The name "BitNet b1.58" naturally raises the question: why 1.58 bits? The answer lies in the fundamental relationship between the number of distinct values a weight can take and the number of bits required to represent those values in the binary system.

In information theory, the number of bits required to represent a value from a set of $N$ distinct possibilities is given by $\log_2(N)$. In the original BitNet, each weight could take one of two values: $+1$ or $-1$. The number of bits required to represent a binary value is $\log_2(2) = 1$ bit, which is why the original BitNet is referred to as a "1-bit" model.

In BitNet b1.58, each weight can take one of three values: $-1$, $0$, or $+1$. The number of bits required to represent a ternary value is $\log_2(3) \approx 1.585$ bits. This is the mathematical origin of the "1.58 bits" in the model's name — it reflects the fact that each weight in the model carries approximately 1.58 bits of information, as it can distinguish among three distinct states rather than two.

This is a subtle but important point. While it might seem natural to think of the model as using "2 bits" (since you need at least 2 binary digits to represent three values: $00$, $01$, and $10$), the information-theoretic measure of $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$ bits more accurately captures the actual information content of each weight. One of the three possible 2-bit patterns (e.g., $11$) is unused, meaning that a naive 2-bit representation would be wasteful. The 1.58-bit framing highlights that ternary weights are, in a precise information-theoretic sense, more efficient than a full 2-bit representation would suggest.

The significance of this measure extends beyond mere nomenclature. It positions BitNet b1.58 in a precise location on the spectrum of model quantization, between the extreme of 1-bit (binary) weights and the more conventional 4-bit or 8-bit quantization approaches. The fact that 1.58 bits is sufficient to match the performance of 16-bit models (at sufficient scale) is a powerful demonstration of the redundancy present in traditional full-precision LLM weights. It suggests that the vast majority of the information encoded in 16-bit floating-point weights is, in a sense, wasted — the model's functional behavior can be captured with less than 10% of the bits typically used.

This insight has profound implications for the future of LLM design. If 1.58 bits per weight is sufficient to match full-precision performance, then the field should reconsider the default assumption that high precision is necessary for effective language modeling. BitNet b1.58 defines a new scaling law and recipe for training new generations of LLMs that are both high-performance and cost-effective. Furthermore, the ternary weight representation enables a new computation paradigm and opens the door for designing specific hardware optimized for 1-bit LLMs — hardware that could exploit the simplicity of ternary arithmetic to achieve dramatic improvements in speed, energy efficiency, and cost.

# The BitNet b1.58 Architecture

## The BitNet Architecture: Replacing nn.Linear with BitLinear

The BitNet architecture represents a fundamental reimagining of the Transformer model, one of the most widely used architectures in modern natural language processing. At its core, the standard Transformer relies heavily on linear layers—implemented in popular deep learning frameworks as `nn.Linear`—to perform matrix multiplications that constitute the bulk of the model's computation. In a conventional Transformer, these linear layers store weights as 16-bit floating-point numbers (either FP16 or BF16), and the matrix multiplication operations involve floating-point additions and multiplications, which are computationally expensive in terms of both time and energy.

BitNet's key innovation is to replace every standard `nn.Linear` operation with a specialized module called `BitLinear`. The critical distinction is that `BitLinear` stores its parameters not as full-precision floating-point values, but as highly quantized values—in the case of BitNet b1.58, each weight is constrained to be one of three values: $-1$, $0$, or $+1$. This ternary representation is what gives rise to the "1.58-bit" designation, since representing three possible values requires $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$ bits in the binary system.

The implications of this replacement are profound. When all weights are ternary, the matrix multiplication that lies at the heart of every linear transformation no longer requires any floating-point multiplications. Instead, multiplying an input by a weight that is $-1$, $0$, or $+1$ simply negates, zeroes out, or preserves the input value, respectively. The accumulation of these results then only requires integer additions. This transformation from floating-point multiply-accumulate operations to integer addition operations yields enormous savings in energy consumption and opens the door to fundamentally new approaches to hardware design for language model inference. The BitNet b1.58 architecture inherits this computational paradigm entirely from the original BitNet, while introducing several important modifications that enhance both its modeling capability and its practical deployability.

## Training from Scratch: 1.58-bit Weights and 8-bit Activations

A crucial aspect of BitNet b1.58 is that it is trained from scratch with quantized parameters, rather than being trained at full precision and then quantized after the fact. This stands in contrast to the widely used post-training quantization techniques, which take a model that has already been trained at 16-bit precision and attempt to reduce its precision for inference. The authors note that post-training quantization is inherently sub-optimal, as the model was never trained to operate effectively at reduced precision.

By training from scratch with 1.58-bit weights, the model learns to represent its knowledge entirely within the constraints of the ternary weight space. Every parameter throughout the entire model—the attention matrices, the feed-forward network matrices, and all other linear transformations—takes on values of exactly $-1$, $0$, or $+1$. There are no exceptions and no mixed-precision components within the core model parameters.

The activations, however, are quantized to 8 bits rather than to the same 1.58-bit precision as the weights. This asymmetry between weight and activation precision is a deliberate design choice. Activations, which represent the flow of information through the network, benefit from higher resolution to maintain the fidelity of the representations being passed between layers. The 8-bit activation quantization provides sufficient dynamic range while still being significantly more efficient than the 16-bit activations used in standard Transformer models. As we will discuss later, this reduction in activation precision from 16 bits to 8 bits also has important implications for long-sequence inference, as it directly impacts the size of the key-value (KV) cache.

## Weight Quantization: The Absmean Quantization Function

The central technical challenge in training a model with ternary weights is the process of quantization—converting the continuous-valued weights produced by gradient descent into discrete values from the set $\{-1, 0, +1\}$. BitNet b1.58 introduces a specific quantization function called the **absmean quantization function** to accomplish this transformation.

The absmean quantization function operates in two conceptual steps. First, it scales the weight matrix by dividing each element by a scaling factor $\gamma$, which is computed as the average of the absolute values of all elements in the weight matrix. This normalization step centers the distribution of weights around a range where they can be meaningfully rounded to one of the three target values. Second, each scaled weight value is rounded to the nearest integer and then clipped to ensure it falls within the range $[-1, +1]$.

Formally, given a weight matrix $W$ of dimensions $n \times m$, the quantized weight matrix $\widetilde{W}$ is computed as:

$$\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$$

where the scaling factor $\gamma$ is defined as:

$$\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$$

and $\epsilon$ is a small constant added for numerical stability to prevent division by zero. The use of the average absolute value as the scaling factor is what gives this function its name—"absmean" referring to the absolute mean of the weight matrix.

This approach is elegant in its simplicity. By normalizing the weights by their average magnitude, the function ensures that weights that are large in magnitude relative to the average will be quantized to $+1$ or $-1$, while weights that are small in magnitude will be quantized to $0$. This creates a natural thresholding mechanism that is adaptive to the specific distribution of each weight matrix, rather than relying on fixed thresholds that might not be appropriate for all layers or all stages of training.

## The RoundClip Mechanism and Weight Scaling

The RoundClip function is a composite operation that combines rounding and clipping into a single transformation. It is defined as:

$$\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x)))$$

To understand this function, it is helpful to break it down into its constituent operations. The innermost operation is $\mathrm{round}(x)$, which rounds the input $x$ to the nearest integer. For example, $\mathrm{round}(0.3) = 0$, $\mathrm{round}(0.7) = 1$, $\mathrm{round}(-0.6) = -1$, and $\mathrm{round}(1.8) = 2$. The rounding operation maps the continuous values to integers but does not constrain them to any particular range.

The clipping operation, implemented through the combination of $\max$ and $\min$ functions, then constrains the rounded values to lie within the interval $[a, b]$. In the context of BitNet b1.58, the clipping bounds are $a = -1$ and $b = +1$, so the function constrains all outputs to be one of $\{-1, 0, +1\}$. The $\min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))$ operation ensures that no value exceeds the upper bound $b$, and the $\max(a, \cdot)$ operation ensures that no value falls below the lower bound $a$. Values that round to $+2$ or $-2$ (which can happen when a weight is significantly larger than the average absolute value) are clipped back to $+1$ or $-1$, respectively.

The scaling factor $\gamma$ plays a critical role in determining how the weight values are distributed across the three quantization levels. Because $\gamma$ is the mean of the absolute values of the weight matrix, approximately half of the weights (those with absolute values greater than $\gamma$) will be scaled to values with magnitude greater than 1 before rounding, and approximately half (those with absolute values less than $\gamma$) will be scaled to values with magnitude less than 1. After rounding, the former group will be assigned to $+1$ or $-1$, while the latter group will largely be assigned to $0$, depending on the exact distribution. This creates a natural sparsity in the quantized weight matrix, as many weights will be quantized to zero, which as we will see has important implications for the model's modeling capabilities.

## Activation Quantization: Per-Token Scaling and Zero-Point Removal

While the weight quantization in BitNet b1.58 represents a departure from the original BitNet, the activation quantization follows a similar approach with one important modification. In the original BitNet, activations were scaled to the range $[0, Q_b]$ before applying non-linear functions, where $Q_b$ represents the maximum value representable in the quantization scheme. This approach required the use of zero-point quantization, a technique from the quantization literature that handles the asymmetry of activation distributions that are not centered around zero.

In BitNet b1.58, the authors make a simplifying change: activations are scaled to the range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ on a per-token basis, and the zero-point quantization is removed entirely. Per-token scaling means that for each token in a sequence, the activation values are independently scaled based on the range of values present in that specific token's activation vector. This is in contrast to per-tensor scaling, where a single scaling factor would be applied to the entire activation tensor.

The motivation for removing the zero-point quantization is primarily one of implementation simplicity and system-level optimization. Zero-point quantization introduces additional computational overhead during both the forward pass and the backward pass, as the zero-point offset must be tracked and applied throughout the quantization and dequantization process. By scaling activations symmetrically around zero (to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ rather than $[0, Q_b]$), the quantization process becomes simpler and more amenable to hardware optimization.

Importantly, the authors note that this simplification introduces negligible effects on performance. This finding suggests that the asymmetric activation distributions that motivated the use of zero-point quantization in prior work do not pose a significant challenge for the BitNet b1.58 architecture, possibly because the ternary weights already provide sufficient flexibility to compensate for any asymmetry in the activation distributions.

## LLaMA-alike Components: RMSNorm, SwiGLU, and Rotary Embedding

Beyond the quantization functions, BitNet b1.58 incorporates several architectural components from the LLaMA family of models. LLaMA has become the de facto backbone for open-source large language models, and its architectural choices have been widely adopted and validated by the research community. By aligning with these choices, BitNet b1.58 ensures that it can benefit from the ecosystem of tools and software that have been developed around LLaMA.

The first LLaMA-alike component is **RMSNorm** (Root Mean Square Normalization), which replaces the standard LayerNorm used in the original Transformer architecture. RMSNorm normalizes activations by dividing each element by the root mean square of the activation vector, without subtracting the mean. This simplification reduces computational overhead while maintaining the normalization benefits that help stabilize training.

The second component is **SwiGLU** (Swish-Gated Linear Unit), which is used as the activation function in the feed-forward network. SwiGLU extends the standard GLU (Gated Linear Unit) by using the Swish activation function as the gating mechanism. The feed-forward network in a SwiGLU-based Transformer has a structure where the output is computed as the element-wise product of two linear transformations, with one of them passed through a Swish activation. This gated mechanism has been shown to improve model performance compared to the standard ReLU-based feed-forward networks.

The third component is **rotary embedding** (RoPE), which provides a method for encoding positional information in the attention mechanism. Unlike absolute positional embeddings, which add a fixed positional encoding to the input, rotary embeddings apply a rotation to the query and key vectors based on their position in the sequence. This rotation-based approach allows the model to naturally capture relative positional relationships and has become a standard choice in modern Transformer-based language models.

Finally, BitNet b1.58 removes all biases from the model. Biases are additive parameters that are typically added to the output of linear transformations. Removing biases is consistent with the philosophy of the quantized architecture, as it reduces the number of parameters and simplifies the model structure without significantly impacting performance, as has been observed in the LLaMA models.

## Modeling Capability: Feature Filtering through Explicit Zero Weights

One of the most significant innovations in BitNet b1.58 compared to the original BitNet is the inclusion of $0$ as a possible weight value. The original BitNet used binary weights constrained to $\{-1, +1\}$, meaning every weight had a non-zero value. BitNet b1.58 expands this to ternary weights $\{-1, 0, +1\}$, and this seemingly small change has profound implications for the model's modeling capabilities.

The inclusion of explicit zero weights provides what the authors describe as "explicit support for feature filtering." When a weight is $0$, it effectively disconnects the corresponding input feature from the output, as the contribution of that input to the output is exactly zero regardless of the input value. This is qualitatively different from the binary case, where every input feature always contributes to the output (either positively or negatively). With ternary weights, the model can learn to selectively ignore certain input features for certain outputs, creating a form of intrinsic sparsity in the weight matrices.

This feature filtering capability addresses a fundamental limitation of binary-weight models. In a binary model with weights $\{-1, +1\}$, every neuron in a layer is connected to every neuron in the previous layer with a non-zero weight. The model can only modulate the strength of these connections by adjusting the magnitude of the activations, not by completely removing a connection. The ternary weights in BitNet b1.58 remove this constraint, allowing the model to learn sparse connectivity patterns that can more efficiently represent certain types of computations.

The authors note that this explicit feature filtering "significantly improves the performance of 1-bit LLMs." This improvement is borne out in the experimental results, where BitNet b1.58 is able to match the performance of full-precision FP16 LLaMA models of the same size and training configuration, starting from a model size of 3 billion parameters. The original binary BitNet, without the ability to zero out weights, would struggle to achieve this level of performance, as it lacks the flexibility to represent sparse transformations.

## Ecosystem Compatibility: Integration with Huggingface, vLLM, and llama.cpp

A critical practical consideration in the design of BitNet b1.58 is its compatibility with the existing open-source ecosystem for large language models. The authors explicitly note that their adoption of LLaMA-alike components is motivated by a desire to "embrace the open-source community," and they highlight the integration with three major open-source projects: Huggingface, vLLM, and llama.cpp.

**Huggingface** provides the most widely used libraries and tools for working with Transformer-based language models, including the `transformers` library for model loading and inference, and the `huggingface_hub` for model distribution. By using LLaMA-alike components, BitNet b1.58 can be integrated into the Huggingface ecosystem with minimal effort, allowing researchers and practitioners to load, fine-tune, and deploy BitNet b1.58 models using familiar interfaces.

**vLLM** is a high-throughput inference engine designed specifically for large language models. It implements techniques such as paged attention for efficient memory management and continuous batching for improved throughput. Compatibility with vLLM means that BitNet b1.58 can benefit from these optimization techniques for production deployment scenarios where inference throughput is a critical concern.

**llama.cpp** is a popular lightweight inference framework that enables running LLaMA-family models on consumer hardware, including CPUs and low-end GPUs. It is particularly important for democratizing access to language models, as it allows users without access to expensive data center hardware to run models locally. The compatibility with llama.cpp means that BitNet b1.58 models, with their significantly reduced memory and energy requirements, are particularly well-suited for deployment on resource-constrained devices, including edge and mobile devices.

The emphasis on ecosystem compatibility reflects an understanding that the practical impact of a new model architecture depends not only on its technical merits but also on its accessibility to the broader community. By designing BitNet b1.58 to be a drop-in replacement for LLaMA components while replacing the linear layers with BitLinear modules, the authors ensure that the model can be adopted incrementally, with existing tools and workflows requiring only minimal modifications.

# Evaluating Performance and Efficiency

The promise of 1-bit large language models is compelling: vastly reduced memory, faster computation, and lower energy consumption. But these advantages mean nothing if the models cannot perform their core function of understanding and generating language. In this chapter, we examine the experimental evaluation of BitNet b1.58, the 1.58-bit LLM variant where every parameter takes on values in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. The evaluation is twofold: first, we consider whether the model achieves competitive modeling performance compared to traditional full-precision models, and second, we quantify the hardware efficiency gains that make BitNet b1.58 a potential paradigm shift in LLM deployment. Understanding both dimensions is essential for appreciating why BitNet b1.58 represents not merely an incremental optimization but a new scaling law for LLM architecture.

# Experimental Setup: RedPajama Dataset and FP16 LLaMA Baseline

A fair experimental comparison requires careful control over the conditions under which models are trained and evaluated. In the case of BitNet b1.58, the researchers designed experiments to isolate the effect of the quantization strategy by holding constant as many other variables as possible. The primary baseline for comparison is a reproduced FP16 LLaMA LLM, chosen because the LLaMA architecture has become the de facto backbone for open-source LLMs. By reproducing the FP16 LLaMA rather than using an off-the-shelf model, the researchers ensured that both models were trained under identical conditions, eliminating confounding factors such as differences in training data quality, optimization hyperparameters, or tokenization strategies.

Both BitNet b1.58 and the FP16 LLaMA baseline were pre-trained on the RedPajama dataset for 100 billion tokens. The choice to use a fixed token budget is critical because the number of training tokens is one of the most important determinants of LLM performance. By holding the number of training tokens constant across both architectures and across different model sizes, the researchers could attribute any performance differences to the architectures themselves rather than to differences in training data exposure. This experimental design allows for a clean comparison: if BitNet b1.58 matches or exceeds the performance of the FP16 baseline with the same model size and training tokens, the conclusion must be that the 1.58-bit quantization does not fundamentally limit modeling capability.

The evaluation covered multiple model sizes, allowing the researchers to observe how performance and efficiency scale with model capacity. This is particularly important because quantization effects may vary with scale. A model that performs poorly at small sizes might close the gap at larger sizes, or vice versa. By testing at 3B, 3.9B, 7B, 13B, and 70B parameter counts, the researchers could identify the threshold at which BitNet b1.58 becomes competitive with full precision, an insight that has significant implications for practical deployment decisions.

For measuring inference efficiency, the researchers employed the FasterTransformer codebase, which is specifically optimized for LLM inference latency on GPU devices. This choice ensures that the latency and memory measurements reflect the best-case scenario for the FP16 baseline rather than an unoptimized implementation. For BitNet b1.58, a 2-bit kernel from Ladder was integrated to handle the ternary weight representation efficiently. The researchers report the time per output token as the primary latency metric, as this is the major cost during autoregressive inference where tokens are generated one at a time. This metric directly reflects the user experience in interactive applications and is the bottleneck for most LLM use cases.

# Evaluating Perplexity on WikiText2 and C4 Datasets

Perplexity is one of the most fundamental metrics for evaluating language models. It measures how well a model predicts a sequence of text, quantifying the model's uncertainty about the next token given the preceding context. A lower perplexity indicates that the model assigns higher probability to the actual next token, reflecting better language understanding. Formally, perplexity is defined as the exponentiated average negative log-likelihood over a sequence, and it can be interpreted as the effective number of equally likely choices the model considers for each token. A model with perplexity of 1 would be perfectly certain about every token, while higher values indicate greater uncertainty.

For evaluating BitNet b1.58, the researchers used two standard benchmarks: WikiText2 and C4. WikiText2 is a well-known dataset derived from Wikipedia articles, providing a measure of performance on relatively clean, structured text. C4, the Colossal Clean Crawled Corpus, is a larger dataset derived from web text that has been filtered for quality, providing a broader measure of performance on more diverse language. Together, these datasets give a comprehensive picture of the model's language modeling capability across different text distributions.

The perplexity results reveal a critical finding: BitNet b1.58 matches the full precision LLaMA LLM at the 3B model size in terms of perplexity. This is a remarkable result because it demonstrates that constraining every parameter to one of three values $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ does not inherently limit the model's ability to capture the statistical patterns of natural language, provided the model is large enough. Below the 3B threshold, there is a performance gap, suggesting that very small models need the additional representational capacity of full-precision weights. But at 3B parameters and above, the ternary representation suffices.

The efficiency advantages at this performance-equivalent point are substantial. At the 3B model size, BitNet b1.58 is $2.71\times$ faster and uses $3.55\times$ less GPU memory than the FP16 LLaMA baseline. This means that for the same language modeling quality, the 1.58-bit model can be deployed on hardware with significantly less memory and can generate text more than twice as fast. In practical terms, this could mean the difference between requiring an expensive data center GPU and being able to run on more modest hardware.

Even more striking is the comparison between BitNet b1.58 at 3.9B parameters and the LLaMA LLM at 3B parameters. The 1.58-bit model with 3.9B parameters is $2.4\times$ faster and consumes $3.32\times$ less memory than the FP16 model at 3B, while performing significantly better in terms of perplexity. This demonstrates that BitNet b1.58 is a Pareto improvement: there exist configurations where the 1.58-bit model dominates the full-precision model on both quality and efficiency dimensions simultaneously. No trade-off is required; the 1.58-bit model simply wins on all fronts at this operating point.

# Zero-Shot Performance on End Tasks

While perplexity provides a measure of raw language modeling capability, it does not directly assess whether the model can perform useful tasks. Zero-shot evaluation addresses this by testing the model's ability to perform specific language tasks without any task-specific training or fine-tuning. The model is presented with a task and must use its pre-trained knowledge to produce the correct answer, typically by selecting from given options or generating text that matches expected patterns. Zero-shot performance is a strong indicator of the model's generalization capabilities and its potential usefulness in practical applications.

The researchers evaluated zero-shot performance on a comprehensive suite of language tasks. ARC-Easy and ARC-Challenge test the model's ability to answer grade-school science questions, with the Challenge version containing questions that are more difficult for both retrieval-based and statistical methods. Hellaswag evaluates commonsense reasoning by asking the model to complete scenarios with the most plausible ending. Winogrande tests pronoun resolution and commonsense understanding through fill-in-the-blank challenges. PIQA assesses physical commonsense reasoning about interactions with objects. OpenbookQA tests science question answering with the aid of a provided knowledge base. BoolQ evaluates the model's ability to answer yes-or-no questions based on provided passages. Together, these tasks span a wide range of reasoning types, from factual knowledge to commonsense understanding.

The evaluation was conducted using the lm-evaluation-harness pipeline, a standardized framework that ensures consistent evaluation protocols across different models and tasks. This standardization is important for ensuring that performance differences reflect genuine model capabilities rather than differences in evaluation methodology.

The zero-shot results follow the same pattern as the perplexity results: the performance gap between BitNet b1.58 and the FP16 LLaMA LLM narrows as the model size increases, and BitNet b1.58 matches the full precision baseline starting from the 3B size. This convergence across diverse tasks suggests that the 1.58-bit quantization does not selectively impair certain types of reasoning while preserving others. Instead, the ternary representation appears to be sufficient for the full range of capabilities that emerge in LLMs at this scale.

The Pareto improvement observed in perplexity is confirmed in the end-task results. BitNet b1.58 at 3.9B parameters outperforms the LLaMA LLM at 3B parameters on the zero-shot benchmarks, while simultaneously achieving lower memory usage and latency. This result is particularly significant because it demonstrates that the efficiency advantages of 1.58-bit quantization do not come at the cost of reduced task performance. On the contrary, the 1.58-bit model can be both more capable and more efficient than a full-precision model of comparable or even slightly larger size.

# GPU Memory and Latency Optimization Using 2-bit Kernels

The efficiency advantages of BitNet b1.58 become more pronounced as models scale to larger sizes. The researchers extended their evaluation to 7B, 13B, and 70B parameter models to understand how the speed and memory benefits grow with scale. The results show that the speed-up increases with model size: at 70B parameters, BitNet b1.58 is $4.1\times$ faster than the FP16 LLaMA baseline. This trend can be understood by examining the composition of computation within a Transformer model.

The bulk of parameters in a Transformer LLM reside in the linear layers, specifically the attention projections and the feedforward network. These are precisely the layers that BitNet b1.58 quantizes to ternary values. As the model size grows, the proportion of computation and memory devoted to these linear layers increases, while components that remain in full precision, such as embeddings, constitute a smaller fraction of the total. Consequently, the efficiency gains from quantizing the linear layers have a greater impact on overall performance as the model scales.

Memory consumption follows a similar trend. The embedding layers remain at full precision in BitNet b1.58, and their memory proportion is smaller for larger models. This means that the memory savings from ternary weights become a larger fraction of the total model memory as the model grows. The combination of reduced weight precision and the growing dominance of quantized components leads to increasingly favorable memory and latency characteristics at larger scales.

An important caveat is that both the latency and memory measurements were performed using a 2-bit kernel. While ternary values $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ theoretically require $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$ bits per parameter, the actual implementation uses 2-bit representations due to hardware constraints. This means there is still room for optimization. A kernel that could pack ternary values more efficiently, approaching the theoretical 1.58-bit density, could further reduce memory usage and potentially improve latency. The current results, while already demonstrating substantial improvements, may represent a conservative estimate of the achievable efficiency gains.

# Energy Consumption Analysis: FP16 Operations vs. INT8 Addition

Energy consumption is a critical consideration for LLM deployment, both because of the economic cost of electricity and the environmental impact of large-scale computing. The researchers analyzed the energy consumption of BitNet b1.58 and the FP16 LLaMA LLM, focusing primarily on matrix multiplication since it contributes the most to the computational cost of LLMs.

The analysis reveals a fundamental difference in the composition of energy costs between the two architectures. The majority of computation in BitNet b1.58 consists of INT8 addition operations, which arise because multiplying by ternary weights $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ can be implemented as addition, subtraction, or no operation. In contrast, the FP16 LLaMA LLM requires both FP16 addition and FP16 multiplication for matrix multiplication. Floating-point multiplication is significantly more energy-intensive than integer addition, as it involves complex operations including exponent comparison, mantissa multiplication, and normalization.

According to established energy models for 7nm chip technology, BitNet b1.58 saves $71.4\times$ arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication compared to the FP16 baseline. This enormous energy reduction stems from replacing expensive floating-point multiplications with simple integer additions. The fundamental insight is that the ternary weight representation transforms the most expensive operation in neural network inference into one of the cheapest.

The researchers also estimated end-to-end energy consumption for models processing 512 tokens, accounting for all components of the model rather than just matrix multiplication. The results show that as the model size scales, BitNet b1.58 becomes increasingly more efficient in terms of energy consumption relative to the FP16 baseline. This trend is explained by the growing proportion of nn.Linear operations in larger models: as the contribution of matrix multiplication to total energy consumption increases with model size, the savings from replacing FP16 operations with INT8 additions become more significant. Other components of the model that are not quantized contribute a smaller fraction of the total cost at larger scales, amplifying the relative advantage of the 1.58-bit approach.

# Throughput Scaling: Batch Size and Sequence Length Comparisons on A100 GPUs

Throughput, measured in tokens generated per unit time, is a critical metric for practical LLM deployment. While latency measures the time to produce a single token, throughput measures how many tokens the system can produce across all concurrent requests. High throughput is essential for serving multiple users simultaneously and for processing large volumes of text efficiently.

The researchers compared the throughput of BitNet b1.58 and the FP16 LLaMA LLM, both with 70B parameters, on two 80GB A100 GPU cards. The experimental setup used pipeline parallelism to enable the 70B LLaMA model to run across the two devices, as the model's memory requirements exceed the capacity of a single GPU. Pipeline parallelism splits the model across devices and processes micro-batches sequentially through the pipeline stages, introducing some overhead but enabling the deployment of models too large for a single device.

The experiment increased the batch size until the GPU memory limit was reached, with a fixed sequence length of 512 tokens. This approach reveals the maximum throughput each model can sustain given the available hardware resources. The results are striking: BitNet b1.58 at 70B parameters can support up to $11\times$ the batch size of the FP16 LLaMA model, resulting in $8.9\times$ higher throughput.

The ability to support a much larger batch size is a direct consequence of the reduced memory footprint per parameter. With ternary weights requiring approximately 1.58 bits instead of 16 bits, the memory saved can be reallocated to processing more sequences in parallel. The throughput improvement, while slightly less than the batch size improvement due to overheads associated with larger batches, still represents nearly an order of magnitude increase in the number of tokens the system can process.

These results demonstrate that BitNet b1.58 enables a new scaling law with respect to model performance and inference cost. The researchers provide concrete equivalences that illustrate the implications: a 13B BitNet b1.58 model is more efficient than a 3B FP16 LLM in terms of latency, memory usage, and energy consumption. A 30B BitNet b1.58 model is more efficient than a 7B FP16 LLM. A 70B BitNet b1.58 model is more efficient than a 13B FP16 LLM. In each case, the 1.58-bit model can be significantly larger while still consuming fewer resources than a smaller full-precision model. This means that for a given hardware budget, deploying BitNet b1.58 allows for a larger, more capable model than would be possible with FP16, fundamentally changing the trade-off between model quality and deployment cost.

# Scalability in Training Tokens: The 2T Tokens Experiment and Generalization

The number of training tokens is a crucial factor for LLM performance, with research showing that models continue to improve as they are trained on more data, provided the model has sufficient capacity. To test the scalability of BitNet b1.58 in terms of training tokens, the researchers trained a model with 2 trillion tokens, following the data recipe of StableLM-3B, which is described as the state-of-the-art open-source 3B model.

This experiment serves multiple purposes. First, it tests whether the 1.58-bit quantization continues to benefit from increased training data, or whether the constrained representation limits the model's ability to absorb information from additional tokens. Second, by following an established data recipe, the experiment provides a direct comparison to a well-regarded model trained under similar conditions. Third, training on 2 trillion tokens, which is substantially more than the 100 billion tokens used in the primary experiments, tests whether the architecture scales gracefully to much larger training budgets.

The evaluation benchmark consisted of Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, and ARC-Easy. SciQ tests science question answering, LAMBADA evaluates the model's ability to predict the last word of a passage given its context, and the other tasks have been described previously. For tasks measured with both accuracy and normalized accuracy, the researchers take the average of the two metrics, providing a balanced assessment that accounts for both raw correctness and calibration.

The results show that BitNet b1.58 achieves superior performance on all end tasks compared to StableLM-3B at 2T tokens. This is a significant finding because it demonstrates that 1.58-bit LLMs have strong generalization capabilities and can effectively utilize large amounts of training data. The ternary weight representation does not impose a bottleneck on learning; instead, the model continues to improve with more training, matching or exceeding the performance of models with full-precision weights trained under comparable conditions.

This result has important implications for the future of LLM training. If 1.58-bit models can match full-precision performance while offering dramatic efficiency gains, then the economics of training and deploying large models fundamentally change. Training can proceed with the same data and scale, but the resulting models are cheaper to store, faster to run, and more energy-efficient to operate. The combination of competitive quality and dramatically improved efficiency positions BitNet b1.58 as a potential new standard for LLM architecture, defining a new scaling law and recipe for training new generations of models that are both high-performance and cost-effective.

# The New Scaling Law and Pareto Improvements

# Defining the New Scaling Law for 1.58-bit LLMs

In the field of Large Language Models (LLMs), a scaling law traditionally describes how model performance, such as perplexity, improves as we increase the number of parameters and training tokens. However, increasing the parameter count in conventional full-precision models—typically represented in $16$-bit floating-point formats like FP16 or BF16—linearly increases the inference cost. This cost manifests in higher memory usage to store the weights, greater latency due to more computation, and increased energy consumption from power-hungry floating-point addition and multiplication operations. The introduction of BitNet b1.58 fundamentally disrupts this dynamic, establishing a new scaling law with respect to model performance and inference cost. 

BitNet b1.58 constrains every single parameter of the LLM to ternary values of $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, which requires only $1.58$ bits to represent in the binary system. By doing so, the matrix multiplication operations that make up the bulk of LLM computation no longer require floating-point multiplications. Instead, they rely solely on integer addition, which is orders of magnitude more energy-efficient. This shift means that as a $1.58$-bit model scales up in size, its inference cost grows at a drastically slower rate compared to a traditional FP16 model. For instance, the paper demonstrates that BitNet b1.58 matches the perplexity of a full-precision FP16 LLaMA LLM at a $3\text{B}$ model size while being $2.71$ times faster and using $3.55$ times less GPU memory. This new scaling law effectively redefines the trade-off between model size and cost, allowing for the deployment of much larger, more capable models at a fraction of the traditional resource expense.

# Efficiency Equivalencies: 13B b1.58 vs. 3B FP16 LLM

To fully grasp the impact of the new scaling law, the paper provides specific efficiency equivalencies that compare the inference costs of $1.58$-bit models against much smaller $16$-bit baselines. The first such equivalence states that a $13\text{B}$ BitNet b1.58 model is more efficient, in terms of latency, memory usage, and energy consumption, than a $3\text{B}$ FP16 LLM. Intuitively, a $13$ billion parameter model has over four times the modeling capacity of a $3$ billion parameter model. In a traditional $16$-bit paradigm, scaling to $13\text{B}$ would require a massive increase in memory and computational resources. 

However, because every weight in the $13\text{B}$ BitNet b1.58 model is restricted to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the memory footprint required to store the model is drastically reduced, allowing it to fit into a smaller memory envelope than a $3\text{B}$ FP16 model. Furthermore, the matrix multiplications are simplified to integer additions, cutting down both the time per output token (latency) and the arithmetic operations energy consumption. This equivalence illustrates a profound advantage: by moving to $1.58$ bits, we can build models with substantially more parameters—leading to better task performance and lower perplexity—while actually reducing the physical resources required to run them below that of a much smaller, traditional FP16 model.

# Efficiency Equivalencies: 30B b1.58 vs. 7B FP16 LLM

The second efficiency equivalence outlined in the paper scales this concept up further: a $30\text{B}$ BitNet b1.58 model is more efficient, in terms of latency, memory usage, and energy consumption, than a $7\text{B}$ FP16 LLM. A $30$ billion parameter model is approximately $4.2$ times larger than a $7$ billion parameter model. In standard LLM design, a $7\text{B}$ FP16 model is already quite expensive to deploy, requiring significant GPU memory and bandwidth to transfer weights from DRAM to on-chip accelerators. 

The paper notes that as model size scales, the speed-up and memory savings of BitNet b1.58 compared to FP16 baselines actually increase. This is because the primary computational bottleneck in LLMs comes from the linear layers (e.g., nn.Linear), and the cost of these layers grows with the model size. In a $30\text{B}$ b1.58 model, this growth is handled through highly optimized integer additions rather than costly floating-point operations. The paper estimates that BitNet b1.58 saves $71.4$ times the arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on $7\text{nm}$ chips compared to FP16 models. Consequently, a $30\text{B}$ b1.58 model, which would naturally offer far superior performance, operates with lower latency, occupies less memory, and consumes less energy than a $7\text{B}$ FP16 model. This shifts the paradigm of how large models can be deployed, making $30\text{B}$-scale models accessible under the resource constraints previously reserved for much smaller $7\text{B}$ models.

# Efficiency Equivalencies: 70B b1.58 vs. 13B FP16 LLM

The third equivalence demonstrates the extreme end of this scaling law: a $70\text{B}$ BitNet b1.58 model is more efficient, in terms of latency, memory usage, and energy consumption, than a $13\text{B}$ FP16 LLM. A $70$ billion parameter model is over five times larger than a $13$ billion parameter model. The paper provides concrete metrics to illustrate this, noting that a $70\text{B}$ BitNet b1.58 model is $4.1$ times faster than an FP16 LLaMA LLM of the exact same size. 

When evaluating throughput on two $80\text{GB}$ A100 cards, the $70\text{B}$ b1.58 model can support up to $11$ times the batch size of the FP16 baseline, resulting in an $8.9$ times higher throughput. These massive efficiency gains are achieved because the $1.58$-bit architecture fundamentally alters the arithmetic requirements of the network. Even though the $70\text{B}$ b1.58 model is vastly larger and more capable than a $13\text{B}$ FP16 model, the fundamental efficiency of its $1.58$-bit matrix multiplication means it costs less in every major inference metric. Furthermore, the paper notes that these latency and memory metrics were measured using a $2$-bit kernel, suggesting there is still room for optimization to reduce the cost even further. This equivalence proves that we can scale models into the tens of billions of parameters while maintaining the inference cost profile of a substantially smaller, traditional model.

# Understanding Pareto Improvements in LLM Design

In economics and engineering, a Pareto improvement is a change to a system that improves at least one metric without making any other metric worse. In the context of LLM design, achieving a Pareto improvement means increasing model performance (such as perplexity or end-task accuracy) without increasing the inference cost, or alternatively, reducing the inference cost without sacrificing performance. The paper explicitly states that BitNet b1.58 represents a Pareto improvement over the state-of-the-art LLM models, defining a new Pareto frontier for model design.

The results demonstrate that BitNet b1.58 matches the full-precision FP16 baseline in terms of both perplexity and end-task performance starting from a $3\text{B}$ model size, when using the same configuration of model size and training tokens. For instance, a $3.9\text{B}$ BitNet b1.58 model performs significantly better than a $3\text{B}$ FP16 LLaMA LLM, while simultaneously being $2.4$ times faster and consuming $3.32$ times less memory. Any point on the old Pareto frontier, defined by FP16 models, is now dominated by a point on the new frontier defined by $1.58$-bit models. You can always choose a BitNet b1.58 model that either matches the performance of an FP16 model at a fraction of the cost, or vastly exceeds the performance of an FP16 model for the exact same cost. The ability to scale up to $70\text{B}$ parameters while remaining more efficient than a $13\text{B}$ FP16 model proves that $1.58$-bit LLMs allow us to push the boundaries of model performance without hitting the traditional computational, memory, and energy walls.

The introduction of BitNet b1.58 marks a fundamental shift in how we think about large language model architecture and deployment. By constraining every parameter to ternary values $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, BitNet b1.58 demonstrates that it is possible to match the performance of full-precision (FP16) Transformer LLMs while dramatically reducing computational costs. As established in earlier chapters, BitNet b1.58 achieves comparable perplexity and end-task performance to FP16 LLaMA baselines starting from a 3B model size, while being significantly faster, more memory-efficient, and less energy-intensive. ## 1-bit Mixture-of-Experts (MoE): Reducing Memory and Communication Overhead

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures have emerged as a cost-effective approach for scaling large language models. The core idea behind MoE is to replace a single feedforward network in a Transformer with multiple expert networks, where only a subset of experts is activated for any given input. This allows the model to increase its total parameter count—and thus its capacity—without a proportional increase in computation FLOPs, since only a fraction of the experts are used per token. In principle, this makes MoE models highly efficient: one can build a model with hundreds of billions of parameters while only requiring the computational cost of activating a few billion per inference step.

However, despite the computational savings, MoE models face significant deployment challenges that limit their practical application. The first and most pressing challenge is memory consumption. Even though only a subset of experts is activated during inference, all experts must reside in memory because any expert could be selected for any given input token. This means that the memory requirements of an MoE model scale with the total number of parameters across all experts, not just the activated subset. For large MoE models, this memory burden can be enormous, requiring multiple high-memory GPUs or TPUs distributed across several devices. The cost of such infrastructure is prohibitive for many organizations and researchers.

The second major challenge is inter-chip communication overhead. When experts are distributed across multiple chips or devices—as is typically necessary given the memory constraints described above—the routing mechanism must send activations from the device where the input is processed to the device where the selected expert resides. This introduces significant network communication costs, as activations must be transferred across interconnects between devices. The bandwidth of these interconnects is often a bottleneck, and the latency of cross-device communication can erode the computational savings that MoE is supposed to provide. In extreme cases, the communication overhead can become the dominant cost, making the MoE model slower than a dense model of equivalent activated parameters.

BitNet b1.58 addresses both of these challenges through its dramatically reduced memory footprint. Because every parameter in BitNet b1.58 is ternary $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, each weight can be represented using approximately 1.58 bits in the binary system ($\log_2 3 \approx 1.58$). This is a reduction of roughly an order of magnitude compared to FP16 weights, which require 16 bits each. For MoE models, this means that the total memory required to store all experts is reduced by a similar factor. An MoE model that previously required, say, eight GPUs to house all its experts might fit on a single GPU or a much smaller cluster when implemented with 1.58-bit weights. This directly addresses the memory consumption challenge, reducing the number of devices required for deployment and lowering both the capital and operational costs.

Moreover, the reduced memory footprint has cascading benefits for communication overhead. If more experts can fit on a single chip, fewer activations need to be transmitted across inter-chip interconnects. In the ideal case, if the entire MoE model can be placed on a single chip, there would be no inter-chip communication overhead at all. Even when multiple chips are still required, the reduced parameter size means that each expert occupies less memory, allowing more experts to be co-located on the same device and reducing the frequency and volume of cross-device transfers. Additionally, because BitNet b1.58 uses 8-bit activations (compared to 16-bit activations in FP16 models), the size of the activations being routed between experts is also halved, further reducing communication costs.

The implications of this are profound. MoE models, which have been primarily the domain of large-scale industry labs due to their infrastructure demands, could become accessible to a much broader range of researchers and practitioners. The combination of MoE's computational efficiency with BitNet b1.58's memory and communication efficiency could enable the training and deployment of models with trillions of parameters at a fraction of the current cost. This represents a new frontier for scaling LLMs, where the trade-off between model capacity and infrastructure cost is fundamentally redefined.

## Native Support for Long Sequences: Compressing KV Caches

The ability to handle long input sequences has become a critical requirement for modern LLMs. Applications such as document summarization, multi-turn dialogue, code generation, and retrieval-augmented generation all benefit from—or require—context windows that span thousands or even tens of thousands of tokens. As the demand for longer context lengths grows, so too does the technical challenge of supporting them efficiently.

One of the primary bottlenecks for long sequence inference is the memory consumption introduced by the key-value (KV) cache. During autoregressive generation, a Transformer-based LLM must recompute the keys and values for all previous tokens at each step, or it can cache them to avoid redundant computation. The KV cache stores the key and value tensors for every token in the sequence across all attention layers and heads, allowing the model to attend to previous tokens without recomputing their representations. While this caching strategy is essential for efficient autoregressive inference, its memory cost grows linearly with the sequence length and can become prohibitively large for long contexts.

In a standard FP16 LLM, both keys and values are stored as 16-bit floating-point numbers. For a model with a large number of attention heads and layers, the KV cache for a long sequence can consume tens of gigabytes of memory, often exceeding the memory footprint of the model weights themselves. This limits the maximum context length that can be supported on a given device and imposes a significant cost on long-sequence applications.

BitNet b1.58 represents a significant step toward natively supporting long sequences by reducing the precision of activations from 16 bits to 8 bits. Since the KV cache stores activations—specifically, the keys and values computed by the attention mechanism—the halving of activation precision directly translates to a halving of the KV cache memory footprint. Given the same memory resources, a BitNet b1.58 model can support a context length that is twice as long as its FP16 counterpart. This is a substantial improvement that comes with negligible effect on performance, as demonstrated by the experiments showing that BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 baselines in both perplexity and end-task accuracy.

Furthermore, the paper suggests that the KV cache for 1.58-bit LLMs can be further losslessly compressed to 4 bits or even lower. This additional compression would allow for another doubling—or more—of the supported context length without additional memory. The term "lossless" here is important: it implies that the compression does not degrade model performance, distinguishing this approach from aggressive quantization methods that trade accuracy for memory savings. While the specific techniques for achieving this further compression are left as future work, the foundation laid by BitNet b1.58's 8-bit activations makes this a natural and promising direction.

The practical implications of KV cache compression are far-reaching. With longer supported context lengths, LLMs can process entire books, lengthy code repositories, or extended conversation histories in a single pass. This reduces the need for chunking strategies or sliding window approaches that can lose information across boundaries. It also enables more sophisticated retrieval-augmented generation pipelines, where a larger retrieved context can be incorporated into the model's input. By addressing the memory bottleneck of the KV cache, BitNet b1.58 paves the way for a new generation of LLMs that natively handle long sequences without the prohibitive memory costs that currently constrain them.

## Deploying LLMs on Edge and Mobile Devices

Edge and mobile devices represent one of the largest and most impactful frontiers for LLM deployment. Smartphones, tablets, laptops, embedded systems, and IoT devices are ubiquitous, and bringing LLM capabilities directly to these devices would enable a wide range of applications—from offline assistants and real-time translation to on-device content generation and privacy-preserving AI—that are not possible with cloud-dependent models. However, deploying LLMs on edge and mobile devices has been fundamentally constrained by two critical limitations: memory capacity and computational power.

The memory limitations of edge devices are severe. A typical smartphone might have 8 to 16 GB of RAM, shared between the operating system, applications, and any model that is loaded. Even a relatively small 3B parameter FP16 LLM requires approximately 6 GB of memory for weights alone, leaving little room for activations, KV caches, and the application itself. Larger models, such as 7B or 13B parameter LLMs, are entirely out of reach for most mobile devices. This memory constraint effectively caps the size—and thus the capability—of LLMs that can be deployed on edge devices.

The computational limitations are equally constraining. Edge devices typically lack the specialized hardware (such as GPUs or TPUs with high-bandwidth memory) that is used for efficient LLM inference in data centers. Their CPUs and mobile GPUs are optimized for different workloads and have limited arithmetic throughput. The bulk of LLM inference consists of matrix multiplication, which in FP16 requires floating-point addition and multiplication operations. These operations are computationally expensive and energy-intensive, making real-time LLM inference on edge devices impractical for all but the smallest models.

BitNet b1.58 directly addresses both of these limitations. From a memory perspective, the 1.58-bit weight representation reduces the memory footprint of the model by roughly an order of magnitude compared to FP16. A 3B parameter BitNet b1.58 model requires only a fraction of the memory of its FP16 counterpart, making it feasible to load on devices with limited RAM. As the paper demonstrates, BitNet b1.58 3B uses 3.55 times less GPU memory than LLaMA LLM 3B, and this ratio improves as model size increases. For edge devices, where every megabyte counts, this reduction is transformative.

From a computational and energy perspective, the benefits are even more significant. The matrix multiplication in BitNet b1.58 involves only integer addition—specifically, INT8 addition—rather than the floating-point addition and multiplication required by FP16 models. As noted in the paper, BitNet b1.58 saves 71.4 times arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips compared to FP16. For battery-powered edge and mobile devices, energy efficiency is paramount, as it directly determines battery life and thermal management. A model that requires 71.4 times less energy for its dominant computation can run faster and longer on the same battery, or can be run at all on devices where FP16 inference would be prohibitive.

The reduced energy consumption also has implications for thermal design. Edge devices have limited cooling capacity, and sustained high-power computation can lead to thermal throttling, where the device reduces its clock speed to prevent overheating. The lower power consumption of BitNet b1.58 means that the model can sustain higher performance for longer periods without triggering thermal throttling, resulting in a more consistent and reliable user experience.

Taken together, these properties make BitNet b1.58 a natural fit for edge and mobile deployment. The combination of reduced memory, computation, and energy requirements allows models of meaningful capability to be deployed on devices that were previously unable to support LLMs. This enables a wide range of applications that were previously not possible, from on-device assistants that operate without internet connectivity to privacy-preserving AI that processes sensitive data locally without sending it to the cloud. The potential impact on the capabilities of edge and mobile devices is substantial, and BitNet b1.58 represents a significant step toward realizing this potential.

## CPU-Friendly Execution for 1.58-bit Models

An important and often overlooked advantage of BitNet b1.58 is its compatibility with CPU-based execution. While GPUs and specialized accelerators have become the dominant hardware for LLM training and inference, CPUs remain the primary processors in edge devices, mobile phones, laptops, and many server environments. The architecture and computational characteristics of CPUs differ significantly from those of GPUs, and models that are efficient on GPUs are not necessarily efficient on CPUs.

The primary computational bottleneck for LLM inference on CPUs is the cost of floating-point arithmetic. CPUs are generally optimized for integer operations and have relatively limited floating-point throughput compared to GPUs. In an FP16 LLM, the matrix multiplication operations that dominate inference require floating-point addition and multiplication, which are relatively slow and energy-intensive on a CPU. This makes real-time LLM inference on CPUs challenging, even for small models.

BitNet b1.58 fundamentally changes this computational profile. Because the weights are constrained to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the matrix multiplication no longer requires any multiplication operations at all. Instead, multiplying an activation by a ternary weight $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ is equivalent to a sign change, zeroing, or identity operation, all of which can be performed with simple integer arithmetic. The accumulation of these products then reduces to integer addition, which is one of the most fundamental and efficiently supported operations on any CPU.

This shift from floating-point multiplication and addition to integer addition has several important consequences for CPU execution. First, integer addition is typically much faster than floating-point operations on CPUs, as it is handled by the arithmetic logic unit (ALU) which is highly optimized for integer arithmetic. Second, integer operations consume significantly less energy than floating-point operations, which is critical for battery-powered devices and thermally constrained environments. Third, the simplicity of integer addition allows for more efficient use of CPU pipelines and vector instructions (such as SIMD instructions), as there is no need for the complex floating-point units that can become a bottleneck.

Furthermore, the reduced memory footprint of BitNet b1.58 means that more of the model can fit in the CPU's cache hierarchy. Cache locality is a critical factor for CPU performance, as accessing data from L1, L2, or L3 cache is orders of magnitude faster than accessing it from main memory. An FP16 model that is too large to fit in cache will suffer from frequent cache misses, where the CPU must wait for data to be fetched from RAM. A 1.58-bit model, being roughly an order of magnitude smaller, is much more likely to fit within the cache hierarchy, reducing cache misses and improving throughput.

The paper explicitly notes that 1.58-bit LLMs are more friendly to CPU devices, which are the main processors used in edge and mobile environments. This CPU-friendliness is a direct consequence of the integer-only arithmetic and reduced memory footprint, and it means that BitNet b1.58 can be efficiently executed on devices that do not have GPUs or specialized AI accelerators. This is particularly important for democratizing access to LLMs, as it allows models to run on the hardware that people already own, rather than requiring expensive specialized equipment. The ability to run capable LLMs on standard CPUs also simplifies deployment, as it eliminates the need for complex GPU programming, driver management, and hardware-specific optimization.

## The Vision for 1-bit LLM Specific Hardware Optimizations

The introduction of BitNet b1.58 not only improves the efficiency of LLMs on existing hardware but also motivates the design of entirely new hardware architectures specifically optimized for 1-bit computation. To understand why this is significant, it is helpful to consider the co-evolution of hardware and software in the AI field.

Most existing AI hardware—including GPUs, TPUs, and neural processing units (NPUs)—has been designed with the assumption that neural network computations involve floating-point arithmetic, typically in FP16 or BF16 precision. These hardware platforms dedicate substantial silicon area to floating-point units (FPUs) that can perform high-precision multiplication and addition. While these FPUs are essential for training and inference with traditional models, they are largely unnecessary for 1-bit LLMs, where the dominant operation is integer addition.

This mismatch between the computational requirements of 1-bit LLMs and the capabilities of existing hardware represents a significant inefficiency. When a BitNet b1.58 model runs on a GPU, much of the GPU's silicon—including its FPUs—is effectively idle or underutilized, as the model's matrix multiplications only require integer addition. The GPU's resources could be much better used if they were reallocated to integer arithmetic units, memory bandwidth, or other components that are actually exercised by 1-bit computation.

The paper envisions and calls for the design of new hardware and systems specifically optimized for 1-bit LLMs. This vision is grounded in the observation that the new computation paradigm enabled by BitNet—where matrix multiplication requires almost no multiplication operations—fundamentally changes the hardware requirements for LLM inference. A chip designed specifically for 1-bit LLMs could replace the large and power-hungry floating-point units with a greater number of simpler integer addition units, dramatically increasing the throughput of the operations that actually matter for 1-bit computation while reducing power consumption and die area.

Recent work, such as Groq's Language Processing Units (LPUs), has already demonstrated the potential of building specialized hardware for LLMs. LPUs are designed with a focus on the specific computational patterns of LLM inference, and they have shown promising results in terms of speed and efficiency. The paper suggests going a step further: designing hardware that is not just optimized for LLMs in general, but specifically for the 1-bit computation paradigm introduced by BitNet.

Such hardware could offer several key advantages. First, by eliminating floating-point units and reallocating that silicon to integer arithmetic, the chip could achieve much higher computational density—more operations per unit of area and per unit of time. Second, the simplified arithmetic logic could reduce power consumption, allowing for higher clock speeds or more cores within the same power envelope. Third, the reduced precision of weights (1.58 bits) and activations (8 bits) reduces the memory bandwidth requirements, as less data needs to be transferred between memory and compute units. This is particularly important because memory bandwidth, not compute, is often the actual bottleneck in LLM inference. A chip designed for 1-bit computation could optimize its memory subsystem for the smaller data sizes, using narrower data paths and smaller buffers.

Furthermore, the regularity of 1-bit computation lends itself well to custom hardware design. Ternary weights and integer activations result in highly predictable and uniform computation patterns, which can be efficiently mapped to fixed-function hardware pipelines. Unlike floating-point computation, which requires complex normalization, rounding, and exception handling, integer addition is a simple and deterministic operation that can be implemented with minimal logic. This regularity also simplifies the design of systolic arrays and other parallel computation structures that are commonly used in AI accelerators.

The paper also notes that the benefits of 1-bit-specific hardware extend beyond inference to training. While the current work focuses on inference costs, the same principles apply to the training phase, where the majority of computation consists of matrix multiplication. A hardware platform optimized for 1-bit computation could accelerate both training and inference, reducing the cost and time required to develop new models.

In summary, the vision for 1-bit LLM specific hardware represents a long-term direction that could fundamentally reshape the landscape of AI hardware. By aligning the hardware design with the computational requirements of 1-bit models, it becomes possible to build chips that are simultaneously faster, more energy-efficient, and more cost-effective than current general-purpose AI accelerators. This vision complements the software innovations of BitNet b1.58 and underscores the transformative potential of the 1-bit LLM paradigm. Just as the transition from CPUs to GPUs enabled the deep learning revolution, the transition from floating-point to 1-bit hardware could enable the next generation of efficient, scalable, and accessible AI systems. The work on BitNet b1.58 provides both the motivation and the foundation for this hardware revolution, and it calls on the broader community to pursue this vision of specialized 1-bit computation.
