\title{Blogs about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

# Beyond Zeros and Ones: Understanding the 1.58-Bit LLM Revolution

Large language models keep getting bigger, but our hardware budgets don't. While the industry has been squeezing models into 4-bit quantization schemes as a post-training afterthought, a new paper from Microsoft Research asks a more radical question: what if we train models from scratch to operate on just three values?

The answer is BitNet b1.58, and it might change how we think about LLM computation entirely.

## What Does "1.58 Bits" Actually Mean?

When we say a model uses 1-bit weights, we typically mean each parameter is either 0 or 1. BitNet b1.58 goes a step further by making every weight ternary: each parameter takes one of three values: $\{-1, 0, 1\}$.

Here's where the 1.58 comes from. In information theory, the number of bits needed to represent a value with $N$ possible states is $\log_2(N)$. For ternary values where $N = 3$:

$$\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$$

So "1.58 bits" is simply the information-theoretic cost of storing one of three possible states. It's a clever naming convention that signals this model sits between traditional 1-bit binary systems and 2-bit systems, while taking advantage of the additional expressiveness that a third value provides.

## Why Add Zero to Binary Weights?

The original BitNet used binary weights of $\{-1, 1\}$. BitNet b1.58 introduces 0 as a third option, and this isn't just a minor tweak. The inclusion of 0 provides explicit support for feature filtering. When a weight is 0, that connection is effectively pruned, allowing the model to selectively ignore certain features rather than always applying either a positive or negative influence.

This matters for performance. The paper shows that BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision (FP16) Transformer LLMs in both perplexity and downstream task performance starting at around 3B parameters, using the same training token budget. The original binary BitNet couldn't quite close that gap.

## The Math: From Multiplication to Addition

To understand why 1.58-bit models are interesting from a hardware perspective, we need to look at what actually happens during matrix multiplication in a standard Transformer.

In a conventional FP16 LLM, the bulk of computation comes from floating-point addition and multiplication. Matrix multiplication between weights $W$ and activations $x$ involves computing $Wx$, which requires multiplying pairs of 16-bit floats and adding the results.

BitNet b1.58 changes this fundamentally. Since weights are constrained to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, multiplying by a weight becomes trivial:

- Multiply by $1$: keep the value as-is
- Multiply by $-1$: negate the value
- Multiply by $0$: zero it out

No floating-point multiplication is needed for the weight-activation products. The entire matrix multiplication reduces to integer addition operations on the 8-bit activations. As the paper notes, this is a new computation paradigm where "almost no multiplication operations" are required.

## How Weights Become Ternary

BitNet b1.58 uses an `absmean` quantization function to convert trained weights into ternary values during training. The process works as follows.

First, compute the average absolute value of the weight matrix:

$$\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$$

Then scale and round each weight to the nearest integer in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$:

$$\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$$

where $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ clamps values to the range $[-1, 1]$ and rounds to the nearest integer. This means weights near zero become 0, weights significantly positive become 1, and weights significantly negative become $-1$.

The activations are quantized to 8-bit integers using per-token scaling to the range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, without zero-point quantization. This simplifies implementation while having negligible impact on performance.

## The Numbers: Speed, Memory, and Energy

The paper provides concrete comparisons between BitNet b1.58 and a reproduced FP16 LLaMA model, both trained on 100 billion tokens from the RedPajama dataset.

At 3B parameters, BitNet b1.58 matches LLaMA's perplexity while being 2.71× faster and using 3.55× less GPU memory. At 3.9B parameters, BitNet b1.58 actually outperforms LLaMA 3B on end tasks while still being faster and more memory-efficient.

The advantages scale with model size. At 70B parameters, BitNet b1.58 is 4.1× faster than the FP16 baseline. On two 80GB A100 GPUs, the 70B BitNet b1.58 supports 11× the batch size of LLaMA 70B, delivering 8.9× higher throughput.

Energy consumption tells an even more dramatic story. Since matrix multiplication in BitNet b1.58 consists primarily of INT8 addition rather than FP16 addition and multiplication, the paper estimates 71.4× lower arithmetic operation energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips.

## A New Scaling Law

One of the most interesting findings is that 1.58-bit models redefine the cost-performance tradeoff. The paper establishes these equivalences:

- A 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 3B FP16 LLM
- A 30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 7B FP16 LLM  
- A 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 13B FP16 LLM

In other words, you can run a model 4× larger in 1.58-bit precision while still being cheaper to serve than a smaller FP16 model. This opens up a different scaling strategy where model capacity can grow without proportionally growing inference cost.

## LLaMA Compatibility

An important practical detail: BitNet b1.58 adopts LLaMA-like architectural components, including RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and no biases. This means it can integrate with popular open-source tools like Hugging Face, vLLM, and llama.cpp with minimal effort. The researchers clearly designed this with the existing ecosystem in mind.

## Why This Matters

Post-training quantization to 4-bit has become standard practice, but it's fundamentally a compromise. You train in full precision, then compress afterward, accepting some accuracy loss. BitNet b1.58 takes a different approach: train with quantization baked in from the start, so the model learns to work within the ternary constraint.

The results suggest this approach works. When trained on 2 trillion tokens following the StableLM-3B recipe, BitNet b1.58 outperforms the state-of-the-art open-source 3B model on all evaluated tasks. The ternary constraint doesn't seem to limit generalization, at least not at this scale.

The paper also hints at future directions that could further amplify these gains. Mixture-of-Experts models, which are currently limited by memory and inter-chip communication overhead, could benefit enormously from reduced parameter size. Long-context applications, currently bottlenecked by KV cache memory, could effectively double their context length thanks to 8-bit activations. And edge devices, constrained by memory and compute, become viable targets for models that need neither multiplication nor high-precision storage.

Perhaps most provocatively, the authors call for new hardware designed specifically for 1-bit LLMs. Current GPUs and accelerators are optimized for floating-point operations. If the future of LLMs involves mostly integer addition over ternary weights, the optimal hardware looks quite different from what we have today.

Whether or not 1.58-bit models become the dominant paradigm, BitNet b1.58 makes a compelling case that the era of treating quantization as an afterthought may be coming to an end.
