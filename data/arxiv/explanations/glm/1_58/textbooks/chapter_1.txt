\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 1: Introduction to 1-bit LLMs and Motivation

## The Scaling Challenge in Large Language Models

In recent years, the field of artificial intelligence has witnessed a rapid and transformative growth in the size and capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs). These models, built primarily on the Transformer architecture, have demonstrated remarkable performance across a wide range of natural language processing tasks, from text generation and translation to question answering and code completion. However, this impressive trajectory of scaling has come at a steep cost. As LLMs have grown from hundreds of millions to hundreds of billions of parameters, they have introduced significant challenges for deployment and raised serious concerns about their environmental and economic impact due to high energy consumption.

The core of the challenge lies in the fundamental nature of how these models are represented and computed. Vanilla LLMs operate using 16-bit floating-point values, denoted as FP16 or BF16, meaning that every single parameter in the model is stored as a 16-bit number. Since the bulk of any LLM's computation consists of matrix multiplication operations, the major computational cost arises from the floating-point addition and multiplication operations that these matrix multiplications require. Floating-point multiplication is an especially expensive operation, consuming significantly more energy and requiring more complex circuitry than simpler arithmetic operations. As models scale to billions and trillions of parameters, the sheer volume of these floating-point operations creates a computational bottleneck that limits how fast and how efficiently these models can run.

This scaling challenge is not merely an engineering inconvenience; it has profound implications for the accessibility and sustainability of AI technology. Training and deploying large models requires massive clusters of specialized hardware, such as GPUs or TPUs, which consume enormous amounts of electricity. The environmental footprint of training a single large LLM has been compared to that of several cars over their entire lifetimes. Furthermore, the economic cost of deploying such models — whether in cloud environments or on edge devices — can be prohibitive, limiting the democratization of AI technology to a small number of well-resourced organizations. These challenges motivate the search for fundamentally new approaches to representing and computing LLMs that can maintain their impressive performance while dramatically reducing their computational, memory, and energy costs.

## Limitations of Post-Training Quantization

One widely adopted approach to address the computational and memory challenges of LLMs is post-training quantization. This technique involves taking a model that has already been trained at full precision (i.e., FP16 or BF16) and reducing the precision of its weights and activations after the fact, creating what is known as a low-bit model for inference. The trend in the field has been to progressively move from 16-bit representations to lower bit-widths, such as 4-bit variants, in an effort to reduce memory usage and accelerate computation.

While post-training quantization is widely used in industry LLMs and does provide meaningful reductions in memory and computational requirements, it is fundamentally sub-optimal. The key issue is that the model was originally trained under the assumption that its weights and activations would be represented at full precision. When the precision is reduced after training, the model must contend with quantization errors — the discrepancies between the original full-precision values and their quantized approximations — that it was never trained to handle. These errors accumulate across the many layers and operations of the model, leading to degradation in performance. The model has not learned to be robust to the information loss that quantization introduces, and as a result, the quality of the model's outputs suffers, particularly as the bit-width is reduced to very low levels.

This limitation suggests that simply applying quantization after training is a compromise rather than a solution. A more principled approach would be to design models that are natively trained at low precision from the very beginning, allowing the model to learn and adapt to the constraints of low-bit representation during the training process itself. This is the key insight that motivates the development of 1-bit model architectures, which we will explore in subsequent sections.

## The Memory Bandwidth Bottleneck: DRAM to SRAM Transfer

Beyond the computational cost of floating-point arithmetic, there is another critical bottleneck in LLM inference that is often overlooked: the cost of transferring model parameters from memory to the processing units that perform the computation. During inference, model parameters must be transferred from DRAM (Dynamic Random Access Memory), where they are stored, to the memory of an on-chip accelerator such as SRAM (Static Random Access Memory), where the actual computation takes place. This transfer process can be extremely expensive in terms of both time and energy.

The reason this transfer is so costly lies in the architecture of modern computing systems. DRAM is relatively inexpensive and can store large amounts of data, but it is physically separated from the processing units and requires significant time and energy to access. SRAM, on the other hand, is much faster and more energy-efficient to access but is significantly more expensive to manufacture and thus typically available in much smaller capacities. There have been attempts to enlarge SRAM to improve throughput by reducing the frequency of DRAM-to-SRAM transfers, but this approach introduces significantly higher hardware costs, making it an uneconomical solution for large-scale LLM deployment.

This memory bandwidth bottleneck is particularly problematic for LLMs because of their enormous parameter counts. Every time a forward pass is performed, billions of parameters must be loaded from DRAM to the on-chip memory. The time spent loading these weights can dominate the total inference time, especially for large models, creating a situation where the processing units are starved for data and spend much of their time idle, waiting for the next batch of weights to arrive.

Compared to full-precision models, 1-bit LLMs have a much lower memory footprint from both a capacity and bandwidth standpoint. Since each parameter is represented by only 1 or 1.58 bits instead of 16 bits, the total amount of data that must be transferred from DRAM to SRAM is reduced by roughly an order of magnitude. This dramatic reduction in memory traffic can significantly reduce the cost and time of loading weights, leading to faster and more efficient inference. This advantage becomes increasingly significant as models scale to larger sizes, where the memory bandwidth bottleneck becomes even more pronounced.

## The Emergence of 1-bit Model Architectures (BitNet)

Given the limitations of post-training quantization and the persistent challenges of computational and memory costs, recent work on 1-bit model architectures, such as BitNet, presents a promising and fundamentally different direction for reducing the cost of LLMs while maintaining their performance. The core idea behind BitNet is to train models natively with binary weights — that is, each weight in the model takes on one of two values, typically $+1$ or $-1$ — rather than training at full precision and then quantizing.

The implications of this approach are profound. In vanilla LLMs, where weights are 16-bit floating-point values, the matrix multiplication operations that form the backbone of the model's computation require floating-point addition and multiplication. Floating-point multiplication, in particular, is a complex and energy-intensive operation that involves aligning exponents, multiplying mantissas, and normalizing the result. In contrast, when weights are constrained to $+1$ or $-1$, the matrix multiplication simplifies dramatically. Multiplying any activation value by $+1$ leaves it unchanged, and multiplying by $-1$ simply negates it. As a result, the matrix multiplication in BitNet only involves integer addition operations, completely eliminating the need for expensive multiplication. This saves orders of magnitude in energy cost for LLMs.

This energy saving has a cascading effect on performance. The fundamental limit to compute performance in many chips is power — the amount of heat that can be dissipated determines how fast the chip can operate. By dramatically reducing the energy required for each operation, BitNet effectively allows chips to operate at higher clock speeds or process more data in parallel within the same power budget. In other words, the energy savings can be translated directly into faster computation, making BitNet not just more efficient but also potentially faster than its full-precision counterparts.

BitNet represents a new computation paradigm for LLMs, one that is fundamentally aligned with the constraints and opportunities of low-bit representation. By designing the model architecture and training process around the assumption of binary weights, rather than treating low-bit representation as an afterthought, BitNet opens the door to a new generation of LLMs that are both high-performance and highly efficient.

## From BitNet to BitNet b1.58: Introducing Ternary Weights {-1, 0, 1}

While the original BitNet demonstrated the potential of 1-bit model architectures, it also revealed opportunities for improvement. In this work, the authors introduce BitNet b1.58, a significant variant of BitNet in which every single parameter (or weight) of the LLM is ternary, taking on values from the set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. This seemingly small addition of the value $0$ to the original binary set $\{-1, 1\}$ has profound implications for the model's capabilities and performance.

The inclusion of $0$ as a possible weight value provides a crucial advantage: explicit support for feature filtering. In a neural network, each weight determines the strength of the connection between two neurons. A weight of $+1$ indicates a strong positive connection, $-1$ indicates a strong negative connection, and $0$ indicates no connection at all. In the original BitNet, every connection was either positive or negative — there was no way for the model to "turn off" a connection entirely. By introducing the value $0$, BitNet b1.58 gains the ability to selectively filter features, effectively pruning unimportant connections during the training process itself. This significantly improves the modeling capability of the network, allowing it to learn more nuanced and efficient representations.

The quantization function used in BitNet b1.58 is based on an \emph{absmean} approach. Specifically, the weight matrix $W$ is first scaled by its average absolute value $\gamma$, which is computed as:

$$\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$$

where $n$ and $m$ are the dimensions of the weight matrix. Each weight is then divided by $\gamma$ (plus a small constant $\epsilon$ for numerical stability) and rounded to the nearest integer, clipped to the range $[-1, 1]$:

$$\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$$

where the $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ function is defined as:

$$\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\left(a, \min\left(b, \mathrm{round}(x)\right)\right)$$

This quantization process maps the continuous learned weights to the discrete set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ in a principled manner, preserving the relative magnitudes and signs of the original weights as much as possible.

For activations, BitNet b1.58 follows the same quantization approach as the original BitNet, with one modification: activations are scaled to the range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token, rather than being scaled to $[0, Q_b]$ before non-linear functions. This change eliminates the need for zero-point quantization, which simplifies both implementation and system-level optimization, while introducing negligible effects on performance.

BitNet b1.58 also adopts components from the LLaMA architecture, which has become the de facto backbone for open-source LLMs. Specifically, it uses RMSNorm for normalization, SwiGLU as the activation function, rotary embedding for positional encoding, and removes all biases from the model. This design choice ensures that BitNet b1.58 can be integrated into popular open-source software frameworks with minimal effort, facilitating adoption and further development by the research community.

Importantly, BitNet b1.58 retains all the benefits of the original 1-bit BitNet, including its new computation paradigm that requires almost no multiplication operations for matrix multiplication. The addition of the $0$ weight value does not change this fundamental advantage — multiplying any activation by $0$ simply produces $0$, which can be handled trivially in hardware. At the same time, BitNet b1.58 is much more efficient than FP16 LLM baselines in terms of memory consumption, throughput, and latency. The authors' experiments demonstrate that BitNet b1.58 can match full precision (FP16) baselines in terms of both perplexity and end-task performance, starting from a 3B model size, when using the same configuration (e.g., model size, training tokens, etc.). This is a remarkable result, showing that the explicit feature filtering enabled by ternary weights closes the performance gap that existed with binary weights.

## The Mathematical Significance of 1.58 Bits in the Binary System

The name "BitNet b1.58" naturally raises the question: why 1.58 bits? The answer lies in the fundamental relationship between the number of distinct values a weight can take and the number of bits required to represent those values in the binary system.

In information theory, the number of bits required to represent a value from a set of $N$ distinct possibilities is given by $\log_2(N)$. In the original BitNet, each weight could take one of two values: $+1$ or $-1$. The number of bits required to represent a binary value is $\log_2(2) = 1$ bit, which is why the original BitNet is referred to as a "1-bit" model.

In BitNet b1.58, each weight can take one of three values: $-1$, $0$, or $+1$. The number of bits required to represent a ternary value is $\log_2(3) \approx 1.585$ bits. This is the mathematical origin of the "1.58 bits" in the model's name — it reflects the fact that each weight in the model carries approximately 1.58 bits of information, as it can distinguish among three distinct states rather than two.

This is a subtle but important point. While it might seem natural to think of the model as using "2 bits" (since you need at least 2 binary digits to represent three values: $00$, $01$, and $10$), the information-theoretic measure of $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$ bits more accurately captures the actual information content of each weight. One of the three possible 2-bit patterns (e.g., $11$) is unused, meaning that a naive 2-bit representation would be wasteful. The 1.58-bit framing highlights that ternary weights are, in a precise information-theoretic sense, more efficient than a full 2-bit representation would suggest.

The significance of this measure extends beyond mere nomenclature. It positions BitNet b1.58 in a precise location on the spectrum of model quantization, between the extreme of 1-bit (binary) weights and the more conventional 4-bit or 8-bit quantization approaches. The fact that 1.58 bits is sufficient to match the performance of 16-bit models (at sufficient scale) is a powerful demonstration of the redundancy present in traditional full-precision LLM weights. It suggests that the vast majority of the information encoded in 16-bit floating-point weights is, in a sense, wasted — the model's functional behavior can be captured with less than 10% of the bits typically used.

This insight has profound implications for the future of LLM design. If 1.58 bits per weight is sufficient to match full-precision performance, then the field should reconsider the default assumption that high precision is necessary for effective language modeling. BitNet b1.58 defines a new scaling law and recipe for training new generations of LLMs that are both high-performance and cost-effective. Furthermore, the ternary weight representation enables a new computation paradigm and opens the door for designing specific hardware optimized for 1-bit LLMs — hardware that could exploit the simplicity of ternary arithmetic to achieve dramatic improvements in speed, energy efficiency, and cost.
