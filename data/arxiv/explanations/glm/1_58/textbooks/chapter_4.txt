\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 4: The New Scaling Law and Pareto Improvements

# Defining the New Scaling Law for 1.58-bit LLMs

In the field of Large Language Models (LLMs), a scaling law traditionally describes how model performance, such as perplexity, improves as we increase the number of parameters and training tokens. However, increasing the parameter count in conventional full-precision models—typically represented in $16$-bit floating-point formats like FP16 or BF16—linearly increases the inference cost. This cost manifests in higher memory usage to store the weights, greater latency due to more computation, and increased energy consumption from power-hungry floating-point addition and multiplication operations. The introduction of BitNet b1.58 fundamentally disrupts this dynamic, establishing a new scaling law with respect to model performance and inference cost. 

BitNet b1.58 constrains every single parameter of the LLM to ternary values of $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, which requires only $1.58$ bits to represent in the binary system. By doing so, the matrix multiplication operations that make up the bulk of LLM computation no longer require floating-point multiplications. Instead, they rely solely on integer addition, which is orders of magnitude more energy-efficient. This shift means that as a $1.58$-bit model scales up in size, its inference cost grows at a drastically slower rate compared to a traditional FP16 model. For instance, the paper demonstrates that BitNet b1.58 matches the perplexity of a full-precision FP16 LLaMA LLM at a $3\text{B}$ model size while being $2.71$ times faster and using $3.55$ times less GPU memory. This new scaling law effectively redefines the trade-off between model size and cost, allowing for the deployment of much larger, more capable models at a fraction of the traditional resource expense.

# Efficiency Equivalencies: 13B b1.58 vs. 3B FP16 LLM

To fully grasp the impact of the new scaling law, the paper provides specific efficiency equivalencies that compare the inference costs of $1.58$-bit models against much smaller $16$-bit baselines. The first such equivalence states that a $13\text{B}$ BitNet b1.58 model is more efficient, in terms of latency, memory usage, and energy consumption, than a $3\text{B}$ FP16 LLM. Intuitively, a $13$ billion parameter model has over four times the modeling capacity of a $3$ billion parameter model. In a traditional $16$-bit paradigm, scaling to $13\text{B}$ would require a massive increase in memory and computational resources. 

However, because every weight in the $13\text{B}$ BitNet b1.58 model is restricted to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the memory footprint required to store the model is drastically reduced, allowing it to fit into a smaller memory envelope than a $3\text{B}$ FP16 model. Furthermore, the matrix multiplications are simplified to integer additions, cutting down both the time per output token (latency) and the arithmetic operations energy consumption. This equivalence illustrates a profound advantage: by moving to $1.58$ bits, we can build models with substantially more parameters—leading to better task performance and lower perplexity—while actually reducing the physical resources required to run them below that of a much smaller, traditional FP16 model.

# Efficiency Equivalencies: 30B b1.58 vs. 7B FP16 LLM

The second efficiency equivalence outlined in the paper scales this concept up further: a $30\text{B}$ BitNet b1.58 model is more efficient, in terms of latency, memory usage, and energy consumption, than a $7\text{B}$ FP16 LLM. A $30$ billion parameter model is approximately $4.2$ times larger than a $7$ billion parameter model. In standard LLM design, a $7\text{B}$ FP16 model is already quite expensive to deploy, requiring significant GPU memory and bandwidth to transfer weights from DRAM to on-chip accelerators. 

The paper notes that as model size scales, the speed-up and memory savings of BitNet b1.58 compared to FP16 baselines actually increase. This is because the primary computational bottleneck in LLMs comes from the linear layers (e.g., nn.Linear), and the cost of these layers grows with the model size. In a $30\text{B}$ b1.58 model, this growth is handled through highly optimized integer additions rather than costly floating-point operations. The paper estimates that BitNet b1.58 saves $71.4$ times the arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on $7\text{nm}$ chips compared to FP16 models. Consequently, a $30\text{B}$ b1.58 model, which would naturally offer far superior performance, operates with lower latency, occupies less memory, and consumes less energy than a $7\text{B}$ FP16 model. This shifts the paradigm of how large models can be deployed, making $30\text{B}$-scale models accessible under the resource constraints previously reserved for much smaller $7\text{B}$ models.

# Efficiency Equivalencies: 70B b1.58 vs. 13B FP16 LLM

The third equivalence demonstrates the extreme end of this scaling law: a $70\text{B}$ BitNet b1.58 model is more efficient, in terms of latency, memory usage, and energy consumption, than a $13\text{B}$ FP16 LLM. A $70$ billion parameter model is over five times larger than a $13$ billion parameter model. The paper provides concrete metrics to illustrate this, noting that a $70\text{B}$ BitNet b1.58 model is $4.1$ times faster than an FP16 LLaMA LLM of the exact same size. 

When evaluating throughput on two $80\text{GB}$ A100 cards, the $70\text{B}$ b1.58 model can support up to $11$ times the batch size of the FP16 baseline, resulting in an $8.9$ times higher throughput. These massive efficiency gains are achieved because the $1.58$-bit architecture fundamentally alters the arithmetic requirements of the network. Even though the $70\text{B}$ b1.58 model is vastly larger and more capable than a $13\text{B}$ FP16 model, the fundamental efficiency of its $1.58$-bit matrix multiplication means it costs less in every major inference metric. Furthermore, the paper notes that these latency and memory metrics were measured using a $2$-bit kernel, suggesting there is still room for optimization to reduce the cost even further. This equivalence proves that we can scale models into the tens of billions of parameters while maintaining the inference cost profile of a substantially smaller, traditional model.

# Understanding Pareto Improvements in LLM Design

In economics and engineering, a Pareto improvement is a change to a system that improves at least one metric without making any other metric worse. In the context of LLM design, achieving a Pareto improvement means increasing model performance (such as perplexity or end-task accuracy) without increasing the inference cost, or alternatively, reducing the inference cost without sacrificing performance. The paper explicitly states that BitNet b1.58 represents a Pareto improvement over the state-of-the-art LLM models, defining a new Pareto frontier for model design.

The results demonstrate that BitNet b1.58 matches the full-precision FP16 baseline in terms of both perplexity and end-task performance starting from a $3\text{B}$ model size, when using the same configuration of model size and training tokens. For instance, a $3.9\text{B}$ BitNet b1.58 model performs significantly better than a $3\text{B}$ FP16 LLaMA LLM, while simultaneously being $2.4$ times faster and consuming $3.32$ times less memory. Any point on the old Pareto frontier, defined by FP16 models, is now dominated by a point on the new frontier defined by $1.58$-bit models. You can always choose a BitNet b1.58 model that either matches the performance of an FP16 model at a fraction of the cost, or vastly exceeds the performance of an FP16 model for the exact same cost. The ability to scale up to $70\text{B}$ parameters while remaining more efficient than a $13\text{B}$ FP16 model proves that $1.58$-bit LLMs allow us to push the boundaries of model performance without hitting the traditional computational, memory, and energy walls.
