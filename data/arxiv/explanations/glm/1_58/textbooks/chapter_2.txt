Chapter 2: The BitNet b1.58 Architecture

## The BitNet Architecture: Replacing nn.Linear with BitLinear

The BitNet architecture represents a fundamental reimagining of the Transformer model, one of the most widely used architectures in modern natural language processing. At its core, the standard Transformer relies heavily on linear layers—implemented in popular deep learning frameworks as `nn.Linear`—to perform matrix multiplications that constitute the bulk of the model's computation. In a conventional Transformer, these linear layers store weights as 16-bit floating-point numbers (either FP16 or BF16), and the matrix multiplication operations involve floating-point additions and multiplications, which are computationally expensive in terms of both time and energy.

BitNet's key innovation is to replace every standard `nn.Linear` operation with a specialized module called `BitLinear`. The critical distinction is that `BitLinear` stores its parameters not as full-precision floating-point values, but as highly quantized values—in the case of BitNet b1.58, each weight is constrained to be one of three values: $-1$, $0$, or $+1$. This ternary representation is what gives rise to the "1.58-bit" designation, since representing three possible values requires $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$ bits in the binary system.

The implications of this replacement are profound. When all weights are ternary, the matrix multiplication that lies at the heart of every linear transformation no longer requires any floating-point multiplications. Instead, multiplying an input by a weight that is $-1$, $0$, or $+1$ simply negates, zeroes out, or preserves the input value, respectively. The accumulation of these results then only requires integer additions. This transformation from floating-point multiply-accumulate operations to integer addition operations yields enormous savings in energy consumption and opens the door to fundamentally new approaches to hardware design for language model inference. The BitNet b1.58 architecture inherits this computational paradigm entirely from the original BitNet, while introducing several important modifications that enhance both its modeling capability and its practical deployability.

## Training from Scratch: 1.58-bit Weights and 8-bit Activations

A crucial aspect of BitNet b1.58 is that it is trained from scratch with quantized parameters, rather than being trained at full precision and then quantized after the fact. This stands in contrast to the widely used post-training quantization techniques, which take a model that has already been trained at 16-bit precision and attempt to reduce its precision for inference. The authors note that post-training quantization is inherently sub-optimal, as the model was never trained to operate effectively at reduced precision.

By training from scratch with 1.58-bit weights, the model learns to represent its knowledge entirely within the constraints of the ternary weight space. Every parameter throughout the entire model—the attention matrices, the feed-forward network matrices, and all other linear transformations—takes on values of exactly $-1$, $0$, or $+1$. There are no exceptions and no mixed-precision components within the core model parameters.

The activations, however, are quantized to 8 bits rather than to the same 1.58-bit precision as the weights. This asymmetry between weight and activation precision is a deliberate design choice. Activations, which represent the flow of information through the network, benefit from higher resolution to maintain the fidelity of the representations being passed between layers. The 8-bit activation quantization provides sufficient dynamic range while still being significantly more efficient than the 16-bit activations used in standard Transformer models. As we will discuss later, this reduction in activation precision from 16 bits to 8 bits also has important implications for long-sequence inference, as it directly impacts the size of the key-value (KV) cache.

## Weight Quantization: The Absmean Quantization Function

The central technical challenge in training a model with ternary weights is the process of quantization—converting the continuous-valued weights produced by gradient descent into discrete values from the set $\{-1, 0, +1\}$. BitNet b1.58 introduces a specific quantization function called the **absmean quantization function** to accomplish this transformation.

The absmean quantization function operates in two conceptual steps. First, it scales the weight matrix by dividing each element by a scaling factor $\gamma$, which is computed as the average of the absolute values of all elements in the weight matrix. This normalization step centers the distribution of weights around a range where they can be meaningfully rounded to one of the three target values. Second, each scaled weight value is rounded to the nearest integer and then clipped to ensure it falls within the range $[-1, +1]$.

Formally, given a weight matrix $W$ of dimensions $n \times m$, the quantized weight matrix $\widetilde{W}$ is computed as:

$$\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$$

where the scaling factor $\gamma$ is defined as:

$$\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$$

and $\epsilon$ is a small constant added for numerical stability to prevent division by zero. The use of the average absolute value as the scaling factor is what gives this function its name—"absmean" referring to the absolute mean of the weight matrix.

This approach is elegant in its simplicity. By normalizing the weights by their average magnitude, the function ensures that weights that are large in magnitude relative to the average will be quantized to $+1$ or $-1$, while weights that are small in magnitude will be quantized to $0$. This creates a natural thresholding mechanism that is adaptive to the specific distribution of each weight matrix, rather than relying on fixed thresholds that might not be appropriate for all layers or all stages of training.

## The RoundClip Mechanism and Weight Scaling

The RoundClip function is a composite operation that combines rounding and clipping into a single transformation. It is defined as:

$$\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x)))$$

To understand this function, it is helpful to break it down into its constituent operations. The innermost operation is $\mathrm{round}(x)$, which rounds the input $x$ to the nearest integer. For example, $\mathrm{round}(0.3) = 0$, $\mathrm{round}(0.7) = 1$, $\mathrm{round}(-0.6) = -1$, and $\mathrm{round}(1.8) = 2$. The rounding operation maps the continuous values to integers but does not constrain them to any particular range.

The clipping operation, implemented through the combination of $\max$ and $\min$ functions, then constrains the rounded values to lie within the interval $[a, b]$. In the context of BitNet b1.58, the clipping bounds are $a = -1$ and $b = +1$, so the function constrains all outputs to be one of $\{-1, 0, +1\}$. The $\min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))$ operation ensures that no value exceeds the upper bound $b$, and the $\max(a, \cdot)$ operation ensures that no value falls below the lower bound $a$. Values that round to $+2$ or $-2$ (which can happen when a weight is significantly larger than the average absolute value) are clipped back to $+1$ or $-1$, respectively.

The scaling factor $\gamma$ plays a critical role in determining how the weight values are distributed across the three quantization levels. Because $\gamma$ is the mean of the absolute values of the weight matrix, approximately half of the weights (those with absolute values greater than $\gamma$) will be scaled to values with magnitude greater than 1 before rounding, and approximately half (those with absolute values less than $\gamma$) will be scaled to values with magnitude less than 1. After rounding, the former group will be assigned to $+1$ or $-1$, while the latter group will largely be assigned to $0$, depending on the exact distribution. This creates a natural sparsity in the quantized weight matrix, as many weights will be quantized to zero, which as we will see has important implications for the model's modeling capabilities.

## Activation Quantization: Per-Token Scaling and Zero-Point Removal

While the weight quantization in BitNet b1.58 represents a departure from the original BitNet, the activation quantization follows a similar approach with one important modification. In the original BitNet, activations were scaled to the range $[0, Q_b]$ before applying non-linear functions, where $Q_b$ represents the maximum value representable in the quantization scheme. This approach required the use of zero-point quantization, a technique from the quantization literature that handles the asymmetry of activation distributions that are not centered around zero.

In BitNet b1.58, the authors make a simplifying change: activations are scaled to the range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ on a per-token basis, and the zero-point quantization is removed entirely. Per-token scaling means that for each token in a sequence, the activation values are independently scaled based on the range of values present in that specific token's activation vector. This is in contrast to per-tensor scaling, where a single scaling factor would be applied to the entire activation tensor.

The motivation for removing the zero-point quantization is primarily one of implementation simplicity and system-level optimization. Zero-point quantization introduces additional computational overhead during both the forward pass and the backward pass, as the zero-point offset must be tracked and applied throughout the quantization and dequantization process. By scaling activations symmetrically around zero (to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ rather than $[0, Q_b]$), the quantization process becomes simpler and more amenable to hardware optimization.

Importantly, the authors note that this simplification introduces negligible effects on performance. This finding suggests that the asymmetric activation distributions that motivated the use of zero-point quantization in prior work do not pose a significant challenge for the BitNet b1.58 architecture, possibly because the ternary weights already provide sufficient flexibility to compensate for any asymmetry in the activation distributions.

## LLaMA-alike Components: RMSNorm, SwiGLU, and Rotary Embedding

Beyond the quantization functions, BitNet b1.58 incorporates several architectural components from the LLaMA family of models. LLaMA has become the de facto backbone for open-source large language models, and its architectural choices have been widely adopted and validated by the research community. By aligning with these choices, BitNet b1.58 ensures that it can benefit from the ecosystem of tools and software that have been developed around LLaMA.

The first LLaMA-alike component is **RMSNorm** (Root Mean Square Normalization), which replaces the standard LayerNorm used in the original Transformer architecture. RMSNorm normalizes activations by dividing each element by the root mean square of the activation vector, without subtracting the mean. This simplification reduces computational overhead while maintaining the normalization benefits that help stabilize training.

The second component is **SwiGLU** (Swish-Gated Linear Unit), which is used as the activation function in the feed-forward network. SwiGLU extends the standard GLU (Gated Linear Unit) by using the Swish activation function as the gating mechanism. The feed-forward network in a SwiGLU-based Transformer has a structure where the output is computed as the element-wise product of two linear transformations, with one of them passed through a Swish activation. This gated mechanism has been shown to improve model performance compared to the standard ReLU-based feed-forward networks.

The third component is **rotary embedding** (RoPE), which provides a method for encoding positional information in the attention mechanism. Unlike absolute positional embeddings, which add a fixed positional encoding to the input, rotary embeddings apply a rotation to the query and key vectors based on their position in the sequence. This rotation-based approach allows the model to naturally capture relative positional relationships and has become a standard choice in modern Transformer-based language models.

Finally, BitNet b1.58 removes all biases from the model. Biases are additive parameters that are typically added to the output of linear transformations. Removing biases is consistent with the philosophy of the quantized architecture, as it reduces the number of parameters and simplifies the model structure without significantly impacting performance, as has been observed in the LLaMA models.

## Modeling Capability: Feature Filtering through Explicit Zero Weights

One of the most significant innovations in BitNet b1.58 compared to the original BitNet is the inclusion of $0$ as a possible weight value. The original BitNet used binary weights constrained to $\{-1, +1\}$, meaning every weight had a non-zero value. BitNet b1.58 expands this to ternary weights $\{-1, 0, +1\}$, and this seemingly small change has profound implications for the model's modeling capabilities.

The inclusion of explicit zero weights provides what the authors describe as "explicit support for feature filtering." When a weight is $0$, it effectively disconnects the corresponding input feature from the output, as the contribution of that input to the output is exactly zero regardless of the input value. This is qualitatively different from the binary case, where every input feature always contributes to the output (either positively or negatively). With ternary weights, the model can learn to selectively ignore certain input features for certain outputs, creating a form of intrinsic sparsity in the weight matrices.

This feature filtering capability addresses a fundamental limitation of binary-weight models. In a binary model with weights $\{-1, +1\}$, every neuron in a layer is connected to every neuron in the previous layer with a non-zero weight. The model can only modulate the strength of these connections by adjusting the magnitude of the activations, not by completely removing a connection. The ternary weights in BitNet b1.58 remove this constraint, allowing the model to learn sparse connectivity patterns that can more efficiently represent certain types of computations.

The authors note that this explicit feature filtering "significantly improves the performance of 1-bit LLMs." This improvement is borne out in the experimental results, where BitNet b1.58 is able to match the performance of full-precision FP16 LLaMA models of the same size and training configuration, starting from a model size of 3 billion parameters. The original binary BitNet, without the ability to zero out weights, would struggle to achieve this level of performance, as it lacks the flexibility to represent sparse transformations.

## Ecosystem Compatibility: Integration with Huggingface, vLLM, and llama.cpp

A critical practical consideration in the design of BitNet b1.58 is its compatibility with the existing open-source ecosystem for large language models. The authors explicitly note that their adoption of LLaMA-alike components is motivated by a desire to "embrace the open-source community," and they highlight the integration with three major open-source projects: Huggingface, vLLM, and llama.cpp.

**Huggingface** provides the most widely used libraries and tools for working with Transformer-based language models, including the `transformers` library for model loading and inference, and the `huggingface_hub` for model distribution. By using LLaMA-alike components, BitNet b1.58 can be integrated into the Huggingface ecosystem with minimal effort, allowing researchers and practitioners to load, fine-tune, and deploy BitNet b1.58 models using familiar interfaces.

**vLLM** is a high-throughput inference engine designed specifically for large language models. It implements techniques such as paged attention for efficient memory management and continuous batching for improved throughput. Compatibility with vLLM means that BitNet b1.58 can benefit from these optimization techniques for production deployment scenarios where inference throughput is a critical concern.

**llama.cpp** is a popular lightweight inference framework that enables running LLaMA-family models on consumer hardware, including CPUs and low-end GPUs. It is particularly important for democratizing access to language models, as it allows users without access to expensive data center hardware to run models locally. The compatibility with llama.cpp means that BitNet b1.58 models, with their significantly reduced memory and energy requirements, are particularly well-suited for deployment on resource-constrained devices, including edge and mobile devices.

The emphasis on ecosystem compatibility reflects an understanding that the practical impact of a new model architecture depends not only on its technical merits but also on its accessibility to the broader community. By designing BitNet b1.58 to be a drop-in replacement for LLaMA components while replacing the linear layers with BitLinear modules, the authors ensure that the model can be adopted incrementally, with existing tools and workflows requiring only minimal modifications.
