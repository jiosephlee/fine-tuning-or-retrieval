# Introduction to Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models

# The Role of Reinforcement Learning in Advancing Language Model Capabilities

Reinforcement learning has emerged as a pivotal paradigm for scaling language models, representing a fundamental shift in how we train these systems to tackle increasingly sophisticated problems. While traditional supervised learning approaches have been enormously successful in teaching language models to predict the next token in a sequence, reinforcement learning introduces a fundamentally different optimization framework — one in which the model learns by interacting with an environment, receiving feedback in the form of rewards, and adjusting its behavior to maximize those rewards over time. This shift from prediction to interaction has opened up new possibilities for what language models can achieve.

To understand why reinforcement learning has become so important, it helps to consider the limitations of supervised learning. In supervised learning, a language model is trained on pairs of inputs and desired outputs, learning to mimic the patterns in the training data. However, this approach has a critical limitation: it can only teach the model to reproduce behaviors that are already represented in the training data. If we want a model to solve a novel mathematics problem or write a correct program for a previously unseen specification, supervised learning alone may be insufficient — there may be no example in the training data that directly teaches the model how to approach that specific problem.

Reinforcement learning addresses this limitation by allowing the model to explore different responses to a query and learn from the feedback it receives. In the context of language models, this typically involves presenting the model with a query $x$, allowing it to generate a response $y$, and then scoring that response with a verifier $r$ that produces a reward $r(x, y) \in [0, 1]$. The model then adjusts its parameters to increase the likelihood of generating responses that receive high rewards and decrease the likelihood of generating responses that receive low rewards. Through this iterative process of generation, evaluation, and adjustment, the model gradually learns to produce better responses.

The notation used throughout this chapter follows standard conventions in the field. An autoregressive language model parameterized by $\theta$ is defined as a policy $\pi_\theta$. Given a query $x$ from a query set $\mathcal{D}$ and a response $y$ to that query, the likelihood of the response under the policy is denoted as $\pi_\theta(y | x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_t | x, y_{<t})$, where $|y|$ denotes the number of tokens in $y$ and $y_{<t}$ denotes the tokens preceding position $t$. This factorization reflects the autoregressive nature of language generation: each token is generated conditional on the query and all previously generated tokens.

The significance of reinforcement learning for language models extends beyond simply improving performance on benchmarks. By enabling models to learn from feedback rather than just from examples, RL opens the door to a form of learning that is more aligned with how we evaluate the quality of language model outputs. When we use a language model to solve a math problem, we care whether the answer is correct — not whether the response matches some reference solution. Reinforcement learning allows us to optimize directly for this kind of outcome-based evaluation, making it a natural fit for tasks where the quality of a response can be objectively assessed.

# Deep Reasoning and Long-Form Generation Tasks Enabled by Large-Scale RL

One of the most remarkable developments enabled by large-scale reinforcement learning is the ability of language models to undertake deeper and longer reasoning processes. Through substantial investment in RL training, language models have developed the capability to tackle sophisticated problems that were previously beyond their reach, including competition-level mathematics and programming. These tasks require not just surface-level pattern matching, but the ability to construct multi-step arguments, verify intermediate results, and maintain coherence over extended sequences of reasoning.

The connection between reinforcement learning and deep reasoning is natural when we consider the nature of the tasks involved. Competition-level mathematics problems, for example, often require a model to explore different approaches, identify productive strategies, and work through complex calculations — processes that unfold over many tokens of generation. Similarly, programming tasks require the model to understand a specification, design an algorithm, implement it correctly, and consider edge cases. These are not tasks that can be solved with a single token or a short response; they require sustained, coherent generation over long sequences.

Reinforcement learning is particularly well-suited to training models for these tasks because the reward signal — whether a mathematical answer is correct, or whether a program passes its test cases — naturally evaluates the quality of the entire reasoning process, not just individual tokens. When a model generates a long response to solve a mathematics problem, the verifier $r$ evaluates the final answer and assigns a reward $r(x, y) \in [0, 1]$ to the entire sequence. This sequence-level evaluation aligns naturally with the structure of the task: the quality of the reasoning process is judged by whether it leads to the correct answer, and the model is incentivized to improve its reasoning strategy as a whole.

However, the shift toward long-form generation also introduces significant technical challenges. As response lengths increase, the computational cost of training grows substantially, necessitating larger rollout batch sizes to maximize hardware utilization. To improve sample efficiency, it has become standard practice to partition a large batch of rollout data into multiple mini-batches for gradient updates. This procedure introduces what is known as an off-policy learning setting: the responses $y$ used for training are sampled from an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ rather than the current policy $\pi_\theta$ being optimized. This mismatch between the sampling distribution and the optimization target is a fundamental challenge that must be addressed by any practical RL algorithm, and it is the source of many of the stability issues that we will discuss in subsequent sections.

The importance of this off-policy setting cannot be overstated. When we sample responses from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ but want to optimize $\pi_\theta$, we need some mechanism to account for the fact that the responses we have were generated by a different policy than the one we are trying to improve. This is where importance sampling comes in — a technique that allows us to estimate expectations under one distribution using samples from another. As we will see, the correct application of importance sampling is central to the design of stable and effective RL algorithms for language models.

# Stability Challenges in Training Gigantic Language Models with RL

As language models have grown in size and complexity, maintaining stable and robust training dynamics has become the foremost prerequisite for successfully scaling reinforcement learning with greater computational investment. Current state-of-the-art RL algorithms, exemplified by Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), exhibit severe stability issues when training gigantic language models. These issues are not merely inconveniences — they can fundamentally undermine the training process and prevent researchers from pushing the capability boundaries of language models through continued RL training.

To understand the sources of instability, we must first understand the algorithms that are currently in use. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a foundational algorithm that constrains policy updates within a proximal region of the old policy through a clipping mechanism. The PPO objective is:

$$\mathcal{J}_\text{PPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)} \left[ \frac{1}{|y|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \min \left( w_t(\theta) \widehat{A}_t, \, \mathrm{clip}(w_t(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon) \widehat{A}_t \right) \right],$$

where the importance ratio of token $y_t$ is defined as $w_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t | x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t | x, y_{<t})}$, the advantage $\widehat{A}_t$ is estimated by a value model, and $\varepsilon$ is the clipping range. A critical limitation of PPO is its heavy reliance on the value model, which typically has a similar size to the policy model, introducing considerable memory and computational burden. Furthermore, the effectiveness of PPO hinges on the reliability of its value estimates, and acquiring a reliable value model that can scale to longer responses and more complex tasks is inherently challenging.

GRPO addresses the value model problem by computing the relative advantage of each response within a group of responses to the same query, thereby bypassing the need for a separate value model. The GRPO objective is:

$$\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \min \left( w_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t}, \, \mathrm{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon) \widehat{A}_{i,t} \right) \right],$$

where $G$ is the group size, and the advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ is computed as:

$$\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)},$$

with all tokens in $y_i$ sharing the same advantage. While GRPO eliminates the value model, it introduces a different and arguably more fundamental problem through its use of token-level importance ratios.

The core issue identified in the paper is that GRPO's objective is ill-posed due to a misapplication of importance sampling weights. The principle of importance sampling states that to estimate the expectation of a function $f$ under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$ using samples from a behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$, we write:

$$\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} [f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}} \left[ \frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)} f(z) \right].$$

Crucially, this relies on averaging over multiple samples ($N \gg 1$) from the behavior distribution for the importance weight to effectively correct for the distributional mismatch. In GRPO, however, the importance weight $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$ is applied at each token position $t$, based on a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from each next-token distribution. This single-sample weight fails to perform the intended distribution-correction role and instead introduces high-variance noise into the training gradients.

This noise is particularly problematic because it accumulates over long sequences. As response length increases — which is exactly what is needed for deep reasoning tasks — the cumulative effect of this per-token noise grows, and the clipping mechanism can actually exacerbate the problem. The combination of high-variance token-level importance weights, sequence length effects, and clipping creates a perfect storm of instability that can derail training.

# Model Collapse: Causes, Consequences, and Irreversibility

The instability issues described above do not merely slow down training or cause temporary setbacks — they can lead to catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. Model collapse is a failure mode in which the language model's performance degrades catastrophically during RL training, rendering it unable to perform the tasks it was previously capable of. The paper documents that this collapse is often irreversible: once it occurs, resuming training is unavailing, even when reverting to a previous checkpoint and meticulously tuning hyperparameters such as the clipping ranges, extending generation length, or switching the RL queries.

The causes of model collapse can be traced directly to the ill-posed nature of the GRPO objective. The token-level importance weights introduce high-variance noise into the training gradients, and this noise progressively accumulates with increased response length. The clipping mechanism, which is supposed to prevent overly off-policy samples from being involved in gradient estimation, actually amplifies the problem in subtle ways. When a token-level importance ratio falls outside the clipping range, that token's contribution to the gradient is modified, but the modification is applied independently to each token. This means that the gradient signal can be distorted in unpredictable ways, with different tokens receiving different treatment based on their individual importance ratios.

To understand why this leads to irreversible collapse, consider the gradient of the GRPO objective (with $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$):

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right].$$

Notice that the tokens are weighted according to their respective importance weights $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$. These weights can vary among $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ (for $\widehat{A}_i > 0$) or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ (for $\widehat{A}_i < 0$), and these unequal weights are not negligible. Their impact accumulates and leads to unpredictable consequences as training progresses. Over many gradient updates, these unequal and noisy per-token weights can push the model's parameters into regions of the loss landscape from which recovery is impossible — the model has effectively forgotten how to generate coherent or useful responses.

The irreversibility of this collapse is particularly troubling from a practical standpoint. In typical training scenarios, if a model's performance degrades, one might expect to recover by reverting to an earlier checkpoint or adjusting hyperparameters. However, the paper reports that once collapse occurs in GRPO training, these remedies are ineffective. This suggests that the collapse is not simply a matter of the model being in a bad region of parameter space that could be escaped with better hyperparameters; rather, it appears that the fundamental mechanism driving the training — the noisy token-level gradients — is itself incapable of guiding the model back to productive learning.

The problem is further compounded for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, which introduce their own unique stability challenges. In MoE models, the sparse activation nature means that different experts (sub-networks) are activated for different tokens. The paper found that when using GRPO, the expert-activation volatility of MoE models can prevent RL training from converging properly. After gradient updates, the experts activated for the same response can change significantly — for example, with the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, roughly 10% of the experts activated under the new policy $\pi_\theta$ are different from those under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ for the same rollout sample. This phenomenon makes the token-level importance ratios fluctuate drastically, further invalidating them and hindering normal convergence.

# Overview of GSPO as a Solution: Sequence-Level Optimization for Stable Training

Given the fundamental issues with token-level optimization in GRPO, the paper proposes Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), a new RL algorithm that addresses these problems by moving the unit of optimization from the token level to the sequence level. The key insight motivating GSPO is a core principle: the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of reward. Since the reward is granted to the entire sequence, applying off-policy correction at the token level is problematic. GSPO addresses this by defining the importance ratio based on sequence likelihood and performing sequence-level clipping, rewarding, and optimization.

The GSPO objective is defined as:

$$\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \min \left( s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i, \, \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon) \widehat{A}_i \right) \right],$$

where the advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ is computed in the same group-based manner as in GRPO:

$$\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)},$$

and the crucial innovation is the definition of the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ based on sequence likelihood:

$$s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \right).$$

This sequence-level importance ratio has a clear theoretical meaning: it reflects how far the response $y$ sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)$ deviates from $\pi_\theta(\cdot | x)$, which naturally aligns with the sequence-level reward and serves as a meaningful indicator for the clipping mechanism. GSPO applies clipping to entire responses rather than individual tokens, excluding overly off-policy samples from gradient estimation in a way that matches the sequence-level nature of both rewarding and optimization.

An important design choice in GSPO is the use of length normalization in $s_i(\theta)$, achieved by raising the sequence likelihood ratio to the power of $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$. This normalization serves two critical purposes. First, it reduces the variance of the importance ratio. Without normalization, the likelihood changes of a few tokens could result in dramatic fluctuations of the sequence-level importance ratio, especially for long sequences where the product of many per-token ratios can become extremely large or small. Second, it controls the importance ratio within a unified numerical range, so that responses of different lengths can be compared fairly and the same clipping range can be applied to all. It is worth noting that the clipping ranges in GSPO differ in order of magnitude from those in GRPO due to the distinct definitions of importance ratios.

The advantage of GSPO over GRPO becomes clear when we examine the gradient. The gradient of the GSPO objective (omitting clipping) is:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right].$$

The fundamental distinction between GSPO and GRPO lies in how they weight the gradients of the log likelihoods of tokens. In GRPO, tokens are weighted by their individual and unequal importance weights, which can vary significantly and introduce instability. In GSPO, all tokens in a response are weighted equally by the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$, eliminating this instability factor entirely. This equal weighting ensures that the gradient signal is coherent and aligned with the sequence-level reward, rather than being distorted by per-token noise.

The paper also introduces a token-level objective variant called GSPO-token, designed for scenarios like multi-turn RL where finer-grained advantage adjustment is desired. The GSPO-token objective is:

$$\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \min \left( s_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t}, \, \mathrm{clip}(s_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon) \widehat{A}_{i,t} \right) \right],$$

where $s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})]}$, and $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ denotes the stop-gradient operation. The term $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})]}$ has a numerical value of 1, so $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$. When all tokens share the same advantage ($\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$), GSPO-token and GSPO are numerically identical in objective, clipping condition, and gradient, while GSPO-token enjoys the flexibility of adjusting advantages per token.

Empirically, GSPO demonstrates significant superiority over GRPO. In experiments with a cold-start model fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, GSPO proceeds stably throughout training and delivers continuous performance improvement through increasing training compute, regularly updating the query set, and extending generation length. Notably, despite clipping significantly more tokens than GRPO — a difference of two orders of magnitude in clipping fractions — GSPO still achieves higher training efficiency. This counter-intuitive finding indicates that GRPO's token-level gradient estimates are inherently noisy and inefficient, while GSPO's sequence-level approach provides a more reliable and effective learning signal.

For MoE models specifically, GSPO fundamentally resolves the expert-activation volatility issue. Because GSPO focuses only on the sequence likelihood $\pi_\theta(y_i | x)$ and is not sensitive to individual token likelihoods $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$, and since the MoE model always maintains its language modeling capability, the sequence likelihood does not fluctuate drastically even when expert routing changes. This eliminates the need for complex workarounds like the Routing Replay strategy that was previously required for GRPO training of MoE models, simplifying the training process and allowing the model to leverage its full capacity.

# The Contributions of GSPO to the Qwen3 Model Family

The practical impact of GSPO extends far beyond theoretical improvements. The algorithm has been successfully applied to the RL training of the latest Qwen3 models, contributing to remarkable improvements in their performance. This real-world validation demonstrates that GSPO is not merely an academic contribution but a practical algorithmic foundation that enables the continued advancement of large-scale RL training with language models.

The contributions of GSPO to the Qwen3 model family span several dimensions. First, GSPO has enabled stable and continuous training of large language models at scale, delivering performance improvements on challenging benchmarks including AIME'24 (average Pass@1 over 32 samplings), LiveCodeBench (202410-202502, average Pass@1 over 8 samplings), and CodeForces (Elo Rating). The training with GSPO proceeds stably throughout, and the model can deliver continuous performance improvement through increasing training compute, regularly updating the query set, and extending the generation length. This stability is in stark contrast to the catastrophic and irreversible model collapse that can occur with GRPO.

Second, GSPO has demonstrated superior training efficiency over GRPO, achieving better training accuracy and benchmark performance under the same training compute and consumed queries. This efficiency gain is particularly significant given the computational cost of RL training for large language models. The paper notes that despite clipping a much larger fraction of tokens — two orders of magnitude more than GRPO — GSPO still achieves higher training efficiency. This suggests that the tokens clipped by GSPO are ones that would have contributed noisy or misleading gradient signals, and their removal actually improves the quality of learning.

Third, GSPO has inherently resolved the stability challenges in RL training of large Mixture-of-Experts models, eliminating the need for complex stabilization strategies. Previously, training MoE models with GRPO required a technique called Routing Replay, which caches the activated experts in the old policy and replays these routing modes when computing importance ratios. While this approach enabled GRPO to converge, it incurred additional memory and communication overhead and could limit the actual capacity of the MoE model. GSPO, by focusing on sequence likelihood rather than individual token likelihoods, is not sensitive to expert routing changes and thus eliminates the need for Routing Replay entirely. This not only simplifies the training process but also allows the MoE model to leverage its full capacity without artificial constraints.

Fourth, GSPO has the potential for simplifying RL infrastructure. Given the precision discrepancies between training engines (such as Megatron) and inference engines (such as SGLang and vLLM), it is common practice to use the training engine to recompute the likelihoods of sampled responses under the old policy. However, since GSPO uses only sequence-level likelihoods rather than token-level likelihoods for optimization, and sequence-level likelihoods are much more tolerant of precision discrepancies, GSPO makes it possible to directly use the likelihoods returned by the inference engine for optimization. This avoids the need for recomputation with the training engine, which can be especially beneficial in scenarios like partial rollout and multi-turn RL, and in training-inference disaggregated frameworks.

These contributions collectively demonstrate that GSPO is a robust and scalable algorithmic foundation for RL training of language models. By addressing the fundamental issues in GRPO's design — the ill-posed token-level objective, the high-variance noise from single-sample importance weights, and the mismatch between the unit of optimization and the unit of reward — GSPO provides a principled and empirically validated approach to stable, efficient, and performant RL training. The success of GSPO with the Qwen3 model family suggests that it will continue to play a crucial role as researchers push the boundaries of what language models can achieve through large-scale reinforcement learning.

# Foundations: PPO and GRPO

## Notation and Preliminaries

Before delving into the specifics of reinforcement learning algorithms for large language models, it is essential to establish a clear and consistent notational framework. In the context of this chapter, an autoregressive language model parameterized by $\theta$ is defined as a policy $\pi_\theta$. The policy takes as input a query, denoted by $x$, and produces a response, denoted by $y$. The set of all queries from which we sample is denoted as $\mathcal{D}$. Given a response $y$ to a query $x$, the likelihood of that response under the policy $\pi_\theta$ is expressed as $\pi_\theta(y | x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_t | x, y_{<t})$, where $|y|$ denotes the number of tokens in the response $y$, and $y_{<t}$ refers to the sequence of tokens preceding position $t$. This factorization reflects the autoregressive nature of language generation, where each token is generated conditioned on the query and all previously generated tokens.

A query-response pair $(x, y)$ can be scored by a verifier $r$, which produces a reward $r(x, y) \in [0, 1]$. The verifier serves as the feedback mechanism that evaluates the quality of a generated response, assigning a scalar reward that quantifies how desirable the response is. This reward signal is the driving force behind reinforcement learning: by optimizing the policy to generate responses that receive higher rewards, the model gradually improves its performance on the task at hand. The restriction of the reward to the interval $[0, 1]$ is a design choice that bounds the magnitude of the reward signal, which can help in maintaining numerical stability during training.

Understanding these basic components — the policy, the query, the response, the verifier, and the reward — provides the foundation upon which the more complex algorithmic machinery of PPO and GRPO is built. The interplay between these elements, combined with the mathematical tools of importance sampling, advantage estimation, and clipping, forms the core of modern reinforcement learning algorithms for language models.

## Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO): Objective Function and Clipping Mechanism

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a foundational reinforcement learning algorithm that addresses a key challenge in policy optimization: how to make meaningful improvements to the policy without taking excessively large steps that could destabilize training. The central idea behind PPO is to constrain the policy update to remain within a proximal region of the old policy, ensuring that the new policy does not deviate too drastically from the policy that generated the training data. This constraint is implemented through a clipping mechanism that limits the magnitude of the importance ratio, a quantity that measures how much the new policy differs from the old policy.

Using samples generated from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, PPO optimizes the following objective function:

$$\mathcal{J}_\text{PPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)} \left[ \frac{1}{|y|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \min \left( w_{t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{t}, \, \mathrm{clip} \left( w_{t}(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon \right) \widehat{A}_{t} \right) \right],$$

where $\varepsilon$ is the clipping range, a hyperparameter that controls how far the policy is allowed to deviate from the old policy. The expectation is taken over queries sampled from the query set $\mathcal{D}$ and responses sampled from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. The objective is averaged over the tokens in the response, as indicated by the $\frac{1}{|y|}$ factor and the summation over $t$ from $1$ to $|y|$.

The clipping mechanism works as follows. The $\min$ operator compares two quantities: the unclipped product $w_{t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{t}$ and the clipped product $\mathrm{clip}(w_{t}(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon) \widehat{A}_{t}$. By taking the minimum of these two terms, the objective function effectively prevents the policy from exploiting large deviations from the old policy. When the importance ratio $w_{t}(\theta)$ falls within the range $[1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon]$, the clipping has no effect, and the objective reduces to the unclipped version. However, when $w_{t}(\theta)$ exceeds $1 + \varepsilon$ or falls below $1 - \varepsilon$, the clipped value is used instead, effectively capping the contribution of that token to the gradient.

The clipping mechanism thus serves a dual purpose. First, it limits the variance of the gradient estimates by bounding the importance ratios, which can otherwise fluctuate widely. Second, it prevents the policy from making overly aggressive updates based on individual samples that may not be representative of the overall distribution. By constraining the policy to remain close to the old policy, PPO ensures that the optimization proceeds in a stable and controlled manner, making it one of the most widely used reinforcement learning algorithms in practice.

## Token-Level Importance Ratios in PPO: Definition and Role

A central concept in PPO is the token-level importance ratio, which quantifies how much the current policy $\pi_\theta$ differs from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ at each token position. Specifically, the importance ratio of token $y_t$ is defined as:

$$w_{t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t | x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t | x, y_{<t})}.$$

This ratio compares the probability that the current policy assigns to token $y_t$ (given the query $x$ and the preceding tokens $y_{<t}$) with the probability that the old policy assigned to the same token. When $w_{t}(\theta) = 1$, the two policies agree on the likelihood of that token, indicating no change in the policy's behavior at that position. When $w_{t}(\theta) > 1$, the current policy assigns a higher probability to the token than the old policy did, suggesting that the policy has shifted toward generating that token more frequently. Conversely, when $w_{t}(\theta) < 1$, the current policy assigns a lower probability, indicating a shift away from that token.

The role of the importance ratio in PPO is rooted in the principle of importance sampling, a statistical technique for estimating expectations under one distribution using samples drawn from another. In the context of PPO, the responses used for training are sampled from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, but the objective is to optimize the current policy $\pi_\theta$. The importance ratio corrects for this distributional mismatch by reweighting each sample according to the ratio of the two distributions. In theory, this allows the algorithm to compute an unbiased estimate of the gradient under the current policy using data generated by the old policy.

However, the application of importance sampling at the token level in PPO relies on the assumption that the importance weight $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ effectively corrects for the distributional mismatch between the target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$ and the behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$. Crucially, this correction is only valid when averaging over multiple samples ($N \gg 1$) from the behavior distribution. In PPO, the importance weight at each token position is based on a single sample $y_t$ from the next-token distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x, y_{<t})$, which means the distribution-correction role of importance sampling is not fully realized. This limitation becomes particularly problematic in the context of long sequences, where the accumulation of token-level noise can lead to high-variance gradients and training instability, a issue that GSPO explicitly addresses.

## Value Models in PPO: Purpose, Computational Burden, and Scalability Challenges

A distinguishing feature of PPO is its reliance on a value model for estimating the advantage $\widehat{A}_{t}$ of each token $y_t$. The advantage function measures how much better an action (in this case, generating a particular token) is compared to the average action expected under the current policy. It is a critical component of the PPO objective, as it determines the direction and magnitude of the policy update: tokens with positive advantages are reinforced, while tokens with negative advantages are discouraged.

The value model, also known as a critic, estimates the expected future reward from a given state. In the context of language models, the state at position $t$ consists of the query $x$ and the tokens generated so far $y_{<t}$. The value model predicts the expected cumulative reward that the policy will receive starting from this state, providing a baseline against which the actual reward can be compared. The advantage is then computed as the difference between the actual reward (or an estimate thereof) and the value model's prediction, capturing the incremental benefit of the specific action taken.

While the value model plays a crucial role in the theoretical foundations of PPO, its practical implementation introduces significant challenges. First, the value model typically has a similar size to the policy model, meaning it requires a comparable amount of memory and computational resources. This effectively doubles the memory and computational burden of training, as both the policy model and the value model must be stored, updated, and evaluated during each training step. For large language models, this overhead can be prohibitive, limiting the scalability of the algorithm.

Furthermore, the algorithmic effectiveness of PPO hinges on the reliability of the value model's estimates. If the value model produces inaccurate predictions, the advantage estimates will be unreliable, leading to suboptimal or even harmful policy updates. Acquiring a reliable value model is inherently challenging, as it requires training a separate model on the same data and ensuring that its predictions are well-calibrated. This challenge is compounded when scaling to longer responses and more complex tasks, where the value model must accurately predict rewards over extended horizons. The difficulty of maintaining a reliable value model at scale is one of the key motivations for the development of GRPO, which eliminates the need for a value model entirely.

## Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO): Eliminating the Value Model

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) was developed to address one of the most significant practical limitations of PPO: the heavy reliance on a value model. By eliminating the value model entirely, GRPO reduces the memory and computational burden of training while simplifying the algorithmic pipeline. The key insight behind GRPO is that the advantage of a response can be estimated relative to other responses within the same group, without the need for a separate value model to provide a baseline.

GRPO achieves this by generating multiple responses to the same query and using the relative rewards of these responses to compute advantages. Specifically, GRPO optimizes the following objective:

$$\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \min \left( w_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t}, \, \mathrm{clip} \left( w_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon \right) \widehat{A}_{i,t} \right) \right],$$

where $G$ is the group size, representing the number of responses generated for each query $x$. The importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ for the $i$-th response and the $t$-th token is defined as:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}.$$

The structure of the GRPO objective closely mirrors that of PPO, with the $\min$ operator and clipping mechanism serving the same purpose of constraining policy updates. However, the critical difference lies in how the advantages are computed. Instead of relying on a value model to estimate the advantage of each token, GRPO computes advantages based on the relative rewards of responses within the group. This eliminates the need for a value model, significantly reducing the computational and memory requirements of training.

The expectation in the GRPO objective is taken over queries sampled from $\mathcal{D}$ and groups of $G$ responses sampled from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. The outer average over the $G$ responses (normalized by $\frac{1}{G}$) and the inner average over the tokens in each response (normalized by $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$) ensure that the objective is properly scaled across different group sizes and response lengths. This formulation allows GRPO to leverage the information from multiple responses to the same query, providing a richer signal for policy optimization than PPO's single-response approach.

## Group-Based Advantage Estimation: Normalizing Rewards Across Multiple Responses

The hallmark of GRPO is its group-based advantage estimation, which replaces the value model used in PPO with a simpler and more efficient mechanism. For each query $x$, GRPO generates $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ and computes the reward $r(x, y_i)$ for each response. The advantage $\widehat{A}_{i}$ of the $i$-th response is then computed as:

$$\widehat{A}_{i} = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}{\mathrm{std}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}.$$

This formula normalizes the rewards across the group by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation. The mean $\mathrm{mean}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)$ serves as a baseline, representing the average quality of responses to the query. By subtracting this baseline from each reward, the advantage captures how much better or worse a particular response is compared to the average response in the group. The division by the standard deviation $\mathrm{std}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)$ further normalizes the advantages, ensuring that they are on a consistent scale regardless of the absolute magnitude of the rewards.

This normalization has several important consequences. First, it provides a natural form of credit assignment: responses that receive higher-than-average rewards are assigned positive advantages, encouraging the policy to generate similar responses in the future, while responses that receive lower-than-average rewards are assigned negative advantages, discouraging the policy from generating similar responses. Second, the standardization by the standard deviation ensures that the advantages are not dominated by queries with high-variance rewards, providing a balanced training signal across different queries. Third, this approach is entirely data-driven, requiring no additional model or training procedure, which simplifies the implementation and reduces the computational cost.

The group-based advantage estimation is a key innovation of GRPO, as it provides a principled way to estimate advantages without the need for a value model. By leveraging the diversity of responses within a group, GRPO can effectively distinguish between good and bad responses, guiding the policy toward generating higher-quality outputs. This approach is particularly well-suited for tasks where rewards can be reliably computed by a verifier, such as mathematics and programming, where the correctness of a response can be objectively determined.

## Shared Advantage Across Tokens in GRPO

A notable characteristic of GRPO is that all tokens within a response share the same advantage. In the GRPO objective, the advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ of token $y_{i,t}$ is set equal to the response-level advantage $\widehat{A}_{i}$:

$$\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i} = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}{\mathrm{std}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}.$$

This means that every token in the $i$-th response receives the same advantage signal, regardless of its position or individual contribution to the overall quality of the response. The rationale for this design is that the reward $r(x, y_i)$ is granted at the sequence level, evaluating the response as a whole rather than individual tokens. Since the verifier assesses the entire response, it is natural to propagate the same advantage signal to all tokens within that response.

The implication of shared advantages is that the policy update treats all tokens in a response equally in terms of the reward signal. If a response receives a positive advantage, all tokens in that response are reinforced; if it receives a negative advantage, all tokens are discouraged. This is a simplification compared to PPO, where the value model can potentially assign different advantages to different tokens based on their expected future rewards. However, this simplification is justified by the observation that, in practice, it is difficult to reliably attribute credit to individual tokens, especially for complex reasoning tasks where the relationship between individual tokens and the final reward is not straightforward.

Despite this simplification, the shared advantage design has an important consequence that becomes apparent when examining the gradient of the GRPO objective. The gradient of GRPO can be expressed as:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \widehat{A}_{i} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right].$$

This expression reveals that, while the advantage is shared across tokens, the gradient contribution of each token is weighted by its individual token-level importance ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$. These unequal weights can vary significantly across tokens, ranging from $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ when $\widehat{A}_{i} > 0$ or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ when $\widehat{A}_{i} < 0$. The variation in these weights introduces a source of instability, as the impact of individual token-level importance ratios can accumulate over long sequences and lead to unpredictable consequences as training progresses. This observation is one of the key motivations for the development of GSPO, which weights all tokens in a response equally by using a sequence-level importance ratio.

## Practical Limitations of PPO and GRPO: Memory, Computational Overhead, and Stability

Both PPO and GRPO, despite their widespread adoption, suffer from practical limitations that hinder their scalability to large language models and long-response tasks. These limitations span memory and computational overhead, as well as fundamental stability issues that can lead to catastrophic model collapse.

The primary limitation of PPO is its reliance on a value model. The value model typically has a similar size to the policy model, introducing a considerable memory and computational burden. Training two large models simultaneously — the policy model and the value model — effectively doubles the resource requirements, making PPO expensive to scale. Moreover, the algorithmic effectiveness of PPO hinges on the reliability of the value model's estimates. While acquiring a reliable value model is inherently challenging, ensuring its scalability to longer responses and more complex tasks presents an even greater challenge. As the response length increases, the value model must make accurate predictions over longer horizons, which becomes increasingly difficult. This scalability challenge limits the applicability of PPO to tasks that require long-form reasoning, such as competition-level mathematics and programming.

GRPO addresses the value model limitation by eliminating it entirely, replacing it with group-based advantage estimation. However, GRPO introduces its own set of challenges, primarily related to training stability. The fundamental issue identified in GRPO is the misapplication of importance sampling weights at the token level. The principle of importance sampling requires averaging over multiple samples from the behavior distribution for the importance weight to effectively correct for the distributional mismatch. In GRPO, the importance weight $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$ is applied at each token position based on a single sample from the next-token distribution. This fails to perform the intended distribution-correction role and instead introduces high-variance noise into the training gradients.

This noise accumulates over long sequences and is further exacerbated by the clipping mechanism. As the response length increases, the cumulative effect of token-level noise becomes more pronounced, potentially leading to catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. Empirically, this collapse has been observed to be irreversible: once it occurs, resuming training is unavailing, even when reverting to a previous checkpoint, meticulously tuning hyperparameters such as the clipping ranges, extending the generation length, or switching the RL queries. This is a severe limitation, as it means that a single instability event can effectively terminate the training process, wasting significant computational resources.

The stability issues in GRPO are particularly acute when training large models, especially Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, on long-response tasks. The sparse activation nature of MoE models introduces additional challenges, as the experts activated for the same response can change significantly after gradient updates, causing the token-level importance ratios to fluctuate drastically and further invalidating them. This phenomenon, known as expert-activation volatility, can prevent RL training from converging properly and necessitates complex workarounds such as the Routing Replay strategy, which incurs additional memory and communication overhead and limits the actual capacity of the MoE model.

These practical limitations — the computational burden of value models in PPO, the instability of token-level importance ratios in GRPO, and the compounded challenges of MoE training — collectively motivate the development of GSPO. By redefining the importance ratio at the sequence level and aligning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward, GSPO addresses the fundamental issues that plague its predecessors, providing a more stable, efficient, and performant foundation for reinforcement learning with large language models.

# The Instability Problem: Why GRPO's Objective is Ill-Posed

# Off-Policy Learning in RL: Mini-Batch Gradient Updates and Distributional Mismatch

Reinforcement learning for large language models operates under a set of practical constraints that inevitably introduce a gap between the policy that generates responses and the policy being optimized. To understand this gap, we must first consider the computational demands of modern RL training. As models grow larger—both in parameter count and in architectural sparsity, as seen in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models—and as the responses they generate become longer to accommodate deeper reasoning, a single rollout batch must be very large to maximize hardware utilization. This means that a substantial volume of text is generated from the model before any gradient updates occur.

To improve sample efficiency, the standard practice is to partition this large batch of rollout data into multiple mini-batches, each of which is used for a separate gradient update. The idea is straightforward: rather than generating fresh responses for every single gradient step—an expensive operation—we reuse the same batch of generated responses across multiple updates. However, this seemingly benign efficiency measure introduces a fundamental complication. After the first gradient update, the model parameters have changed, meaning the current policy $\pi_\theta$ no longer matches the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ that was used to generate the responses. The training data is now "off-policy": the responses $y$ in the batch were sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, but the gradients are being computed with respect to $\pi_\theta$.

This distributional mismatch is the root cause of several challenges in RL training. The clipping mechanism in PPO and GRPO is designed precisely to manage this mismatch: it prevents samples that have become too far "off-policy"—that is, samples whose likelihood under $\pi_\theta$ differs too much from their likelihood under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$—from contributing to gradient estimation. The clipping mechanism constrains the importance ratio, which measures how much the policy has shifted, to a bounded range $[1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon]$. When the ratio falls outside this range, the corresponding sample's contribution is clipped, preventing large, destabilizing updates. While this mechanism is well-intentioned, we will see that in GRPO, the very quantity being clipped is itself problematic, rendering the clipping mechanism not just insufficient but actively harmful.

# The Principle of Importance Sampling: Correct Usage and Requirements

To diagnose the flaw in GRPO, we must revisit the foundational technique that motivates the use of importance weights in off-policy reinforcement learning: importance sampling. Importance sampling is a statistical method for estimating properties of a particular distribution using samples drawn from a different distribution. In the context of RL, it allows us to estimate the expected value of some function under the current (target) policy using data generated by an older (behavior) policy.

The core idea is elegant. Suppose we wish to compute the expectation of a function $f$ under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$, but we only have samples drawn from a different distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$. Direct evaluation of $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)]$ is impossible because we cannot sample from $\pi_\text{tar}$. However, we can rewrite this expectation using the samples from $\pi_\text{beh}$ by reweighting each sample by the ratio of the two densities:

$$\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}}\left[\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)} f(z)\right].$$

The weight $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ corrects for the distributional mismatch: samples that are more likely under the target distribution than under the behavior distribution receive a higher weight, and vice versa. In the RL setting, $\pi_\text{tar}$ corresponds to the current policy $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_\text{beh}$ corresponds to the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$.

However, this formulation carries a critical requirement that is often overlooked: the correction is only valid in expectation, and the expectation must be approximated by averaging over multiple samples. The importance weight $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ effectively corrects for the distributional mismatch only when we average over many samples ($N \gg 1$) from the behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$. With a single sample, the importance weight is just a single noisy scalar that does not perform any meaningful distribution correction. This is because the entire point of importance sampling is that, in aggregate, the reweighted samples reconstruct the target distribution. A single reweighted sample cannot achieve this reconstruction—it is merely one draw from a noisy distribution.

# Importance Sampling Formulation: Target vs. Behavior Distributions and the Role of Multiple Samples

Let us elaborate on why multiple samples are essential for importance sampling to function correctly. Consider the unbiasedness property: a single sample $z$ drawn from $\pi_\text{beh}$ and reweighted by $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ does give an unbiased estimate of $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)]$. That is, in expectation over the sampling process, the reweighted estimate is correct. However, unbiasedness alone is not sufficient for practical utility. The variance of the importance sampling estimator depends critically on the number of samples and the discrepancy between the target and behavior distributions.

When the target and behavior distributions are close, the importance weights are close to 1, and the variance of the estimator is low even with a modest number of samples. But when the distributions diverge—as they do after multiple gradient updates in mini-batch training—the importance weights can become very large for some samples and very small for others, leading to high variance. This high variance is the fundamental challenge of importance sampling, and it is mitigated only by averaging over many samples.

The role of multiple samples is thus twofold. First, averaging reduces the variance of the estimator: the variance of the mean of $N$ independent samples decreases as $\frac{1}{N}$. Second, and more fundamentally, the distribution-correction property of importance sampling is an aggregate property. It is only when we have many samples from $\pi_\text{beh}$, each appropriately reweighted, that the empirical distribution of the reweighted samples approximates $\pi_\text{tar}$. A single sample, no matter how it is reweighted, cannot approximate a distribution. This distinction between the correctness of an individual weight and the correctness of distribution correction in aggregate is the crux of the problem we will identify in GRPO.

# GRPO's Misapplication: Token-Level Importance Weights from Single Samples

With the principles of importance sampling established, we can now pinpoint the fundamental flaw in GRPO's design. Recall that in GRPO, the importance ratio is defined at the token level:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}.$$

This ratio is applied at each token position $t$ in the response $y_i$. The intention is to correct for the off-policy mismatch between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ at the granularity of individual tokens.

However, consider what this token-level importance weight actually represents. At each position $t$, the model has a next-token distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x, y_{i,<t})$ under the old policy. From this distribution, exactly one token $y_{i,t}$ was sampled. The importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is then computed as the ratio of the probability of that single sampled token under the current policy to its probability under the old policy. This is an importance weight derived from a single sample—specifically, a single sample from the next-token distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x, y_{i,<t})$.

As we established in the previous sections, importance sampling requires averaging over multiple samples ($N \gg 1$) from the behavior distribution for the importance weight to perform its intended role of distribution correction. Here, there is only one sample per token position. The token-level importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is therefore not performing distribution correction at all. It is a single noisy scalar that attempts to reweight a single observation, but it cannot reconstruct or correct for the full next-token distribution from which the token was drawn.

# Why Token-Level Importance Weights Fail to Perform Distribution Correction

To understand why this failure matters, consider what distribution correction should achieve in the RL context. When we compute gradients using off-policy data, the ideal correction would ensure that the gradient estimate is the same as if we had sampled from $\pi_\theta$ directly. At the token level, this would mean that the empirical distribution of reweighted tokens at each position approximates the next-token distribution under $\pi_\theta$. But with only one token sampled at each position, there is nothing to approximate a distribution from. The single token $y_{i,t}$ is either kept (with some weight) or not, and this binary outcome cannot capture the full distributional structure of $\pi_\theta(\cdot | x, y_{i,<t})$.

The consequence of this failure is that the token-level importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ does not reduce the bias introduced by off-policy data. Instead, it introduces additional noise. Each token's gradient contribution is scaled by a weight that is a single noisy draw, and this weight can vary arbitrarily across tokens within the same response. Some tokens may receive very large weights (if they happen to be more likely under $\pi_\theta$ than under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$), while others may receive very small weights. This variability is not the structured, distribution-correcting variability of proper importance sampling—it is unstructured noise arising from the mismatch between the single-sample setting and the multi-sample requirement of importance sampling.

Furthermore, the gradient of the GRPO objective reveals the mechanism by which this noise enters the training. The gradient of GRPO is:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right].$$

Here, each token's gradient $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ is weighted by the token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. Because these weights are unequal across tokens—they can vary within the range $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ for positive advantages $\widehat{A}_i > 0$, or within $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ for negative advantages $\widehat{A}_i < 0$—different tokens in the same response receive different gradient scaling. These unequal weights are not negligible, and as we will see, their effects compound over the course of training.

# Accumulation of High-Variance Noise Over Long Sequences

The noise introduced by token-level importance weights is not merely a local issue at each token position; it accumulates over the length of a response. In modern RL training of language models, particularly for tasks involving sophisticated reasoning such as competition-level mathematics and programming, responses can be very long. The GRPO objective averages the token-level contributions over all tokens in a response, dividing by $|y_i|$. While this averaging might seem to mitigate the noise, the problem is that the noise is not independent across tokens—it is structured by the sequential nature of autoregressive generation.

As the response length $|y_i|$ increases, more token-level importance weights are involved in the gradient estimate, each contributing its own noise. The cumulative effect of these noisy weights grows with the sequence length. A few tokens with extreme importance ratios can disproportionately influence the gradient, even when averaged over many tokens. This is particularly problematic because the importance ratios are products of sequential dependencies: the probability of a token at position $t$ depends on all preceding tokens, and errors in the importance ratio at one position propagate to subsequent positions through the autoregressive structure.

The situation is further aggravated in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. In MoE models, the experts activated for a given token can change significantly after a gradient update. Empirically, with the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, after each RL gradient update, roughly 10% of the experts activated under the new policy $\pi_\theta$ differ from those activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ for the same rollout sample. This phenomenon, which becomes more prominent in deeper MoE models, causes the token-level importance ratios to fluctuate even more drastically, further invalidating them as reliable correction weights. The high-variance noise from token-level importance weights thus accumulates not only over sequence length but also over the depth and complexity of the model architecture.

# How the Clipping Mechanism Amplifies the Instability

The clipping mechanism, designed to prevent overly off-policy samples from destabilizing training, paradoxically amplifies the very instability it is meant to control. To understand this, consider how clipping interacts with the noisy token-level importance weights in GRPO.

The clipping mechanism in GRPO clips each token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ to the range $[1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]$. When a token's importance ratio exceeds this range, its contribution to the objective is replaced by the clipped version. The intention is to prevent tokens with extreme importance ratios from dominating the gradient. However, because the token-level importance ratios are already noisy—being derived from single samples rather than proper importance sampling—the clipping mechanism does not remove the noise. Instead, it truncates the noisy weights, creating a biased and irregular pattern of which tokens contribute to the gradient and which do not.

The problem is that clipping creates an asymmetry. For tokens with positive advantages ($\widehat{A}_i > 0$), the importance ratio is clipped to the range $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$, meaning that tokens with ratios above $1+\varepsilon$ have their contributions capped. For tokens with negative advantages ($\widehat{A}_i < 0$), the importance ratio is clipped to the range $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$, meaning that tokens with ratios below $1-\varepsilon$ have their contributions bounded from below. This asymmetric clipping, applied to already-noisy weights, creates a systematic bias in the gradient estimate. Some tokens that should contribute are silenced, while others that should be downweighted are amplified, and the pattern of which tokens are affected changes unpredictably across training steps.

This amplification effect is particularly insidious because it is gradual. The noise from clipping does not cause immediate divergence; rather, it progressively corrupts the gradient estimates over many training steps. Each mini-batch update introduces a small amount of biased noise, and this noise compounds as training proceeds. The clipping mechanism, rather than serving as a stabilizer, becomes a source of systematic distortion that, combined with the accumulation of noise over long sequences, progressively degrades the quality of the policy.

# Empirical Observations of Catastrophic and Irreversible Model Collapse

The theoretical analysis above is not merely speculative—it is confirmed by extensive empirical observations. When training large language models with GRPO, particularly on long-response tasks, the training process frequently undergoes catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. This phenomenon has been observed in practice, including during the development of models such as Qwen3 and MiniMax-M1.

The collapse manifests as a sudden and severe degradation of the model's capabilities. The model may stop generating coherent text, lose its reasoning ability, or produce degenerate outputs. What makes this collapse particularly troubling is its irreversibility. Once the collapse occurs, standard recovery strategies are unavailing. Reverting to a previous checkpoint and meticulously tuning hyperparameters—such as adjusting the clipping ranges—does not restore the model. Extending the generation length does not help. Switching the RL queries to a different set of problems does not reverse the damage. The model appears to have entered a region of parameter space from which it cannot recover through any practical intervention.

This irreversibility is consistent with the theoretical analysis. The noise from token-level importance weights, amplified by clipping and accumulated over long sequences, progressively corrupts the model's representations. Once the corruption reaches a critical threshold, the model's next-token distributions become so distorted that the importance ratios themselves become meaningless, creating a feedback loop of degradation. The model has effectively been driven into a region where the off-policy correction mechanism, which was supposed to stabilize training, has become the very source of instability.

The observation that this collapse is more prevalent with larger models, longer responses, and MoE architectures further supports the diagnosis. Larger models have more parameters that can be corrupted by noisy gradients. Longer responses accumulate more noise. MoE models introduce additional volatility in the importance ratios through expert-activation changes. All of these factors scale with the very dimensions along which modern language models are being developed, making the instability problem not just an inconvenience but a fundamental barrier to scaling RL training.

# The Core Principle: Matching the Unit of Optimization to the Unit of Reward

The analysis of GRPO's instability points to a fundamental principle for the design of RL algorithms for language models: the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of reward. In language model RL, rewards are granted to entire sequences, not individual tokens. A verifier $r$ evaluates a complete query-response pair $(x, y)$ and produces a scalar reward $r(x, y) \in [0, 1]$. The advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ computed in GRPO is a sequence-level quantity, derived from the relative ranking of rewards within a group of responses to the same query. All tokens in a response share the same advantage value.

Despite this sequence-level rewarding, GRPO applies off-policy correction at the token level. This mismatch between the unit of reward (sequences) and the unit of correction (tokens) is the root cause of the instability. The token-level importance weights attempt to correct for distributional mismatch at a granularity that is finer than the granularity at which rewards—and therefore learning signals—are defined. This mismatch is not merely a technical inconvenience; it violates the basic principle of importance sampling, which requires that the correction be applied at the same level as the function being estimated.

This principle—that the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward—motivates the exploration of sequence-level optimization as an alternative to token-level optimization. If the reward is granted to the entire sequence, then the off-policy correction should also be applied at the sequence level. The sequence-level importance weight $\frac{\pi_\theta(y | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y | x)}$ has a clear theoretical meaning: it reflects how far the response $y$ sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)$ deviates from the distribution $\pi_\theta(\cdot | x)$. This is a well-defined quantity that naturally aligns with the sequence-level reward and can serve as a meaningful indicator for the clipping mechanism.

By shifting the level of optimization from tokens to sequences, we can ensure that the importance sampling correction is applied where it is meaningful—where the reward signal lives—and avoid the high-variance noise that arises from misapplying importance weights at the token level. This insight forms the foundation for the Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) algorithm, which we will introduce in the next chapter. The key realization is that the instability of GRPO is not a bug to be patched with better hyperparameters or auxiliary techniques; it is a fundamental consequence of an ill-posed objective that violates the basic principles of importance sampling and the alignment between reward and optimization.

## Introduction

Reinforcement learning has become a cornerstone of modern language model training, enabling models to tackle sophisticated reasoning tasks such as competition-level mathematics and programming. However, the prevailing algorithms for training large language models—most notably Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)—suffer from severe instability issues that can lead to catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. This chapter introduces the Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) algorithm, which addresses these fundamental limitations by redefining the importance ratio at the sequence level and performing sequence-level clipping, rewarding, and optimization. We will build up the algorithm step by step, beginning with the definition of the sequence-level importance ratio, proceeding through the objective function and its gradient analysis, and culminating in a detailed comparison with GRPO that illuminates precisely why GSPO achieves superior stability and efficiency.

# Sequence-Level Importance Ratio: Definition and Theoretical Meaning

The central innovation of GSPO lies in its redefinition of the importance ratio. To appreciate this innovation, recall that in GRPO, the importance ratio is defined at the token level as $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$, comparing the probability of a single token under the current and old policies. The paper identifies that this token-level ratio is a misapplication of the importance sampling principle. Importance sampling corrects for distributional mismatch by re-weighting samples drawn from a behavior distribution to estimate expectations under a target distribution, but this correction relies on averaging over multiple samples from the behavior distribution. Since the token-level weight is based on a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from each next-token distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x, y_{i,<t})$, it fails to perform the intended distribution-correction role.

In contrast, GSPO defines the importance ratio at the sequence level. For a response $y_i$ to a query $x$, the sequence-level importance ratio is defined as:

$$s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}\right).$$

The numerator $\pi_\theta(y_i | x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ is the full likelihood of the response under the current policy, and the denominator $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)$ is the likelihood under the old policy. The ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}$ has a clear theoretical meaning: it reflects how far the response $y_i$, which was sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)$, deviates from the distribution $\pi_\theta(\cdot | x)$. This is precisely the kind of distributional correction that importance sampling was designed to address—re-weighting a sample drawn from one distribution to account for the fact that we wish to optimize under another.

This sequence-level definition naturally aligns with the sequence-level reward. Since the reward $r(x, y_i)$ is granted to the entire response $y_i$ rather than to individual tokens, the unit of the importance ratio should match the unit of the reward. The paper articulates this as a core principle: the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of the reward. By defining the importance ratio at the sequence level, GSPO ensures this alignment, which the token-level importance ratio in GRPO fundamentally violates.

The sequence-level importance ratio also serves as a meaningful indicator for the clipping mechanism. Just as the token-level ratio in GRPO is clipped to prevent overly off-policy samples from influencing gradient estimation, the sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$ measures the overall policy drift for an entire response, and clipping it prevents responses that are too far from the old policy from being used in gradient estimation. This is a more coherent application of the clipping principle, as it operates at the same granularity as both the reward and the optimization target.

# Length Normalization in the Importance Ratio: Motivation and Benefits for Variance Reduction

A critical design choice in the definition of $s_i(\theta)$ is the inclusion of length normalization, achieved through the exponent $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$. This is not merely a cosmetic adjustment but serves essential practical and theoretical purposes.

The raw sequence-level likelihood ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}$ is a product of $|y_i|$ token-level ratios. When $|y_i|$ is large, this product can become extremely small or extremely large, depending on whether the individual token ratios tend to be below or above one. Even if each token-level ratio deviates only slightly from one, the cumulative effect across hundreds or thousands of tokens can produce a sequence-level ratio that spans many orders of magnitude. This means that the likelihood changes of a few tokens can result in dramatic fluctuations of the sequence-level importance ratio, making it numerically unstable and difficult to work with in practice.

By taking the $|y_i|$-th root—equivalently, by averaging the log-likelihood ratios across tokens before exponentiating—the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is brought into a much more controlled numerical range. The expression $\exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}\right)$ computes the geometric mean of the token-level ratios, which is bounded in a range that is far more stable than the raw product. This normalization reduces the variance of the importance ratio across responses of different lengths, ensuring that the clipping mechanism can operate with a single, unified set of clipping ranges.

Without length normalization, responses of different lengths would produce importance ratios at vastly different scales, requiring different clipping thresholds for different response lengths. This would make the algorithm impractical, as one would need to adaptively set clipping ranges based on the length of each response. With normalization, all importance ratios $s_i(\theta)$ are comparable across responses, regardless of their lengths, and a single pair of clipping bounds suffices. This is a crucial practical benefit that makes the sequence-level approach feasible.

# The GSPO Objective Function: Sequence-Level Clipping and Group-Based Advantages

With the sequence-level importance ratio defined, we can now present the full GSPO objective function. GSPO adopts the group-based advantage estimation introduced by GRPO, which eliminates the need for a separate value model. For a query $x$, a group of $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ is sampled from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)$. Each response is scored by a verifier $r$, yielding rewards $r(x, y_i) \in [0, 1]$. The advantage of each response is then computed as the normalized reward:

$$\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \text{mean}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}{\text{std}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}.$$

This advantage measures how much better or worse a particular response is relative to the other responses in the group. A response that receives a reward above the group mean gets a positive advantage, while one below the mean gets a negative advantage. The division by the standard deviation normalizes the scale of the advantages, making them comparable across different queries and reward distributions.

The GSPO objective function is then defined as:

$$\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\min\left(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\, \text{clip}\left(s_i(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon\right)\widehat{A}_i\right)\right].$$

The structure of this objective mirrors that of PPO and GRPO, with the $\min$ and clip operators working together to form a lower bound on the policy improvement objective. The key difference is that the clipping is applied to the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ rather than to token-level ratios. When the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ falls outside the range $[1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]$, the clipped version is used in the objective, which effectively prevents responses that have drifted too far from the old policy from contributing to the gradient. This is the mechanism that prevents overly off-policy samples from being involved in gradient estimation.

The use of the $\min$ operator ensures pessimistic bounding: for positive advantages ($\widehat{A}_i > 0$), the objective takes the minimum of the clipped and unclipped versions, which prevents the algorithm from over-optimizing toward responses that have already moved too far from the old policy. For negative advantages ($\widehat{A}_i < 0$), the $\min$ operator effectively selects the unclipped or clipped version depending on which provides the more conservative (i.e., less negative) gradient, again preventing over-optimization. This mechanism, inherited from PPO, is preserved in GSPO but applied at the sequence level, ensuring that the unit of clipping matches both the unit of reward and the unit of optimization.

# Clipping at the Sequence Level vs. Token Level: Conceptual Differences

The distinction between clipping at the sequence level and clipping at the token level is not merely a matter of granularity; it reflects fundamentally different philosophies about what the clipping mechanism is protecting against and how off-policy corrections should be applied.

In GRPO, the clipping operates on each token's importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ independently. For each token $y_{i,t}$, if its individual ratio falls outside $[1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]$, that token's contribution is clipped. This means that within a single response, some tokens may be clipped while others are not, depending on how each token's probability has changed between the old and current policies. The paper observes that this token-level clipping leads to a situation where the gradient weights for tokens within the same response are unequal and can vary widely, introducing high-variance noise into the training gradients.

In GSPO, the clipping operates on the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$, which is a single scalar per response. Either the entire response is clipped or it is not. There is no per-token variation in clipping status within a response. This means that when a response is not clipped, all of its tokens contribute to the gradient with equal weight (as we will see in the gradient analysis), and when it is clipped, the entire response is affected uniformly. This is a more coherent design because the reward is assigned at the sequence level, and the clipping mechanism should operate at the same level.

The paper provides a striking empirical observation that highlights this conceptual difference: the fractions of clipped tokens differ by two orders of magnitude between GSPO and GRPO. Despite clipping significantly more tokens—and consequently using fewer tokens for gradient estimation—GSPO still achieves higher training efficiency than GRPO. This counter-intuitive finding suggests that GRPO's token-level gradient estimates are inherently noisy and inefficient, and that the tokens it does use contribute poor-quality signal. In contrast, GSPO's sequence-level approach provides a more reliable and effective learning signal, even though it discards a larger fraction of the data through clipping.

# Differences in Clipping Range Magnitudes Between GSPO and GRPO

An important practical difference between GSPO and GRPO is the magnitude of the clipping ranges $\varepsilon$ used in each algorithm. Because the importance ratios are defined differently—with GSPO using a length-normalized geometric mean of token-level ratios and GRPO using individual token-level ratios—the numerical scales of these ratios are fundamentally different, and consequently the clipping ranges must be set at different orders of magnitude.

In the experiments reported in the paper, GSPO uses left and right clipping ranges of $3 \times 10^{-4}$ and $4 \times 10^{-4}$, respectively. In contrast, GRPO uses left and right clipping ranges of $0.2$ and $0.27$, respectively. This represents a difference of roughly three orders of magnitude in the clipping thresholds.

The reason for this disparity is intuitive once one understands the definitions. The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is the geometric mean of the token-level ratios. Even if individual token-level ratios deviate substantially from one (say, by 20%, which would trigger clipping in GRPO), when averaged in log space across many tokens, the deviations tend to cancel out, producing a geometric mean much closer to one. A deviation of $3 \times 10^{-4}$ in the geometric mean corresponds to a very small average per-token log-ratio change, but this small deviation is meaningful at the sequence level because it represents the aggregate behavior across the entire response. In contrast, the token-level ratios in GRPO can vary dramatically from token to token, and a clipping range of $0.2$ to $0.27$ is needed to allow for this per-token variability while still preventing extreme outliers.

This difference in clipping range magnitudes is not merely a tuning detail; it reflects the different statistical properties of the two importance ratio definitions. The sequence-level ratio is inherently lower-variance due to the averaging effect, and thus requires tighter clipping bounds to detect meaningful policy drift. The token-level ratio is high-variance and requires looser bounds to avoid clipping too aggressively. The paper notes that adjusting the clipping ranges does not alter the fundamental disparity in the fractions of clipped tokens between the two algorithms, suggesting that the difference is intrinsic to the algorithmic design rather than a hyperparameter artifact.

# Gradient Analysis of GSPO: Deriving the Gradient of the Sequence-Level Objective

To understand the mechanism by which GSPO achieves its superior stability, we need to examine the gradient of its objective function. The paper derives this gradient (omitting the clipping mechanism for brevity) as follows.

Starting from the GSPO objective:

$$\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i\right],$$

we take the gradient with respect to $\theta$. Using the fact that $\nabla_\theta s_i(\theta) = s_i(\theta) \cdot \nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)$ (the standard log-derivative trick), the gradient becomes:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i \cdot \nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)\right].$$

Now, the key step is to expand $\nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)$. Recall that:

$$\log s_i(\theta) = \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}.$$

Since $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ does not depend on $\theta$, its gradient is zero, and we have:

$$\nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta) = \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}).$$

Substituting back, the full gradient of the GSPO objective is:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}\widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})\right].$$

This is a remarkable result. The gradient of the GSPO objective can be decomposed into a sum over tokens, where each token's gradient $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ is weighted by the same scalar coefficient: $s_i(\theta) \cdot \widehat{A}_i$. In other words, all tokens within a response receive equal weight in the gradient. The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ acts as a single, uniform multiplier applied to every token's gradient contribution, scaled by the advantage $\widehat{A}_i$.

This is the direct consequence of the length normalization in the definition of $s_i(\theta)$. The $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ factor in the logarithm causes the gradient of $\log s_i(\theta)$ to be an unweighted average of the per-token log-likelihood gradients. When this average is multiplied by $s_i(\theta)$, the result is that every token in the response contributes equally to the gradient, modulo the shared scaling factor. This is a property that GRPO does not possess, as we shall see next.

# Gradient Analysis of GRPO: Deriving the Gradient for Comparison

To appreciate the significance of the equal weighting property in GSPO, we now derive the gradient of the GRPO objective for comparison. The GRPO objective is:

$$\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i,t}\right],$$

where $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$ and $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$ (all tokens in a response share the same advantage). Taking the gradient:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})\right].$$

Here, each token's gradient $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ is weighted by its own individual importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$. This is the critical difference from GSPO: in GRPO, the weight applied to each token's gradient varies from token to token, depending on how much that specific token's probability has changed between the old and current policies.

The paper highlights that the fundamental distinction between GSPO and GRPO lies precisely in how they weight the gradients of the log-likelihoods of tokens. Both algorithms compute a sum of per-token log-likelihood gradients, scaled by the advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ and averaged over the group and the response length. But the per-token scaling factor is what differs: GSPO uses a single, uniform weight $s_i(\theta)$ for all tokens, while GRPO uses individual, varying weights $w_{i,t}(\theta)$.

# Key Insight: Equal vs. Unequal Weighting of Token Gradients in GSPO and GRPO

The comparison of the two gradient expressions reveals the fundamental insight of the paper. In GSPO, the gradient for response $y_i$ is:

$$s_i(\theta) \cdot \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}),$$

where all tokens share the weight $s_i(\theta) \cdot \widehat{A}_i$. In GRPO, the gradient for response $y_i$ is:

$$\widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}w_{i,t}(\theta) \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}),$$

where each token has its own weight $w_{i,t}(\theta) \cdot \widehat{A}_i$.

The paper emphasizes that in GRPO, these unequal weights can vary significantly. For responses with positive advantages ($\widehat{A}_i > 0$), the clipping mechanism allows token weights to range in $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$. For responses with negative advantages ($\widehat{A}_i < 0$), the clipping allows weights to range in $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$. This means that within a single response, some tokens may receive a weight close to zero (effectively not contributing to the gradient), while others may receive a weight of $1+\varepsilon$ or even unbounded (in the negative advantage case). These unequal weights are not negligible; they represent a substantial source of variability in the gradient signal.

The paper argues that the impact of these unequal weights accumulates over long sequences and is exacerbated by the clipping mechanism. As the response length grows, the number of tokens with varying weights increases, and the cumulative effect of these weight disparities becomes more pronounced. This accumulated noise progressively degrades the quality of the gradient estimate, eventually leading to the catastrophic and irreversible model collapse that the paper identifies as a critical problem with GRPO.

In contrast, GSPO's equal weighting eliminates this source of variability entirely. By using a single, sequence-level weight $s_i(\theta)$ for all tokens, GSPO ensures that the gradient for each response is simply the average of the per-token log-likelihood gradients, uniformly scaled. This produces a gradient signal that is more coherent, lower-variance, and more directly aligned with the sequence-level reward signal.

# How GSPO Eliminates the Instability Factor of Unequal Token Weights

The instability introduced by unequal token weights in GRPO is not merely a theoretical concern; it has severe practical consequences that the paper documents in detail. The paper observes that when training large models on long-response tasks, the accumulation of high-variance noise from unequal token weights leads to model collapse that is often irreversible. Once collapse occurs, resuming training is unavailing—even reverting to a previous checkpoint and meticulously tuning hyperparameters (such as the clipping ranges), extending the generation length, or switching the RL queries cannot recover the model.

GSPO eliminates this instability factor through its equal weighting of token gradients. Because the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is applied as a single scalar to all tokens in a response, there is no opportunity for per-token weight disparities to accumulate and introduce noise. The gradient for each response is a clean, uniformly weighted average of per-token contributions, scaled by the advantage and the sequence-level importance ratio.

This design choice also has significant implications for the training of Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. In MoE models, the experts activated for a given response can change significantly after gradient updates—roughly 10% of activated experts may differ between the old and new policies for the same rollout sample. This causes the token-level importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ in GRPO to fluctuate drastically, further invalidating them as importance sampling weights. The paper notes that this phenomenon, which the authors call expert-activation volatility, becomes more prominent in deeper MoE models and prevents RL training from converging properly. To address this, the authors previously had to employ a technique called Routing Replay, which caches the activated experts from the old policy and reuses them when computing importance ratios under the new policy. This workaround incurs additional memory and communication overhead and limits the model's effective capacity.

GSPO fundamentally resolves this issue because it focuses only on the sequence likelihood $\pi_\theta(y_i | x)$ and is not sensitive to individual token likelihoods $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$. Since the MoE model always maintains its overall language modeling capability, the sequence likelihood does not fluctuate drastically even when individual expert activations change. This means that the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ remains stable, and GSPO can converge normally without any need for Routing Replay or similar workarounds. This not only simplifies and stabilizes the training process but also allows the model to leverage its full capacity without artificial constraints.

Furthermore, GSPO's use of sequence-level likelihoods has implications for RL infrastructure. In practice, due to precision discrepancies between training engines (e.g., Megatron) and inference engines (e.g., SGLang and vLLM), it is typically necessary to recompute the likelihoods of sampled responses under the old policy using the training engine. However, because sequence-level likelihoods are much more tolerant of precision discrepancies than token-level likelihoods, GSPO makes it feasible to directly use the likelihoods returned by the inference engine for optimization, avoiding the need for recomputation. This is particularly beneficial in scenarios such as partial rollout, multi-turn RL, and training-inference disaggregated frameworks, where the overhead of recomputation can be substantial.

In summary, GSPO eliminates the instability factor of unequal token weights by replacing the per-token importance ratios with a single, length-normalized, sequence-level importance ratio. This change ensures that all tokens within a response contribute equally to the gradient, aligning the optimization with the sequence-level reward, reducing variance, and providing a more reliable learning signal. The result is an algorithm that is more stable, more efficient, and better suited to the demands of training large language models—including the large MoE models that represent the frontier of language model development.

# GSPO-token: A Token-Level Objective Variant

# Motivation for Token-Level Advantage Adjustment: Multi-Turn RL Scenarios

The Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) algorithm, as introduced in earlier sections, operates at the sequence level: it defines the importance ratio based on the full sequence likelihood, applies clipping to entire responses, and assigns a single advantage value to all tokens within a given response. This design is well motivated by the principle that the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of reward, and it yields substantial gains in training stability and efficiency over token-level approaches such as GRPO. However, there exist scenarios in which the sequence-level assignment of advantages may be too coarse, and a finer-grained, token-wise adjustment of advantages becomes desirable.

The primary motivating scenario discussed in the paper is multi-turn reinforcement learning. In multi-turn RL, a single response from the model may span multiple interaction turns, each of which could involve a different sub-query, a different environmental response, or a different stage of a conversational or agentic process. Within such a multi-turn response, the quality and importance of individual turns may vary significantly. Some turns may represent critical reasoning steps that lead to a correct answer, while others may contain filler, hedging, or even locally suboptimal decisions that nevertheless do not harm the overall outcome. Assigning the same advantage to every token across all turns—as the standard GSPO formulation does—fails to capture these intra-response distinctions.

To address this need, the paper introduces a token-level objective variant of GSPO, called GSPO-token. The goal of GSPO-token is to retain the core theoretical benefits of GSPO—specifically, the use of sequence-level importance ratios and sequence-level clipping—while allowing the advantage to be customized on a per-token basis. This enables the algorithm to distinguish between tokens within a response, directing the learning signal more precisely toward the tokens that matter most for the task at hand. As we shall see, GSPO-token achieves this flexibility through a carefully designed formulation involving stop-gradient operations, and it reduces exactly to standard GSPO when per-token advantages are set to be uniform.

# Formulation of GSPO-token: Incorporating Stop-Gradient Operations

The GSPO-token objective is formulated to combine the sequence-level importance ratio of GSPO with the flexibility of token-level advantage assignment. The objective function is defined as:

$$\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \min \left( s_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t}, \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon \right) \widehat{A}_{i,t} \right) \right],$$

where $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ is the advantage assigned to token $y_{i,t}$, which may now differ across tokens within the same response. The critical innovation lies in the definition of the per-token importance ratio $s_{i,t}(\theta)$, which is constructed as:

$$s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg} \left[ s_i(\theta) \right] \cdot \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right]}.$$

Here, $s_i(\theta)$ is the same sequence-level importance ratio used in standard GSPO, defined as:

$$s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \right).$$

The notation $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ denotes the stop-gradient operation, which takes only the numerical value of its argument but blocks the flow of gradients through it. In PyTorch, this corresponds to the \texttt{detach} operation. The term $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})]}$ thus has a numerical value of exactly 1, since the numerator and the detached denominator are numerically identical. Consequently, $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$, the sequence-level importance ratio.

This construction is elegant because it achieves two seemingly contradictory goals simultaneously. On one hand, the numerical value of $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ matches the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$, so the clipping mechanism still operates at the sequence level—entire responses are clipped based on how far they deviate from the old policy, exactly as in standard GSPO. On the other hand, the gradient of $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ with respect to $\theta$ flows only through the numerator $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$, because the stop-gradient operations block gradient flow through $s_i(\theta)$ and through the denominator $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$. This means that while the forward pass produces the same values as GSPO, the backward pass distributes gradients in a way that naturally accommodates per-token advantages.

# The Role of Stop-Gradient (Detach) in Maintaining Numerical Equivalence

The stop-gradient operation plays a central and deliberate role in the GSPO-token formulation. To understand why it is necessary, consider what would happen without it. If one simply defined $s_{i,t}(\theta) = s_i(\theta)$ and then attempted to assign per-token advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$, the gradient of the objective would involve the gradient of $s_i(\theta)$, which depends on the log-likelihoods of all tokens in the sequence. This would couple the per-token advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ with the gradients of log-likelihoods across all token positions, producing a complex and potentially unwieldy gradient expression that does not cleanly separate the per-token advantage assignment from the sequence-level importance weighting.

The stop-gradient trick elegantly resolves this issue. By detaching $s_i(\theta)$ in the construction of $s_{i,t}(\theta)$, the gradient of $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ with respect to $\theta$ is computed only through the un-detached numerator $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$. Specifically, since $s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})]}$, applying the product rule and noting that the stop-gradient terms are treated as constants during differentiation, we obtain:

$$\nabla_\theta s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})]}.$$

Since the stop-gradient terms are numerically equal to their non-detached counterparts, this simplifies numerically to:

$$\nabla_\theta s_{i,t}(\theta) = s_i(\theta) \cdot \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} = s_i(\theta) \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}).$$

This result is crucial: the gradient of $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ factors into the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ multiplied by the gradient of the log-likelihood of the individual token $y_{i,t}$. The sequence-level ratio acts as a uniform multiplicative weight applied to every token's gradient within the response, while the per-token advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ can independently modulate the contribution of each token. This cleanly separates the two concerns: the importance ratio correction remains at the sequence level (as in GSPO), while the advantage assignment can be at the token level (as desired for multi-turn RL).

The stop-gradient thus serves as the bridge that allows GSPO-token to inherit the stability properties of GSPO's sequence-level importance weighting while gaining the flexibility of token-level advantage customization. Without it, the gradient computation would not decompose so cleanly, and the numerical equivalence with standard GSPO under uniform advantages would not hold.

# Derivation of the GSPO-token Gradient

The paper derives the gradient of the GSPO-token objective in a step-by-step manner. For clarity, the clipping mechanism is omitted in the derivation, as it does not affect the fundamental structure of the gradient (clipping simply selects, for each term, the minimum of two expressions, and the gradient flows through whichever branch is active).

Starting from the objective:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta) = \nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} s_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t} \right].$$

Since the expectation is over samples from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, which does not depend on $\theta$, the gradient can be moved inside the expectation. Furthermore, the advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ are computed from the rewards $r(x, y_i)$ and do not depend on $\theta$. Thus, the gradient applies only to $s_{i,t}(\theta)$:

$$= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \widehat{A}_{i,t} \nabla_\theta s_{i,t}(\theta) \right].$$

Substituting the gradient of $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ derived in the previous section, where $\nabla_\theta s_{i,t}(\theta) = s_i(\theta) \cdot \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$, we obtain:

$$= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} s_i(\theta) \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \widehat{A}_{i,t} \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \right].$$

Recognizing that $\frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} = \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$, and expanding the definition of $s_i(\theta)$, we arrive at the final form:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \widehat{A}_{i,t} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right].$$

This gradient expression reveals the fundamental structure of GSPO-token. The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$ serves as a uniform multiplicative weight applied to all tokens within response $y_i$. Within the inner sum over tokens, each token's gradient $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ is scaled by its own advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. This stands in contrast to GRPO, where each token's gradient is weighted by a potentially different token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, introducing high-variance noise. In GSPO-token, the importance ratio is sequence-level and uniform across tokens, preserving the stability benefits of GSPO, while the advantage is free to vary per token, providing the desired flexibility.

# Numerical Equivalence Between GSPO-token and GSPO Under Uniform Advantages

A key property of GSPO-token is that it is numerically identical to standard GSPO when the per-token advantages are set to be uniform across all tokens within each response—that is, when $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$ for all $t$. The paper establishes this equivalence across three dimensions: the optimization objective, the clipping condition, and the theoretical gradient.

First, consider the optimization objective. In GSPO-token, $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$ because the ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})]}$ evaluates to 1. When $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$, the inner sum $\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} s_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t}$ becomes $\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i = s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i$, which is exactly the per-response term in the GSPO objective. Thus, the two objectives produce the same numerical values.

Second, consider the clipping condition. Since $s_{i,t}(\theta) = s_i(\theta)$ numerically, the clip operation $\mathrm{clip}(s_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon)$ produces the same result as $\mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon)$. Therefore, the decision of whether to clip a particular response is identical in both algorithms: clipping is determined by the sequence-level importance ratio in both cases.

Third, consider the theoretical gradient. The GSPO gradient is:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right],$$

while the GSPO-token gradient is:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta) = \mathbb{E} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} s_i(\theta) \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \widehat{A}_{i,t} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right].$$

When $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$ for all $t$, the factor $\widehat{A}_i$ can be pulled out of the inner sum in the GSPO-token gradient, yielding exactly the GSPO gradient. The two expressions are therefore algebraically identical.

This equivalence is reassuring because it confirms that GSPO-token is a strict generalization of GSPO. When the additional flexibility of per-token advantages is not needed, GSPO-token reduces exactly to GSPO with no change in behavior. There is no penalty—no additional noise, no altered clipping dynamics, no shifted gradients—for using the GSPO-token formulation even when uniform advantages are assigned. The stop-gradient construction is designed precisely to ensure this seamless reduction.

# Flexibility of GSPO-token for Per-Token Advantage Customization

While GSPO-token is numerically identical to GSPO under uniform advantages, its true value emerges when per-token advantages are allowed to differ. In this case, the GSPO-token gradient becomes:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta) = \mathbb{E} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} s_i(\theta) \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \widehat{A}_{i,t} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right],$$

where $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ can now vary across token positions. This means that the learning signal applied to each token's log-likelihood gradient is independently modulated by its own advantage, while the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ continues to serve as a uniform weight that ensures the off-policy correction remains stable and grounded in the principles of importance sampling.

This flexibility is particularly valuable in scenarios where different parts of a response contribute differently to the overall outcome. For example, in multi-turn RL, certain turns may be more critical to the final reward than others. By assigning higher advantages to tokens in those critical turns and lower (or even negative) advantages to tokens in less important turns, the model can be guided to focus its learning on the most impactful parts of its responses. The sequence-level importance ratio still ensures that the off-policy correction and clipping operate at the response level, preventing the high-variance noise that plagues GRPO's token-level importance ratios.

It is worth emphasizing that this per-token flexibility does not come at the cost of the stability benefits that GSPO provides over GRPO. The key distinction from GRPO, as identified in the gradient analysis, is that GSPO and GSPO-token both weight all tokens in a response equally through the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$, whereas GRPO assigns potentially wildly varying weights $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ to different tokens. In GSPO-token, even when $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ varies across tokens, the importance ratio weighting remains uniform, so the instability factor identified in GRPO—unequal and potentially unbounded token-level importance weights—does not arise. The per-token variation enters only through the advantage, which is a bounded and interpretable quantity, rather than through the importance ratio, which in GRPO can fluctuate unpredictably.

# Practical Use Cases: When to Use GSPO vs. GSPO-token

The choice between GSPO and GSPO-token depends on whether the application scenario calls for per-token advantage differentiation. The paper identifies multi-turn RL as the primary scenario in which GSPO-token is beneficial. In multi-turn settings, a response may consist of multiple interaction rounds with an environment or user, and the quality of each round can vary. GSPO-token allows the advantage to be customized for each token, enabling finer-grained control over which parts of the response the model is encouraged to reinforce or discourage.

For standard single-turn RL, where a reward is assigned to an entire response and there is no meaningful way to distinguish the contribution of individual tokens within that response, the standard GSPO formulation is the natural choice. Since GSPO-token reduces exactly to GSPO when uniform advantages are used, there is no practical difference between the two in this setting, and the simpler GSPO formulation may be preferred for its conceptual clarity and directness.

It is also notable that GSPO-token preserves the key benefits of GSPO that make it superior to GRPO. These include the inherent stability of sequence-level importance ratios, the elimination of the high-variance noise introduced by token-level importance weights, and the robustness to expert-activation volatility in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. Because GSPO-token weights all tokens in a response equally through the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$—rather than through per-token importance ratios as GRPO does—it retains these benefits regardless of how the per-token advantages are assigned. The clipping mechanism in GSPO-token also continues to operate at the sequence level, since $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$, ensuring that overly off-policy responses are excluded from gradient estimation in the same manner as in standard GSPO.

In summary, GSPO-token should be viewed as a flexible extension of GSPO that broadens its applicability to scenarios requiring token-level advantage control, without sacrificing any of the theoretical or practical advantages that make GSPO a stable and efficient algorithm for training large language models. The two formulations form a unified family: GSPO is the special case of GSPO-token with uniform per-token advantages, and GSPO-token is the general case that enables the additional flexibility needed for more complex RL scenarios such as multi-turn interactions.

# Empirical Evaluation and Analysis

# Experimental Setup: Cold-Start Model, Training Configuration, and Benchmarks

To rigorously evaluate the merits of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) against the established Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm, a carefully controlled experimental framework is essential. The experiments employ a cold-start model that has been fine-tuned from the Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model. This particular base model is a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture with 30 billion total parameters and 3 billion activated parameters, making it an ideal testbed for assessing both the performance and stability of reinforcement learning algorithms under realistic conditions. The choice of an MoE model is particularly significant because, as discussed in earlier chapters, MoE models introduce unique stability challenges during RL training due to expert-activation volatility — challenges that GSPO is specifically designed to address.

The training procedure follows a standard yet demanding configuration. During RL training, each batch of rollout data is partitioned into four mini-batches for gradient updates. This mini-batch partitioning is a critical design choice that reflects common practice in large-scale RL training: to maximize hardware utilization, a large rollout batch is generated, and then this batch is divided into multiple smaller mini-batches, each of which is used for a separate gradient update step. This procedure inevitably introduces an off-policy learning setting, where the responses being optimized were sampled from an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ rather than the current policy $\pi_\theta$ being updated. This off-policy discrepancy is precisely what necessitates the clipping mechanisms in both GSPO and GRPO, as these mechanisms prevent overly off-policy samples from exerting undue influence on gradient estimation.

The evaluation of model performance is conducted across three prestigious and widely recognized benchmarks that span competition-level mathematics and programming. The first is AIME'24, the American Invitational Mathematics Examination from 2024, on which performance is measured as the average Pass@1 over 32 samplings. This benchmark tests the model's ability to solve sophisticated mathematical problems that require deep reasoning. The second is LiveCodeBench, specifically covering the period from October 2024 to February 2025, on which performance is measured as the average Pass@1 over 8 samplings. This benchmark evaluates the model's programming capabilities on contemporary coding challenges. The third is CodeForces, a competitive programming platform, on which performance is measured using the Elo Rating system, providing a standardized competitive ranking that reflects the model's coding proficiency relative to human competitors. Together, these three benchmarks provide a comprehensive assessment of the model's reasoning and problem-solving capabilities — the very capabilities that large-scale RL training aims to enhance.

# Mini-Batch Partitioning and Clipping Range Settings for Fair Comparison

A fair comparison between GSPO and GRPO requires careful attention to the clipping range hyperparameters, as the two algorithms define importance ratios at fundamentally different levels and thus operate on different numerical scales. In GRPO, the importance ratio is defined at the token level as $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$, which represents the ratio of probabilities for a single token under the current and old policies. In GSPO, the importance ratio is defined at the sequence level as $s_{i}(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$, which represents the length-normalized geometric mean of the per-token importance ratios across the entire response. Due to this geometric averaging, the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ naturally exhibits much smaller deviations from unity compared to individual token-level ratios, necessitating a tighter clipping range.

For the GSPO experiments, the left and right clipping ranges in the objective are set to $3 \times 10^{-4}$ and $4 \times 10^{-4}$, respectively. These values are orders of magnitude smaller than the clipping ranges used in GRPO. For the GRPO baseline, the left and right clipping ranges are set to $0.2$ and $0.27$, respectively. These GRPO clipping ranges were carefully tuned to ensure a fair comparison, meaning that significant effort was invested in finding the best-performing clipping configuration for GRPO. The asymmetric clipping ranges — where the right clipping bound is set slightly higher than the left — reflect a deliberate design choice that allows the policy to move somewhat more freely in the direction of increasing the importance ratio (i.e., making a response more likely) than in the direction of decreasing it. This asymmetry is applied consistently in both algorithms.

A critical distinction in the experimental setup concerns the training stability strategies required by each algorithm. GRPO, when applied to MoE models, necessitates the use of a specialized training strategy called Routing Replay to achieve normal convergence. Routing Replay involves caching the activated experts under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and reusing these routing modes when computing importance ratios under the current policy $\pi_\theta$, thereby ensuring that the token-level importance ratios remain stable across gradient updates. GSPO, by contrast, completely obviates the need for this strategy — it is fully capable of computing importance ratios conventionally, converging normally, and optimizing stably without any such workaround. This difference is not merely a convenience; it has significant implications for training efficiency, memory overhead, and model capacity, as will be discussed in detail in subsequent sections.

# Training Reward Curves: Stability and Convergence Comparison

The training reward curves provide the most direct visualization of the stability and convergence behavior of the two algorithms. When examining these curves, the most striking observation is that GSPO training proceeds stably throughout the entire training process. This stability is not merely a matter of avoiding catastrophic failure — it represents a fundamental property of the algorithm's design. Because GSPO defines its importance ratio at the sequence level, it avoids the high-variance noise that plagues GRPO's token-level importance weights. As a result, the gradient estimates in GSPO are more reliable, and the policy updates proceed smoothly without the erratic fluctuations that can destabilize training.

In contrast, GRPO training on the same MoE model exhibits significant stability challenges. Without the Routing Replay strategy, GRPO training fails to converge properly due to the expert-activation volatility inherent in MoE models. After each gradient update, roughly $10\%$ of the experts activated for the same response under the new policy $\pi_\theta$ differ from those activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. This phenomenon causes the token-level importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ to fluctuate drastically, further invalidating their already questionable distributional correction role. Even with Routing Replay in place, GRPO training remains more prone to instability, particularly as training compute increases and response lengths grow.

The stability of GSPO has a profound practical implication: it enables continuous performance improvement through straightforward scaling. Specifically, GSPO can deliver sustained performance gains by increasing the training compute, regularly updating the query set, and extending the generation length. Each of these scaling strategies is important in practice. Increasing training compute allows the model to learn from more data and refine its policy over more gradient steps. Regularly updating the query set ensures that the model is continually exposed to fresh, diverse problems, preventing overfitting to a fixed set of training queries. Extending the generation length allows the model to undertake deeper and longer reasoning processes, which is critical for tackling sophisticated problems in mathematics and programming. The fact that GSPO can accommodate all three of these scaling strategies simultaneously, without succumbing to instability, is a testament to its robust algorithmic foundation.

The stability advantage of GSPO becomes even more significant in light of the catastrophic consequences of instability in GRPO. As discussed in earlier chapters, once model collapse occurs in GRPO training, it is often irreversible. Resuming training proves unavailing even when reverting to a previous checkpoint, meticulously tuning hyperparameters such as clipping ranges, extending generation length, or switching the RL queries. This irreversibility means that the computational investment in training up to the point of collapse is entirely lost — a severe penalty that makes GRPO training of large models a high-risk endeavor. GSPO, by eliminating the root cause of this instability, transforms large-scale RL training from a precarious undertaking into a reliable and predictable process.

# Benchmark Performance: Pass@1 and Elo Rating Comparisons

Beyond training stability, the ultimate measure of an RL algorithm's effectiveness is the performance of the trained model on held-out benchmarks. The experimental results demonstrate that GSPO achieves superior benchmark performance compared to GRPO across all three evaluation benchmarks, providing compelling evidence that the algorithmic improvements in GSPO translate into tangible capability gains.

On AIME'24, which measures average Pass@1 over 32 samplings, the GSPO-trained model achieves higher accuracy than the GRPO-trained model. AIME problems are renowned for their difficulty, requiring creative mathematical reasoning and multi-step problem-solving. The Pass@1 metric, which measures the probability of generating a correct solution in a single attempt, is a stringent measure of the model's genuine problem-solving ability — unlike Pass@k metrics that benefit from multiple sampling attempts, Pass@1 rewards models that can reliably produce correct solutions. The fact that GSPO outperforms GRPO on this metric indicates that the sequence-level optimization of GSPO leads to more reliable and consistent reasoning capabilities.

On LiveCodeBench, which measures average Pass@1 over 8 samplings on coding challenges from October 2024 to February 2025, the GSPO-trained model similarly surpasses the GRPO-trained model. This benchmark is particularly valuable because it covers a recent time period, reducing the likelihood that the problems were included in the pre-training data and thus providing a more genuine assessment of the model's generalization capabilities. The programming tasks on LiveCodeBench require not only correct logic but also precise implementation, making them a comprehensive test of the model's coding proficiency.

On CodeForces, where performance is measured using the Elo Rating system, the GSPO-trained model achieves a higher rating than the GRPO-trained model. The Elo Rating system, originally developed for chess and widely adopted in competitive programming, provides a relative measure of skill that accounts for the difficulty of opponents and the consistency of performance. A higher Elo Rating on CodeForces signifies that the model can compete at a higher level in competitive programming, which involves not only solving individual problems but also doing so efficiently under time constraints. The improvement in Elo Rating thus reflects a holistic enhancement in the model's programming capabilities.

The consistent superiority of GSPO across three diverse benchmarks — spanning pure mathematics, software engineering, and competitive programming — demonstrates that the benefits of sequence-level optimization are not limited to a specific domain or task type. Rather, the more reliable gradient estimates and the alignment between sequence-level rewarding and optimization in GSPO lead to broadly applicable improvements in the model's reasoning and problem-solving capabilities.

# Training Efficiency: GSPO's Superior Performance Under Equal Compute and Query Consumption

Training efficiency is a critical consideration in large-scale RL, where computational resources are expensive and the cost of generating rollout data constitutes a significant portion of the total training budget. The experimental results reveal that GSPO demonstrates superior training efficiency over GRPO, achieving better training accuracy and benchmark performance under the same training compute and consumed queries.

This efficiency advantage can be understood through the lens of the gradient analysis presented in earlier chapters. In GRPO, the gradient of the objective weights each token's log-likelihood gradient by its individual token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$. These weights vary among tokens within the same response, creating unequal and potentially unpredictable gradient contributions. For tokens where the advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ is positive, the token-level weights can range over $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$, while for tokens where the advantage is negative, the weights can range over $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$. The unequal weighting means that some tokens receive disproportionately large or small gradient updates relative to their actual importance, leading to inefficient use of training signal.

In GSPO, by contrast, the gradient weights all tokens in a response equally. The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$ serves as a uniform scaling factor applied to the average of all per-token log-likelihood gradients. This equal weighting ensures that every token in a response contributes to the gradient in a balanced and predictable manner, eliminating the instability factor of GRPO. The result is that each training sample provides a cleaner, more reliable learning signal, allowing the model to extract more useful information from the same amount of data.

The efficiency advantage of GSPO has compounding effects over the course of training. Because each gradient update is more reliable, the policy improves more consistently with each step, leading to faster convergence and better final performance for a given computational budget. This means that for the same amount of GPU-hours and the same number of generated responses (queries), GSPO achieves higher capability than GRPO — a practical advantage that translates directly into reduced training costs and faster iteration cycles in model development. In the resource-intensive world of large language model training, where each training run can cost substantial computational resources, this efficiency gain represents a significant practical benefit.

# The Clipping Fraction Observation: Two Orders of Magnitude Difference Between GSPO and GRPO

One of the most intriguing experimental findings involves the comparison of clipping fractions — that is, the proportion of tokens or responses that are clipped by the clipping mechanism and thus excluded from gradient estimation. The experimental data reveals a striking difference of two orders of magnitude in the fractions of clipped tokens between GSPO and GRPO. This disparity is remarkably robust: adjusting the clipping ranges does not alter the disparity in magnitude, indicating that the difference is a fundamental property of the algorithms rather than an artifact of hyperparameter choices.

To understand this observation, it is important to consider what the clipping mechanism does in each algorithm. In GRPO, clipping is applied at the token level: for each token $y_{i,t}$, if the token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ falls outside the range $[1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]$, that token's contribution to the objective is clipped, effectively preventing it from participating in gradient estimation. Given that token-level importance ratios can fluctuate significantly — especially in MoE models where expert activations may change between the old and current policies — it might seem surprising that relatively few tokens are clipped in GRPO. However, the clipping ranges for GRPO ($0.2$ to $0.27$) are relatively wide, allowing token-level importance ratios to deviate substantially from unity before triggering clipping.

In GSPO, clipping is applied at the sequence level: for each response $y_i$, if the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ falls outside the clipping range, the entire response is clipped. The clipping ranges for GSPO ($3 \times 10^{-4}$ to $4 \times 10^{-4}$) are orders of magnitude tighter than those for GRPO, reflecting the fact that the length-normalized geometric mean of per-token ratios naturally exhibits much smaller deviations from unity. Despite these tighter clipping ranges, GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens — two orders of magnitude more — because the sequence-level importance ratio is a more sensitive indicator of policy divergence. Even small per-token deviations, when aggregated across an entire sequence through the geometric mean, can push the sequence-level ratio outside the tight clipping bounds. When a response is clipped in GSPO, all of its tokens are effectively excluded from gradient estimation, leading to a much higher aggregate fraction of clipped tokens.

# Counter-Intuitive Finding: More Clipping Leads to Better Efficiency

The clipping fraction observation gives rise to a profoundly counter-intuitive finding: despite clipping significantly more tokens and consequently using fewer tokens for training (or gradient estimation), GSPO still achieves higher training efficiency than GRPO. This result challenges the conventional intuition that more training data (i.e., more unclipped tokens participating in gradient estimation) should lead to better learning. The finding suggests that the quality of the learning signal matters far more than its quantity.

The intuition behind this counter-intuitive result can be understood through the lens of signal quality. In GRPO, the token-level gradient estimates that survive clipping are not necessarily the most reliable ones — they are simply the ones whose token-level importance ratios happen to fall within the clipping bounds. Many of these surviving gradient estimates carry the high-variance noise introduced by the ill-posed token-level importance weights. This noise means that a significant portion of the gradient signal in GRPO is essentially random, pushing the policy in unpredictable directions rather than toward genuinely better responses. Training with noisy gradients is not just inefficient — it can be actively harmful, as the noise accumulates over training steps and can eventually destabilize the entire training process.

In GSPO, the responses that survive clipping are those whose sequence-level importance ratios indicate that they are still sufficiently on-policy to provide reliable gradient estimates. The sequence-level importance ratio, being a length-normalized geometric mean, aggregates information across the entire response and provides a much more robust measure of policy divergence than any individual token-level ratio. By clipping at this level, GSPO ensures that only responses with reliable, well-calibrated gradient signals participate in training. The responses that are clipped — those whose sequence-level ratios fall outside the tight bounds — are precisely the ones that would have introduced noise and instability into the training process. In effect, GSPO's aggressive clipping acts as a stringent quality filter, ensuring that every gradient update is based on reliable information.

This finding — that clipping a much larger fraction of tokens leads to superior training efficiency — provides powerful additional evidence that GRPO's token-level gradient estimates are inherently noisy and inefficient for sample exploitation. It demonstrates that the problem with GRPO is not that it clips too many or too few tokens, but that its fundamental approach of token-level importance weighting produces unreliable gradient estimates regardless of the clipping configuration. The fact that GSPO achieves better results with less data (fewer unclipped tokens) is a clear indication that its sequence-level approach provides a more reliable and effective learning signal, making better use of each training sample that passes its quality filter.

# Evidence That GRPO's Token-Level Gradient Estimates Are Inherently Noisy

The clipping fraction observation, combined with the gradient analysis from earlier chapters, provides converging evidence that GRPO's token-level gradient estimates are inherently noisy. This conclusion is supported by multiple independent lines of reasoning, each pointing to the same fundamental issue.

The first line of evidence comes from the theoretical analysis of importance sampling. The principle of importance sampling requires averaging over multiple samples ($N \gg 1$) from the behavior distribution for the importance weight to effectively correct for the distributional mismatch. GRPO applies the importance weight $\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$ at each token position, but this weight is based on a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from each next-token distribution. It therefore fails to perform the intended distribution-correction role and instead introduces high-variance noise into the training gradients.

The second line of evidence comes from the gradient analysis. In GRPO, the gradient weights each token's log-likelihood gradient by its individual token-level importance ratio, creating unequal weights that vary among $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ for positive advantages or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ for negative advantages. These unequal weights are not negligible, and their impact accumulates over long sequences and over many training steps, leading to unpredictable consequences. GSPO, by weighting all tokens equally, eliminates this instability factor entirely.

The third line of evidence comes from the empirical observation that GSPO, despite clipping two orders of magnitude more tokens, achieves higher training efficiency. This finding is only explicable if the gradient estimates that GRPO retains (i.e., the unclipped token-level estimates) contain substantial noise that degrades the quality of learning. If GRPO's unclipped gradient estimates were reliable, having more of them should lead to better or at least comparable efficiency — but the opposite is observed.

The fourth line of evidence comes from the MoE training experience. In MoE models, the expert-activation volatility causes token-level importance ratios to fluctuate drastically, further invalidating their distributional correction role. GRPO requires the Routing Replay strategy to mitigate this issue, while GSPO — which operates at the sequence level and is insensitive to individual token likelihoods — does not. This difference further underscores that the token-level importance ratios in GRPO are inherently unreliable, especially in architectures where per-token probabilities can change for structural reasons (like expert routing) rather than for policy improvement reasons.

Taken together, these four lines of evidence present a compelling case that the token-level approach in GRPO is fundamentally flawed. The noise introduced by token-level importance weighting is not a minor inefficiency that can be tuned away with better hyperparameters — it is a structural property of the algorithm that becomes increasingly problematic as models grow larger, responses grow longer, and architectures become more complex. GSPO's sequence-level approach, by contrast, provides a principled and robust solution that aligns with the fundamental principles of importance sampling and the sequence-level nature of the reward signal.

# GSPO's Role in the RL Training of the Qwen3 Models

The ultimate validation of any algorithmic innovation is its successful application to real-world, large-scale model training. The experimental results confirm that GSPO has been successfully applied to the RL training of the latest Qwen3 models, providing strong proof of its efficacy in unleashing the power of RL scaling for large language models. This application is not merely a demonstration of feasibility — it represents the culmination of the algorithmic insights, stability improvements, and efficiency gains discussed throughout this chapter, applied at the scale necessary to produce state-of-the-art language models.

The role of GSPO in the Qwen3 training pipeline is multifaceted. First, its inherent stability eliminates the risk of catastrophic and irreversible model collapse that plagues GRPO training, allowing the training process to proceed reliably over extended periods without the need for constant monitoring and intervention. This reliability is essential for scaling RL training to the computational budgets required for frontier model development. Second, its superior training efficiency means that more capability is extracted from each unit of computational investment, enabling faster iteration and more effective use of resources. Third, its compatibility with MoE architectures — eliminating the need for Routing Replay and its associated overhead — allows the MoE model to leverage its full capacity without artificial constraints, which is particularly important for Qwen3 models that employ MoE architectures.

The success of GSPO in the Qwen3 training pipeline demonstrates that the algorithmic choices in GSPO are not merely theoretically motivated but practically impactful. The transition from GRPO to GSPO in the Qwen3 training pipeline represents a shift from a token-level optimization paradigm that is ill-posed for sequence-level rewards to a sequence-level optimization paradigm that properly aligns the unit of optimization with the unit of reward. This alignment, combined with the more reliable gradient estimates and the more effective clipping mechanism, enables the sustained and stable scaling of RL training that is necessary to push the capability boundaries of large language models.

The application of GSPO to Qwen3 models also has broader implications for the field of reinforcement learning for language models. It demonstrates that the fundamental principles of importance sampling, when properly applied, can lead to substantial improvements in training stability and efficiency — improvements that are not merely incremental but transformative, enabling new levels of scale and capability. As RL continues to play a pivotal role in the development of language models, GSPO provides a robust and scalable algorithmic foundation that will enable continued advancement, laying the groundwork for future breakthroughs in machine intelligence.

# GSPO for Mixture-of-Experts Models

# Background on Mixture-of-Experts Models and Sparse Activation

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models represent a powerful architectural paradigm in large language model design, offering a pathway to scale model parameters dramatically while keeping computational costs manageable. In a traditional dense model, every token passes through all parameters of the network during both forward and backward passes. In contrast, an MoE model employs a routing mechanism that directs each token to only a subset of "experts"—specialized sub-networks within the model. This property is known as sparse activation: only a fraction of the model's total parameters are active for any given token at any given time. The result is that the model can have a very large total parameter count (enabling greater expressive capacity) while maintaining a comparatively modest active parameter count during inference and training (keeping computational cost feasible).

The sparse activation nature of MoE models, while advantageous for scaling, introduces unique stability challenges when these models are trained with reinforcement learning. The paper highlights this issue in the context of the Qwen3-30B-A3B model, a 48-layer MoE architecture. In such models, the routing decisions—determining which experts process which tokens—are learned parameters that shift as the model is updated. This dynamic routing behavior becomes a source of instability during RL training, as we shall explore in detail throughout this chapter. The fundamental tension is that RL training relies on comparing the behavior of the current policy to that of a previous policy, but when the routing structure itself changes between policy versions, the comparison becomes unreliable. This problem is specific to MoE models and does not arise in dense models, where the network topology is fixed and every token always passes through the same parameters.

# Expert-Activation Volatility: How Gradient Updates Change Activated Experts

The core stability challenge in MoE RL training is what the paper terms "expert-activation volatility." To understand this phenomenon, consider how RL training proceeds in practice. A large batch of rollout data is generated using the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. During these rollouts, each token in each response is routed to a specific set of experts by the MoE routing mechanism. The routing decisions depend on the model parameters at the time of generation. After the rollout data is collected, gradient updates are performed to optimize the policy, yielding a new policy $\pi_{\theta}$. The problem arises because, after even a single gradient update, the routing decisions can change substantially. That is, when the same response is processed under the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$, different experts may be activated for the same tokens compared to what was activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$.

This expert-activation volatility is a direct consequence of the learned routing mechanism in MoE models. The router—which determines which experts handle each token—is part of the model parameters that get updated during gradient steps. Even a small change in the router's parameters can alter the routing decisions for a non-trivial fraction of tokens. Since the router makes decisions at every layer for every token, the compounding effect across layers can be significant. The paper notes that this phenomenon becomes more prominent in deeper MoE models, where the cumulative effect of routing changes across many layers amplifies the overall volatility. This creates a fundamental mismatch: the rollout data was generated with one set of expert activations, but the current policy computes likelihoods using a potentially different set of expert activations for the same tokens.

# Quantifying Expert Volatility: Approximately 10% Expert Change Per Update in Qwen3-30B-A3B

The paper provides a concrete quantification of the expert-activation volatility problem using the Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, which has 48 layers. The authors measured how many experts change activation between the old policy and the new policy for the same rollout sample after each RL gradient update. The finding is striking: approximately 10% of the experts activated under the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$ are different from those activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ for the same response.

To put this in perspective, this means that for every ten expert activations involved in processing a given token across the layers of the model, roughly one of them changes after a single gradient update. This is not a negligible perturbation. The routing decisions are integral to how the model computes the probability of each token, and when 10% of the expert assignments change, the token-level probability computations are significantly altered. This level of volatility occurs after just one gradient update; over multiple mini-batch updates within a single training step, the cumulative divergence could be even larger. The paper observes that this phenomenon becomes more prominent in deeper MoE models, suggesting that the problem would worsen as models are scaled to have more layers. This quantitative finding underscores the severity of the expert-activation volatility problem and motivates the need for a solution.

# How Expert Volatility Invalidates Token-Level Importance Ratios in GRPO

The expert-activation volatility problem has a direct and detrimental impact on the GRPO algorithm's token-level importance ratios. Recall that in GRPO, the importance ratio for each token is defined as:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}.$$

This ratio is meant to correct for the distributional mismatch between the old policy (from which responses were sampled) and the current policy (which is being optimized). The effectiveness of this correction depends on the stability and meaningfulness of the ratio. However, when the experts activated for token $y_{i,t}$ differ between $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, the numerator and denominator of this ratio are computed by fundamentally different sub-networks. The ratio then reflects not only the change in policy parameters but also the change in network topology as experienced by that token.

As the paper discusses in its motivation and gradient analysis sections, the token-level importance weight in GRPO is already problematic because it is based on a single sample from each next-token distribution, introducing high-variance noise into the training gradients. The expert-activation volatility of MoE models exacerbates this problem dramatically. The token-level importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ fluctuate drastically because the underlying computation path changes between the old and new policies. This fluctuation further invalidates the importance ratios, rendering them unreliable as correction factors for off-policy learning. The consequence is that the gradient estimates in GRPO become noisy and unstable, hindering normal convergence of RL training. In the worst cases, this instability can lead to catastrophic and irreversible model collapse, where the model's performance degrades precipitously and cannot be recovered even by reverting to earlier checkpoints or adjusting hyperparameters.

# Routing Replay: Caching and Replaying Expert Routing Modes Across Updates

To address the expert-activation volatility problem in GRPO training of MoE models, the authors previously developed a training strategy called Routing Replay. The core idea is to ensure consistency in the network topology used to compute the token-level importance ratios, thereby restoring their stability. Specifically, during the rollout phase under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, the activated experts for each token in each response are cached—meaning the routing decisions are stored alongside the generated tokens. Then, when computing the importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ during the gradient update phase, these cached routing modes are "replayed" in the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$.

In practice, this means that when the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$ processes a token $y_{i,t}$ to compute $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$, it does not use its own (potentially different) routing decisions. Instead, it forces the token through the same experts that were activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. By doing so, both $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ are computed using the same activated network for each token. This eliminates the fluctuation caused by expert-activation changes and ensures that the token-level importance ratios reflect only the change in policy parameters, not the change in routing topology. The paper demonstrates that Routing Replay is essential for the normal convergence of GRPO training on MoE models—without it, the training fails to converge properly due to the instability introduced by expert-activation volatility.

# Limitations of Routing Replay: Memory, Communication Overhead, and Reduced Model Capacity

While Routing Replay successfully stabilizes GRPO training for MoE models, it comes with significant practical drawbacks. First, caching the activated experts for every token in every response introduces additional memory overhead. In large-scale RL training where rollout batches can be enormous, storing routing information for all tokens across all layers of a deep MoE model is non-trivial. Second, replaying the routing modes requires additional communication overhead in distributed training settings. The cached routing information must be propagated and synchronized across devices, adding complexity and latency to the training pipeline.

Perhaps most critically, Routing Replay limits the actual capacity of the MoE model. By forcing the new policy to use the old policy's routing decisions, the model is prevented from leveraging its full architectural flexibility. The MoE model's power lies in its ability to dynamically route tokens to the most appropriate experts, and by freezing the routing decisions to match the old policy, this dynamic capability is artificially constrained. The model cannot fully exercise its learned routing during the importance ratio computation, which means the training signal is computed on a restricted version of the model rather than the full model. This limitation could potentially hinder the model from reaching its full performance potential. In essence, Routing Replay is a workaround that trades model flexibility and training efficiency for stability—a compromise that, while necessary under GRPO, is far from ideal.

# How GSPO Resolves Expert-Activation Volatility: Insensitivity to Individual Token Likelihoods

GSPO offers a fundamentally different and more elegant solution to the expert-activation volatility problem. The key insight is that GSPO's importance ratio is defined at the sequence level rather than the token level. Recall that GSPO defines the importance ratio as:

$$s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}\right).$$

While this formula does involve individual token likelihoods in its computation, the critical point is that it aggregates them into a single sequence-level quantity through the geometric mean (with length normalization). The gradient analysis in the paper reveals the profound difference: in GRPO, each token's gradient is weighted by its own potentially volatile importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, while in GSPO, all tokens in a response share the same importance weight $s_i(\theta)$, which is the sequence-level ratio. This means that GSPO is fundamentally insensitive to the individual token likelihood fluctuations caused by expert-activation changes. Even if the routing for a particular token changes between the old and new policies, causing its individual token-level ratio to fluctuate, this fluctuation is averaged out across the entire sequence. The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ depends on the overall sequence likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)$, which is a product of all token probabilities in the response, and this aggregate quantity is far more stable than any individual token probability.

# Sequence Likelihood Stability in MoE Models Despite Routing Changes

The stability of the sequence likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)$ in MoE models, even when routing decisions change, is the theoretical foundation that makes GSPO effective for MoE training. The paper explains this stability with a key insight: "Since the MoE model always maintains its language modeling capability, the sequence likelihood will not fluctuate drastically." What this means is that while the specific experts activated for individual tokens may change after a gradient update, the model as a whole still functions as a competent language model. The total probability assigned to a given response sequence—the product of all token probabilities—remains relatively stable because the model's overall language modeling capability is preserved.

To build intuition, consider that in an MoE model, different experts may learn similar or overlapping representations, especially when the number of experts is large. When a token is routed to a different expert after a gradient update, the new expert may produce a similar probability for that token, even though the internal computation path is different. While individual token probabilities may shift slightly, these shifts tend to average out across the many tokens in a response. The sequence likelihood, being the product of all token probabilities (or equivalently, the sum of log probabilities), is a smoothed aggregate that is robust to local fluctuations. This is precisely why GSPO's sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ remains stable and meaningful, even in the presence of expert-activation volatility that would render token-level ratios unreliable. The geometric mean with length normalization further enhances this stability by ensuring that the importance ratio is not dominated by a few outlier tokens.

# Elimination of Routing Replay: Simplification and Full Capacity Utilization

The ultimate benefit of GSPO for MoE training is the complete elimination of the need for Routing Replay. The paper demonstrates that GSPO converges normally and optimizes stably on MoE models without any routing caching or replaying mechanism. This is a significant practical advantage with multiple dimensions of improvement.

First, the training process is simplified. There is no need to implement the complex caching logic for expert activations, no need to manage the communication of routing information across distributed training devices, and no need to override the model's natural routing decisions during importance ratio computation. The RL training infrastructure becomes cleaner and more maintainable. Second, the memory and communication overhead associated with Routing Replay is entirely eliminated, freeing up resources for other uses such as larger batch sizes or longer responses. Third, and perhaps most importantly, the MoE model can leverage its full capacity during training. The model is free to route tokens to whichever experts it deems most appropriate under the current policy, without any artificial constraints imposed by the need to match the old policy's routing. This means the model can fully exercise its learned routing mechanism and develop its full expressive power.

The paper shows that GSPO achieves stable training throughout the RL process on the Qwen3-30B-A3B model without Routing Replay, while GRPO requires Routing Replay to avoid training divergence. This elimination of complex workarounds not only simplifies and stabilizes the training process but also has broader implications for RL infrastructure design. As the paper notes, GSPO's focus on sequence-level likelihoods (rather than token-level likelihoods) also makes it more tolerant of precision discrepancies between training and inference engines, potentially eliminating the need to recompute likelihoods with the training engine. Together, these benefits make GSPO a particularly well-suited algorithm for large-scale RL training of MoE models, and the authors report that these merits contributed to the exceptional performance improvements in the latest Qwen3 models.

# Implications for RL Infrastructure

## Precision Discrepancies Between Training and Inference Engines

In modern reinforcement learning pipelines for large language models, the training and inference components are often handled by separate software systems with distinct implementations. Training engines such as Megatron are designed for distributed gradient computation and parameter updates, while inference engines such as SGLang and vLLM are optimized for high-throughput, low-latency generation. Despite both systems being tasked with evaluating the same model and producing token probabilities, they frequently yield slightly different numerical results due to differences in how they implement numerical operations.

These differences arise from a variety of sources. Training and inference engines may use different precision formats, different kernel implementations for operations like matrix multiplications and softmax computations, or different numerical reduction orders. Even when the same underlying model weights are shared between the two engines, the likelihoods computed for a given token can differ by small amounts. While these discrepancies may seem negligible at the level of an individual token probability, they can have non-trivial consequences depending on how the likelihoods are used downstream in the RL training loop.

Understanding the nature and origin of these precision discrepancies is essential for appreciating the infrastructure advantages that GSPO provides. The key insight is that the severity of the impact of these discrepancies depends on the granularity at which likelihoods are consumed—whether at the token level or the sequence level. As we will see, this distinction has profound implications for how RL training systems can be architected.

## Current Practice: Recomputing Likelihoods with Training Engines

Because of the precision discrepancies described above, practitioners have found it necessary to recompute the likelihoods of sampled responses using the training engine itself, rather than relying on the likelihoods returned directly by the inference engine. This practice has become a standard step in RL training pipelines that use algorithms like GRPO.

To understand why this recomputation is performed, recall that in GRPO the importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$ is computed at the token level. The denominator of this ratio requires the likelihood of each token under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. If the inference engine generates a response $y_i$ and returns its token-level likelihoods under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, those likelihoods may differ slightly from what the training engine would compute for the same tokens. When these slightly discrepant token-level likelihoods are combined with the training engine's computation of $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ in the numerator, the resulting importance ratio can be distorted. Since the importance ratio directly determines the gradient weighting, even small systematic biases in the token-level likelihoods can introduce noise into the optimization process.

To avoid this problem, practitioners recompute the old policy likelihoods $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ for all sampled tokens using the training engine. This ensures that both the numerator and denominator of the importance ratio are computed by the same system with the same numerical precision, eliminating the discrepancy. However, this recomputation comes at a significant cost: the training engine must perform a full forward pass over all sampled responses, which consumes considerable memory and computational resources that could otherwise be dedicated to gradient computation and model updates. This overhead scales with the number of sampled responses, the sequence length, and the model size, creating a practical bottleneck in large-scale RL training.

## Why Sequence-Level Likelihoods Are More Tolerant of Precision Discrepancies

The central observation that makes GSPO advantageous for RL infrastructure is that sequence-level likelihoods are inherently more tolerant of the precision discrepancies between training and inference engines than token-level likelihoods. To understand why, we must consider how the small numerical errors at the token level accumulate—or rather, average out—when aggregated to the sequence level.

Recall that in GSPO, the importance ratio is defined as:

$$s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \right).$$

The sequence likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ is the product of all token probabilities, and taking the logarithm converts this product into a sum. The importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ then involves averaging the log-ratios over all tokens in the sequence. When precision discrepancies introduce small errors into individual token likelihoods, these errors appear as small perturbations in the individual $\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$ terms. Because $s_i(\theta)$ averages these terms over $|y_i|$ tokens, the perturbations tend to cancel one another out. If the per-token errors are roughly zero-mean and independent across positions, their average will tend toward zero as the sequence length grows, making $s_i(\theta)$ remarkably stable.

In contrast, token-level importance ratios in GRPO do not benefit from this averaging effect. Each $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ depends on a single token's likelihood ratio, so the full magnitude of the precision discrepancy at that position is directly reflected in the importance ratio. There is no opportunity for errors to cancel across tokens. Moreover, because the clipping mechanism in GRPO is applied at the token level, a precision-induced perturbation can push a token's importance ratio across the clipping boundary, causing that token to be either unnecessarily clipped or incorrectly included in the gradient computation. This introduces a form of noise that is both pernicious and difficult to control.

The intuition is analogous to the statistical principle that sample means are more stable estimators than individual samples. The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ acts as a smoothed estimator of the distributional shift between the old and current policies, while the token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is a noisy, high-variance estimator. This smoothing property is what makes the sequence-level likelihood robust to the numerical imprecisions inherent in cross-engine likelihood computation.

## Direct Use of Inference Engine Likelihoods in GSPO

Given the tolerance of sequence-level likelihoods to precision discrepancies, GSPO opens the possibility of directly using the likelihoods returned by the inference engine for optimization, without the need for recomputation by the training engine. This is a significant departure from the standard practice necessitated by token-level algorithms like GRPO.

When the inference engine generates a response $y_i$, it naturally computes the token-level likelihoods $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ as part of the generation process (since autoregressive generation requires evaluating the probability distribution over the vocabulary at each step). These token-level likelihoods can be multiplied together—or equivalently, their log-probabilities can be summed—to obtain the sequence-level likelihood $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i | x)$. Because GSPO's importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ depends on the sequence likelihood rather than individual token likelihoods, the small numerical discrepancies between the inference engine and the training engine are averaged out and do not meaningfully distort $s_i(\theta)$.

This means that the inference engine's likelihood output is sufficient for computing the denominator of $s_i(\theta)$. The numerator, $\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)$, is computed by the training engine during the forward pass that is already required for gradient computation. Since both the numerator and denominator are sequence-level quantities and the denominator's precision discrepancies are attenuated by averaging, the resulting importance ratio is reliable enough to use directly in the clipped objective without requiring a separate recomputation step.

Eliminating the recomputation step has a cascading effect on system efficiency. The training engine no longer needs to dedicate a forward pass to evaluating old policy likelihoods on sampled data, freeing up GPU memory and compute cycles. This reduction in overhead is particularly valuable at scale, where the cost of recomputing likelihoods across thousands of sampled responses per batch can be substantial.

## Benefits for Partial Rollout Scenarios

Partial rollout is a scenario in which the inference engine generates only a portion of the complete response—or generates responses in an incremental or streaming fashion—rather than producing the entire batch of responses in a single, complete pass. This can arise for various operational reasons, such as memory constraints on the inference side, the need to prioritize certain queries, or the desire to interleave generation with other computational tasks.

In partial rollout scenarios, recomputing likelihoods with the training engine becomes particularly problematic. The training engine must wait for the inference engine to complete generation for the full batch before it can begin recomputation, or it must handle incomplete responses that arrive incrementally. This introduces synchronization barriers and underutilization of the training engine's resources. The training engine may be idle while waiting for the inference engine to finish, or it may need to perform multiple recomputation passes as responses trickle in.

GSPO's ability to use inference engine likelihoods directly alleviates these problems. Since the inference engine provides the sequence-level likelihoods as part of its generation output, there is no need for the training engine to wait and recompute. The training engine can begin processing partial rollout data as it arrives, using the likelihoods provided by the inference engine. This enables a more pipelined and efficient use of both training and inference resources, as the two engines can operate with greater independence and less synchronization overhead.

## Benefits for Multi-Turn RL

Multi-turn RL involves scenarios where the language model engages in extended interactions, producing multiple responses across several turns of dialogue or interaction with an environment. In such settings, the model may generate a response, receive feedback or new context, generate another response, and so on. Each turn may involve a different context that depends on the previous turns, and the reward may be assigned based on the entire multi-turn trajectory.

The complexity of multi-turn RL introduces additional challenges for likelihood recomputation. The training engine must replay the entire multi-turn interaction, recomputing token-level likelihoods for each turn under the old policy. This requires careful tracking of the conversation state, context management, and ensuring that the recomputation faithfully reproduces the conditions under which the original responses were generated. Any discrepancy in how the training and inference engines handle context formatting or state management can further compound the numerical discrepancies.

With GSPO, the sequence-level likelihoods computed by the inference engine during generation can be used directly, sidestepping the need for the training engine to replay the entire multi-turn trajectory. Since the inference engine already computes these likelihoods as part of the generation process for each turn, it can return them alongside the generated responses. The sequence-level nature of GSPO's importance ratio ensures that the precision discrepancies between engines remain tolerable even across multiple turns.

Furthermore, GSPO-token, the token-level objective variant described in the paper, can be particularly useful in multi-turn RL. As shown in Equation~\eqref{equ:gspo-token}, GSPO-token allows token-wise advantage customization $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ while still using the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ for off-policy correction. This provides the flexibility to assign different advantages to tokens at different turns—perhaps weighting later turns more heavily when the task outcome becomes clearer—while retaining the stability benefits of sequence-level importance ratios. This combination of flexibility and robustness is uniquely suited to the multi-turn setting.

## Benefits for Training-Inference Disaggregated Frameworks

Training-inference disaggregation is an architectural pattern in which the training and inference systems are physically separated, potentially running on different hardware, in different processes, or even in different geographic locations. This separation allows each system to be independently optimized for its respective workload: the inference engine can be configured for maximum generation throughput, while the training engine can be tuned for efficient gradient computation and parameter updates.

In such disaggregated frameworks, the recomputation of likelihoods by the training engine creates a tight coupling between the two systems. The training engine requires access to all generated responses along with their complete token-level context, and it must perform a forward pass that essentially replicates part of the inference engine's computation. This coupling undermines the very goal of disaggregation, as it creates data dependencies, communication overhead, and synchronization constraints between the two systems.

GSPO relaxes this coupling by enabling the inference engine to provide the likelihood information that GSPO needs directly. Since GSPO only requires sequence-level likelihoods—which, as discussed, are tolerant of precision discrepancies—the inference engine's own likelihood computations are sufficient. The training engine can receive the generated responses along with their sequence-level likelihoods and proceed with optimization without needing to recompute anything. This allows the training and inference systems to operate with greater independence, communicating only the essential data (responses, rewards, and sequence-level likelihoods) rather than requiring the training engine to reproduce the inference engine's internal computations.

The practical benefits of this decoupling include reduced communication overhead between training and inference clusters, simplified deployment architectures, and the ability to scale training and inference resources independently. In large-scale RL training, where communication bandwidth and synchronization latency can become bottlenecks, this simplification is valuable.

## Potential for Simplified RL Infrastructure Design

The cumulative effect of the infrastructure benefits described above is a simplified RL training pipeline. By eliminating the need for likelihood recomputation, GSPO removes a component that adds complexity to the system in multiple ways: it requires additional forward passes on the training engine, it creates synchronization barriers between training and inference, it complicates the handling of partial rollouts and multi-turn interactions, and it couples systems that would otherwise benefit from independence.

With GSPO, the RL training loop can be conceptualized more straightforwardly. The inference engine generates responses and returns them with sequence-level likelihoods. The training engine computes the current policy's sequence-level likelihoods, forms the importance ratios $s_i(\theta)$, evaluates the group-based advantages $\widehat{A}_i$, and performs the clipped optimization. The training engine never needs to replay or recompute the old policy's token-level likelihoods, as these are already available from the inference engine at the sequence level.

This simplification has the potential to make RL training systems easier to build, deploy, and maintain. It reduces the engineering effort required to ensure numerical consistency between training and inference engines, eliminates the need for complex context reconstruction logic in multi-turn settings, and allows for more flexible resource allocation between training and inference. As RL training continues to scale to larger models, longer responses, and more complex multi-turn interactions, these infrastructure simplifications become increasingly important for maintaining practical and sustainable training pipelines.

The infrastructure advantages of GSPO are not incidental but follow directly from its theoretical foundation. By defining importance ratios at the sequence level, GSPO aligns the optimization objective with the natural unit of reward, and this alignment resonates through the entire training system. The statistical smoothing that makes sequence-level likelihoods robust to numerical discrepancies is the same property that makes them robust to the token-level noise that destabilizes GRPO. In this way, the theoretical soundness of GSPO and its practical infrastructure benefits are two manifestations of the same core principle: that the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward.

# Conclusion and Future Directions

# Summary of GSPO's Key Innovations

The development of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) represents a fundamental rethinking of how reinforcement learning algorithms should be designed for large language models. At the heart of GSPO are three interconnected innovations that distinguish it from its predecessor, GRPO, and from the broader family of policy optimization algorithms like PPO: the redefinition of importance ratios at the sequence level, the application of clipping at the sequence level, and the alignment of the optimization objective with the unit at which rewards are assigned.

The first and most central innovation is the redefinition of the importance ratio. In GRPO, the importance ratio for a response $y_i$ to a query $x$ is computed at the token level, defined as $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$ for each token position $t$. GSPO replaces this with a sequence-level importance ratio defined as $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$, which can equivalently be expressed as $\exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}\right)$. This formulation is the geometric mean of the per-token importance ratios, normalized by sequence length. The length normalization is a critical design choice: without it, the likelihood changes of even a few tokens could cause dramatic fluctuations in the sequence-level importance ratio, and responses of different lengths would require different clipping ranges. By normalizing, GSPO controls the importance ratio within a unified numerical range, making the clipping mechanism practical and consistent across responses of varying lengths.

The second innovation is the application of clipping at the sequence level rather than the token level. In GRPO, clipping is applied to each token's importance ratio individually, with the clipping range $\varepsilon$ constraining $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ to the range $[1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon]$. In GSPO, the clipping operates on $s_i(\theta)$, the sequence-level importance ratio, so that entire responses are either included in or excluded from gradient estimation based on how far they have deviated from the old policy. This means that when a response is deemed too "off-policy," all of its tokens are clipped together, rather than having some tokens clipped and others not based on their individual importance ratios. It is worth noting that because the sequence-level importance ratio has a different numerical scale than the token-level one, the clipping ranges in GSPO and GRPO typically differ in order of magnitude. For instance, in the paper's experiments, GSPO uses clipping ranges of $3 \times 10^{-4}$ and $4 \times 10^{-4}$ for the left and right bounds, respectively, while GRPO uses $0.2$ and $0.27$.

The third innovation is the alignment of the optimization objective with the sequence-level reward. The GSPO objective is $\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot | x)} \left[\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \min\left(s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i, \text{clip}(s_i(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon) \widehat{A}_i\right)\right]$, where the advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ is computed from group-relative rewards: $\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \text{mean}(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\text{std}(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}$. Since the reward $r(x, y_i)$ is assigned to the entire sequence $y_i$, and the importance ratio and clipping both operate at the sequence level, the entire pipeline—from reward computation to importance weighting to gradient estimation—is consistent in its treatment of the response as a single unit. This alignment addresses a core principle identified in the paper: the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of reward.

# Theoretical Grounding: Alignment with the Basic Principle of Importance Sampling

The theoretical justification for GSPO rests on a careful reexamination of the importance sampling principle and how it should be applied in the context of language model training. Importance sampling is a technique for estimating the expectation of a function $f$ under a target distribution $\pi_{\text{tar}}$ using samples drawn from a behavior distribution $\pi_{\text{beh}}$. The fundamental identity is $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)} f(z)\right]$. For this reweighting to correctly correct for the distributional mismatch, it is essential that the importance weight $\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}$ be applied with access to multiple samples ($N \gg 1$) from the behavior distribution. The statistical validity of the correction depends on the law of large numbers averaging out the variance of the individual weights.

The paper identifies that GRPO's application of token-level importance ratios violates this principle. In GRPO, the importance weight $\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$ is applied at each token position $t$, but this weight is based on a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from each next-token distribution $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot | x, y_{i,<t})$. Because there is only one sample per token position, the importance weight cannot perform its intended distribution-correction role. Instead, it introduces high-variance noise into the training gradients. This noise accumulates over long sequences—where there are many token positions, each contributing its own noisy weight—and is further exacerbated by the clipping mechanism, which can amplify the effects of extreme weight values.

In contrast, GSPO's sequence-level importance ratio $\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y | x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y | x)}$ has a clear and theoretically valid interpretation: it measures how far a response $y$, sampled from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot | x)$, deviates from the current policy $\pi_{\theta}(\cdot | x)$. This is precisely the quantity that importance sampling is meant to correct for, and it naturally aligns with the sequence-level reward. The sequence likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y | x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_{\theta}(y_t | x, y_{<t})$ aggregates information across all tokens in the response, providing a more stable and meaningful measure of policy deviation than any individual token-level ratio.

The gradient analysis in the paper further illuminates this theoretical grounding. The gradient of the GSPO objective (with clipping omitted for brevity) is $\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot | x)} \left[\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})\right]$. The key observation is that the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ scales the entire sum of per-token log-likelihood gradients equally. In contrast, the GRPO gradient is $\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot | x)} \left[\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})\right]$, where each token's gradient is weighted by its own potentially noisy and unequal importance weight. These unequal weights can range across $(0, 1 + \varepsilon]$ for positive advantages or $[1 - \varepsilon, +\infty)$ for negative advantages, and their cumulative effect over training can lead to unpredictable consequences. By weighting all tokens in a response equally through the sequence-level ratio, GSPO eliminates this source of instability.

# Key Empirical Findings: Stability, Efficiency, and Performance over GRPO

The empirical evaluation of GSPO, conducted using a cold-start model fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, demonstrates clear advantages over GRPO across multiple dimensions. The experiments measure training reward curves as well as model performance on several benchmarks: AIME'24 (average Pass@1 over 32 samplings), LiveCodeBench (202410-202502, average Pass@1 over 8 samplings), and CodeForces (Elo Rating). During training, each batch of rollout data is partitioned into four mini-batches for gradient updates, introducing the off-policy learning setting that necessitates importance sampling and clipping.

The first and perhaps most critical finding is training stability. The paper reports that GSPO training proceeds stably throughout the entire training process. This stands in stark contrast to GRPO, which exhibits severe stability issues when training large language models, often resulting in catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. The paper describes that once collapse occurs in GRPO, resuming training is unavailing—even reverting to a previous checkpoint, meticulously tuning hyperparameters such as clipping ranges, extending generation length, or switching the RL queries cannot recover the model. GSPO's stability is not merely a marginal improvement but a qualitative difference: it transforms a fundamentally unstable training process into a reliable one.

The second finding is training efficiency. Under the same training compute and consumed queries, GSPO achieves better training accuracy and benchmark performance than GRPO. This means that GSPO extracts more useful learning signal per unit of computational resources invested. The paper demonstrates that GSPO can deliver continuous performance improvement through increasing training compute, regularly updating the query set, and extending the generation length. This property is essential for RL scaling, as it means that additional computational investment reliably translates into improved model capabilities—a relationship that cannot be taken for granted given the instability issues of previous algorithms.

The third finding is a counter-intuitive observation regarding clipping fractions. GSPO clips significantly more tokens than GRPO—by approximately two orders of magnitude in the fraction of clipped tokens. Despite clipping a much larger proportion of tokens and consequently using fewer tokens for gradient estimation, GSPO still achieves higher training efficiency. This finding is initially surprising: one might expect that discarding more data would harm training. However, the paper interprets this as evidence that GRPO's token-level gradient estimates are inherently noisy and inefficient. The tokens that GRPO does not clip may contribute unreliable gradients due to the invalidity of per-token importance weights, while GSPO's sequence-level clipping ensures that only coherent, reliable learning signals contribute to gradient estimation. The high clipping fraction in GSPO reflects a more stringent and meaningful quality threshold for gradient contributions, not a wasteful discarding of useful data.

# Resolution of MoE Training Stability Challenges

One of the most significant practical contributions of GSPO is its resolution of the stability challenges specific to Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models in RL training. MoE models, which use sparse activation to achieve greater model capacity, introduce unique difficulties for RL that do not arise in dense models. The paper identifies a specific problem called expert-activation volatility: after one or more gradient updates, the experts activated for the same response can change significantly between the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$. For the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, approximately 10% of the experts activated under the new policy differ from those under the old policy for the same rollout sample.

This expert-activation volatility is particularly damaging for GRPO because GRPO's token-level importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$ are computed by running the same token through potentially different sets of activated experts in the old and new policies. When the routing changes, the numerator and denominator of the importance ratio are computed by fundamentally different subnetworks, causing the ratio to fluctuate drastically and lose its meaning as a measure of policy deviation. This problem becomes more prominent in deeper MoE models and hinders the normal convergence of RL training.

The previous workaround for this problem was a technique called Routing Replay. In this approach, the activated experts under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ are cached and then "replayed" when computing the importance ratios under $\pi_{\theta}$. By forcing both policies to use the same activated network for each token, Routing Replay restores the stability of the token-level importance ratios. The paper confirms that this technique is essential for GRPO training of MoE models to converge properly. However, Routing Replay comes with significant costs: it incurs additional memory and communication overhead, and it limits the actual capacity of the MoE model by preventing the model from leveraging its full routing flexibility.

GSPO fundamentally resolves this issue without requiring any such workaround. The key insight is that GSPO focuses only on the sequence likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)$ and is not sensitive to individual token likelihoods $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$. While expert routing may change for individual tokens, the MoE model always maintains its overall language modeling capability, so the sequence likelihood does not fluctuate drastically. This means that GSPO can compute the importance ratios $s_i(\theta)$ conventionally, without caching or replaying routing decisions, and still converge normally and optimize stably. By eliminating the need for Routing Replay, GSPO not only simplifies and stabilizes the training process but also allows the MoE model to leverage its full capacity without artificial constraints on routing.

# Infrastructure Simplification Potential

Beyond its algorithmic advantages, GSPO has the potential to simplify the infrastructure required for large-scale RL training of language models. In practice, a common architectural pattern for RL training involves using separate engines for training and inference: a training engine such as Megatron for gradient computation and parameter updates, and an inference engine such as SGLang or vLLM for generating responses. These engines often have precision discrepancies—differences in numerical representations and computation patterns that can cause the same input to produce slightly different outputs.

In GRPO and other token-level algorithms, these precision discrepancies are problematic because the token-level importance ratios require precise per-token likelihoods from both the old and current policies. To ensure consistency, practitioners typically use the training engine to recompute the likelihoods of sampled responses under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, rather than trusting the likelihoods returned by the inference engine. This recomputation step adds significant overhead, as it requires running the full model forward pass on all sampled responses through the training engine, duplicating work that the inference engine has already done.

GSPO ameliorates this problem because it uses only sequence-level likelihoods for optimization, and sequence-level likelihoods are inherently more tolerant of precision discrepancies. The product of per-token probabilities aggregates information across the entire sequence, and small numerical differences at the token level tend to average out when combined into a sequence-level likelihood. This robustness means that GSPO makes it feasible to directly use the likelihoods returned by the inference engine for optimization, bypassing the need for recomputation with the training engine.

This simplification is particularly beneficial in several scenarios. In partial rollout, where only a subset of the model's responses are used for updates, avoiding recomputation reduces the overhead proportional to the rollout size. In multi-turn RL, where responses are generated interactively across multiple turns, the ability to use inference-engine likelihoods directly streamlines the pipeline. In training-inference disaggregated frameworks, where the training and inference systems may be physically separated, reducing the data that must be transferred and recomputed between them lowers communication costs and simplifies system design. By making the RL infrastructure simpler and more efficient, GSPO reduces the engineering burden of scaling RL training, allowing researchers and practitioners to focus on algorithmic improvements rather than infrastructure workarounds.

# GSPO as a Scalable Algorithmic Foundation for Continued RL Scaling

The culmination of GSPO's innovations—its theoretical grounding, empirical superiority, resolution of MoE training challenges, and infrastructure simplification potential—positions it as a scalable algorithmic foundation for continued RL training of large language models. The paper provides strong evidence for this claim through the successful application of GSPO to the RL training of the latest Qwen3 models, which contributed to remarkable improvements in their performance.

The scalability of GSPO can be understood along several dimensions. First, in terms of model size, GSPO's stability does not degrade as models grow larger—a property that cannot be taken for granted, as GRPO's instability becomes more severe with larger models. The elimination of high-variance token-level importance ratios removes a source of instability that would otherwise be amplified at scale. Second, in terms of model sparsity, GSPO's ability to handle MoE models without Routing Replay means that the efficient sparse architectures that are increasingly important for scaling can be trained with RL as straightforwardly as dense models. Third, in terms of response length, GSPO's sequence-level approach avoids the accumulation of token-level noise over long sequences, making it suitable for the long reasoning processes that are increasingly important for sophisticated tasks such as competition-level mathematics and programming.

The paper also introduces a token-level variant of GSPO, called GSPO-token, which provides additional flexibility for scenarios requiring finer-grained advantage adjustment, such as multi-turn RL. GSPO-token defines $s_{i,t}(\theta) = \text{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\text{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})]}$, where $\text{sg}[\cdot]$ denotes stopping the gradient (corresponding to the detach operation in PyTorch). The term $\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\text{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})]}$ has a numerical value of 1, so $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$, but the gradient computation allows per-token advantage customization. The gradient of GSPO-token is $\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO-token}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot | x)} \left[\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \widehat{A}_{i,t} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})\right]$, which is identical to the GSPO gradient when $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i$ for all tokens, while allowing token-wise advantage adjustment when needed. This variant ensures that the GSPO framework can adapt to a range of training scenarios without sacrificing its core stability properties.

# Future Directions: Scaling RL with GSPO and Anticipated Advances in Intelligence

The introduction of GSPO opens several promising directions for future research and development in large-scale RL training of language models. The paper envisions GSPO as a robust and scalable algorithmic cornerstone that will enable the continued advancement of large-scale RL training, and several specific avenues for future work emerge from the findings.

First, GSPO's stability properties make it feasible to push the boundaries of RL scaling further than previously possible. The paper demonstrates that GSPO can deliver continuous performance improvement through increasing training compute, regularly updating the query set, and extending generation length. A natural future direction is to explore how far these scaling axes can be pushed: how large can models become before GSPO encounters new stability challenges, how long can generated responses be, and how diverse and challenging can the query sets be? The fact that GSPO has already been successfully applied to the Qwen3 models provides a strong starting point, but the limits of GSPO's scalability remain to be explored.

Second, the infrastructure simplification potential of GSPO suggests opportunities for new system designs. If sequence-level likelihoods from inference engines can be used directly for optimization, this opens the door to more efficient training-inference disaggregated frameworks, novel partial rollout strategies, and streamlined multi-turn RL pipelines. Future work could explore how to fully realize these infrastructure benefits, potentially redesigning RL training systems around the assumption of sequence-level optimization.

Third, the GSPO-token variant points toward richer advantage estimation schemes. While GSPO uses a single sequence-level advantage for all tokens in a response, GSPO-token allows per-token advantages to be customized. Future research could investigate more sophisticated advantage estimation methods that assign different advantages to different parts of a response—for example, giving higher advantages to tokens that contribute more to the correctness of a mathematical proof or to the key reasoning steps in a programming solution. The GSPO framework, with its stable sequence-level importance weighting, provides a secure foundation for experimenting with such fine-grained advantage signals without introducing the instability that plagued token-level approaches in GRPO.

Fourth, the resolution of MoE training challenges raises questions about how GSPO interacts with other architectural innovations. As models continue to evolve—with new forms of sparsity, new routing mechanisms, or hybrid dense-sparse architectures—GSPO's sequence-level focus may provide a robust interface that is insensitive to architectural details at the token level. Exploring GSPO's behavior with emerging architectures could reveal new opportunities for efficient and stable RL training.

Finally, the paper anticipates that scaling RL with GSPO will lead to fundamental advances in intelligence. The ability to train larger models, with longer reasoning processes, on more challenging problems, without the constant threat of catastrophic collapse, creates the conditions for language models to develop increasingly sophisticated capabilities. The success of GSPO in the Qwen3 models is evidence that stable, efficient RL training can translate directly into improved model performance on competition-level mathematics, programming, and other complex reasoning tasks. As GSPO enables further scaling along the dimensions of model size, response length, and task complexity, the resulting advances in model capability may push the boundaries of what language models can achieve, bringing us closer to the goal of artificial general intelligence. The paper's closing vision—that GSPO will serve as a scalable algorithmic cornerstone for continued RL scaling—positions it not just as an incremental improvement over GRPO, but as a foundational algorithm that could shape the trajectory of large-scale RL research for years to come.
