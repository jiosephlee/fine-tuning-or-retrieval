\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does the token-level importance ratio in GRPO fail as a valid importance sampling weight?
Question:
In the GSPO paper, the authors argue that GRPO's token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$ is a misapplication of importance sampling. They state that because this weight is based on a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from each next-token distribution, it "fails to perform the intended distribution-correction role." However, importance sampling can technically be applied with any number of samples, including $N=1$. So what exactly makes the token-level ratio invalid? Is the issue that the expectation being estimated is over sequences, not tokens? Or is it about variance accumulation across the token-level ratios within a sequence? I would appreciate a more precise explanation of why the single-sample argument invalidates the token-level importance weight.
Answer:
The core issue is not simply that $N=1$ makes importance sampling impractical — as you note, a single-sample importance weight is still unbiased in principle. The problem is more structural: the token-level ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is being used as an importance weight for the **wrong distribution**. It corrects for the mismatch between next-token distributions, but the expectation being estimated in RL is over **sequences**, not individual tokens. This mismatch, combined with the single-sample nature of each token-level ratio, is what invalidates the weight.

To see this clearly, consider what proper importance sampling requires in this context. The RL objective involves an expectation over sequences $y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot | x)$, and when using samples from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, the correct importance weight is the sequence-level ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y | x)}$. By the chain rule of autoregressive generation, this factorizes as $\prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t | x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t | x, y_{<t})} = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} w_{i,t}(\theta)$. The crucial point is that this **product** is the valid importance weight for the sequence, not the individual factors used separately.

GRPO does not use this product. Instead, it applies each factor $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ independently as a per-token multiplier on the advantage $\widehat{A}_i$. The result is that each token-level ratio is acting as an importance weight for the next-token distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x, y_{i,<t})$, but no expectation is being taken over that distribution — the advantage is a sequence-level quantity shared by all tokens. With a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from each next-token distribution, the ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is simply a scalar that depends on the particular token that happened to be sampled. It does not average over multiple draws from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x, y_{i,<t})$, so it never performs the distribution-correction role that importance sampling requires. In contrast, at the sequence level, each response $y_i$ is one sample from the sequence distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)$, and the group of $G$ responses provides the multiple samples needed for the sequence-level importance weight to function as intended.

The practical consequence becomes visible in the gradient analysis. The GRPO gradient weights each token's gradient $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ by a **different** factor $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, which can fluctuate across the range $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ depending on the advantage sign. These per-token weights are neither equal nor controlled — they inject high-variance noise into the gradient estimate that accumulates across long sequences. The GSPO gradient, by contrast, weights all tokens in a response by the same sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta) = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log w_{i,t}(\theta)\right)$, which is the geometric mean of the token-level ratios. This averaging stabilizes the weight and ensures every token in the sequence receives a consistent correction signal that matches the sequence-level reward.

So to directly answer your question: the primary issue is the **unit mismatch** — the expectation is over sequences, but the importance correction is applied at the token level, where it corresponds to the wrong distribution. The single-sample argument reinforces this: even if you wanted to do importance sampling at the token level (which would be misdirected), a single sample from each next-token distribution cannot perform meaningful distribution correction. The variance accumulation you mention is the downstream symptom: because the per-token ratios are noisy, uncontrolled multipliers rather than a coherent importance weight, their independent application across tokens produces unstable gradient estimates that compound over long responses and are further distorted by the clipping mechanism.

### Why is the length-normalized geometric mean used for the sequence-level importance ratio in GSPO?
Question:
GSPO defines the sequence-level importance ratio as $s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \right)$. The paper says length normalization is adopted to "reduce the variance and to control $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range." But why is the geometric mean (i.e., the $1/|y_i|$ exponent) the right choice here? Wouldn't the raw sequence-level ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}$ be the theoretically correct importance weight for sequence-level importance sampling? Does using the geometric mean break the theoretical guarantees of importance sampling, or is it still a valid importance weight in some sense?
Answer:
The short answer is that the raw sequence-level ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)}$ is indeed the theoretically correct importance weight, but it is practically unusable for long sequences, and the geometric mean is a deliberate engineering compromise that preserves the key benefits of sequence-level optimization while remaining numerically stable.

The problem with the raw ratio is that it is a product of $|y_i|$ token-level ratios. Since each token ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}$ can deviate from 1, their product grows or shrinks exponentially with sequence length. For a response of 1000 tokens, even a tiny per-token drift compounds into a wildly extreme value. As the paper explains, this means "the likelihood changes of a few tokens can result in dramatic fluctuations of the sequence-level importance ratio, and the importance ratios of responses with different lengths will require varying clipping ranges." You would need length-dependent clipping thresholds, and the variance of the importance weights would make gradient estimates extremely noisy.

The geometric mean solves this by taking the $1/|y_i|$ power, which converts the product into an average (in log space). The importance ratio becomes $s_i(\theta) = \exp\!\bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid \cdots)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} \mid \cdots)}\bigr)$, which is the exponential of the average per-token log-ratio. This keeps $s_i(\theta)$ in a range that is roughly independent of sequence length, allowing a single clipping range (the paper uses $[3\times10^{-4}, 4\times10^{-4}]$) to work across all responses regardless of length.

Now, does this break the theoretical guarantees of importance sampling? Strictly speaking, yes — the geometric mean is not a valid importance weight. The correct importance weight for a sequence $y_i$ sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ is exactly $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)}$, and raising it to the $1/|y_i|$ power means the resulting estimator is no longer an unbiased estimate of the expectation under $\pi_\theta$. However, the paper's central argument is that this trade-off is not only acceptable but preferable, for two reasons.

First, the paper argues that GRPO's token-level importance weights are themselves invalid importance weights. Importance sampling requires averaging over many samples from the behavior distribution for the weight to correct the distributional mismatch, but GRPO applies a per-token weight based on a single sample from each next-token distribution. This introduces high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences. So the comparison is not "exact importance sampling vs. approximate" — it is "approximate at the sequence level" vs. "invalid at the token level."

Second, the gradient analysis in the paper (Equation for $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}$) reveals what the geometric mean actually does in practice. The gradient becomes:

$$\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})\right]$$

The key structural property is that $s_i(\theta)$ appears as a single scalar multiplier outside the token gradient sum, meaning all tokens within a response receive equal gradient weight. In contrast, GRPO's gradient has each token weighted by its own individual importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, which can vary across the range $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ and produces unequal, unstable per-token weighting. The geometric mean thus serves as a length-normalized signal of how "off-policy" the entire sequence is, applied uniformly to all tokens — which the paper identifies as the desirable behavior when the reward is sequence-level.

In summary, the geometric mean is not a theoretically exact importance weight, and the paper does not claim it is. It is a practical surrogate chosen to keep the importance ratio in a bounded, length-independent range so that sequence-level clipping and optimization can work reliably. The paper's position is that this pragmatic choice is far more sound than GRPO's token-level weights, which fail to satisfy the requirements of importance sampling in the first place, and that the empirical results — including stable training of large MoE models without routing replay — validate this design.

### How does GSPO weight all tokens equally in the gradient while GRPO does not?
Question:
The paper's gradient analysis (Section 3.2) claims that the key distinction between GSPO and GRPO is how they weight the gradients of log likelihoods of tokens. The GSPO gradient is $\sum_{t} s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$, while the GRPO gradient is $\sum_t \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} w_{i,t}(\theta) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$. The paper says GSPO weights all tokens equally because $s_i(\theta)$ is shared across all tokens, whereas GRPO uses per-token weights $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. But in GSPO, doesn't $s_i(\theta)$ still multiply each token's gradient? So isn't the weighting still uneven in some sense—it's just that the weight is the same for all tokens? Is the claim simply that a uniform weight across tokens is more stable than per-token varying weights?
Answer:
You've essentially identified the correct interpretation, but there's a subtle and important distinction worth clarifying about what "equal weighting" means here and why it matters.

The key difference is whether the importance ratio sits **outside** or **inside** the sum over tokens. In GSPO, $s_i(\theta)$ is a single scalar associated with the entire response, so it factors out of the token-level sum. Each token's gradient $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ is multiplied by the same factor $s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i / |y_i|$. In GRPO, the per-token importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ lives **inside** the sum, so each token's gradient receives a different multiplicative weight. That is the entire distinction the paper is drawing: within a given response, GSPO applies a **uniform** weight across all tokens, while GRPO applies **non-uniform** per-token weights.

You are correct that $s_i(\theta)$ is not literally $1$, so it's not "unweighted" in an absolute sense. But the paper's claim of "equal weighting" is about the *relative* weighting across tokens within a response, not about the weight being unity. No token in a response is preferentially up-weighted or down-weighted relative to the others — they all share the same scaling factor.

The deeper point is why this matters. As the paper argues in Section 3, the per-token importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) / \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ does not actually serve as a valid importance sampling correction because it is computed from a single sample at each position, rather than being averaged over many samples from the behavior distribution. Instead of correcting distributional mismatch, it injects high-variance noise into the gradient. Because these weights vary across tokens — the paper notes they can range over $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ for positive advantages or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ for negative advantages — some tokens receive disproportionately large gradient contributions while others are nearly zeroed out, in ways that do not reflect the actual reward signal (which is sequence-level). Over long sequences, this noise accumulates and is further amplified by the clipping mechanism, which the paper identifies as a root cause of training instability and model collapse.

In GSPO, by contrast, $s_i(\theta) = \left(\pi_\theta(y_i|x) / \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)\right)^{1/|y_i|}$ captures how far the **entire response** deviates from the old policy. This has a valid importance sampling interpretation at the sequence level, matching the level at which rewards are assigned. The gradient then becomes a clean average of per-token log-likelihood gradients, all scaled by the same factor. The gradient direction is simply "adjust the policy toward higher-reward responses," without per-token distortion.

So yes, your summary is essentially right: a uniform weight across tokens is more stable than per-token varying weights. But the reason isn't just that uniform weighting is simpler — it's that the per-token weights in GRPO are not performing their intended function (importance sampling correction) and instead introduce noise that compounds with sequence length, whereas the shared weight $s_i(\theta)$ in GSPO is a theoretically grounded, sequence-level correction that leaves the relative contributions of tokens within a response undistorted.

### Why does the GSPO-token variant use stop-gradient, and how does it preserve the sequence-level importance ratio?
Question:
The GSPO-token variant defines $s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})]}$, where $\mathrm{sg}$ stops the gradient. The paper says this is numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$ since the second fraction equals 1. But in the gradient derivation, the $s_i(\theta)$ factor reappears without stop-gradient. I'm confused: if $s_i(\theta)$ is detached in the forward pass, how does its gradient flow through in the backward pass? Is the stop-gradient only on the token-level ratio part, while the sequence-level $s_i(\theta)$ retains its gradient? What is the purpose of this construction versus directly using $s_i(\theta) \cdot \widehat{A}_{i,t}$?
Answer:
# The Stop-Gradient Trick in GSPO-token: Decoupling Forward Numerics from Backward Gradient Flow

The short answer is: the stop-gradient construction is a deliberate "rewrite" that keeps the forward-pass value equal to the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ (so clipping is applied uniformly across a response), while restructuring the backward-pass gradient so that each token receives its *own* advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ as its gradient weight. If you used $s_i(\theta)\cdot\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ directly, the per-token advantage customization would be silently destroyed by the chain rule. Let me unpack this.

## What the construction actually does

Recall the definition:
$$s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}\!\left[s_i(\theta)\right]\cdot \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}\!\left[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\right]}.$$

In the **forward pass**, the second factor is literally $1$, so $s_{i,t}(\theta) \equiv s_i(\theta)$ numerically. This is what the paper means by "numerically equal." Consequently, in the objective of Equation (6), the clipping test $\mathrm{clip}(s_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)$ uses the *same* sequence-level ratio for every token in $y_i$. That preserves the central GSPO design principle: clipping, like rewarding, is applied at the sequence level.

In the **backward pass**, autograd treats $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ as a constant. So the only $\theta$-dependent piece of $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is the numerator $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$. Differentiating:
$$\nabla_\theta s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}\!\left[s_i(\theta)\right]\cdot \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}\!\left[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\right]} = s_i(\theta)\cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),$$
where the last equality uses the fact that the detached quantities are numerically equal to their non-detached counterparts. This is exactly the term that produces Equation (7) in the paper:
$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO-token}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_i s_i(\theta)\cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \widehat{A}_{i,t}\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\right].$$

So the answer to "how does $s_i(\theta)$ reappear without stop-gradient in the gradient?" is that it *doesn't* reappear as a differentiable quantity — it appears as the **numerical coefficient** $s_i(\theta)$ multiplying a per-token gradient. The gradient flows only through the token-level log-likelihood; $s_i(\theta)$ merely scales it. This is precisely the same form as the GSPO gradient (Equation 4), just with $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ replacing $\widehat{A}_i$.

## Why not directly use $s_i(\theta)\cdot\widehat{A}_{i,t}$?

This is the crux. Suppose you wrote the objective directly as $\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ without any stop-gradient. Because
$$s_i(\theta)=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t'} \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t'}\mid x,y_{i,<t'})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t'}\mid x,y_{i,<t'})}\right),$$
its gradient couples *every* token to *every* other token:
$$\nabla_\theta s_i(\theta)=s_i(\theta)\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t'}\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t'}\mid x,y_{i,<t'}).$$

Plugging into the objective,
$$\nabla_\theta\!\left[\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i,t}\right]=s_i(\theta)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \widehat{A}_{i,t}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t'}\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t'}\mid x,y_{i,<t'})\right).$$

Notice what happened: the per-token advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ has collapsed into the **sequence mean** $\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \widehat{A}_{i,t}$. Every token's gradient is now weighted by the *average* advantage over the whole response, not by its own $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. The entire point of GSPO-token — "to allow token-wise advantage customization" — would be lost.

The stop-gradient construction surgically fixes this: by detaching $s_i(\theta)$ in the forward pass, you prevent the chain rule from propagating gradients through the full sequence-level log-likelihood, and by attaching gradient only to the per-token numerator $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$, you ensure that the gradient at position $t$ is weighted by $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ (not by the average), while still carrying the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ as an overall scale factor.

## Intuition

Think of $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ as a "split" object: its **value** is the sequence-level ratio (forward), but its **differential** is the token-level one (backward). The stop-gradient is the mechanism that performs this split. It lets the algorithm keep two desirable properties that would otherwise be incompatible:

- **Forward**: a single, low-variance, length-normalized sequence-level importance ratio that drives clipping decisions, matching the unit of reward.
- **Backward**: a per-token gradient signal whose magnitude at each position is modulated by that position's own advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$.

This is consistent with the broader philosophy of the paper — that "the unit of optimization objective should match the unit of reward" — but generalized to the case where rewards (or advantage estimates) may be decomposed at the token level, as in multi-turn RL. When $\widehat{A}_{i,t}=\widehat{A}_i$ for all $t$, GSPO-token reduces exactly to GSPO in objective, clipping, and gradient, as the paper notes; the stop-gradient is what makes this equivalence hold without arithmetic contortions.

### Why does GSPO clip far more tokens than GRPO but still achieve better training efficiency?
Question:
The paper reports a counter-intuitive finding: GSPO clips two orders of magnitude more tokens than GRPO (Figure 2), yet achieves higher training efficiency. The authors interpret this as evidence that GRPO's token-level gradient estimates are "inherently noisy and inefficient." But if GSPO is discarding so many more samples from gradient estimation, shouldn't it have less data to learn from? Is the argument that the clipped samples in GSPO are genuinely harmful (off-policy), so removing them is beneficial, while in GRPO the per-token clipping doesn't effectively remove harmful samples? Also, how can clipping at the sequence level remove more tokens than clipping at the token level—shouldn't token-level clipping be more fine-grained and thus clip fewer tokens?
Answer:
The counter-intuitive observation—that GSPO clips far more tokens yet learns more efficiently—makes sense once you carefully distinguish between the *granularity* of clipping and the *rate* of clipping, and understand why the clipped samples are harmful in each algorithm.

**Why sequence-level clipping clips more tokens than token-level clipping**

Token-level clipping is more fine-grained in the sense that each token is independently evaluated against the clipping range. However, "fine-grained" does not imply "clips fewer tokens." The actual clipping rate depends on two factors: the clipping range and the distribution of the importance ratio.

In GRPO, each token's importance ratio is $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}$, and the clipping range is relatively wide (the paper uses $[1-0.2, 1+0.27]$). After a single gradient update, most individual token probabilities change only slightly, so $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ typically stays within this wide range and few tokens are clipped.

In GSPO, the sequence-level ratio is $s_i(\theta) = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log w_{i,t}(\theta)\right)$, which is the geometric mean of all per-token ratios. The clipping range is dramatically tighter (the paper uses $[1-3\times10^{-4}, 1+4\times10^{-4}]$). Even though each individual $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ may stay well within GRPO's wide range, small systematic shifts across many tokens compound in the geometric mean. A per-token shift of, say, $+0.01$ in $\log w_{i,t}$ would leave each token unclipped under GRPO, but the averaged log-ratio $\bar{\delta} = 0.01$ would push $s_i(\theta)$ far outside GSPO's tight range. Crucially, when GSPO clips a sequence, *every token in that sequence* is clipped, since they all share the same $s_i(\theta)$. So the combination of a tighter range and whole-sequence clipping causes GSPO to clip orders of magnitude more tokens.

**Why clipping more tokens improves efficiency**

The key argument is indeed that GSPO's clipped samples are genuinely harmful because they are off-policy at the *sequence level*—the level at which rewards are assigned and at which importance sampling is actually valid. The paper's motivation (Section 3) emphasizes that importance sampling requires averaging over many samples to correct distributional mismatch, but GRPO applies the importance weight $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}$ to a *single* token draw from each next-token distribution. This single-sample weight cannot perform the intended distribution correction; instead, it injects high-variance noise into the gradient.

The gradient analysis in Section 3.2 makes this precise. In GRPO, each token's log-likelihood gradient is weighted by its own importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, and these unequal weights vary across $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ depending on the sign of the advantage. These per-token weight discrepancies introduce noise that accumulates over long sequences. GSPO, by contrast, weights all tokens in a response equally with the shared factor $s_i(\theta)$, eliminating this source of instability entirely.

So the answer to your question is yes: in GSPO, the clipped sequences are genuinely off-policy at the meaningful level (the sequence), and removing their gradient contributions prevents harmful updates. In GRPO, per-token clipping is ineffective at removing harmful samples because individual token ratios can look fine even when the sequence as a whole has drifted off-policy. The noise from GRPO's per-token importance weights persists regardless of whether individual tokens are clipped, because the problem is structural—it comes from applying single-sample importance weights at the token level, not from individual tokens being too far off-policy. GSPO's aggressive sequence-level clipping effectively removes entire off-policy sequences from gradient estimation, and the remaining (unclipped) sequences provide a cleaner, lower-variance learning signal. Having fewer but higher-quality gradient estimates turns out to be more efficient than having more but noisier ones.

### How does GSPO resolve the expert-activation volatility problem in MoE models without Routing Replay?
Question:
The paper explains that in MoE models, after a gradient update, roughly 10% of experts activated for the same response change between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, causing token-level importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ to fluctuate drastically. The previous solution (Routing Replay) cached and reused old routing modes. The paper claims GSPO avoids this because it "focuses only on the sequence likelihood and is not sensitive to the individual token likelihood." But $s_i(\theta)$ is computed from token-level likelihoods (via the geometric mean of token ratios). If the experts change for some tokens, won't those token likelihoods change, affecting $s_i(\theta)$? Is the argument that the averaging in $s_i(\theta)$ smooths out the fluctuations from the ~10% of affected tokens?
Answer:
You're right that $s_i(\theta)$ is computed from token-level likelihoods and that expert switching affects some tokens. The resolution has two parts: the averaging does help smooth things out, but the more critical point is how $s_i(\theta)$ enters the **gradient** compared to $w_{i,t}(\theta)$.

First, on the averaging: yes, the geometric mean dilutes the impact of the affected tokens. Since $s_i(\theta) = \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \log w_{i,t}(\theta)\right)$, if 10% of tokens have large log-ratio perturbations of magnitude $\delta$ while the rest are near zero, $s_i(\theta) \approx \exp(0.1\,\delta)$, which is a much milder fluctuation than $e^\delta$ for those individual tokens. The paper explicitly notes this design choice: the length normalization "reduce[s] the variance and control[s] $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range," preventing "the likelihood changes of a few tokens" from causing "dramatic fluctuations of the sequence-level importance ratio."

But the deeper mechanism lies in the gradient analysis in Section 3.2. The key distinction is how the two algorithms weight the per-token gradient contributions $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})$:

- In **GRPO**, each token's gradient is scaled by its own $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, which for the $\sim 10\%$ of tokens with switched experts can be extreme. These tokens then dominate the gradient estimate with disproportionately large (or clipped-to-zero) weights, injecting high-variance noise.
- In **GSPO**, every token in the response shares the **same** scalar $s_i(\theta)$ as its gradient weight. The per-token fluctuations are averaged into one mild, sequence-level scalar, and then applied uniformly.

So it's not just that $s_i(\theta)$ fluctuates less than individual $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ values—though that's true—it's that GSPO structurally prevents any single token's likelihood perturbation from disproportionately influencing the gradient. The paper states this directly: "GSPO weights all the tokens in a response equally, eliminating this instability factor of GRPO."

Regarding the paper's claim that GSPO is "not sensitive to the individual token likelihood": this should be understood in the sense that the optimization signal doesn't depend on per-token likelihood ratios individually, only on their aggregate. Even when expert switching causes certain tokens to have very different likelihoods under $\pi_\theta$ versus $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, the model's overall language modeling capability is preserved, so the sequence likelihood ratio $s_i(\theta)$ remains a stable and meaningful measure of policy drift. The instability in GRPO wasn't really about the sequence likelihood—it was about the noisy, unequal per-token weighting in the gradient, which GSPO eliminates by construction.

### Why are the clipping ranges in GSPO (3e-4, 4e-4) so much smaller than in GRPO (0.2, 0.27)?
Question:
The paper mentions that GSPO uses clipping ranges of $[1-3\times10^{-4}, 1+4\times10^{-4}]$ while GRPO uses $[0.8, 1.27]$. The paper briefly notes that "the clipping ranges in GSPO and in previous algorithms typically differ in order of magnitude due to the distinct definitions of importance ratios." But this is a huge difference—GRPO allows the ratio to deviate by ~20-27% while GSPO allows only ~0.03-0.04%. Why is such a tiny clipping range sufficient for GSPO? Is it because $s_i(\theta)$, being a geometric mean of token ratios, naturally has much lower variance than individual token ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$? And are the asymmetric clipping ranges (different left and right bounds) motivated by something specific?
Answer:
The dramatic difference in clipping ranges stems directly from the mathematical relationship between the two algorithms' importance ratios. Your intuition is correct: $s_i(\theta)$, being a length-normalized geometric mean of token ratios, has far lower variance than individual token ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, which both necessitates and justifies a much tighter clipping range.

To see why, consider how $s_i(\theta)$ is constructed:

$$s_i(\theta) = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}\right) = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log w_{i,t}(\theta)\right)$$

This is the exponential of the arithmetic mean of the log token ratios. If we let $\ell_t = \log w_{i,t}(\theta)$ and $\bar{\ell} = \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \ell_t$, then $s_i(\theta) = e^{\bar{\ell}}$. When the policy has changed only slightly after a few gradient updates, the mean of $\ell_t$ is close to zero, and the variance of the average $\bar{\ell}$ shrinks as $\sigma^2/|y_i|$ (assuming approximate independence of per-token log-ratios). For a response of length $|y_i| = 1000$, this is a variance reduction factor of roughly $1000$, or a standard deviation reduction of $\sqrt{1000} \approx 31.6$.

This alone does not fully account for the $\sim 667\times$ difference between $0.2$ and $3\times10^{-4}$. The remaining gap comes from the fact that GRPO's wide clipping range is not calibrated to the typical change in token probabilities after one gradient step — individual token log-probabilities typically change by far less than 20%. Instead, the wide range in GRPO is necessary because the per-token importance weights $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ are inherently noisy and unreliable, as the paper argues in Section 3. The paper identifies that GRPO's token-level importance weights "fail to perform the intended distribution-correction role" and instead "introduce high-variance noise into the training gradients." A wide clipping range is essentially a band-aid that prevents excessive clipping due to this noise. In GSPO, $s_i(\theta)$ provides a reliable, low-variance measure of sequence-level drift, so a tight range accurately identifies genuinely off-policy sequences. The paper explicitly notes that "we adopt length normalization in $s_i(\theta)$ to reduce the variance and to control $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range."

This is also consistent with the paper's empirical observation in Section 5.2 that GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens than GRPO — by two orders of magnitude — yet achieves superior training efficiency. The tight clipping range is aggressive, but the clipped sequences were contributing noise rather than useful gradient signal. Because GSPO clips at the sequence level, when a sequence is deemed off-policy it is excluded entirely, and the remaining non-clipped sequences provide a cleaner learning signal. In GRPO, the wide range means very few tokens are clipped, but the unclipped per-token weights $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ still introduce uneven, high-variance weighting into the gradient (as shown in the gradient analysis of Section 4.2, where GRPO weights each token's gradient by its own $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ while GSPO weights all tokens equally by $s_i(\theta)$).

Regarding the asymmetric clipping ranges, the paper does not provide an explicit theoretical justification, but the pattern is consistent across both algorithms: GSPO uses $3\times10^{-4}$ below and $4\times10^{-4}$ above (ratio $\approx 1.33$), while GRPO uses $0.2$ below and $0.27$ above (ratio $= 1.35$). A natural explanation is that the asymmetry targets roughly symmetric clipping in log space. Since both $s_i(\theta)$ and $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ are positive ratios, symmetric bounds in log space — i.e., $-\log(1 - \epsilon_{\text{left}}) \approx \log(1 + \epsilon_{\text{right}})$ — translate to slightly asymmetric bounds in the original space, with $\epsilon_{\text{right}} > \epsilon_{\text{left}}$. For GSPO, $-\log(1 - 3\times10^{-4}) \approx 3\times10^{-4}$ and $\log(1 + 4\times10^{-4}) \approx 4\times10^{-4}$, which are close in magnitude. The similar asymmetry ratios across both algorithms suggest a shared design principle, likely tuned empirically as the paper states the ranges were "carefully tuned."

### What does "model collapse" mean in the context of GRPO training, and why is it irreversible?
Question:
The paper repeatedly mentions that GRPO can lead to "catastrophic and irreversible model collapse" during RL training of large language models. The authors state that once collapse occurs, resuming training is unavailing even when reverting to a previous checkpoint, tuning hyperparameters, extending generation length, or switching queries. What exactly constitutes "model collapse" here—is it the model generating degenerate/repetitive outputs, losing its reasoning ability, or something else? And what makes it irreversible? If you revert to a previous checkpoint, shouldn't you be able to resume from a known-good state? Is the irreversibility due to the optimizer state (e.g., momentum in Adam) being corrupted, or is there something deeper going on?
Answer:
## Answer

The paper uses "model collapse" to describe a catastrophic failure mode during GRPO training where the model's training dynamics become unstable to the point that the model can no longer be productively trained, losing its ability to continue improving through RL. The paper does not give a precise behavioral description of what a collapsed model outputs (e.g., repetitive text vs. degraded reasoning), but it frames collapse as the endpoint of accumulating gradient noise that renders continued training futile.

The root cause, as the paper identifies, is that GRPO **misapplies importance sampling at the token level**. Importance sampling is designed to correct for distributional mismatch between a target distribution $\pi_{\text{tar}}$ and a behavior distribution $\pi_{\text{beh}}$ by re-weighting samples: $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)} f(z)\right]$. Crucially, the paper emphasizes that this correction is statistically valid only when averaged over many samples ($N \gg 1$). GRPO instead applies the token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}$ at each position $t$, where each weight is computed from a single sample $y_{i,t}$. This fails to perform the intended distribution correction and instead injects high-variance noise into the gradients.

The paper's gradient analysis in Section 3.2 makes the consequence concrete. In GRPO, different tokens within the same response receive unequal gradient weights that can vary over $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ for positive advantages or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ for negative advantages. These unequal weights accumulate over long sequences and interact poorly with the clipping mechanism, producing "unpredictable consequences" as training progresses. In contrast, GSPO weights all tokens in a response equally by using the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}$, which eliminates this source of instability.

Regarding **irreversibility**: the paper does not attribute it to corrupted optimizer state (e.g., Adam momentum). Instead, it frames the problem as a fundamental flaw in the objective function itself—GRPO's objective is "ill-posed." The irreversibility is reported as an empirical observation: "Once the collapse occurs, resuming training is unavailing, even when reverting to a previous checkpoint and meticulously tuning hyperparameters (e.g., the clipping ranges), extending generation length, or switching the RL queries."

The reason reverting to a known-good checkpoint does not help is that the instability is **inherent to the algorithm**, not to a particular transient state of the model or optimizer. If you restore a healthy checkpoint but continue using the same ill-posed token-level objective, the same high-variance noise will re-enter the gradients and drive the model back toward collapse. The paper's evidence for this is that they tried multiple remediation strategies—checkpoint reversion, hyperparameter tuning, generation length extension, and query set changes—all of which failed. The solution is not to patch around the symptoms but to redesign the objective, which is exactly what GSPO does by moving importance weighting and clipping to the sequence level.

It is worth noting that the paper does not fully probe the fine-grained mechanism of irreversibility (e.g., whether Adam momentum contributes). It presents the irreversibility as an empirical finding and addresses it by proposing an algorithm that avoids the instability in the first place. The broader lesson connects to a principle the paper states explicitly: **the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward**. Since rewards are assigned to entire sequences, applying off-policy correction at the token level is fundamentally mismatched, and no amount of checkpoint management can fix an objective whose gradients are systematically noisy by construction.

### How does GSPO's sequence-level approach help with precision discrepancies between training and inference engines?
Question:
In Section 5.4, the paper claims that GSPO can directly use likelihoods from the inference engine (e.g., SGLang, vLLM) without recomputing them in the training engine (e.g., Megatron), because sequence-level likelihoods are "more tolerant of precision discrepancies." Why would a sequence-level likelihood $\pi_\theta(y|x) = \prod_t \pi_\theta(y_t|x,y_{<t})$ be more robust to floating-point precision differences than individual token likelihoods? Since the sequence likelihood is a product of token likelihoods, wouldn't precision errors compound? Or is the argument that the geometric mean in $s_i(\theta)$ averages out the precision noise? What specific precision discrepancies exist between training and inference engines that cause problems at the token level but not the sequence level?
Answer:
# How GSPO's Sequence-Level Approach Handles Precision Discrepancies

The key insight lies in understanding that GSPO's importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is not the raw product of token likelihoods but rather a **geometric mean** — and this distinction is exactly what prevents precision errors from compounding.

## The Geometric Mean vs. the Raw Product

You're right that the sequence likelihood is a product of token likelihoods, and that precision errors in a raw product would compound. But GSPO doesn't use the raw product. Instead, it defines:

$$s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}\right)$$

The critical operation is the $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ normalization. In log-space, this converts what would be a **sum** of per-token log-ratios (where errors accumulate) into an **average** (where errors cancel). If the per-token precision discrepancy between the inference and training engines introduces an error $\delta_t$ in each token's log-probability, the error in $\log s_i(\theta)$ becomes $\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \delta_t$ rather than $\sum_t \delta_t$. Assuming these errors are roughly zero-mean — which is reasonable since floating-point rounding errors from different kernel implementations (different attention variants, different reduction orders, BF16 vs FP32 accumulation) are not systematically biased in one direction — the average converges toward zero as sequence length grows, with variance shrinking as $\sigma^2/|y_i|$ by the law of large numbers. Without the geometric mean normalization, the raw product's log-error would be $\sum_t \delta_t$ with variance growing linearly in $|y_i|$, which is precisely the compounding you suspected — but the normalization averts this.

## Why Token-Level Ratios Are Fragile

In GRPO, the importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}$ is computed per token, mixing precision from two engines at every position. Each token's ratio independently carries the full, unaveraged precision noise $\delta_t$. This matters in two ways:

**Clipping decisions become unreliable.** GRPO clips each token individually based on whether $w_{i,t}$ falls within $[1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]$. With per-token precision noise, tokens near the boundary may be incorrectly clipped or passed through based on noise rather than genuine policy drift. Since the paper observes that clipping fractions differ by two orders of magnitude between GSPO and GRPO (Section 5.3), these noisy per-token clipping decisions represent a substantial source of wasted or misdirected gradient signal.

**Gradients are differentially weighted by noise.** The GRPO gradient weights each token's gradient by its own noisy $w_{i,t}(\theta)$:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO} \propto \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t w_{i,t}(\theta)\, \widehat{A}_i\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$$

Different tokens receive different noise-contaminated weights, so the gradient direction itself is corrupted — some tokens are upweighted, others downweighted, based on nothing more than floating-point artifacts.

## Why GSPO Is Robust

GSPO's gradient tells a fundamentally different story:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} \propto s_i(\theta) \cdot \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$$

Here, $s_i(\theta)$ is a **single scalar** that uniformly weights all token gradients in the response. Even if $s_i(\theta)$ carries a small residual bias from precision discrepancies, this bias uniformly scales the entire sequence's gradient — it changes the magnitude but not the direction of the update. A uniform scaling is far more benign than per-token differential weighting, because the gradient direction (which determines which directions in parameter space the model moves) remains clean. Moreover, the gradient itself only involves $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$, which is computed entirely within the training engine — the old policy likelihoods from the inference engine only enter through the scalar $s_i(\theta)$, which has been denoised by averaging.

## The Practical Implication

The specific discrepancies between engines like Megatron and SGLang/vLLM arise from different floating-point precisions (BF16 vs FP32 accumulation), different kernel implementations (e.g., FlashAttention variants with different numerical reduction orders), different softmax computations, and — particularly relevant for MoE models — different expert routing computations. At the token level, these produce per-token log-probability differences that are individually small but, when used to form independent per-token importance ratios, inject noise directly into the gradient. At the sequence level, the geometric mean averages these differences out, making it feasible to use the inference engine's likelihoods directly and skip the expensive recomputation in the training engine — a significant infrastructure simplification, especially for multi-turn RL and training-inference disaggregated setups where recomputation is costly.

### Is GSPO's sequence-level importance ratio still a valid importance sampling estimator?
Question:
The paper motivates GSPO by arguing that the importance sampling weight should match the unit of reward (sequence level). The standard importance sampling estimator for a sequence-level expectation would use the ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}$. However, GSPO uses the length-normalized version $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}\right)^{1/|y|}$. Does this modified ratio still yield an unbiased estimator of the sequence-level expectation? It seems like the normalization changes the expected value of the estimator. Is GSPO sacrificing unbiasedness for lower variance, similar to how PPO's clipping sacrifices unbiasedness for stability? The paper doesn't explicitly discuss whether GSPO's estimator is unbiased.
Answer:
You're right to suspect that the length-normalized ratio is not a valid importance sampling weight. The standard importance sampling estimator for a sequence-level expectation $\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}[f(y)]$ using samples from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ requires the weight $\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}$, which satisfies the normalization condition $\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}\right] = 1$. GSPO's length-normalized ratio $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}\right)^{1/|y|}$ does not satisfy this condition. In general, $\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\left[\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}\right)^{1/|y|}\right] \neq 1$ because the exponent $1/|y|$ varies with $y$ and the mapping $R \mapsto R^{1/|y|}$ is concave (for $|y| > 1$), so Jensen's inequality tells us the expectation deviates from $1$. Therefore, $s_i(\theta)$ yields a biased estimator of the sequence-level expectation, and the paper does not explicitly acknowledge this.

That said, the paper's framing is more nuanced than it might appear at first glance. The paper motivates the sequence-level approach by first arguing that the *unnormalized* ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}$ is the theoretically correct importance weight — this is what "aligning with the basic principle of importance sampling" refers to. The length normalization is then introduced as a practical modification, with the paper noting it is adopted "to reduce the variance and to control $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range." Without normalization, the product of many per-token ratios can explode or vanish for long sequences, and different response lengths would require different clipping ranges. So the normalization is explicitly a variance-reduction technique, acknowledged as such, even if the resulting bias is not discussed.

Your comparison to PPO's clipping is apt. PPO's clipping mechanism sacrifices unbiasedness for stability, and GSPO's length normalization does something analogous at the level of the importance weight itself, before clipping even enters the picture. In fact, GSPO makes two such sacrifices: the length normalization biases the weight, and the clipping (applied at the sequence level) further biases the gradient. The paper seems to treat these as acceptable engineering trade-offs, supported by the empirical results showing superior stability and efficiency.

What the paper's gradient analysis (Section 3.2) reveals is perhaps more important for understanding why GSPO works despite this bias. The gradient of the GSPO objective weights all tokens in a response equally by $s_i(\theta)$, whereas GRPO weights each token by its individual token-level ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, which can vary wildly across tokens. The paper argues these unequal weights are the source of instability in GRPO — they introduce high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences and is amplified by clipping. GSPO's equal weighting eliminates this instability factor entirely. From this perspective, the length-normalized ratio serves less as a importance sampling correction and more as a stable per-sequence scaling factor for the gradient, which happens to be close to $1$ when $\pi_\theta \approx \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ (the on-policy regime) and provides a meaningful clipping signal when the policies diverge.

So to summarize: no, $s_i(\theta)$ is not a valid importance sampling estimator in the strict sense, and yes, GSPO is sacrificing unbiasedness for lower variance and stability, analogous to PPO's clipping. The paper does not explicitly discuss this bias, which is a gap in its theoretical analysis. The paper's actual argument rests on the gradient analysis showing that equal token weighting is more stable than GRPO's unequal weighting, and on empirical evidence that this trade-off is beneficial in practice.
