\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### How does GSPO weight all tokens equally in the gradient while GRPO does not?
Question:
The paper's gradient analysis (Section 3.2) claims that the key distinction between GSPO and GRPO is how they weight the gradients of log likelihoods of tokens. The GSPO gradient is $\sum_{t} s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$, while the GRPO gradient is $\sum_t \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} w_{i,t}(\theta) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$. The paper says GSPO weights all tokens equally because $s_i(\theta)$ is shared across all tokens, whereas GRPO uses per-token weights $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. But in GSPO, doesn't $s_i(\theta)$ still multiply each token's gradient? So isn't the weighting still uneven in some sense—it's just that the weight is the same for all tokens? Is the claim simply that a uniform weight across tokens is more stable than per-token varying weights?
Answer:
You've essentially identified the correct interpretation, but there's a subtle and important distinction worth clarifying about what "equal weighting" means here and why it matters.

The key difference is whether the importance ratio sits **outside** or **inside** the sum over tokens. In GSPO, $s_i(\theta)$ is a single scalar associated with the entire response, so it factors out of the token-level sum. Each token's gradient $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ is multiplied by the same factor $s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i / |y_i|$. In GRPO, the per-token importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ lives **inside** the sum, so each token's gradient receives a different multiplicative weight. That is the entire distinction the paper is drawing: within a given response, GSPO applies a **uniform** weight across all tokens, while GRPO applies **non-uniform** per-token weights.

You are correct that $s_i(\theta)$ is not literally $1$, so it's not "unweighted" in an absolute sense. But the paper's claim of "equal weighting" is about the *relative* weighting across tokens within a response, not about the weight being unity. No token in a response is preferentially up-weighted or down-weighted relative to the others — they all share the same scaling factor.

The deeper point is why this matters. As the paper argues in Section 3, the per-token importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) / \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ does not actually serve as a valid importance sampling correction because it is computed from a single sample at each position, rather than being averaged over many samples from the behavior distribution. Instead of correcting distributional mismatch, it injects high-variance noise into the gradient. Because these weights vary across tokens — the paper notes they can range over $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ for positive advantages or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ for negative advantages — some tokens receive disproportionately large gradient contributions while others are nearly zeroed out, in ways that do not reflect the actual reward signal (which is sequence-level). Over long sequences, this noise accumulates and is further amplified by the clipping mechanism, which the paper identifies as a root cause of training instability and model collapse.

In GSPO, by contrast, $s_i(\theta) = \left(\pi_\theta(y_i|x) / \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)\right)^{1/|y_i|}$ captures how far the **entire response** deviates from the old policy. This has a valid importance sampling interpretation at the sequence level, matching the level at which rewards are assigned. The gradient then becomes a clean average of per-token log-likelihood gradients, all scaled by the same factor. The gradient direction is simply "adjust the policy toward higher-reward responses," without per-token distortion.

So yes, your summary is essentially right: a uniform weight across tokens is more stable than per-token varying weights. But the reason isn't just that uniform weighting is simpler — it's that the per-token weights in GRPO are not performing their intended function (importance sampling correction) and instead introduce noise that compounds with sequence length, whereas the shared weight $s_i(\theta)$ in GSPO is a theoretically grounded, sequence-level correction that leaves the relative contributions of tokens within a response undistorted.
