\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Is GSPO's sequence-level importance ratio still a valid importance sampling estimator?
Question:
The paper motivates GSPO by arguing that the importance sampling weight should match the unit of reward (sequence level). The standard importance sampling estimator for a sequence-level expectation would use the ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}$. However, GSPO uses the length-normalized version $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}\right)^{1/|y|}$. Does this modified ratio still yield an unbiased estimator of the sequence-level expectation? It seems like the normalization changes the expected value of the estimator. Is GSPO sacrificing unbiasedness for lower variance, similar to how PPO's clipping sacrifices unbiasedness for stability? The paper doesn't explicitly discuss whether GSPO's estimator is unbiased.
Answer:
You're right to suspect that the length-normalized ratio is not a valid importance sampling weight. The standard importance sampling estimator for a sequence-level expectation $\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}[f(y)]$ using samples from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ requires the weight $\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}$, which satisfies the normalization condition $\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}\right] = 1$. GSPO's length-normalized ratio $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}\right)^{1/|y|}$ does not satisfy this condition. In general, $\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\left[\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}\right)^{1/|y|}\right] \neq 1$ because the exponent $1/|y|$ varies with $y$ and the mapping $R \mapsto R^{1/|y|}$ is concave (for $|y| > 1$), so Jensen's inequality tells us the expectation deviates from $1$. Therefore, $s_i(\theta)$ yields a biased estimator of the sequence-level expectation, and the paper does not explicitly acknowledge this.

That said, the paper's framing is more nuanced than it might appear at first glance. The paper motivates the sequence-level approach by first arguing that the *unnormalized* ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}$ is the theoretically correct importance weight — this is what "aligning with the basic principle of importance sampling" refers to. The length normalization is then introduced as a practical modification, with the paper noting it is adopted "to reduce the variance and to control $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range." Without normalization, the product of many per-token ratios can explode or vanish for long sequences, and different response lengths would require different clipping ranges. So the normalization is explicitly a variance-reduction technique, acknowledged as such, even if the resulting bias is not discussed.

Your comparison to PPO's clipping is apt. PPO's clipping mechanism sacrifices unbiasedness for stability, and GSPO's length normalization does something analogous at the level of the importance weight itself, before clipping even enters the picture. In fact, GSPO makes two such sacrifices: the length normalization biases the weight, and the clipping (applied at the sequence level) further biases the gradient. The paper seems to treat these as acceptable engineering trade-offs, supported by the empirical results showing superior stability and efficiency.

What the paper's gradient analysis (Section 3.2) reveals is perhaps more important for understanding why GSPO works despite this bias. The gradient of the GSPO objective weights all tokens in a response equally by $s_i(\theta)$, whereas GRPO weights each token by its individual token-level ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, which can vary wildly across tokens. The paper argues these unequal weights are the source of instability in GRPO — they introduce high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences and is amplified by clipping. GSPO's equal weighting eliminates this instability factor entirely. From this perspective, the length-normalized ratio serves less as a importance sampling correction and more as a stable per-sequence scaling factor for the gradient, which happens to be close to $1$ when $\pi_\theta \approx \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ (the on-policy regime) and provides a meaningful clipping signal when the policies diverge.

So to summarize: no, $s_i(\theta)$ is not a valid importance sampling estimator in the strict sense, and yes, GSPO is sacrificing unbiasedness for lower variance and stability, analogous to PPO's clipping. The paper does not explicitly discuss this bias, which is a gap in its theoretical analysis. The paper's actual argument rests on the gradient analysis showing that equal token weighting is more stable than GRPO's unequal weighting, and on empirical evidence that this trade-off is beneficial in practice.
