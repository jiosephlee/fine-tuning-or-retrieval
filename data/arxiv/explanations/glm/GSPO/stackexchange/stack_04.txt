\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does the GSPO-token variant use stop-gradient, and how does it preserve the sequence-level importance ratio?
Question:
The GSPO-token variant defines $s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})]}$, where $\mathrm{sg}$ stops the gradient. The paper says this is numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$ since the second fraction equals 1. But in the gradient derivation, the $s_i(\theta)$ factor reappears without stop-gradient. I'm confused: if $s_i(\theta)$ is detached in the forward pass, how does its gradient flow through in the backward pass? Is the stop-gradient only on the token-level ratio part, while the sequence-level $s_i(\theta)$ retains its gradient? What is the purpose of this construction versus directly using $s_i(\theta) \cdot \widehat{A}_{i,t}$?
Answer:
# The Stop-Gradient Trick in GSPO-token: Decoupling Forward Numerics from Backward Gradient Flow

The short answer is: the stop-gradient construction is a deliberate "rewrite" that keeps the forward-pass value equal to the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ (so clipping is applied uniformly across a response), while restructuring the backward-pass gradient so that each token receives its *own* advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ as its gradient weight. If you used $s_i(\theta)\cdot\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ directly, the per-token advantage customization would be silently destroyed by the chain rule. Let me unpack this.

## What the construction actually does

Recall the definition:
$$s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}\!\left[s_i(\theta)\right]\cdot \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}\!\left[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\right]}.$$

In the **forward pass**, the second factor is literally $1$, so $s_{i,t}(\theta) \equiv s_i(\theta)$ numerically. This is what the paper means by "numerically equal." Consequently, in the objective of Equation (6), the clipping test $\mathrm{clip}(s_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)$ uses the *same* sequence-level ratio for every token in $y_i$. That preserves the central GSPO design principle: clipping, like rewarding, is applied at the sequence level.

In the **backward pass**, autograd treats $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ as a constant. So the only $\theta$-dependent piece of $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is the numerator $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$. Differentiating:
$$\nabla_\theta s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}\!\left[s_i(\theta)\right]\cdot \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}\!\left[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\right]} = s_i(\theta)\cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),$$
where the last equality uses the fact that the detached quantities are numerically equal to their non-detached counterparts. This is exactly the term that produces Equation (7) in the paper:
$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO-token}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_i s_i(\theta)\cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \widehat{A}_{i,t}\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\right].$$

So the answer to "how does $s_i(\theta)$ reappear without stop-gradient in the gradient?" is that it *doesn't* reappear as a differentiable quantity — it appears as the **numerical coefficient** $s_i(\theta)$ multiplying a per-token gradient. The gradient flows only through the token-level log-likelihood; $s_i(\theta)$ merely scales it. This is precisely the same form as the GSPO gradient (Equation 4), just with $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ replacing $\widehat{A}_i$.

## Why not directly use $s_i(\theta)\cdot\widehat{A}_{i,t}$?

This is the crux. Suppose you wrote the objective directly as $\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ without any stop-gradient. Because
$$s_i(\theta)=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t'} \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t'}\mid x,y_{i,<t'})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t'}\mid x,y_{i,<t'})}\right),$$
its gradient couples *every* token to *every* other token:
$$\nabla_\theta s_i(\theta)=s_i(\theta)\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t'}\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t'}\mid x,y_{i,<t'}).$$

Plugging into the objective,
$$\nabla_\theta\!\left[\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i,t}\right]=s_i(\theta)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \widehat{A}_{i,t}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t'}\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t'}\mid x,y_{i,<t'})\right).$$

Notice what happened: the per-token advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ has collapsed into the **sequence mean** $\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \widehat{A}_{i,t}$. Every token's gradient is now weighted by the *average* advantage over the whole response, not by its own $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. The entire point of GSPO-token — "to allow token-wise advantage customization" — would be lost.

The stop-gradient construction surgically fixes this: by detaching $s_i(\theta)$ in the forward pass, you prevent the chain rule from propagating gradients through the full sequence-level log-likelihood, and by attaching gradient only to the per-token numerator $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$, you ensure that the gradient at position $t$ is weighted by $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ (not by the average), while still carrying the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ as an overall scale factor.

## Intuition

Think of $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ as a "split" object: its **value** is the sequence-level ratio (forward), but its **differential** is the token-level one (backward). The stop-gradient is the mechanism that performs this split. It lets the algorithm keep two desirable properties that would otherwise be incompatible:

- **Forward**: a single, low-variance, length-normalized sequence-level importance ratio that drives clipping decisions, matching the unit of reward.
- **Backward**: a per-token gradient signal whose magnitude at each position is modulated by that position's own advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$.

This is consistent with the broader philosophy of the paper — that "the unit of optimization objective should match the unit of reward" — but generalized to the case where rewards (or advantage estimates) may be decomposed at the token level, as in multi-turn RL. When $\widehat{A}_{i,t}=\widehat{A}_i$ for all $t$, GSPO-token reduces exactly to GSPO in objective, clipping, and gradient, as the paper notes; the stop-gradient is what makes this equivalence hold without arithmetic contortions.
