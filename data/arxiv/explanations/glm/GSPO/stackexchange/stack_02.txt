\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why is the length-normalized geometric mean used for the sequence-level importance ratio in GSPO?
Question:
GSPO defines the sequence-level importance ratio as $s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \right)$. The paper says length normalization is adopted to "reduce the variance and to control $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range." But why is the geometric mean (i.e., the $1/|y_i|$ exponent) the right choice here? Wouldn't the raw sequence-level ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}$ be the theoretically correct importance weight for sequence-level importance sampling? Does using the geometric mean break the theoretical guarantees of importance sampling, or is it still a valid importance weight in some sense?
Answer:
The short answer is that the raw sequence-level ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)}$ is indeed the theoretically correct importance weight, but it is practically unusable for long sequences, and the geometric mean is a deliberate engineering compromise that preserves the key benefits of sequence-level optimization while remaining numerically stable.

The problem with the raw ratio is that it is a product of $|y_i|$ token-level ratios. Since each token ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}$ can deviate from 1, their product grows or shrinks exponentially with sequence length. For a response of 1000 tokens, even a tiny per-token drift compounds into a wildly extreme value. As the paper explains, this means "the likelihood changes of a few tokens can result in dramatic fluctuations of the sequence-level importance ratio, and the importance ratios of responses with different lengths will require varying clipping ranges." You would need length-dependent clipping thresholds, and the variance of the importance weights would make gradient estimates extremely noisy.

The geometric mean solves this by taking the $1/|y_i|$ power, which converts the product into an average (in log space). The importance ratio becomes $s_i(\theta) = \exp\!\bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid \cdots)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} \mid \cdots)}\bigr)$, which is the exponential of the average per-token log-ratio. This keeps $s_i(\theta)$ in a range that is roughly independent of sequence length, allowing a single clipping range (the paper uses $[3\times10^{-4}, 4\times10^{-4}]$) to work across all responses regardless of length.

Now, does this break the theoretical guarantees of importance sampling? Strictly speaking, yes — the geometric mean is not a valid importance weight. The correct importance weight for a sequence $y_i$ sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ is exactly $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)}$, and raising it to the $1/|y_i|$ power means the resulting estimator is no longer an unbiased estimate of the expectation under $\pi_\theta$. However, the paper's central argument is that this trade-off is not only acceptable but preferable, for two reasons.

First, the paper argues that GRPO's token-level importance weights are themselves invalid importance weights. Importance sampling requires averaging over many samples from the behavior distribution for the weight to correct the distributional mismatch, but GRPO applies a per-token weight based on a single sample from each next-token distribution. This introduces high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences. So the comparison is not "exact importance sampling vs. approximate" — it is "approximate at the sequence level" vs. "invalid at the token level."

Second, the gradient analysis in the paper (Equation for $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}$) reveals what the geometric mean actually does in practice. The gradient becomes:

$$\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})\right]$$

The key structural property is that $s_i(\theta)$ appears as a single scalar multiplier outside the token gradient sum, meaning all tokens within a response receive equal gradient weight. In contrast, GRPO's gradient has each token weighted by its own individual importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, which can vary across the range $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ and produces unequal, unstable per-token weighting. The geometric mean thus serves as a length-normalized signal of how "off-policy" the entire sequence is, applied uniformly to all tokens — which the paper identifies as the desirable behavior when the reward is sequence-level.

In summary, the geometric mean is not a theoretically exact importance weight, and the paper does not claim it is. It is a practical surrogate chosen to keep the importance ratio in a bounded, length-independent range so that sequence-level clipping and optimization can work reliably. The paper's position is that this pragmatic choice is far more sound than GRPO's token-level weights, which fail to satisfy the requirements of importance sampling in the first place, and that the empirical results — including stable training of large MoE models without routing replay — validate this design.
