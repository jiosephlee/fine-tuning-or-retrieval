\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### How does GSPO resolve the expert-activation volatility problem in MoE models without Routing Replay?
Question:
The paper explains that in MoE models, after a gradient update, roughly 10% of experts activated for the same response change between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, causing token-level importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ to fluctuate drastically. The previous solution (Routing Replay) cached and reused old routing modes. The paper claims GSPO avoids this because it "focuses only on the sequence likelihood and is not sensitive to the individual token likelihood." But $s_i(\theta)$ is computed from token-level likelihoods (via the geometric mean of token ratios). If the experts change for some tokens, won't those token likelihoods change, affecting $s_i(\theta)$? Is the argument that the averaging in $s_i(\theta)$ smooths out the fluctuations from the ~10% of affected tokens?
Answer:
You're right that $s_i(\theta)$ is computed from token-level likelihoods and that expert switching affects some tokens. The resolution has two parts: the averaging does help smooth things out, but the more critical point is how $s_i(\theta)$ enters the **gradient** compared to $w_{i,t}(\theta)$.

First, on the averaging: yes, the geometric mean dilutes the impact of the affected tokens. Since $s_i(\theta) = \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \log w_{i,t}(\theta)\right)$, if 10% of tokens have large log-ratio perturbations of magnitude $\delta$ while the rest are near zero, $s_i(\theta) \approx \exp(0.1\,\delta)$, which is a much milder fluctuation than $e^\delta$ for those individual tokens. The paper explicitly notes this design choice: the length normalization "reduce[s] the variance and control[s] $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range," preventing "the likelihood changes of a few tokens" from causing "dramatic fluctuations of the sequence-level importance ratio."

But the deeper mechanism lies in the gradient analysis in Section 3.2. The key distinction is how the two algorithms weight the per-token gradient contributions $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})$:

- In **GRPO**, each token's gradient is scaled by its own $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, which for the $\sim 10\%$ of tokens with switched experts can be extreme. These tokens then dominate the gradient estimate with disproportionately large (or clipped-to-zero) weights, injecting high-variance noise.
- In **GSPO**, every token in the response shares the **same** scalar $s_i(\theta)$ as its gradient weight. The per-token fluctuations are averaged into one mild, sequence-level scalar, and then applied uniformly.

So it's not just that $s_i(\theta)$ fluctuates less than individual $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ values—though that's true—it's that GSPO structurally prevents any single token's likelihood perturbation from disproportionately influencing the gradient. The paper states this directly: "GSPO weights all the tokens in a response equally, eliminating this instability factor of GRPO."

Regarding the paper's claim that GSPO is "not sensitive to the individual token likelihood": this should be understood in the sense that the optimization signal doesn't depend on per-token likelihood ratios individually, only on their aggregate. Even when expert switching causes certain tokens to have very different likelihoods under $\pi_\theta$ versus $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, the model's overall language modeling capability is preserved, so the sequence likelihood ratio $s_i(\theta)$ remains a stable and meaningful measure of policy drift. The instability in GRPO wasn't really about the sequence likelihood—it was about the noisy, unequal per-token weighting in the gradient, which GSPO eliminates by construction.
