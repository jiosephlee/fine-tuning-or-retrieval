\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### What does "model collapse" mean in the context of GRPO training, and why is it irreversible?
Question:
The paper repeatedly mentions that GRPO can lead to "catastrophic and irreversible model collapse" during RL training of large language models. The authors state that once collapse occurs, resuming training is unavailing even when reverting to a previous checkpoint, tuning hyperparameters, extending generation length, or switching queries. What exactly constitutes "model collapse" here—is it the model generating degenerate/repetitive outputs, losing its reasoning ability, or something else? And what makes it irreversible? If you revert to a previous checkpoint, shouldn't you be able to resume from a known-good state? Is the irreversibility due to the optimizer state (e.g., momentum in Adam) being corrupted, or is there something deeper going on?
Answer:
## Answer

The paper uses "model collapse" to describe a catastrophic failure mode during GRPO training where the model's training dynamics become unstable to the point that the model can no longer be productively trained, losing its ability to continue improving through RL. The paper does not give a precise behavioral description of what a collapsed model outputs (e.g., repetitive text vs. degraded reasoning), but it frames collapse as the endpoint of accumulating gradient noise that renders continued training futile.

The root cause, as the paper identifies, is that GRPO **misapplies importance sampling at the token level**. Importance sampling is designed to correct for distributional mismatch between a target distribution $\pi_{\text{tar}}$ and a behavior distribution $\pi_{\text{beh}}$ by re-weighting samples: $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)} f(z)\right]$. Crucially, the paper emphasizes that this correction is statistically valid only when averaged over many samples ($N \gg 1$). GRPO instead applies the token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}$ at each position $t$, where each weight is computed from a single sample $y_{i,t}$. This fails to perform the intended distribution correction and instead injects high-variance noise into the gradients.

The paper's gradient analysis in Section 3.2 makes the consequence concrete. In GRPO, different tokens within the same response receive unequal gradient weights that can vary over $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ for positive advantages or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ for negative advantages. These unequal weights accumulate over long sequences and interact poorly with the clipping mechanism, producing "unpredictable consequences" as training progresses. In contrast, GSPO weights all tokens in a response equally by using the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}$, which eliminates this source of instability.

Regarding **irreversibility**: the paper does not attribute it to corrupted optimizer state (e.g., Adam momentum). Instead, it frames the problem as a fundamental flaw in the objective function itself—GRPO's objective is "ill-posed." The irreversibility is reported as an empirical observation: "Once the collapse occurs, resuming training is unavailing, even when reverting to a previous checkpoint and meticulously tuning hyperparameters (e.g., the clipping ranges), extending generation length, or switching the RL queries."

The reason reverting to a known-good checkpoint does not help is that the instability is **inherent to the algorithm**, not to a particular transient state of the model or optimizer. If you restore a healthy checkpoint but continue using the same ill-posed token-level objective, the same high-variance noise will re-enter the gradients and drive the model back toward collapse. The paper's evidence for this is that they tried multiple remediation strategies—checkpoint reversion, hyperparameter tuning, generation length extension, and query set changes—all of which failed. The solution is not to patch around the symptoms but to redesign the objective, which is exactly what GSPO does by moving importance weighting and clipping to the sequence level.

It is worth noting that the paper does not fully probe the fine-grained mechanism of irreversibility (e.g., whether Adam momentum contributes). It presents the irreversibility as an empirical finding and addresses it by proposing an algorithm that avoids the instability in the first place. The broader lesson connects to a principle the paper states explicitly: **the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward**. Since rewards are assigned to entire sequences, applying off-policy correction at the token level is fundamentally mismatched, and no amount of checkpoint management can fix an objective whose gradients are systematically noisy by construction.
