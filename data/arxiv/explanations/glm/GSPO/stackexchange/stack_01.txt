\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does the token-level importance ratio in GRPO fail as a valid importance sampling weight?
Question:
In the GSPO paper, the authors argue that GRPO's token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$ is a misapplication of importance sampling. They state that because this weight is based on a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from each next-token distribution, it "fails to perform the intended distribution-correction role." However, importance sampling can technically be applied with any number of samples, including $N=1$. So what exactly makes the token-level ratio invalid? Is the issue that the expectation being estimated is over sequences, not tokens? Or is it about variance accumulation across the token-level ratios within a sequence? I would appreciate a more precise explanation of why the single-sample argument invalidates the token-level importance weight.
Answer:
The core issue is not simply that $N=1$ makes importance sampling impractical — as you note, a single-sample importance weight is still unbiased in principle. The problem is more structural: the token-level ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is being used as an importance weight for the **wrong distribution**. It corrects for the mismatch between next-token distributions, but the expectation being estimated in RL is over **sequences**, not individual tokens. This mismatch, combined with the single-sample nature of each token-level ratio, is what invalidates the weight.

To see this clearly, consider what proper importance sampling requires in this context. The RL objective involves an expectation over sequences $y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot | x)$, and when using samples from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, the correct importance weight is the sequence-level ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y | x)}$. By the chain rule of autoregressive generation, this factorizes as $\prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t | x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t | x, y_{<t})} = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} w_{i,t}(\theta)$. The crucial point is that this **product** is the valid importance weight for the sequence, not the individual factors used separately.

GRPO does not use this product. Instead, it applies each factor $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ independently as a per-token multiplier on the advantage $\widehat{A}_i$. The result is that each token-level ratio is acting as an importance weight for the next-token distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x, y_{i,<t})$, but no expectation is being taken over that distribution — the advantage is a sequence-level quantity shared by all tokens. With a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from each next-token distribution, the ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is simply a scalar that depends on the particular token that happened to be sampled. It does not average over multiple draws from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x, y_{i,<t})$, so it never performs the distribution-correction role that importance sampling requires. In contrast, at the sequence level, each response $y_i$ is one sample from the sequence distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)$, and the group of $G$ responses provides the multiple samples needed for the sequence-level importance weight to function as intended.

The practical consequence becomes visible in the gradient analysis. The GRPO gradient weights each token's gradient $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ by a **different** factor $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, which can fluctuate across the range $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ depending on the advantage sign. These per-token weights are neither equal nor controlled — they inject high-variance noise into the gradient estimate that accumulates across long sequences. The GSPO gradient, by contrast, weights all tokens in a response by the same sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta) = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log w_{i,t}(\theta)\right)$, which is the geometric mean of the token-level ratios. This averaging stabilizes the weight and ensures every token in the sequence receives a consistent correction signal that matches the sequence-level reward.

So to directly answer your question: the primary issue is the **unit mismatch** — the expectation is over sequences, but the importance correction is applied at the token level, where it corresponds to the wrong distribution. The single-sample argument reinforces this: even if you wanted to do importance sampling at the token level (which would be misdirected), a single sample from each next-token distribution cannot perform meaningful distribution correction. The variance accumulation you mention is the downstream symptom: because the per-token ratios are noisy, uncontrolled multipliers rather than a coherent importance weight, their independent application across tokens produces unstable gradient estimates that compound over long responses and are further distorted by the clipping mechanism.
