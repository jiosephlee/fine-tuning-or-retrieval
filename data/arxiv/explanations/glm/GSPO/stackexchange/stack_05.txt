\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does GSPO clip far more tokens than GRPO but still achieve better training efficiency?
Question:
The paper reports a counter-intuitive finding: GSPO clips two orders of magnitude more tokens than GRPO (Figure 2), yet achieves higher training efficiency. The authors interpret this as evidence that GRPO's token-level gradient estimates are "inherently noisy and inefficient." But if GSPO is discarding so many more samples from gradient estimation, shouldn't it have less data to learn from? Is the argument that the clipped samples in GSPO are genuinely harmful (off-policy), so removing them is beneficial, while in GRPO the per-token clipping doesn't effectively remove harmful samples? Also, how can clipping at the sequence level remove more tokens than clipping at the token level—shouldn't token-level clipping be more fine-grained and thus clip fewer tokens?
Answer:
The counter-intuitive observation—that GSPO clips far more tokens yet learns more efficiently—makes sense once you carefully distinguish between the *granularity* of clipping and the *rate* of clipping, and understand why the clipped samples are harmful in each algorithm.

**Why sequence-level clipping clips more tokens than token-level clipping**

Token-level clipping is more fine-grained in the sense that each token is independently evaluated against the clipping range. However, "fine-grained" does not imply "clips fewer tokens." The actual clipping rate depends on two factors: the clipping range and the distribution of the importance ratio.

In GRPO, each token's importance ratio is $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}$, and the clipping range is relatively wide (the paper uses $[1-0.2, 1+0.27]$). After a single gradient update, most individual token probabilities change only slightly, so $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ typically stays within this wide range and few tokens are clipped.

In GSPO, the sequence-level ratio is $s_i(\theta) = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log w_{i,t}(\theta)\right)$, which is the geometric mean of all per-token ratios. The clipping range is dramatically tighter (the paper uses $[1-3\times10^{-4}, 1+4\times10^{-4}]$). Even though each individual $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ may stay well within GRPO's wide range, small systematic shifts across many tokens compound in the geometric mean. A per-token shift of, say, $+0.01$ in $\log w_{i,t}$ would leave each token unclipped under GRPO, but the averaged log-ratio $\bar{\delta} = 0.01$ would push $s_i(\theta)$ far outside GSPO's tight range. Crucially, when GSPO clips a sequence, *every token in that sequence* is clipped, since they all share the same $s_i(\theta)$. So the combination of a tighter range and whole-sequence clipping causes GSPO to clip orders of magnitude more tokens.

**Why clipping more tokens improves efficiency**

The key argument is indeed that GSPO's clipped samples are genuinely harmful because they are off-policy at the *sequence level*—the level at which rewards are assigned and at which importance sampling is actually valid. The paper's motivation (Section 3) emphasizes that importance sampling requires averaging over many samples to correct distributional mismatch, but GRPO applies the importance weight $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}$ to a *single* token draw from each next-token distribution. This single-sample weight cannot perform the intended distribution correction; instead, it injects high-variance noise into the gradient.

The gradient analysis in Section 3.2 makes this precise. In GRPO, each token's log-likelihood gradient is weighted by its own importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, and these unequal weights vary across $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ depending on the sign of the advantage. These per-token weight discrepancies introduce noise that accumulates over long sequences. GSPO, by contrast, weights all tokens in a response equally with the shared factor $s_i(\theta)$, eliminating this source of instability entirely.

So the answer to your question is yes: in GSPO, the clipped sequences are genuinely off-policy at the meaningful level (the sequence), and removing their gradient contributions prevents harmful updates. In GRPO, per-token clipping is ineffective at removing harmful samples because individual token ratios can look fine even when the sequence as a whole has drifted off-policy. The noise from GRPO's per-token importance weights persists regardless of whether individual tokens are clipped, because the problem is structural—it comes from applying single-sample importance weights at the token level, not from individual tokens being too far off-policy. GSPO's aggressive sequence-level clipping effectively removes entire off-policy sequences from gradient estimation, and the remaining (unclipped) sequences provide a cleaner, lower-variance learning signal. Having fewer but higher-quality gradient estimates turns out to be more efficient than having more but noisier ones.
