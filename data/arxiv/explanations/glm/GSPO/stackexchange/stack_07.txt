\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why are the clipping ranges in GSPO (3e-4, 4e-4) so much smaller than in GRPO (0.2, 0.27)?
Question:
The paper mentions that GSPO uses clipping ranges of $[1-3\times10^{-4}, 1+4\times10^{-4}]$ while GRPO uses $[0.8, 1.27]$. The paper briefly notes that "the clipping ranges in GSPO and in previous algorithms typically differ in order of magnitude due to the distinct definitions of importance ratios." But this is a huge difference—GRPO allows the ratio to deviate by ~20-27% while GSPO allows only ~0.03-0.04%. Why is such a tiny clipping range sufficient for GSPO? Is it because $s_i(\theta)$, being a geometric mean of token ratios, naturally has much lower variance than individual token ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$? And are the asymmetric clipping ranges (different left and right bounds) motivated by something specific?
Answer:
The dramatic difference in clipping ranges stems directly from the mathematical relationship between the two algorithms' importance ratios. Your intuition is correct: $s_i(\theta)$, being a length-normalized geometric mean of token ratios, has far lower variance than individual token ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, which both necessitates and justifies a much tighter clipping range.

To see why, consider how $s_i(\theta)$ is constructed:

$$s_i(\theta) = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}\right) = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log w_{i,t}(\theta)\right)$$

This is the exponential of the arithmetic mean of the log token ratios. If we let $\ell_t = \log w_{i,t}(\theta)$ and $\bar{\ell} = \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \ell_t$, then $s_i(\theta) = e^{\bar{\ell}}$. When the policy has changed only slightly after a few gradient updates, the mean of $\ell_t$ is close to zero, and the variance of the average $\bar{\ell}$ shrinks as $\sigma^2/|y_i|$ (assuming approximate independence of per-token log-ratios). For a response of length $|y_i| = 1000$, this is a variance reduction factor of roughly $1000$, or a standard deviation reduction of $\sqrt{1000} \approx 31.6$.

This alone does not fully account for the $\sim 667\times$ difference between $0.2$ and $3\times10^{-4}$. The remaining gap comes from the fact that GRPO's wide clipping range is not calibrated to the typical change in token probabilities after one gradient step — individual token log-probabilities typically change by far less than 20%. Instead, the wide range in GRPO is necessary because the per-token importance weights $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ are inherently noisy and unreliable, as the paper argues in Section 3. The paper identifies that GRPO's token-level importance weights "fail to perform the intended distribution-correction role" and instead "introduce high-variance noise into the training gradients." A wide clipping range is essentially a band-aid that prevents excessive clipping due to this noise. In GSPO, $s_i(\theta)$ provides a reliable, low-variance measure of sequence-level drift, so a tight range accurately identifies genuinely off-policy sequences. The paper explicitly notes that "we adopt length normalization in $s_i(\theta)$ to reduce the variance and to control $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range."

This is also consistent with the paper's empirical observation in Section 5.2 that GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens than GRPO — by two orders of magnitude — yet achieves superior training efficiency. The tight clipping range is aggressive, but the clipped sequences were contributing noise rather than useful gradient signal. Because GSPO clips at the sequence level, when a sequence is deemed off-policy it is excluded entirely, and the remaining non-clipped sequences provide a cleaner learning signal. In GRPO, the wide range means very few tokens are clipped, but the unclipped per-token weights $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ still introduce uneven, high-variance weighting into the gradient (as shown in the gradient analysis of Section 4.2, where GRPO weights each token's gradient by its own $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ while GSPO weights all tokens equally by $s_i(\theta)$).

Regarding the asymmetric clipping ranges, the paper does not provide an explicit theoretical justification, but the pattern is consistent across both algorithms: GSPO uses $3\times10^{-4}$ below and $4\times10^{-4}$ above (ratio $\approx 1.33$), while GRPO uses $0.2$ below and $0.27$ above (ratio $= 1.35$). A natural explanation is that the asymmetry targets roughly symmetric clipping in log space. Since both $s_i(\theta)$ and $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ are positive ratios, symmetric bounds in log space — i.e., $-\log(1 - \epsilon_{\text{left}}) \approx \log(1 + \epsilon_{\text{right}})$ — translate to slightly asymmetric bounds in the original space, with $\epsilon_{\text{right}} > \epsilon_{\text{left}}$. For GSPO, $-\log(1 - 3\times10^{-4}) \approx 3\times10^{-4}$ and $\log(1 + 4\times10^{-4}) \approx 4\times10^{-4}$, which are close in magnitude. The similar asymmetry ratios across both algorithms suggest a shared design principle, likely tuned empirically as the paper states the ranges were "carefully tuned."
