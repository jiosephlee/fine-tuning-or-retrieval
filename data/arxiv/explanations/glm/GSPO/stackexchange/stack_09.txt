\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### How does GSPO's sequence-level approach help with precision discrepancies between training and inference engines?
Question:
In Section 5.4, the paper claims that GSPO can directly use likelihoods from the inference engine (e.g., SGLang, vLLM) without recomputing them in the training engine (e.g., Megatron), because sequence-level likelihoods are "more tolerant of precision discrepancies." Why would a sequence-level likelihood $\pi_\theta(y|x) = \prod_t \pi_\theta(y_t|x,y_{<t})$ be more robust to floating-point precision differences than individual token likelihoods? Since the sequence likelihood is a product of token likelihoods, wouldn't precision errors compound? Or is the argument that the geometric mean in $s_i(\theta)$ averages out the precision noise? What specific precision discrepancies exist between training and inference engines that cause problems at the token level but not the sequence level?
Answer:
# How GSPO's Sequence-Level Approach Handles Precision Discrepancies

The key insight lies in understanding that GSPO's importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is not the raw product of token likelihoods but rather a **geometric mean** — and this distinction is exactly what prevents precision errors from compounding.

## The Geometric Mean vs. the Raw Product

You're right that the sequence likelihood is a product of token likelihoods, and that precision errors in a raw product would compound. But GSPO doesn't use the raw product. Instead, it defines:

$$s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}\right)$$

The critical operation is the $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ normalization. In log-space, this converts what would be a **sum** of per-token log-ratios (where errors accumulate) into an **average** (where errors cancel). If the per-token precision discrepancy between the inference and training engines introduces an error $\delta_t$ in each token's log-probability, the error in $\log s_i(\theta)$ becomes $\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \delta_t$ rather than $\sum_t \delta_t$. Assuming these errors are roughly zero-mean — which is reasonable since floating-point rounding errors from different kernel implementations (different attention variants, different reduction orders, BF16 vs FP32 accumulation) are not systematically biased in one direction — the average converges toward zero as sequence length grows, with variance shrinking as $\sigma^2/|y_i|$ by the law of large numbers. Without the geometric mean normalization, the raw product's log-error would be $\sum_t \delta_t$ with variance growing linearly in $|y_i|$, which is precisely the compounding you suspected — but the normalization averts this.

## Why Token-Level Ratios Are Fragile

In GRPO, the importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}$ is computed per token, mixing precision from two engines at every position. Each token's ratio independently carries the full, unaveraged precision noise $\delta_t$. This matters in two ways:

**Clipping decisions become unreliable.** GRPO clips each token individually based on whether $w_{i,t}$ falls within $[1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]$. With per-token precision noise, tokens near the boundary may be incorrectly clipped or passed through based on noise rather than genuine policy drift. Since the paper observes that clipping fractions differ by two orders of magnitude between GSPO and GRPO (Section 5.3), these noisy per-token clipping decisions represent a substantial source of wasted or misdirected gradient signal.

**Gradients are differentially weighted by noise.** The GRPO gradient weights each token's gradient by its own noisy $w_{i,t}(\theta)$:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO} \propto \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t w_{i,t}(\theta)\, \widehat{A}_i\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$$

Different tokens receive different noise-contaminated weights, so the gradient direction itself is corrupted — some tokens are upweighted, others downweighted, based on nothing more than floating-point artifacts.

## Why GSPO Is Robust

GSPO's gradient tells a fundamentally different story:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} \propto s_i(\theta) \cdot \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$$

Here, $s_i(\theta)$ is a **single scalar** that uniformly weights all token gradients in the response. Even if $s_i(\theta)$ carries a small residual bias from precision discrepancies, this bias uniformly scales the entire sequence's gradient — it changes the magnitude but not the direction of the update. A uniform scaling is far more benign than per-token differential weighting, because the gradient direction (which determines which directions in parameter space the model moves) remains clean. Moreover, the gradient itself only involves $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$, which is computed entirely within the training engine — the old policy likelihoods from the inference engine only enter through the scalar $s_i(\theta)$, which has been denoised by averaging.

## The Practical Implication

The specific discrepancies between engines like Megatron and SGLang/vLLM arise from different floating-point precisions (BF16 vs FP32 accumulation), different kernel implementations (e.g., FlashAttention variants with different numerical reduction orders), different softmax computations, and — particularly relevant for MoE models — different expert routing computations. At the token level, these produce per-token log-probability differences that are individually small but, when used to form independent per-token importance ratios, inject noise directly into the gradient. At the sequence level, the geometric mean averages these differences out, making it feasible to use the inference engine's likelihoods directly and skip the expensive recomputation in the training engine — a significant infrastructure simplification, especially for multi-turn RL and training-inference disaggregated setups where recomputation is costly.
