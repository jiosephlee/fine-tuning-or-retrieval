\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 3: The Instability Problem: Why GRPO's Objective is Ill-Posed

# Off-Policy Learning in RL: Mini-Batch Gradient Updates and Distributional Mismatch

Reinforcement learning for large language models operates under a set of practical constraints that inevitably introduce a gap between the policy that generates responses and the policy being optimized. To understand this gap, we must first consider the computational demands of modern RL training. As models grow larger—both in parameter count and in architectural sparsity, as seen in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models—and as the responses they generate become longer to accommodate deeper reasoning, a single rollout batch must be very large to maximize hardware utilization. This means that a substantial volume of text is generated from the model before any gradient updates occur.

To improve sample efficiency, the standard practice is to partition this large batch of rollout data into multiple mini-batches, each of which is used for a separate gradient update. The idea is straightforward: rather than generating fresh responses for every single gradient step—an expensive operation—we reuse the same batch of generated responses across multiple updates. However, this seemingly benign efficiency measure introduces a fundamental complication. After the first gradient update, the model parameters have changed, meaning the current policy $\pi_\theta$ no longer matches the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ that was used to generate the responses. The training data is now "off-policy": the responses $y$ in the batch were sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, but the gradients are being computed with respect to $\pi_\theta$.

This distributional mismatch is the root cause of several challenges in RL training. The clipping mechanism in PPO and GRPO is designed precisely to manage this mismatch: it prevents samples that have become too far "off-policy"—that is, samples whose likelihood under $\pi_\theta$ differs too much from their likelihood under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$—from contributing to gradient estimation. The clipping mechanism constrains the importance ratio, which measures how much the policy has shifted, to a bounded range $[1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon]$. When the ratio falls outside this range, the corresponding sample's contribution is clipped, preventing large, destabilizing updates. While this mechanism is well-intentioned, we will see that in GRPO, the very quantity being clipped is itself problematic, rendering the clipping mechanism not just insufficient but actively harmful.

# The Principle of Importance Sampling: Correct Usage and Requirements

To diagnose the flaw in GRPO, we must revisit the foundational technique that motivates the use of importance weights in off-policy reinforcement learning: importance sampling. Importance sampling is a statistical method for estimating properties of a particular distribution using samples drawn from a different distribution. In the context of RL, it allows us to estimate the expected value of some function under the current (target) policy using data generated by an older (behavior) policy.

The core idea is elegant. Suppose we wish to compute the expectation of a function $f$ under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$, but we only have samples drawn from a different distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$. Direct evaluation of $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)]$ is impossible because we cannot sample from $\pi_\text{tar}$. However, we can rewrite this expectation using the samples from $\pi_\text{beh}$ by reweighting each sample by the ratio of the two densities:

$$\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}}\left[\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)} f(z)\right].$$

The weight $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ corrects for the distributional mismatch: samples that are more likely under the target distribution than under the behavior distribution receive a higher weight, and vice versa. In the RL setting, $\pi_\text{tar}$ corresponds to the current policy $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_\text{beh}$ corresponds to the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$.

However, this formulation carries a critical requirement that is often overlooked: the correction is only valid in expectation, and the expectation must be approximated by averaging over multiple samples. The importance weight $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ effectively corrects for the distributional mismatch only when we average over many samples ($N \gg 1$) from the behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$. With a single sample, the importance weight is just a single noisy scalar that does not perform any meaningful distribution correction. This is because the entire point of importance sampling is that, in aggregate, the reweighted samples reconstruct the target distribution. A single reweighted sample cannot achieve this reconstruction—it is merely one draw from a noisy distribution.

# Importance Sampling Formulation: Target vs. Behavior Distributions and the Role of Multiple Samples

Let us elaborate on why multiple samples are essential for importance sampling to function correctly. Consider the unbiasedness property: a single sample $z$ drawn from $\pi_\text{beh}$ and reweighted by $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ does give an unbiased estimate of $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)]$. That is, in expectation over the sampling process, the reweighted estimate is correct. However, unbiasedness alone is not sufficient for practical utility. The variance of the importance sampling estimator depends critically on the number of samples and the discrepancy between the target and behavior distributions.

When the target and behavior distributions are close, the importance weights are close to 1, and the variance of the estimator is low even with a modest number of samples. But when the distributions diverge—as they do after multiple gradient updates in mini-batch training—the importance weights can become very large for some samples and very small for others, leading to high variance. This high variance is the fundamental challenge of importance sampling, and it is mitigated only by averaging over many samples.

The role of multiple samples is thus twofold. First, averaging reduces the variance of the estimator: the variance of the mean of $N$ independent samples decreases as $\frac{1}{N}$. Second, and more fundamentally, the distribution-correction property of importance sampling is an aggregate property. It is only when we have many samples from $\pi_\text{beh}$, each appropriately reweighted, that the empirical distribution of the reweighted samples approximates $\pi_\text{tar}$. A single sample, no matter how it is reweighted, cannot approximate a distribution. This distinction between the correctness of an individual weight and the correctness of distribution correction in aggregate is the crux of the problem we will identify in GRPO.

# GRPO's Misapplication: Token-Level Importance Weights from Single Samples

With the principles of importance sampling established, we can now pinpoint the fundamental flaw in GRPO's design. Recall that in GRPO, the importance ratio is defined at the token level:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}.$$

This ratio is applied at each token position $t$ in the response $y_i$. The intention is to correct for the off-policy mismatch between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ at the granularity of individual tokens.

However, consider what this token-level importance weight actually represents. At each position $t$, the model has a next-token distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x, y_{i,<t})$ under the old policy. From this distribution, exactly one token $y_{i,t}$ was sampled. The importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is then computed as the ratio of the probability of that single sampled token under the current policy to its probability under the old policy. This is an importance weight derived from a single sample—specifically, a single sample from the next-token distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x, y_{i,<t})$.

As we established in the previous sections, importance sampling requires averaging over multiple samples ($N \gg 1$) from the behavior distribution for the importance weight to perform its intended role of distribution correction. Here, there is only one sample per token position. The token-level importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is therefore not performing distribution correction at all. It is a single noisy scalar that attempts to reweight a single observation, but it cannot reconstruct or correct for the full next-token distribution from which the token was drawn.

# Why Token-Level Importance Weights Fail to Perform Distribution Correction

To understand why this failure matters, consider what distribution correction should achieve in the RL context. When we compute gradients using off-policy data, the ideal correction would ensure that the gradient estimate is the same as if we had sampled from $\pi_\theta$ directly. At the token level, this would mean that the empirical distribution of reweighted tokens at each position approximates the next-token distribution under $\pi_\theta$. But with only one token sampled at each position, there is nothing to approximate a distribution from. The single token $y_{i,t}$ is either kept (with some weight) or not, and this binary outcome cannot capture the full distributional structure of $\pi_\theta(\cdot | x, y_{i,<t})$.

The consequence of this failure is that the token-level importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ does not reduce the bias introduced by off-policy data. Instead, it introduces additional noise. Each token's gradient contribution is scaled by a weight that is a single noisy draw, and this weight can vary arbitrarily across tokens within the same response. Some tokens may receive very large weights (if they happen to be more likely under $\pi_\theta$ than under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$), while others may receive very small weights. This variability is not the structured, distribution-correcting variability of proper importance sampling—it is unstructured noise arising from the mismatch between the single-sample setting and the multi-sample requirement of importance sampling.

Furthermore, the gradient of the GRPO objective reveals the mechanism by which this noise enters the training. The gradient of GRPO is:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right].$$

Here, each token's gradient $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ is weighted by the token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. Because these weights are unequal across tokens—they can vary within the range $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ for positive advantages $\widehat{A}_i > 0$, or within $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ for negative advantages $\widehat{A}_i < 0$—different tokens in the same response receive different gradient scaling. These unequal weights are not negligible, and as we will see, their effects compound over the course of training.

# Accumulation of High-Variance Noise Over Long Sequences

The noise introduced by token-level importance weights is not merely a local issue at each token position; it accumulates over the length of a response. In modern RL training of language models, particularly for tasks involving sophisticated reasoning such as competition-level mathematics and programming, responses can be very long. The GRPO objective averages the token-level contributions over all tokens in a response, dividing by $|y_i|$. While this averaging might seem to mitigate the noise, the problem is that the noise is not independent across tokens—it is structured by the sequential nature of autoregressive generation.

As the response length $|y_i|$ increases, more token-level importance weights are involved in the gradient estimate, each contributing its own noise. The cumulative effect of these noisy weights grows with the sequence length. A few tokens with extreme importance ratios can disproportionately influence the gradient, even when averaged over many tokens. This is particularly problematic because the importance ratios are products of sequential dependencies: the probability of a token at position $t$ depends on all preceding tokens, and errors in the importance ratio at one position propagate to subsequent positions through the autoregressive structure.

The situation is further aggravated in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. In MoE models, the experts activated for a given token can change significantly after a gradient update. Empirically, with the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, after each RL gradient update, roughly 10% of the experts activated under the new policy $\pi_\theta$ differ from those activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ for the same rollout sample. This phenomenon, which becomes more prominent in deeper MoE models, causes the token-level importance ratios to fluctuate even more drastically, further invalidating them as reliable correction weights. The high-variance noise from token-level importance weights thus accumulates not only over sequence length but also over the depth and complexity of the model architecture.

# How the Clipping Mechanism Amplifies the Instability

The clipping mechanism, designed to prevent overly off-policy samples from destabilizing training, paradoxically amplifies the very instability it is meant to control. To understand this, consider how clipping interacts with the noisy token-level importance weights in GRPO.

The clipping mechanism in GRPO clips each token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ to the range $[1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]$. When a token's importance ratio exceeds this range, its contribution to the objective is replaced by the clipped version. The intention is to prevent tokens with extreme importance ratios from dominating the gradient. However, because the token-level importance ratios are already noisy—being derived from single samples rather than proper importance sampling—the clipping mechanism does not remove the noise. Instead, it truncates the noisy weights, creating a biased and irregular pattern of which tokens contribute to the gradient and which do not.

The problem is that clipping creates an asymmetry. For tokens with positive advantages ($\widehat{A}_i > 0$), the importance ratio is clipped to the range $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$, meaning that tokens with ratios above $1+\varepsilon$ have their contributions capped. For tokens with negative advantages ($\widehat{A}_i < 0$), the importance ratio is clipped to the range $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$, meaning that tokens with ratios below $1-\varepsilon$ have their contributions bounded from below. This asymmetric clipping, applied to already-noisy weights, creates a systematic bias in the gradient estimate. Some tokens that should contribute are silenced, while others that should be downweighted are amplified, and the pattern of which tokens are affected changes unpredictably across training steps.

This amplification effect is particularly insidious because it is gradual. The noise from clipping does not cause immediate divergence; rather, it progressively corrupts the gradient estimates over many training steps. Each mini-batch update introduces a small amount of biased noise, and this noise compounds as training proceeds. The clipping mechanism, rather than serving as a stabilizer, becomes a source of systematic distortion that, combined with the accumulation of noise over long sequences, progressively degrades the quality of the policy.

# Empirical Observations of Catastrophic and Irreversible Model Collapse

The theoretical analysis above is not merely speculative—it is confirmed by extensive empirical observations. When training large language models with GRPO, particularly on long-response tasks, the training process frequently undergoes catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. This phenomenon has been observed in practice, including during the development of models such as Qwen3 and MiniMax-M1.

The collapse manifests as a sudden and severe degradation of the model's capabilities. The model may stop generating coherent text, lose its reasoning ability, or produce degenerate outputs. What makes this collapse particularly troubling is its irreversibility. Once the collapse occurs, standard recovery strategies are unavailing. Reverting to a previous checkpoint and meticulously tuning hyperparameters—such as adjusting the clipping ranges—does not restore the model. Extending the generation length does not help. Switching the RL queries to a different set of problems does not reverse the damage. The model appears to have entered a region of parameter space from which it cannot recover through any practical intervention.

This irreversibility is consistent with the theoretical analysis. The noise from token-level importance weights, amplified by clipping and accumulated over long sequences, progressively corrupts the model's representations. Once the corruption reaches a critical threshold, the model's next-token distributions become so distorted that the importance ratios themselves become meaningless, creating a feedback loop of degradation. The model has effectively been driven into a region where the off-policy correction mechanism, which was supposed to stabilize training, has become the very source of instability.

The observation that this collapse is more prevalent with larger models, longer responses, and MoE architectures further supports the diagnosis. Larger models have more parameters that can be corrupted by noisy gradients. Longer responses accumulate more noise. MoE models introduce additional volatility in the importance ratios through expert-activation changes. All of these factors scale with the very dimensions along which modern language models are being developed, making the instability problem not just an inconvenience but a fundamental barrier to scaling RL training.

# The Core Principle: Matching the Unit of Optimization to the Unit of Reward

The analysis of GRPO's instability points to a fundamental principle for the design of RL algorithms for language models: the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of reward. In language model RL, rewards are granted to entire sequences, not individual tokens. A verifier $r$ evaluates a complete query-response pair $(x, y)$ and produces a scalar reward $r(x, y) \in [0, 1]$. The advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ computed in GRPO is a sequence-level quantity, derived from the relative ranking of rewards within a group of responses to the same query. All tokens in a response share the same advantage value.

Despite this sequence-level rewarding, GRPO applies off-policy correction at the token level. This mismatch between the unit of reward (sequences) and the unit of correction (tokens) is the root cause of the instability. The token-level importance weights attempt to correct for distributional mismatch at a granularity that is finer than the granularity at which rewards—and therefore learning signals—are defined. This mismatch is not merely a technical inconvenience; it violates the basic principle of importance sampling, which requires that the correction be applied at the same level as the function being estimated.

This principle—that the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward—motivates the exploration of sequence-level optimization as an alternative to token-level optimization. If the reward is granted to the entire sequence, then the off-policy correction should also be applied at the sequence level. The sequence-level importance weight $\frac{\pi_\theta(y | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y | x)}$ has a clear theoretical meaning: it reflects how far the response $y$ sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)$ deviates from the distribution $\pi_\theta(\cdot | x)$. This is a well-defined quantity that naturally aligns with the sequence-level reward and can serve as a meaningful indicator for the clipping mechanism.

By shifting the level of optimization from tokens to sequences, we can ensure that the importance sampling correction is applied where it is meaningful—where the reward signal lives—and avoid the high-variance noise that arises from misapplying importance weights at the token level. This insight forms the foundation for the Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) algorithm, which we will introduce in the next chapter. The key realization is that the instability of GRPO is not a bug to be patched with better hyperparameters or auxiliary techniques; it is a fundamental consequence of an ill-posed objective that violates the basic principles of importance sampling and the alignment between reward and optimization.
