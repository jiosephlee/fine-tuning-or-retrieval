\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 7: GSPO for Mixture-of-Experts Models

# Background on Mixture-of-Experts Models and Sparse Activation

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models represent a powerful architectural paradigm in large language model design, offering a pathway to scale model parameters dramatically while keeping computational costs manageable. In a traditional dense model, every token passes through all parameters of the network during both forward and backward passes. In contrast, an MoE model employs a routing mechanism that directs each token to only a subset of "experts"—specialized sub-networks within the model. This property is known as sparse activation: only a fraction of the model's total parameters are active for any given token at any given time. The result is that the model can have a very large total parameter count (enabling greater expressive capacity) while maintaining a comparatively modest active parameter count during inference and training (keeping computational cost feasible).

The sparse activation nature of MoE models, while advantageous for scaling, introduces unique stability challenges when these models are trained with reinforcement learning. The paper highlights this issue in the context of the Qwen3-30B-A3B model, a 48-layer MoE architecture. In such models, the routing decisions—determining which experts process which tokens—are learned parameters that shift as the model is updated. This dynamic routing behavior becomes a source of instability during RL training, as we shall explore in detail throughout this chapter. The fundamental tension is that RL training relies on comparing the behavior of the current policy to that of a previous policy, but when the routing structure itself changes between policy versions, the comparison becomes unreliable. This problem is specific to MoE models and does not arise in dense models, where the network topology is fixed and every token always passes through the same parameters.

# Expert-Activation Volatility: How Gradient Updates Change Activated Experts

The core stability challenge in MoE RL training is what the paper terms "expert-activation volatility." To understand this phenomenon, consider how RL training proceeds in practice. A large batch of rollout data is generated using the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. During these rollouts, each token in each response is routed to a specific set of experts by the MoE routing mechanism. The routing decisions depend on the model parameters at the time of generation. After the rollout data is collected, gradient updates are performed to optimize the policy, yielding a new policy $\pi_{\theta}$. The problem arises because, after even a single gradient update, the routing decisions can change substantially. That is, when the same response is processed under the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$, different experts may be activated for the same tokens compared to what was activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$.

This expert-activation volatility is a direct consequence of the learned routing mechanism in MoE models. The router—which determines which experts handle each token—is part of the model parameters that get updated during gradient steps. Even a small change in the router's parameters can alter the routing decisions for a non-trivial fraction of tokens. Since the router makes decisions at every layer for every token, the compounding effect across layers can be significant. The paper notes that this phenomenon becomes more prominent in deeper MoE models, where the cumulative effect of routing changes across many layers amplifies the overall volatility. This creates a fundamental mismatch: the rollout data was generated with one set of expert activations, but the current policy computes likelihoods using a potentially different set of expert activations for the same tokens.

# Quantifying Expert Volatility: Approximately 10% Expert Change Per Update in Qwen3-30B-A3B

The paper provides a concrete quantification of the expert-activation volatility problem using the Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, which has 48 layers. The authors measured how many experts change activation between the old policy and the new policy for the same rollout sample after each RL gradient update. The finding is striking: approximately 10% of the experts activated under the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$ are different from those activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ for the same response.

To put this in perspective, this means that for every ten expert activations involved in processing a given token across the layers of the model, roughly one of them changes after a single gradient update. This is not a negligible perturbation. The routing decisions are integral to how the model computes the probability of each token, and when 10% of the expert assignments change, the token-level probability computations are significantly altered. This level of volatility occurs after just one gradient update; over multiple mini-batch updates within a single training step, the cumulative divergence could be even larger. The paper observes that this phenomenon becomes more prominent in deeper MoE models, suggesting that the problem would worsen as models are scaled to have more layers. This quantitative finding underscores the severity of the expert-activation volatility problem and motivates the need for a solution.

# How Expert Volatility Invalidates Token-Level Importance Ratios in GRPO

The expert-activation volatility problem has a direct and detrimental impact on the GRPO algorithm's token-level importance ratios. Recall that in GRPO, the importance ratio for each token is defined as:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}.$$

This ratio is meant to correct for the distributional mismatch between the old policy (from which responses were sampled) and the current policy (which is being optimized). The effectiveness of this correction depends on the stability and meaningfulness of the ratio. However, when the experts activated for token $y_{i,t}$ differ between $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, the numerator and denominator of this ratio are computed by fundamentally different sub-networks. The ratio then reflects not only the change in policy parameters but also the change in network topology as experienced by that token.

As the paper discusses in its motivation and gradient analysis sections, the token-level importance weight in GRPO is already problematic because it is based on a single sample from each next-token distribution, introducing high-variance noise into the training gradients. The expert-activation volatility of MoE models exacerbates this problem dramatically. The token-level importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ fluctuate drastically because the underlying computation path changes between the old and new policies. This fluctuation further invalidates the importance ratios, rendering them unreliable as correction factors for off-policy learning. The consequence is that the gradient estimates in GRPO become noisy and unstable, hindering normal convergence of RL training. In the worst cases, this instability can lead to catastrophic and irreversible model collapse, where the model's performance degrades precipitously and cannot be recovered even by reverting to earlier checkpoints or adjusting hyperparameters.

# Routing Replay: Caching and Replaying Expert Routing Modes Across Updates

To address the expert-activation volatility problem in GRPO training of MoE models, the authors previously developed a training strategy called Routing Replay. The core idea is to ensure consistency in the network topology used to compute the token-level importance ratios, thereby restoring their stability. Specifically, during the rollout phase under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, the activated experts for each token in each response are cached—meaning the routing decisions are stored alongside the generated tokens. Then, when computing the importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ during the gradient update phase, these cached routing modes are "replayed" in the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$.

In practice, this means that when the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$ processes a token $y_{i,t}$ to compute $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$, it does not use its own (potentially different) routing decisions. Instead, it forces the token through the same experts that were activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. By doing so, both $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ are computed using the same activated network for each token. This eliminates the fluctuation caused by expert-activation changes and ensures that the token-level importance ratios reflect only the change in policy parameters, not the change in routing topology. The paper demonstrates that Routing Replay is essential for the normal convergence of GRPO training on MoE models—without it, the training fails to converge properly due to the instability introduced by expert-activation volatility.

# Limitations of Routing Replay: Memory, Communication Overhead, and Reduced Model Capacity

While Routing Replay successfully stabilizes GRPO training for MoE models, it comes with significant practical drawbacks. First, caching the activated experts for every token in every response introduces additional memory overhead. In large-scale RL training where rollout batches can be enormous, storing routing information for all tokens across all layers of a deep MoE model is non-trivial. Second, replaying the routing modes requires additional communication overhead in distributed training settings. The cached routing information must be propagated and synchronized across devices, adding complexity and latency to the training pipeline.

Perhaps most critically, Routing Replay limits the actual capacity of the MoE model. By forcing the new policy to use the old policy's routing decisions, the model is prevented from leveraging its full architectural flexibility. The MoE model's power lies in its ability to dynamically route tokens to the most appropriate experts, and by freezing the routing decisions to match the old policy, this dynamic capability is artificially constrained. The model cannot fully exercise its learned routing during the importance ratio computation, which means the training signal is computed on a restricted version of the model rather than the full model. This limitation could potentially hinder the model from reaching its full performance potential. In essence, Routing Replay is a workaround that trades model flexibility and training efficiency for stability—a compromise that, while necessary under GRPO, is far from ideal.

# How GSPO Resolves Expert-Activation Volatility: Insensitivity to Individual Token Likelihoods

GSPO offers a fundamentally different and more elegant solution to the expert-activation volatility problem. The key insight is that GSPO's importance ratio is defined at the sequence level rather than the token level. Recall that GSPO defines the importance ratio as:

$$s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}\right).$$

While this formula does involve individual token likelihoods in its computation, the critical point is that it aggregates them into a single sequence-level quantity through the geometric mean (with length normalization). The gradient analysis in the paper reveals the profound difference: in GRPO, each token's gradient is weighted by its own potentially volatile importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, while in GSPO, all tokens in a response share the same importance weight $s_i(\theta)$, which is the sequence-level ratio. This means that GSPO is fundamentally insensitive to the individual token likelihood fluctuations caused by expert-activation changes. Even if the routing for a particular token changes between the old and new policies, causing its individual token-level ratio to fluctuate, this fluctuation is averaged out across the entire sequence. The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ depends on the overall sequence likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)$, which is a product of all token probabilities in the response, and this aggregate quantity is far more stable than any individual token probability.

# Sequence Likelihood Stability in MoE Models Despite Routing Changes

The stability of the sequence likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)$ in MoE models, even when routing decisions change, is the theoretical foundation that makes GSPO effective for MoE training. The paper explains this stability with a key insight: "Since the MoE model always maintains its language modeling capability, the sequence likelihood will not fluctuate drastically." What this means is that while the specific experts activated for individual tokens may change after a gradient update, the model as a whole still functions as a competent language model. The total probability assigned to a given response sequence—the product of all token probabilities—remains relatively stable because the model's overall language modeling capability is preserved.

To build intuition, consider that in an MoE model, different experts may learn similar or overlapping representations, especially when the number of experts is large. When a token is routed to a different expert after a gradient update, the new expert may produce a similar probability for that token, even though the internal computation path is different. While individual token probabilities may shift slightly, these shifts tend to average out across the many tokens in a response. The sequence likelihood, being the product of all token probabilities (or equivalently, the sum of log probabilities), is a smoothed aggregate that is robust to local fluctuations. This is precisely why GSPO's sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ remains stable and meaningful, even in the presence of expert-activation volatility that would render token-level ratios unreliable. The geometric mean with length normalization further enhances this stability by ensuring that the importance ratio is not dominated by a few outlier tokens.

# Elimination of Routing Replay: Simplification and Full Capacity Utilization

The ultimate benefit of GSPO for MoE training is the complete elimination of the need for Routing Replay. The paper demonstrates that GSPO converges normally and optimizes stably on MoE models without any routing caching or replaying mechanism. This is a significant practical advantage with multiple dimensions of improvement.

First, the training process is simplified. There is no need to implement the complex caching logic for expert activations, no need to manage the communication of routing information across distributed training devices, and no need to override the model's natural routing decisions during importance ratio computation. The RL training infrastructure becomes cleaner and more maintainable. Second, the memory and communication overhead associated with Routing Replay is entirely eliminated, freeing up resources for other uses such as larger batch sizes or longer responses. Third, and perhaps most importantly, the MoE model can leverage its full capacity during training. The model is free to route tokens to whichever experts it deems most appropriate under the current policy, without any artificial constraints imposed by the need to match the old policy's routing. This means the model can fully exercise its learned routing mechanism and develop its full expressive power.

The paper shows that GSPO achieves stable training throughout the RL process on the Qwen3-30B-A3B model without Routing Replay, while GRPO requires Routing Replay to avoid training divergence. This elimination of complex workarounds not only simplifies and stabilizes the training process but also has broader implications for RL infrastructure design. As the paper notes, GSPO's focus on sequence-level likelihoods (rather than token-level likelihoods) also makes it more tolerant of precision discrepancies between training and inference engines, potentially eliminating the need to recompute likelihoods with the training engine. Together, these benefits make GSPO a particularly well-suited algorithm for large-scale RL training of MoE models, and the authors report that these merits contributed to the exceptional performance improvements in the latest Qwen3 models.
