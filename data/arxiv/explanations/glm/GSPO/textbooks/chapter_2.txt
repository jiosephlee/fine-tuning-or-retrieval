Chapter 2: Foundations: PPO and GRPO

## Notation and Preliminaries

Before delving into the specifics of reinforcement learning algorithms for large language models, it is essential to establish a clear and consistent notational framework. In the context of this chapter, an autoregressive language model parameterized by $\theta$ is defined as a policy $\pi_\theta$. The policy takes as input a query, denoted by $x$, and produces a response, denoted by $y$. The set of all queries from which we sample is denoted as $\mathcal{D}$. Given a response $y$ to a query $x$, the likelihood of that response under the policy $\pi_\theta$ is expressed as $\pi_\theta(y | x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_t | x, y_{<t})$, where $|y|$ denotes the number of tokens in the response $y$, and $y_{<t}$ refers to the sequence of tokens preceding position $t$. This factorization reflects the autoregressive nature of language generation, where each token is generated conditioned on the query and all previously generated tokens.

A query-response pair $(x, y)$ can be scored by a verifier $r$, which produces a reward $r(x, y) \in [0, 1]$. The verifier serves as the feedback mechanism that evaluates the quality of a generated response, assigning a scalar reward that quantifies how desirable the response is. This reward signal is the driving force behind reinforcement learning: by optimizing the policy to generate responses that receive higher rewards, the model gradually improves its performance on the task at hand. The restriction of the reward to the interval $[0, 1]$ is a design choice that bounds the magnitude of the reward signal, which can help in maintaining numerical stability during training.

Understanding these basic components — the policy, the query, the response, the verifier, and the reward — provides the foundation upon which the more complex algorithmic machinery of PPO and GRPO is built. The interplay between these elements, combined with the mathematical tools of importance sampling, advantage estimation, and clipping, forms the core of modern reinforcement learning algorithms for language models.

## Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO): Objective Function and Clipping Mechanism

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a foundational reinforcement learning algorithm that addresses a key challenge in policy optimization: how to make meaningful improvements to the policy without taking excessively large steps that could destabilize training. The central idea behind PPO is to constrain the policy update to remain within a proximal region of the old policy, ensuring that the new policy does not deviate too drastically from the policy that generated the training data. This constraint is implemented through a clipping mechanism that limits the magnitude of the importance ratio, a quantity that measures how much the new policy differs from the old policy.

Using samples generated from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, PPO optimizes the following objective function:

$$\mathcal{J}_\text{PPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)} \left[ \frac{1}{|y|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \min \left( w_{t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{t}, \, \mathrm{clip} \left( w_{t}(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon \right) \widehat{A}_{t} \right) \right],$$

where $\varepsilon$ is the clipping range, a hyperparameter that controls how far the policy is allowed to deviate from the old policy. The expectation is taken over queries sampled from the query set $\mathcal{D}$ and responses sampled from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. The objective is averaged over the tokens in the response, as indicated by the $\frac{1}{|y|}$ factor and the summation over $t$ from $1$ to $|y|$.

The clipping mechanism works as follows. The $\min$ operator compares two quantities: the unclipped product $w_{t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{t}$ and the clipped product $\mathrm{clip}(w_{t}(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon) \widehat{A}_{t}$. By taking the minimum of these two terms, the objective function effectively prevents the policy from exploiting large deviations from the old policy. When the importance ratio $w_{t}(\theta)$ falls within the range $[1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon]$, the clipping has no effect, and the objective reduces to the unclipped version. However, when $w_{t}(\theta)$ exceeds $1 + \varepsilon$ or falls below $1 - \varepsilon$, the clipped value is used instead, effectively capping the contribution of that token to the gradient.

The clipping mechanism thus serves a dual purpose. First, it limits the variance of the gradient estimates by bounding the importance ratios, which can otherwise fluctuate widely. Second, it prevents the policy from making overly aggressive updates based on individual samples that may not be representative of the overall distribution. By constraining the policy to remain close to the old policy, PPO ensures that the optimization proceeds in a stable and controlled manner, making it one of the most widely used reinforcement learning algorithms in practice.

## Token-Level Importance Ratios in PPO: Definition and Role

A central concept in PPO is the token-level importance ratio, which quantifies how much the current policy $\pi_\theta$ differs from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ at each token position. Specifically, the importance ratio of token $y_t$ is defined as:

$$w_{t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t | x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t | x, y_{<t})}.$$

This ratio compares the probability that the current policy assigns to token $y_t$ (given the query $x$ and the preceding tokens $y_{<t}$) with the probability that the old policy assigned to the same token. When $w_{t}(\theta) = 1$, the two policies agree on the likelihood of that token, indicating no change in the policy's behavior at that position. When $w_{t}(\theta) > 1$, the current policy assigns a higher probability to the token than the old policy did, suggesting that the policy has shifted toward generating that token more frequently. Conversely, when $w_{t}(\theta) < 1$, the current policy assigns a lower probability, indicating a shift away from that token.

The role of the importance ratio in PPO is rooted in the principle of importance sampling, a statistical technique for estimating expectations under one distribution using samples drawn from another. In the context of PPO, the responses used for training are sampled from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, but the objective is to optimize the current policy $\pi_\theta$. The importance ratio corrects for this distributional mismatch by reweighting each sample according to the ratio of the two distributions. In theory, this allows the algorithm to compute an unbiased estimate of the gradient under the current policy using data generated by the old policy.

However, the application of importance sampling at the token level in PPO relies on the assumption that the importance weight $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ effectively corrects for the distributional mismatch between the target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$ and the behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$. Crucially, this correction is only valid when averaging over multiple samples ($N \gg 1$) from the behavior distribution. In PPO, the importance weight at each token position is based on a single sample $y_t$ from the next-token distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x, y_{<t})$, which means the distribution-correction role of importance sampling is not fully realized. This limitation becomes particularly problematic in the context of long sequences, where the accumulation of token-level noise can lead to high-variance gradients and training instability, a issue that GSPO explicitly addresses.

## Value Models in PPO: Purpose, Computational Burden, and Scalability Challenges

A distinguishing feature of PPO is its reliance on a value model for estimating the advantage $\widehat{A}_{t}$ of each token $y_t$. The advantage function measures how much better an action (in this case, generating a particular token) is compared to the average action expected under the current policy. It is a critical component of the PPO objective, as it determines the direction and magnitude of the policy update: tokens with positive advantages are reinforced, while tokens with negative advantages are discouraged.

The value model, also known as a critic, estimates the expected future reward from a given state. In the context of language models, the state at position $t$ consists of the query $x$ and the tokens generated so far $y_{<t}$. The value model predicts the expected cumulative reward that the policy will receive starting from this state, providing a baseline against which the actual reward can be compared. The advantage is then computed as the difference between the actual reward (or an estimate thereof) and the value model's prediction, capturing the incremental benefit of the specific action taken.

While the value model plays a crucial role in the theoretical foundations of PPO, its practical implementation introduces significant challenges. First, the value model typically has a similar size to the policy model, meaning it requires a comparable amount of memory and computational resources. This effectively doubles the memory and computational burden of training, as both the policy model and the value model must be stored, updated, and evaluated during each training step. For large language models, this overhead can be prohibitive, limiting the scalability of the algorithm.

Furthermore, the algorithmic effectiveness of PPO hinges on the reliability of the value model's estimates. If the value model produces inaccurate predictions, the advantage estimates will be unreliable, leading to suboptimal or even harmful policy updates. Acquiring a reliable value model is inherently challenging, as it requires training a separate model on the same data and ensuring that its predictions are well-calibrated. This challenge is compounded when scaling to longer responses and more complex tasks, where the value model must accurately predict rewards over extended horizons. The difficulty of maintaining a reliable value model at scale is one of the key motivations for the development of GRPO, which eliminates the need for a value model entirely.

## Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO): Eliminating the Value Model

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) was developed to address one of the most significant practical limitations of PPO: the heavy reliance on a value model. By eliminating the value model entirely, GRPO reduces the memory and computational burden of training while simplifying the algorithmic pipeline. The key insight behind GRPO is that the advantage of a response can be estimated relative to other responses within the same group, without the need for a separate value model to provide a baseline.

GRPO achieves this by generating multiple responses to the same query and using the relative rewards of these responses to compute advantages. Specifically, GRPO optimizes the following objective:

$$\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \min \left( w_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t}, \, \mathrm{clip} \left( w_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon \right) \widehat{A}_{i,t} \right) \right],$$

where $G$ is the group size, representing the number of responses generated for each query $x$. The importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ for the $i$-th response and the $t$-th token is defined as:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}.$$

The structure of the GRPO objective closely mirrors that of PPO, with the $\min$ operator and clipping mechanism serving the same purpose of constraining policy updates. However, the critical difference lies in how the advantages are computed. Instead of relying on a value model to estimate the advantage of each token, GRPO computes advantages based on the relative rewards of responses within the group. This eliminates the need for a value model, significantly reducing the computational and memory requirements of training.

The expectation in the GRPO objective is taken over queries sampled from $\mathcal{D}$ and groups of $G$ responses sampled from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. The outer average over the $G$ responses (normalized by $\frac{1}{G}$) and the inner average over the tokens in each response (normalized by $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$) ensure that the objective is properly scaled across different group sizes and response lengths. This formulation allows GRPO to leverage the information from multiple responses to the same query, providing a richer signal for policy optimization than PPO's single-response approach.

## Group-Based Advantage Estimation: Normalizing Rewards Across Multiple Responses

The hallmark of GRPO is its group-based advantage estimation, which replaces the value model used in PPO with a simpler and more efficient mechanism. For each query $x$, GRPO generates $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ and computes the reward $r(x, y_i)$ for each response. The advantage $\widehat{A}_{i}$ of the $i$-th response is then computed as:

$$\widehat{A}_{i} = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}{\mathrm{std}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}.$$

This formula normalizes the rewards across the group by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation. The mean $\mathrm{mean}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)$ serves as a baseline, representing the average quality of responses to the query. By subtracting this baseline from each reward, the advantage captures how much better or worse a particular response is compared to the average response in the group. The division by the standard deviation $\mathrm{std}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)$ further normalizes the advantages, ensuring that they are on a consistent scale regardless of the absolute magnitude of the rewards.

This normalization has several important consequences. First, it provides a natural form of credit assignment: responses that receive higher-than-average rewards are assigned positive advantages, encouraging the policy to generate similar responses in the future, while responses that receive lower-than-average rewards are assigned negative advantages, discouraging the policy from generating similar responses. Second, the standardization by the standard deviation ensures that the advantages are not dominated by queries with high-variance rewards, providing a balanced training signal across different queries. Third, this approach is entirely data-driven, requiring no additional model or training procedure, which simplifies the implementation and reduces the computational cost.

The group-based advantage estimation is a key innovation of GRPO, as it provides a principled way to estimate advantages without the need for a value model. By leveraging the diversity of responses within a group, GRPO can effectively distinguish between good and bad responses, guiding the policy toward generating higher-quality outputs. This approach is particularly well-suited for tasks where rewards can be reliably computed by a verifier, such as mathematics and programming, where the correctness of a response can be objectively determined.

## Shared Advantage Across Tokens in GRPO

A notable characteristic of GRPO is that all tokens within a response share the same advantage. In the GRPO objective, the advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ of token $y_{i,t}$ is set equal to the response-level advantage $\widehat{A}_{i}$:

$$\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i} = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}{\mathrm{std}\left(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G\right)}.$$

This means that every token in the $i$-th response receives the same advantage signal, regardless of its position or individual contribution to the overall quality of the response. The rationale for this design is that the reward $r(x, y_i)$ is granted at the sequence level, evaluating the response as a whole rather than individual tokens. Since the verifier assesses the entire response, it is natural to propagate the same advantage signal to all tokens within that response.

The implication of shared advantages is that the policy update treats all tokens in a response equally in terms of the reward signal. If a response receives a positive advantage, all tokens in that response are reinforced; if it receives a negative advantage, all tokens are discouraged. This is a simplification compared to PPO, where the value model can potentially assign different advantages to different tokens based on their expected future rewards. However, this simplification is justified by the observation that, in practice, it is difficult to reliably attribute credit to individual tokens, especially for complex reasoning tasks where the relationship between individual tokens and the final reward is not straightforward.

Despite this simplification, the shared advantage design has an important consequence that becomes apparent when examining the gradient of the GRPO objective. The gradient of GRPO can be expressed as:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x)} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \widehat{A}_{i} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right].$$

This expression reveals that, while the advantage is shared across tokens, the gradient contribution of each token is weighted by its individual token-level importance ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$. These unequal weights can vary significantly across tokens, ranging from $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ when $\widehat{A}_{i} > 0$ or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ when $\widehat{A}_{i} < 0$. The variation in these weights introduces a source of instability, as the impact of individual token-level importance ratios can accumulate over long sequences and lead to unpredictable consequences as training progresses. This observation is one of the key motivations for the development of GSPO, which weights all tokens in a response equally by using a sequence-level importance ratio.

## Practical Limitations of PPO and GRPO: Memory, Computational Overhead, and Stability

Both PPO and GRPO, despite their widespread adoption, suffer from practical limitations that hinder their scalability to large language models and long-response tasks. These limitations span memory and computational overhead, as well as fundamental stability issues that can lead to catastrophic model collapse.

The primary limitation of PPO is its reliance on a value model. The value model typically has a similar size to the policy model, introducing a considerable memory and computational burden. Training two large models simultaneously — the policy model and the value model — effectively doubles the resource requirements, making PPO expensive to scale. Moreover, the algorithmic effectiveness of PPO hinges on the reliability of the value model's estimates. While acquiring a reliable value model is inherently challenging, ensuring its scalability to longer responses and more complex tasks presents an even greater challenge. As the response length increases, the value model must make accurate predictions over longer horizons, which becomes increasingly difficult. This scalability challenge limits the applicability of PPO to tasks that require long-form reasoning, such as competition-level mathematics and programming.

GRPO addresses the value model limitation by eliminating it entirely, replacing it with group-based advantage estimation. However, GRPO introduces its own set of challenges, primarily related to training stability. The fundamental issue identified in GRPO is the misapplication of importance sampling weights at the token level. The principle of importance sampling requires averaging over multiple samples from the behavior distribution for the importance weight to effectively correct for the distributional mismatch. In GRPO, the importance weight $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$ is applied at each token position based on a single sample from the next-token distribution. This fails to perform the intended distribution-correction role and instead introduces high-variance noise into the training gradients.

This noise accumulates over long sequences and is further exacerbated by the clipping mechanism. As the response length increases, the cumulative effect of token-level noise becomes more pronounced, potentially leading to catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. Empirically, this collapse has been observed to be irreversible: once it occurs, resuming training is unavailing, even when reverting to a previous checkpoint, meticulously tuning hyperparameters such as the clipping ranges, extending the generation length, or switching the RL queries. This is a severe limitation, as it means that a single instability event can effectively terminate the training process, wasting significant computational resources.

The stability issues in GRPO are particularly acute when training large models, especially Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, on long-response tasks. The sparse activation nature of MoE models introduces additional challenges, as the experts activated for the same response can change significantly after gradient updates, causing the token-level importance ratios to fluctuate drastically and further invalidating them. This phenomenon, known as expert-activation volatility, can prevent RL training from converging properly and necessitates complex workarounds such as the Routing Replay strategy, which incurs additional memory and communication overhead and limits the actual capacity of the MoE model.

These practical limitations — the computational burden of value models in PPO, the instability of token-level importance ratios in GRPO, and the compounded challenges of MoE training — collectively motivate the development of GSPO. By redefining the importance ratio at the sequence level and aligning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward, GSPO addresses the fundamental issues that plague its predecessors, providing a more stable, efficient, and performant foundation for reinforcement learning with large language models.
