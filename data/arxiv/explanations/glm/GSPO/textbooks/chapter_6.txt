\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 6: Empirical Evaluation and Analysis

# Experimental Setup: Cold-Start Model, Training Configuration, and Benchmarks

To rigorously evaluate the merits of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) against the established Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm, a carefully controlled experimental framework is essential. The experiments employ a cold-start model that has been fine-tuned from the Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model. This particular base model is a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture with 30 billion total parameters and 3 billion activated parameters, making it an ideal testbed for assessing both the performance and stability of reinforcement learning algorithms under realistic conditions. The choice of an MoE model is particularly significant because, as discussed in earlier chapters, MoE models introduce unique stability challenges during RL training due to expert-activation volatility — challenges that GSPO is specifically designed to address.

The training procedure follows a standard yet demanding configuration. During RL training, each batch of rollout data is partitioned into four mini-batches for gradient updates. This mini-batch partitioning is a critical design choice that reflects common practice in large-scale RL training: to maximize hardware utilization, a large rollout batch is generated, and then this batch is divided into multiple smaller mini-batches, each of which is used for a separate gradient update step. This procedure inevitably introduces an off-policy learning setting, where the responses being optimized were sampled from an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ rather than the current policy $\pi_\theta$ being updated. This off-policy discrepancy is precisely what necessitates the clipping mechanisms in both GSPO and GRPO, as these mechanisms prevent overly off-policy samples from exerting undue influence on gradient estimation.

The evaluation of model performance is conducted across three prestigious and widely recognized benchmarks that span competition-level mathematics and programming. The first is AIME'24, the American Invitational Mathematics Examination from 2024, on which performance is measured as the average Pass@1 over 32 samplings. This benchmark tests the model's ability to solve sophisticated mathematical problems that require deep reasoning. The second is LiveCodeBench, specifically covering the period from October 2024 to February 2025, on which performance is measured as the average Pass@1 over 8 samplings. This benchmark evaluates the model's programming capabilities on contemporary coding challenges. The third is CodeForces, a competitive programming platform, on which performance is measured using the Elo Rating system, providing a standardized competitive ranking that reflects the model's coding proficiency relative to human competitors. Together, these three benchmarks provide a comprehensive assessment of the model's reasoning and problem-solving capabilities — the very capabilities that large-scale RL training aims to enhance.

# Mini-Batch Partitioning and Clipping Range Settings for Fair Comparison

A fair comparison between GSPO and GRPO requires careful attention to the clipping range hyperparameters, as the two algorithms define importance ratios at fundamentally different levels and thus operate on different numerical scales. In GRPO, the importance ratio is defined at the token level as $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$, which represents the ratio of probabilities for a single token under the current and old policies. In GSPO, the importance ratio is defined at the sequence level as $s_{i}(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$, which represents the length-normalized geometric mean of the per-token importance ratios across the entire response. Due to this geometric averaging, the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ naturally exhibits much smaller deviations from unity compared to individual token-level ratios, necessitating a tighter clipping range.

For the GSPO experiments, the left and right clipping ranges in the objective are set to $3 \times 10^{-4}$ and $4 \times 10^{-4}$, respectively. These values are orders of magnitude smaller than the clipping ranges used in GRPO. For the GRPO baseline, the left and right clipping ranges are set to $0.2$ and $0.27$, respectively. These GRPO clipping ranges were carefully tuned to ensure a fair comparison, meaning that significant effort was invested in finding the best-performing clipping configuration for GRPO. The asymmetric clipping ranges — where the right clipping bound is set slightly higher than the left — reflect a deliberate design choice that allows the policy to move somewhat more freely in the direction of increasing the importance ratio (i.e., making a response more likely) than in the direction of decreasing it. This asymmetry is applied consistently in both algorithms.

A critical distinction in the experimental setup concerns the training stability strategies required by each algorithm. GRPO, when applied to MoE models, necessitates the use of a specialized training strategy called Routing Replay to achieve normal convergence. Routing Replay involves caching the activated experts under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and reusing these routing modes when computing importance ratios under the current policy $\pi_\theta$, thereby ensuring that the token-level importance ratios remain stable across gradient updates. GSPO, by contrast, completely obviates the need for this strategy — it is fully capable of computing importance ratios conventionally, converging normally, and optimizing stably without any such workaround. This difference is not merely a convenience; it has significant implications for training efficiency, memory overhead, and model capacity, as will be discussed in detail in subsequent sections.

# Training Reward Curves: Stability and Convergence Comparison

The training reward curves provide the most direct visualization of the stability and convergence behavior of the two algorithms. When examining these curves, the most striking observation is that GSPO training proceeds stably throughout the entire training process. This stability is not merely a matter of avoiding catastrophic failure — it represents a fundamental property of the algorithm's design. Because GSPO defines its importance ratio at the sequence level, it avoids the high-variance noise that plagues GRPO's token-level importance weights. As a result, the gradient estimates in GSPO are more reliable, and the policy updates proceed smoothly without the erratic fluctuations that can destabilize training.

In contrast, GRPO training on the same MoE model exhibits significant stability challenges. Without the Routing Replay strategy, GRPO training fails to converge properly due to the expert-activation volatility inherent in MoE models. After each gradient update, roughly $10\%$ of the experts activated for the same response under the new policy $\pi_\theta$ differ from those activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. This phenomenon causes the token-level importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ to fluctuate drastically, further invalidating their already questionable distributional correction role. Even with Routing Replay in place, GRPO training remains more prone to instability, particularly as training compute increases and response lengths grow.

The stability of GSPO has a profound practical implication: it enables continuous performance improvement through straightforward scaling. Specifically, GSPO can deliver sustained performance gains by increasing the training compute, regularly updating the query set, and extending the generation length. Each of these scaling strategies is important in practice. Increasing training compute allows the model to learn from more data and refine its policy over more gradient steps. Regularly updating the query set ensures that the model is continually exposed to fresh, diverse problems, preventing overfitting to a fixed set of training queries. Extending the generation length allows the model to undertake deeper and longer reasoning processes, which is critical for tackling sophisticated problems in mathematics and programming. The fact that GSPO can accommodate all three of these scaling strategies simultaneously, without succumbing to instability, is a testament to its robust algorithmic foundation.

The stability advantage of GSPO becomes even more significant in light of the catastrophic consequences of instability in GRPO. As discussed in earlier chapters, once model collapse occurs in GRPO training, it is often irreversible. Resuming training proves unavailing even when reverting to a previous checkpoint, meticulously tuning hyperparameters such as clipping ranges, extending generation length, or switching the RL queries. This irreversibility means that the computational investment in training up to the point of collapse is entirely lost — a severe penalty that makes GRPO training of large models a high-risk endeavor. GSPO, by eliminating the root cause of this instability, transforms large-scale RL training from a precarious undertaking into a reliable and predictable process.

# Benchmark Performance: Pass@1 and Elo Rating Comparisons

Beyond training stability, the ultimate measure of an RL algorithm's effectiveness is the performance of the trained model on held-out benchmarks. The experimental results demonstrate that GSPO achieves superior benchmark performance compared to GRPO across all three evaluation benchmarks, providing compelling evidence that the algorithmic improvements in GSPO translate into tangible capability gains.

On AIME'24, which measures average Pass@1 over 32 samplings, the GSPO-trained model achieves higher accuracy than the GRPO-trained model. AIME problems are renowned for their difficulty, requiring creative mathematical reasoning and multi-step problem-solving. The Pass@1 metric, which measures the probability of generating a correct solution in a single attempt, is a stringent measure of the model's genuine problem-solving ability — unlike Pass@k metrics that benefit from multiple sampling attempts, Pass@1 rewards models that can reliably produce correct solutions. The fact that GSPO outperforms GRPO on this metric indicates that the sequence-level optimization of GSPO leads to more reliable and consistent reasoning capabilities.

On LiveCodeBench, which measures average Pass@1 over 8 samplings on coding challenges from October 2024 to February 2025, the GSPO-trained model similarly surpasses the GRPO-trained model. This benchmark is particularly valuable because it covers a recent time period, reducing the likelihood that the problems were included in the pre-training data and thus providing a more genuine assessment of the model's generalization capabilities. The programming tasks on LiveCodeBench require not only correct logic but also precise implementation, making them a comprehensive test of the model's coding proficiency.

On CodeForces, where performance is measured using the Elo Rating system, the GSPO-trained model achieves a higher rating than the GRPO-trained model. The Elo Rating system, originally developed for chess and widely adopted in competitive programming, provides a relative measure of skill that accounts for the difficulty of opponents and the consistency of performance. A higher Elo Rating on CodeForces signifies that the model can compete at a higher level in competitive programming, which involves not only solving individual problems but also doing so efficiently under time constraints. The improvement in Elo Rating thus reflects a holistic enhancement in the model's programming capabilities.

The consistent superiority of GSPO across three diverse benchmarks — spanning pure mathematics, software engineering, and competitive programming — demonstrates that the benefits of sequence-level optimization are not limited to a specific domain or task type. Rather, the more reliable gradient estimates and the alignment between sequence-level rewarding and optimization in GSPO lead to broadly applicable improvements in the model's reasoning and problem-solving capabilities.

# Training Efficiency: GSPO's Superior Performance Under Equal Compute and Query Consumption

Training efficiency is a critical consideration in large-scale RL, where computational resources are expensive and the cost of generating rollout data constitutes a significant portion of the total training budget. The experimental results reveal that GSPO demonstrates superior training efficiency over GRPO, achieving better training accuracy and benchmark performance under the same training compute and consumed queries.

This efficiency advantage can be understood through the lens of the gradient analysis presented in earlier chapters. In GRPO, the gradient of the objective weights each token's log-likelihood gradient by its individual token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$. These weights vary among tokens within the same response, creating unequal and potentially unpredictable gradient contributions. For tokens where the advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ is positive, the token-level weights can range over $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$, while for tokens where the advantage is negative, the weights can range over $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$. The unequal weighting means that some tokens receive disproportionately large or small gradient updates relative to their actual importance, leading to inefficient use of training signal.

In GSPO, by contrast, the gradient weights all tokens in a response equally. The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$ serves as a uniform scaling factor applied to the average of all per-token log-likelihood gradients. This equal weighting ensures that every token in a response contributes to the gradient in a balanced and predictable manner, eliminating the instability factor of GRPO. The result is that each training sample provides a cleaner, more reliable learning signal, allowing the model to extract more useful information from the same amount of data.

The efficiency advantage of GSPO has compounding effects over the course of training. Because each gradient update is more reliable, the policy improves more consistently with each step, leading to faster convergence and better final performance for a given computational budget. This means that for the same amount of GPU-hours and the same number of generated responses (queries), GSPO achieves higher capability than GRPO — a practical advantage that translates directly into reduced training costs and faster iteration cycles in model development. In the resource-intensive world of large language model training, where each training run can cost substantial computational resources, this efficiency gain represents a significant practical benefit.

# The Clipping Fraction Observation: Two Orders of Magnitude Difference Between GSPO and GRPO

One of the most intriguing experimental findings involves the comparison of clipping fractions — that is, the proportion of tokens or responses that are clipped by the clipping mechanism and thus excluded from gradient estimation. The experimental data reveals a striking difference of two orders of magnitude in the fractions of clipped tokens between GSPO and GRPO. This disparity is remarkably robust: adjusting the clipping ranges does not alter the disparity in magnitude, indicating that the difference is a fundamental property of the algorithms rather than an artifact of hyperparameter choices.

To understand this observation, it is important to consider what the clipping mechanism does in each algorithm. In GRPO, clipping is applied at the token level: for each token $y_{i,t}$, if the token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ falls outside the range $[1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]$, that token's contribution to the objective is clipped, effectively preventing it from participating in gradient estimation. Given that token-level importance ratios can fluctuate significantly — especially in MoE models where expert activations may change between the old and current policies — it might seem surprising that relatively few tokens are clipped in GRPO. However, the clipping ranges for GRPO ($0.2$ to $0.27$) are relatively wide, allowing token-level importance ratios to deviate substantially from unity before triggering clipping.

In GSPO, clipping is applied at the sequence level: for each response $y_i$, if the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ falls outside the clipping range, the entire response is clipped. The clipping ranges for GSPO ($3 \times 10^{-4}$ to $4 \times 10^{-4}$) are orders of magnitude tighter than those for GRPO, reflecting the fact that the length-normalized geometric mean of per-token ratios naturally exhibits much smaller deviations from unity. Despite these tighter clipping ranges, GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens — two orders of magnitude more — because the sequence-level importance ratio is a more sensitive indicator of policy divergence. Even small per-token deviations, when aggregated across an entire sequence through the geometric mean, can push the sequence-level ratio outside the tight clipping bounds. When a response is clipped in GSPO, all of its tokens are effectively excluded from gradient estimation, leading to a much higher aggregate fraction of clipped tokens.

# Counter-Intuitive Finding: More Clipping Leads to Better Efficiency

The clipping fraction observation gives rise to a profoundly counter-intuitive finding: despite clipping significantly more tokens and consequently using fewer tokens for training (or gradient estimation), GSPO still achieves higher training efficiency than GRPO. This result challenges the conventional intuition that more training data (i.e., more unclipped tokens participating in gradient estimation) should lead to better learning. The finding suggests that the quality of the learning signal matters far more than its quantity.

The intuition behind this counter-intuitive result can be understood through the lens of signal quality. In GRPO, the token-level gradient estimates that survive clipping are not necessarily the most reliable ones — they are simply the ones whose token-level importance ratios happen to fall within the clipping bounds. Many of these surviving gradient estimates carry the high-variance noise introduced by the ill-posed token-level importance weights. This noise means that a significant portion of the gradient signal in GRPO is essentially random, pushing the policy in unpredictable directions rather than toward genuinely better responses. Training with noisy gradients is not just inefficient — it can be actively harmful, as the noise accumulates over training steps and can eventually destabilize the entire training process.

In GSPO, the responses that survive clipping are those whose sequence-level importance ratios indicate that they are still sufficiently on-policy to provide reliable gradient estimates. The sequence-level importance ratio, being a length-normalized geometric mean, aggregates information across the entire response and provides a much more robust measure of policy divergence than any individual token-level ratio. By clipping at this level, GSPO ensures that only responses with reliable, well-calibrated gradient signals participate in training. The responses that are clipped — those whose sequence-level ratios fall outside the tight bounds — are precisely the ones that would have introduced noise and instability into the training process. In effect, GSPO's aggressive clipping acts as a stringent quality filter, ensuring that every gradient update is based on reliable information.

This finding — that clipping a much larger fraction of tokens leads to superior training efficiency — provides powerful additional evidence that GRPO's token-level gradient estimates are inherently noisy and inefficient for sample exploitation. It demonstrates that the problem with GRPO is not that it clips too many or too few tokens, but that its fundamental approach of token-level importance weighting produces unreliable gradient estimates regardless of the clipping configuration. The fact that GSPO achieves better results with less data (fewer unclipped tokens) is a clear indication that its sequence-level approach provides a more reliable and effective learning signal, making better use of each training sample that passes its quality filter.

# Evidence That GRPO's Token-Level Gradient Estimates Are Inherently Noisy

The clipping fraction observation, combined with the gradient analysis from earlier chapters, provides converging evidence that GRPO's token-level gradient estimates are inherently noisy. This conclusion is supported by multiple independent lines of reasoning, each pointing to the same fundamental issue.

The first line of evidence comes from the theoretical analysis of importance sampling. The principle of importance sampling requires averaging over multiple samples ($N \gg 1$) from the behavior distribution for the importance weight to effectively correct for the distributional mismatch. GRPO applies the importance weight $\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$ at each token position, but this weight is based on a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from each next-token distribution. It therefore fails to perform the intended distribution-correction role and instead introduces high-variance noise into the training gradients.

The second line of evidence comes from the gradient analysis. In GRPO, the gradient weights each token's log-likelihood gradient by its individual token-level importance ratio, creating unequal weights that vary among $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ for positive advantages or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ for negative advantages. These unequal weights are not negligible, and their impact accumulates over long sequences and over many training steps, leading to unpredictable consequences. GSPO, by weighting all tokens equally, eliminates this instability factor entirely.

The third line of evidence comes from the empirical observation that GSPO, despite clipping two orders of magnitude more tokens, achieves higher training efficiency. This finding is only explicable if the gradient estimates that GRPO retains (i.e., the unclipped token-level estimates) contain substantial noise that degrades the quality of learning. If GRPO's unclipped gradient estimates were reliable, having more of them should lead to better or at least comparable efficiency — but the opposite is observed.

The fourth line of evidence comes from the MoE training experience. In MoE models, the expert-activation volatility causes token-level importance ratios to fluctuate drastically, further invalidating their distributional correction role. GRPO requires the Routing Replay strategy to mitigate this issue, while GSPO — which operates at the sequence level and is insensitive to individual token likelihoods — does not. This difference further underscores that the token-level importance ratios in GRPO are inherently unreliable, especially in architectures where per-token probabilities can change for structural reasons (like expert routing) rather than for policy improvement reasons.

Taken together, these four lines of evidence present a compelling case that the token-level approach in GRPO is fundamentally flawed. The noise introduced by token-level importance weighting is not a minor inefficiency that can be tuned away with better hyperparameters — it is a structural property of the algorithm that becomes increasingly problematic as models grow larger, responses grow longer, and architectures become more complex. GSPO's sequence-level approach, by contrast, provides a principled and robust solution that aligns with the fundamental principles of importance sampling and the sequence-level nature of the reward signal.

# GSPO's Role in the RL Training of the Qwen3 Models

The ultimate validation of any algorithmic innovation is its successful application to real-world, large-scale model training. The experimental results confirm that GSPO has been successfully applied to the RL training of the latest Qwen3 models, providing strong proof of its efficacy in unleashing the power of RL scaling for large language models. This application is not merely a demonstration of feasibility — it represents the culmination of the algorithmic insights, stability improvements, and efficiency gains discussed throughout this chapter, applied at the scale necessary to produce state-of-the-art language models.

The role of GSPO in the Qwen3 training pipeline is multifaceted. First, its inherent stability eliminates the risk of catastrophic and irreversible model collapse that plagues GRPO training, allowing the training process to proceed reliably over extended periods without the need for constant monitoring and intervention. This reliability is essential for scaling RL training to the computational budgets required for frontier model development. Second, its superior training efficiency means that more capability is extracted from each unit of computational investment, enabling faster iteration and more effective use of resources. Third, its compatibility with MoE architectures — eliminating the need for Routing Replay and its associated overhead — allows the MoE model to leverage its full capacity without artificial constraints, which is particularly important for Qwen3 models that employ MoE architectures.

The success of GSPO in the Qwen3 training pipeline demonstrates that the algorithmic choices in GSPO are not merely theoretically motivated but practically impactful. The transition from GRPO to GSPO in the Qwen3 training pipeline represents a shift from a token-level optimization paradigm that is ill-posed for sequence-level rewards to a sequence-level optimization paradigm that properly aligns the unit of optimization with the unit of reward. This alignment, combined with the more reliable gradient estimates and the more effective clipping mechanism, enables the sustained and stable scaling of RL training that is necessary to push the capability boundaries of large language models.

The application of GSPO to Qwen3 models also has broader implications for the field of reinforcement learning for language models. It demonstrates that the fundamental principles of importance sampling, when properly applied, can lead to substantial improvements in training stability and efficiency — improvements that are not merely incremental but transformative, enabling new levels of scale and capability. As RL continues to play a pivotal role in the development of language models, GSPO provides a robust and scalable algorithmic foundation that will enable continued advancement, laying the groundwork for future breakthroughs in machine intelligence.
