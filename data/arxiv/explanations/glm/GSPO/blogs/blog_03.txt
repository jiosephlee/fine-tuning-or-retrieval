\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

# More Clipping, Better Results: The Counterintuitive Secret Behind GSPO's Training Efficiency

When you think about training reinforcement learning algorithms for large language models, more clipping sounds like it should hurt. After all, clipping exists to exclude data from gradient estimation—throwing away information. So it was more than a little surprising when the authors of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) discovered that their algorithm clips roughly two orders of magnitude more tokens than the baseline GRPO algorithm, yet still achieves higher training efficiency and better benchmark performance.

This counterintuitive result is one of the most revealing findings in the paper, and it tells us something fundamental about the quality of learning signals in RL for language models.

## The Setup: Token-Level vs. Sequence-Level Importance Ratios

To understand why this result matters, we need to look at how GSPO and GRPO differ. Both algorithms generate a group of $G$ responses to each query and compute relative advantages based on the rewards of those responses. The key difference lies in how they define the importance ratio—the weight used to correct for the off-policy nature of the training data.

GRPO, following the PPO tradition, defines the importance ratio at the token level:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$$

Each token gets its own importance weight, and clipping is applied per token. If a token's ratio falls outside the range $[1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon]$, it gets clipped.

GSPO takes a fundamentally different approach. It defines the importance ratio at the sequence level using the geometric mean of per-token likelihood ratios:

$$s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}\right)$$

Clipping is then applied to this sequence-level ratio. If an entire response is too far off-policy, the whole response gets clipped—not individual tokens within it.

## The Problem with Token-Level Importance Weights

The paper identifies a fundamental flaw in GRPO's design: the token-level importance weight is a misapplication of importance sampling. The principle of importance sampling relies on averaging over multiple samples from the behavior distribution for the importance weight to effectively correct for the distributional mismatch. But GRPO applies the importance weight $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$ at each token position based on a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from the next-token distribution. This fails to perform the intended distribution correction and instead introduces high-variance noise into the training gradients.

The gradient analysis in the paper makes this concrete. In GRPO, the gradient weights each token's log-likelihood gradient by its individual importance weight:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})\right]$$

These weights vary across tokens—falling in the range $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ for positive advantages or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ for negative advantages—and their unequal impact accumulates over long sequences, leading to unpredictable consequences as training progresses.

In contrast, GSPO weights all tokens in a response equally:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})\right]$$

The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ acts as a single, unified weight for the entire response. Every token's gradient is scaled by the same factor, eliminating the instability that arises from unequal per-token weighting.

## The Two-Orders-of-Magnitude Clipping Gap

Here is where the counterintuitive finding comes in. The authors observed that the fraction of clipped tokens differs by two orders of magnitude between GSPO and GRPO. This disparity holds regardless of how the clipping ranges are adjusted. GSPO uses much tighter clipping ranges (left and right at 3e-4 and 4e-4, respectively, compared to GRPO's 0.2 and 0.27), but the magnitude difference in clipping fractions persists even when these ranges are varied.

Despite clipping vastly more tokens and consequently using fewer samples for gradient estimation, GSPO still achieves higher training efficiency. Under the same training compute and consumed queries, GSPO delivers better training accuracy and benchmark performance across AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces evaluations.

## Why Clipping More Can Be Better

This result flips conventional intuition on its head, but it makes perfect sense once you understand what is being clipped and why.

In GRPO, the token-level importance ratios are noisy. They fluctuate based on single-token probability estimates that do not meaningfully correct for the off-policy distributional mismatch. When you clip at the token level in GRPO, you are applying a band-aid to individual noisy estimates—removing the worst offenders but still leaving behind a gradient signal contaminated by the remaining unclipped but still unreliable weights.

GSPO, by contrast, operates at the sequence level. The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ has a clear theoretical meaning: it reflects how far an entire response deviates from the old policy to the current policy. This aligns naturally with the sequence-level reward. When GSPO clips a response, it is making a principled decision: this entire response is too far off-policy to provide a reliable learning signal, so exclude it entirely.

The fact that GSPO clips more aggressively is not a sign of inefficiency—it is a sign of precision. The algorithm is correctly identifying and excluding responses that would contribute unreliable gradients. What remains is a cleaner, more consistent learning signal. The two-orders-of-magnitude gap in clipping fractions is essentially a diagnostic: it reveals just how noisy GRPO's token-level gradient estimates actually are, and how much of GRPO's training is driven by unreliable per-token weights that happen to fall within the clipping range but still contribute noise.

## What This Tells Us About RL Learning Signals

This finding carries a broader lesson for RL in language models. The unit of optimization should match the unit of reward. Since rewards are granted to entire sequences, applying off-policy correction at the token level introduces a mismatch that manifests as gradient noise. The clipping mechanism, rather than masking this problem, partially exposes it—GSPO's willingness to clip far more data is actually a feature, not a bug, because the data being clipped was contributing noise rather than signal.

The result also suggests that sample exploitation in RL for LLMs is not about using every available token for gradient estimation. It is about ensuring that the tokens you do use are weighted by a reliable, theoretically grounded importance ratio. GSPO's sequence-level approach provides this reliability, and the empirical results—stable training, continuous performance improvement through scaling, and successful application to the Qwen3 models—confirm that a cleaner signal beats a noisier one, even when the cleaner signal comes from a much smaller fraction of the data.
