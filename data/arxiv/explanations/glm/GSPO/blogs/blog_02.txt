# Taming the Experts: How GSPO Solved Mixture-of-Experts Training Instability Without Band-Aids

Training large language models with reinforcement learning is hard enough. Training *Mixture-of-Experts* (MoE) models with RL? That's been a recipe for instability—until now. One of the most practically impactful contributions of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) is that it quietly solves a problem that previously required awkward, costly workarounds: the notorious expert-activation volatility in MoE RL training. Let's unpack what that problem is, how people used to patch it, and how GSPO makes the patch obsolete.

## The Problem: Experts Keep Switching on You

MoE models are sparse by design. For any given token, only a subset of "experts" (sub-networks) within each layer actually activate. This is what makes them efficient—but it's also what makes RL training tricky.

When you sample a response $y$ from an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and then perform a gradient update to obtain a new policy $\pi_\theta$, the experts that activate for the *same response* can change significantly. The paper reports a concrete number: for the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, roughly 10% of the experts activated under the new policy differ from those activated under the old policy for the same rollout sample. And this problem gets worse as MoE models get deeper.

Why does this matter? Because algorithms like GRPO rely on token-level importance ratios:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$$

These ratios are supposed to correct for the distributional mismatch between the old and new policies. But when the underlying network activations keep shifting between the old and new policy, these ratios fluctuate drastically and lose their meaning. The importance sampling correction that GRPO depends on becomes unreliable noise, and training fails to converge properly.

## The Old Workaround: Routing Replay

Before GSPO, the solution was a technique called **Routing Replay**. The idea is straightforward: cache which experts were activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ for each token, and then force the new policy $\pi_\theta$ to use those *same* routing decisions when computing the importance ratios. By sharing the same activated network between old and new policies for each token, you restore stability to the token-level importance ratios and ensure that gradient updates are applied to a consistent set of activated experts.

It works—but at a cost. Routing Replay incurs additional memory and communication overhead from caching and replaying routing modes. Worse, it artificially constrains the model: by reusing old routing patterns rather than letting the model route freely, you limit the actual capacity of the MoE during training. You're essentially telling the model, "Use these experts because that's what you did before," even if the updated policy would naturally prefer different ones.

In short, Routing Replay is a band-aid: it stops the bleeding but restricts the model and adds engineering complexity.

## The GSPO Solution: Focus on the Sequence, Not the Tokens

GSPO takes a fundamentally different approach. Instead of trying to stabilize token-level importance ratios (which are inherently fragile in MoE settings), it redefines the importance ratio at the *sequence level*:

$$s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}\right)$$

This is the geometric mean of the per-token likelihood ratios—a single scalar per response that captures how far the sequence has drifted from the old policy.

The key insight for MoE training is that while individual token likelihoods $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ can swing wildly as expert routing shifts, the *sequence likelihood* $\pi_\theta(y_i | x)$ remains much more stable. The MoE model always maintains its overall language modeling capability, so the total probability it assigns to a given response doesn't fluctuate drastically even if the internal routing changes. GSPO only cares about the sequence likelihood, making it insensitive to which specific experts fire for each token.

The result? GSPO computes importance ratios conventionally—no routing caches, no replay—and converges normally. The expert-activation volatility that plagued GRPO is simply irrelevant to GSPO's optimization signal.

## Why This Matters Beyond MoE

Eliminating the need for Routing Replay has benefits that ripple outward. Routing Replay added overhead and constrained model capacity; removing it lets the MoE model leverage its full capacity during RL training and simplifies the training pipeline.

There's also an infrastructure benefit. In practice, training engines (like Megatron) and inference engines (like SGLang or vLLM) have precision discrepancies, so practitioners often recompute response likelihoods under the old policy using the training engine. But because GSPO operates on sequence-level likelihoods—which are far more tolerant of minor precision differences than token-level ones—it becomes feasible to directly use the likelihoods returned by the inference engine. This avoids the expensive recomputation step, which is especially valuable in scenarios like partial rollout, multi-turn RL, and training-inference disaggregated frameworks.

## The Bigger Picture

The MoE stability story is a microcosm of GSPO's broader philosophy: the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward. Rewards are assigned to entire sequences, so the importance ratio and clipping mechanism should operate at the sequence level too. When you apply corrections at the token level—as GRPO does—you introduce high-variance noise that accumulates over long responses and, in MoE models, is further amplified by routing volatility.

GSPO's sequence-level approach doesn't just fix the MoE problem in isolation. It addresses the root cause: a misapplication of importance sampling weights. By grounding the importance ratio in sequence likelihood, GSPO provides a more reliable learning signal, clips entire responses rather than individual tokens, and stabilizes training across the board. The fact that this simultaneously solves the MoE routing problem, eliminates Routing Replay, and simplifies RL infrastructure is a testament to getting the fundamentals right.

The paper notes that these merits directly contributed to the performance improvements in the latest Qwen3 models. When the algorithmic foundation is solid, the engineering becomes simpler, the model can use its full capacity, and scaling RL becomes tractable. Sometimes the best fix for a complex problem isn't a more elaborate workaround—it's a simpler, more principled approach that makes the workaround unnecessary.
