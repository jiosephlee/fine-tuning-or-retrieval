# From Tokens to Sequences: Why AI Training Was Breaking Down—And How a Simple Shift Fixed It

## The Instability Problem in RL Training
Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a pivotal tool for scaling large language models (LLMs), enabling them to tackle sophisticated problems in math and programming through deeper reasoning. However, scaling RL brings a major challenge: maintaining stable training dynamics. Current state-of-the-art algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) often suffer from severe stability issues, leading to catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. Once this collapse occurs, resuming training is often unavailing, even when reverting to previous checkpoints or tweaking hyperparameters. 

## The Token-Level Trap in GRPO
Why does GRPO break down? The root cause lies in a fundamental misapplication of importance sampling weights. Importance sampling is a technique used to estimate the expectation of a function under a target distribution by re-weighting samples drawn from a behavior distribution. This re-weighting relies on averaging over multiple samples to correct for distributional mismatches.

In GRPO, this importance weight is applied at the token level. The importance ratio for a token $y_t$ is defined as $w_{t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{t} | x, y_{<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{t} | x,y_{<t})}$. Because this weight is based on a single sample from the next-token distribution, it fails to perform its intended distribution-correction role. Instead, it introduces high-variance noise into the training gradients. As LLMs generate longer responses, this noise accumulates and is exacerbated by the clipping mechanism, ultimately precipitating model collapse.

## Matching the Unit of Optimization to the Reward
The core insight to fix this is straightforward: the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of the reward. In language generation, a reward $r(x, y)$ is granted to an entire sequence $y$, not to individual tokens. Applying off-policy correction at the token level is inherently problematic. If the reward is sequence-level, the optimization and importance sampling should also be sequence-level.

## Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)
To address this, the researchers propose Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO). Instead of looking at individual tokens, GSPO defines the importance ratio based on sequence likelihood. The sequence-level importance weight $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y | x)}$ has a clear theoretical meaning: it reflects how far a response $y$ sampled from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ deviates from the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$.

GSPO defines its importance ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$ using length normalization to keep the numerical range stable:
$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right).$$

By applying clipping to entire responses rather than individual tokens, GSPO ensures that the optimization aligns perfectly with sequence-level rewarding.

## A Tale of Two Gradients
The fundamental distinction between GSPO and GRPO lies in how they weight the gradients of the log likelihoods of tokens. 

In GRPO, the gradient weights tokens according to their respective token-level importance weights. These unequal weights can vary significantly, and their unpredictable impact accumulates over time, leading to instability.

In contrast, the gradient of the GSPO objective can be derived as:
$$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \widehat{A}_{i} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right].$$

Notice that in GSPO, all the tokens in a response are weighted equally. The sequence-level importance ratio scales the average of the token gradients uniformly, completely eliminating the instability factor caused by unequal token weighting in GRPO. 

Interestingly, empirical results show that GSPO clips significantly more tokens than GRPO—by up to two orders of magnitude. Despite using fewer tokens for gradient estimation, GSPO achieves higher training efficiency, proving that GRPO's token-level estimates are inherently noisy and inefficient for sample exploitation.

## Resolving Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Instability
GSPO's sequence-level approach provides a massive benefit for training Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. MoE models suffer from "expert-activation volatility": after a gradient update, the specific experts activated for a given response can change significantly. This causes token-level importance ratios to fluctuate wildly, breaking GRPO training. 

Previously, this required a complex "Routing Replay" strategy to cache and reuse old routing modes. Because GSPO only cares about the overall sequence likelihood and is insensitive to individual token likelihoods, it is unaffected by expert-activation volatility. GSPO entirely eliminates the need for Routing Replay, simplifying the training process and allowing the MoE model to leverage its full capacity without artificial constraints.

## Conclusion
By shifting the focus from tokens to sequences, GSPO aligns the unit of optimization with the unit of reward. This simple but theoretically grounded shift fixes the fundamental instability of GRPO, resulting in dramatically more stable and efficient RL training. The efficacy of GSPO has been successfully applied to the RL training of the latest Qwen3 models, paving the way for the continued advancement of large-scale RL with language models.



# Taming the Experts: How GSPO Solved Mixture-of-Experts Training Instability Without Band-Aids

Training large language models with reinforcement learning is hard enough. Training *Mixture-of-Experts* (MoE) models with RL? That's been a recipe for instability—until now. One of the most practically impactful contributions of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) is that it quietly solves a problem that previously required awkward, costly workarounds: the notorious expert-activation volatility in MoE RL training. Let's unpack what that problem is, how people used to patch it, and how GSPO makes the patch obsolete.

## The Problem: Experts Keep Switching on You

MoE models are sparse by design. For any given token, only a subset of "experts" (sub-networks) within each layer actually activate. This is what makes them efficient—but it's also what makes RL training tricky.

When you sample a response $y$ from an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and then perform a gradient update to obtain a new policy $\pi_\theta$, the experts that activate for the *same response* can change significantly. The paper reports a concrete number: for the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, roughly 10% of the experts activated under the new policy differ from those activated under the old policy for the same rollout sample. And this problem gets worse as MoE models get deeper.

Why does this matter? Because algorithms like GRPO rely on token-level importance ratios:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$$

These ratios are supposed to correct for the distributional mismatch between the old and new policies. But when the underlying network activations keep shifting between the old and new policy, these ratios fluctuate drastically and lose their meaning. The importance sampling correction that GRPO depends on becomes unreliable noise, and training fails to converge properly.

## The Old Workaround: Routing Replay

Before GSPO, the solution was a technique called **Routing Replay**. The idea is straightforward: cache which experts were activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ for each token, and then force the new policy $\pi_\theta$ to use those *same* routing decisions when computing the importance ratios. By sharing the same activated network between old and new policies for each token, you restore stability to the token-level importance ratios and ensure that gradient updates are applied to a consistent set of activated experts.

It works—but at a cost. Routing Replay incurs additional memory and communication overhead from caching and replaying routing modes. Worse, it artificially constrains the model: by reusing old routing patterns rather than letting the model route freely, you limit the actual capacity of the MoE during training. You're essentially telling the model, "Use these experts because that's what you did before," even if the updated policy would naturally prefer different ones.

In short, Routing Replay is a band-aid: it stops the bleeding but restricts the model and adds engineering complexity.

## The GSPO Solution: Focus on the Sequence, Not the Tokens

GSPO takes a fundamentally different approach. Instead of trying to stabilize token-level importance ratios (which are inherently fragile in MoE settings), it redefines the importance ratio at the *sequence level*:

$$s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}\right)$$

This is the geometric mean of the per-token likelihood ratios—a single scalar per response that captures how far the sequence has drifted from the old policy.

The key insight for MoE training is that while individual token likelihoods $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ can swing wildly as expert routing shifts, the *sequence likelihood* $\pi_\theta(y_i | x)$ remains much more stable. The MoE model always maintains its overall language modeling capability, so the total probability it assigns to a given response doesn't fluctuate drastically even if the internal routing changes. GSPO only cares about the sequence likelihood, making it insensitive to which specific experts fire for each token.

The result? GSPO computes importance ratios conventionally—no routing caches, no replay—and converges normally. The expert-activation volatility that plagued GRPO is simply irrelevant to GSPO's optimization signal.

## Why This Matters Beyond MoE

Eliminating the need for Routing Replay has benefits that ripple outward. Routing Replay added overhead and constrained model capacity; removing it lets the MoE model leverage its full capacity during RL training and simplifies the training pipeline.

There's also an infrastructure benefit. In practice, training engines (like Megatron) and inference engines (like SGLang or vLLM) have precision discrepancies, so practitioners often recompute response likelihoods under the old policy using the training engine. But because GSPO operates on sequence-level likelihoods—which are far more tolerant of minor precision differences than token-level ones—it becomes feasible to directly use the likelihoods returned by the inference engine. This avoids the expensive recomputation step, which is especially valuable in scenarios like partial rollout, multi-turn RL, and training-inference disaggregated frameworks.

## The Bigger Picture

The MoE stability story is a microcosm of GSPO's broader philosophy: the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward. Rewards are assigned to entire sequences, so the importance ratio and clipping mechanism should operate at the sequence level too. When you apply corrections at the token level—as GRPO does—you introduce high-variance noise that accumulates over long responses and, in MoE models, is further amplified by routing volatility.

GSPO's sequence-level approach doesn't just fix the MoE problem in isolation. It addresses the root cause: a misapplication of importance sampling weights. By grounding the importance ratio in sequence likelihood, GSPO provides a more reliable learning signal, clips entire responses rather than individual tokens, and stabilizes training across the board. The fact that this simultaneously solves the MoE routing problem, eliminates Routing Replay, and simplifies RL infrastructure is a testament to getting the fundamentals right.

The paper notes that these merits directly contributed to the performance improvements in the latest Qwen3 models. When the algorithmic foundation is solid, the engineering becomes simpler, the model can use its full capacity, and scaling RL becomes tractable. Sometimes the best fix for a complex problem isn't a more elaborate workaround—it's a simpler, more principled approach that makes the workaround unnecessary.



# More Clipping, Better Results: The Counterintuitive Secret Behind GSPO's Training Efficiency

When you think about training reinforcement learning algorithms for large language models, more clipping sounds like it should hurt. After all, clipping exists to exclude data from gradient estimation—throwing away information. So it was more than a little surprising when the authors of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) discovered that their algorithm clips roughly two orders of magnitude more tokens than the baseline GRPO algorithm, yet still achieves higher training efficiency and better benchmark performance.

This counterintuitive result is one of the most revealing findings in the paper, and it tells us something fundamental about the quality of learning signals in RL for language models.

## The Setup: Token-Level vs. Sequence-Level Importance Ratios

To understand why this result matters, we need to look at how GSPO and GRPO differ. Both algorithms generate a group of $G$ responses to each query and compute relative advantages based on the rewards of those responses. The key difference lies in how they define the importance ratio—the weight used to correct for the off-policy nature of the training data.

GRPO, following the PPO tradition, defines the importance ratio at the token level:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$$

Each token gets its own importance weight, and clipping is applied per token. If a token's ratio falls outside the range $[1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon]$, it gets clipped.

GSPO takes a fundamentally different approach. It defines the importance ratio at the sequence level using the geometric mean of per-token likelihood ratios:

$$s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}\right)$$

Clipping is then applied to this sequence-level ratio. If an entire response is too far off-policy, the whole response gets clipped—not individual tokens within it.

## The Problem with Token-Level Importance Weights

The paper identifies a fundamental flaw in GRPO's design: the token-level importance weight is a misapplication of importance sampling. The principle of importance sampling relies on averaging over multiple samples from the behavior distribution for the importance weight to effectively correct for the distributional mismatch. But GRPO applies the importance weight $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$ at each token position based on a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from the next-token distribution. This fails to perform the intended distribution correction and instead introduces high-variance noise into the training gradients.

The gradient analysis in the paper makes this concrete. In GRPO, the gradient weights each token's log-likelihood gradient by its individual importance weight:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})\right]$$

These weights vary across tokens—falling in the range $(0, 1+\varepsilon]$ for positive advantages or $[1-\varepsilon, +\infty)$ for negative advantages—and their unequal impact accumulates over long sequences, leading to unpredictable consequences as training progresses.

In contrast, GSPO weights all tokens in a response equally:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})\right]$$

The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ acts as a single, unified weight for the entire response. Every token's gradient is scaled by the same factor, eliminating the instability that arises from unequal per-token weighting.

## The Two-Orders-of-Magnitude Clipping Gap

Here is where the counterintuitive finding comes in. The authors observed that the fraction of clipped tokens differs by two orders of magnitude between GSPO and GRPO. This disparity holds regardless of how the clipping ranges are adjusted. GSPO uses much tighter clipping ranges (left and right at 3e-4 and 4e-4, respectively, compared to GRPO's 0.2 and 0.27), but the magnitude difference in clipping fractions persists even when these ranges are varied.

Despite clipping vastly more tokens and consequently using fewer samples for gradient estimation, GSPO still achieves higher training efficiency. Under the same training compute and consumed queries, GSPO delivers better training accuracy and benchmark performance across AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces evaluations.

## Why Clipping More Can Be Better

This result flips conventional intuition on its head, but it makes perfect sense once you understand what is being clipped and why.

In GRPO, the token-level importance ratios are noisy. They fluctuate based on single-token probability estimates that do not meaningfully correct for the off-policy distributional mismatch. When you clip at the token level in GRPO, you are applying a band-aid to individual noisy estimates—removing the worst offenders but still leaving behind a gradient signal contaminated by the remaining unclipped but still unreliable weights.

GSPO, by contrast, operates at the sequence level. The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ has a clear theoretical meaning: it reflects how far an entire response deviates from the old policy to the current policy. This aligns naturally with the sequence-level reward. When GSPO clips a response, it is making a principled decision: this entire response is too far off-policy to provide a reliable learning signal, so exclude it entirely.

The fact that GSPO clips more aggressively is not a sign of inefficiency—it is a sign of precision. The algorithm is correctly identifying and excluding responses that would contribute unreliable gradients. What remains is a cleaner, more consistent learning signal. The two-orders-of-magnitude gap in clipping fractions is essentially a diagnostic: it reveals just how noisy GRPO's token-level gradient estimates actually are, and how much of GRPO's training is driven by unreliable per-token weights that happen to fall within the clipping range but still contribute noise.

## What This Tells Us About RL Learning Signals

This finding carries a broader lesson for RL in language models. The unit of optimization should match the unit of reward. Since rewards are granted to entire sequences, applying off-policy correction at the token level introduces a mismatch that manifests as gradient noise. The clipping mechanism, rather than masking this problem, partially exposes it—GSPO's willingness to clip far more data is actually a feature, not a bug, because the data being clipped was contributing noise rather than signal.

The result also suggests that sample exploitation in RL for LLMs is not about using every available token for gradient estimation. It is about ensuring that the tokens you do use are weighted by a reliable, theoretically grounded importance ratio. GSPO's sequence-level approach provides this reliability, and the empirical results—stable training, continuous performance improvement through scaling, and successful application to the Qwen3 models—confirm that a cleaner signal beats a noisier one, even when the cleaner signal comes from a much smaller fraction of the data.



# Inside Qwen3: How a New Reinforcement Learning Algorithm Unlocked the Next Leap in AI Reasoning

Reinforcement learning has become the engine behind the reasoning capabilities of modern language models. From competition-level mathematics to complex programming tasks, large-scale RL training pushes models to think deeper and longer. But as models grow larger and responses grow longer, a fundamental problem has been quietly undermining progress: training instability. Models would sometimes collapse irreversibly mid-training, wasting enormous amounts of compute and stalling capability advances.

This is the story of how a new algorithm called **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** solved these stability issues at scale, powered the latest Qwen3 models, and became what its creators call a "scalable algorithmic cornerstone" for the future of RL with language models.

## The Bottleneck: When GRPO Breaks Down

The dominant RL algorithm for training large language models today is **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**. GRPO improved upon Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) by eliminating the need for a separate value model — a major memory and computational burden. Instead, GRPO computes relative advantages by comparing rewards across a group of responses to the same query.

Despite its elegance, GRPO has a critical flaw that becomes catastrophic at scale. The issue lies in how GRPO applies **importance sampling** — a technique for correcting the mismatch between the policy that generated the data and the policy being optimized.

Importance sampling works by re-weighting samples drawn from a behavior distribution $\pi_{\text{beh}}$ to estimate expectations under a target distribution $\pi_{\text{tar}}$. The key requirement is that the importance weight $\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}$ must be computed over many samples to effectively correct the distributional mismatch.

GRPO, however, applies importance weights at the **token level**. For each token $y_{i,t}$ in a response, it computes:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$$

Since this weight is based on a single sample from each next-token distribution, it fails to perform proper distribution correction. Instead, it injects **high-variance noise** into the training gradients. This noise accumulates over long sequences and is amplified by GRPO's clipping mechanism, ultimately leading to catastrophic and irreversible model collapse.

The authors observed that once collapse occurs, nothing helps — not reverting to previous checkpoints, tuning clipping ranges, extending generation length, or switching queries. The problem is fundamental.

## The Core Insight: Match the Unit of Optimization to the Unit of Reward

This observation led to a deceptively simple principle: **the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of reward**. Since rewards are granted to entire sequences, applying off-policy correction at the token level is inherently problematic.

GSPO takes this principle seriously. Instead of computing importance ratios per token, GSPO defines the importance ratio based on **sequence likelihood**:

$$s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}\right)$$

The length normalization (the $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ exponent) is crucial. Without it, likelihood changes in just a few tokens could cause wild fluctuations in the sequence-level ratio, and responses of different lengths would need different clipping ranges. Normalization keeps the ratio within a unified numerical range and reduces variance.

GSPO then applies clipping at the **sequence level**, excluding entire overly off-policy responses from gradient estimation rather than clipping individual tokens. The full objective is:

$$\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot | x)} \left[\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G \min\left(s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i, \text{clip}(s_i(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon) \widehat{A}_i\right)\right]$$

where the advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ is computed as the normalized reward within the group of responses, just as in GRPO.

## Why This Matters: A Gradient-Level Comparison

The fundamental distinction between GSPO and GRPO becomes clear when comparing their gradients. In GRPO, each token's gradient is weighted by its own importance ratio:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})\right]$$

These weights vary across tokens — sometimes ranging from near zero to $1+\varepsilon$ — creating unpredictable, unequal gradient contributions that accumulate over long sequences.

In GSPO, all tokens in a response share the **same** weight:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})\right]$$

Every token in the response gets equal treatment. The sequence-level importance ratio acts as a uniform multiplier on the entire response's gradient, eliminating the instability factor that plagued GRPO.

## Real-World Results: Qwen3 and Beyond

The proof is in the training. The authors applied GSPO to a cold-start model fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, comparing against carefully tuned GRPO as a baseline. Each batch of rollout data was partitioned into four mini-batches for gradient updates, creating a realistic off-policy training setting.

The results were striking. GSPO maintained stable training throughout, delivering continuous performance improvement as training compute increased, the query set was regularly updated, and generation length was extended. On three challenging benchmarks, GSPO demonstrated superior training efficiency:

- **AIME'24** (competition mathematics): Average Pass@1 over 32 samplings
- **LiveCodeBench** (code generation, 202410-202502): Average Pass@1 over 8 samplings
- **CodeForces** (competitive programming): Elo Rating

Under the same training compute and consumed queries, GSPO consistently achieved better training accuracy and benchmark performance than GRPO. Most importantly, GSPO was successfully applied to the RL training of the latest **Qwen3 models**, contributing to their exceptional performance improvements.

## A Counterintuitive Finding: More Clipping, Better Results

One of the most curious observations from the experiments involves clipping fractions. GSPO clips at the sequence level rather than the token level, and the authors found that GSPO clips roughly **two orders of magnitude more** tokens than GRPO. Despite using far fewer tokens for gradient estimation, GSPO still achieves higher training efficiency.

This counterintuitive finding — clipping more tokens yet training better — suggests that GRPO's token-level gradient estimates are inherently noisy and inefficient. Many of the tokens GRPO uses for training are actually injecting noise rather than useful signal. GSPO's sequence-level approach provides a more reliable learning signal by filtering out entire off-policy responses while keeping the on-policy ones intact.

## Solving the MoE Stability Problem

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models present unique stability challenges for RL training due to their sparse activation. With GRPO, the authors discovered severe **expert-activation volatility**: after each gradient update, roughly 10% of the experts activated for the same response would change in the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model. This made token-level importance ratios fluctuate drastically, further invalidating them and preventing normal convergence.

Their previous workaround was a strategy called **Routing Replay**, which caches the activated experts under the old policy and reuses those routing modes when computing importance ratios under the new policy. While effective, this approach adds memory and communication overhead and artificially limits the model's capacity.

GSPO resolves this problem fundamentally. Because it focuses on sequence likelihood rather than individual token likelihoods, it is insensitive to which specific experts activate. The MoE model always maintains its language modeling capability, so the sequence likelihood remains stable even as expert routing shifts. GSPO eliminates the need for Routing Replay entirely, simplifying the training process and allowing the model to leverage its full capacity.

## Simplifying RL Infrastructure

GSPO also has practical benefits for RL infrastructure. In practice, precision discrepancies between training engines (like Megatron) and inference engines (like SGLang and vLLM) typically require recomputing response likelihoods under the old policy using the training engine. Since GSPO operates on sequence-level likelihoods — which are much more tolerant of precision discrepancies than token-level likelihoods — it becomes feasible to directly use likelihoods returned by the inference engine.

This seemingly small change avoids the need for expensive recomputation and is especially beneficial in scenarios like partial rollout, multi-turn RL, and training-inference disaggregated frameworks. The result is a simpler, more efficient RL pipeline.

## Looking Forward: A Scalable Algorithmic Cornerstone

The story of GSPO is ultimately about removing the barriers that have prevented RL from scaling with language models. By grounding the importance ratio in sequence likelihood — the theoretically correct unit for importance sampling in this context — GSPO eliminates the noise source that caused GRPO to collapse. By resolving MoE instability natively, it removes the need for complex workarounds. By simplifying infrastructure requirements, it reduces the engineering burden of large-scale RL.

The authors envision GSPO as a robust and scalable algorithmic foundation that will enable continued advancement of large-scale RL training. As the latest Qwen3 models have demonstrated, GSPO can unleash the power of RL scaling — and with a stable algorithmic cornerstone in place, the path forward involves scaling RL further and anticipating the fundamental advances in intelligence that result.

For researchers and engineers working on RL with language models, GSPO offers a clear lesson: getting the fundamentals of importance sampling right matters more than clever engineering workarounds. Sometimes the most impactful innovation is recognizing when the prevailing approach is ill-posed and having the courage to redesign from first principles.



# Less Infrastructure, More Intelligence: How GSPO Could Simplify the AI Training Pipeline

When scaling reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models (LLMs), the spotlight usually falls on algorithmic improvements, reward design, or model architecture. However, the underlying plumbing—the RL infrastructure—often dictates what is practically possible. A major infrastructural bottleneck arises from numerical precision discrepancies between training engines (like Megatron) and inference engines (like vLLM or SGLang). 

Recently introduced alongside the latest Qwen3 models, Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) is a new RL algorithm that not only improves training stability and performance but also has the potential to dramatically simplify this infrastructure overhead.

## The Hidden Cost of RL Training: Precision Discrepancies

In algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), policy updates rely on token-level importance ratios. For a token $y_t$ generated by the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, the importance ratio is calculated as $w_{t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{t} | x, y_{<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{t} | x,y_{<t})}$. Because token-level probabilities are highly sensitive, even minute precision differences between the inference engine that generated the tokens and the training engine computing the gradients can introduce significant noise into the optimization process.

To mitigate this, practitioners typically have to use the training engine to recompute the likelihoods of sampled responses under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. This recomputation step is computationally expensive and severely complicates the training pipeline, especially in distributed environments.

## Enter GSPO: Sequence-Level Optimization

GSPO fundamentally rethinks how importance ratios are calculated. Instead of applying importance weights at the token level, GSPO defines the importance ratio based on sequence likelihood. 

Specifically, GSPO calculates the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ for a response $y_i$ as:

$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right)$$

By taking the geometric mean of token-level ratios, GSPO effectively averages out the variance. The length normalization ensures that the importance ratio remains within a unified numerical range regardless of response length.

## Streamlining the Infrastructure

Because GSPO operates on sequence-level likelihoods, it is much more tolerant of the minor numerical precision discrepancies between training and inference engines. The averaging effect of the sequence likelihood acts as a buffer against the extreme fluctuations that can occur at the individual token level.

This robustness has a profound implication for RL infrastructure: it makes it possible to directly use the likelihoods returned by the inference engine for optimization, thereby avoiding the need for recomputation with the training engine entirely.

By eliminating this recomputation step, GSPO removes a significant computational and logistical burden. This streamlining is particularly beneficial for:
- **Partial rollout**: Efficiently managing and updating policies without a full synchronous recomputation pass on the training side.
- **Multi-turn RL**: Reducing the overhead of calculating likelihoods for long, multi-turn interactions where inference and training engines might frequently drift.
- **Training-inference disaggregated frameworks**: Allowing a cleaner separation between generation and training, since the training engine no longer needs to redundantly "double-check" the inference engine's probability calculations.

## A Leaner Path to Intelligence

While the paper highlights GSPO's superior training stability and performance—particularly its ability to stabilize Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) RL training without complex workarounds like Routing Replay—its infrastructural benefits are equally compelling. 

By aligning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward (the sequence), GSPO not only fixes the theoretical misapplication of importance sampling found in token-level methods but also clears the way for leaner, simpler RL pipelines. As the AI community continues to scale RL to unlock deeper reasoning capabilities, algorithms like GSPO ensure that we spend less time fighting infrastructure overhead and more time scaling intelligence.
