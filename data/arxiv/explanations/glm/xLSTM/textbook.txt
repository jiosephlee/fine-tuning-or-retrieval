# Introduction: The LSTM Legacy and Its Limitations

#

## The Vanishing Gradient Problem in Recurrent Neural Networks

To understand why Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks were invented, we must first confront a fundamental obstacle that plagued early recurrent neural networks (RNNs): the vanishing gradient problem. When training an RNN using gradient-based methods, the network must propagate error signals backward through time across many sequential steps. Each step multiplies the gradient by a factor related to the recurrent weights and activation function derivatives. When sequences are long, these repeated multiplications cause the gradient to either shrink exponentially toward zero (vanishing) or grow exponentially toward infinity (exploding). The vanishing gradient problem is especially insidious because it prevents the network from learning dependencies between distant elements in a sequence. An RNN that cannot propagate gradients across hundreds of time steps simply cannot learn to associate a word at the beginning of a paragraph with a word at the end. The network's ability to store and retrieve information over long timescales is fundamentally compromised.

This problem was identified and analyzed in the early 1990s by Hochreiter, who formalized the diagnosis of vanishing gradients in recurrent networks. The core mathematical issue is that if a recurrent connection has a weight with magnitude less than one, repeated application of that weight across time steps causes the gradient signal to decay exponentially. If the weight has magnitude greater than one, the signal explodes. Maintaining gradients at a useful magnitude across long sequences requires a mechanism that does not rely on repeated multiplication by potentially small or large factors. This insight directly motivated the design of the LSTM, which introduced a structural solution to the vanishing gradient problem rather than relying on careful initialization or clipping strategies.

#

## The Core LSTM Ideas: Constant Error Carousel and Gating

The LSTM solved the vanishing gradient problem through two interlocking ideas: the constant error carousel and gating. The constant error carousel is the central mechanism by which information persists across time steps through an additive update rather than a multiplicative one. The core update equation of the LSTM is:

$$c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t, \quad h_t = o_t \, \psi(c_t)$$

Here, $c_t$ is the cell state at time step $t$, which serves as the network's memory. The cell state is updated additively: the previous cell state $c_{t-1}$ is scaled by the forget gate $f_t$ and added to the cell input $z_t$ scaled by the input gate $i_t$. The hidden state $h_t$ is then derived from the cell state by applying a squashing function $\psi$ and modulating the result with the output gate $o_t$. The crucial insight is that the gradient flowing through $c_t$ to $c_{t-1}$ passes through a simple additive pathway. When the forget gate $f_t$ is close to one, the gradient flows backward through time with minimal attenuation, creating what is called a constant error carousel: the error signal circulates through the recurrent structure without being multiplied by potentially vanishing factors at each step.

Gating is the second essential idea. The three gates—input, forget, and output—control the flow of information into, out of, and within the memory cell. Each gate is computed as a sigmoid activation, $\sigma(x) = 1/(1+\exp(-x))$, which produces values between zero and one. The input gate $i_t$ determines how much of the new input $z_t$ is written into the cell state. The forget gate $f_t$ determines how much of the previous cell state $c_{t-1}$ is retained. The output gate $o_t$ determines how much of the cell state is exposed as the hidden state. The cell input $z_t$ is itself a transformed version of the input $\mathbf{x}_t$ combined with the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$, passed through an activation function $\varphi$ (typically $\tanh$). Together, these gates give the LSTM the ability to selectively store, maintain, and retrieve information over arbitrarily long time spans, which is precisely what vanilla RNNs could not do.

The forget gate was a later addition to the original LSTM formulation, introduced by Gers and colleagues. Without a forget gate, the cell state could only accumulate information, with no mechanism for resetting or selectively discarding outdated content. The forget gate provides a principled way for the network to learn when to clear its memory, which is essential for tasks where context changes and old information becomes irrelevant. The full set of LSTM update equations includes pre-activations for each gate, each computed as a linear combination of the input $\mathbf{x}_t$, the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$, and a bias term, followed by the appropriate activation function. This structure—input, forget, and output gates controlling a scalar memory cell—became the standard LSTM formulation and has remained largely unchanged for over two decades.

## Historical Successes of LSTMs

Since their introduction in the 1990s, LSTMs have been applied to an extraordinary range of domains and have achieved remarkable successes across many of them. In text generation, LSTMs demonstrated the ability to produce coherent passages of text character by character, learning not only statistical regularities but also syntactic and semantic structure. In handwriting generation, LSTMs produced realistic handwritten sequences that captured the stylistic variations of human writing. These early demonstrations were compelling evidence that gated recurrent architectures could model complex sequential data with long-range dependencies.

One of the most impactful applications of LSTMs was in sequence-to-sequence machine translation. The encoder-decoder architecture, in which an LSTM encodes a source sentence into a fixed-length vector and another LSTM decodes that vector into a target sentence, became the dominant approach to translation before the Transformer era. This architecture demonstrated that LSTMs could capture the meaning of an entire sentence in their memory cells and then generate a translation in another language, a task that requires understanding long-range syntactic and semantic relationships. LSTMs were also applied to evaluating computer programs, where they processed source code character by character and learned to predict or assess program properties.

Beyond language, LSTMs were used for image captioning, where they generated textual descriptions of images by processing visual features extracted by convolutional networks. In hydrology, LSTMs were employed for rainfall-runoff modeling and flooding warning systems, where they processed time series of meteorological measurements to predict river discharge. These applications demonstrated that LSTMs were not limited to text but could handle any sequential data with complex temporal dependencies.

In reinforcement learning, LSTMs achieved some of the most impressive results in the field. The AlphaStar model, which played StarCraft II at grandmaster level, used LSTM-based architectures to process the long histories of game states that are essential for strategic decision-making. Similarly, the OpenAI Five model for Dota 2 relied on LSTMs to maintain context over the course of matches that could last tens of minutes. LSTMs were also used in the magnetic controller for nuclear fusion, where they controlled plasma in a tokamak reactor. These reinforcement learning applications highlighted a key strength of LSTMs: their ability to maintain relevant information over very long horizons and to use that information for sequential decision-making under uncertainty. LSTMs have stood the test of time and remain in use for highly relevant applications even as Transformers have dominated other domains.

#

## LSTM's Ability to Learn Abstractions

One of the most fascinating properties of LSTMs is their ability to learn abstractions—that is, to extract semantic information from sequential data and store it in their memory cells in structured, interpretable ways. This capability was revealed through analyses of trained LSTM models, which showed that individual memory cells developed specialized roles corresponding to meaningful linguistic and semantic concepts.

Number neurons were identified in LSTM language models, cells that specifically tracked numerical quantities in text. When processing a sequence containing numbers, these neurons would activate in patterns that reflected the numerical values, suggesting that the LSTM had learned to represent numeric information distinctly from other types of content. Syntax neurons were another discovery: cells that tracked syntactic structures such as whether a clause was open or closed, or whether a particular grammatical construction was in progress. These neurons effectively served as a stack or counter, maintaining syntactic state across long stretches of text.

Linguistic neurons extended this finding further, with individual cells responding to higher-level linguistic features such as topic, register, or discourse structure. Sentiment neurons were perhaps the most striking discovery: individual cells in LSTM language models were found to encode the emotional valence of text, with their activation levels correlating strongly with whether the input expressed positive or negative sentiment. Remarkably, these sentiment neurons emerged without any explicit supervision for sentiment; they arose simply from training on the next-token prediction objective. This finding demonstrated that LSTMs were not merely memorizing surface statistics but were learning to extract and represent abstract semantic properties of their input.

This capacity for abstraction is a fundamental reason why LSTMs remained competitive for so long and why they are worth revisiting. The ability to compress semantic information into scalar memory cells and to maintain that information over long sequences is a powerful form of inductive bias. The discovery of interpretable, functionally specialized neurons within LSTM models suggests that the architecture's design—gating, additive updates, and scalar memory—is well-suited to learning the kinds of abstractions that underlie language understanding and other cognitive tasks. This property motivates the question of whether the LSTM architecture, when extended and scaled, can recapture ground that has been lost to Transformers.

## Limitation 1: Inability to Revise Storage Decisions

Despite their many successes, LSTMs suffer from three main limitations that have constrained their applicability at scale. The first limitation is the inability to revise storage decisions. Once the LSTM has written information into its cell state, it cannot easily overwrite or revise that information when newer, more relevant information arrives. This problem stems from the use of sigmoid gates, which produce values in the range $(0, 1)$. When the input gate and forget gate use sigmoid activations, the update to the cell state is always a convex combination of the previous state and the new input. If the forget gate is close to one and the input gate is close to zero, the cell state barely changes. If the forget gate is small, the previous state is largely erased, but there is no mechanism for the gate to "decisively" select new information over old information in a way that reflects strong preferences.

This limitation is exemplified by the Nearest Neighbor Search problem. In this task, a reference vector is provided, and a sequence of vectors must be scanned sequentially to find the vector most similar to the reference. At the end of the sequence, the model must output the value attached to the most similar vector. The challenge is that as the model scans the sequence, it may encounter a vector that is quite similar to the reference and store the corresponding value. Later, it may encounter an even more similar vector, but the LSTM struggles to revise its stored value. The sigmoid gates make it difficult to decisively overwrite the previously stored value, because the gate values are bounded and the update is a smooth interpolation rather than a sharp replacement. The mean squared error of a standard LSTM on this task remains high, indicating that it cannot effectively revise its storage decisions.

The xLSTM addresses this limitation through exponential gating. By replacing the sigmoid activation with an exponential activation for the input and forget gates, the gates can take values much larger than one, allowing the network to decisively overwrite old information with new information. The exponential function $\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ is unbounded above, which means the input gate can amplify the new input to any degree, effectively allowing the network to say "this new information is much more important than what I had before" and to replace the old cell state content accordingly. This is a fundamental change to the gating mechanism that directly addresses the first limitation of the original LSTM.

#

## Limitation 2: Limited Storage Capacities Due to Scalar Cell States

The second limitation of LSTMs is limited storage capacities. In the original LSTM, the cell state is a scalar $c \in \mathbb{R}$. Even when multiple scalar cells are combined into a vector $\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$, each individual cell can only store a single number. This means that all information must be compressed into scalar values, which severely limits the amount and complexity of information that can be stored. For many tasks, this is not a problem because the relevant information can be adequately represented as a small number of scalar quantities. But for tasks that require storing rich, structured information—such as the association between multiple keys and their corresponding values—scalar memory is insufficient.

This limitation is exemplified by the Rare Token Prediction problem. When evaluated on Wikitext-103, LSTMs perform worse on tokens that appear rarely in the training data. The perplexity of token prediction is higher for rare tokens than for frequent ones, because the LSTM's scalar memory cells cannot store enough information to distinguish and recall rare tokens. A rare token requires the network to maintain a specific representation that differentiates it from all other tokens, but scalar memory cells have limited capacity for such fine-grained distinctions. The more tokens the model needs to distinguish, the more information must be compressed into each scalar cell, and eventually the capacity is exhausted.

The xLSTM addresses this limitation by introducing a matrix memory for the mLSTM variant. Instead of a scalar cell state $c \in \mathbb{R}$, the mLSTM uses a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, which dramatically increases the storage capacity. The matrix memory is updated using a covariance (outer product) rule, where key-value pairs are stored as outer products $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. Retrieval is performed by multiplying the query vector $\mathbf{q}_t$ with the matrix memory, which allows the network to retrieve stored values based on similarity between the query and stored keys. This matrix memory structure, inspired by Bidirectional Associative Memories and Fast Weight Programmers, provides exponentially more storage capacity than scalar cells, enabling the model to handle rare tokens and complex associative recall tasks that are beyond the reach of the original LSTM.

#

## Limitation 3: Lack of Parallelizability Due to Memory Mixing

The third limitation of LSTMs is the lack of parallelizability, which arises from memory mixing. In the LSTM, the hidden state $h_{t-1}$ from the previous time step is used as input to the computation of the cell input, gates, and hidden state at the current time step $t$. These hidden-hidden recurrent connections create a sequential dependency: the computation at time step $t$ cannot proceed until the computation at time step $t-1$ is complete. This means that processing a sequence of length $N$ requires $N$ sequential computational steps, regardless of the amount of parallel hardware available. This is in stark contrast to the Transformer, where self-attention can be computed in parallel across all positions in a sequence.

Memory mixing refers specifically to the recurrent connections from the hidden state vector $\mathbf{h}$ to the cell inputs and gates through recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R}_z$, $\mathbf{R}_i$, $\mathbf{R}_f$, and $\mathbf{R}_o$. These matrices mix the outputs of different memory cells across time steps, allowing information stored in one cell to influence the updates of other cells. While memory mixing enhances the expressiveness of the model—it enables the LSTM to solve state tracking problems that are beyond the reach of models without recurrent hidden connections—it fundamentally prevents parallelization. The sequential dependency chain created by these connections means that the entire sequence must be processed one step at a time.

The xLSTM addresses this limitation differently for its two variants. The sLSTM retains memory mixing and therefore remains sequential, but a fast CUDA implementation with GPU memory optimizations brings its speed to within a factor of two of the parallel mLSTM. The mLSTM, on the other hand, abandons memory mixing entirely. By removing the hidden-hidden recurrent connections, the mLSTM's recurrence can be reformulated into a parallel version, analogous to how FlashAttention enables parallel computation of attention. The mLSTM uses input-dependent gates that depend only on the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$, not on the previous hidden state, which breaks the sequential dependency chain and allows the recurrence to be computed in parallel across time steps. This design choice trades the expressiveness gained from memory mixing for the computational efficiency gained from parallelization, and the xLSTM architecture compensates for this trade-off by combining both sLSTM and mLSTM blocks.

## The Rise of Transformers and the Research Question

The three limitations of LSTMs—inability to revise storage decisions, limited storage capacities, and lack of parallelizability—created an opening for alternative architectures. The Transformer, introduced in 2017, addressed the parallelizability limitation directly through its self-attention mechanism, which computes interactions between all pairs of positions in a sequence simultaneously. This parallelizable computation meant that Transformers could be trained on modern hardware (GPUs and TPUs) far more efficiently than LSTMs, which was a decisive advantage as model sizes grew into the billions of parameters. The Transformer's self-attention also provided a form of associative memory: each position could attend to any other position, effectively providing direct access to all past information without the need to compress it into a fixed-size hidden state. This addressed, at least partially, the storage capacity limitation of LSTMs.

The success of Transformers was rapid and comprehensive. They replaced LSTMs in machine translation, then in language modeling, and eventually became the foundation of all major Large Language Models, including GPT-3, ChatGPT, GPT-4, Llama, PaLM, Chinchilla, and many others. The scalability of Transformers—their ability to be trained efficiently at massive scale on modern hardware—was the key factor in their dominance. LSTMs, despite their historical successes and their ability to learn abstractions, were left behind because they could not be trained as efficiently at scale.

This raises a simple but profound question: How far do we get in language modeling when scaling LSTMs to billions of parameters, leveraging the latest techniques from modern LLMs, but mitigating the known limitations of LSTMs? The xLSTM paper sets out to answer this question by introducing two key modifications to the LSTM: exponential gating with appropriate normalization and stabilization techniques, and modified memory structures. The sLSTM retains the scalar memory and scalar update of the original LSTM but adds exponential gating and a new form of memory mixing through multiple heads. The mLSTM introduces a matrix memory with a covariance update rule, enabling much larger storage capacity and full parallelizability. By integrating these extensions into residual block backbones and stacking them into architectures, the xLSTM aims to demonstrate that the LSTM architecture, properly modernized, can compete with state-of-the-art Transformers and State Space Models in both performance and scaling behavior. The experiments in the paper—ranging from synthetic tasks to large-scale language modeling on 300B tokens—provide evidence that the answer to the research question is at least as encouraging as "at least as far as current technologies like Transformers or State Space Models."

# Review of the Original LSTM Architecture

The Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) was introduced by Hochreiter and colleagues in the 1990s to overcome a fundamental problem that plagued recurrent neural networks: the vanishing gradient problem. When training recurrent networks to process sequential data, gradients that flow backward through time tend to shrink exponentially, making it extremely difficult for the network to learn long-range dependencies. The LSTM addressed this problem through two central ideas—the constant error carousel and gating—which together form the backbone of one of the most influential architectures in deep learning history. Before we can understand how the Extended LSTM (xLSTM) improves upon the original, we must first develop a thorough understanding of the original LSTM's mathematical structure, its components, and the roles they play in enabling effective sequence modeling.

# The LSTM Memory Cell as a Central Processing and Storage Unit

At the heart of the LSTM architecture lies the memory cell, a scalar processing and storage unit that serves as the network's primary mechanism for maintaining information across time steps. The original LSTM idea introduced this scalar memory cell as a means of preserving information over long sequences without suffering from the vanishing gradient problem that afflicted earlier recurrent neural networks. The key insight was that by using an additive update rule—rather than a multiplicative one—the cell state could maintain a pathway through which gradients could flow backward through time without being repeatedly multiplied by small values that would cause them to vanish.

The memory cell operates as a persistent storage location that accumulates and retains information deemed relevant by the network's gating mechanisms. At each time step $t$, the cell state $c_t$ holds a scalar value that represents the network's current memory of the sequence it has processed so far. This memory is not simply overwritten at each step; rather, it is selectively updated through a carefully controlled process that allows the network to add new information, remove old information, or maintain existing information as needed. The design of the memory cell as a scalar quantity is important to understand, because it means that all the information the LSTM wishes to retain at any given time step must be compressed into a single numerical value. This compression into a scalar memory is one of the fundamental limitations that the xLSTM later addresses through the introduction of matrix memory, but for the original LSTM, the scalar memory cell is the foundational building block upon which all other components operate.

The memory cell's role as a central processing and storage unit means that it sits at the intersection of the network's input processing and output generation. Information flows into the cell through the cell input, moderated by the input gate, and existing information in the cell is either retained or discarded based on the forget gate. The cell state is then transformed and emitted as the hidden state through the output gate. This architecture effectively decouples the network's memory from its output, allowing the LSTM to store information that may be useful at a much later point in the sequence without being forced to expose it as part of the hidden state at every time step.

# Cell State Update Rule: The Constant Error Carousel

The most critical innovation of the LSTM is the cell state update rule, often referred to as the constant error carousel. This update rule is expressed as:

$$c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t$$

Here, $c_{t-1}$ is the cell state from the previous time step, $f_t$ is the forget gate value, $i_t$ is the input gate value, and $z_t$ is the cell input. The equation describes an additive update: the new cell state is a weighted combination of the previous cell state (scaled by the forget gate) and the new candidate information (scaled by the input gate). This is fundamentally different from the update rules used in vanilla recurrent neural networks, where the hidden state is typically computed as a nonlinear function of the previous hidden state and the current input, leading to repeated multiplication of weight matrices that causes gradients to vanish or explode.

The term "constant error carousel" captures the essential property of this update rule. When gradients are computed during backpropagation through time, the derivative of $c_t$ with respect to $c_{t-1}$ includes a term proportional to $f_t$. If the forget gate $f_t$ is set to a value near one, the gradient flows backward through the cell state almost unchanged, as if riding on a carousel that carries the error signal through time without diminishing it. This additive structure ensures that the gradient pathway through the cell state does not involve repeated application of nonlinear activation functions or matrix multiplications that would compress the gradient signal. Instead, the gradient can flow freely along the cell state's additive pathway, allowing the network to learn dependencies that span hundreds or even thousands of time steps.

The forget gate $f_t$ controls how much of the previous cell state is retained. When $f_t$ is close to one, the cell state is preserved almost entirely, enabling long-term memory. When $f_t$ is close to zero, the previous cell state is effectively erased, allowing the network to reset its memory when the stored information is no longer relevant. The input gate $i_t$ controls how much new information from the cell input $z_t$ is added to the cell state. Together, these two gates provide the network with a flexible mechanism for deciding what to remember and what to forget at each time step.

# Input Gate, Forget Gate, and Output Gate: Roles and Sigmoid Activations

The LSTM memory cell contains three gates: the input gate, the forget gate, and the output gate. Each gate serves a distinct and crucial role in controlling the flow of information through the memory cell, and each uses a sigmoid activation function $\sigma(x) = 1/(1+\exp(-x))$ to produce values in the range $(0, 1)$. This sigmoid activation is important because it allows each gate to act as a smooth, differentiable switch that can selectively permit or restrict information flow.

The input gate $i_t$ controls the extent to which new information is written into the cell state. It is computed as:

$$i_t = \sigma(\tilde{i}_t), \quad \tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_i \, h_{t-1} + b_i$$

where $\mathbf{w}_i$ is the input weight vector connecting the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ to the input gate, $r_i$ is the recurrent weight connecting the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$ to the input gate, and $b_i$ is a bias term. When the input gate value is close to one, the cell input $z_t$ is fully added to the cell state; when it is close to zero, the cell input is effectively ignored. This allows the network to selectively incorporate new information based on the current input and the context provided by the previous hidden state.

The forget gate $f_t$ controls the extent to which the previous cell state is retained. It is computed as:

$$f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t), \quad \tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_f \, h_{t-1} + b_f$$

where the weight vector $\mathbf{w}_f$, recurrent weight $r_f$, and bias $b_f$ are specific to the forget gate. The forget gate was not part of the original LSTM formulation but was introduced by Gers et al. (2000). Its addition was a significant improvement, as it gave the LSTM the ability to explicitly forget information that was no longer relevant, preventing the cell state from growing unboundedly and allowing the network to reset its memory at appropriate points in the sequence. Without a forget gate, the cell state could only accumulate information, with no mechanism for controlled removal.

The output gate $o_t$ controls the extent to which the cell state is exposed as the hidden state. It is computed as:

$$o_t = \sigma(\tilde{o}_t), \quad \tilde{o}_t = \mathbf{w}_o^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_o \, h_{t-1} + b_o$$

where the parameters are specific to the output gate. The output gate allows the network to keep information stored in the cell state without necessarily emitting it as part of the hidden state at every time step. This decoupling of storage from output is a key feature of the LSTM, as it enables the network to maintain information that may only become relevant much later in the sequence. The hidden state $h_t$ is then computed by applying the output gate to the squashed cell state:

$$h_t = o_t \, \tilde{h}_t, \quad \tilde{h}_t = \psi(c_t)$$

where $\psi$ is a squashing function applied to the cell state. The output gate thus acts as a filter on the cell state, determining what portion of the stored memory is relevant for the current time step's output and for the recurrent connections that feed into the next time step.

# Cell Input and Hidden State Activation Functions

The LSTM employs two activation functions that serve distinct purposes within the memory cell: the cell input activation function $\varphi$ and the hidden state activation function $\psi$. Both are typically chosen to be the $\tanh$ function, which squashes its input to the range $(-1, 1)$. Understanding the role of each activation function is essential for grasping how the LSTM processes and transforms information.

The cell input $z_t$ is computed by applying the activation function $\varphi$ to a pre-activation value:

$$z_t = \varphi(\tilde{z}_t), \quad \tilde{z}_t = \mathbf{w}_z^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_z \, h_{t-1} + b_z$$

The cell input represents the candidate information that could be written into the cell state. The activation function $\varphi$ ensures that the candidate value is bounded, preventing the cell state from growing without limit due to large input values. By squashing the pre-activation $\tilde{z}_t$ to a bounded range, $\varphi$ provides a stable, normalized candidate that the input gate can selectively admit into the cell state.

The hidden state activation function $\psi$ is applied to the cell state to produce a squashed version $\tilde{h}_t = \psi(c_t)$ before the output gate is applied. This function is necessary because the cell state $c_t$ is, in principle, unbounded—it is the sum of gated contributions from all previous time steps, and without any normalization or squashing, it could grow arbitrarily large. The function $\psi$ normalizes or squashes the cell state to a bounded range, ensuring that the hidden state $h_t = o_t \, \psi(c_t)$ remains numerically stable. Typically, $\psi$ is the $\tanh$ function, which maps the cell state to $(-1, 1)$, though other choices are possible.

The use of $\tanh$ for both $\varphi$ and $\psi$ has important implications. Since $\tanh$ produces values in $(-1, 1)$, the cell input $z_t$ can carry both positive and negative information, allowing the network to both increase and decrease the cell state through the gated additive update. Similarly, the squashed cell state $\psi(c_t)$ can represent both positive and negative memories, providing a richer representational capacity than would be possible with a strictly non-negative activation. The choice of $\tanh$ also provides smooth, differentiable nonlinearities that facilitate gradient-based optimization.

# Complete LSTM Forward Pass Equations

Having examined each component individually, we now assemble the complete LSTM forward pass. At each time step $t$, the LSTM memory cell processes the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$ to produce the new cell state $c_t$ and the hidden state $h_t$. The complete set of update rules is:

$$c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t \quad \text{(cell state)}$$

$$h_t = o_t \, \tilde{h}_t, \quad \tilde{h}_t = \psi(c_t) \quad \text{(hidden state)}$$

$$z_t = \varphi(\tilde{z}_t), \quad \tilde{z}_t = \mathbf{w}_z^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_z \, h_{t-1} + b_z \quad \text{(cell input)}$$

$$i_t = \sigma(\tilde{i}_t), \quad \tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_i \, h_{t-1} + b_i \quad \text{(input gate)}$$

$$f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t), \quad \tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_f \, h_{t-1} + b_f \quad \text{(forget gate)}$$

$$o_t = \sigma(\tilde{o}_t), \quad \tilde{o}_t = \mathbf{w}_o^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_o \, h_{t-1} + b_o \quad \text{(output gate)}$$

Each equation follows the same structural pattern: a pre-activation value is computed as a linear combination of the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$, plus a bias term, and then an activation function is applied. The weight vectors $\mathbf{w}_z$, $\mathbf{w}_i$, $\mathbf{w}_f$, and $\mathbf{w}_o$ correspond to the input weights connecting $\mathbf{x}_t$ to the cell input, input gate, forget gate, and output gate, respectively. The recurrent weights $r_z$, $r_i$, $r_f$, and $r_o$ connect the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$ to each of these same components. The bias terms $b_z$, $b_i$, $b_f$, and $b_o$ provide learnable offsets for each component.

The forward pass proceeds sequentially through time. At each time step, the network first computes the pre-activations for all gates and the cell input based on the current input and the previous hidden state. It then applies the appropriate activation functions to obtain the gate values and the cell input. The cell state is updated via the constant error carousel, and the hidden state is computed by squashing the cell state and applying the output gate. This hidden state is then passed to the next time step, where it serves as the recurrent input for the subsequent update.

# Recurrent Weight Connections: Memory Mixing via Hidden-Hidden Connections

A defining feature of the original LSTM is the presence of recurrent weight connections that link the hidden state from one time step to the components of the memory cell at the next time step. These connections—parameterized by $r_z$, $r_i$, $r_f$, and $r_o$—create what is known as memory mixing, a mechanism by which information from the hidden state at time $t-1$ influences the computation of the cell input, input gate, forget gate, and output gate at time $t$.

Memory mixing through these hidden-hidden connections is a double-edged sword. On one hand, it enriches the network's computational capabilities by allowing the hidden state to directly influence the gating decisions at the next time step. This means the network can use its current understanding of the sequence—represented by $h_{t-1}$—to make informed decisions about what to store, what to forget, and what to output at the current time step. The hidden state carries a compressed summary of the network's processing so far, and the recurrent connections allow this summary to guide future computations in a context-dependent manner.

On the other hand, these recurrent connections enforce sequential processing. Because the computation at time $t$ depends on the hidden state $h_{t-1}$ from the previous time step, the forward pass must be carried out one time step at a time, in strict sequential order. This sequential dependency is the source of the LSTM's lack of parallelizability, which the paper identifies as one of its three main limitations. Unlike the Transformer, whose self-attention mechanism can be computed in parallel across all positions in a sequence, the LSTM's recurrent structure means that processing a sequence of length $N$ requires $N$ sequential steps, each dependent on the result of the previous one.

This trade-off between expressivity and parallelizability is central to understanding the motivation behind the xLSTM architecture. The sLSTM retains memory mixing and thus preserves the expressivity benefits of recurrent connections, but it sacrifices parallelizability. The mLSTM, by contrast, abandons memory mixing entirely, removing the hidden-hidden connections to enable full parallelization, but it compensates for the loss of memory mixing by introducing a matrix memory with a covariance update rule.

# Extension to Multiple Scalar Memory Cells with Recurrent Weight Matrices

While the original LSTM was formulated with a single scalar memory cell, later formulations extended the architecture to use multiple scalar memory cells combined in a vector $\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$. This extension allows the network to maintain $d$ independent memory cells, each with its own cell state, effectively increasing the network's storage capacity from a single scalar to a $d$-dimensional vector. With multiple memory cells, the scalar weights and scalar recurrent connections of the original formulation are replaced by weight matrices and recurrent weight matrices.

Specifically, the recurrent weights $r_z$, $r_i$, $r_f$, and $r_o$ become recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R}_z$, $\mathbf{R}_i$, $\mathbf{R}_f$, and $\mathbf{R}_o \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, which mix the cell outputs of the memory cells. The vectorized cell state $\mathbf{c}_t$ is updated according to the same constant error carousel principle, but now each element of the vector is updated in parallel:

$$\mathbf{c}_t = \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \mathbf{z}_t$$

where $\odot$ denotes element-wise (Hadamard) multiplication, and $\mathbf{f}_t$, $\mathbf{i}_t$, and $\mathbf{z}_t$ are now vectors of gate values and cell inputs, respectively. The recurrent weight matrices allow the hidden state vector $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ to influence the computation of all gates and the cell input through matrix-vector multiplications, enabling what the paper refers to as memory mixing across cells. The recurrent weight matrix $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ mixes the outputs of the memory cells, creating a fully connected recurrent layer where each cell can influence every other cell through the recurrent connections.

This extension to multiple memory cells with recurrent weight matrices is significant because it substantially increases the representational capacity of the LSTM. With $d$ memory cells, the network can simultaneously track $d$ different pieces of information, each with its own gating dynamics. The recurrent weight matrices allow these cells to interact and share information, creating a rich, high-dimensional memory system. However, the fundamental limitation of scalar storage persists: each individual memory cell still stores only a single scalar value. This means that while the vector of memory cells can collectively represent $d$ values, each cell remains a scalar memory with limited capacity for storing complex, high-dimensional information. This limitation is precisely what the mLSTM addresses by replacing the scalar memory with a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, which can store and retrieve information through matrix operations rather than scalar accumulations.

# Ablation Studies Confirming the Importance of All LSTM Components

The LSTM architecture, with its multiple gates, activation functions, and recurrent connections, may at first appear overly complex. One might reasonably ask whether all these components are truly necessary, or whether some could be removed without significant performance loss. This question was addressed through ablation studies, most notably by Greff et al. (2015), who conducted a systematic investigation of the LSTM's components.

Ablation studies are a standard methodology in machine learning research for understanding the contribution of individual components within an architecture. The approach involves systematically removing or disabling specific components and measuring the resulting performance change. If a component is truly important, removing it should lead to a noticeable degradation in performance. If a component is redundant or unnecessary, its removal should have little to no effect.

The ablation studies by Greff et al. (2015) showed that all components of the LSTM memory cell are crucial to its performance. The input gate, forget gate, output gate, cell input activation, and hidden state activation all contribute meaningfully to the network's ability to model sequential data. Removing any one of these components leads to measurable performance degradation, confirming that the LSTM's architecture is not accidentally over-engineered but rather carefully balanced, with each part playing a distinct and necessary role.

This finding has important implications for understanding the xLSTM modifications. The xLSTM does not remove any of the core LSTM components; instead, it enhances them. The exponential gating of the sLSTM replaces the sigmoid activation of the input and forget gates with exponential activation, while preserving the gate structure itself. The mLSTM replaces the scalar memory with a matrix memory but retains the gating concept. The ablation results of the original LSTM thus provide a foundation for understanding why the xLSTM's modifications are designed as enhancements to the existing structure rather than as wholesale replacements, and they underscore the importance of the gating mechanisms that have proven so central to the LSTM's success across a wide range of applications.

# xLSTM: Overview of Key Innovations

## Motivation: Overcoming the Three LSTM Limitations

The Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) architecture, introduced in the 1990s, represented a breakthrough in recurrent neural network design through two central ideas: the constant error carousel and gating. The constant error carousel is an additive update of the cell state that avoids the vanishing gradient problem, while gating mechanisms—input, forget, and output gates—moderate what information enters, persists in, and exits the memory cell. For over two decades, LSTMs stood at the forefront of sequence modeling, powering applications from text generation and machine translation to reinforcement learning systems like AlphaStar and OpenAI Five. However, the advent of Transformers in 2017, with their parallelizable self-attention mechanism, marked a turning point. Transformers could be trained at scale far more efficiently than LSTMs, and LSTMs fell out of favor for large language modeling.

The authors of the xLSTM paper identify three fundamental limitations of the original LSTM that prevented it from scaling competitively with Transformers. The first limitation is the inability to revise storage decisions. Once the LSTM has stored a value in its cell state, revising that decision—for example, overwriting a previously stored value when a more relevant one is encountered later—proves difficult. The paper demonstrates this via the Nearest Neighbor Search problem, where a sequence must be scanned for the most similar vector to a reference, and the attached value must be output at the end. The LSTM struggles because its sigmoid gates, which produce values in the range $(0, 1)$, cannot easily zero out or dominate a previously stored value. The second limitation is limited storage capacity. The original LSTM compresses information into a scalar cell state $c \in \mathbb{R}$. When tokens are rare and require more detailed representation, this scalar bottleneck degrades performance, as shown by the paper's Rare Token Prediction experiment on Wikitext-103. The third limitation is the lack of parallelizability. The LSTM's hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections—what the paper calls "memory mixing"—enforce sequential processing, making it impossible to parallelize training across the sequence dimension in the way Transformers can.

These three limitations motivate the two key innovations of xLSTM: exponential gating with appropriate normalization and stabilization, and modified memory structures. The central question the paper poses is: how far can language modeling go when these limitations are overcome and LSTMs are scaled to billions of parameters using modern techniques?

## Innovation 1: Exponential Gating with Normalization and Stabilization

The first major innovation of xLSTM is the replacement of sigmoid activation functions for the input and forget gates with exponential activation functions. In the original LSTM, the input gate $i_t$ and forget gate $f_t$ use the sigmoid function $\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + \exp(-x))$, which squashes gate values into the range $(0, 1)$. While this bounded range is convenient for stability, it limits the network's ability to make decisive storage decisions. A sigmoid gate can never fully open (value exactly $1$) or fully close (value exactly $0$), and more critically, it can never amplify a new input beyond the magnitude of what is already stored. This is precisely why the LSTM struggles with the Nearest Neighbor Search problem: when a more similar vector is found later in the sequence, the input gate cannot generate a value large enough to overwrite the previously stored value.

Exponential gating replaces $\sigma$ with $\exp$ for the input gate and optionally for the forget gate. The exponential function $\exp(x)$ produces values in $(0, \infty)$, allowing gates to take on values greater than $1$. This means a new input can be weighted strongly enough to effectively overwrite or dominate previous cell content. The input gate becomes $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$, and the forget gate can be either $f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)$ or $f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)$. This simple but powerful change gives the network the ability to revise storage decisions decisively.

However, exponential gating introduces a numerical challenge: exponential functions can produce very large values, leading to overflow. To address this, the paper introduces a stabilization state $m_t$ that tracks the maximum of the logarithmic gate values over time. Specifically, the stabilizer state is computed as $m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t))$. The stabilized input and forget gates are then computed as $i'_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t)$ and $f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t)$. This stabilization technique, building on prior work by Milakov (2018), ensures that the largest exponential value is normalized away, preventing overflow while preserving the relative magnitudes of the gates. Importantly, the paper proves in the appendix that replacing $i_t$ with $i'_t$ and $f_t$ with $f'_t$ does not change the network output or the gradients with respect to the parameters, so stabilization is numerically exact.

Exponential gating also necessitates a normalization mechanism. With sigmoid gates, the cell state is bounded by the squashing function $\psi$ (typically $\tanh$), which keeps values in a controlled range. With exponential gates, the cell state can grow unboundedly, so a normalizer state $n_t$ is introduced. This normalizer state accumulates the product of the input gate and all future forget gates, effectively tracking the total gate strength. The hidden state is then computed as the ratio of the cell state to the normalizer state, $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$, which normalizes the cell content by how strongly the gates have acted. This normalization ensures that the hidden state remains well-behaved even when individual gate values are large.

## Innovation 2: Modified Memory Structures

The second major innovation of xLSTM is the modification of the LSTM memory structure. The original LSTM uses a scalar memory cell $c \in \mathbb{R}$, which compresses all stored information into a single number. This scalar bottleneck limits the amount of information that can be simultaneously stored and retrieved, leading to the Rare Token Prediction problem where the LSTM underperforms on tokens that require richer representations.

The xLSTM paper introduces two distinct memory structures, each giving rise to a new LSTM variant. The first variant, sLSTM, retains the scalar memory but augments it with exponential gating and a new form of memory mixing. The scalar memory is updated by the same constant error carousel mechanism as the original LSTM—$c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$—but now with exponential gates and the normalizer state. The sLSTM preserves the hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections that enable memory mixing, meaning information from one memory cell can influence the inputs and gates of other cells. This memory mixing is a powerful feature for state tracking and sequence modeling, but it inherently prevents parallelization.

The second variant, mLSTM, replaces the scalar memory with a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. Instead of storing a scalar value, the mLSTM stores key-value pairs in a matrix using a covariance (outer product) update rule: $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. Here, $\mathbf{k}_t$ is a key vector and $\mathbf{v}_t$ is a value vector at time $t$, adopting the Transformer terminology. Retrieval is performed by querying the matrix memory: at a later time $t + \tau$, a query vector $\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau}$ retrieves the stored value via matrix multiplication $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$. This matrix memory dramatically increases the storage capacity compared to the scalar memory, addressing the limited storage capacity limitation directly.

The covariance update rule has deep roots in the literature on Bidirectional Associative Memories (BAMs) and Fast Weight Programmers. The paper notes that the covariance update is optimal for maximal separability of retrieved binary vectors, equivalent to maximizing the signal-to-noise ratio. By integrating this update rule into the LSTM framework—with the forget gate acting as a decay rate and the input gate as a learning rate—the mLSTM gains both the storage capacity of matrix memories and the temporal dynamics of gated recurrent networks. Crucially, because the mLSTM abandons hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections (no memory mixing), its recurrence can be reformulated into a parallel version analogous to parallel attention computations, enabling efficient training at scale.

## sLSTM: Scalar Memory, Scalar Update, and New Memory Mixing

The sLSTM is the first of the two new LSTM variants introduced by xLSTM. It retains the scalar memory structure of the original LSTM but augments it with exponential gating and a normalizer state. The forward pass of the sLSTM is defined by the following equations. The cell state is updated as $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$, where $f_t$ is the forget gate and $i_t$ is the input gate. The normalizer state is updated as $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t$, tracking the cumulative gate strength over time. The hidden state is then $h_t = o_t \tilde{h}_t$, where $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$ is the normalized cell state and $o_t$ is the output gate. The cell input is $z_t = \varphi(\tilde{z}_t)$, where $\tilde{z}_t = \mathbf{w}_z^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_z h_{t-1} + b_z$. The input gate is $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ with $\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_i h_{t-1} + b_i$. The forget gate can be either $f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)$ or $f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)$, with $\tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_f h_{t-1} + b_f$. The output gate remains sigmoid: $o_t = \sigma(\tilde{o}_t)$ with $\tilde{o}_t = \mathbf{w}_o^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_o h_{t-1} + b_o$.

A key distinguishing feature of the sLSTM is its preservation of memory mixing. In the original LSTM, when multiple scalar memory cells are combined into a vector $\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$, recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ mix the outputs of the hidden state across cells. The sLSTM inherits this structure, using recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R}_z$, $\mathbf{R}_i$, $\mathbf{R}_f$, and $\mathbf{R}_o$ to connect the hidden state vector $\mathbf{h}$ to the cell input and the three gates. This memory mixing allows information stored in one cell to influence the gating and input decisions of other cells, enabling complex temporal dynamics and state tracking capabilities.

The sLSTM introduces a new dimension to memory mixing through the concept of multiple heads. The sLSTM can have multiple heads, each containing multiple memory cells, with memory mixing occurring within each head but not across heads. This means that cells within the same head can share information through recurrent connections, but cells in different heads operate independently. The combination of multiple heads with exponential gating establishes what the paper calls a "new way of memory mixing." This is significant because memory mixing is what enables the sLSTM to solve state tracking problems—problems that Transformers and State Space Models, which lack memory mixing, cannot solve. The paper demonstrates this on formal language tasks like the parity problem, where models without memory mixing fail entirely.

The stabilization of the sLSTM's exponential gates uses the stabilizer state $m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t))$, producing stabilized gates $i'_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t)$ and $f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t)$. These stabilized gates replace the raw exponential gates in the forward pass without changing the output or gradients, ensuring numerical stability during training.

## mLSTM: Matrix Memory, Covariance Update Rule, and Full Parallelizability

The mLSTM is the second new LSTM variant, designed to overcome the limited storage capacity of the original LSTM. Its defining feature is the replacement of the scalar cell state $c$ with a matrix cell state $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This matrix memory stores key-value pairs using the covariance update rule and retrieves them via matrix multiplication with a query vector. The paper draws an explicit connection to Bidirectional Associative Memories, where the goal is to store an association between a key vector $\mathbf{k}_t$ and a value vector $\mathbf{v}_t$ such that later, given a query $\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau}$, the correct value can be retrieved.

The mLSTM forward pass is defined as follows. The cell state (matrix memory) is updated as $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$, where the forget gate $f_t$ controls decay of the existing memory and the input gate $i_t$ controls the rate at which the new key-value pair is stored. The normalizer state is a vector updated as $\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{k}_t$, tracking the weighted sum of key vectors. The hidden state is $\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t$, where $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t / \max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1\}$. The query, key, and value inputs are projected from the input $\mathbf{x}_t$: $\mathbf{q}_t = \mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_q$, $\mathbf{k}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}} \mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_k$, and $\mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_v$. The input gate is $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ with $\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + b_i$, the forget gate is $f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)$ or $f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)$ with $\tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + b_f$, and the output gate is $\mathbf{o}_t = \sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t)$ with $\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t = \mathbf{W}_o \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_o$.

An important detail in the mLSTM is the normalization of the retrieved hidden state. Because the dot product between the query and the normalizer state, $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$, can be close to zero—leading to division by a very small number—the paper uses the absolute value of this dot product and lower-bounds it by a threshold of $1.0$. This ensures numerical stability during retrieval. The paper notes that a layer normalization is applied before projecting inputs to keys and values, ensuring that keys and values have zero mean, which is a condition for the optimality of the covariance update rule.

The most practically significant property of the mLSTM is its full parallelizability. Because the mLSTM has no memory mixing—no hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections—its recurrence can be reformulated into a parallel computation. This is analogous to how FlashAttention enables parallel computation of attention, and the paper draws explicit connections to parallel formulations used in linear attention methods like GLA (Gated Linear Attention). The forget and input gates depend only on the input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and a bias, not on the previous hidden state, which breaks the sequential dependency that prevents parallelization in the original LSTM and the sLSTM. This parallelizability is essential for training at the scale of billions of parameters, making the mLSTM the workhorse of the xLSTM architecture for large language modeling.

The mLSTM uses the same stabilization technique as the sLSTM for its exponential gates, with the stabilizer state $m_t$ ensuring numerical stability. While the matrix memory introduces $O(d^2)$ computational complexity for the matrix update and retrieval operations, the paper notes that these computations involve standard matrix operations that can be parallelized efficiently on GPUs. Furthermore, the matrix memory does not require additional parameters—it is a dynamic state updated during the forward pass—so the computational overhead does not increase the parameter count.

## Multiple Memory Cells and Multiple Heads in sLSTM vs. mLSTM

Both sLSTM and mLSTM can be extended to have multiple memory cells, but the way they interact with multiple cells and heads differs fundamentally between the two variants due to the presence or absence of memory mixing.

In the sLSTM, multiple memory cells are combined into a vector, and recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R}_z$, $\mathbf{R}_i$, $\mathbf{R}_f$, and $\mathbf{R}_o$ connect the hidden state vector to the cell inputs and gates, enabling memory mixing across cells. The hidden state of one cell can influence the gating decisions and inputs of other cells, creating a rich web of inter-cell communication. The sLSTM further introduces the concept of multiple heads: cells are grouped into heads, with memory mixing occurring within each head but not across heads. This means that within a single head, all cells share information through recurrent connections, but cells belonging to different heads are isolated from each other. This head structure allows the sLSTM to maintain diverse, parallel processing streams—each head can specialize in different aspects of the sequence—while still benefiting from memory mixing within each stream. The introduction of heads, combined with exponential gating, creates what the paper describes as a "new way of memory mixing" that is unique to the sLSTM.

In the mLSTM, the situation is simpler. Because the mLSTM has no memory mixing—no hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections—there is no interaction between cells through recurrent weights. As a result, the paper states that for the mLSTM, multiple heads and multiple cells are equivalent. Adding another cell or another head simply means adding another independent matrix memory with its own key-value storage and retrieval mechanism. There is no notion of one cell's output influencing another cell's gates or inputs. This independence is precisely what enables parallelization: each cell can be processed independently, and the computation across the sequence dimension can be parallelized without any sequential dependencies.

This distinction has important implications for the expressive power of the two variants. The sLSTM's memory mixing enables it to solve state tracking problems, which the paper demonstrates on formal language tasks. Models without memory mixing—Transformers, State Space Models, and the mLSTM—cannot solve certain regular grammar tasks like parity. On the other hand, the mLSTM's lack of memory mixing is what enables its parallelizability, which is essential for efficient training at scale. The xLSTM architecture therefore combines both variants, leveraging the sLSTM's state tracking capabilities and the mLSTM's parallelizability and storage capacity.

## Integration into Residual Block Backbones to Form xLSTM Blocks

The sLSTM and mLSTM are not used in isolation; they are integrated into residual block structures to form what the paper calls xLSTM blocks. The design of these blocks is motivated by Cover's Theorem, which states that patterns non-linearly embedded in a higher-dimensional space are more likely to be linearly separable than in the original space. Separating different histories or contexts is a prerequisite for correctly predicting the next sequence element, so the xLSTM blocks are designed to non-linearly summarize the past in a high-dimensional space.

The paper describes two residual block architectures. The first is a post up-projection block, used for the sLSTM. In this design, the sLSTM first non-linearly summarizes the past in the original (lower-dimensional) space. Then, a linear layer maps this summary into a higher-dimensional space, a non-linear activation function is applied, and another linear layer maps back to the original space. This is analogous to the feed-forward network blocks used in Transformers. The post up-projection design is appropriate for the sLSTM because the sLSTM's memory mixing operates in the original space, and the up-projection provides the additional non-linear capacity for separating different histories.

The second architecture is a pre up-projection block, used for the mLSTM. In this design, the input is first linearly mapped to a high-dimensional space, then the mLSTM non-linearly summarizes the past in this high-dimensional space, and finally a linear layer maps back to the original space. The pre up-projection design is chosen for the mLSTM because the matrix memory's storage capacity scales with the dimensionality of the space it operates in—by performing the mLSTM computation in a higher-dimensional space, the memory capacity is effectively increased. This is analogous to the architecture used in State Space Models like Mamba.

Both block types are designed as residual blocks, meaning the block's transformation is added to its input: the output of the block is the input plus the block's transformation. This residual structure facilitates gradient flow through deep networks, enabling the stacking of many blocks without degradation of the signal. The paper notes that these blocks incorporate the gating techniques from the original LSTM—input-dependent and hidden-dependent gating with bias terms—transferred to the new architectures.

## Residual Stacking of xLSTM Blocks into xLSTM Architectures

The xLSTM architecture is constructed by residually stacking the xLSTM blocks described above. The paper adopts the pre-LayerNorm residual backbone, which is the most commonly used architecture in contemporary Large Language Models. In a pre-LayerNorm design, layer normalization is applied before the block's transformation rather than after, which has been shown to improve training stability in deep networks. This means that for each block, the input is first normalized, then passed through the block's transformation (sLSTM or mLSTM with up-projection), and the result is added to the original input via the residual connection.

The architecture combines both sLSTM-based and mLSTM-based blocks in a configurable ratio. The paper introduces the notation xLSTM[$a$:$b$] to denote the ratio of mLSTM-based to sLSTM-based blocks. For example, xLSTM[7:1] means that out of every eight blocks, seven are mLSTM-based and one is sLSTM-based. For a common total of 48 blocks, this would translate to 42 mLSTM-based blocks and 6 sLSTM-based blocks. This mixing of block types allows the architecture to benefit from both the mLSTM's parallelizability and storage capacity and the sLSTM's memory mixing and state tracking capabilities. The experiments in the paper explore different ratios, including xLSTM[1:0] (pure mLSTM) and xLSTM[7:1], finding that both configurations perform competitively, with xLSTM[1:0] performing better on certain language tasks and xLSTM[7:1] benefiting from the sLSTM's state tracking on others.

The xLSTM architecture has favorable computational properties compared to Transformers. While Transformers have quadratic computation and memory complexity with respect to the sequence length due to the attention mechanism, xLSTM networks have linear computation and constant memory complexity with respect to the sequence length. This is because the recurrent memory—whether the scalar memory of the sLSTM or the matrix memory of the mLSTM—maintains a fixed-size state that is updated at each time step, regardless of how long the sequence is. This makes xLSTM particularly well-suited for applications requiring long contexts, as the paper demonstrates through sequence length extrapolation experiments where xLSTM maintains low perplexity on contexts up to 16,384 tokens despite being trained on contexts of length 2,048. The constant memory footprint also makes xLSTM attractive for industrial applications and edge deployment, where memory efficiency is critical.

The combination of exponential gating, matrix memory, memory mixing, and residual block stacking yields an architecture that the paper shows performs favorably compared to state-of-the-art Transformers and State Space Models across a range of language modeling tasks, synthetic benchmarks, and scaling analyses. The scaling laws presented in the paper indicate that xLSTM achieves a favorable offset relative to competitors, suggesting that larger xLSTM models will continue to be competitive with or superior to Transformer-based and SSM-based Large Language Models.

# sLSTM: Scalar Memory with Exponential Gating

The sLSTM (scalar Long Short-Term Memory) is one of two new variants introduced in the xLSTM family. It retains the scalar memory structure of the original LSTM — meaning each memory cell stores a single scalar value — but introduces a critical modification: exponential gating. This seemingly simple change, replacing the sigmoid activation function on the input and forget gates with an exponential activation function, has profound consequences for the model's ability to revise its storage decisions. Combined with a new normalization scheme, a stabilization mechanism to prevent numerical overflow, and an enriched memory mixing structure that leverages multiple heads, the sLSTM overcomes one of the three core limitations of the original LSTM while preserving the recurrent, state-tracking capabilities that made LSTMs powerful in the first place. In this chapter, we will build up the sLSTM from motivation through complete mathematical formulation, carefully explaining each component and why it is necessary.

# Motivation: Empowering LSTMs to Revise Storage Decisions

The original LSTM, as introduced by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, employs sigmoid gates to control the flow of information into and out of its memory cell. The input gate $i_t$ and forget gate $f_t$ both use the sigmoid activation function $\sigma(x) = 1/(1+\exp(-x))$, which squashes their values into the range $(0, 1)$. This design has served the LSTM well across decades of applications, but it introduces a subtle yet significant limitation: the inability to effectively revise storage decisions once they have been made.

To understand this limitation concretely, consider the Nearest Neighbor Search problem described in the paper. In this task, a reference vector is given, and a sequence of vectors must be scanned sequentially to find the most similar vector to the reference. Once the scan is complete, the value attached to the most similar vector must be produced as output. The challenge arises when the model encounters a vector that is more similar to the reference than a previously stored candidate. The model must then revise its stored value — it must forget the old candidate and replace it with the new, more similar one.

The problem with sigmoid gates is that their maximum value is $1$, and in practice they rarely reach values very close to $1$. When the forget gate is, say, $0.9$, it means that $90\%$ of the old cell state is retained. The input gate, similarly bounded below $1$, can only add a limited amount of new information. The result is that the cell state becomes a weighted average of historical inputs rather than a decisive overwrite. When a more similar vector is found later in the sequence, the model cannot cleanly replace the old stored value; instead, the old and new values blend together, producing an incorrect output. Experiments in the paper show that the original LSTM struggles significantly on this task, as measured by mean squared error.

The root cause is mathematical. With sigmoid gates, the cell state update $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$ involves gates bounded by $1$, so the new input $z_t$ can never fully dominate over the existing state $c_{t-1}$. The exponential activation function, by contrast, is unbounded above: $\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ can take any positive value. This means the input gate can become arbitrarily large relative to the forget gate, allowing a new input to completely overwrite the stored state. Similarly, the forget gate, when exponential, can become very large, but more importantly, the ratio between input and forget gates becomes more flexible, enabling decisive revisions. The exponential gating thus gives the sLSTM the ability to say, in effect, "this new piece of information is so important that I should completely replace what I had before," something the sigmoid-gated LSTM could never do cleanly.

This motivation directly connects to the broader goal of the xLSTM paper: to overcome the known limitations of the original LSTM while preserving its strengths. The inability to revise storage decisions was identified as one of three core limitations, and exponential gating is the primary mechanism by which the sLSTM addresses it.

# Exponential Activation Functions for Input and Forget Gates

The central architectural change in the sLSTM is the replacement of sigmoid activation functions with exponential activation functions for the input and forget gates. In the original LSTM, these gates are computed as $i_t = \sigma(\tilde{i}_t)$ and $f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)$, where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function. In the sLSTM, the input gate is always exponential: $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$. The forget gate can optionally use either the sigmoid or the exponential activation: $f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)$ or $f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)$. The output gate $o_t$ remains sigmoid, as in the original LSTM.

The pre-activation values $\tilde{i}_t$ and $\tilde{f}_t$ are computed in the same manner as in the original LSTM, through linear combinations of the input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$, plus a bias term:

$$\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_i h_{t-1} + b_i$$

$$\tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_f h_{t-1} + b_f$$

The key difference is what happens when these pre-activations are passed through the gate activation function. The sigmoid function maps any real-valued input to $(0, 1)$, effectively acting as a soft switch that can never be fully on or fully off. The exponential function, by contrast, maps any real-valued input to $(0, \infty)$, removing the upper bound entirely.

This unboundedness is the source of the revision capability. When the pre-activation $\tilde{i}_t$ is large and positive, $\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ becomes very large, meaning the input gate allows a large amount of new information to flow into the cell state. When the pre-activation $\tilde{f}_t$ is large and negative, $\exp(\tilde{f}_t)$ approaches zero, meaning the forget gate erases the old cell state. The combination allows for decisive overwrites: a large input gate paired with a small forget gate completely replaces the stored content.

It is worth noting that the exponential function is always positive, just like the sigmoid. This is important because the gates act as multiplicative factors in the cell state update, and negative gate values would change the sign of the stored information, which would be undesirable. The exponential function preserves positivity while removing the upper bound.

The choice to keep the output gate as sigmoid is deliberate. The output gate controls how much of the cell state is revealed as the hidden state, and this is naturally a value between $0$ and $1$ — a gating operation in the traditional sense. The revision problem is specifically about what enters and what is forgotten from the cell state, not about what is output, so the exponential activation is applied only where it addresses the identified limitation.

The paper also notes that the option to use either sigmoid or exponential for the forget gate provides flexibility. In some configurations, a sigmoid forget gate may suffice, particularly when the revision capability is primarily driven by the exponential input gate. The ablation studies in the paper explore different gating configurations and conclude that having each gate learnable and influenced by the input has an incremental positive effect, supporting the use of exponential gates where they provide the most benefit.

# The Normalizer State: Summing Products of Input and Forget Gates

Exponential gating introduces a new challenge: because the gates are no longer bounded by $1$, the cell state $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$ can grow without bound. In the original LSTM, the cell state is kept in check partly by the sigmoid gates (which are at most $1$) and partly by the squashing function $\psi$ (typically $\tanh$) applied before outputting the hidden state. With exponential gates, the cell state can become extremely large, leading to numerical instability and making it difficult to interpret the stored value.

To address this, the sLSTM introduces a normalizer state $n_t$ that tracks the accumulated strength of the gates over time. The normalizer state is updated in parallel with the cell state, using the same gates but without the cell input:

$$n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t$$

This equation mirrors the cell state update $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$, but with the cell input $z_t$ replaced by $1$. The normalizer state thus accumulates the product of the input gate and all subsequent forget gates, providing a running sum of how much "gate strength" has been applied to the cell. If the gates have been large (as they can be with exponential activation), the normalizer state will be correspondingly large.

The hidden state is then computed by dividing the cell state by the normalizer state:

$$\tilde{h}_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}$$

$$h_t = o_t \tilde{h}_t$$

This normalization serves a dual purpose. First, it stabilizes the hidden state: even if the cell state has grown very large due to exponential gating, the normalizer state grows proportionally, so the ratio $c_t / n_t$ remains at a manageable scale. Second, it provides a natural form of attention-like weighting: the normalized hidden state $\tilde{h}_t$ is essentially a weighted average of past cell inputs $z_t$, where each input is weighted by the product of its input gate and all subsequent forget gates, and the normalizer state is the sum of these weights. This is analogous to how attention mechanisms normalize their output by dividing by the sum of attention weights.

The intuition is clearest when we consider the unrolled form. The cell state at time $t$ can be written as:

$$c_t = \sum_{\tau=1}^{t} \left( \prod_{s=\tau+1}^{t} f_s \right) i_\tau z_\tau$$

and the normalizer state as:

$$n_t = \sum_{\tau=1}^{t} \left( \prod_{s=\tau+1}^{t} f_s \right) i_\tau$$

The normalized hidden state $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$ is therefore a weighted average of the cell inputs $z_\tau$, with weights proportional to the product of the input gate at time $\tau$ and all forget gates between $\tau+1$ and $t$. When the exponential input gate at some time $\tau^*$ is very large, that input dominates both the numerator and the denominator, and $\tilde{h}_t \approx z_{\tau^*}$, effectively retrieving the most strongly stored input. This is precisely the behavior needed for the Nearest Neighbor Search task: when a more similar vector is found, its exponential input gate becomes large enough to dominate the weighted average, and the stored value is effectively revised.

The normalizer state is thus an essential companion to exponential gating. Without it, the unbounded cell state would make training unstable and the model's output would be uninterpretable. With it, the sLSTM achieves a stable, normalized output while retaining the ability to decisively revise its stored content.

# Complete sLSTM Forward Pass Equations

Having motivated the individual components, we now present the complete sLSTM forward pass. At each time step $t$, the sLSTM computes the following quantities:

The cell state is updated by the constant error carousel, moderated by the forget and input gates:

$$c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t$$

The normalizer state accumulates the gate strengths:

$$n_t = f_t \, n_{t-1} + i_t$$

The hidden state is the output gate applied to the normalized cell state:

$$h_t = o_t \, \tilde{h}_t, \quad \tilde{h}_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}$$

The cell input is computed from the input vector and the previous hidden state, passed through an activation function $\varphi$ (typically $\tanh$):

$$z_t = \varphi(\tilde{z}_t), \quad \tilde{z}_t = \mathbf{w}_z^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_z \, h_{t-1} + b_z$$

The input gate uses an exponential activation:

$$i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \quad \tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_i \, h_{t-1} + b_i$$

The forget gate uses either a sigmoid or an exponential activation:

$$f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t) \;\text{OR}\; \exp(\tilde{f}_t), \quad \tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_f \, h_{t-1} + b_f$$

The output gate uses a sigmoid activation:

$$o_t = \sigma(\tilde{o}_t), \quad \tilde{o}_t = \mathbf{w}_o^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_o \, h_{t-1} + b_o$$

Here, $\mathbf{w}_z$, $\mathbf{w}_i$, $\mathbf{w}_f$, and $\mathbf{w}_o$ are the input weight vectors connecting the input $\mathbf{x}_t$ to the cell input, input gate, forget gate, and output gate, respectively. The scalars $r_z$, $r_i$, $r_f$, and $r_o$ are recurrent weights connecting the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$ to the same four components. The terms $b_z$, $b_i$, $b_f$, and $b_o$ are bias terms. The functions $\varphi$ and $\psi$ (the latter now replaced by normalization) are activation functions, with $\varphi$ typically being $\tanh$.

Comparing these equations to the original LSTM, the structural differences are clear. The cell state update retains the same form — the constant error carousel — which is crucial for avoiding the vanishing gradient problem. The key changes are: (1) the input gate uses $\exp$ instead of $\sigma$, (2) the forget gate optionally uses $\exp$ instead of $\sigma$, (3) a new normalizer state $n_t$ is introduced, (4) the hidden state is computed as $c_t / n_t$ instead of $\psi(c_t)$, and (5) the output gate remains sigmoid. These changes collectively transform the LSTM from a model that blends information into a model that can decisively store and revise information.

# Stabilization via the Stabilizer State $m_t$ to Prevent Overflow

While the normalizer state $n_t$ keeps the *ratio* $c_t / n_t$ bounded, the individual values of $c_t$ and $n_t$ themselves can grow extremely large when exponential gates are used. The exponential function can produce very large values, and when these accumulate over many time steps through the recurrent update, the cell state and normalizer state can overflow standard floating-point representations. This is a practical concern: even though the ratio $c_t / n_t$ may be well-behaved, computing it requires computing $c_t$ and $n_t$ individually, and if either overflows, the result is numerically meaningless.

To address this, the sLSTM introduces a stabilizer state $m_t$ that tracks the maximum of the log-gate values over time. The stabilizer state is computed as:

$$m_t = \max\left(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \; \log(i_t)\right)$$

This equation has an intuitive interpretation. The term $\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}$ represents the accumulated log-forget-gate value up to the previous time step, propagated forward by the current forget gate. The term $\log(i_t)$ is the log of the current input gate. The stabilizer state takes the maximum of these two, which is the largest contributor to the cell state and normalizer state at the current time step. Since the exponential function is monotonic, the maximum in log space corresponds to the maximum in the original space.

The stabilizer state $m_t$ is then used to rescale the input and forget gates, producing stabilized versions:

$$i'_t = \exp\left(\log(i_t) - m_t\right) = \exp\left(\tilde{i}_t - m_t\right)$$

$$f'_t = \exp\left(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t\right)$$

The stabilized input gate $i'_t$ subtracts the stabilizer state from the log of the input gate before exponentiating, effectively dividing $i_t$ by $\exp(m_t)$. The stabilized forget gate $f'_t$ similarly rescales by shifting the accumulated log-forget value by the difference $m_{t-1} - m_t$, which accounts for the change in the stabilizer state from one step to the next.

The key insight is that $m_t$ represents the dominant scale of the gates at time $t$. By subtracting this scale before exponentiating, the stabilized gates are kept at a manageable magnitude. At least one of the stabilized gates (whichever contributed the maximum to $m_t$) will have value $1$, and the others will be at most $1$. This is analogous to the well-known "log-sum-exp trick" used for numerical stability in softmax computations, where the maximum logit is subtracted before exponentiating.

The paper credits this stabilization technique to Milakov (2018), and it is essential for making exponential gating practical. Without it, the exponential gates would frequently overflow during training, especially over long sequences, making the sLSTM unusable for realistic applications.

# Stabilized Input and Forget Gate Computations

Let us examine the stabilized gate computations in more detail to understand exactly how they work and why they preserve the model's behavior.

The stabilized input gate is:

$$i'_t = \exp\left(\tilde{i}_t - m_t\right)$$

Since $m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t))$ and $\log(i_t) = \tilde{i}_t$ (because $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$, so $\log(i_t) = \tilde{i}_t$), the stabilizer state is at least $\tilde{i}_t$. Therefore, $\tilde{i}_t - m_t \leq 0$, which means $i'_t \leq 1$. The stabilized input gate is thus bounded above by $1$, regardless of how large the original exponential input gate $i_t$ might be.

The stabilized forget gate is:

$$f'_t = \exp\left(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t\right)$$

Similarly, since $m_t \geq \log(f_t) + m_{t-1}$ (by definition of the maximum), we have $\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t \leq 0$, so $f'_t \leq 1$. The stabilized forget gate is also bounded above by $1$.

When the input gate dominates (i.e., $\log(i_t) > \log(f_t) + m_{t-1}$), then $m_t = \log(i_t) = \tilde{i}_t$, and we get $i'_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - \tilde{i}_t) = \exp(0) = 1$, and $f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - \tilde{i}_t)$, which is less than $1$. In this case, the input gate is the dominant contributor, and the stabilized input gate is set to $1$ while the stabilized forget gate is scaled down.

When the forget gate dominates (i.e., $\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} > \log(i_t)$), then $m_t = \log(f_t) + m_{t-1}$, and we get $f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - \log(f_t) - m_{t-1}) = \exp(0) = 1$, and $i'_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - \log(f_t) - m_{t-1})$, which is less than $1$. In this case, the forget gate is the dominant contributor, and the stabilized forget gate is set to $1$ while the stabilized input gate is scaled down.

In both cases, the stabilized gates are bounded by $1$, preventing overflow, while the relative magnitudes of the gates are preserved. This is the essence of the stabilization: it shifts the gates to a manageable scale without altering their relative proportions.

# Proof That Stabilization Does Not Change Network Output or Gradients

A natural concern with the stabilization technique is whether it alters the model's behavior. If the stabilized gates $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ are different from the original gates $i_t$ and $f_t$, one might worry that the model's output or its training dynamics change. The paper addresses this concern directly, stating that replacing $f_t$ by $f'_t$ and $i_t$ by $i'_t$ in the forward pass "does neither change the output of the whole network nor the derivatives of the loss with respect to the parameters." The proof, referenced in the appendix, relies on the following observation.

Consider the cell state update with the stabilized gates:

$$c'_t = f'_t \, c'_{t-1} + i'_t \, z_t$$

and the normalizer state:

$$n'_t = f'_t \, n'_{t-1} + i'_t$$

We can show by induction that $c'_t = c_t / \exp(m_t)$ and $n'_t = n_t / \exp(m_t)$ at every time step. For the base case, assume the initial states satisfy $c'_0 = c_0 / \exp(m_0)$ and $n'_0 = n_0 / \exp(m_0)$. For the inductive step, assume $c'_{t-1} = c_{t-1} / \exp(m_{t-1})$ and $n'_{t-1} = n_{t-1} / \exp(m_{t-1})$. Then:

$$c'_t = f'_t \, \frac{c_{t-1}}{\exp(m_{t-1})} + i'_t \, z_t$$

Substituting the stabilized gate expressions:

$$c'_t = \exp\left(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t\right) \frac{c_{t-1}}{\exp(m_{t-1})} + \exp\left(\tilde{i}_t - m_t\right) z_t$$

$$= \frac{f_t \, \exp(m_{t-1})}{\exp(m_t)} \frac{c_{t-1}}{\exp(m_{t-1})} + \frac{\exp(\tilde{i}_t)}{\exp(m_t)} z_t$$

$$= \frac{f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t}{\exp(m_t)} = \frac{c_t}{\exp(m_t)}$$

The same calculation applies to the normalizer state, giving $n'_t = n_t / \exp(m_t)$. Therefore, the normalized hidden state is:

$$\tilde{h}'_t = \frac{c'_t}{n'_t} = \frac{c_t / \exp(m_t)}{n_t / \exp(m_t)} = \frac{c_t}{n_t} = \tilde{h}_t$$

The $\exp(m_t)$ factors cancel exactly, so the hidden state is identical whether or not stabilization is used. Since the output of every layer in the network depends only on the hidden states, the entire network output is unchanged.

For the gradients, the argument follows from the fact that the forward pass produces identical outputs. Since the loss is computed from the forward pass outputs, and the stabilized forward pass produces the same outputs as the unstabilized one, the loss is the same. The gradients of the loss with respect to the parameters are computed via backpropagation through the forward pass, and since the forward pass outputs are identical at every step, the gradients must also be identical. More formally, the Jacobian of the hidden state with respect to any parameter is the same in both cases because the hidden state values are the same and the functional relationships (after cancellation) are the same.

This proof is reassuring: the stabilization is purely a numerical technique that has no effect on the model's mathematical behavior. It allows the sLSTM to be trained with exponential gates without any concern about altering the model's semantics or learning dynamics.

# New Memory Mixing: Multiple Memory Cells with Recurrent Connections

The original LSTM can have multiple scalar memory cells combined into a vector $\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$, with recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ that mix the outputs of the cells. This memory mixing — the recurrent connections from the hidden state vector back to the cell inputs and gates — is a defining feature of LSTMs and is what enables them to solve state tracking problems. The sLSTM inherits and extends this capability.

In the sLSTM, multiple memory cells enable memory mixing via recurrent connections $\mathbf{R}_z$, $\mathbf{R}_i$, $\mathbf{R}_f$, and $\mathbf{R}_o$ from the hidden state vector $\mathbf{h}$ to the cell input $\mathbf{z}$ and the gates $\mathbf{i}$, $\mathbf{f}$, $\mathbf{o}$, respectively. These recurrent weight matrices are full $d \times d$ matrices (in the vector case), allowing each memory cell to influence every other cell's input and gates. This is the mechanism by which information stored in one cell can affect what is stored in another cell at the next time step.

The new aspect of memory mixing in the sLSTM is the effect of exponential gating. In the original LSTM, the recurrent connections feed into sigmoid gates, so the influence of one cell on another is bounded by the sigmoid's range. In the sLSTM, the recurrent connections feed into exponential input gates (and optionally exponential forget gates), which means the influence of one cell on another can be much stronger and more decisive. A cell that detects an important pattern can, through the recurrent connection, cause another cell's input gate to become very large, decisively overwriting that cell's content. This creates a richer and more dynamic form of memory mixing than was possible with the original LSTM.

The paper emphasizes that memory mixing is what distinguishes the sLSTM (and the original LSTM) from models like Retention, RWKV, and HGRN2, which do not allow memory mixing. The absence of memory mixing in these models means they cannot solve state tracking problems — problems that require tracking the state of a computation, such as evaluating code or tracking entities in a long narrative. The sLSTM's memory mixing, enhanced by exponential gating, gives it greater expressiveness than state space models and Transformers in this regard, as established by theoretical work on the expressivity of recurrent models.

It is worth noting that the presence of memory mixing is what makes the sLSTM not parallelizable across the time dimension. The recurrent connections from $h_{t-1}$ to the gates and cell input at time $t$ create a sequential dependency: the computation at time $t$ cannot proceed until the hidden state at time $t-1$ has been computed. This is in contrast to the mLSTM, which abandons memory mixing to achieve full parallelizability. The sLSTM thus trades parallelizability for expressiveness, a trade-off that the xLSTM architecture manages by combining both variants in a single network.

# Multiple Heads in sLSTM: Memory Mixing Within Heads but Not Across Heads

The sLSTM introduces the concept of multiple heads, a structural innovation that provides additional flexibility in how memory mixing is organized. A head in the sLSTM is a group of memory cells that share recurrent connections — that is, cells within the same head can mix their memories through the recurrent weight matrices, but cells in different heads cannot influence each other through recurrent connections.

This multi-head structure means that each head maintains its own independent memory mixing dynamics. Within a head, the memory cells interact through the full recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R}_z$, $\mathbf{R}_i$, $\mathbf{R}_f$, and $\mathbf{R}_o$, allowing complex state tracking within that head's memory. Across heads, there is no recurrent connection, so each head's memory evolves independently of the others.

The motivation for this design is twofold. First, it allows the model to maintain multiple independent "threads" of memory, each tracking different aspects of the input sequence. For example, one head might track syntactic structure while another tracks semantic content, and the two can evolve without interfering with each other. Second, it provides a computational benefit: the recurrent weight matrices are block-diagonal (one block per head), which reduces the number of parameters and the computational cost of the recurrent connections compared to a single large matrix connecting all cells.

The paper notes that this introduction of heads for sLSTM, together with exponential gating, "establishes a new way of memory mixing." The combination is important: with sigmoid gates, the multi-head structure would still provide independent memory threads, but the mixing within each head would be limited by the bounded gates. With exponential gates, the mixing within each head becomes much more powerful: a cell in one head can decisively influence the storage decisions of other cells in the same head, enabling more complex state tracking dynamics within each head while maintaining independence across heads.

It is also noted that for the mLSTM, multiple heads and multiple cells are equivalent, because the mLSTM has no memory mixing. Since there are no recurrent connections in the mLSTM, there is no distinction between having multiple heads and having multiple cells — both simply mean having multiple independent matrix memories. The sLSTM, by contrast, has a meaningful distinction between heads and cells because memory mixing occurs within heads but not across them.

# The Interplay Between Exponential Gating and Memory Mixing

The two key innovations of the sLSTM — exponential gating and the multi-head memory mixing structure — are not independent additions but rather complementary modifications that work together to create a more powerful recurrent model. Understanding their interplay is essential for appreciating the sLSTM's design.

Exponential gating addresses the limitation of revising storage decisions. By allowing the input and forget gates to take values greater than $1$, the model can decisively overwrite its stored content when new, more relevant information arrives. This is the primary mechanism by which the sLSTM solves problems like the Nearest Neighbor Search task, where the original LSTM fails due to its inability to revise.

Memory mixing addresses a different but related capability: the ability to track complex states over time. Through recurrent connections, the model can implement state machines, counters, and other computational structures that require one piece of stored information to influence what is stored elsewhere. The paper cites theoretical results showing that LSTMs with memory mixing are more expressive than state space models and Transformers, which lack this capability.

The combination of exponential gating and memory mixing creates a synergy. Consider a state tracking problem where the model must count occurrences of a particular pattern and take action when the count reaches a threshold. With sigmoid gates and memory mixing, the model can track the count (through recurrent connections that increment a counter cell), but it may struggle to decisively act when the threshold is reached because the sigmoid gates limit how strongly the threshold-crossing event can influence the output. With exponential gates, the threshold-crossing event can produce a very large input gate, decisively changing the stored state. With exponential gates but no memory mixing, the model could decisively overwrite a single cell's content but could not coordinate across multiple cells to implement the counting logic. Only the combination — exponential gates with memory mixing — provides both the decisiveness and the coordination needed for complex state tracking.

The multi-head structure adds a further dimension to this interplay. By organizing memory cells into independent heads, the model can maintain multiple parallel state tracking processes, each enhanced by exponential gating within the head. This allows the sLSTM to simultaneously track multiple aspects of the input sequence — for instance, tracking the depth of nested parentheses in one head while tracking the gender of a referent in another — with each head's dynamics benefitting from the decisiveness of exponential gates.

The experimental results in the paper confirm the importance of this interplay. On formal language tasks that require state tracking (such as the parity task, which requires counting the number of 1s modulo 2), the sLSTM with exponential gating and memory mixing succeeds where models without memory mixing (like Transformers and state space models) fail. The paper shows that xLSTM configurations incorporating sLSTM blocks (such as xLSTM[0:1], which uses only sLSTM) achieve high accuracy on these tasks, while models without memory mixing perform at chance level. This directly validates the theoretical prediction that memory mixing, enhanced by exponential gating, provides the sLSTM with state tracking capabilities that are fundamentally unavailable to models that lack recurrent hidden-hidden connections.

At the same time, the ablation studies in the paper demonstrate that both exponential gating and the architectural improvements (residual blocks, up-projection) contribute to the sLSTM's strong performance on language modeling tasks. The studies morph a vanilla LSTM architecture step by step into an xLSTM architecture, showing that each change — integrating LSTM layers into pre-LayerNorm residual backbones, adding post up-projection blocks, introducing exponential gating, and adding matrix memory — contributes incrementally to performance. The exponential gating and the architectural improvements together account for the strong performance improvement observed in the final xLSTM models.

In summary, the sLSTM represents a careful balance of innovations. Exponential gating provides the ability to revise storage decisions, addressing a core limitation of the original LSTM. The normalizer state and stabilizer state make exponential gating numerically practical. Memory mixing, enhanced by exponential gating and organized into independent heads, provides state tracking capabilities that distinguish the sLSTM from parallelizable alternatives. The result is a recurrent model that retains the strengths of the original LSTM — the constant error carousel, gating, and recurrent connections — while overcoming its key weakness, and that does so in a way that is complementary to the mLSTM's approach of parallelizable matrix memory. Together, the sLSTM and mLSTM form the building blocks of the xLSTM architecture, which combines the expressiveness of the sLSTM with the scalability of the mLSTM.

# mLSTM: Matrix Memory with Covariance Updates

## Motivation: Enhancing Storage Capacities Beyond Scalar Cell States

The original Long Short-Term Memory architecture, despite its tremendous success across a wide range of sequence modeling tasks, suffers from a fundamental limitation in its storage capacity. In the traditional LSTM, information must be compressed into a scalar cell state $c \in \mathbb{R}$ at each time step. This means that regardless of how rich or complex the incoming information at a given time step might be, it must ultimately be funneled into a single real number for storage in the memory cell. While this design has proven sufficient for many applications, it creates a bottleneck when the model needs to retain detailed, high-dimensional information over extended sequences.

The paper illustrates this limitation through the Rare Token Prediction problem. When evaluated on the Wikitext-103 dataset, the original LSTM performs notably worse on rare tokens compared to more frequent ones. This degradation occurs precisely because rare tokens require the model to store specific, detailed information that does not compress well into a scalar value. Frequent tokens benefit from repeated exposure, allowing the model to form robust representations even with limited per-instance storage. Rare tokens, however, demand that the model faithfully preserve the full richness of each occurrence, a task at which scalar memory fundamentally struggles.

The motivation behind the mLSTM is to directly address this storage bottleneck by replacing the scalar memory cell with a matrix-valued memory. Rather than forcing all stored information through the narrow channel of a single real number, the mLSTM maintains a full matrix that can encode rich, structured relationships between vectors. This increase in storage capacity is not merely a matter of having more numbers to work with; it fundamentally changes the kind of information that can be stored and retrieved. With a matrix memory, the model can maintain associative relationships between keys and values, storing not just "how much" of something to remember, but "what" is associated with "what." This associative capability is what enables the mLSTM to handle rare token prediction and similar tasks that demand precise, high-capacity recall.

## From Scalar Memory to Matrix Memory

The central architectural change in mLSTM is the replacement of the scalar memory cell $c \in \mathbb{R}$ with a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This change has profound implications for how information is stored, updated, and retrieved within the network.

In the original LSTM, the cell state update follows the form $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$, where a scalar forget gate $f_t$ scales the previous scalar cell state and a scalar input gate $i_t$ scales the incoming scalar cell input $z_t$. The entire history of the sequence is distilled into this single number, which is then squashed by an activation function and modulated by an output gate to produce the hidden state. The scalar nature of this memory means that the LSTM can only maintain a single "channel" of information at each time step, and any new information must be blended with or overwrite the existing content through the gating mechanism.

The matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, by contrast, provides $d^2$ degrees of freedom for storing information. Retrieval from this matrix memory is performed via matrix multiplication: given a query vector $\mathbf{q} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the retrieved information is $\mathbf{C} \mathbf{q} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, which is a $d$-dimensional vector rather than a scalar. This means that each retrieval operation can access a rich, structured representation rather than a single value. The matrix can encode correlations, associations, and patterns that would be impossible to represent in a scalar.

The trade-off for this increased capacity is computational cost. While the scalar LSTM update requires $O(1)$ operations per time step for the memory update (ignoring the cost of computing gates), the matrix memory update requires $O(d^2)$ operations, and retrieval requires $O(d^2)$ operations as well. However, as the paper notes, these matrix operations can be parallelized effectively on GPUs, so the wall-clock time overhead is minor in practice. Furthermore, the matrix memory does not require any parameters itself—it is purely a state that is updated through the gating mechanism. This is an important distinction from approaches that use parameterized memory, as it keeps the parameter count of the model independent of the memory capacity.

## Key-Value Storage and Retrieval Paradigm

The mLSTM adopts the key-value storage paradigm, borrowing terminology from the Transformer architecture. At each time step $t$, the model processes the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and produces three vectors: a key $\mathbf{k}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$, a value $\mathbf{v}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$, and, at retrieval time, a query $\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau} \in \mathbb{R}^d$. The key and value vectors define what should be stored in the matrix memory, while the query vector determines what should be retrieved.

The storage operation writes the outer product $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ into the matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t$. This outer product is a rank-one matrix that encodes the associative relationship between the key and value: any future query that is similar to the key $\mathbf{k}_t$ will, when multiplied against the matrix, produce a result aligned with the value $\mathbf{v}_t$. The retrieval operation is then simply $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_{t+\tau}$, which computes a weighted sum of all stored values, where each value is weighted by the dot product between its associated key and the current query.

This paradigm is powerful because it decouples the storage of information (determined by keys and values) from its retrieval (determined by queries). The model can store information at one time step and selectively retrieve it later based on the similarity between the current context (encoded in the query) and the stored keys. This is exactly the kind of associative recall that the scalar LSTM struggles with, but which the matrix memory handles naturally through the geometry of the outer product and matrix-vector multiplication.

The use of Transformer terminology—keys, values, and queries—is intentional and illuminating. In self-attention, the Transformer computes attention weights as dot products between queries and keys, then uses these weights to combine values. The mLSTM achieves a similar effect but through a recurrent matrix memory: the matrix $\mathbf{C}_t$ accumulates outer products of past keys and values, and the query retrieves a weighted combination of values based on the geometric relationships encoded in the matrix. The crucial difference is that the mLSTM maintains this associative memory in a fixed-size matrix, giving it constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length, whereas the Transformer must store all past key-value pairs explicitly, leading to quadratic complexity.

## Bidirectional Associative Memories and the Covariance Update Rule

The key-value storage paradigm used by mLSTM has its roots in the concept of Bidirectional Associative Memories (BAMs), which were introduced in the early 1970s by researchers including Kohonen, Anderson, and Nakano. A Bidirectional Associative Memory is a type of neural network that can store pairs of patterns and retrieve one member of a pair when presented with the other. If a BAM has stored the pair $(\mathbf{k}, \mathbf{v})$, then presenting $\mathbf{k}$ as input should produce $\mathbf{v}$ as output, and presenting $\mathbf{v}$ should produce $\mathbf{k}$, hence the term "bidirectional."

The mechanism by which BAMs store and retrieve information is the covariance update rule, which is given by:

$$\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$$

This rule states that the memory matrix is updated by adding the outer product of the value vector and the key vector. The outer product $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ is a $d \times d$ matrix where the $(i,j)$-th entry is $v_{t,i} \cdot k_{t,j}$. This matrix encodes the correlation (covariance) between the components of the value and key vectors, which is why it is called the covariance update rule.

The paper notes an important detail: a layer normalization is applied before projecting inputs to keys and values, which ensures that the key and value vectors have zero mean. This zero-mean property is significant because it means the outer product $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ genuinely captures the covariance structure of the inputs rather than being dominated by their mean values. Without centering, the outer product would include a large rank-one component proportional to the product of the means, which would overwhelm the informative covariance structure.

The covariance update rule has a deep connection to the study of associative memory in neural networks. When multiple key-value pairs are stored in the matrix through successive applications of the covariance update rule, the resulting matrix $\mathbf{C} = \sum_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ acts as a linear associator. Given a query $\mathbf{q}$, the retrieval $\mathbf{C} \mathbf{q} = \sum_t \mathbf{v}_t (\mathbf{k}_t^\top \mathbf{q})$ computes a weighted sum of all stored values, where each value is weighted by the dot product between its corresponding key and the query. If the keys are approximately orthogonal (as is often the case in high-dimensional spaces), then a query that matches one key closely will retrieve its associated value while suppressing the contributions from other stored pairs.

## Optimality of Covariance Updates for Maximal Separability of Binary Vectors

The paper highlights that the covariance update rule is optimal for achieving maximal separability of retrieved binary vectors, which is equivalent to achieving a maximal signal-to-noise ratio. This optimality result, attributed to Dayan (1991), provides a theoretical justification for why the covariance update rule is a natural choice for the matrix memory of the mLSTM.

To understand this optimality, consider the scenario where the keys and values are binary vectors, meaning each component is either $+1$ or $-1$. When we store multiple key-value pairs in the matrix using the covariance update rule, and then retrieve using a query that is one of the stored keys, the retrieved vector will contain both signal (the correct value associated with that key) and noise (contributions from other stored key-value pairs). The signal-to-noise ratio determines how reliably the correct value can be recovered.

The covariance update rule maximizes this signal-to-noise ratio because it leverages the zero-mean property of the binary vectors. When the keys have zero mean (equal numbers of $+1$ and $-1$ components), the cross-terms in the retrieval—the contributions from keys other than the query key—tend to cancel out due to the orthogonality of random binary vectors in high-dimensional spaces. The outer product structure ensures that the signal (the dot product of the query with its matching key, which is $d$ for perfectly aligned binary vectors) grows linearly with the dimension $d$, while the noise (the sum of dot products with non-matching keys) grows only as $\sqrt{d}$ due to the central limit theorem. This means the signal-to-noise ratio improves as $\sqrt{d}$, making the retrieval increasingly reliable in higher dimensions.

The paper also notes that even higher separability is possible when one limits retrieval to pairwise interactions and concedes quadratic complexity, as done in attention mechanisms. This refers to the fact that Transformer self-attention, which explicitly computes all pairwise interactions between tokens, can achieve perfect separability at the cost of $O(n^2)$ computation for sequence length $n$. The covariance update rule, by contrast, achieves near-optimal separability with constant memory and linear time complexity, making it a more efficient choice for large-scale language modeling.

## Connection to Fast Weight Programmers

The covariance update rule used in mLSTM has a rich history in neural network research, particularly through its connection to Fast Weight Programmers. Fast Weight Programmers, introduced by Schmidhuber in 1992, are a class of models that maintain a "fast weight matrix" that is rapidly updated based on the input stream, in contrast to the "slow weights" that are learned through gradient descent over many examples.

The basic idea of Fast Weight Programmers is to use the outer product of vectors to write information into a weight matrix, and then use this matrix to process subsequent inputs. This is precisely the mechanism of the covariance update rule: the matrix $\mathbf{C}$ is "programmed" by the outer products $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ and then used to process queries. The connection between Fast Weight Programmers and the mLSTM matrix memory is therefore direct and fundamental.

The paper notes that Fast Weight Programmers were later extended by incorporating a constant decay rate multiplied to the previous matrix $\mathbf{C}_{t-1}$ and a constant learning rate multiplied to the outer product $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. These additions introduced forgetting (through decay) and controlled learning (through the learning rate) into the fast weight framework. In the mLSTM, these constant rates are replaced by adaptive gates: the forget gate $f_t$ serves as a data-dependent decay rate, and the input gate $i_t$ serves as a data-dependent learning rate. This adaptive gating is a key innovation that distinguishes mLSTM from earlier Fast Weight Programmers and connects the associative memory framework to the LSTM gating philosophy.

The evolution from Fast Weight Programmers to mLSTM represents a synthesis of two powerful ideas: the high-capacity associative memory of fast weight matrices and the adaptive, gated memory management of LSTMs. By bringing these ideas together, the mLSTM achieves both the storage capacity needed for complex language modeling tasks and the selective, context-dependent memory management that makes LSTMs effective at learning temporal dependencies.

## Integrating the Covariance Update Rule into the LSTM Framework

The integration of the covariance update rule into the LSTM framework is the key insight that gives the mLSTM its identity. In the original LSTM, the cell state update $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$ combines a forget gate (controlling how much of the previous state is retained) and an input gate (controlling how much new information is written). The mLSTM generalizes this to the matrix setting by replacing the scalar forget and input operations with their matrix counterparts:

$$\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$$

Here, the forget gate $f_t$ acts as a decay rate applied to the entire matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_{t-1}$. Rather than being a fixed constant as in early Fast Weight Programmers, this decay rate is computed at each time step based on the current input, allowing the model to dynamically decide how much of its stored associations to retain. When the forget gate value is close to zero, the model effectively "clears" its matrix memory, discarding old associations that are no longer relevant. When the forget gate is close to one, the model preserves its accumulated knowledge.

Similarly, the input gate $i_t$ acts as a learning rate that controls how strongly the new key-value association $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ is written into the matrix memory. A large input gate value means the current key-value pair is written with high intensity, potentially overwriting or dominating previous associations. A small input gate value means the current pair is written weakly, allowing existing associations to remain dominant. This data-dependent learning rate is a significant advancement over the constant learning rates of earlier Fast Weight Programmers, as it allows the model to selectively decide which pieces of information are worth storing strongly.

The output gate $\mathbf{o}_t$ serves the same role as in the original LSTM: it scales the retrieved vector to produce the hidden state. After the matrix memory retrieves $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t / \max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1\}$, the output gate modulates this retrieved information element-wise: $\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t$. This allows the model to control which dimensions of the retrieved information are passed forward to subsequent layers and time steps.

A critical difference between mLSTM and the original LSTM (and the sLSTM) is that the gates in mLSTM do not depend on the previous hidden state $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$. In the original LSTM, the gate pre-activations include a recurrent term $r \cdot h_{t-1}$, which creates a sequential dependency: one cannot compute the gates at time $t$ without first computing the hidden state at time $t-1$. The mLSTM removes these recurrent connections, computing gates solely from the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$. This design choice is what enables the parallelization of the mLSTM, at the cost of losing the memory mixing that recurrent connections provide.

## Normalizer State for mLSTM

A crucial component of the mLSTM is the normalizer state $\mathbf{n}_t$, which serves the same conceptual role as the normalizer state in the sLSTM but adapted for the matrix memory setting. The normalizer state tracks the accumulated strength of the input and forget gates over time, providing a reference against which the retrieved memory content can be normalized.

The normalizer state update is:

$$\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{k}_t$$

This equation mirrors the cell state update but with the key vector $\mathbf{k}_t$ replacing the outer product $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. The normalizer state can be understood as a weighted sum of key vectors, where each key vector is weighted by the product of its input gate and all subsequent forget gates. This accumulation of the key vectors, gated by the same factors that gate the cell state, provides the appropriate normalization scale for retrieval.

The role of the normalizer state becomes clear when we examine the retrieval operation. The raw retrieval from the matrix memory is $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$, which expands to:

$$\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t = \sum_{\tau=1}^{t} \left(\prod_{s=\tau+1}^{t} f_s\right) i_\tau \mathbf{v}_\tau (\mathbf{k}_\tau^\top \mathbf{q}_t)$$

This is a sum of value vectors $\mathbf{v}_\tau$, each weighted by the dot product $\mathbf{k}_\tau^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ (the similarity between the stored key and the current query) and by the product of gates $\left(\prod_{s=\tau+1}^{t} f_s\right) i_\tau$ (the accumulated gating strength). The normalizer state, when dotted with the query, gives:

$$\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t = \sum_{\tau=1}^{t} \left(\prod_{s=\tau+1}^{t} f_s\right) i_\tau (\mathbf{k}_\tau^\top \mathbf{q}_t)$$

This is the same sum of gate products and key-query similarities, but without the value vectors. Dividing the raw retrieval by this normalizer therefore produces a weighted average of the stored values, where the weights are determined by key-query similarity and accumulated gate strength. This normalization ensures that the output magnitude remains stable regardless of how many items have been stored or how large the gate values have become, which is essential for training stability.

The introduction of exponential gates makes this normalization particularly important. With sigmoid gates, the gate values are bounded between 0 and 1, so the accumulated products remain bounded. With exponential gates, the gate values can be arbitrarily large, and without normalization, the retrieved values could grow without bound. The normalizer state grows proportionally to the cell state, ensuring that the ratio $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t / (\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t)$ remains well-behaved.

## Handling Near-Zero Normalizer Values

While the normalizer state $\mathbf{n}_t$ provides essential normalization for the retrieval operation, a practical challenge arises when the dot product $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ is close to zero. This situation can occur when the query vector $\mathbf{q}_t$ is approximately orthogonal to the normalizer state, which can happen early in the sequence when little has been stored, or when the forget gate has decayed the normalizer state to near zero. In such cases, dividing by a near-zero value would produce numerically unstable or undefined results.

The mLSTM addresses this challenge by using the absolute value of the dot product and lower-bounding it by a threshold, which is typically set to 1.0. The retrieval formula is:

$$\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1\}}$$

Taking the absolute value $|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|$ ensures that the normalization is always positive, regardless of the sign of the dot product. This is important because the dot product between the normalizer (a sum of key vectors) and the query can be negative if the query and keys tend to point in opposite directions. Using the absolute value prevents sign flips in the normalized output that would occur if the dot product crossed zero.

The lower bound of 1.0 serves as a safety mechanism. When $|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t| < 1$, the denominator is clamped to 1, meaning the raw retrieval $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$ is passed through without amplification. This prevents the division from producing excessively large values when the normalizer is small. The choice of 1.0 as the threshold is a practical default; it means that when the accumulated gating strength is weak, the model effectively skips normalization and outputs the raw retrieval, which will naturally be small in magnitude when little has been stored.

This technique was previously used in the Retention model, and the paper adopts it for the mLSTM. It is a simple but effective solution that balances the need for normalization (when the normalizer is large enough to be meaningful) with the need for numerical stability (when the normalizer is too small to safely divide by). The threshold acts as a switch: when there is sufficient accumulated evidence in the normalizer state, the retrieval is normalized; when there is not, the raw retrieval is used directly, with its naturally small magnitude preventing any problematic behavior.

## Complete mLSTM Forward Pass Equations

Having examined each component of the mLSTM, we now present the complete forward pass equations. At each time step $t$, given the input $\mathbf{x}_t$, the mLSTM computes:

The cell state (matrix memory) is updated as:
$$\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$$

The normalizer state is updated as:
$$\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{k}_t$$

The hidden state is computed as:
$$\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t, \quad \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1\}}$$

The query, key, and value inputs are projected from the current input:
$$\mathbf{q}_t = \mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_q$$
$$\mathbf{k}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}} \mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_k$$
$$\mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_v$$

The input gate uses exponential activation:
$$i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \quad \tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + b_i$$

The forget gate can use either sigmoid or exponential activation:
$$f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t) \text{ OR } \exp(\tilde{f}_t), \quad \tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + b_f$$

The output gate uses sigmoid activation:
$$\mathbf{o}_t = \sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t), \quad \tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t = \mathbf{W}_o \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_o$$

Several aspects of these equations deserve attention. First, the key projection includes a scaling factor of $1/\sqrt{d}$, which normalizes the key vectors to have approximately unit scale regardless of the dimension $d$. This scaling is important for the numerical stability of the dot products $\mathbf{k}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ that appear in both the cell state retrieval and the normalizer state. Without this scaling, the dot products would grow with $\sqrt{d}$, potentially causing numerical issues in high dimensions.

Second, the input and forget gates are computed using weight vectors $\mathbf{w}_i$ and $\mathbf{w}_f$ (not matrices), and they depend only on the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ (not on the previous hidden state). This means the gates produce scalar values $i_t$ and $f_t$ that are applied uniformly across all dimensions of the matrix memory. The scalar nature of these gates is essential for the parallelization of the mLSTM, as we will discuss in the next section.

Third, the output gate $\mathbf{o}_t$ is a vector (not a scalar), computed using a weight matrix $\mathbf{W}_o$. This allows the output gate to selectively emphasize or suppress different dimensions of the retrieved vector $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t$, providing finer-grained control over the hidden state than a scalar output gate would allow. The element-wise multiplication $\mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t$ means each dimension of the retrieved information can be independently gated.

Fourth, the absence of recurrent connections in the gate computations is notable. In the original LSTM and in the sLSTM, the gate pre-activations include terms like $r_i h_{t-1}$ that depend on the previous hidden state. The mLSTM gates depend only on $\mathbf{x}_t$, which means all gate values for all time steps can be computed in parallel from the input sequence, before any recurrent processing begins.

## Exponential Gating in mLSTM and Stabilization Techniques

The mLSTM employs exponential activation functions for the input gate and optionally for the forget gate, replacing the sigmoid activation used in the original LSTM. The exponential function $\exp(x)$ is unbounded above, which means the gate values can grow arbitrarily large. This is a double-edged sword: on one hand, it allows the model to write new information into the matrix memory with much greater intensity than is possible with sigmoid gates (which are bounded between 0 and 1), and it allows the forget gate to decay old information much more aggressively. On the other hand, the unbounded nature of the exponential can lead to numerical overflow, where the gate values become so large that they exceed the representational capacity of floating-point numbers.

To address this numerical challenge, the mLSTM employs the same stabilization technique used in the sLSTM. The stabilization relies on an additional state $m_t$ that tracks the maximum of the log-gate values:

$$m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t))$$

The stabilizer state $m_t$ accumulates the log of the forget gate over time (since the forget gate acts multiplicatively on the cell state) and takes the element-wise maximum with the log of the current input gate. This gives the logarithm of the dominant scale of the cell state at time $t$.

Using the stabilizer state, the stabilized input and forget gates are computed as:

$$i'_t = \exp(\log(i_t) - m_t) = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t)$$
$$f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t)$$

The key insight is that the stabilized gates are computed in the log domain and then exponentiated with a subtraction of the stabilizer state, which ensures the exponentiated values are bounded. Since $m_t \geq \log(i_t)$ by construction, we have $i'_t \leq \exp(0) = 1$. Similarly, since $m_t \geq \log(f_t) + m_{t-1}$, we have $f'_t \leq 1$. The stabilized gates are therefore bounded between 0 and 1, preventing overflow.

Crucially, the paper proves that replacing $f_t$ with $f'_t$ and $i_t$ with $i'_t$ in the forward pass does not change the output of the network nor the derivatives of the loss with respect to the parameters. This means the stabilization is purely a numerical technique that has no effect on the mathematical behavior of the model—it produces exactly the same results as the unstabilized version would produce if infinite precision arithmetic were available. This is an important property, as it means the stabilization does not introduce any approximation or trade-off; it simply makes the computation numerically feasible.

The stabilization technique works by factoring out the dominant scale from the cell state and normalizer state. Instead of maintaining the cell state $\mathbf{C}_t$ at its true (potentially very large) scale, the stabilized computation maintains $\mathbf{C}_t / \exp(m_t)$, which is bounded. The normalizer state is similarly stabilized. The retrieved output $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t / \max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1\}$ is a ratio, so the scale factor $\exp(m_t)$ cancels out, producing the same result as the unstabilized computation.

## Query, Key, and Value Input Projections

The mLSTM projects the input $\mathbf{x}_t$ into three different vector spaces to produce the query, key, and value vectors. These projections are linear transformations defined by weight matrices and bias vectors, analogous to the query, key, and value projections in Transformer self-attention.

The query projection is:
$$\mathbf{q}_t = \mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_q$$

The key projection includes a scaling factor:
$$\mathbf{k}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}} \mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_k$$

The value projection is:
$$\mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_v$$

The weight matrices $\mathbf{W}_q, \mathbf{W}_k, \mathbf{W}_v \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_{\text{in}}}$ map from the input dimension $d_{\text{in}}$ to the memory dimension $d$, and the bias vectors $\mathbf{b}_q, \mathbf{b}_k, \mathbf{b}_v \in \mathbb{R}^d$ provide additional learnable offsets. These are the parameters that are learned during training; the matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t$ itself is not a parameter but a state that is updated through the gating mechanism.

The $1/\sqrt{d}$ scaling factor in the key projection serves a normalization purpose similar to the scaling used in Transformer attention. The dot product $\mathbf{k}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ that appears in the retrieval operation involves the sum of $d$ terms, each of which is the product of a key component and a query component. Without scaling, the variance of this dot product would grow linearly with $d$, causing the magnitude of the dot product to grow as $\sqrt{d}$. By scaling the keys by $1/\sqrt{d}$, the dot product $\mathbf{k}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ has approximately unit variance regardless of $d$, which keeps the retrieval numerically stable across different model sizes.

The separation of the input into three distinct projections—query, key, and value—gives the model flexibility in how it processes information. The same input $\mathbf{x}_t$ can serve different roles: as a key, it determines what "address" the current information should be stored at in the matrix memory; as a value, it determines what "content" should be stored; and as a query, it determines what information should be retrieved from memory. These three projections allow the model to learn to extract different aspects of the input for these different purposes, which is crucial for effective associative memory.

The output gate projection uses a full weight matrix $\mathbf{W}_o$ rather than a weight vector, reflecting the fact that the output gate is a vector that gates each dimension of the hidden state independently. This is in contrast to the input and forget gates, which are scalars applied uniformly to the matrix memory. The difference arises because the input and forget gates control the overall write and decay rates of the matrix memory, which naturally apply uniformly across all stored associations, while the output gate controls which aspects of the retrieved information to pass forward, which benefits from dimension-specific gating.

## Parallelization: Reformulating the Recurrence for Parallel Computation

One of the most significant advantages of the mLSTM over the original LSTM and the sLSTM is its ability to be parallelized across the time dimension. This parallelization is made possible by the absence of memory mixing—that is, the absence of recurrent connections that depend on the previous hidden state.

In the original LSTM, the gate computations at time $t$ depend on $h_{t-1}$, the hidden state from the previous time step. This creates a sequential chain: one must compute $h_1$ before computing the gates at $t=2$, compute $h_2$ before computing the gates at $t=3$, and so on. This sequential dependency means the forward pass cannot be parallelized across time steps, leading to $O(n)$ sequential operations for a sequence of length $n$.

The mLSTM eliminates this dependency by computing all gates solely from the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$, without any reference to $h_{t-1}$. The input gate, forget gate, query, key, and value at every time step can therefore be computed simultaneously from the input sequence, in a single matrix multiplication. This is a significant computational advantage, as it allows the gate computations to leverage the massive parallelism of modern GPUs.

The recurrent part of the mLSTM—the updates to the cell state $\mathbf{C}_t$ and normalizer state $\mathbf{n}_t$—still involves a sequential dependency through the forget gate: $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. However, this recurrence has a special structure that can be reformulated for parallel computation. The cell state at time $t$ can be written as a sum:

$$\mathbf{C}_t = \sum_{\tau=1}^{t} \left(\prod_{s=\tau+1}^{t} f_s\right) i_\tau \mathbf{v}_\tau \mathbf{k}_\tau^\top$$

This is a weighted sum of outer products, where each outer product is weighted by the product of forget gates from its time step to the current time step. The products of forget gates $\prod_{s=\tau+1}^{t} f_s$ are prefix products (or cumulative products) that can be computed efficiently using parallel scan algorithms. These algorithms, which are well-studied in the parallel computing literature, can compute all prefix products in $O(\log n)$ parallel steps rather than $O(n)$ sequential steps.

The paper draws an analogy to FlashAttention and Gated Linear Attention (GLA), which similarly reformulate sequential attention computations into parallel forms. The mLSTM's parallel formulation allows it to be trained efficiently on modern hardware, where the ability to parallelize across the time dimension is crucial for scaling to long sequences and large model sizes. This parallelizability is one of the key factors that allows the mLSTM to compete with Transformers, which are inherently parallelizable through their self-attention mechanism.

Despite this parallelization advantage, the paper notes that the current CUDA kernels for mLSTM are not fully optimized and are approximately 4 times slower than FlashAttention or the scan used in Mamba. However, the paper suggests that faster CUDA kernels could be obtained following the approach of FlashAttention, indicating that the parallelization potential of mLSTM has not yet been fully realized in the current implementation. The matrix operations involved in the mLSTM update and retrieval can be parallelized using standard GPU matrix operations, which mitigates the computational cost in practice.

## Equivalence of Multiple Heads and Multiple Cells in mLSTM

The mLSTM can have multiple memory cells, similar to the original LSTM where multiple scalar memory cells are combined into a vector. However, a distinctive feature of the mLSTM is that multiple heads and multiple cells are equivalent, which is a direct consequence of the absence of memory mixing.

In the sLSTM, multiple memory cells enable memory mixing through recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R}_z, \mathbf{R}_i, \mathbf{R}_f, \mathbf{R}_o$ that connect the hidden state vector (which combines all cells) back to the cell inputs and gates. These recurrent connections allow information stored in one cell to influence the updates of other cells, creating a form of inter-cell communication. Additionally, the sLSTM can have multiple heads, where memory mixing occurs within each head (across cells) but not across heads. This head structure, combined with exponential gating, establishes a new form of memory mixing in the sLSTM.

In the mLSTM, however, there are no recurrent connections from the hidden state to the gates or cell inputs. The gates at each time step depend only on the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$, not on any previous hidden state. This means there is no mechanism for information stored in one memory cell to influence the updates of another memory cell. Each memory cell operates independently, processing the same input but maintaining its own matrix memory and producing its own output.

Because of this independence, having multiple memory cells is equivalent to having multiple heads. In both cases, the model maintains multiple independent matrix memories, each updated by the same gates (or independently computed gates) and each producing its own contribution to the output. The distinction between "cells" and "heads" that is meaningful in the sLSTM (where cells within a head interact but heads do not) collapses in the mLSTM, where no interaction occurs in either case.

This equivalence simplifies the architecture of the mLSTM. Rather than needing to design separate cell and head structures, the model can simply specify a number of independent matrix memories, each of which processes the input in parallel. The outputs of these memories can then be combined (e.g., by concatenation and projection) to form the final hidden state. This architectural simplicity is a benefit of the mLSTM's design choice to sacrifice memory mixing in favor of parallelizability.

The absence of memory mixing is both a strength and a limitation of the mLSTM. It is a strength because it enables parallelization and architectural simplicity. It is a limitation because memory mixing allows the sLSTM to solve state tracking problems that are beyond the reach of models without memory mixing, as demonstrated by the formal language experiments in the paper. This is why the xLSTM architecture combines both mLSTM and sLSTM blocks: the mLSTM blocks provide high-capacity, parallelizable processing, while the sLSTM blocks provide the memory mixing needed for state tracking and more expressive sequence processing. The typical ratio used in the experiments is xLSTM[7:1], meaning seven mLSTM blocks for every one sLSTM block, suggesting that the parallelizable matrix memory of the mLSTM forms the backbone of the architecture, with the sLSTM providing complementary capabilities that the mLSTM cannot achieve alone.

# xLSTM Architecture: Blocks and Stacking

The core innovations of xLSTM—exponential gating and novel memory structures—do not by themselves constitute a complete neural network architecture. To build a model capable of language modeling at scale, the sLSTM and mLSTM memory cells must be embedded into larger computational units that can effectively process, transform, and propagate information across many layers. This chapter explains how sLSTM and mLSTM are integrated into residual block architectures, how these blocks are stacked to form complete xLSTM architectures, and the theoretical and practical rationale guiding these design decisions.

# Cover's Theorem: Non-Linear Separation in High-Dimensional Spaces

A central design principle of xLSTM blocks is the idea that the past should be non-linearly summarized in a high-dimensional space. The motivation for this principle comes from Cover's Theorem, a classical result from the theory of pattern classification. Cover's Theorem states that patterns which are non-linearly embedded into a higher-dimensional space can more likely be linearly separated than in the original, lower-dimensional space. In other words, by mapping data through a non-linear function into a space with more dimensions, one increases the probability that complex, intertwined patterns become separable by simple linear decision boundaries.

The relevance of Cover's Theorem to sequence modeling is direct and profound. The goal of an xLSTM block is to non-linearly summarize the past—meaning all the information from previous sequence elements—so that different histories or contexts can be better separated from one another. This separation is a prerequisite for correctly predicting the next sequence element, such as the next token in a language model. If two distinct histories produce representations that are too similar, the model will struggle to make different predictions for them, leading to poor performance. By lifting the representation into a higher-dimensional space and applying non-linear transformations, the xLSTM architecture increases the separability of different histories, thereby improving its predictive capability.

In the xLSTM architecture, Cover's Theorem provides the justification for incorporating up-projection steps—operations that linearly map from the original model dimension to a higher-dimensional space, apply non-linear activations, and then map back down. These up-projection steps create the high-dimensional non-linear embedding that Cover's Theorem suggests is beneficial for separability. The two block types in xLSTM differ in where this up-projection occurs relative to the recurrent memory operation, but both share the same underlying rationale rooted in Cover's Theorem.

# Block Type (i): Post Up-Projection Residual Block for sLSTM

The first block type used in xLSTM is the post up-projection residual block, which is designed to house the sLSTM. The term "post up-projection" refers to the fact that the up-projection step comes after the recurrent memory operation in the forward pass of the block. This design is analogous to the block structure used in Transformers.

In a post up-projection block, the sLSTM first non-linearly summarizes the past in the original model dimension. That is, the sLSTM takes the current input, processes it through its scalar memory cells with exponential gating and memory mixing, and produces a hidden state that summarizes the sequence history up to the current time step. This summarization happens entirely within the original dimensionality of the model's hidden space. After this recurrent summarization step, the block linearly maps the resulting representation into a high-dimensional space, applies a non-linear activation function in that high-dimensional space, and then linearly maps back down to the original space. This up-projection-down-projection sequence is a feed-forward network that further processes the sLSTM's output, expanding its representational power.

The choice of post up-projection for the sLSTM is deliberate. The sLSTM operates with a scalar memory and includes memory mixing through recurrent hidden-to-hidden connections. Because the sLSTM's memory mixing requires sequential processing, the recurrent operation must take place in the original dimensionality where the recurrent weight matrices are defined. The up-projection then serves to expand the sLSTM's output into a richer representation space where non-linear feed-forward transformations can extract and amplify useful features. The high-dimensional space is used for the feed-forward processing, not for the recurrent memory itself, which is consistent with the sLSTM's scalar memory structure.

# Block Type (ii): Pre Up-Projection Residual Block for mLSTM

The second block type is the pre up-projection residual block, which is designed to house the mLSTM. The term "pre up-projection" indicates that the up-projection step comes before the recurrent memory operation. This design is analogous to the block structure used in State Space Models.

In a pre up-projection block, the input is first linearly mapped from the original model dimension into a high-dimensional space. The mLSTM then performs its recurrent memory operation—storing and retrieving key-value pairs through its matrix memory with the covariance update rule—entirely within this high-dimensional space. After the mLSTM has summarized the past in the high-dimensional space, the block linearly maps the result back down to the original model dimension. The non-linear summarization of the past thus takes place in the high-dimensional space, not in the original space.

The critical advantage of this design for the mLSTM is that the matrix memory operates in the high-dimensional space, giving it a much larger memory capacity. Recall that the mLSTM stores a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ as its cell state, where $d$ is the dimension of the space in which the mLSTM operates. By performing the up-projection before the mLSTM, the effective dimension $d$ of the matrix memory is increased, which increases the storage capacity of the memory. The matrix memory can store more key-value associations when it operates in a higher-dimensional space, directly addressing the limited storage capacity that was one of the three main limitations of the original LSTM.

Since the mLSTM has no memory mixing—meaning there are no hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections—it is fully parallelizable. The pre up-projection does not introduce any sequential dependency, because the linear projection can be applied to all time steps independently before the parallel recurrent computation. This preserves the parallelizability that is a key advantage of the mLSTM over the original LSTM and the sLSTM.

# Rationale for Block Assignment: sLSTM Uses Post Up-Projection, mLSTM Uses Pre Up-Projection

The assignment of post up-projection to sLSTM and pre up-projection to mLSTM is not arbitrary but is motivated by the distinct characteristics of each memory type. The paper provides clear reasoning for this design choice.

For the sLSTM, the post up-projection block is used. The sLSTM features a scalar memory and, crucially, memory mixing through recurrent hidden-to-hidden connections. The memory mixing means that the hidden state from one time step directly influences the computation at the next time step through the recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{z}}$, $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{i}}$, $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{f}}$, and $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{o}}$. These recurrent weights are defined within the model's original dimensionality. Therefore, the recurrent summarization must take place in the original space, and the up-projection can only be applied afterward as a feed-forward transformation. Placing the up-projection before the sLSTM would break the memory mixing, because the recurrent connections would need to operate in the high-dimensional space, dramatically increasing the number of parameters in the recurrent weight matrices and the computational cost of the sequential processing.

For the mLSTM, the pre up-projection block is used. The mLSTM features a matrix memory with a covariance update rule, and it has no memory mixing. Because there are no hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections, there is no constraint forcing the recurrent operation to take place in the original dimensionality. The mLSTM can operate in any dimension, and operating in a higher dimension directly increases the memory capacity of the matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. The paper explicitly states that the pre up-projection block is chosen for the mLSTM "since the memory capacity becomes larger in the high-dimensional space." Since the mLSTM is parallelizable, the up-projection can be applied to all time steps simultaneously before the parallel recurrent computation, so the pre up-projection does not introduce any sequential bottleneck.

In summary, the block assignment respects the structural requirements of each memory type: the sLSTM's memory mixing constrains it to operate in the original space with post up-projection, while the mLSTM's lack of memory mixing and its matrix memory structure make it ideal for pre up-projection, which maximizes memory capacity.

# Non-Linear Summarization of the Past in High-Dimensional Space

Both xLSTM block types share the common goal of non-linearly summarizing the past in a high-dimensional space, but they achieve this goal through different orderings of operations. Understanding what "non-linear summarization of the past" means is essential for grasping the xLSTM architecture.

The "past" in a sequence model refers to all the information from previous time steps that is relevant for predicting future elements. In an autoregressive language model, for example, the past consists of all preceding tokens, and the model must use this context to predict the next token. The recurrent memory cells (sLSTM or mLSTM) are the mechanisms that accumulate and store this information over time. The cell state at time $t$ contains a summary of the sequence up to that point.

The term "non-linear summarization" refers to the fact that the recurrent memory operation involves non-linear transformations. In both sLSTM and mLSTM, the input and forget gates use exponential or sigmoid activation functions, and the cell input and output involve non-linear activation functions such as $\tanh$. These non-linearities allow the model to selectively store, forget, and retrieve information, creating a compressed, non-linear representation of the entire sequence history.

The "high-dimensional space" is introduced through the up-projection step. In both block types, the representation is mapped to a space of higher dimensionality than the model's base dimension. Cover's Theorem tells us that this high-dimensional non-linear embedding improves the separability of different histories, which is crucial for making accurate predictions. In the post up-projection block, the high-dimensional non-linear transformation is applied as a feed-forward network after the sLSTM has summarized the past in the original space. In the pre up-projection block, the mLSTM itself performs the non-linear summarization directly in the high-dimensional space, leveraging the increased dimensionality for greater memory capacity.

The interplay between the recurrent memory and the high-dimensional projection is what gives xLSTM blocks their power. The recurrent memory provides the ability to accumulate information over long sequences, while the high-dimensional projection provides the representational capacity to distinguish between different histories and extract meaningful features for prediction.

# Pre-LayerNorm Residual Backbones as Used in Contemporary LLMs

The xLSTM architecture relies on pre-LayerNorm residual backbones, which are the standard architectural pattern used in contemporary Large Language Models. LayerNorm, short for Layer Normalization, is a technique that normalizes the activations of a layer across the feature dimension, helping to stabilize training by reducing internal covariate shift. The "pre" in pre-LayerNorm indicates that the normalization is applied before the main transformation of each block, rather than after.

In a pre-LayerNorm residual backbone, each residual block receives its input, applies LayerNorm to the input, processes the normalized input through the block's main transformation (either the sLSTM or mLSTM memory operation, followed by the appropriate projection), and then adds the result to the original un-normalized input through a residual connection. This can be described as follows: if the input to a block is $\mathbf{x}$, the output is $\mathbf{x} + \text{Block}(\text{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{x}))$, where $\text{Block}$ represents the main computation of the block. The residual connection ensures that the gradient can flow directly through the network, mitigating the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks and enabling the construction of architectures with many layers.

The choice of pre-LayerNorm over post-LayerNorm is significant. Post-LayerNorm, where normalization is applied after the block's transformation and before the residual addition, was used in the original Transformer architecture. However, pre-LayerNorm has been found to be more stable for training very deep models, particularly at large scale. Since xLSTM is designed to be scaled to billions of parameters, comparable to modern Large Language Models, the use of pre-LayerNorm is a natural and pragmatic choice that aligns with established best practices in the field. The paper explicitly states that the xLSTM architecture relies on "the most commonly used pre-LayerNorm residual backbones as used in contemporary Large Language Models."

# Residual Stacking of xLSTM Blocks

An xLSTM architecture is constructed by residually stacking multiple xLSTM blocks on top of one another. The concept of residual stacking builds on the residual connection framework introduced by ResNet, where each layer's output is added to its input, allowing the network to learn residual functions rather than full transformations. This approach has been instrumental in enabling the training of very deep neural networks, including both deep convolutional networks and Transformers.

In the xLSTM architecture, the stacking works as follows. The input sequence is first embedded into a continuous representation. This representation is then passed through a series of xLSTM blocks, each of which is a residual block containing either an sLSTM (with post up-projection) or an mLSTM (with pre up-projection). Each block applies its pre-LayerNorm, processes the normalized input through its memory operation and projection, and adds the result to the input via a residual connection. The output of one block becomes the input to the next block. After passing through all blocks, the final representation is used to produce predictions, such as logits over a vocabulary for next-token prediction.

The depth of the stack—that is, the number of xLSTM blocks—is a key architectural hyperparameter that determines the model's capacity. Deeper stacks allow the model to learn more complex transformations and abstractions, but they also increase the computational cost and the risk of training instability. The residual connections and pre-LayerNorm are essential for making deep stacks trainable, as they ensure stable gradient flow and activation magnitudes throughout the network.

The paper draws an explicit parallel to the construction of other large deep learning models, noting that "like almost all contemporary large deep learning models, xLSTM architectures are constructed by residually stacking building blocks." This construction principle enabled the success of deep convolutional networks like ResNet and of Transformers, which are the backbone of modern Large Language Models such as GPT-3, ChatGPT, GPT-4, Llama, and many others. By adopting the same construction principle, xLSTM ensures that it can benefit from the same training stability and scaling properties that have made these models successful.

# Notation: xLSTM[$a$:$b$] for Ratio of mLSTM to sLSTM Blocks

An xLSTM architecture typically contains a mixture of mLSTM-based and sLSTM-based blocks. To describe the composition of a particular architecture, the paper introduces the notation xLSTM[$a$:$b$], where $a/b$ denotes the ratio of mLSTM-based blocks to sLSTM-based blocks. For example, xLSTM[7:1] means that out of every eight blocks, seven are mLSTM-based blocks and one is an sLSTM-based block. For a model with a total of 48 blocks, this ratio translates to 42 mLSTM-based blocks and 6 sLSTM-based blocks.

This notation is important because the two types of blocks serve complementary roles. The mLSTM-based blocks provide large memory capacity through their matrix memory and are fully parallelizable, making them efficient for training and inference. The sLSTM-based blocks provide memory mixing, which enables state tracking—the ability to solve problems that require tracking the internal state of a process, such as evaluating code or tracking entities in a long narrative. State tracking is a capability that models without memory mixing, such as Transformers and State Space Models, fundamentally lack. By mixing both types of blocks, xLSTM architectures combine the strengths of both: the memory capacity and parallelizability of mLSTM, and the state tracking ability of sLSTM.

The notation also encompasses the extreme cases. xLSTM[1:0] denotes an architecture composed entirely of mLSTM-based blocks, with no sLSTM blocks. xLSTM[0:1] denotes an architecture composed entirely of sLSTM-based blocks, with no mLSTM blocks. These extreme cases are useful for ablation studies and for understanding the individual contributions of each block type.

# Architecture Design Considerations and Block Placement

The design of xLSTM architectures involves several considerations that guide the placement and ratio of mLSTM and sLSTM blocks within the residual stack. The paper establishes several key principles.

First, all experiments described in the paper use pre up-projection blocks for mLSTM and post up-projection blocks for sLSTM. This consistent assignment ensures that each block type is always paired with the projection strategy that best suits its memory structure, as discussed earlier. The mLSTM always benefits from the enlarged memory capacity of the high-dimensional space, while the sLSTM always retains its memory mixing in the original space.

Second, the ratio of mLSTM to sLSTM blocks is a design choice that balances parallelizability and memory capacity against state tracking capability. Since sLSTM blocks are not parallelizable due to memory mixing, they introduce a sequential processing bottleneck. A higher proportion of mLSTM blocks improves training and inference efficiency, while a higher proportion of sLSTM blocks enhances the model's state tracking ability. The xLSTM[7:1] ratio, used in many of the paper's experiments, represents a design that is heavily weighted toward the parallelizable mLSTM while still incorporating enough sLSTM blocks to provide state tracking capabilities.

Third, the overall depth and width of the architecture are determined by the target model size. The paper trains models ranging from 125M to 1.3B parameters, with architectures that match GPT-3 models in size—for example, a model with embedding dimension 1024 and 24 residual blocks. The number of blocks, the model dimension, and the up-projection factor all contribute to the total parameter count and must be balanced to achieve the desired model size while maintaining training stability.

Fourth, the initialization of forget gates must be chosen carefully, as noted in the paper's discussion of limitations. Since the forget gate controls how much of the past memory is retained, its initial value can significantly affect training dynamics, especially in the early stages. This is a practical consideration that must be addressed when designing and training xLSTM architectures.

Finally, the paper notes that the architecture and hyperparameters were not fully optimized, particularly for larger xLSTM architectures, due to the expensive computational load of large language experiments. This suggests that there is room for improvement in architecture design through more extensive optimization, and that the current results likely represent a lower bound on xLSTM's potential performance. The scaling laws observed in the experiments indicate that larger xLSTM models will continue to perform favorably compared to Transformers and State Space Models, making further architecture optimization a promising direction for future work.

# Memory and Computational Complexity Considerations

## Linear Computation Complexity with Respect to Sequence Length

A fundamental distinction between xLSTM networks and Transformers lies in how their computational demands scale with the length of the input sequence. Transformers rely on self-attention, a mechanism that requires every token in a sequence to attend to every other token. This pairwise comparison inherently produces a computational complexity that is quadratic in the sequence length. If the sequence length is denoted by $L$, then the self-attention mechanism requires $O(L^2)$ operations because each of the $L$ tokens must compute attention scores against all $L$ tokens, including itself. As sequences grow longer, this quadratic scaling becomes a severe bottleneck, both in terms of computation time and memory usage for storing the attention matrix.

In contrast, xLSTM networks process sequences through recurrent updates to their memory states. At each time step $t$, the network updates its cell state and produces a hidden state based on the current input and the previous state. Because each step involves a fixed amount of computation—updating gates, computing the cell state, and producing the hidden state—the total computational cost scales linearly with the sequence length, that is, $O(L)$. Whether the sequence contains a hundred tokens or a hundred thousand, each new token requires the same fixed computational effort to process. This linear scaling is a property inherited from the original LSTM architecture and preserved in both the sLSTM and mLSTM variants of xLSTM.

This difference in scaling has profound practical implications. For tasks involving very long sequences—such as processing entire documents, long code files, or extended conversations—the quadratic cost of Transformers can become prohibitive, requiring enormous computational resources and memory. xLSTM networks, by virtue of their linear complexity, can handle such sequences far more efficiently. The paper demonstrates this advantage empirically, showing that while the Llama Transformer model exhibits quadratic scaling in text generation time, xLSTM and other recurrent models like Mamba and RWKV-4 scale linearly, maintaining fast generation times even as the sequence grows long.

## Constant Memory Complexity with Respect to Sequence Length

Beyond computational complexity, the memory complexity of xLSTM networks is also a critical advantage over Transformers. In a Transformer, the self-attention mechanism requires storing an $L \times L$ attention matrix, where $L$ is the sequence length. This means that the memory required grows quadratically with the sequence length, as $O(L^2)$. Additionally, during inference, Transformers typically employ a key-value cache that stores the keys and values for all previously processed tokens. This cache grows linearly with the sequence length, meaning that longer contexts require proportionally more memory to store the cached representations. For very long sequences, this cache can become a significant memory burden.

xLSTM networks, on the other hand, maintain a constant memory complexity with respect to the sequence length. The memory of an xLSTM is contained entirely within its cell state, which has a fixed size regardless of how many tokens have been processed. In the sLSTM, the cell state is a scalar (or a vector of scalars when multiple memory cells are used), and in the mLSTM, the cell state is a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, where $d$ is the hidden dimension. In both cases, the size of the memory state depends only on the model's dimensionality, not on the sequence length. As the network processes each new token, it updates this fixed-size state in place, overwriting or modifying previous content as needed, without requiring additional storage for past tokens.

This constant memory complexity means that xLSTM networks can, in principle, process sequences of arbitrary length without running out of memory, as long as the fixed-size state can be maintained. The paper highlights this property by showing that xLSTM models can handle context lengths up to 16,384 tokens during inference, even when trained on a context length of only 2,048 tokens, while maintaining low perplexity. This is in stark contrast to Transformer models, which would require memory proportional to the context length for their key-value cache.

## Compressive Memory: Suitability for Industrial and Edge Applications

The constant memory complexity of xLSTM networks reflects a fundamentally compressive approach to memory. Rather than storing all past information explicitly—as a Transformer does with its key-value cache—xLSTM networks compress the history of the sequence into a fixed-size state. Each new piece of information is integrated into this state through the gating mechanisms, and older information may be decayed or overwritten depending on the forget and input gates. This compression means that the network does not retain a perfect record of all past tokens but instead maintains a summary that is sufficient for predicting future tokens.

The compressive nature of xLSTM memory makes it particularly well suited for industrial applications and implementations on edge devices. Edge devices, such as mobile phones, embedded systems, and Internet of Things (IoT) devices, typically have stringent memory and computational constraints. A model that requires memory quadratic in the sequence length, like a Transformer, would quickly exhaust the available resources on such devices when processing long sequences. xLSTM networks, with their constant memory footprint and linear computational cost, can operate within these constraints, making them viable for deployment in resource-limited environments.

Furthermore, the paper notes that xLSTM networks are well suited for industrial applications more broadly. In industrial settings, models may need to process continuous streams of data over extended periods—such as sensor readings from manufacturing equipment, monitoring signals from power grids, or hydrological data for flood warnings, an application where LSTMs have already proven successful. The constant memory complexity ensures that the model can process these streams indefinitely without its memory requirements growing over time. The linear computational complexity ensures that each new data point can be processed in a fixed amount of time, enabling real-time or near-real-time processing.

## mLSTM Matrix Memory: No Parameters Required but Computationally Expensive

The mLSTM variant of xLSTM introduces a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, which significantly increases the storage capacity of the network compared to the scalar memory of the original LSTM or the sLSTM. This matrix memory is updated using a covariance (outer product) update rule, where a key-value pair $(\mathbf{k}_t, \mathbf{v}_t)$ is stored via the outer product $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. The update rule is $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$, where $f_t$ is the forget gate and $i_t$ is the input gate. Retrieval is performed by multiplying the query vector $\mathbf{q}_t$ with the cell state matrix: $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t / \max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1\}$.

An important property of this matrix memory is that it does not require additional parameters. The matrix $\mathbf{C}$ is a state that is computed and updated at each time step, but it is not a learnable parameter of the model. The learnable parameters of the mLSTM are the weight matrices $\mathbf{W}_q$, $\mathbf{W}_k$, $\mathbf{W}_v$, $\mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{o}}$, the weight vectors $\mathbf{w}_i$, $\mathbf{w}_f$, and the bias terms, which are used to project the input $\mathbf{x}_t$ into queries, keys, values, and gates. The matrix memory itself is purely a runtime state. This stands in contrast to attention-based mechanisms in Transformers, where the attention computation, while not requiring explicit memory parameters, does require storing all keys and values for the duration of the sequence.

However, the matrix memory of mLSTM is computationally expensive. The cell state $\mathbf{C}$ is a $d \times d$ matrix, and updating it at each time step involves computing the outer product $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ (which is $O(d^2)$) and adding it to the previous state (also $O(d^2)$). Retrieval via the matrix-vector product $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$ is $O(d^2)$. These operations are more expensive than the scalar updates of the sLSTM, which are $O(d)$ per time step. The paper acknowledges this increased computational cost, noting that the $d \times d$ matrix memory and the $d \times d$ update are computationally expensive operations.

## Trade-off Between Memory Capacity and Computational Complexity

The introduction of the matrix memory in mLSTM represents a deliberate trade-off between memory capacity and computational complexity. The original LSTM and the sLSTM use a scalar memory cell, which compresses all information into a single scalar value per memory cell. While this is computationally efficient—requiring only $O(d)$ operations per time step—it limits the amount of information that can be stored. The paper illustrates this limitation through the "Rare Token Prediction" task, where the original LSTM performs worse on rare tokens because its limited storage capacity cannot adequately retain information about infrequently occurring tokens.

The mLSTM addresses this limitation by expanding the memory from a scalar $c \in \mathbb{R}$ to a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This matrix can store $d^2$ values, dramatically increasing the storage capacity and enabling the network to retain more detailed information about past tokens. The paper demonstrates this improved capacity through the Multi-Query Associative Recall (MQAR) task, where xLSTM models with mLSTM blocks perform best among all non-Transformer models, even for the most difficult task variant with 256 key-value pairs.

However, this increased capacity comes at the cost of higher computational complexity. While the sLSTM's scalar updates are $O(d)$ per time step, the mLSTM's matrix updates are $O(d^2)$ per time step. The paper explicitly states that this is a trade-off: "We trade off memory capacity against computational complexity." The matrix memory provides greater capacity to store and retrieve information, but each update and retrieval operation is more expensive due to the matrix operations involved.

Despite this increased per-step cost, the overall complexity remains linear in the sequence length $L$, as each step requires a fixed $O(d^2)$ computation. The total cost is $O(L \cdot d^2)$, which is still linear in $L$ and thus more favorable than the $O(L^2 \cdot d)$ complexity of Transformer self-attention for sufficiently long sequences.

## Parallel Computation of mLSTM Matrix Operations on GPUs

Although the mLSTM's matrix operations are computationally expensive, they have a significant compensating advantage: they can be parallelized on GPUs. The key insight is that the mLSTM, unlike the sLSTM, does not have memory mixing—that is, there are no hidden-hidden recurrent connections that would enforce sequential processing. The gates in mLSTM depend only on the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the bias terms, not on the previous hidden state $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$. This is evident from the forward pass equations, where the input gate, forget gate, and output gate are all computed as functions of $\mathbf{x}_t$ alone: $\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + b_i$, $\tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + b_f$, and $\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t = \mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{o}} \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_{\mathbf{o}}$. Similarly, the query, key, and value inputs are all linear projections of $\mathbf{x}_t$.

Because the gates and inputs at each time step can be computed independently of the gates and inputs at other time steps, the computation of these quantities across all time steps can be parallelized. The recurrent update of the cell state $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ can also be reformulated into a parallel form, analogous to how parallel scans are used in other recurrent architectures. The paper refers to this as a parallel version of the recurrence and notes that details are provided in the appendix.

Furthermore, the matrix operations themselves—outer products, matrix-vector products, and element-wise operations—are precisely the types of operations that GPUs are highly optimized to perform. Modern GPUs excel at dense matrix multiplication and can execute these operations with high throughput. The paper notes that "the computations can be done in parallel on GPUs, therefore these computations have only a minor effect on the wall clock time." This means that despite the theoretical $O(d^2)$ cost per step, the actual time taken to perform these operations on a GPU may be quite small relative to other parts of the computation, because the GPU can execute many operations in parallel.

## Parallelizability Comparison: mLSTM vs. sLSTM

The parallelizability of mLSTM and sLSTM differs fundamentally due to the presence or absence of memory mixing. Memory mixing refers to the hidden-hidden recurrent connections, where the previous hidden state $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ is used in computing the current time step's gates and cell input. In the sLSTM, these connections are present: the cell input, input gate, forget gate, and output gate all depend on the previous hidden state through recurrent weights $r_z$, $r_i$, $r_f$, and $r_o$. Specifically, $\tilde{z}_t = \mathbf{w}_z^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_z h_{t-1} + b_z$, and similarly for the gates. Because $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ depends on the computation at time step $t-1$, which in turn depends on step $t-2$, and so on, the sLSTM must process the sequence sequentially, one time step at a time. This sequential dependency chain is what prevents parallelization.

The mLSTM, by contrast, has no memory mixing. The gates and the query, key, and value projections depend only on the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and not on the previous hidden state. This independence means that the computations at different time steps do not depend on each other's results (for the gate and projection computations), allowing them to be performed in parallel. The recurrent update of the cell state can also be parallelized using techniques analogous to parallel scans, as mentioned above.

The paper clearly states this distinction: "While mLSTM is parallelizable analog to FlashAttention or GLA, sLSTM is not parallelizable due to the memory mixing (hidden-hidden connections)." This difference has significant implications for training efficiency. During training, the mLSTM can process entire sequences in parallel, leveraging the full computational power of the GPU, similar to how Transformers process sequences in parallel during training. The sLSTM, by contrast, must process sequences sequentially, which can slow down training.

## Parallelization Analogy to FlashAttention and GLA

The paper draws an analogy between the parallelization of mLSTM and two existing methods: FlashAttention and Gated Linear Attention (GLA). FlashAttention is a technique developed to compute attention more efficiently on GPUs by reducing memory reads and writes through tiling and recomputation. It allows the attention mechanism to be computed without materializing the full $L \times L$ attention matrix in memory, instead processing the computation in blocks that fit within the GPU's fast on-chip memory (SRAM). This significantly reduces the memory bandwidth bottleneck of attention, enabling faster computation.

GLA, or Gated Linear Attention, is a method that linearizes the attention mechanism by replacing the softmax normalization with a gating mechanism and computing attention in a recurrent form that can be parallelized. Like FlashAttention, GLA uses techniques to parallelize what would otherwise be a sequential computation, enabling efficient GPU utilization.

The mLSTM's parallelization is analogous to these methods in that it reformulates a recurrent computation into a form that can be executed in parallel on a GPU. The key similarity is that all three methods—FlashAttention, GLA, and mLSTM—find ways to express computations that are inherently sequential (attention over a sequence, or recurrent updates over time) in a way that allows parallel execution. For mLSTM, this is possible because the absence of memory mixing means the per-step computations are independent, and the recurrent update can be reformulated as a parallel scan.

This parallelization is crucial for practical training of large models. Without it, the sequential nature of the recurrence would make training prohibitively slow for large-scale language modeling tasks. By enabling parallel processing, the mLSTM can be trained at scale using the same hardware acceleration that has made Transformers practical for large language models.

## CUDA Implementation of sLSTM with GPU Memory Optimizations

Because the sLSTM cannot be parallelized due to its memory mixing, its implementation requires special attention to achieve acceptable performance. The paper reports the development of a fast CUDA implementation for sLSTM that includes GPU memory optimizations at the register level. CUDA (Compute Unified Device Architecture) is NVIDIA's parallel computing platform, which allows developers to write programs that execute on the GPU. By implementing the sLSTM directly in CUDA, the authors can take advantage of low-level GPU features that are not accessible through higher-level frameworks like PyTorch.

The optimization to the register level is particularly significant. GPU memory is organized in a hierarchy, ranging from global memory (large but slow) to shared memory (smaller but faster) to registers (smallest but fastest). By keeping frequently accessed data in registers, the implementation can minimize the number of slow memory accesses, significantly improving throughput. The sequential nature of sLSTM means that at each time step, the previous hidden state and cell state must be accessed to compute the current step's values. By keeping these states in registers, the implementation can avoid the latency of reading from and writing to global memory at each step.

The paper describes this implementation as involving "GPU memory optimizations to the register level," indicating a careful, low-level engineering effort to make the sequential sLSTM computation as fast as possible. This kind of optimization is essential because, without it, the sequential processing of sLSTM could become a major bottleneck in training and inference, potentially negating the advantages of the xLSTM architecture.

## Performance Comparison: sLSTM CUDA Kernel vs. mLSTM

Despite the sLSTM's lack of parallelizability, the paper reports that the custom CUDA implementation achieves performance that is competitive with the parallel mLSTM. Specifically, the sLSTM CUDA kernel is "typically less than two times slower than mLSTM." This is a remarkable result given the fundamental difference in parallelizability between the two variants.

The fact that the sLSTM is less than two times slower—rather than orders of magnitude slower—can be attributed to several factors. First, the mLSTM's matrix operations, while parallelizable, are computationally expensive due to the $d \times d$ matrix updates. The total amount of computation per step is higher for mLSTM than for sLSTM, even though mLSTM can parallelize across time steps. Second, the sLSTM's scalar operations are inherently cheaper per step, requiring only $O(d)$ computation compared to mLSTM's $O(d^2)$. Third, the register-level optimizations in the CUDA kernel minimize memory access overhead, allowing the sequential computation to proceed efficiently.

This performance comparison has important implications for the design of xLSTM architectures. The paper uses a notation xLSTM[$a$:$b$] to denote the ratio of mLSTM-based blocks to sLSTM-based blocks. For example, xLSTM[7:1] means that out of eight blocks, seven are mLSTM-based and one is sLSTM-based. The fact that sLSTM blocks are not prohibitively slower than mLSTM blocks means that architectures can include a meaningful number of sLSTM blocks without significantly impacting overall training and inference speed. The sLSTM blocks contribute capabilities that mLSTM blocks cannot provide—specifically, memory mixing, which enables state tracking and is important for tasks like evaluating code or tracking entities in long narratives.

The paper also notes, in its discussion of limitations, that the current CUDA kernels for mLSTM are not fully optimized and are about four times slower than FlashAttention or the scan used in Mamba. This suggests that further optimization of the mLSTM kernels, in the spirit of FlashAttention, could improve performance even further. Similarly, the sLSTM kernel, while already achieving less than 2x slowdown relative to mLSTM, could potentially be improved with additional optimization. These observations indicate that the current implementation, while functional, has not yet reached its full performance potential, and that future engineering efforts could further reduce the gap between xLSTM and competing architectures like Transformers and State Space Models.

# Related Work: Positioning xLSTM in the Landscape

The development of xLSTM does not occur in isolation. It is situated within a rich and rapidly evolving landscape of sequence modeling architectures, each motivated by different limitations of the Transformer's quadratic attention mechanism or the traditional LSTM's sequential processing bottleneck. To understand where xLSTM stands and why its specific design choices matter, we must survey the major families of methods that have emerged as alternatives to or evolutions of the original LSTM. These include linear attention methods that seek to reduce the computational cost of self-attention, state space models that draw on continuous-time dynamical systems, recurrent neural networks that revisit and modernize the RNN paradigm, and the gating mechanisms that permeate many of these architectures. We also examine the covariance update rule, a concept central to xLSTM's matrix memory, and how it appears in other models. Finally, we identify the models most closely related to xLSTM and articulate the distinctive advantages that xLSTM holds, particularly through its sLSTM variant's memory mixing and the resulting expressiveness gains.

# Linear Attention Methods

The Transformer's self-attention mechanism, while powerful, scales quadratically with the sequence length, making it costly for very long contexts. A line of research has sought to make attention linear in the context length, thereby enabling the processing of much longer sequences without prohibitive computational expense. The Synthesizer, for instance, takes an radical approach by learning synthetic attention weights without any token--token interactions at all. Rather than computing attention scores as dot products between query and key vectors, the Synthesizer generates attention weights from each token independently, severing the pairwise interaction that defines standard attention. This eliminates the quadratic bottleneck but also sacrifices the relational structure that makes attention so effective.

Linformer approaches the problem from a different angle. It realizes self-attention through a low-rank matrix approximation, projecting the attention matrix into a lower-dimensional space. By constraining the attention matrix to be low-rank, Linformer can even linearly approximate the full attention computation, achieving linear complexity in the context length while retaining some of the structure of standard attention.

The Linear Transformer goes further by linearizing the attention mechanism itself. The key insight is that if the kernel function used in the attention computation can be decomposed, then the attention sum can be reorganized to separate the query-dependent terms from the key-value-dependent terms. This reorganization allows the key-value accumulation to be computed incrementally, converting the quadratic attention into a linear recurrence. Performer adopts a similar goal but achieves linearization through a different mechanism: it approximates the softmax attention using positive orthogonal random features. By sampling random features that approximate the exponential kernel underlying softmax, Performer can compute attention in linear time with provable approximation guarantees.

Two further methods replace attention with long convolutions. The Structured Global Convolution (SGConv) substitutes attention with structured global convolutions that can be computed efficiently using fast Fourier transforms or similar techniques. The Hyena Hierarchy similarly builds on long convolutions but organizes them in a hierarchical structure, allowing the model to capture patterns at multiple scales within the sequence. Both approaches achieve linear complexity while providing a different inductive bias than attention --- one based on convolutional filtering rather than explicit pairwise token interactions.

# State Space Models

State Space Models (SSMs) have become one of the most prominent families of sequence models in recent years, offering linear complexity in the context length while demonstrating competitive performance against Transformers. These models draw inspiration from continuous-time state space formulations common in control theory and signal processing, where a latent state evolves over time according to linear dynamics, and observations are generated as projections of this state.

The Structured State Space sequence model (S4) was one of the first models in this family to demonstrate that SSMs could rival Transformers on language and long-range sequence tasks. S4 parameterizes the state transition matrix with a special structure (initially based on the HiPPO framework for polynomial approximation) that allows the recurrence to be computed efficiently while capturing long-range dependencies. The key innovation was the use of a structured parameterization that makes the state matrix well-conditioned and allows the model to be trained effectively.

Following S4, the Diagonal State Space (DSS) model simplified the state matrix to be diagonal, which dramatically reduces the number of parameters and computational cost while retaining much of the expressiveness. The Gated State Space (GSS) model further incorporated gating mechanisms into the SSM framework, recognizing that gates --- a hallmark of LSTMs --- can improve the selective processing of information. The S5 model extended the SSM framework with additional architectural innovations, and the Bidirectional Gated SSM (BiGS) introduced bidirectional processing with gating, allowing the model to attend to both past and future context.

The H3 model represents another important development in the SSM family, combining state space dynamics with attention-like mechanisms to capture both local and global patterns. Most recently, Mamba has emerged as a highly influential SSM, introducing selective state spaces that allow the model to dynamically control what information flows through the state. Mamba achieves this by making the state transition parameters input-dependent, effectively introducing a form of gating that enables the model to selectively remember or forget information based on the current input. This input-dependent gating brings Mamba closer to the LSTM family in spirit, though it remains firmly within the SSM paradigm.

# Recurrent Neural Networks

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) have experienced a resurgence as researchers revisit them as alternatives to Transformers, drawn by their linear complexity in context length. Modern RNN approaches seek to overcome the limitations that originally led to the dominance of Transformers and LSTMs --- namely, training instability, difficulty with long-range dependencies, and lack of parallelizability.

Deep Linear Recurrent Units (LRUs) have shown promising results for language modeling by using linear recurrences with carefully designed initialization and normalization. The linearity of the recurrence makes these models more stable and easier to train than traditional RNNs, though the lack of non-linear state transitions can limit expressiveness. The Hierarchically Gated Linear RNN (HGRN) and its successor HGRN2 build on this line of work by introducing hierarchical gating structures that allow the model to control information flow across multiple levels of abstraction. HGRN2 in particular incorporates concepts from the covariance update rule, bringing it closer to the matrix memory approach used in xLSTM's mLSTM.

RWKV is perhaps the most well-known RNN approach to large language modeling. RWKV combines the parallel training capability of Transformers with the efficient inference of RNNs. During training, RWKV can be computed in parallel across the sequence (similar to Transformers), while during inference it processes tokens sequentially as an RNN, maintaining constant memory regardless of context length. RWKV has demonstrated competitive performance with Transformers at various scales, and its later versions (RWKV-5 and RWKV-6) have incorporated covariance update rules and more sophisticated gating, bringing them conceptually closer to xLSTM. The evolution of RWKV illustrates how the RNN paradigm can be modernized to compete with Transformers while retaining the computational advantages of sequential processing at inference time.

# Gating Mechanisms Across Modern Architectures

Gating is one of the most enduring ideas from the original LSTM, and it has been rediscovered and reinterpreted across many recent architectures. The insight that information flow through a network should be selectively controlled --- allowing the model to decide what to remember, what to forget, and what to output --- has proven valuable far beyond the original LSTM context.

HGRN and HGRN2 both use gating to control the flow of information through their hierarchical recurrent structures. Gated Linear Attention (GLA) applies gating to the linear attention framework, using gates to selectively weight the contributions of different tokens to the attention output. The Gated State Space (GSS) models incorporate gating into the state space framework, and the Bidirectional Gated SSM (BiGS) extends this to bidirectional processing. Moving Average Equipped Gated Attention (MEGA) combines gating with moving average operations to capture both local and global patterns. RWKV uses gating extensively throughout its architecture, with multiple gate types controlling different aspects of information flow. Mamba, as mentioned, uses input-dependent gating within its selective state space framework to achieve dynamic control over information retention.

This widespread adoption of gating across diverse architectures underscores its fundamental importance in sequence modeling. The original LSTM's insight --- that a simple multiplicative gate can solve the vanishing gradient problem and enable selective memory --- has proven to be one of the most powerful ideas in deep learning. xLSTM's exponential gating represents a further evolution of this idea, making gates more expressive and enabling the model to revise storage decisions more effectively than standard sigmoid gating allows.

# Covariance Update Rule in Other Works

The covariance update rule is a concept with deep roots in associative memory research, and it appears in several modern architectures beyond xLSTM. The update rule, which stores a key-value pair by adding their outer product to a matrix memory, was originally studied in the context of Bidirectional Associative Memories. This rule is optimal for maximal separability of retrieved binary vectors, which is equivalent to maximizing the signal-to-noise ratio in retrieval.

Fast Weight Programmers, introduced in the early 1990s, use a mechanism equivalent to the covariance update rule. In this framework, a "slow" network programs the weights of a "fast" network by writing outer products of vectors into a weight matrix. This allows the fast network to rapidly adapt its behavior based on the history of inputs it has processed. Later work equipped Fast Weight Programmers with a constant decay rate multiplied to the existing memory and a constant learning rate multiplied to the new outer product, which directly parallels the forget gate and input gate in xLSTM's mLSTM.

Several other modern models also build on the covariance update rule. RWKV-5 and RWKV-6 incorporate this update mechanism as part of their memory structure. Retention, another attention-alternative model, uses a similar approach to maintain a matrix memory that stores key-value associations. The Linear Transformer, by reorganizing the attention computation, effectively implements a form of covariance update where keys and values are accumulated over time. HGRN2 also draws on this concept. The widespread use of the covariance update rule across these models demonstrates its effectiveness as a memory mechanism, and xLSTM's mLSTM integrates it into the LSTM framework in a way that combines the strengths of associative memory with the gating and normalization techniques of LSTMs.

# Most Related Models: Retention, RWKV, and HGRN2

Among all the models surveyed, the conceptually closest to xLSTM are Retention, RWKV, and HGRN2. These three models share with xLSTM the core concepts of matrix memory and/or gating, placing them in the same architectural neighborhood. However, there is a crucial difference that distinguishes xLSTM, particularly its sLSTM variant, from all of these related models: none of them allow memory mixing.

Memory mixing refers to the hidden-hidden recurrent connections that allow information from one hidden state to influence the computation of hidden states at the next time step. In the original LSTM, these recurrent connections --- implemented through recurrent weight matrices that connect the previous hidden state to the current cell input, input gate, forget gate, and output gate --- enable the network to mix and transform information across memory cells and across time steps. This is a fundamentally different mode of information flow than what is possible with models that only use input-dependent gates without recurrent connections.

Retention, RWKV, and HGRN2 all process information in a way that is driven by the current input and the accumulated memory state, but without the hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections that characterize the LSTM family. While they can maintain and selectively update memory, they lack the ability to use the current hidden state as an input to the next step's gate computations in the way that LSTMs do. This distinction has important consequences for the expressiveness of these models, as we discuss next.

# The Unique Advantage of sLSTM: Memory Mixing Enables State Tracking

The key advantage that sLSTM retains over its closest competitors is memory mixing, and this advantage has concrete implications for what problems the model can solve. Memory mixing, through the hidden-hidden recurrent connections, enables the model to perform state tracking --- the ability to maintain and update an internal state that reflects the history of the sequence in a way that goes beyond simple accumulation.

State tracking is required for tasks such as evaluating code or tracking entities in a long narrative. When evaluating code, for example, the model must maintain a representation of the program state --- variable values, control flow position, function call stack --- and update this state based on each new instruction. This requires the model to not just store information but to actively process and transform it based on the current state, which is exactly what memory mixing enables. Similarly, tracking entities in a long narrative requires the model to maintain a representation of each entity and update it as new information about that entity is revealed, which again requires state tracking capabilities.

Models without memory mixing, including State Space Models and Transformers, are fundamentally less powerful in this regard. The sLSTM's ability to mix information across memory cells and across time steps through recurrent connections gives it the expressiveness needed for state tracking. This is not merely a theoretical advantage: the experiments in the paper show that models without memory mixing, such as Transformers and State Space Models, cannot solve tasks like regular grammars or the parity problem, which require state tracking. The sLSTM, with its exponential gating and memory mixing, can solve these tasks effectively.

# Expressiveness: LSTMs with Memory Mixing Are More Expressive Than SSMs and Transformers

The expressiveness advantage of LSTMs with memory mixing is not merely an empirical observation --- it is grounded in theoretical analysis. Research has shown that LSTMs are more expressive than State Space Models and Transformers, and this expressiveness gap is directly attributable to memory mixing. State tracking, the ability that memory mixing provides, is a computational capability that sits at a higher level of the Chomsky hierarchy than what models without memory mixing can achieve.

The key distinction is between models that can compute functions in the class of regular languages and those that cannot. State tracking, which requires maintaining and updating a finite state machine based on the input sequence, is exactly the kind of computation needed to recognize regular languages. The parity task, which requires tracking whether the number of certain symbols seen so far is even or odd, is a classic example of a regular language recognition problem. Models without memory mixing, such as Transformers and State Space Models, have been shown to be fundamentally less powerful than RNNs in this regard. They lack the recurrent connections needed to implement the state transitions of a finite state machine.

The experiments in the paper confirm this theoretical prediction. On formal language tasks designed to test state tracking abilities, models without memory mixing --- including Transformers, State Space Models, and the mLSTM variant of xLSTM --- cannot solve tasks like the parity problem. The sLSTM, with its memory mixing, can solve these tasks. This is a concrete demonstration of the expressiveness advantage that memory mixing provides, and it has practical implications: any task that requires tracking state over a sequence, from code evaluation to entity tracking in narratives, benefits from the expressiveness that memory mixing enables.

# Residually Stacked Architectures in Deep Learning

The final piece of the xLSTM story, and one shared with virtually all modern deep learning architectures, is the use of residually stacked building blocks. Residual connections, introduced in the context of deep convolutional networks, allow gradients to flow more easily through deep networks by providing skip connections around each block. This innovation enabled the training of much deeper networks than was previously possible, and it has become a standard component of virtually all modern deep learning architectures.

Transformers, which are the backbone of current Large Language Models, use residual stacking as a core architectural principle. Each Transformer layer consists of a self-attention block and a feed-forward block, each wrapped in a residual connection. This pattern is repeated many times, with models like GPT-3 using 96 layers. The success of this approach has been demonstrated across a wide range of Large Language Models, including GPT-3, ChatGPT, GPT-4, Megatron-LM, Gopher, ERNIE 3.0 Titan, GLaM, Chinese M6, AlexaTM 20B, OPT, Chinchilla, BLOOM, GLM-130B, LaMDA, PaLM, Llama, and Gemini.

xLSTM follows this same architectural principle. xLSTM architectures are constructed by residually stacking xLSTM blocks, using pre-LayerNorm residual backbones as is standard in contemporary Large Language Models. Each xLSTM block contains either an sLSTM or mLSTM variant, wrapped in a residual connection with appropriate up-projection and down-projection to manage the dimensionality of the representation. The pre-LayerNorm normalization, applied before each block rather than after, helps stabilize training in deep networks. This construction ensures that xLSTM benefits from the same deep network training dynamics that have made Transformers so successful, while the internal computations of each block leverage the enhanced LSTM mechanisms --- exponential gating, matrix memory, and memory mixing --- that give xLSTM its distinctive capabilities. By combining the proven architectural framework of residual stacking with the innovations in LSTM design, xLSTM achieves a model that is both expressive and scalable, positioning it as a serious competitor to Transformers and State Space Models in the landscape of sequence modeling.

# Experiments I: Synthetic Tasks and Long Range Arena

## Formal Language Tasks: Testing Exponential Gating with Memory Mixing for State Tracking

The first set of synthetic experiments in the xLSTM paper is designed to validate one of the central innovations of the architecture: exponential gating combined with memory mixing. As discussed in earlier chapters, the sLSTM variant retains the hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections that constitute memory mixing, while the mLSTM variant abandons them in favor of parallelizability. The question these experiments address is whether memory mixing — and the exponential gating that enhances it — confers a fundamentally greater expressive power on the model, particularly for tasks that require state tracking.

State tracking is a class of computational problems in which a model must maintain an internal state that evolves as a function of the input sequence, often in ways that cannot be captured by simple statistical patterns. Examples include evaluating code, tracking entities in a long narrative, and recognizing formal languages defined by grammars such as regular expressions or context-free grammars. The theoretical work of Merrill and others has shown that Transformers and State Space Models without memory mixing are fundamentally less powerful than RNNs with memory mixing, precisely because they cannot perform the kind of state tracking that these tasks require. The formal language tasks serve as a concrete, controlled testbed for demonstrating this distinction.

The xLSTM paper draws on the formal language evaluation framework introduced by Deletang et al., which provides a suite of tasks designed to probe different aspects of sequence modeling capability. These tasks are constructed from well-defined formal languages — such as regular grammars and context-free grammars — where the correct output at each position depends on the structure of the preceding input in a precise, rule-governed way. A canonical example is the parity task, a regular grammar problem in which the model must determine whether the number of a particular symbol seen so far is even or odd. Solving this task requires the model to maintain and update a single bit of state across the entire sequence, which is the essence of state tracking.

The exponential gating mechanism introduced in sLSTM plays a critical role here. In the original LSTM, the input and forget gates use sigmoid activations, which produce values in the range $(0, 1)$. This means that the gates can only partially open or close, and the information stored in the cell state is always subject to some degree of attenuation or dilution. With exponential gating, the input gate $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ and the forget gate $f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)$ can take on values greater than one, allowing the model to make sharp, decisive updates to its memory. This is crucial for state tracking tasks, where the model must sometimes completely overwrite a previously stored value when a new, more relevant piece of information arrives. The Nearest Neighbor Search problem, discussed in the introduction of the paper, exemplifies this: when a more similar vector is found later in the sequence, the model must be able to revise its stored value, something the original LSTM with sigmoid gating struggles to do.

## Extended Formal Language Tasks with Multi-Length Training for Length Extrapolation

A key extension made by the xLSTM authors to the original formal language benchmark of Deletang et al. is the introduction of multi-length training. In the original framework, models are trained and evaluated on sequences of a fixed length, which provides limited insight into whether the model has truly learned the underlying grammar or has merely memorized patterns at a particular scale. Multi-length training addresses this limitation by exposing the model to sequences of varying lengths during training, thereby encouraging it to learn the general rule governing the language rather than overfitting to a specific sequence length.

This extension is particularly important for evaluating length extrapolation — the ability of a model to perform well on sequences longer than those encountered during training. Length extrapolation is a critical property for language models, as real-world applications often involve contexts that extend well beyond the training distribution. For recurrent architectures like xLSTM, which maintain a fixed-size memory state regardless of sequence length, length extrapolation is theoretically possible because the same computational mechanism is applied at every time step. In contrast, architectures like Transformers, which rely on attention over all previous positions, face a quadratic increase in computational cost with sequence length and often degrade in performance when the context exceeds the training length.

The multi-length training protocol involves generating training sequences across a range of lengths and then evaluating the model on sequences that extend beyond this range. The accuracy of each method is measured only on the tokens that are relevant to the task, and the accuracy is scaled between $0$ (random performance) and $1$ (perfect performance). This focused evaluation ensures that the metric directly reflects the model's ability to perform the specific state tracking computation required by the grammar, rather than being diluted by tokens that do not contribute to the task.

## Comparison Methods: xLSTM Variants and Baselines

To provide a comprehensive comparison, the paper evaluates several xLSTM configurations alongside a diverse set of baseline models. The xLSTM variants tested are specified using the notation xLSTM[$a$:$b$], where $a/b$ denotes the ratio of mLSTM-based blocks to sLSTM-based blocks in the architecture. Three configurations are tested: xLSTM[0:1], which consists entirely of sLSTM-based blocks and thus fully retains memory mixing; xLSTM[1:0], which consists entirely of mLSTM-based blocks and thus has no memory mixing but benefits from matrix memory and parallelizability; and xLSTM[1:1], which uses an equal mixture of mLSTM and sLSTM blocks, balancing the strengths of both variants.

All xLSTM variants use 2-block architectures for these synthetic experiments, keeping the models small to isolate the effects of the architectural innovations rather than scale. For the sLSTM-based blocks, the post up-projection residual block is used, while for the mLSTM-based blocks, the pre up-projection residual block is employed. This follows the design principles outlined in the architecture section: sLSTM blocks summarize the past in the original space before up-projecting, while mLSTM blocks up-project first to leverage the larger memory capacity available in the high-dimensional space.

The baseline methods span the major families of contemporary sequence models. From the Transformer family, Llama is included as a representative modern Transformer. From the State Space Model family, Mamba and Hyena are tested. From the RNN family, RWKV (both as a standalone model and within blocks), Retention, and the original LSTM are included. Additionally, an LSTM variant placed within Transformer-style blocks (denoted LSTM (Block)) is tested to control for the effect of the block architecture independently of the core recurrent computation. This diverse set of baselines allows the experiments to disentangle the contributions of different architectural choices — gating, memory structure, memory mixing, and block design — to the overall performance on state tracking tasks.

## Results: State Tracking Capability Distinguishes RNNs from Transformers and SSMs

The results of the formal language experiments provide clear evidence that memory mixing is essential for solving state tracking problems. Models without memory mixing — including Transformers like Llama, State Space Models like Mamba, and other architectures that lack hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections — fail to solve regular grammar tasks such as the parity task. This failure is not a matter of capacity or scale; it is a fundamental limitation of the computational class to which these models belong. Without the ability to maintain and update an internal state through recurrent connections, these models cannot implement the finite-state automata required to recognize regular languages.

In contrast, models that retain memory mixing — including the original LSTM, LSTM in blocks, and all xLSTM variants that include sLSTM blocks — successfully solve these tasks. The xLSTM[0:1] configuration, which uses only sLSTM blocks with exponential gating, demonstrates the strongest performance among the state-tracking-capable models. This confirms that the combination of exponential gating and memory mixing in sLSTM is not merely a marginal improvement over the original LSTM, but a meaningful enhancement that sharpens the model's ability to make decisive state updates.

The xLSTM[1:0] configuration, which uses only mLSTM blocks without memory mixing, falls into the same computational class as Transformers and State Space Models with respect to state tracking. As expected, it struggles with the regular grammar tasks that require state tracking. However, the xLSTM[1:1] configuration, which blends mLSTM and sLSTM blocks, recovers the state tracking capability through its sLSTM component while simultaneously benefiting from the matrix memory of the mLSTM component. This demonstrates the complementary nature of the two variants and validates the design decision to integrate both into the xLSTM architecture.

These findings are consistent with the theoretical results of Merrill and Deletang, who showed that Transformers and State Space Models are fundamentally less powerful than RNNs in terms of the classes of formal languages they can recognize. The experiments provide concrete, empirical confirmation of this theoretical result and show that xLSTM, through its sLSTM component, preserves the expressive power that makes RNNs uniquely suited for state tracking tasks.

## Multi-Query Associative Recall: Testing Matrix Memory Capacity

While the formal language tasks validate the state tracking capabilities of sLSTM, the Multi-Query Associative Recall (MQAR) task is designed to test the other major innovation of xLSTM: the matrix memory of mLSTM. The MQAR task, introduced by Arora et al., is a memory-intensive benchmark in which a model must store a set of key-value pairs presented in a sequence and later retrieve the correct value when queried with a key.

The task is structured as follows: for each sequence, key-value pairs are randomly chosen from a large vocabulary. These pairs are presented sequentially to the model. At later positions in the sequence, the model is presented with a query key and must output the corresponding value that was previously stored. This setup directly tests the model's ability to store and retrieve structured information — a capability that is central to language modeling, where the model must keep track of entities, attributes, and relationships across long contexts.

The matrix memory of mLSTM is specifically designed to address the storage capacity limitation of the original LSTM. In the original LSTM, information must be compressed into scalar cell states, which severely limits the amount of structured information that can be stored. The mLSTM replaces this scalar memory with a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, using a covariance update rule $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ to store key-value pairs. Retrieval is then performed via matrix multiplication: given a query $\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau}$, the stored value is retrieved as $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_{t+\tau} / \max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_{t+\tau}|, 1\}$. This matrix memory provides a dramatically larger storage capacity compared to the scalar memory of the original LSTM, as the number of stored associations scales with the dimensionality of the matrix rather than being limited to a single scalar per cell.

The MQAR task thus serves as a direct probe of whether the matrix memory of mLSTM translates into improved associative recall performance, and how this performance compares to other architectures with different memory mechanisms.

## Enhanced MQAR Difficulty: Scaling to 256 Key-Value Pairs and 2048 Context Length

To provide a more rigorous test of memory capacity than the original MQAR benchmark, the xLSTM authors significantly enhance the difficulty of the task in two ways. First, they increase the number of key-value pairs that must be memorized, scaling up to $256$ pairs per sequence. This pushes the memory demands well beyond what a scalar memory could reasonably handle, as each additional pair requires the model to maintain a distinct association without overwriting previously stored information.

Second, they extend the context length up to $2048$ tokens. This increase in context length compounds the difficulty, as the model must not only store more pairs but also retain them across longer spans before they are queried. The combination of more key-value pairs and longer contexts creates a more stringent test of the model's memory capacity and its ability to maintain stored information over time without degradation.

These enhancements are designed to create a broad and challenging evaluation that can differentiate between models with varying memory architectures. Transformers, which use attention over all previous positions, have a memory capacity that is effectively exponential in the coding dimension, as shown by Ramsauer et al. This makes them the gold standard for associative recall tasks. The question is whether the matrix memory of mLSTM, combined with the gating mechanisms inherited from the LSTM framework, can approach this level of performance.

The models compared on this enhanced MQAR task include 2-block architectures of Llama, Mamba, RWKV-5, RWKV-6, xLSTM[1:1], and xLSTM[1:0]. The models are evaluated by their accuracy at correctly recalling the value associated with each queried key. The use of 2-block architectures keeps the comparison focused on the memory mechanism rather than model scale, ensuring that differences in performance reflect architectural rather than capacity differences.

## MQAR Results: xLSTM[1:1] Performs Best Among Non-Transformer Models

The results of the MQAR experiments reveal a clear hierarchy of performance. As expected, Transformers (represented by Llama) achieve the highest accuracy, consistent with their exponential memory capacity. Among all non-Transformer models, xLSTM[1:1] performs best, demonstrating that the matrix memory of mLSTM provides a substantial advantage in associative recall tasks. This result holds across different difficulty levels, including the most challenging setting with $256$ key-value pairs.

The strong performance of xLSTM[1:1] can be attributed to the synergistic combination of mLSTM and sLSTM blocks. The mLSTM blocks provide the matrix memory that enables the storage and retrieval of a large number of key-value associations, while the sLSTM blocks contribute state tracking capabilities that may help manage the flow of information through the network. The pre up-projection design of the mLSTM blocks is also relevant here, as it allows the matrix memory to operate in a high-dimensional space where more associations can be stored, following the principle that memory capacity increases with the dimensionality of the representation.

The xLSTM[1:0] configuration, which uses only mLSTM blocks, also performs well but is slightly outperformed by xLSTM[1:1] on the most difficult tasks. This suggests that while the matrix memory alone provides strong associative recall capabilities, the addition of sLSTM blocks with their memory mixing and state tracking capabilities provides an additional benefit. The complementary strengths of the two variants are most evident at the highest difficulty levels, where the demands on both storage capacity and information management are greatest.

## Role of sLSTM Blocks in Leveraging Memory Capacity

A particularly interesting finding from the MQAR experiments is that the inclusion of sLSTM blocks does not diminish the memory capacity of the overall architecture. One might intuitively expect that replacing some mLSTM blocks with sLSTM blocks — which use scalar rather than matrix memory — would reduce the total storage capacity and thus degrade performance on memory-intensive tasks. The results show the opposite: the sLSTM blocks leverage rather than diminish the memory capacity, with the xLSTM[1:1] configuration outperforming xLSTM[1:0] at the most difficult task setting with $256$ key-value pairs.

This finding suggests that the sLSTM blocks play a complementary role that goes beyond their scalar memory. The memory mixing provided by the hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections in sLSTM may help route and organize information across the network, ensuring that the matrix memory in the mLSTM blocks is used efficiently. The exponential gating in sLSTM allows for sharp, decisive updates that can help the model manage the flow of key-value associations, perhaps by selectively attending to the most relevant information at each step.

Furthermore, the multi-head structure of sLSTM, with memory mixing within each head but not across heads, provides a structured form of information routing. Each head can specialize in maintaining different aspects of the sequence state, and the memory mixing within each head allows for complex interactions that the scalar memory alone could not capture. This structured routing may help the mLSTM blocks focus their matrix memory on storing the associations that are most critical for the task, rather than being overloaded with all information in the sequence.

## Extrapolation Analysis: Enhanced Memory for Generalizing to Longer Contexts

Beyond the raw accuracy on the MQAR task, the paper also provides an extrapolation analysis that tests whether xLSTM's enhanced memory capacities allow it to generalize to contexts longer than those seen during training. This is a critical property for practical applications, where the context length at inference time may exceed the training context length.

The extrapolation analysis is conducted by training models on contexts of a certain length and then evaluating them on longer contexts. The results, detailed in the appendix, indicate that xLSTM's enhanced memory capacities — particularly the matrix memory of mLSTM — support extrapolation to longer contexts. This is in contrast to architectures that rely on attention over all previous positions, which often struggle when the context length exceeds the training range because the positional encodings or attention patterns learned during training do not generalize to longer sequences.

The ability of xLSTM to extrapolate to longer contexts is rooted in the nature of its memory mechanism. The matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t$ is updated at each time step using the same covariance update rule, regardless of the sequence length. The forget gate $f_t$ and input gate $i_t$ control what is stored and what is forgotten, and these gates operate on the current input and the previous state, not on the entire history. This means that the computational mechanism is invariant to sequence length — the same operations are applied whether the sequence is $512$ or $2048$ tokens long. As long as the memory capacity is not exceeded — that is, as long as the matrix does not become overloaded with too many associations — the model can continue to store and retrieve information effectively at longer context lengths.

This extrapolation capability is particularly relevant given the results from the large-scale language modeling experiments, where xLSTM models trained on context length $2048$ maintain low perplexities when evaluated on contexts up to $16384$ tokens. The synthetic MQAR extrapolation analysis provides a controlled confirmation of this capability, showing that the matrix memory mechanism generalizes beyond the training distribution in a fundamental way.

## Long Range Arena: Assessing Long Sequence Processing Across Diverse Tasks

The Long Range Arena (LRA), introduced by Tay et al., is a benchmark suite designed to evaluate the ability of sequence models to process long sequences across a diverse range of tasks. Unlike the formal language and MQAR tasks, which focus on specific capabilities (state tracking and associative recall, respectively), the LRA provides a broader evaluation of long-context processing abilities. The tasks in the LRA span different domains and challenge different aspects of sequence modeling, including the ability to capture long-range dependencies, to process structured sequences, and to maintain information over extended contexts.

The inclusion of the LRA in the xLSTM evaluation serves several purposes. First, it provides a standardized benchmark that allows comparison with a wide range of previously published results, positioning xLSTM within the broader landscape of sequence models. Second, the diversity of tasks in the LRA ensures that the evaluation is not biased toward any single capability — a model that performs well on the LRA must demonstrate robustness across multiple types of long-context challenges. Third, the LRA tasks often involve sequences that are much longer than those in typical language modeling benchmarks, providing a stringent test of the model's ability to maintain and use information over extended spans.

For the LRA evaluation, the xLSTM architecture is compared against other methods across the full suite of tasks. The consistent strong performance of xLSTM across all tasks in the LRA suggests that the architecture is remarkably efficient in handling the different aspects of long context problems. This efficiency can be attributed to the combination of the two xLSTM variants: the mLSTM blocks provide the matrix memory capacity needed to store information over long spans, while the sLSTM blocks provide the state tracking and memory mixing needed to process and organize that information. The pre-LayerNorm residual backbone, borrowed from modern Transformer architectures, ensures stable training of deep stacks of these blocks, while the up-projection designs in the blocks provide the high-dimensional representations needed for effective separation of different histories.

## xLSTM's Consistent Strong Performance on Long Context Problems

The overall picture that emerges from the synthetic experiments is one of consistent, robust performance across a diverse set of challenging tasks. On formal language tasks, xLSTM variants with sLSTM blocks demonstrate state tracking capabilities that Transformers and State Space Models fundamentally lack. On the MQAR task, xLSTM variants with mLSTM blocks demonstrate memory capacities that approach those of Transformers and significantly exceed those of other non-Transformer models. On the Long Range Arena, xLSTM demonstrates broad competence across diverse long-context tasks.

This consistency is a key finding because it suggests that the xLSTM architecture does not rely on task-specific tricks or narrow optimizations. Instead, the two core innovations — exponential gating with memory mixing in sLSTM, and matrix memory with covariance updates in mLSTM — address fundamental limitations of the original LSTM in a way that generalizes across different types of sequence modeling challenges. The exponential gating enables sharp, decisive memory updates that are essential for both state tracking and information revision. The matrix memory provides the storage capacity needed for associative recall and long-context processing. The integration of these innovations into residual block architectures, following principles from Cover's Theorem on the separability of non-linearly embedded patterns in high-dimensional spaces, ensures that the model can effectively leverage these capabilities in deep, stacked architectures.

The strong performance on synthetic tasks provides a foundation for the larger-scale language modeling experiments that follow. By demonstrating that the architectural innovations of xLSTM are grounded in fundamental computational capabilities — state tracking, associative recall, and long-context processing — the synthetic experiments provide confidence that the favorable performance of xLSTM on language modeling benchmarks is attributable to these genuine improvements in the model's computational power, rather than to incidental advantages in training dynamics or scale. The synthetic tasks thus serve as a bridge between the theoretical motivation for xLSTM and its empirical performance on real-world language modeling problems.

# Experiments II: Method Comparison and Ablation Studies

## Training Setup: 15B Tokens from SlimPajama in an Auto-Regressive Setting

The central question driving the xLSTM research is how far one can push language modeling when scaling LSTMs to billions of parameters while mitigating their known limitations. To address this question empirically, the authors design a controlled comparison in which xLSTM and a wide range of competing architectures are trained under identical conditions. The training corpus consists of 15 billion tokens drawn from SlimPajama, a large-scale, open-source language modeling dataset. All models are trained in the same auto-regressive setting, meaning that each model predicts the next token in a sequence given the preceding tokens, and the loss is computed over these predictions. This auto-regressive framework is the standard paradigm for training large language models and ensures that every method — whether Transformer, State Space Model, RNN, or xLSTM — is evaluated on the same objective with the same data, thereby making the comparison fair and directly informative about the relative merits of each architecture.

The choice of 15 billion tokens is significant. It is large enough to push the models into a regime where architectural differences become apparent — small enough training budgets can mask differences between methods, as all models might appear to perform similarly when undertrained — yet it is still manageable for training multiple model variants and conducting ablation studies. By holding the dataset, token count, and training procedure constant across all methods, any observed differences in validation perplexity can be attributed to the architectures themselves rather than to confounds in data or optimization.

## Methods Compared

The comparison is broad and inclusive, spanning the major families of sequence modeling architectures that have emerged as competitors to the original LSTM. On the Transformer side, two landmark models are included: GPT-3, which uses shared weights for token and output embeddings (and therefore has slightly fewer parameters than the others at the same configuration), and Llama, which represents a more recent, highly optimized Transformer architecture. From the State Space Model family, two methods are included: H3 and Mamba, both of which have demonstrated strong performance in language modeling while maintaining linear complexity in sequence length. From the RNN family, multiple generations of RWKV are included — RWKV-4, RWKV-5, and RWKV-6 — showcasing the progression of this architecture over time. Additional RNN-based methods include HGRN and HGRN2, which incorporate hierarchical gating mechanisms. From the linear Transformer family, which seeks to replace the quadratic attention mechanism with linear alternatives, the comparison includes GLA (Gated Linear Attention), RetNet (Retention), and Hyena, the latter using structured global convolutions. Finally, the xLSTM variants themselves are included: xLSTM[1:0], which uses exclusively mLSTM-based blocks, and xLSTM[7:1], which uses a ratio of seven mLSTM-based blocks to one sLSTM-based block out of every eight blocks.

This comprehensive selection ensures that xLSTM is benchmarked against the strongest available competitors from each major paradigm. The inclusion of multiple methods from each family also allows one to observe trends within families, such as the progression from RWKV-4 through RWKV-6, and to situate xLSTM's performance relative to the state of the art in each category.

## Model Categorization: Transformers, State Space Models, and RNNs/Linear Transformers

To structure the comparison, the authors categorize all methods into three groups. The first category is Transformers, which includes GPT-3 and Llama. These models rely on self-attention, which computes pairwise interactions between all tokens in a sequence, yielding quadratic complexity in sequence length but strong representational power. The second category is State Space Models (SSMs), which includes H3 and Mamba. These models originate from continuous-time state space formulations and have been adapted for discrete sequence modeling, offering linear complexity in sequence length while maintaining competitive performance. The third category encompasses Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) together with linear Transformers. This group includes RWKV-4, RWKV-5, RWKV-6, HGRN, HGRN2, GLA, RetNet, and Hyena. Linear Transformers are included here because, like RNNs, they are linear methods that substitute the Transformer attention mechanism with alternatives that avoid quadratic complexity. The xLSTM variants, while rooted in the LSTM tradition, are evaluated alongside all three categories, and their position relative to each is a key outcome of the comparison.

This categorization is not merely organizational; it reflects fundamental differences in how each family handles sequence processing. Transformers attend to all previous tokens simultaneously, SSMs project inputs through learned state transitions, and RNNs maintain a compressed hidden state that is updated sequentially. The xLSTM, with its sLSTM and mLSTM components, bridges aspects of RNNs (through memory mixing in sLSTM) and aspects of attention-like mechanisms (through the matrix memory and query-key-value retrieval in mLSTM), making its comparison to all three categories especially instructive.

## Model Size Matching: Approximately 350M Parameters

To ensure a fair comparison, all models are matched in size to a GPT-3 model with 350 million parameters. This matching is achieved by setting the embedding dimension to 1024 and using 24 residual blocks across all architectures. The only exception is GPT-3 itself, which uses shared weights for token and output embeddings, resulting in slightly fewer parameters than the other models at the same configuration. This size matching is critical: without it, differences in performance could be attributed to differences in model capacity rather than architectural innovation. By holding the parameter count approximately constant, the comparison isolates the effect of architectural design choices — gating mechanisms, memory structures, parallelizability, and so on — on language modeling performance.

The choice of 350M parameters is also practically motivated. At this scale, models are large enough to exhibit meaningful language modeling capabilities and to reveal architectural strengths and weaknesses, yet small enough that training on 15 billion tokens is computationally feasible for a large number of method variants. This scale has been used in prior work as a standard benchmark size, making the results interpretable in the context of the broader literature.

## Results: xLSTM Outperforms All Existing Methods in Validation Perplexity

The results of the comparison are striking. When evaluated by validation perplexity on the SlimPajama validation set, xLSTM outperforms all existing methods across all three categories — Transformers, State Space Models, and RNNs/linear Transformers. This result is significant because it demonstrates that the modifications introduced in xLSTM — exponential gating, matrix memory, and the integration of sLSTM and mLSTM into residual block architectures — are not merely incremental refinements but yield tangible improvements in language modeling quality.

Perplexity is a standard metric for language models, measuring how well a probability model predicts a sample. Lower perplexity indicates better predictive performance. The fact that xLSTM achieves the lowest validation perplexity among all tested methods, including the highly optimized Llama Transformer and the Mamba State Space Model, provides strong evidence that the xLSTM architecture is a competitive alternative to these dominant paradigms. Importantly, this advantage is observed at a controlled scale of 350M parameters, and the scaling behavior discussed below suggests that the advantage may persist or grow at larger scales.

Both xLSTM[1:0] (pure mLSTM) and xLSTM[7:1] (a mixture of mLSTM and sLSTM blocks) perform well, with xLSTM[7:1] achieving particularly strong results. This indicates that the sLSTM component, with its memory mixing and exponential gating, contributes positively to the architecture's overall performance, complementing the parallelizable matrix memory of mLSTM rather than detracting from it.

## Scaling Behavior Comparison: xLSTM Shows Favorable Scaling Trends

Beyond the point comparison at 350M parameters, the authors assess the scaling behavior of each method by examining how validation perplexity changes as a function of model size or training compute. This analysis follows the methodology established in the scaling laws literature, where the relationship between model performance and scale is characterized by power-law functions. Such scaling behavior is critically important for predicting how architectures will perform at the much larger scales used in production language models.

The results indicate that xLSTM exhibits favorable scaling trends. When plotting validation perplexity against a scaling axis, xLSTM demonstrates a trajectory that suggests it will continue to perform competitively as model size increases. This is a crucial finding: an architecture that performs well at small scale but scales poorly would be of limited practical interest, while one that maintains or improves its relative position as scale increases is a strong candidate for large-scale deployment. The scaling behavior observed for xLSTM indicates that it not only matches but in some cases exceeds the scaling efficiency of Transformers and State Space Models, suggesting that larger xLSTM models will be serious competitors to current large language models built on Transformer technology.

## Ablation Study Methodology: Morphing Vanilla LSTM into xLSTM Step by Step

To understand which modifications contribute most to xLSTM's strong performance, the authors conduct a carefully designed ablation study. Rather than removing components from the full xLSTM architecture — which can introduce confounds when components interact synergistically — the authors take the opposite approach: they begin with a vanilla LSTM architecture and incrementally add the modifications that transform it into xLSTM, measuring performance at each stage. This "morphing" approach traces a path from the original LSTM to the final xLSTM, attributing performance changes at each step to the specific modification introduced.

This methodology is particularly informative because the modifications in xLSTM are multifaceted, including changes to the backbone architecture, the gating mechanism, and the memory structure. By introducing these changes one at a time and observing their individual effects, the authors can decompose the total improvement into contributions from each component. The ablation study thus provides a detailed account of how each innovation — from residual backbones to exponential gating to matrix memory — builds on the previous ones to yield the final architecture's performance.

## Ablation Step 1: Integrating LSTM into Pre-LayerNorm Residual Backbones

The first modification applied to the vanilla LSTM is the integration of its layers into a pre-LayerNorm residual backbone. Residual connections, which add the input of a block to its output, have been shown to facilitate the training of deep networks by providing gradient pathways that mitigate the vanishing gradient problem. Pre-LayerNorm, in which layer normalization is applied to the input of each sublayer rather than to its output, is the most commonly used variant in contemporary large language models, as it stabilizes training and allows for deeper architectures.

The vanilla LSTM, as originally formulated, does not use residual connections or layer normalization in this manner. By embedding LSTM layers into this modern backbone, the authors provide the architecture with the same structural advantages enjoyed by Transformers and other modern models. This step isolates the effect of the backbone alone: any performance improvement observed at this stage can be attributed to the residual connections and layer normalization, rather than to changes in the LSTM cell itself. The results show that this integration yields a measurable improvement, confirming that the backbone architecture plays an important role even before any modifications to the LSTM's internal mechanics are introduced.

## Ablation Step 2: Extending to a Post Up-Projection Block

The second modification extends the architecture to use a post up-projection block. This block structure, inspired by the design used in Transformers, first non-linearly summarizes the past in the original-dimensional space, then linearly maps into a higher-dimensional space, applies a non-linear activation function, and linearly maps back to the original space. The theoretical justification for this design comes from Cover's Theorem, which states that patterns non-linearly embedded in a higher-dimensional space are more likely to be linearly separable than in the original space. For language modeling, better separation of different histories or contexts is a prerequisite for correctly predicting the next token.

The post up-projection block gives the sLSTM — which retains the scalar memory of the original LSTM — access to a larger representational space without increasing the dimensionality of the recurrent state itself. The up-projection and down-projection operations are parameterized by learned weight matrices, and the non-linear activation between them enables the block to capture complex transformations of the summarized history. The results of this ablation step show further improvement over the previous stage, indicating that the post up-projection block contributes meaningfully to the architecture's performance by enhancing its representational capacity.

## Ablation Step 3: Adding Exponential Gating and Matrix Memory

The third and final modification in the morphing process adds the two core innovations of xLSTM: exponential gating and matrix memory. Exponential gating replaces the sigmoid activation functions of the input and forget gates with exponential activation functions, which increases the range of gate values and enables the network to revise storage decisions more effectively. The matrix memory, introduced in the mLSTM, replaces the scalar cell state with a matrix-valued memory that stores key-value pairs using a covariance update rule, dramatically increasing the storage capacity of the memory cell.

The addition of these two components completes the transformation from vanilla LSTM to xLSTM. The results show a substantial performance improvement at this stage, confirming that the exponential gating and matrix memory are the most impactful modifications. Importantly, the ablation study attributes the strong performance improvement to both of these innovations, rather than to one alone. This is a key finding: neither exponential gating nor matrix memory alone would be sufficient to account for the full improvement; rather, they work synergistically, with exponential gating enabling more flexible control over what is stored and forgotten, and the matrix memory providing the capacity to store richer information.

## Attributing Performance Improvement: Both Exponential Gating and Matrix Memory Contribute

The ablation study's central conclusion is that both exponential gating and matrix memory make substantial, independent contributions to xLSTM's performance. This finding is important because it validates the dual-pronged approach taken in xLSTM's design. Exponential gating addresses the first limitation of the original LSTM — the inability to revise storage decisions — by allowing gates to take on a wider range of values, including values greater than one. This enables the network to "overwrite" or "revise" previously stored information more decisively than is possible with sigmoid gates, which are bounded between zero and one. The matrix memory addresses the second limitation — limited storage capacity — by replacing the scalar cell state with a matrix that can store multiple key-value associations simultaneously, enabling richer retrieval through query-key interactions.

The fact that both modifications contribute independently suggests that they address distinct bottlenecks in the original LSTM. Exponential gating improves the dynamics of information flow into and out of the memory, while the matrix memory expands the representational capacity of the memory itself. Together, they transform the LSTM from a model with a single scalar memory and bounded gates into one with a rich, matrix-valued memory and flexible, unbounded gates, while preserving the constant error carousel mechanism that makes LSTMs effective at capturing long-range dependencies.

## Gating Mechanism Ablation: Learnable Input-Dependent Gates Have Incremental Positive Effects

Given the importance of gating in RNNs and State Space Models, the authors conduct an additional ablation study focused specifically on gating mechanisms. Different gating configurations are tested to determine the effect of making each gate learnable and dependent on the input. The gates in question — input gate, forget gate, and output gate — can vary in how they are parameterized: they can be constant (not learned), learned but input-independent, or learned and input-dependent (i.e., computed as a function of the current input).

The results of this ablation indicate that having each gate learnable and influenced by the input has an incremental positive effect on performance. In other words, the more expressive and adaptive the gating mechanism, the better the model performs. This finding is consistent with the design philosophy of the original LSTM, where gates were introduced precisely to allow the network to learn when to store, forget, and output information. The ablation confirms that this principle extends to the xLSTM setting: exponential gates that are both learnable and input-dependent provide the network with maximum flexibility in controlling information flow, and this flexibility translates directly into improved language modeling performance.

The incremental nature of the improvements — where each additional degree of gating sophistication adds a modest but consistent gain — suggests that the components of the gating mechanism are complementary rather than redundant. The input gate controls what new information enters the memory, the forget gate controls what old information is discarded, and the output gate controls what information is passed to the next layer. Making each of these gates responsive to the current input allows the network to adapt its memory management strategy to the specific content of each sequence, which is essential for the diverse and context-dependent patterns present in natural language.

## Individual Backbone Component Ablation Studies

Beyond the main ablation study that traces the path from vanilla LSTM to xLSTM, the authors also conduct additional studies on the individual components of the backbone architecture. These studies, discussed in further detail in the appendix, examine the contribution of specific design choices within the xLSTM blocks, such as the up-projection and down-projection dimensions, the choice of activation functions, and the arrangement of pre-LayerNorm and post-LayerNorm operations.

These finer-grained ablations serve to validate the specific architectural choices made in designing the xLSTM blocks and to identify any components whose contribution is particularly large or surprisingly small. By systematically varying each component while holding others constant, the authors can build a comprehensive picture of which aspects of the architecture are essential and which are matters of convention or convenience. Such studies are important for guiding future development: if a component is found to contribute little, it may be simplified or removed to improve efficiency; if a component is found to be critical, it should be preserved and potentially emphasized in future iterations.

The individual backbone component ablations complement the main morphing study by providing a more granular view of the architecture's design space. Together, these studies establish that xLSTM's strong performance is not the result of a single lucky modification but rather the product of carefully chosen, individually validated components that work together to overcome the limitations of the original LSTM while preserving its core strengths.

# Experiments III: xLSTM as a Large Language Model

## Large-scale training setup: 300B tokens from SlimPajama, model sizes 125M to 1.3B

The culmination of the experimental evaluation of xLSTM involves large-scale language modeling experiments that test the potential of xLSTM as a Large Language Model (LLM). For these experiments, the training data is substantially increased compared to the earlier comparison experiments. Specifically, the models are trained on 300 billion tokens from the SlimPajama dataset, which is the same number of tokens used in other prominent large-scale studies such as Mamba and Griffin. This large training corpus ensures that the models have sufficient exposure to diverse linguistic patterns and content, which is essential for drawing meaningful conclusions about their capabilities and scaling behavior.

The model sizes explored in these experiments span a significant range: 125 million, 350 million, 760 million, and 1.3 billion parameters. This range allows the researchers to assess how each method performs as the model capacity increases, and it provides the data necessary to estimate scaling laws that can be extrapolated to even larger model sizes. The 1.3 billion parameter models, in particular, serve as the basis for the most demanding tests, including sequence length extrapolation and inference performance benchmarks. By evaluating across this range of model sizes, the experiments can distinguish between methods that merely perform well at small scales and those that maintain or improve their advantage as they grow larger.

## Comparison methods: xLSTM, RWKV-4, Llama, and Mamba

For the large-scale language modeling comparison, four methods are selected, each representing a different class of sequence modeling architecture. xLSTM represents the new extended LSTM family proposed in the paper. Llama represents the Transformer architecture, which has become the dominant paradigm for large language models. Mamba represents State Space Models (SSMs), a newer class of linear-in-context-length models that have shown promising results. RWKV-4 represents the Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) class, which, like xLSTM, processes sequences in a recurrent manner but uses a different architectural approach.

The selection of RWKV-4 as the RNN representative, rather than the more recent RWKV-5 or RWKV-6, is a practical decision. At the time the 300 billion token experiments were started, a reasonable training precision setting had not yet been found for RWKV-5, RWKV-6, or HGRN2. This highlights a practical challenge in comparing methods at scale: newer methods may not always have stable training configurations ready when large-scale experiments are launched. By choosing one representative from each method class, the comparison is kept manageable while still covering the major architectural paradigms in sequence modeling.

## Sequence length extrapolation: training on 2048, testing up to 16384

One of the most critical properties of a language model is its ability to handle context lengths longer than those seen during training, a property known as sequence length extrapolation. This capability is essential for practical applications where the context window at inference time may differ from the training context length. In this experiment, the 1.3 billion parameter models of xLSTM, RWKV-4, Llama, and Mamba are all trained on a context length of 2048 tokens. After training, they are tested on context lengths extending up to 16,384 tokens, which is eight times the training context length.

This experimental design directly tests whether the models can generalize to longer contexts or whether they degrade when the sequence length exceeds what they encountered during training. For Transformer-based models like Llama, which rely on absolute or relative positional encodings, extrapolation can be particularly challenging because the positional patterns at longer contexts were never encountered during training. For recurrent models like xLSTM and RWKV-4, the recurrent nature of their processing means they can, in principle, process arbitrarily long sequences, but whether they maintain performance quality at longer contexts depends on how well their memory mechanisms handle the increased information load.

## Extrapolation results: xLSTM maintains low perplexities for longer contexts

The results of the sequence length extrapolation experiment reveal a significant advantage for xLSTM. In contrast to the other methods tested, xLSTM models maintain low perplexities even as the context length extends well beyond the training length of 2048. This means that xLSTM continues to make accurate next-token predictions even when processing sequences that are much longer than those it was trained on. The ability to maintain low perplexity at longer contexts suggests that xLSTM's memory mechanisms—both the scalar memory with exponential gating in sLSTM and the matrix memory with covariance updates in mLSTM—can effectively store and retrieve information over extended sequences without degradation.

The other methods in the comparison do not exhibit the same level of robustness to context length increases. This result is particularly important because it suggests that xLSTM could be deployed in applications requiring long context windows without needing to retrain on longer sequences, which would be computationally expensive. The ability to extrapolate to longer contexts is a property that becomes increasingly valuable as the demand for processing long documents, extended conversations, and other long-context applications grows.

## Validation perplexity results across model sizes: xLSTM variants are best

Validation perplexity is a standard metric for evaluating language models, measuring how well a model predicts the next token on a held-out validation set. Lower perplexity indicates better predictive performance. For all model sizes tested—125 million, 350 million, 760 million, and 1.3 billion parameters—both xLSTM[1:0] (pure mLSTM) and xLSTM[7:1] (a ratio of seven mLSTM-based blocks to one sLSTM-based block) achieve the lowest validation set perplexities among all compared methods.

The consistency of this result across all model sizes is noteworthy. It demonstrates that xLSTM's advantage is not limited to a particular scale but rather persists as the models grow larger. The fact that both xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1] perform well indicates that both the mLSTM with its matrix memory and the combination of mLSTM with sLSTM blocks contribute to strong language modeling performance. The xLSTM[7:1] variant, which incorporates sLSTM blocks alongside mLSTM blocks, benefits from the memory mixing and state tracking capabilities of sLSTM while retaining the parallelizable matrix memory of mLSTM for the majority of the network's blocks.

## Downstream task evaluation: common sense reasoning tasks

In addition to validation perplexity, the models are evaluated on downstream tasks that measure common sense reasoning. These tasks assess the models' ability to apply knowledge learned during pre-training to specific reasoning challenges, which is a more direct measure of the models' practical utility. The downstream tasks include benchmarks that test various aspects of language understanding and reasoning, providing a more comprehensive picture of model capabilities than perplexity alone.

The evaluation on downstream tasks is conducted for all model sizes, allowing the researchers to observe how the different architectures perform as they scale. This is particularly important because a model might achieve good perplexity but still struggle on tasks that require more structured reasoning or specific types of knowledge retrieval. By evaluating across multiple tasks and model sizes, the researchers can identify whether a method has consistent advantages or whether its strengths are limited to particular types of problems.

## Downstream results: xLSTM best in vast majority of tasks across model sizes

The results on downstream tasks show that xLSTM is the best-performing method in the vast majority of tasks and across all model sizes. This is a strong result, as it indicates that xLSTM's advantages are not limited to the perplexity metric but extend to more applied reasoning tasks. The only exception is the ARC (AI2 Reasoning Challenge) task, where Mamba is the best method in some cases. This suggests that while xLSTM is generally superior, there may be specific types of reasoning where other architectures have particular strengths.

The consistency of xLSTM's strong performance across downstream tasks and model sizes provides further evidence that the architectural innovations—exponential gating, matrix memory, and the integration of sLSTM and mLSTM blocks—contribute to genuinely better language understanding, not just better optimization of the perplexity objective. This distinction is important because perplexity and downstream task performance do not always correlate perfectly, and a model that optimizes perplexity well might not necessarily perform well on tasks that require more nuanced understanding.

## PALOMA language benchmark: 571 text domains ranging from news to Reddit

The PALOMA language benchmark provides an especially thorough evaluation of language modeling capabilities by testing next-token prediction performance across 571 different text domains. These domains range from formal sources like nytimes.com to informal social media content like r/depression on Reddit. This breadth of domains is important because it tests whether a model can generalize across very different styles, topics, and registers of language, rather than performing well only on a narrow subset of text types.

The PALOMA benchmark is particularly valuable for comparing models because it can reveal weaknesses that might be hidden when evaluating on a single validation set. For example, a model might perform well on formal text but poorly on conversational text, or vice versa. By evaluating across 571 domains, the benchmark provides a comprehensive picture of each model's strengths and weaknesses across the full spectrum of language use. The results are grouped into language modeling benchmarks (seven columns) and fine-grained domain benchmarks (five columns), allowing for both broad and detailed comparisons.

## PALOMA results: xLSTM[1:0] outperforms competitors on 85.1% to 99.8% of domains

The PALOMA results provide compelling evidence for xLSTM's superiority in language modeling. The xLSTM[1:0] variant (pure mLSTM) performs better than xLSTM[7:1] on these language tasks, suggesting that the matrix memory's enhanced storage capacity is particularly beneficial for the diverse text domains in the PALOMA benchmark. The specific comparison statistics are striking: xLSTM[1:0] achieves a lower perplexity than Mamba in 568 out of 571 domains (99.5%), a lower perplexity than Llama in 486 out of 571 domains (85.1%), and a lower perplexity than RWKV-4 in 570 out of 571 domains (99.8%).

These numbers demonstrate that xLSTM's advantage is not confined to a particular type of text but is broadly applicable across the vast majority of text domains tested. The comparison with Llama is particularly noteworthy: even though Transformers have been the dominant architecture for large language models, xLSTM achieves lower perplexity in 85.1% of the tested domains. The near-complete dominance over Mamba and RWKV-4 (99.5% and 99.8%, respectively) further underscores the effectiveness of xLSTM's architectural innovations for language modeling across diverse text types.

## Scaling law analysis: power-law behavior and extrapolation to larger models

Scaling law analysis is a critical tool for understanding how models will perform at scales beyond what has been directly tested. By fitting power-law curves to the relationship between model size and performance (typically measured in loss or perplexity), researchers can extrapolate to predict how a method will perform at larger scales. This analysis follows the methodology established in landmark scaling studies, which showed that language model performance follows predictable power-law relationships with model size.

The scaling law analysis in this experiment uses the data from all four model sizes (125M, 350M, 760M, 1.3B) to estimate the power-law relationship for each method. This allows the researchers to compare not just the absolute performance at each size, but also the rate at which performance improves as the models grow. A method with a steeper scaling curve would eventually overtake a method with a higher intercept but shallower curve, making the scaling behavior as important as the absolute performance at any given size.

## Scaling law results: xLSTM outperforms Mamba by similar margin as Mamba outperforms Llama

The scaling law results reveal a clear hierarchy among the methods. RWKV-4 performs worst, followed by Llama, then Mamba, and finally xLSTM performs best. Importantly, the margin by which xLSTM outperforms Mamba is similar to the margin by which Mamba outperforms Llama. This is a significant finding because it suggests that xLSTM represents a substantial step forward in the scaling behavior of sequence models, comparable to the improvement that State Space Models like Mamba provided over Transformers.

All models share a similar scaling behavior in the sense that they follow power-law relationships with model size, but they differ in their offsets—the constant term in the power-law that determines where each method's curve sits relative to the others. The fact that xLSTM has the lowest offset means that at any given model size, xLSTM is expected to outperform the other methods, and this advantage is projected to continue at larger scales. The scaling behavior indicates that for larger models, xLSTM will continue to perform favorably compared to Transformers and State Space Models, making it a serious competitor for future large language model development.

## Text generation time: linear scaling of xLSTM vs. quadratic scaling of Transformer

The text generation time experiments provide crucial insights into the practical deployment characteristics of xLSTM compared to other models. At the 1.3 billion parameter scale, the text generation time is measured for xLSTM variants and compared against similarly sized Mamba, Llama, and RWKV implementations. All models are tested at batch size 1 with a pre-fill of 16 tokens, which is designed to be maximally favorable for the Transformer model (Llama), since Transformers excel at parallel processing of the pre-fill context.

The results demonstrate a fundamental difference in how the architectures scale with sequence length during generation. xLSTM, along with the other recurrent models (Mamba and RWKV-4), exhibits linear scaling of generation time with sequence length. This linear scaling is a direct consequence of the recurrent nature of these models: each new token requires a constant amount of additional computation regardless of how many tokens have already been generated. In contrast, Llama, as a Transformer, exhibits quadratic scaling of generation time with sequence length. This quadratic scaling arises because the Transformer's self-attention mechanism requires attending to all previously generated tokens, and the number of such tokens grows linearly with sequence length, resulting in quadratic total computation.

## Maximal throughput: xLSTM achieves highest throughput due to constant memory

The maximal throughput experiments measure how many tokens per unit time each model can generate, which is a critical metric for practical deployment in applications serving multiple users. The throughput is measured at different batch sizes and pre-fill lengths, providing a comprehensive picture of each model's efficiency under various operational conditions.

The results show that xLSTM achieves the highest throughput among the compared methods. This advantage is directly attributable to xLSTM's constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length. Unlike Transformers, which must maintain and process a key-value cache that grows with the context length, xLSTM maintains a fixed-size memory state regardless of how long the sequence becomes. This means that the memory required to generate tokens does not increase with sequence length, allowing xLSTM to maintain high throughput even for very long sequences.

## Batch size advantages: xLSTM can use much higher batch sizes than Llama

A particularly important practical advantage of xLSTM's constant memory complexity is the ability to use much higher batch sizes than the Transformer-based Llama model. Because Llama's memory usage grows with both the batch size and the sequence length (due to the key-value cache), increasing the batch size quickly becomes prohibitive for long sequences. In contrast, xLSTM's memory usage grows only with the batch size, not with the sequence length, allowing it to serve many more concurrent requests with the same amount of GPU memory.

This batch size advantage has significant implications for the deployment of language models in production environments. In scenarios where many users are generating text simultaneously, xLSTM can accommodate more users per GPU, reducing the infrastructure costs and improving the overall efficiency of the system. The combination of linear generation time scaling and high batch size capability makes xLSTM particularly well-suited for real-world deployment scenarios where both long context windows and high throughput are required. These practical advantages, combined with the strong performance on perplexity, downstream tasks, and the PALOMA benchmark, position xLSTM as a compelling alternative to Transformers for large-scale language modeling.

# Limitations and Future Directions

The Extended Long Short-Term Memory (xLSTM) architecture represents a significant advancement in recurrent neural network design, demonstrating competitive performance against Transformers and State Space Models in language modeling tasks. However, a thorough understanding of any architecture requires not only an appreciation of its strengths but also a clear-eyed assessment of its current limitations. This chapter examines the constraints of xLSTM as identified by its creators, discusses the empirical evidence surrounding these limitations, and outlines the promising directions for future research and application that the xLSTM framework opens up.

#

## sLSTM Parallelization Limitation

One of the defining features that distinguishes the sLSTM from the mLSTM is the presence of memory mixing, which is the mechanism by which hidden states from one time step influence the hidden states of the next through recurrent connections. Specifically, the sLSTM retains the hidden-hidden recurrent connections that were a hallmark of the original LSTM, where the hidden state vector $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ is used in computing the cell input $\mathbf{z}_t$, the input gate $i_t$, the forget gate $f_t$, and the output gate $o_t$ at the next time step. These recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{z}}$, $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{i}}$, $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{f}}$, and $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{o}}$ create a dependency chain that enforces sequential processing — each time step's computation cannot begin until the previous time step's hidden state has been fully computed.

This sequential dependency is the fundamental reason why the sLSTM cannot be parallelized across the time dimension. Unlike the mLSTM, which abandons memory mixing and thereby enables a parallel formulation analogous to FlashAttention, the sLSTM is inherently sequential in its forward pass. This means that during training, the sLSTM cannot take advantage of the massive parallelism that modern GPUs offer for sequence processing. The computational graph must be unrolled through time, and each step must wait for its predecessor to complete.

It is worth noting that this limitation is not merely an engineering inconvenience but a trade-off that comes with genuine computational power. Memory mixing through hidden-hidden connections is what enables the sLSTM to solve state tracking problems, which are tasks that require the model to maintain and update an internal state in a way that depends on the full history of inputs. Research has shown that models without memory mixing — such as Transformers and State Space Models — are fundamentally less expressive than RNNs with memory mixing, and cannot solve tasks like regular grammars or the parity problem. The sLSTM's ability to perform state tracking is directly tied to its memory mixing, making it more expressive than State Space Models and Transformers in this regard. State tracking is essential for applications such as evaluating code or tracking entities in long narratives, where the model must maintain a precise internal representation that evolves with each input.

Thus, the parallelization limitation of the sLSTM is a deliberate design trade-off: the authors chose to retain memory mixing to preserve the expressive power that comes with state tracking capability, accepting the sequential processing constraint as a necessary cost. This positions the sLSTM as a complementary component to the mLSTM within the xLSTM architecture, where the two variants serve different but synergistic roles.

#

## sLSTM CUDA Kernel Performance

Despite the inherent sequential nature of the sLSTM, the developers of xLSTM invested considerable effort in optimizing its implementation to mitigate the performance gap relative to parallelizable architectures. The authors developed a fast custom CUDA implementation for the sLSTM that includes GPU memory optimizations pushed down to the register level. Register-level optimization is a low-level optimization technique where frequently accessed data is kept in the GPU's registers — the fastest and smallest tier of memory in the GPU hierarchy — rather than in slower shared memory or global memory. This reduces the latency of memory accesses and can significantly improve throughput for sequential operations.

The result of these optimizations is that the sLSTM CUDA kernel is typically less than two times slower than the parallel mLSTM implementation. This is a notable achievement given that the mLSTM benefits from full parallelization across the sequence length, while the sLSTM must process the sequence step by step. A slowdown of less than $2\times$ means that the sLSTM remains practically viable for use within large-scale xLSTM architectures, where it is interleaved with mLSTM blocks. In the xLSTM architecture, the sLSTM blocks appear at a ratio determined by the notation xLSTM[$a$:$b$], where $b$ out of every $a+b$ blocks are sLSTM-based. For example, in an xLSTM[7:1] configuration with 48 total blocks, only 6 blocks are sLSTM-based, so the overall sequential processing overhead is a small fraction of the total computation.

The fact that the sLSTM is less than two times slower than the mLSTM, combined with its strategic placement as a minority of blocks in the architecture, means that the practical impact on wall clock time during training and inference is limited. The sLSTM blocks contribute the state tracking capability and memory mixing that the architecture needs for tasks requiring expressive recurrent computation, while the majority mLSTM blocks provide the parallelizable processing power and matrix memory capacity that enables efficient scaling.

#

## mLSTM CUDA Kernel Optimization

While the mLSTM is fully parallelizable in principle, the practical implementation of its CUDA kernels presents its own set of challenges. The authors acknowledge that the CUDA kernels for the mLSTM are not yet fully optimized, and as a result, the current implementation is approximately four times slower than FlashAttention or the scan operation used in Mamba. FlashAttention is a highly optimized implementation of the Transformer attention mechanism that minimizes memory reads and writes through careful tiling and recomputation strategies. Similarly, the scan operation in Mamba benefits from optimized implementations of parallel prefix scan algorithms that are well-studied and highly tuned.

The $4\times$ slowdown of the mLSTM relative to these highly optimized baselines is significant and represents one of the most pressing engineering challenges for the xLSTM framework. However, the authors suggest that this gap is not fundamental but rather a consequence of the relative immaturity of the mLSTM implementation. They note that faster CUDA kernels could be obtained "in the vein of FlashAttention," meaning that similar optimization techniques — such as memory-efficient tiling, online softmax computation, and kernel fusion — could be applied to the mLSTM's parallel recurrence.

The parallelization of the mLSTM recurrence is analogous to the parallelization strategies used in FlashAttention and Gated Linear Attention (GLA). The key insight is that the mLSTM's matrix memory update and retrieval operations, while involving $d \times d$ matrices, can be reformulated as operations that are amenable to parallel prefix scan algorithms. The cell state update $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ and the normalizer state update $\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{k}_t$ both follow a linear recurrence pattern that can be parallelized using established techniques from the parallel scan literature. The current $4\times$ slowdown thus represents an opportunity rather than a fundamental barrier, and with further kernel development, the mLSTM could approach the efficiency of its Transformer and State Space Model counterparts.

#

## Matrix Memory Computational Complexity

The mLSTM's matrix memory is one of its most distinctive features and a primary source of its enhanced storage capacity. By upgrading the LSTM's scalar memory cell $c \in \mathbb{R}$ to a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, the mLSTM dramatically increases the amount of information that can be stored and retrieved. The covariance update rule $\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ stores key-value pairs as outer products in the matrix memory, and retrieval is performed through matrix-vector multiplication $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$, where $\mathbf{q}_t$ is a query vector. This is the setting of Bidirectional Associative Memories, and the covariance update rule is known to be optimal for maximal separability of retrieved binary vectors, which is equivalent to maximizing the signal-to-noise ratio.

However, this enhanced capacity comes at a computational cost. The matrix memory requires processing $d \times d$ matrices, which means that both the storage and the update operations scale quadratically with the embedding dimension $d$. The cell state update involves a matrix-matrix multiplication (when scaling $\mathbf{C}_{t-1}$ by the scalar forget gate $f_t$) and a rank-1 outer product update (when adding $i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$). The retrieval operation involves a matrix-vector multiplication of a $d \times d$ matrix with a $d$-dimensional query vector, which has $O(d^2)$ complexity. For large embedding dimensions, these operations can be computationally expensive.

Despite this computational complexity, the authors identify several mitigating factors. First and most importantly, the matrix memory does not require any parameters — the memory $\mathbf{C}_t$ is a state that is computed and updated during the forward pass, not a learned weight matrix. This means that the increased computational cost does not translate into increased parameter count, keeping the model's memory footprint (in terms of learnable parameters) manageable. Second, the matrix operations — matrix-matrix multiplication, matrix-vector multiplication, and outer products — are precisely the types of operations that GPUs are designed to handle efficiently. Standard matrix operations can be parallelized on GPUs using highly optimized BLAS (Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms) libraries, so the wall clock time overhead due to the complex memory is described as "minor" relative to the overall computation. The authors explicitly state that "the computations can be done in parallel on GPUs, therefore these computations have only a minor effect on the wall clock time."

This represents a fundamental trade-off in the mLSTM design: memory capacity is traded against computational complexity. The mLSTM gains the ability to store and retrieve rich key-value associations — which is essential for tasks like rare token prediction, where the limited scalar memory of the original LSTM causes it to perform poorly — at the cost of quadratic computation in the embedding dimension. The parallelizability of these matrix operations on GPUs makes this trade-off favorable in practice, particularly for the large embedding dimensions used in modern language models.

#

## Forget Gate Initialization

The forget gate is a critical component of both the sLSTM and mLSTM, controlling how much of the previous memory state is retained at each time step. In the xLSTM framework, the forget gate can use either a sigmoid activation function $\sigma(\tilde{f}_t)$ or an exponential activation function $\exp(\tilde{f}_t)$, where $\tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + b_f$ (or with an additional recurrent term $r_f h_{t-1}$ in the sLSTM case). The choice of initialization for the forget gate's bias term $b_f$ has a significant impact on the training dynamics and the model's ability to learn long-range dependencies.

The authors identify forget gate initialization as a limitation, noting that it "must be chosen carefully." This concern is well-founded in the context of recurrent neural networks. In the original LSTM, the forget gate bias is often initialized to a positive value (such as 1.0) to encourage the forget gate to be open (close to 1) at the beginning of training, which allows gradients to flow more easily through the cell state and mitigates the vanishing gradient problem. If the forget gate is initialized to be closed (close to 0), the cell state is reset at each step, and the model cannot learn long-range dependencies.

In the xLSTM, the situation is more complex due to the use of exponential gating. When the forget gate uses an exponential activation, the gate value $\exp(\tilde{f}_t)$ can grow very large if the pre-activation $\tilde{f}_t$ is positive, or become very small if it is negative. This sensitivity means that the initialization of the forget gate's bias and weights must be calibrated to ensure that the gate values remain in a reasonable range at the start of training. If the forget gate values are too large, the cell state can grow without bound (though the normalizer state helps mitigate this), and if they are too small, the memory is effectively erased at each step.

The stabilization technique introduced for the exponential gates — using the stabilizer state $m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t))$ and the stabilized gates $f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t)$ and $i'_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t)$ — helps prevent numerical overflow during the forward pass. However, the initialization still matters because it determines the starting point of the optimization trajectory. A poor initialization can lead to slow convergence or training instability, particularly in the early stages of training when the model has not yet learned to calibrate its gates appropriately.

The need for careful forget gate initialization is not unique to xLSTM — it is a known challenge in all gated recurrent architectures — but the introduction of exponential gating makes it more acute. The authors' acknowledgment of this as a limitation suggests that further research is needed to develop principled initialization strategies for exponential gates, perhaps drawing on the growing body of work on initialization schemes for modern architectures.

#

## Matrix Memory Capacity for Very Long Contexts

The matrix memory of the mLSTM is a fixed-size state that is independent of the sequence length. This is in contrast to the Transformer's attention mechanism, which maintains a key-value cache that grows linearly with the sequence length. The fixed size of the matrix memory is both an advantage and a potential limitation.

The advantage is clear: because the memory does not grow with the sequence length, the mLSTM has constant memory complexity with respect to the sequence length, as opposed to the Transformer's linear (or, for attention computation, quadratic) memory complexity. This makes the xLSTM well-suited for industrial applications and implementations on edge devices, where memory is constrained. The authors note that "contrary to Transformers, xLSTM networks have a linear computation and a constant memory complexity with respect to the sequence length," and that "since the xLSTM memory is compressive, it is well suited for industrial applications and implementations on the edge."

However, the fixed size of the matrix memory also means that as the sequence length increases, the memory must compress more and more information into the same $d \times d$ matrix. This raises the concern that for very long sequences, the matrix memory could become overloaded — that is, the stored information could interfere with itself, making retrieval unreliable. The authors acknowledge this as a potential limitation, noting that "since the matrix memory is independent of the sequence length, increasing the sequence length might overload the memory for longer context sizes."

The concern can be understood through the lens of the covariance update rule. Each key-value pair stored in the matrix memory contributes a rank-1 update $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ to the cell state. As more pairs are stored, the matrix becomes a sum of many rank-1 components, and retrieval through the query vector $\mathbf{q}_t$ involves computing $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t = \sum_{\tau} (\mathbf{v}_\tau \mathbf{k}_\tau^\top) \mathbf{q}_t$ (with appropriate gating). The quality of retrieval depends on the ability of the query to selectively retrieve the correct value, which in turn depends on the orthogonality of the stored key vectors. As more keys are stored, they are more likely to overlap, leading to interference and degraded retrieval performance.

#

## Empirical Evidence: Contexts Up to 16k

Despite the theoretical concern about matrix memory overload for very long contexts, the authors provide empirical evidence that this limitation does not manifest in practice for context lengths up to 16,384 tokens. In their large-scale language modeling experiments, they trained 1.3B-parameter models of xLSTM, RWKV-4, Llama, and Mamba on a context length of 2048 tokens from the SlimPajama dataset, and then tested these models on context lengths up to 16,384 tokens to assess sequence length extrapolation.

The results of this experiment are highly encouraging for xLSTM. The authors report that "in contrast to other methods, xLSTM models maintain low perplexities for longer contexts." This means that even though the xLSTM was trained on sequences of length 2048, it was able to maintain good performance when evaluated on sequences up to 8 times longer. This is a significant finding, as sequence length extrapolation is a known challenge for many architectures. Transformers, for example, typically require explicit mechanisms (such as rotary position embeddings or length extrapolation techniques) to handle sequences longer than those seen during training.

The authors explicitly state that the potential memory overload "does not appear to be a limitation for contexts up to 16k." This empirical finding suggests that the matrix memory, despite its fixed size, is sufficient to store and retrieve the information needed for language modeling at context lengths that are relevant for many practical applications. The normalizer state and the use of the absolute value of the dot product between the query and normalizer state (with a lower bound of 1.0) help ensure stable retrieval even as more information is stored.

However, the authors do not claim that the matrix memory will remain adequate for arbitrarily long contexts. The empirical evidence supports contexts up to 16k, but further research is needed to determine whether the matrix memory will remain effective for contexts of 32k, 64k, 128k, or longer — context lengths that are increasingly common in modern large language model applications. The scaling behavior of the matrix memory's effective capacity with respect to the embedding dimension $d$ and the number of layers in the architecture is an important area for future investigation.

#

## Incomplete Architecture and Hyperparameter Optimization

The authors are transparent about the fact that the xLSTM architecture and its hyperparameters have not been fully optimized, particularly for larger model sizes. They note that "due to the expensive computational load for large language experiments, we did neither fully optimize the architecture nor the hyperparameters, especially for larger xLSTM architectures." This is a common challenge in deep learning research, where the computational cost of training large models limits the number of configurations that can be explored.

The xLSTM architecture involves several design choices that could be further optimized. These include the ratio of mLSTM to sLSTM blocks (denoted as xLSTM[$a$:$b$]), the number of residual blocks, the embedding dimension, the choice of whether the forget gate uses a sigmoid or exponential activation, the up-projection dimension in the residual blocks, the initialization of the forget gate, the learning rate schedule, and many other hyperparameters. Each of these choices can have a significant impact on the model's performance, and the optimal configuration may vary with the model size.

The ablation studies presented in the paper provide some guidance on the importance of individual components. The authors morphed a vanilla LSTM architecture step-by-step into an xLSTM architecture, first integrating LSTM layers into pre-LayerNorm residual backbones, then extending to a post up-projection block, and finally adding exponential gating and matrix memory. The results showed that both the exponential gating and the matrix memory contributed significantly to the performance improvement. Additionally, the ablation of different gating mechanisms revealed that having each gate be learnable and influenced by the input has an incremental positive effect.

However, these ablation studies were conducted at a relatively small scale (350M parameters trained on 15B tokens), and it is not guaranteed that the findings will transfer to larger scales. The authors' anticipation that "an extensive optimization process is needed for xLSTM to reach its full potential" suggests that the results presented in the paper may represent a lower bound on what the xLSTM architecture can achieve. With more thorough architecture search and hyperparameter tuning — possibly using techniques such as neural architecture search, Bayesian optimization, or population-based training — the xLSTM could potentially achieve even better performance.

The incompleteness of optimization also extends to the training recipe itself. The authors used mixed precision training, but noted that for some methods (RWKV-5, RWKV-6, GLA, HGRN2), the mixed-precision training did not utilize PyTorch's automated mixed precision. While this was not an issue for xLSTM itself, it suggests that the training infrastructure and recipes for recurrent models are still evolving, and further improvements in training stability and efficiency are possible.

#

## Future Potential: Impact on Reinforcement Learning, Time Series Prediction, and Physical Systems Modeling

While the xLSTM paper focuses primarily on language modeling, the authors identify several domains where the xLSTM architecture has the potential to make significant contributions. In their conclusion, they state that "xLSTM has the potential to considerably impact other fields like Reinforcement Learning, Time Series Prediction, or the modeling of physical systems." Each of these domains has characteristics that make them well-suited to the xLSTM's strengths.

Reinforcement learning is a domain where the original LSTM has historically excelled. The authors note that LSTMs are the best performing sequence models in reinforcement learning, citing examples such as the AlphaStar model for StarCraft II, the OpenAI Five model for Dota 2, and models for the magnetic controller for nuclear fusion. In reinforcement learning, agents must maintain an internal representation of the environment state based on the history of observations and actions, which is precisely the kind of state tracking that the sLSTM's memory mixing enables. The sLSTM's ability to solve state tracking problems — which makes it more expressive than Transformers and State Space Models — could be particularly valuable in reinforcement learning settings where the agent must reason about the consequences of actions over many time steps, such as in partially observable Markov decision processes. The exponential gating mechanism, which allows the sLSTM to revise storage decisions, could also be beneficial in reinforcement learning, where the agent may need to update its beliefs about the environment state as new observations arrive.

Time series prediction is another domain where the xLSTM's properties could be advantageous. Time series data often exhibits long-range dependencies, seasonal patterns, and regime changes, all of which require the model to maintain and selectively update a memory of past observations. The mLSTM's matrix memory, with its ability to store key-value associations, could be particularly useful for time series tasks that involve recalling specific historical patterns or events. The constant memory complexity of the xLSTM with respect to sequence length is also a significant advantage for time series prediction, where sequences can be very long and the ability to process them efficiently is crucial. Unlike Transformers, which require growing key-value caches for longer sequences, the xLSTM maintains a fixed-size memory, making it more suitable for streaming or online prediction scenarios.

The modeling of physical systems represents a third domain where the xLSTM could have significant impact. Physical systems often involve complex dynamics that must be tracked over time, including conservation laws, state transitions, and interactions between components. The sLSTM's state tracking capability is directly relevant to this kind of modeling, as it allows the network to maintain and update an internal state that mirrors the physical state of the system. The mLSTM's matrix memory could also be valuable for storing and retrieving information about the system's configuration, such as the positions and velocities of particles or the values of physical fields. The xLSTM's linear computation and constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length make it particularly well-suited for modeling physical systems over long time horizons, where the computational cost of Transformers would grow prohibitively.

More broadly, the xLSTM architecture's combination of expressive power (through the sLSTM's memory mixing and state tracking), storage capacity (through the mLSTM's matrix memory), and computational efficiency (through constant memory complexity and parallelizable mLSTM operations) positions it as a versatile tool for any domain that involves sequential data with long-range dependencies. The scaling laws observed in the language modeling experiments, which indicate that larger xLSTM models will continue to perform favorably compared to Transformers and State Space Models, suggest that the xLSTM's advantages will only become more pronounced as the architecture is scaled up and applied to new domains. The authors' conclusion that xLSTM models are "at least as far as current technologies like Transformers or State Space Models" in language modeling, combined with the potential for further optimization and application to new domains, paints a promising picture for the future of the xLSTM framework.

# Conclusion and Broader Impact

# Answering the Research Question: How Far Do We Get Scaling LSTMs to Billions of Parameters?

The central question that motivated the development of xLSTM was deceptively simple yet profound: How far do we get in language modeling when scaling LSTMs to billions of parameters, leveraging the latest techniques from modern LLMs, but mitigating known limitations of LSTMs? This question arose from a historical context in which LSTMs, despite their tremendous successes across numerous domains, were largely supplanted by Transformers in language modeling after 2017. The Transformer architecture, with its parallelizable self-attention mechanism at its core, marked a new era and outpaced LSTMs at scale. The question was whether the fundamental ideas underlying LSTM — the constant error carousel and gating — could be modernized and scaled to compete with state-of-the-art architectures.

The answer, as the authors put it, is at least as far as current technologies like Transformers or State Space Models. This is a remarkable conclusion given that LSTMs had been largely set aside in favor of Transformer-based approaches for large language modeling. The xLSTM architecture, built upon two key modifications to the original LSTM — exponential gating and novel memory structures — demonstrates that the core LSTM ideas, when properly extended, remain competitive at scale. The experimental evidence spans synthetic tasks, language modeling benchmarks, downstream task evaluation, and scaling law analysis, all of which point toward xLSTM as a viable alternative to Transformers and State Space Models.

It is important to understand the significance of this finding in the broader landscape of deep learning research. The dominance of Transformers in recent years has been so pronounced that alternative architectures have often been dismissed or relegated to niche applications. The xLSTM results challenge this assumption by showing that the sequential, recurrence-based approach of LSTMs, when augmented with the right modifications, can match or exceed the performance of attention-based models. This reopens the exploration space for recurrent architectures in large-scale language modeling and beyond.

The phrase "at least as far" is deliberately measured. The authors acknowledge that their experiments, while extensive, did not fully optimize the architecture or hyperparameters, especially for larger xLSTM configurations, due to the expensive computational load involved in large language experiments. This suggests that the results presented may represent a lower bound on what xLSTM can achieve, and that further optimization could yield even stronger performance.

# Key Contributions: Exponential Gating, Matrix Memory, and New Memory Mixing

The xLSTM architecture introduces two principal modifications to the original LSTM framework, each addressing a specific limitation of the classical LSTM. These modifications are not merely incremental tweaks but represent fundamental changes to how the LSTM processes, stores, and retrieves information. Understanding these contributions in detail is essential for appreciating the architectural advances that enable xLSTM to compete with modern alternatives.

The first major contribution is exponential gating. In the original LSTM, the input and forget gates use sigmoid activation functions, $\sigma(x) = 1/(1+\exp(-x))$, which produce values in the range $(0, 1)$. While this gating mechanism effectively controls the flow of information into and out of the memory cell, it has a critical limitation: it struggles to revise storage decisions once they have been made. The authors illustrate this with the Nearest Neighbor Search problem, where a sequence must be scanned for the most similar vector to a reference vector, and the attached value must be provided at the sequence end. The LSTM struggles to revise a stored value when a more similar vector is found later in the sequence, because the sigmoid gates cannot easily "overwrite" previous storage decisions with sufficient magnitude. Exponential gating replaces the sigmoid activation with an exponential activation, $\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ for the input gate and optionally $\exp(\tilde{f}_t)$ for the forget gate. This change allows the gates to take on values greater than $1$, enabling the network to more aggressively update its memory when new, more relevant information arrives. The exponential activation provides a sharper, more decisive gating signal, which is crucial for tasks that require the model to revise its internal state based on new evidence.

Exponential gating, however, introduces numerical challenges. The exponential function can produce very large values that cause overflow during computation. To address this, the authors introduce a stabilizer state $m_t$ that tracks the maximum of the logarithmic gate values, ensuring that the stabilized gates $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ remain numerically stable without changing the network's output or the derivatives of the loss with respect to the parameters. Additionally, a normalizer state $n_t$ is introduced to normalize the cell state, preventing unbounded growth and ensuring that the hidden state remains well-scaled. The normalizer state accumulates the product of the input gate and all future forget gates, effectively tracking the total "weight" applied to the memory at each time step. The hidden state is then computed as $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$, providing a self-normalizing mechanism that was absent in the original LSTM.

The second major contribution is the modification of the LSTM memory structure, which gives rise to two distinct variants: sLSTM and mLSTM. The sLSTM retains the scalar memory of the original LSTM but augments it with exponential gating and a new form of memory mixing. The mLSTM, on the other hand, replaces the scalar memory cell $c \in \mathbb{R}$ with a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, dramatically increasing the storage capacity of the memory cell. This matrix memory uses a covariance update rule, where a key-value pair $(\mathbf{k}_t, \mathbf{v}_t)$ is stored via an outer product update: $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. Retrieval is then performed by multiplying the matrix memory with a query vector: $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t / \max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1\}$. This is directly analogous to the attention mechanism in Transformers, where queries, keys, and values are used to retrieve relevant information, but the mLSTM accomplishes this within a recurrent framework with constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length.

The matrix memory of mLSTM addresses the limited storage capacity of the original LSTM, which compressed all information into scalar cell states. The authors demonstrate this limitation through the Rare Token Prediction task, where the LSTM performs worse on rare tokens because it cannot adequately store the diverse information needed for their prediction. The mLSTM's matrix memory, being of size $d \times d$, provides exponentially more storage capacity, enabling the model to retain a richer representation of the input history.

The third contribution is a new approach to memory mixing. In the original LSTM, multiple scalar memory cells are combined in a vector, and recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ mix the cell outputs. In sLSTM, this concept is extended through the introduction of multiple heads, where memory mixing occurs within each head but not across heads. This is a new architectural feature that, combined with exponential gating, establishes a new way of mixing information across memory cells. The mLSTM, by contrast, abandons memory mixing entirely — there are no hidden-hidden recurrent connections — which enables it to be fully parallelizable. For mLSTM, multiple heads and multiple cells are equivalent since there is no memory mixing.

The distinction between sLSTM and mLSTM in terms of memory mixing has important implications. The sLSTM, with its memory mixing, is more expressive and can solve state tracking problems that models without memory mixing cannot. The authors show that models without memory mixing, such as Transformers and State Space Models, cannot solve regular grammars like the parity task. The mLSTM, while lacking memory mixing, gains the ability to be parallelized across the sequence dimension, which is crucial for efficient training on modern hardware. The xLSTM architecture integrates both variants, using sLSTM blocks for their expressiveness and mLSTM blocks for their parallelizability and memory capacity.

# Performance Summary: xLSTM Competes Favorably with Transformers and State Space Models

The experimental evaluation of xLSTM is comprehensive, spanning multiple scales, tasks, and comparison methods. The results consistently demonstrate that xLSTM performs favorably when compared to state-of-the-art Transformers and State Space Models, both in terms of performance and scaling behavior.

On synthetic tasks, xLSTM's exponential gating with memory mixing is tested on formal language tasks, including regular grammars such as the parity task. The results show that xLSTM can solve these tasks, while models without memory mixing — including Transformers and State Space Models — cannot. This finding is consistent with theoretical results showing that RNNs with memory mixing are more expressive than Transformers and State Space Models. The sLSTM component of xLSTM is specifically responsible for this capability, as it retains the hidden-hidden recurrent connections that enable state tracking.

On the Multi-Query Associative Recall task, which tests memory capacity by requiring models to memorize and retrieve key-value pairs from sequences, xLSTM performs best among all non-Transformer models. This validates the effectiveness of the matrix memory introduced in mLSTM. Interestingly, the inclusion of sLSTM blocks does not diminish the memory capacity but rather leverages it, as evidenced by the most difficult task with 256 key-value pairs. This finding suggests that the two variants are complementary: mLSTM provides the storage capacity, while sLSTM provides the expressive power for state tracking.

On the Long Range Arena, which evaluates performance on long sequences and large contexts, xLSTM demonstrates consistent strong performance across all tasks, suggesting that the architecture is efficient in handling different aspects of long context problems.

In the method comparison experiment, models are trained on 15B tokens from SlimPajama and evaluated by validation set perplexity. The comparison includes Transformers (GPT-3, Llama), State Space Models (H3, Mamba), Recurrent Neural Networks (RWKV-4, RWKV-5, RWKV-6, HGRN, HGRN2), and linear Transformers (GLA, RetNet, Hyena). The results show that xLSTM outperforms all existing methods in validation perplexity. This is a significant result, as it demonstrates that the xLSTM architecture, with its blend of sLSTM and mLSTM blocks, can surpass the performance of well-established and highly optimized alternative architectures at a scale of 350M parameters.

In the large-scale language modeling experiment, models are trained on 300B tokens from SlimPajama, and the comparison is narrowed to one representative from each method class: RWKV-4 (RNN), Llama (Transformer), and Mamba (SSM). For validation set perplexity, both xLSTM$[1:0]$ (pure mLSTM) and xLSTM$[7:1]$ (seven mLSTM blocks for every one sLSTM block) are the best models across all model sizes (125M, 350M, 760M, 1.3B). On downstream tasks measuring common sense reasoning, xLSTM is the best method in the vast majority of tasks and across all model sizes, with Mamba occasionally outperforming on the ARC task.

On the PALOMA language benchmark, which evaluates next token prediction across 571 text domains ranging from nytimes.com to r/depression on Reddit, xLSTM$[1:0]$ performs better than xLSTM$[7:1]$, suggesting that the pure mLSTM configuration is particularly well-suited for diverse language modeling. The xLSTM$[1:0]$ variant achieves lower perplexity than Mamba in 568 out of 571 (99.5\%) text domains, lower perplexity than Llama in 486 out of 571 (85.1\%) domains, and lower perplexity than RWKV-4 in 570 out of 571 (99.8\%) domains. These results provide strong evidence that xLSTM is a highly capable language model that generalizes well across diverse text domains.

On sequence length extrapolation, xLSTM models maintain low perplexities for context lengths up to 16384, even when trained on a context length of 2048. This is a notable advantage over other methods, which do not extrapolate as gracefully to longer contexts. This capability is particularly relevant for applications that require processing long sequences beyond the training context length.

Finally, in terms of inference efficiency, xLSTM exhibits linear scaling of text generation time with respect to sequence length, in contrast to the quadratic scaling of the Transformer-based Llama model. xLSTM also achieves the highest decoding throughput, as its constant memory complexity allows it to use much higher batch sizes than the Transformer model, which requires a key-value cache that grows with sequence length.

# Scaling Law Implications: Larger xLSTM Models as Serious Competitors to Transformer-Based LLMs

The scaling law analysis is one of the most consequential findings of the xLSTM paper, as it provides a basis for extrapolating performance to model sizes beyond those directly tested. Scaling laws, as established by Kaplan et al. and Brown et al., describe the power-law relationship between model performance (typically measured by loss or perplexity) and model size, training data, and compute budget. By analyzing the scaling behavior of xLSTM alongside Transformers and State Space Models, the authors provide insight into the relative competitiveness of these architectures at larger scales.

In the 15B token experiment, the scaling behavior indicates that xLSTM will perform favorably for larger models. In the 300B token experiment, which provides a more robust basis for scaling analysis due to the twentyfold increase in training data, all models share a similar scaling behavior but with different offsets. The key observation is that xLSTM is better than Mamba, with a similar margin to Mamba as Mamba has to Llama. This means that the performance gap between xLSTM and Mamba is comparable to the gap between Mamba and Llama, suggesting that xLSTM represents a meaningful improvement over the current state of the art.

The significance of scaling laws in this context cannot be overstated. In the regime where all architectures exhibit similar scaling exponents — meaning that the rate of improvement with increasing model size is comparable — the architecture with the lower offset (better performance at any given model size) will maintain its advantage as models grow larger. The xLSTM's favorable offset, combined with its similar scaling exponent, indicates that larger xLSTM models will continue to outperform Transformers and State Space Models of comparable size. This finding positions xLSTM as a serious competitor to current Large Language Models built with Transformer technology.

The scaling law analysis also has implications for the practical deployment of language models. If xLSTM maintains its advantage at larger scales, it could enable the training of more efficient or more capable models for a given compute budget. Furthermore, the constant memory complexity of xLSTM with respect to sequence length — in contrast to the linear or quadratic memory complexity of Transformer key-value caches — means that xLSTM models can process longer contexts without the memory overhead that constrains Transformer-based models. This advantage becomes increasingly significant at larger scales, where the ability to process long contexts efficiently is a critical factor in model utility.

It is worth noting that the authors did not fully optimize the architecture or hyperparameters for larger xLSTM configurations, suggesting that the scaling laws presented may represent a conservative estimate of xLSTM's potential. With extensive optimization, the performance gap between xLSTM and alternative architectures could widen further.

# Broader Impact: Potential Applications Beyond Language Modeling

While the xLSTM paper focuses primarily on language modeling as the benchmark for evaluation, the authors explicitly state that xLSTM has the potential to considerably impact other fields. The original LSTM has been applied across a remarkably diverse set of domains, and the improvements introduced in xLSTM — exponential gating, matrix memory, and enhanced memory mixing — are general architectural innovations that are not specific to language processing.

Reinforcement learning is one field where the authors anticipate significant impact. The original LSTM has been the best performing sequence model in reinforcement learning, powering systems such as the AlphaStar model for StarCraft II, the OpenAI Five model for Dota 2, and models for controlling magnetic confinement in nuclear fusion. In reinforcement learning, agents must maintain an internal representation of the environment state, make decisions based on past observations, and adapt to changing conditions. The sLSTM's ability to solve state tracking problems, which the authors demonstrate through formal language tasks, is directly relevant to the state tracking requirements in reinforcement learning. The ability to revise storage decisions, enabled by exponential gating, is crucial in environments where the agent must update its beliefs based on new observations. Furthermore, the matrix memory of mLSTM provides the capacity to store richer representations of the environment, which could enhance the agent's ability to model complex environments.

Time series prediction is another field where xLSTM could have a significant impact. The original LSTM has been successfully applied to rainfall-runoff modeling and hydrological models for flooding warnings. Time series prediction often involves identifying patterns over long horizons, handling rare events, and revising predictions as new data arrives. The xLSTM's exponential gating addresses the inability to revise storage decisions, which is critical in time series applications where new observations can dramatically change the relevant context. The matrix memory provides the capacity to store a richer history of the time series, which is particularly important for rare event prediction, analogous to the rare token prediction problem in language modeling. The linear computation and constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length make xLSTM well-suited for real-time applications and edge computing, where memory and computational resources are constrained.

The modeling of physical systems is a third field mentioned by the authors. Physical systems often exhibit long-range dependencies, complex dynamics, and the need to track state over extended periods. The sLSTM's state tracking capabilities, combined with the mLSTM's large memory capacity, provide a powerful framework for modeling such systems. The constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length is particularly relevant for physical systems that generate long streams of data, such as sensor networks, scientific simulations, or continuous monitoring systems.

Beyond the specific fields mentioned by the authors, the xLSTM architecture has implications for the broader deep learning community. The success of xLSTM demonstrates that the fundamental ideas of the LSTM — the constant error carousel and gating — remain powerful and relevant, even in an era dominated by attention-based architectures. This finding may encourage researchers to revisit and modernize other classical architectures, potentially uncovering new pathways for advancing the state of the art. The xLSTM also highlights the importance of memory mixing for expressiveness, a property that is absent in Transformers and State Space Models, and which may inspire new architectures that combine the strengths of recurrent and attention-based approaches.

The practical advantages of xLSTM — linear computation and constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length — have significant implications for the deployment of deep learning models. As the demand for processing ever-longer sequences grows, the memory and computational efficiency of the underlying architecture becomes increasingly critical. The xLSTM's compressive memory, which does not grow with sequence length, makes it well-suited for industrial applications and edge implementations where resources are limited. This could enable the deployment of sophisticated sequence models in settings where Transformer-based models are impractical due to their memory requirements.

# The Enduring Legacy of LSTM Ideas and Their Continued Evolution

The xLSTM paper is, in many ways, a testament to the enduring legacy of the ideas introduced by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber in the 1990s. The constant error carousel — the additive update of the cell state that avoids the vanishing gradient problem — and the gating mechanism that controls the flow of information into and out of the memory cell have stood the test of time. These ideas have contributed to numerous deep learning success stories and have been the foundation for the first Large Language Models. The fact that these ideas, when properly extended and modernized, can still compete with the latest architectures is a powerful validation of their fundamental importance.

The evolution from LSTM to xLSTM illustrates a broader pattern in the development of deep learning architectures. Rather than discarding older approaches when new ones emerge, there is value in revisiting and extending classical ideas with modern techniques. The xLSTM incorporates techniques from contemporary Large Language Models, such as residual block backbones and pre-LayerNorm, while introducing novel modifications that address the specific limitations of the original LSTM. This synthesis of classical and modern ideas has proven to be a fruitful approach.

The three limitations of the original LSTM that motivated the development of xLSTM — inability to revise storage decisions, limited storage capacities, and lack of parallelizability — each have been addressed through targeted modifications. The inability to revise storage decisions is addressed by exponential gating, which allows the input and forget gates to take values greater than $1$, enabling more aggressive updates to the memory cell. The limited storage capacity is addressed by the matrix memory of mLSTM, which expands the memory from a scalar to a $d \times d$ matrix. The lack of parallelizability is addressed by the mLSTM's abandonment of memory mixing, which enables the recurrence to be reformulated in a parallel version. The sLSTM retains memory mixing for expressiveness, accepting the trade-off of sequential processing, while benefiting from optimized CUDA implementations that mitigate the speed disadvantage.

The xLSTM architecture, with its integration of sLSTM and mLSTM blocks in residual block backbones, represents a balanced approach that leverages the strengths of both variants. The sLSTM blocks provide the expressive power needed for state tracking and complex reasoning, while the mLSTM blocks provide the memory capacity and parallelizability needed for efficient training and rich representation. The ratio of mLSTM to sLSTM blocks, denoted as xLSTM$[a:b]$, allows for tuning the balance between these two capabilities. The experimental results show that both xLSTM$[1:0]$ (pure mLSTM) and xLSTM$[7:1]$ (a mix favoring mLSTM) perform well, with the optimal configuration depending on the specific task and evaluation criterion.

The continued evolution of LSTM ideas also has implications for our understanding of the theoretical foundations of sequence modeling. The distinction between models with and without memory mixing, and its relationship to expressiveness and the ability to solve state tracking problems, is a fundamental insight that goes beyond the specific architecture of xLSTM. The authors show that models without memory mixing, including Transformers and State Space Models, are fundamentally less powerful than RNNs with memory mixing for certain classes of problems. This finding, supported by theoretical work on the expressiveness of different model classes, highlights the importance of recurrent connections for certain computational tasks and suggests that the trade-off between parallelizability and expressiveness is a fundamental tension in the design of sequence models.

In conclusion, the xLSTM paper demonstrates that the LSTM architecture, when properly extended, remains a powerful and competitive approach for large-scale sequence modeling. The exponential gating, matrix memory, and new memory mixing introduced in xLSTM address the key limitations that had held back the original LSTM, while the integration with modern architectural techniques such as residual block backbones and pre-LayerNorm ensures that the architecture is compatible with contemporary training methodologies. The favorable performance and scaling behavior of xLSTM, combined with its practical advantages in terms of memory and computational efficiency, position it as a serious competitor to Transformer-based Large Language Models and suggest that the legacy of LSTM ideas will continue to evolve and contribute to the advancement of deep learning.
