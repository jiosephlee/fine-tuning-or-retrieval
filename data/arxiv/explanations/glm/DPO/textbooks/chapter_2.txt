Chapter 2: Preliminaries: The Standard RLHF Pipeline

## Phase 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) has emerged as a powerful framework for aligning large language models with human preferences, but before diving into the mechanics of DPO, it is essential to understand the standard RLHF pipeline that DPO seeks to simplify. The RLHF pipeline, as described by Ziegler et al. and later refined in works such as Stiennon et al., Bai et al., and Ouyang et al., typically consists of three phases. The first phase is supervised fine-tuning (SFT), which serves as the foundation upon which the subsequent preference learning stages are built.

Large-scale unsupervised language models are pre-trained on vast corpora of text data, acquiring broad world knowledge and some reasoning skills. However, because these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets, their raw outputs may not align with the specific behaviors desired for a downstream task. For instance, a pre-trained model might generate code that reflects common programming mistakes rather than high-quality coding practices, or it might reproduce common misconceptions present in its training data. To address this, RLHF typically begins by fine-tuning a pre-trained LM with supervised learning on high-quality data tailored to the downstream task(s) of interest, such as dialogue, summarization, or instruction following. This supervised fine-tuning phase produces a model denoted as $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$, which serves as the starting point for the subsequent stages of the RLHF pipeline.

The SFT phase is crucial because it acclimatizes the pre-trained model to the style, format, and quality expected in the target domain. Without this intermediate step, the model's outputs might be too far removed from the distribution of responses that human labelers will later evaluate, making the preference data collection and reward learning stages less effective. In essence, SFT narrows the model's broad capabilities down to the specific kind of task-relevant behavior that the RLHF process will then refine and align with human preferences. The resulting $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ model is used both to generate initial responses for preference data collection and as the initialization for subsequent reward modeling and policy optimization stages.

## Phase 2: Preference Sampling and Reward Modeling

The second phase of the RLHF pipeline involves collecting human preferences and learning a reward model from them. This phase begins by using the SFT model $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ to generate candidate responses. Specifically, the SFT model is prompted with prompts $x$ to produce pairs of answers $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pi^{\text{SFT}}(y \mid x)$. These pairs of responses are then presented to human labelers, who express a preference for one answer over the other. This preference is denoted as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ represents the preferred (or "winning") completion and $y_l$ represents the dispreferred (or "losing") completion among the pair $(y_1, y_2)$.

A critical assumption underlying this process is that the human preferences are generated by some latent reward model $r^*(y, x)$, which represents the true, unobserved reward function that humans implicitly use when evaluating responses. This latent reward model is never directly accessible; instead, we only observe the binary preferences it produces. The goal of the reward modeling phase is therefore to learn a parametric approximation of this latent reward function from the observed preference data.

The preference data is collected into a static dataset $\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$, where each entry consists of a prompt $x^{(i)}$, a preferred response $y_w^{(i)}$, and a dispreferred response $y_l^{(i)}$. This dataset forms the empirical basis upon which the reward model is trained. The use of relative preferences rather than absolute quality ratings is a deliberate design choice, as relative human judgments of response quality are often easier and more reliable to collect than expert demonstrations or absolute scores. Humans are generally better at comparing two options and stating which they prefer than at assigning precise numerical scores to individual responses.

In the context of language models, the reward model $r_\phi(x, y)$ is typically a neural network initialized from the SFT model $\pi^{\text{SFT}}(y \mid x)$. A linear layer is added on top of the final transformer layer to produce a single scalar prediction representing the reward value for a given prompt-response pair. This architectural choice leverages the rich representations already learned during pre-training and SFT, allowing the reward model to effectively evaluate the quality of responses. To ensure a reward function with lower variance, prior works normalize the rewards such that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for all $x$, which helps stabilize the subsequent RL optimization phase.

## The Bradley-Terry Model for Human Preference Distribution

To formally model the relationship between the latent reward function and observed human preferences, the RLHF pipeline relies on a preference model. The Bradley-Terry (BT) model, originally proposed by Bradley and Terry in 1952, is a popular choice for this purpose. The BT model provides a probabilistic framework for modeling pairwise comparisons, stipulating that the probability of one response being preferred over another can be expressed as a function of their respective rewards.

Under the BT model, the human preference distribution $p^*$ is written as:

$$p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}$$

This equation expresses the probability that response $y_1$ is preferred over response $y_2$ given prompt $x$ as a softmax-like function of the latent rewards $r^*(x, y_1)$ and $r^*(x, y_2)$. When the reward for $y_1$ is much higher than the reward for $y_2$, the probability of preferring $y_1$ approaches one. Conversely, when the rewards are equal, the probability is 0.5, reflecting indifference. This formulation elegantly captures the idea that human preferences are stochastic but influenced by the underlying quality of responses as measured by the latent reward function.

An important property of the BT model is that it depends only on the difference of rewards between two completions. That is, $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))$, where $\sigma$ is the logistic (sigmoid) function. This property has significant implications for the identifiability of the reward function, as it means that adding any function that depends only on the prompt $x$ to the reward function does not change the preference distribution. This under-specification is a well-known issue with the Plackett-Luce family of models (of which Bradley-Terry is a special case), and it typically necessitates additional identifiability constraints to achieve guarantees on maximum likelihood estimates.

While the BT model is the most commonly used preference model in RLHF, it is worth noting that more general Plackett-Luce ranking models are also compatible with the framework when access to several ranked answers is available, rather than just pairwise comparisons. The Plackett-Luce model generalizes the BT model to handle full rankings of multiple responses, providing a richer representation of human preferences when such data is available. However, for the common case of binary pairwise preferences, the BT model suffices and is the standard choice in most RLHF implementations.

## Maximum Likelihood Estimation for Reward Models

Given a dataset of human preferences and a preference model such as Bradley-Terry, the next step is to estimate the parameters of the reward model. This is done via maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), which seeks to find the reward model parameters that make the observed preference data most probable under the assumed preference model.

Assuming access to the static dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled from the true preference distribution $p^*$, we parametrize a reward model $r_\phi(x, y)$ and estimate the parameters $\phi$ via maximum likelihood. Framing the problem as binary classification—where the "positive" example is the preferred response and the "negative" example is the dispreferred response—the negative log-likelihood loss is:

$$\mathcal{L}_R(r_\phi, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l))\bigr]$$

where $\sigma$ is the logistic function defined as $\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-z)}$. This loss function has an intuitive interpretation: it penalizes the reward model when it assigns a higher reward to the dispreferred response $y_l$ than to the preferred response $y_w$. When the reward model correctly assigns a much higher reward to $y_w$ compared to $y_l$, the argument to the logistic function is large and positive, making $\sigma(\cdot)$ close to 1 and the log loss close to 0. Conversely, when the reward model incorrectly assigns a higher reward to $y_l$, the loss is large, providing a strong gradient signal to correct the reward model's parameters.

The expectation over the dataset $\mathcal{D}$ means that this loss is averaged over all preference pairs in the training data, ensuring that the reward model learns to generalize across different prompts and response pairs rather than memorizing individual examples. This maximum likelihood estimation procedure is equivalent to training a binary classifier that predicts which of two responses a human would prefer, with the decision boundary determined by the difference in predicted rewards.

The trained reward model $r_\phi(x, y)$ serves as a proxy for the true latent reward function $r^*(x, y)$ throughout the subsequent RL fine-tuning phase. The quality of this proxy is critical to the success of RLHF, as errors in the reward model can lead to suboptimal or even harmful policy behavior. In practice, the reward model may not perfectly capture all nuances of human preferences, and the RL fine-tuning phase must account for potential inaccuracies in the learned reward.

## Phase 3: RL Fine-Tuning and KL-Constrained Reward Maximization

The third and final phase of the standard RLHF pipeline involves using the learned reward function to fine-tune the language model policy through reinforcement learning. During this phase, the learned reward model $r_\phi(x, y)$ provides feedback to the language model, guiding it to produce responses that receive high rewards. However, simply maximizing the learned reward without any constraints would be problematic, as it could lead to the model exploiting idiosyncrasies or errors in the reward model rather than genuinely improving response quality. To address this, the RL fine-tuning phase employs a KL-constrained reward maximization objective.

Following prior works, the optimization is formulated as:

$$\max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_\theta(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_\phi(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\bigl[\pi_\theta(y\mid x) \mid\mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr]$$

where $\beta$ is a parameter controlling the deviation from the base reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, which is typically the initial SFT model $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$. The first term in this objective encourages the policy $\pi_\theta$ to produce responses that receive high rewards according to the learned reward model. The second term, a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence penalty, constrains the policy from deviating too far from the reference policy.

In practice, the language model policy $\pi_\theta$ is also initialized to $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$, so both the policy and the reference model start from the same point. The optimization then iteratively updates the policy to increase expected reward while the KL penalty counterbalances this by pulling the policy back toward the reference distribution. Due to the discrete nature of language generation, this objective is not differentiable and is typically optimized with reinforcement learning algorithms such as REINFORCE, proximal policy optimization (PPO), or variants thereof. The standard approach constructs the reward function as $r(x, y) = r_\phi(x, y) - \beta(\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x) - \log \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x))$ and maximizes it using PPO.

This RL phase is where much of the complexity and instability of the RLHF pipeline arises. Unlike supervised learning, which involves straightforward gradient-based optimization, reinforcement learning requires sampling from the policy during training, estimating value functions, and managing the delicate balance between exploration and exploitation. The need for on-policy sampling makes the procedure computationally expensive, and the interaction between the reward model and the policy can introduce instabilities that require careful hyperparameter tuning to manage.

## The Role of the Reference Policy and Preventing Model Degeneration

The reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ plays a central role in the RL fine-tuning phase, serving as an anchor that prevents the optimized policy from drifting into degenerate or undesirable behavior. The KL-divergence constraint $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_\theta(y\mid x) \mid\mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)]$ measures how much the policy $\pi_\theta$ has diverged from the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, and the penalty term $\beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_\theta(y\mid x) \mid\mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)]$ in the optimization objective penalizes large deviations.

There are several critical reasons why this constraint is essential. First, it prevents the model from deviating too far from the distribution on which the reward model is accurate. The reward model $r_\phi$ is trained on preference data generated by $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$, so it is only reliable for evaluating responses that are similar to those produced by $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$. If the policy drifts far from this distribution, the reward model's predictions become unreliable, and the policy may exploit regions where the reward model assigns spuriously high rewards— a phenomenon known as reward hacking or reward over-optimization. The KL constraint keeps the policy within a region where the reward model's predictions can be trusted.

Second, the constraint helps maintain generation diversity and prevents mode-collapse to single high-reward answers. Without the KL penalty, the policy might converge to producing a narrow set of responses that maximize the learned reward, potentially at the expense of the diversity and naturalness that make language model outputs useful. By keeping the policy close to the reference, the KL constraint encourages the model to explore a broader range of high-quality responses rather than collapsing to a single mode.

The parameter $\beta$ controls the strength of this constraint, creating a fundamental trade-off between reward maximization and policy conservation. A large $\beta$ keeps the policy very close to the reference, potentially limiting the improvement in reward that can be achieved. A small $\beta$ allows the policy to deviate more freely, potentially achieving higher reward but at the risk of degeneration and reward hacking. Finding the right value of $\beta$ is a key practical challenge in RLHF, and different values may be appropriate for different tasks and datasets.

The optimal solution to the KL-constrained reward maximization objective can in fact be expressed in closed form. As shown in prior work, the optimal policy takes the form:

$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$

where $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ is the partition function. This expression shows that the optimal policy is a reweighted version of the reference policy, where each response is upweighted or downweighted exponentially according to its reward. While this closed-form solution is theoretically elegant, it is impractical to use directly because the partition function $Z(x)$ requires summing over all possible responses $y$, which is intractable for language generation. This intractability is one of the key reasons why RL algorithms like PPO are used in practice, and it is precisely this challenge that DPO addresses through its clever reparameterization, which eliminates the need to compute the partition function explicitly.

In summary, the standard RLHF pipeline is a multi-stage process that transforms a pre-trained language model into one that aligns with human preferences. It begins with supervised fine-tuning to adapt the model to the target domain, proceeds to learn a reward model from human preference data using the Bradley-Terry model, and concludes with RL fine-tuning under a KL-divergence constraint to balance reward maximization with policy stability. While this pipeline has produced impressive results, it is also complex, computationally expensive, and prone to instability—limitations that motivate the development of simpler, more direct approaches such as Direct Preference Optimization.
