\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 1: Introduction to Language Model Alignment

## Capabilities and Limitations of Large Unsupervised Language Models

Large unsupervised language models, trained on vast corpora of text data, have demonstrated remarkable and sometimes surprising capabilities. These models acquire broad world knowledge and develop some reasoning skills as a natural consequence of their training on very large datasets. Notable examples include models such as PaLM, GPT-3, LLaMA, and others, which have shown impressive performance across a wide range of tasks. However, the very nature of their training—being completely unsupervised—introduces a fundamental limitation: achieving precise control over their behavior is extremely difficult.

The core issue stems from the fact that these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. The training data reflects the full spectrum of human communication, including both desirable and undesirable behaviors. The model learns to imitate all of it indiscriminately. For instance, a language model trained on internet text will encounter both high-quality and low-quality writing, accurate information and misinformation, helpful and harmful content. Without any mechanism to distinguish between these, the model internalizes the full distribution of behaviors present in its training data, making it challenging to control which behaviors the model ultimately exhibits when deployed.

This limitation becomes particularly apparent when we consider the gap between what a model *knows* and what a model *does*. A language model may have learned sophisticated knowledge about a domain during pre-training, but without additional alignment procedures, there is no guarantee that the model will express that knowledge in a desirable way. The model's outputs are drawn from the broad distribution of human-like text it was trained on, rather than from the specific subset of behaviors that we might want it to exhibit. This gap between capability and controllability is a central challenge that motivates the work on preference learning and alignment methods discussed in this chapter.

## The Need for Steerability and Selecting Desired Behaviors

The distinction between a model's knowledge and its behavior is crucial for building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable. The paper illustrates this point with two compelling examples. First, consider an AI coding assistant: we may want the model to *understand* common programming mistakes so that it can identify and correct them, but when generating code, we want to bias the model toward the high-quality coding abilities present in its training data, which may be relatively rare compared to the abundance of mediocre or buggy code. The model needs to know about mistakes without reproducing them.

The second example is even more striking: a language model might be *aware* of a common misconception believed by 50% of people, having encountered it many times in its training data. However, we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception is true in 50% of queries about it. The model should be knowledgeable about what people believe, but its responses should reflect what is actually true, not the frequency with which different claims appear in the training data. Selecting the model's desired responses and behaviors from its very wide knowledge and abilities is therefore essential.

This need for steerability—that is, the ability to guide the model toward producing outputs that align with human preferences and values—has become a central concern in AI research. The model's raw capabilities, however impressive, are not sufficient on their own. What matters in practice is whether the model can be directed to use those capabilities in ways that are helpful, harmless, and aligned with user intent. This realization has driven the development of preference learning methods, which seek to instill desired behaviors into language models after their initial pre-training phase, effectively shaping the model's outputs to match human expectations.

## Evolution of Preference Learning: From Instruction Tuning to RLHF

The journey toward controlling language model behavior has evolved through several stages, each building upon the last. Self-supervised language models of increasing scale have been shown to complete some tasks zero-shot or with few-shot prompts, demonstrating that pre-training alone endows models with some degree of task generalization. However, their performance on downstream tasks and alignment with user intent can be significantly improved through additional fine-tuning.

The first major refinement came in the form of instruction tuning, where models are fine-tuned on datasets of instructions paired with human-written completions. This procedure enables large language models to generalize to instructions outside of the instruction-tuning set and generally increases their usability. By training on examples of following instructions, the model learns the *format* and *style* of helpful responses, even when encountering novel queries. Instruction tuning represents an important step toward steerability, but it has limitations: it requires expert demonstrations, and it teaches the model what good responses look like without explicitly teaching it to distinguish between better and worse responses.

A key insight that drove further evolution is that *relative* human judgments of response quality are often easier to collect than expert demonstrations. It is typically easier for a human to say which of two responses they prefer than to write a high-quality response from scratch. This realization led to the development of methods that fine-tune language models using datasets of human preferences, where human labelers compare pairs of model-generated responses and indicate which one they prefer. These methods have improved model proficiency in a variety of tasks, including translation, summarization, story-telling, and instruction-following.

The most successful class of methods that emerged from this line of work is reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) or reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF). These methods represent a convergence of two bodies of work: one on training language models with reinforcement learning for various objectives, and another on general methods for learning from human preferences. The RLHF approach involves fitting a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then using reinforcement learning to optimize a language model policy to produce responses that are assigned high reward by the learned reward model, while simultaneously preventing the model from drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF has produced models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the approach is considerably more complex than supervised learning, setting the stage for the innovations that DPO introduces.

## The Complexity and Instability of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)

To understand the motivation behind Direct Preference Optimization, it is essential to appreciate the complexity of the standard RLHF pipeline. RLHF typically proceeds in three phases. First, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is performed by fine-tuning a pre-trained language model on high-quality data for the downstream task of interest—such as dialogue, summarization, or code generation—to obtain a model denoted $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$. This phase adapts the general-purpose model to the specific domain.

In the second phase, preference sampling and reward learning, the SFT model is prompted with inputs $x$ to produce pairs of answers $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pi^{\text{SFT}}(y \mid x)$. These answer pairs are then presented to human labelers who express preferences, denoted $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ represent the preferred and dispreferred completions, respectively. The preferences are assumed to be generated by some latent reward model $r^*(y, x)$, which is not directly accessible. To model these preferences, the Bradley-Terry model is a popular choice, stipulating that the human preference distribution can be written as:

$$p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}$$

Given a static dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D} = \left\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\right\}_{i=1}^N$, a reward model $r_\phi(x, y)$ is parametrized and estimated via maximum likelihood, yielding the negative log-likelihood loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_R(r_\phi, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma\left(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l)\right)\right]$$

where $\sigma$ is the logistic function. In practice, the reward model is often initialized from the SFT model with an added linear layer producing a scalar reward prediction.

The third phase, RL fine-tuning, uses the learned reward function to provide feedback to the language model. The optimization is formulated as:

$$\max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(y \mid x)}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left[\pi_\theta(y \mid x) \| \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\right]$$

where $\beta$ controls the deviation from the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (typically the SFT model), and the KL-divergence constraint prevents the model from deviating too far from the distribution on which the reward model is accurate, maintaining generation diversity and preventing mode-collapse. Due to the discrete nature of language generation, this objective is not differentiable and is typically optimized using reinforcement learning algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

This pipeline is complex and often unstable for several reasons. It involves training multiple language models (the SFT model, the reward model, and the policy model), sampling from the language model policy during the training loop (which incurs significant computational costs), and performing significant hyperparameter tuning. The actor-critic algorithms used in the RL phase, such as PPO, can suffer from high variance in policy gradients. The normalization term in the objective, which can be interpreted as the soft value function of the reference policy, does not affect the optimal solution but without it the policy gradient can have high variance, making learning unstable. While a learned value function can accommodate this normalization term, it adds further complexity. Prior works have attempted to address this by normalizing rewards using a human completion baseline—essentially a single-sample Monte-Carlo estimate of the normalizing term—but this is a crude approximation. These challenges collectively make RLHF a major practical bottleneck in training aligned language models.

## Direct Preference Optimization (DPO): A High-Level Overview

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is introduced as an algorithm that addresses the shortcomings of RLHF by directly optimizing a language model to adhere to human preferences, without the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. The key insight behind DPO is that one can leverage an analytical mapping from reward functions to optimal policies, which enables the transformation of a loss function over reward functions into a loss function over policies. This change-of-variables approach avoids fitting an explicit, standalone reward model while still optimizing under existing models of human preferences, such as the Bradley-Terry model.

The derivation begins with the same RL objective used in RLHF. The optimal solution to the KL-constrained reward maximization problem takes the form:

$$\pi_r(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)$$

where $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)$ is the partition function. While estimating this partition function is expensive, DPO cleverly rearranges this equation to express the reward in terms of the optimal policy:

$$r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

The crucial observation is that the Bradley-Terry preference model depends only on the *difference* of rewards between two completions. When this reparameterization is substituted into the preference model, the partition function $Z(x)$—which depends only on the prompt $x$—cancels out in the difference. This means the human preference probability can be expressed entirely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, without any reference to an explicit reward function or partition function.

With the preference probability now expressed in terms of the policy, DPO formulates a maximum likelihood objective for a parametrized policy $\pi_\theta$, yielding the DPO loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta; \pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}\right)\right]$$

This is a simple binary cross-entropy objective. Intuitively, the DPO update increases the relative log probability of preferred responses over dispreferred responses. Importantly, it incorporates a dynamic, per-example importance weight—specifically, the term $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))$—that prevents the model degeneration that occurs with a naive probability ratio objective. This weighting accounts for how incorrectly the implicit reward model orders the completions, scaled by the KL constraint strength $\beta$.

In essence, the policy network in DPO represents both the language model and the implicit reward model. The DPO pipeline is straightforward: given a dataset of human preferences $\mathcal{D} = \left\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\right\}_{i=1}^N$ and a reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (typically initialized as $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ when available), one simply optimizes the language model $\pi_\theta$ to minimize $\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}$ for a chosen $\beta$. When the SFT model is not available, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is initialized by maximizing the likelihood of preferred completions, mitigating distribution shift between the true reference distribution and the one used by DPO. The algorithm eliminates the need for sampling from the language model during fine-tuning, fitting a separate reward model, or performing extensive hyperparameter tuning, making it stable, performant, and computationally lightweight.

## Key Contributions of the Paper

The primary contribution of the paper is the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) algorithm itself—a simple, RL-free algorithm for training language models from preferences. DPO implicitly optimizes the same objective as existing RLHF algorithms (reward maximization with a KL-divergence constraint) but does so through a simple binary cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning, explicit reward modeling, or sampling from the policy during training.

The paper provides a rigorous theoretical foundation for DPO. It proves that under mild assumptions, all reward classes consistent with the Plackett-Luce (and Bradley-Terry in particular) preference models can be represented with the reparameterization $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}$. This means the DPO reparameterization does not constrain the class of learnable reward models—no generality is lost. The paper also defines an equivalence relation on reward functions, showing that two reward functions are equivalent if they differ by a function of the prompt alone, and demonstrating that rewards from the same equivalence class induce the same preference distribution and the same optimal policy. This theoretical analysis establishes that DPO is not merely a heuristic approximation but a principled method that recovers the exact optimal policy.

On the experimental side, the paper demonstrates DPO's effectiveness across three tasks: controlled sentiment generation, summarization, and single-turn dialogue. In the controlled sentiment setting, DPO achieves the most efficient reward-KL frontier, strictly dominating PPO and even outperforming PPO with access to ground-truth rewards (PPO-GT). In summarization on the Reddit TL;DR dataset, DPO achieves a win rate of approximately 61% against reference summaries at temperature 0.0, exceeding PPO's approximately 57% and also surpassing the Best of $N$ baseline. In single-turn dialogue on the Anthropic HH dataset, DPO is the only computationally efficient method that improves over the preferred completions, performing similarly to or better than the computationally demanding Best of 128 baseline. DPO also demonstrates robustness to distribution shift, continuing to outperform PPO when evaluated on the CNN/DailyMail dataset after being trained on Reddit TL;DR.

These results collectively show that DPO can fine-tune language models to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing methods, while being substantially simpler to implement and train. The paper uses language models with up to 6 billion parameters, and the authors note that exploring scaling DPO to state-of-the-art models orders of magnitude larger is an exciting direction for future work. By reducing the barrier to training language models from human preferences, DPO represents a meaningful advance in making preference learning more accessible and practical.
