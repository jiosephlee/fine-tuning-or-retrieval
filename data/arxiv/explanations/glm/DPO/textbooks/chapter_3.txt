\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 3: Deriving the Direct Preference Optimization Algorithm

## Motivation: Bypassing RL and Explicit Reward Modeling

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has become the dominant paradigm for aligning large language models with human preferences. The standard RLHF pipeline involves three stages: supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reward modeling, and reinforcement learning optimization. While this pipeline has produced impressive results, it introduces significant complexity. The reward modeling phase requires training a separate neural network to approximate human preferences, and the RL phase requires optimizing the language model policy to maximize the learned reward while staying close to a reference policy, typically using algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). This two-stage approach is computationally expensive, sensitive to hyperparameters, and prone to instability.

The key motivation behind Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is to simplify this pipeline dramatically. Rather than first fitting a reward model and then using RL to optimize it, DPO leverages a particular mathematical insight: under certain conditions, the optimal policy for the KL-constrained reward maximization objective can be expressed in closed form in terms of the reward function. This closed-form relationship allows us to reparameterize the reward function in terms of the policy itself. In other words, instead of learning a reward model and then deriving a policy from it, we can directly learn the policy that corresponds to some implicit reward function that is consistent with the human preference data.

This approach eliminates the need for reinforcement learning entirely. There is no need to sample from the language model during training, no need to train a separate reward model, and no need for the complex machinery of actor-critic algorithms. The entire preference learning problem reduces to a simple binary cross-entropy classification loss, making the training procedure stable, computationally lightweight, and straightforward to implement. The intuition is elegant: the language model itself, when compared against a reference model, already encodes all the information needed to serve as a reward model. We simply need to find the right objective function to extract this information.

## Deriving the Optimal Policy for KL-Constrained Reward Maximization

To understand how DPO works, we must first revisit the RL optimization objective used in standard RLHF. The goal is to find a policy $\pi_\theta$ that maximizes the expected reward while remaining close to a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ (typically the SFT model). This is formalized as:

$$\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) \mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr)$$

Here, $r(x, y)$ is the reward function, $\beta$ is a parameter controlling how much the policy is allowed to deviate from the reference, and $\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}$ is the Kullback-Leibler divergence. The KL constraint serves two critical purposes: it prevents the policy from drifting too far from the distribution on which the reward model was trained (where it is likely to be accurate), and it maintains generation diversity by preventing mode-collapse to a single high-reward answer.

The optimal solution to this constrained optimization problem can be derived using techniques from the control as inference literature. Following prior work, one can show that the optimal policy $\pi_r$ takes the form:

$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$

where $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ is the partition function that ensures $\pi_r$ is a valid probability distribution. This result has an intuitive interpretation: the optimal policy is a reweighted version of the reference policy, where each response $y$ is upweighted or downweighted exponentially based on its reward. High-reward responses get boosted, while low-reward responses get suppressed. The partition function $Z(x)$ normalizes these weights so that the result is a proper probability distribution.

While this closed-form solution is mathematically elegant, it has a practical limitation: computing $Z(x)$ requires summing over all possible responses $y$, which is intractable for language models where the space of possible responses is enormous. This is why, in standard RLHF, one resorts to RL algorithms like PPO to approximately optimize the objective rather than computing the optimal policy directly. However, as we will see, DPO finds a clever way to work around this intractability.

## The Change of Variables: Expressing Reward in Terms of Policy

The key insight of DPO is to recognize that while we cannot easily compute $Z(x)$, we can rearrange the optimal policy equation to express the reward function in terms of the policy. Starting from the optimal policy formula:

$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$

we take the logarithm of both sides and perform some algebraic manipulation to isolate $r(x, y)$:

$$r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

This equation is profoundly important. It tells us that any reward function $r(x, y)$ can be expressed in terms of its corresponding optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, and the partition function $Z(x)$. The reward is essentially the log-ratio of the optimal policy to the reference policy, scaled by $\beta$, plus a term that depends only on the prompt $x$.

This reparameterization is the foundation of DPO. Instead of parameterizing a reward model $r_\phi(x, y)$ directly (as in standard RLHF), we can parameterize the reward implicitly through the policy $\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$. The policy network represents both the language model and the implicit reward model simultaneously. This is the sense in which "your language model is secretly a reward model": the log-ratio of the current policy to the reference policy, scaled by $\beta$, defines a valid reward function.

A natural concern is whether this reparameterization restricts the class of reward functions we can represent. The paper proves (Theorem 1) that under mild assumptions, all reward function classes consistent with the Plackett-Luce and Bradley-Terry preference models can be represented in this form. The key observation is that reward functions are only identifiable up to an equivalence class: two reward functions $r(x,y)$ and $r'(x,y)$ are equivalent if $r(x,y) - r'(x,y) = f(x)$ for some function $f$ of the prompt alone. Since preference models like Bradley-Terry depend only on the difference of rewards between two completions, any function of $x$ alone cancels out and does not affect the preference distribution. The DPO reparameterization selects a particular representative from each equivalence class—the one where the partition function equals 1, ensuring $\pi(y \mid x)$ is a valid normalized distribution—but this does not lose any generality in terms of the preference distributions that can be modeled.

## Substituting the Implicit Reward into the Bradley-Terry Model

Now that we can express the reward in terms of the policy, the next step is to substitute this reparameterization into the Bradley-Terry preference model. The Bradley-Terry model specifies that the probability of preferring response $y_1$ over $y_2$ given prompt $x$ is:

$$p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)} = \sigma\left(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2)\right)$$

where $\sigma$ is the logistic (sigmoid) function. Substituting our reparameterized reward $r^*(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$ into this expression, we notice something remarkable: the partition function $Z(x)$ appears identically in both $r^*(x, y_1)$ and $r^*(x, y_2)$, so when we take the difference $r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2)$, the $\beta \log Z(x)$ terms cancel out completely. This is the crucial step that makes the entire approach tractable.

After substitution and cancellation of the partition function, the preference probability becomes:

$$p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}$$

This can be written more compactly as:

$$p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)}\right)$$

This result is the heart of DPO. The probability of a human preference is now expressed entirely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, with no dependence on any explicit reward function or partition function. The intractable partition function, which was the main obstacle to using the closed-form optimal policy, has been completely eliminated through the algebraic structure of the Bradley-Terry model. We now have a direct mapping from policies to preference probabilities, which allows us to learn the policy directly from preference data without ever constructing an explicit reward model.

## Formulating the DPO Maximum Likelihood Objective

With the preference probability expressed in terms of the policy, we can now formulate a maximum likelihood objective to learn the policy parameters $\theta$ directly from preference data. Given a dataset of human preferences $\mathcal{D} = \{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$, where $y_w$ denotes the preferred (winning) completion and $y_l$ denotes the dispreferred (losing) completion, we want to find the policy $\pi_\theta$ that maximizes the likelihood of the observed preferences.

Analogous to the reward modeling objective in standard RLHF (which maximizes the likelihood of preferences under a parameterized reward model), the DPO objective maximizes the likelihood of preferences under the reparameterized model. The negative log-likelihood loss is:

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]$$

This objective has an intuitive interpretation. The term inside the sigmoid function, $\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}$, represents the difference in implicit rewards between the preferred and dispreferred completions. The implicit reward for a completion $y$ is $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$, which measures how much more likely the current policy assigns probability to $y$ relative to the reference policy, scaled by $\beta$. The loss encourages this implicit reward difference to be positive (i.e., the preferred completion receives a higher implicit reward than the dispreferred one), which is exactly what we want.

Minimizing this loss is equivalent to fitting a Bradley-Terry model with the reparameterized reward. Because this procedure is equivalent to fitting a reparametrized Bradley-Terry model, it enjoys certain theoretical properties, such as consistency under suitable assumptions about the preference data distribution. The optimization is a standard supervised learning problem with a binary cross-entropy loss—no reinforcement learning, no sampling from the policy during training, and no separate reward model to train. The entire complexity of RLHF has been distilled into a single, well-understood classification objective.

## Analyzing the DPO Gradient: Dynamic Importance Weighting

To gain deeper mechanistic understanding of how DPO updates the model, it is illuminating to analyze the gradient of the DPO loss with respect to the parameters $\theta$. The gradient can be written as:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\pi_\text{ref}) = -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\sigma\bigl(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w)\bigr)\bigg[\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)\bigg]\bigg]$$

where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ is the implicit reward. This gradient reveals several important properties of the DPO update.

First, the gradient increases the log-probability of the preferred completion $y_w$ (through the $\nabla_\theta \log \pi(y_w \mid x)$ term) and decreases the log-probability of the dispreferred completion $y_l$ (through the $-\nabla_\theta \log \pi(y_l \mid x)$ term). This is the expected behavior: we want the model to assign higher probability to preferred responses and lower probability to dispreferred ones.

Second, and critically, each example is weighted by $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))$, which is the probability (under the current implicit reward model) that the dispreferred completion would actually be preferred. This weight is high when the implicit reward model incorrectly ranks the dispreferred completion above the preferred one—that is, when the model is currently "wrong" about this example. Conversely, the weight is near zero when the model already correctly assigns a higher implicit reward to the preferred completion. This creates a dynamic, per-example importance weighting mechanism: the gradient focuses on examples where the model is currently making mistakes, while largely ignoring examples it already handles correctly.

This weighting is not merely a mathematical artifact; it plays a crucial role in preventing model degeneration. The paper notes that a naive version of this method without the weighting coefficient can cause the language model to degenerate. Without the weighting, every example contributes equally to the gradient regardless of whether the model already handles it correctly, which can lead to excessive optimization on already-correct examples and destabilize training. The dynamic weighting ensures that the optimization effort is allocated efficiently, focusing on the examples that provide the most learning signal. This is analogous to how well-designed loss functions in classification focus gradient updates on misclassified examples rather than wasting capacity on already-correct ones, but here the mechanism emerges naturally from the mathematical structure of the reparameterized Bradley-Terry model rather than being imposed artificially.

## The DPO Pipeline: From Data Collection to Model Optimization

The DPO pipeline is remarkably simple compared to standard RLHF, consisting of two main steps. The first step is data collection. For every prompt $x$, completions $y_1$ and $y_2$ are sampled from the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}(\cdot \mid x)$. These completions are then presented to human labelers who express a preference for one over the other, denoted as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the preferred completion and $y_l$ is the dispreferred one. This process constructs an offline dataset of preferences $\mathcal{D} = \{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$.

The second step is model optimization. The language model $\pi_\theta$ is optimized to minimize the DPO loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given the reference model $\pi_\text{ref}$, the preference dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and a chosen value of $\beta$. The $\beta$ parameter controls the strength of the KL constraint and thus how much the policy is allowed to deviate from the reference policy. Higher values of $\beta$ keep the policy closer to the reference, while lower values allow more deviation.

In practice, one often wants to reuse publicly available preference datasets rather than generating new samples and collecting fresh human preferences. Since existing preference datasets are typically sampled using an SFT model $\pi^\text{SFT}$, the reference model $\pi_\text{ref}$ should be initialized to $\pi^\text{SFT}$ whenever available. This ensures that the reference distribution used in the DPO loss matches the distribution from which the preference data was generated.

However, when the SFT model is not available, the paper describes a practical workaround: initialize $\pi_\text{ref}$ by maximizing the likelihood of the preferred completions, i.e., $\pi_\text{ref} = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]$. This is essentially supervised fine-tuning on the preferred completions. This procedure helps mitigate the distribution shift between the true reference distribution (which is unavailable) and the $\pi_\text{ref}$ used by DPO. If the reference model were significantly different from the distribution that generated the preference data, the log-ratios in the DPO loss would be miscalibrated, potentially leading to suboptimal training. By initializing the reference model this way, we ensure that the implicit reward estimates are reasonable starting points for optimization.

The entire pipeline requires no reinforcement learning, no sampling from the model during training, no reward model fitting, and minimal hyperparameter tuning. The only hyperparameter of note is $\beta$, and the paper reports strong results with virtually no tuning of this parameter. This simplicity is a significant practical advantage, as it reduces the engineering burden and computational cost of aligning language models with human preferences, making the approach accessible to a wider range of practitioners and researchers.
