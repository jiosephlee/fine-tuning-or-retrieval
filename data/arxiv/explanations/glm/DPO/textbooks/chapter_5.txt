\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 5: Experiments and Empirical Evaluation

# Experimental Tasks: Sentiment Generation, Summarization, and Dialogue

The experimental evaluation of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) spans three distinct open-ended text generation tasks, each chosen to probe different aspects of language model steerability and alignment with human preferences. These tasks differ in the nature of the desired behavior, the scale of the models involved, and the source of preference data, providing a comprehensive testbed for comparing DPO against established methods.

The first task, controlled sentiment generation, serves as a well-controlled setting where the ground-truth reward function is known and computable. In this task, the prompt $x$ is a prefix of a movie review drawn from the IMDb dataset, and the policy must generate a completion $y$ with positive sentiment. To enable a precisely controlled evaluation, the authors generate preference pairs over model generations using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, labeling completions such that $p(\text{positive} \mid x, y_w) > p(\text{positive} \mid x, y_l)$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ denote the preferred and dispreferred completions, respectively. The supervised fine-tuning (SFT) model for this task is obtained by fine-tuning GPT-2-large until convergence on reviews from the training split of the IMDb dataset. This task is particularly valuable because the availability of a ground-truth reward function allows the authors to precisely measure the tradeoff between reward maximization and KL-divergence from the reference policy, a quantity that is inaccessible in real-world settings where the true reward function is unknown.

The second task is summarization, where the prompt $x$ is a forum post from Reddit and the policy must generate a summary $y$ capturing the main points of the post. The authors use the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset, along with human preferences gathered by Stiennon et al. The SFT model is fine-tuned on human-written forum post summaries using the TRLX framework. It is important to note that the human preference dataset was gathered on samples from a different, but similarly trained, SFT model, introducing a distribution shift between the data collection process and the model used as the starting point for DPO. This detail reflects the practical reality of preference learning, where preference data may be collected under conditions that do not perfectly match the deployment setting.

The third task is single-turn dialogue, where the prompt $x$ is a human query—which may range from a question about astrophysics to a request for relationship advice—and the policy must produce an engaging and helpful response $y$. The authors use the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless (HH) dialogue dataset, which contains 170k dialogues between a human and an automated assistant. Each transcript concludes with a pair of responses generated by a large language model along with a preference label indicating the human-preferred response. A notable feature of this task is that no pre-trained SFT model is available. To address this, the authors fine-tune an off-the-shelf language model on only the preferred completions to form the SFT model, which then serves as the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ for DPO. Across all three tasks, the algorithms learn a policy from a dataset of preferences $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$.

# Evaluation Methodologies: Reward-KL Frontiers and GPT-4 Win Rates

The evaluation of DPO and competing methods employs two complementary methodologies, each suited to different aspects of performance assessment. The choice of methodology is guided by whether the ground-truth reward function is accessible and by the practical constraints of evaluating open-ended text generation.

In the controlled sentiment generation setting, the authors evaluate each algorithm by its frontier of achieved reward and KL-divergence from the reference policy. This reward-KL frontier is computable because the ground-truth reward function—a pre-trained sentiment classifier—is available. The frontier captures the fundamental tradeoff in the KL-constrained reward maximization objective used in RLHF: a policy that achieves higher reward by deviating further from the reference policy incurs a higher KL penalty, and the frontier characterizes how efficiently each algorithm navigates this tradeoff. By plotting reward against KL-divergence, one can assess whether an algorithm achieves a given level of reward with less deviation from the reference policy, or conversely, achieves higher reward at a fixed level of KL. This is a more informative evaluation than simply reporting reward alone, as achieving slightly higher reward with substantially higher KL-divergence is not necessarily desirable.

In the summarization and single-turn dialogue settings, the ground-truth reward function is not known, precluding the use of reward-KL frontiers. Instead, the authors evaluate algorithms by their win rate against a baseline policy, using GPT-4 as a proxy for human evaluation of summary quality and response helpfulness. For summarization, the baseline consists of reference summaries from the test set of the TL;DR dataset. For dialogue, the baseline is the preferred response in the test dataset. The win rate quantifies the fraction of cases in which GPT-4 judges the model's output as superior to the baseline. To account for the effect of sampling temperature, the authors sample completions at temperatures varying from 0.0 to 1.0 and report win rates across this range. While existing studies suggest that language models can serve as better automated evaluators than existing metrics such as ROUGE, the authors also conduct a human study to validate the use of GPT-4 for evaluation, ensuring that their automated assessments are grounded in human judgment.

# Baseline Methods: SFT, Preferred-FT, Unlikelihood, PPO, and Best of N

To provide a thorough comparison, the authors evaluate DPO alongside several existing approaches to training language models from human preferences. These baselines span a range of methodological complexity, from simple supervised learning to reinforcement learning and rejection sampling, allowing the authors to isolate the contributions of different components in the preference learning pipeline.

The simplest baselines involve zero-shot or few-shot prompting without any fine-tuning. In the summarization task, the authors evaluate zero-shot prompting with GPT-J. In the dialogue task, they evaluate 2-shot prompting with Pythia-2.8B. These baselines establish the performance floor of pre-trained models without task-specific adaptation.

The supervised fine-tuning (SFT) model itself serves as a baseline, representing the performance achievable through standard supervised learning on high-quality demonstrations. Relatedly, the authors evaluate Preferred-FT, which is a model fine-tuned with supervised learning on only the chosen (preferred) completion $y_w$ from the preference dataset. In controlled sentiment and summarization, Preferred-FT starts from the SFT model, while in single-turn dialogue, it starts from a generic language model. Preferred-FT represents a straightforward approach to leveraging preference data: simply training on the preferred responses while ignoring the dispreferred ones.

The Unlikelihood method provides a more sophisticated pseudo-supervised approach. It optimizes the policy to maximize the probability assigned to the preferred completion $y_w$ while simultaneously minimizing the probability assigned to the dispreferred completion $y_l$. An optional coefficient $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ controls the weight on the unlikelihood term (the penalty for the dispreferred response). This method goes beyond Preferred-FT by explicitly penalizing undesirable responses, but it lacks the dynamic importance weighting that DPO incorporates.

The most prominent baseline is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), the standard reinforcement learning algorithm used in RLHF. PPO uses a reward function learned from the preference data and optimizes the language model policy to maximize this reward while constraining the KL-divergence from the reference policy. In the controlled sentiment setting, the authors also evaluate PPO-GT, an oracle variant that has access to the ground-truth reward function rather than a learned one. This allows them to disentangle the contribution of reward model quality from the optimization algorithm itself. For the PPO-GT experiments, the authors use both an off-the-shelf implementation and a modified version that normalizes rewards and tunes hyperparameters for improved performance. These modifications are also applied when running standard PPO with learned rewards.

Finally, the authors consider the Best of $N$ baseline, which samples $N$ responses from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and returns the highest-scoring response according to a reward function learned from the preference dataset. This method is high-performing because it decouples the quality of the reward model from the policy optimization process—instead of fine-tuning the model, it simply selects the best output from multiple candidates at inference time. However, it is computationally impractical even for moderate $N$, as it requires sampling $N$ completions for every query at test time. Despite this practical limitation, Best of $N$ serves as an important upper bound on what can be achieved with a given reward model.

# Evaluating DPO's Efficiency in Controlled Sentiment Generation

The controlled sentiment generation task provides the most direct assessment of how efficiently DPO optimizes the KL-constrained reward maximization objective that underlies RLHF. The objective balances exploitation of reward against the constraint that the policy should not deviate too far from the reference policy. When comparing algorithms, it is essential to account for both the reward achieved and the KL-divergence incurred, as achieving slightly higher reward at the cost of much higher KL-divergence is not necessarily a favorable outcome.

To construct the reward-KL frontier, the authors execute multiple training runs for each algorithm, varying the hyperparameter that controls policy conservativeness. For PPO, they vary the target KL across $\{3, 6, 9, 12\}$. For DPO, they vary $\beta$ across $\{0.05, 0.1, 1, 5\}$. For Unlikelihood, they vary $\alpha$ across $\{0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1\}$. For Preferred-FT, they use different random seeds. This sweep encompasses 22 runs in total, providing a comprehensive picture of each algorithm's performance across a range of operating points. After each 100 training steps until convergence, the authors evaluate each policy on a set of test prompts, computing the average reward under the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL-divergence—that is, the sum of per-timestep KL-divergences—with the reference policy $\text{KL}(\pi \| \pi_{\text{ref}})$.

The results demonstrate that DPO produces by far the most efficient frontier, achieving the highest reward while simultaneously maintaining low KL-divergence. This finding is remarkable for several reasons. First, DPO and PPO optimize the same underlying objective, yet DPO achieves a notably more efficient tradeoff, with DPO's reward-KL frontier strictly dominating that of PPO. This means that for any given level of KL-divergence, DPO achieves equal or higher reward, and for any given level of reward, DPO incurs equal or lower KL-divergence. Second, DPO achieves a better frontier than PPO even when PPO has access to the ground-truth reward function (PPO-GT), which is a significant advantage since PPO-GT eliminates the error introduced by learning a reward model from preference data. This result suggests that the performance advantage of DPO is not merely a consequence of avoiding reward model estimation error, but rather stems from the directness and stability of its optimization approach.

# Scaling DPO to Real-World Datasets (Reddit TL;DR and Anthropic HH)

Having demonstrated DPO's efficiency in the controlled sentiment setting, the authors proceed to evaluate its performance on larger models and more complex, real-world tasks: summarization on the Reddit TL;DR dataset and single-turn dialogue on the Anthropic HH dataset. These tasks present challenges including larger model sizes, more nuanced preference criteria, and the absence of a ground-truth reward function.

For the summarization task, the authors evaluate different methods by sampling completions on the test split of the TL;DR dataset and computing the average GPT-4 win rate against reference completions in the test set. Completions for all methods are sampled at temperatures varying from 0.0 to 1.0. The authors find that DPO achieves a win rate of approximately 61% at a temperature of 0.0, exceeding the performance of PPO, which achieves approximately 57% at its optimal sampling temperature of 0.0. DPO also achieves a higher maximum win rate compared to the Best of $N$ baseline, which is notable given that Best of $N$ decouples reward model quality from policy optimization and serves as a strong upper bound. Importantly, the authors note that they did not meaningfully tune DPO's $\beta$ hyperparameter, suggesting that these results may underestimate DPO's potential.

A key practical finding is that DPO is much more robust to the sampling temperature than PPO. While PPO's performance can degrade to the level of the base GPT-J model at high temperatures, DPO maintains stable performance across the temperature range. This robustness is a significant advantage in deployment, where the optimal sampling temperature may not be known in advance. Preferred-FT, by contrast, does not improve significantly over the SFT model, demonstrating that simply training on preferred completions is insufficient to capture the information encoded in the preference comparisons.

In addition to automated evaluation, the authors compare DPO and PPO head-to-head in human evaluations. DPO samples at temperature 0.25 are preferred over PPO samples at temperature 0 in 58% of cases, providing direct human validation of DPO's superiority.

For the single-turn dialogue task on the Anthropic HH dataset, the authors evaluate methods on a subset of the test split containing one step of human-assistant interaction. GPT-4 evaluations use the preferred completions from the test set as the reference for computing win rates. Since no standard SFT model exists for this task, the authors start with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model and use Preferred-FT to train a reference model on the chosen completions, ensuring that completions are within the distribution of the model before applying DPO. The authors compare DPO against several baselines: the Best of 128 Preferred-FT completions (the authors found that the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at 128 completions for this task), a 2-shot prompted version of the Pythia-2.8B base model, and an RLHF model trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset from a well-known source. Notably, the authors are unable to find a prompt or sampling temperature for the PPO-trained model that yields performance better than the base Pythia-2.8B model, highlighting the difficulty and instability of PPO-based RLHF in this setting.

DPO performs as well as or better than all of these baselines at their best-performing temperatures. Based on the TL;DR results and the fact that both methods optimize the same reward function, the authors consider Best of 128 a rough proxy for PPO-level performance. DPO is the only computationally efficient method that improves over the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset, while also matching or exceeding the performance of the computationally demanding Best of 128 baseline. Furthermore, the authors observe that DPO converges to its best performance relatively quickly, an important practical consideration for training large language models.

# Out-of-Distribution Generalization to New Datasets (CNN/DailyMail)

A critical question for any preference learning method is how well the resulting policy generalizes to input distributions not seen during training. To investigate this, the authors evaluate the PPO and DPO policies trained on the Reddit TL;DR summarization task on a different distribution: news articles from the test split of the CNN/DailyMail dataset. This experiment tests whether the policies learned by DPO and PPO transfer to a new domain, or whether they are overly specialized to the Reddit forum post distribution on which they were trained.

The authors use the best sampling temperatures identified from the TL;DR experiments (temperatures 0 and 0.25) and compute GPT-4 win rates against the ground-truth summaries in the CNN/DailyMail dataset. To adapt the evaluation prompt, they use the same GPT-4 (C) prompt employed for Reddit TL;DR but replace the words "forum post" with "news article." The results show that DPO continues to outperform the PPO policy by a significant margin on this out-of-distribution dataset. This finding provides initial evidence that DPO policies can generalize similarly well to, or better than, PPO policies, even though DPO does not use the additional unlabeled Reddit TL;DR prompts that PPO uses during its RL fine-tuning phase. In standard RLHF, PPO samples from the language model policy during training on prompts from the training distribution, which provides additional exposure to in-distribution data. DPO, by contrast, operates entirely on the fixed preference dataset without any on-policy sampling. Despite this difference, DPO's out-of-distribution performance remains strong, suggesting that the direct preference optimization approach does not lead to overfitting to the training distribution.

# Validating GPT-4 as an Automated Evaluator via Human Judgments

Given the central role of GPT-4 win rates in evaluating summarization and dialogue performance, the authors conduct a human study to verify the reliability of GPT-4's judgments. This validation is essential because automated evaluation by language models is a relatively new methodology, and the validity of the experimental conclusions rests on the assumption that GPT-4 judgments are a reasonable proxy for human preferences.

The authors use the results of the TL;DR summarization experiment and evaluate with two different GPT-4 prompts. The GPT-4 (S) prompt—the "simple" prompt—asks which summary better summarizes the important information in the post. The GPT-4 (C) prompt—the "concise" prompt—additionally asks which summary is more concise. The authors introduce the concise prompt because they observe that GPT-4, with the simple prompt, tends to prefer longer and more repetitive summaries than humans do. The complete prompts are provided in the appendix.

To cover a diversity of sample qualities, the authors perform three comparisons using the highest-performing method (DPO at temperature 0.25), the lowest-performing method (PPO at temperature 1.0), and a middle-performing method (SFT at temperature 0.25). All three methods are compared against greedily-sampled PPO (its best-performing temperature). Due to limited human raters, the authors collect multiple human judgments only for the DPO and PPO-1 comparisons.

The results show that with both prompts, GPT-4 tends to agree with humans about as often as humans agree with each other. This level of agreement suggests that GPT-4 is a reasonable proxy for human evaluation, as its judgments are no more discordant with human preferences than human preferences are with each other—a natural ceiling for inter-annotator reliability. Overall, the GPT-4 (C) prompt generally provides win rates that are more representative of human judgments, and the authors therefore use this prompt for the main results reported in the real-world dataset experiments. This careful validation ensures that the performance comparisons between DPO and competing methods, as measured by GPT-4 win rates, are grounded in reliable automated evaluation that has been calibrated against human judgment.
