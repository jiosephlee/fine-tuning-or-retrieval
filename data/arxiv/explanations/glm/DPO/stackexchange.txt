\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Is DPO just supervised learning on preferred responses?
Question:
The DPO paper states that the method uses "a simple binary cross entropy objective" and eliminates reinforcement learning. Given that binary cross-entropy is a standard supervised learning loss, how is DPO fundamentally different from just doing supervised fine-tuning on the preferred completions $y_w$? The loss in Equation 7 looks like a classification loss, but what exactly is being classified, and why isn't this just supervised learning?
Answer:
# Direct Preference Optimization Is Preference Learning, Not Imitation Learning

The short answer is that DPO is doing supervised learning on *preferences* (relative judgments between two completions), not on preferred completions themselves. This distinction matters substantially and explains both what is being "classified" and why DPO behaves differently from supervised fine-tuning on $y_w$.

## What is actually being classified

The DPO loss in Equation 7 is a binary cross-entropy objective, but the binary variable being predicted is not "is this response good" — it is **"is $y_w$ preferred over $y_l$ given $x$"**. The "classifier" here is parameterized through the implicit reward

$$\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)},$$

and the logit of the binary classifier is the reward difference $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l)$. So DPO trains $\pi_\theta$ such that the Bradley-Terry probability $p(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l))$ matches the empirical preference distribution in the dataset.

In contrast, supervised fine-tuning on preferred completions (the "Preferred-FT" baseline the paper evaluates) would simply minimize $-\log \pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)$. That objective is purely an imitation objective: it makes the model assign higher probability to $y_w$ but never references $y_l$, never references $\pi_\text{ref}$, and has no notion of *relative* preference.

## Why this is not just supervised learning on $y_w$

There are three structural differences, all visible in the loss and its gradient.

**It is contrastive.** The gradient in the paper shows that DPO simultaneously increases $\log \pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)$ *and* decreases $\log \pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)$:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} \propto -\beta\, \mathbb{E}\left[\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w)) \cdot \bigl(\nabla_\theta \log \pi(y_w \mid x) - \nabla_\theta \log \pi(y_l \mid x)\bigr)\right].$$

Preferred-FT only has the first term; it has no signal at all from the dispreferred response. The information contained in "$y_w$ is better than $y_l$" is fundamentally different from the information in "$y_w$ is a good response," and DPO uses the former.

**The logits are log-ratios against a reference policy, not raw log-probabilities.** The implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) - \beta \log \pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)$ bakes in the KL constraint from the RLHF objective in Equation 3. This is what makes DPO equivalent, under the Bradley-Terry model, to *solving* the constrained RL problem in closed form rather than merely imitating good responses. The reference model $\pi_\text{ref}$ (typically the SFT model) acts as an anchor: the "reward" of a response is not its raw probability under $\pi_\theta$, but how much more probable it is *relative to what the SFT model would assign*. This is what prevents the model from drifting arbitrarily far from the SFT policy — exactly the role the KL divergence plays in PPO-based RLHF.

**There is a dynamic, per-example importance weight.** The coefficient $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))$ in the gradient down-weights examples where the implicit reward model already ranks $y_w$ above $y_l$ correctly, and up-weights examples where it is wrong. The paper explicitly notes that removing this weighting — i.e., a naive "increase $y_w$, decrease $y_l$" unlikelihood-style objective — causes the model to degenerate (Appendix Table 6). Preferred-FT has no such mechanism because it has no notion of a current reward estimate.

## Putting it together

A useful way to think about it: DPO *is* supervised learning, but the thing being supervised is a **preference classifier**, and the classifier happens to be parameterized in such a way that, once trained, its *induced optimal policy* under the standard RLHF KL-constrained objective is exactly $\pi_\theta$. The paper's main theorem (Theorem 4.1) establishes that this reparameterization $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ does not restrict the class of representable reward functions, so DPO is not a weaker approximation of RLHF — it is solving the same problem, just with the reward model and the policy tied together through the change of variables.

So the answer to "is DPO just supervised learning on preferred responses?" is: it is supervised learning, but on a fundamentally different target (pairwise preferences under a Bradley-Terry model with a reference-anchored reward parameterization), and that difference is what makes it equivalent to RLHF rather than to imitation. The cross-entropy form is shared with classification, but the underlying probabilistic model, the contrastive structure, the implicit reward parameterization, and the dynamic weighting are what distinguish it from naive supervised fine-tuning.

### Why does the partition function $Z(x)$ cancel out in the DPO derivation?
Question:
In the DPO derivation, Equation 5 expresses the reward as $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$, where $Z(x)$ is the partition function. The paper then says that when substituting this into the Bradley-Terry preference model, "the partition function cancels." I can see that $Z(x)$ depends only on $x$ and not on $y$, and the Bradley-Terry model depends on reward differences $r(x, y_1) - r(x, y_2)$. Is the cancellation simply because $\beta \log Z(x)$ appears in both terms and subtracts to zero? Are there any subtleties I'm missing in this algebraic step?
Answer:
Yes, your understanding is essentially correct: the partition function $Z(x)$ cancels because $\beta \log Z(x)$ depends only on $x$ and not on $y$, so it appears identically in both reward terms and subtracts to zero when the Bradley-Terry model is expressed in terms of a reward difference. Concretely, substituting the reparameterization from Eq. 5 into the Bradley-Terry preference probability $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))$, we get:

$$r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2) = \left[\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1 \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1 \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)\right] - \left[\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2 \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2 \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)\right]$$

$$= \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1 \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1 \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2 \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2 \mid x)}$$

The $\beta \log Z(x)$ terms are identical and cancel directly, yielding Eq. 6 in the paper.

The deeper subtlety, which the paper addresses in Section 4.1, is that this cancellation is not merely a convenient algebraic accident but reflects a fundamental property of the preference model. The paper defines an equivalence relation on reward functions: two rewards $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are equivalent if $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$ for some function $f$ depending only on the prompt. The partition function term $\beta \log Z(x)$ is precisely such a function of $x$ alone. Lemma 1 then establishes that reward functions from the same equivalence class induce the same preference distribution under the Bradley-Terry (and more generally, Plackett-Luce) model. This is a well-known under-specification issue: preference data only identifies rewards up to a prompt-dependent shift, since the BT model depends only on reward differences. The cancellation of $Z(x)$ is the concrete manifestation of this invariance.

This is also why the cancellation is so practically important. The partition function $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)$ requires summing over all possible completions $y$, which is intractable for language generation. Because the BT model only constrains reward differences, this intractable quantity is unidentifiable from preference data and can be safely ignored. Theorem 1 then shows that this does not lose any representational power: every reward equivalence class contains a member of the form $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}$, which is exactly the reparameterization DPO uses. So the algebraic cancellation you identified is the surface-level consequence of a deeper structural property of preference-based learning, and it is what makes DPO tractable without any need to estimate or approximate $Z(x)$.

### What exactly is the 'implicit reward model' in DPO and how can a policy represent a reward?
Question:
The paper defines an implicit reward as $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$ and says "the policy network represents both the language model and the (implicit) reward." In standard RLHF, the reward model is a separate neural network that takes $(x, y)$ and outputs a scalar reward. In DPO, is the reward just defined as the log-ratio between the current policy and a reference policy? If so, how can this simple log-ratio be equivalent to a learned reward model that could have arbitrary complexity? What is the intuition for why this parameterization is sufficient?
Answer:
The key to understanding DPO's implicit reward model is recognizing that in the preference learning framework, reward functions are only identified up to an equivalence class, and the log-ratio parameterization captures every such class without loss of generality.

**Where the log-ratio comes from**

The derivation starts from the standard RLHF objective, which maximizes expected reward subject to a KL-divergence constraint from a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. As shown in Eq. 5 of the paper, the optimal policy for a given reward function $r(x,y)$ takes the form:

$$\pi_r(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\right)$$

where $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\right)$ is a partition function. Rearranging this relationship (Eq. 6) yields:

$$r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

So the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}$ is exactly the first term, with the $\beta \log Z(x)$ term dropped.

**Why dropping $\beta \log Z(x)$ is harmless**

The paper defines an equivalence relation on reward functions: $r$ and $r'$ are equivalent if $r(x,y) - r'(x,y) = f(x)$ for some function $f$ that depends only on the prompt $x$, not the response $y$. Two key lemmas establish that:

1. Under the Bradley-Terry (or Plackett-Luce) preference model, rewards in the same equivalence class induce the same preference distribution. This is because the BT model depends only on the *difference* of rewards, $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r(x,y_1) - r(x,y_2))$, so any function $f(x)$ added to both rewards cancels out.

2. Rewards in the same equivalence class also induce the same optimal policy under the KL-constrained RL objective.

Since $\beta \log Z(x)$ depends only on $x$, the reward $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}$ is in the same equivalence class as the "full" reward $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. Therefore, it produces identical preference predictions and identical optimal policies.

**Why this parameterization is fully general**

Theorem 1 in the paper proves that *every* reward class consistent with the Plackett-Luce/BT framework can be represented in the form $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}$ for some policy $\pi$. The proof constructs an explicit projection: given any reward $r$, one can normalize it by subtracting $\beta \log Z(x)$ (where $Z$ is the partition function of $r$'s optimal policy), and the result has exactly the log-ratio form while remaining in $r$'s equivalence class.

The intuition is that the log-ratio parameterization simply selects a particular representative from each equivalence class — specifically, the one whose partition function equals $1$ (Eq. 11), ensuring $\pi(y \mid x)$ is a properly normalized distribution. Since the equivalence class structure means we never need to distinguish between rewards within a class, selecting one representative per class is sufficient.

**Why a "simple" log-ratio can match a complex learned reward model**

The log-ratio $\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}$ is not simple in the sense that matters. The policy $\pi_\theta$ is a large neural network (e.g., a 6B-parameter transformer), so $\log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ is a highly expressive function of $(x, y)$. The log-ratio inherits all of this complexity. The reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is fixed, so the expressive power of $\hat{r}_\theta$ is essentially that of $\pi_\theta$ itself.

The crucial difference from standard RLHF is not in the *capacity* of the reward representation but in the *training procedure*. Standard RLHF trains a separate reward model $r_\phi$ with a dedicated loss (Eq. 3), then uses RL to find the policy that maximizes it. DPO observes that once you commit to the log-ratio parameterization, you can substitute the reward directly into the Bradley-Terry likelihood and optimize the policy parameters $\theta$ with a simple cross-entropy loss (Eq. 8), skipping both the explicit reward modeling and the RL loop entirely. The policy and the implicit reward are two views of the same object, linked by the closed-form solution to the KL-constrained optimization problem.

### What are the 'mild assumptions' in Theorem 1 and does it guarantee DPO can represent any reward?
Question:
Theorem 1 states: "Under mild assumptions, all reward classes consistent with the Plackett-Luce (and Bradley-Terry in particular) models can be represented with the reparameterization $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$." What exactly are these "mild assumptions"? The proof sketch mentions a projection operator $f$ that normalizes the reward, but I'm unclear on what conditions are needed for this to work. Does this theorem mean DPO can represent any possible reward function, or are there restrictions on what rewards can be learned?
Answer:
The "mild assumptions" in Theorem 1 are primarily that (1) the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)$ has full support (i.e., $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) > 0$ for all $(x, y)$ of interest) and (2) the partition function $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)$ is finite for all $x$. These ensure the reparameterization is well-defined and produces a valid probability distribution.

To understand why these are the needed conditions, consider the proof's construction. Given any reward function $r(x, y)$, the optimal policy under the KL-constrained objective is $\pi_r(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)$. The proof defines a projection operator $f$ that subtracts $\beta \log Z(x)$ from the reward, yielding a reward in the same equivalence class (since $Z(x)$ depends only on $x$). After this subtraction, the reward takes the form $r'(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}$, which is exactly the DPO reparameterization. For this to work, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ must be positive everywhere (otherwise the log ratio is undefined) and $Z(x)$ must be finite (otherwise $\pi_r$ is not a valid distribution).

Regarding your second question: the theorem does **not** say DPO can represent any arbitrary reward function exactly. Rather, it says that for every equivalence class of rewards (where two rewards are equivalent if they differ by a function of $x$ alone), there exists a representative of that class expressible via the DPO reparameterization. Since rewards in the same equivalence class induce identical preference distributions under the Bradley-Terry/Plackett-Luce models (Lemma 1) and identical optimal policies (Lemma 2), this is sufficient for DPO's purposes. The practical restriction is that DPO can only learn rewards that are consistent with the preference model and where the corresponding optimal policy is a proper distribution given $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. If $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ assigns zero probability to some response $y$, for instance, DPO cannot assign a finite reward to that response, which could be a limitation in theory (though in practice language models assign nonzero probability to all token sequences).

Intuitively, the theorem tells us that the DPO reparameterization doesn't restrict the expressiveness of the reward model in any meaningful way—any reward that could be learned by explicitly fitting a reward model and then optimizing it with RL can instead be captured directly through the policy parameterization, provided the support and finiteness conditions hold.

### How does DPO avoid the instability issues of actor-critic algorithms like PPO?
Question:
Section 5.2 discusses instability in PPO and mentions that the normalization term $f(r_\phi, \pi_{\text{ref}}, \beta)$ acts as a "soft value function" that, without it, causes high variance in policy gradients. The paper says "the DPO reparameterization yields a reward function that does not require any baselines." But I don't understand the connection: DPO never computes this normalization term, so how does it avoid the variance issue? Is it because DPO doesn't use policy gradients at all, or is there something about the reparameterization that inherently solves this problem?
Answer:
The short answer is that both of your intuitions are correct, and they are deeply connected: the reparameterization is precisely what enables DPO to avoid policy gradients altogether, and it does so by making the normalization term cancel out analytically rather than needing to be estimated.

To understand why, let's trace through the logic carefully. In the actor-critic formulation (Eq. 6 in the paper), the objective contains the term $f(r_\phi, \pi_{\text{ref}}, \beta) = r_\phi(x,y) - \beta\log\sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp(\frac{1}{\beta}r_\phi(x,y))$, where the second part is the log partition function — the "soft value function." This term does not change the optimal policy (since it depends only on $x$, not $y$), but without it, the policy gradient has high variance because the raw reward $r_\phi(x,y)$ can vary wildly across samples. In PPO, practitioners handle this by either learning a separate value function as a baseline (which adds another neural network that must be trained and can itself be unstable) or by using a single-sample Monte Carlo estimate (which is itself high variance). The fundamental problem is that PPO operates in the policy gradient framework, where you sample $y \sim \pi_\theta$, compute a reward, and need to subtract a baseline to reduce variance — and that baseline is exactly this normalization term, which is intractable to compute exactly because it requires summing over all possible completions $y$.

DPO takes a fundamentally different route. The key reparameterization (Eq. 4) expresses the reward as $r(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta\log Z(x)$, where $Z(x)$ is that same partition function. Now, Theorem 1 in the paper shows that this reparameterization does not lose any generality — it simply selects a specific representative from each equivalence class of reward functions (where two rewards are equivalent if they differ by a function of $x$ alone). The specific representative it selects is the one whose partition function equals $1$, meaning the normalization term is identically zero for that reward. But here's the crucial point: DPO never needs to *compute* this normalization to benefit from it.

The reason is that the Bradley-Terry preference model depends only on the *difference* of rewards: $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 | x) = \sigma(r^*(x,y_1) - r^*(x,y_2))$. When you substitute the reparameterized reward into this expression, the $\beta\log Z(x)$ term appears identically in both $r(x,y_1)$ and $r(x,y_2)$, so it cancels in the difference. What remains is Eq. 5 in the paper, which expresses preference probabilities purely in terms of the policy $\pi^*$ and reference $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, with no partition function anywhere. This yields the DPO loss (Eq. 7), which is a standard binary cross-entropy objective — a supervised learning problem, not a reinforcement learning one.

So the answer to your question is that the reparameterization inherently solves the variance problem, and this is exactly what allows DPO to avoid policy gradients. In PPO, the normalization term is a nuisance you must estimate to reduce variance in a policy gradient. In DPO, the reparameterization ensures the analogous term is a function of $x$ only, which cancels in the preference model, leaving a clean maximum likelihood objective with no sampling, no value function, and no baseline needed. The variance issue in PPO is fundamentally a problem of the policy gradient approach — you need to estimate expectations over sampled trajectories and reduce their variance with baselines. DPO sidesteps this entirely by converting the problem into maximum likelihood estimation on the preference data, where the gradients are computed directly from the data rather than from sampled rollouts. The weighting term $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w))$ in the DPO gradient (shown in the paper's gradient analysis) serves a different purpose — it weights examples by how incorrectly the current model ranks them — but it does not play the role of a variance-reducing baseline; it emerges naturally from the cross-entropy loss.

### Why does the naive probability ratio approach cause model degeneration without the DPO weighting?
Question:
The paper mentions that a "naive probability ratio objective" without the weighting coefficient causes model degeneration (referencing Appendix Table 5). The DPO gradient includes an importance weight $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))$ that gives "higher weight when reward estimate is wrong." What exactly happens when this weight is removed? Does the model collapse, or does it just overfit? What is the mechanistic explanation for why the weighting prevents degeneration?
Answer:
## Answer

The weighting coefficient $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))$ acts as a **self-limiting feedback mechanism** that prevents the model from over-optimizing on examples it has already learned to rank correctly. Removing it doesn't cause an immediate collapse, but rather a gradual degeneration through unbounded over-optimization — the model keeps pushing probability mass toward $y_w$ and away from $y_l$ even after the correct ordering has been achieved.

To understand why, consider what happens at different stages of training. Early in training, the model's implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta$ may incorrectly rate $y_l$ above $y_w$, making $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w) > 0$ and thus the weight $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w)) \approx 1$. The gradient strongly pushes to correct this. But once the model has learned the correct ordering — say $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w) \gg \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l)$ — the weight shrinks toward zero because $\sigma$ of a large negative number is near zero. The gradient essentially vanishes on that example. The model stops pushing.

Without the weight, this natural saturation disappears. The gradient becomes a constant-magnitude push: always increase $\log \pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)$ and always decrease $\log \pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)$, regardless of how extreme the probability ratio has already become. There is no signal telling the model "you've learned this example, stop here." This is essentially the **Unlikelihood** training objective described in the paper's experiments, which optimizes to maximize $y_w$ probability and minimize $y_l$ probability without any moderation.

The mechanistic consequence is that the model drifts arbitrarily far from $\pi_\text{ref}$. Recall that DPO is derived from the KL-constrained RL objective, where the optimal policy satisfies $\pi^*(y \mid x) \propto \pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$. The KL constraint is what keeps the policy from degenerating — it prevents mode collapse and maintains generation diversity. In DPO, this constraint is encoded implicitly through the logistic function in the loss. The sigmoid $\sigma(\cdot)$ in $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ creates a natural equilibrium: when the implicit reward difference $\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l \mid x)}$ is large and positive, the loss is near zero and gradients are small. Strip away the weighting and you strip away this equilibrium — the model is free to push log-probability ratios to extreme values, which manifests as degeneration of generation quality (as reported in Appendix Table 5).

The broader concept here connects to the difference between **contrastive learning with a proper probabilistic model** versus **unregularized contrastive objectives**. DPO's loss is a Bradley-Terry maximum likelihood objective — it models the *probability* that a human would prefer $y_w$ over $y_l$, and the logistic link function ensures the loss saturates once the model's predictions match the data. A naive probability ratio objective discards this probabilistic interpretation and becomes an unbounded push-pull on log-probabilities. The weighting is not a minor numerical detail; it is the mechanism by which DPO enforces the KL constraint that the original RLHF problem requires, without which the policy degenerates just as it would in standard RLHF if you removed the KL penalty term.

### Does DPO truly optimize the same objective as RLHF, or is there an approximation?
Question:
The paper claims DPO "implicitly optimizes the same objective as existing RLHF algorithms (reward maximization with a KL-divergence constraint)." But DPO uses a completely different optimization procedure — a classification loss rather than reinforcement learning. Is the DPO objective mathematically equivalent to the RLHF objective in Equation 3, or is there some approximation being made? If they are truly equivalent, why does DPO achieve better empirical results than PPO in some experiments — shouldn't they converge to the same solution?
Answer:
# Answer

The short answer is that DPO is **mathematically equivalent** to the RLHF objective at the level of the optimal solution — no approximation is made in the derivation. The empirical gap between DPO and PPO arises because PPO is itself an *approximate* solver for Eq. 3, while DPO is an exact maximum-likelihood estimator for an equivalent reformulation. Let me walk through why.

## The derivation contains no approximation

The RLHF objective (Eq. 3) is the KL-constrained reward maximization:

$$\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr].$$

The first key step is that this objective has a **closed-form optimal policy** (Eq. 4):

$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),$$

where $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ is the partition function. Rearranging gives the reward in terms of the policy (Eq. 5):

$$r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).$$

Now comes the crucial, non-approximate step. The Bradley-Terry preference model depends only on the **difference** of rewards, $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))$. Since $Z(x)$ depends only on $x$ (not on $y$), it appears identically in both $r(x, y_1)$ and $r(x, y_2)$ and **cancels exactly** when we take the difference. This yields Eq. 6, which expresses the preference probability purely in terms of $\pi^*$ and $\pi_\text{ref}$, with no partition function and no approximation:

$$p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.$$

DPO then simply performs maximum likelihood on this expression (Eq. 7). Theorem 1 further establishes that the reparameterization $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$ does not lose any generality — every reward equivalence class consistent with the Bradley-Terry (or more general Plackett-Luce) model can be represented this way. Combined with Lemma 2 (equivalent reward functions induce the same optimal policy), this means DPO's optimum coincides with RLHF's optimum, assuming both are solved exactly and the preference data is generated by a reward compatible with the BT model.

## Why, then, does DPO outperform PPO?

The equivalence is at the level of the **global optimum**. PPO does not find the global optimum of Eq. 3 — it is a stochastic, sampling-based approximation. The paper diagnoses the specific sources of PPO's suboptimality in Section 4.2. When you expand the actor-critic objective that PPO implicitly targets (Eq. 10), the normalization term $f(r_\phi, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta) = r_\phi(x,y) - \beta \log \sum_y \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_\phi(x,y)\right)$ acts as a soft value function. This term does not affect the optimal solution, but without it, the policy gradient has high variance, making learning unstable. Prior work approximates this term with a learned value function or a single-sample Monte Carlo baseline, both of which introduce error. DPO's reparameterization **absorbs** this normalization term into the reward definition itself, so no baseline or value function is needed at all — the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ is exact and low-variance.

Additionally, PPO requires sampling completions from the current policy $\pi_\theta$ during training (the "rollout" step), which adds further variance and computational overhead. DPO operates entirely on the fixed offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D}$, computing gradients directly through the policy's log-probabilities. This makes DPO's optimization landscape smoother and its gradients more reliable.

So the situation is analogous to having two algorithms that target the same maximum likelihood estimate: one computes exact gradients of a smooth objective, while the other uses a high-variance stochastic approximation with auxiliary networks that must also be learned. Even though both target the same optimum, the exact method will typically converge more reliably and reach a better solution in practice — which is exactly what the paper observes. DPO's reward-KL frontier strictly dominates PPO's, and notably even dominates PPO-GT (which has access to ground-truth rewards), precisely because PPO's approximation errors persist regardless of reward quality.

## Summary

DPO is not an approximation of RLHF; it is an **exact reformulation**. The KL-constrained objective (Eq. 3) is solved in closed form, the reward is reparameterized in terms of the policy (Eq. 5), the partition function cancels exactly under the Bradley-Terry model, and the resulting maximum-likelihood objective (Eq. 7) has the same global optimum as RLHF. The empirical superiority of DPO over PPO reflects the fact that PPO is an approximate, high-variance solver for Eq. 3, while DPO optimizes an equivalent objective with exact, low-variance gradients and no need for value function estimation or on-policy sampling.

### Why do we need a separate frozen reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ throughout DPO training?
Question:
The paper says $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is typically initialized as $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$, and the DPO loss (Equation 7) involves both $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Why can't we just use the current model $\pi_\theta$ as its own reference (i.e., set $\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi_\theta$)? The reference model appears in the log-ratios in the loss — what would go wrong if it were the same as the model being trained? Also, the paper mentions initializing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by fine-tuning on preferred completions when SFT is unavailable. Why is this particular initialization important?
Answer:
# Why DPO Needs a Separate Frozen Reference Model

The short answer is that if you set $\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi_\theta$, the DPO loss collapses to a constant and provides zero learning signal. But the deeper reason involves what the reference model represents in the derivation.

## The Direct Problem: Loss Collapse

Look at the DPO objective (Equation 7):

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]$$

If $\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi_\theta$, then each log-ratio is $\log 1 = 0$, so the argument of $\sigma$ is always $0$, and the loss becomes $\log 2$ regardless of the data. The gradient vanishes, and no optimization occurs. This alone rules out the proposal.

## The Deeper Reason: What the Reference Model Represents

DPO is derived from the standard KL-constrained RLHF objective (Equation 3):

$$\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}[r(x, y)] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) \| \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)]$$

The reference model here is the anchor policy — the one the KL constraint keeps $\pi_\theta$ from drifting away from. In standard RLHF, this is $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$, and the constraint serves a critical purpose: it prevents the policy from exploiting errors in the (implicit) reward model and from mode-collapsing to a single high-reward answer, as the paper notes in the Preliminaries section.

The key derivation step (Equations 4–5) solves for the optimal policy in closed form and rearranges to express the reward in terms of the policy and reference:

$$r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

This reparameterization is what allows DPO to eliminate the explicit reward model. The implicit reward that $\pi_\theta$ represents is defined as:

$$\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$$

This reward has a precise interpretation: it is the reward function that, if used in the KL-constrained objective with reference $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, would make $\pi_\theta$ the optimal policy. For this interpretation to be meaningful, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ must be a fixed, external reference point. If it moved with $\pi_\theta$, the implicit reward would be trivially zero everywhere and would lose all meaning.

The reference also appears in the gradient (the expression given after Equation 7), where it shapes the per-example importance weight $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))$. This weight down-weights examples the model already handles correctly and up-weights examples where the implicit reward incorrectly prefers $y_l$ over $y_w$. Without a stable reference, this weighting — which the paper identifies as crucial for preventing model degeneration (see the Appendix discussion of the naive unlikelihood baseline) — would be ill-defined and unstable.

## Why the Reference Must Be Frozen

Even if you initialized $\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi_\theta$ at the start of training but allowed both to update together, the optimization landscape would shift underfoot at every step. The gradient would pick up additional terms from the changing reference, corrupting the clean learning signal. The KL constraint would also become vacuous, since $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_\theta \| \pi_\theta] = 0$ at all times, removing the regularizer that prevents mode collapse. Keeping $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ frozen ensures a stable baseline against which progress is measured throughout training.

## Why Initialization with $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ (or Preferred-FT) Matters

The paper explains this in the DPO outline section. The preference dataset $\mathcal{D}$ is typically constructed by sampling completions from $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ and then labeling them with human preferences. The derivation assumes that the preference data comes from the reference distribution. If $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is far from the distribution that actually generated the data, there is a distribution shift that degrades the quality of the implicit reward estimates.

When $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ is available, setting $\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi^{\text{SFT}}$ exactly matches the distribution used to generate the preference data. When it is not available — as in the paper's dialogue experiments with the Anthropic HH dataset — the paper initializes $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by fine-tuning on preferred completions:

$$\pi_\text{ref} = \operatorname*{argmax}_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]$$

This puts the reference model in the right "neighborhood" of distributions, ensuring that the completions in the preference dataset are reasonably likely under $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. The paper explicitly states this procedure "helps mitigate the distribution shift between the true reference distribution which is unavailable, and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ used by DPO." The preferred completions are at least within the support of this fine-tuned model, even if they were originally drawn from a different SFT model, making the log-ratios in the loss meaningful rather than extreme.

In summary, the reference model is the fixed anchor that gives the DPO loss its meaning, its gradient signal, and its regularization. Replacing it with the model being trained would collapse the objective, and initializing it poorly would introduce distribution shift that undermines the implicit reward estimation.

### How should the $\beta$ parameter be chosen in DPO and what are the tradeoffs?
Question:
The $\beta$ parameter controls the KL constraint strength. The paper uses values like $\beta \in \{0.05, 0.1, 1, 5\}$ in the sentiment experiments and says they "did not meaningfully tune DPO's $\beta$ hyperparameter" for summarization. What are the tradeoffs in choosing $\beta$? Does a higher $\beta$ mean the model stays closer to the reference policy (more conservative) or farther from it? Is there a principled way to set this value, or is it purely empirical? Also, how does $\beta$ relate to the KL constraint in the original RLHF objective (Equation 3)?
Answer:
The $\beta$ parameter in DPO directly corresponds to the KL penalty coefficient in the original RLHF objective (Equation 3 in the paper):

$$\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\left[r_{\phi}(x, y)\right] - \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) \| \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\right]$$

Here, $\beta$ is the penalty coefficient on the KL divergence between the learned policy $\pi_\theta$ and the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ (typically the SFT model). A higher $\beta$ imposes a stronger penalty for deviating from $\pi_\text{ref}$, making the resulting policy more conservative. A lower $\beta$ weakens this constraint, allowing the policy to move further from the reference distribution to pursue higher reward.

This relationship becomes especially clear from the closed-form optimal policy derived in the paper (Equation 4):

$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$

When $\beta$ is large, the exponent $\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)$ is small, so the exponential term is close to $1$, and the optimal policy $\pi_r$ stays close to $\pi_\text{ref}$. When $\beta$ is small, the reward dominates: the policy shifts more aggressively toward high-reward responses, at the cost of greater KL divergence from the reference. In the limit $\beta \to \infty$, the policy is unchanged from $\pi_\text{ref}$; in the limit $\beta \to 0$, the policy concentrates entirely on the highest-reward completion, risking mode collapse.

In the DPO loss itself (Equation 7), $\beta$ appears as a scaling factor on the log-probability ratios:

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]$$

The paper defines an implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$, so $\beta$ controls how sensitive this implicit reward is to changes in the policy's log-probabilities relative to the reference. A larger $\beta$ amplifies the reward signal for a given change in log-probability ratio, but through the optimal policy mapping, it also enforces a tighter KL constraint, so the net effect is a more conservative policy that deviates less from $\pi_\text{ref}$.

The practical tradeoffs are as follows. A low $\beta$ allows the model to achieve higher reward on the preference data, but risks over-optimization: the policy may drift far from the reference distribution where the implicit reward model is reliable, potentially degrading generalization or leading to degenerate outputs. The paper notes that this constraint prevents "the model from deviating too far from the distribution on which the reward model is accurate, as well as maintaining the generation diversity and preventing mode-collapse to single high-reward answers." A high $\beta$ keeps the model closer to the reference, preserving fluency and diversity, but may under-exploit the preference signal, resulting in slower learning or suboptimal reward.

Regarding how to choose $\beta$, the paper treats it as primarily empirical. In the controlled sentiment experiments, they sweep over $\beta \in \{0.05, 0.1, 1, 5\}$ to trace out a reward-KL frontier, allowing them to compare DPO against PPO at different points on the tradeoff curve. For summarization and dialogue, they report using $\beta = 0.1$ (from the implementation details) without meaningful tuning, suggesting DPO is relatively robust to this choice across tasks. There is no fully principled, theory-driven method for setting $\beta$ given in the paper; the value depends on the task, the quality of the preference data, and how much deviation from the reference policy is acceptable for the application at hand. In practice, one would select $\beta$ by evaluating the resulting policy on a held-out set, similar to how one would tune any regularization hyperparameter.

### What happens if human preferences don't actually follow the Bradley-Terry model?
Question:
DPO assumes preferences follow the Bradley-Terry model where $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 | x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))$. But what if real human preferences are more complex — for example, intransitive (where A is preferred to B, B to C, but C to A), or dependent on factors not captured by a single scalar reward? The paper mentions Plackett-Luce models as a generalization, but this still assumes a scalar reward ranking. Would DPO still produce a meaningful policy if preferences violate these assumptions, and how would we detect this problem?
Answer:
## Answer

This is an important question that touches on the fundamental assumptions underlying DPO and preference-based learning more broadly. The short answer is: if human preferences systematically violate the Bradley-Terry (BT) model, DPO becomes a misspecified model and will converge to the best-fitting approximation within the BT family, but there is no guarantee this approximation meaningfully captures the true preference structure.

The BT model, $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))$, encodes two strong assumptions. First, it assumes that preferences are **transitive**: if $y_1 \succ y_2$ and $y_2 \succ y_3$, then $y_1 \succ y_3$. This follows directly because the model ranks completions by a scalar reward $r^*$, and scalar comparisons are inherently transitive. If a human exhibits intransitive preferences (preferring A to B, B to C, but C to A), no scalar reward function can reproduce this behavior, and the BT model is fundamentally misspecified. Second, the model assumes preferences depend only on the **difference of a single scalar reward** between two completions, ignoring any higher-dimensional structure—such as preferences that depend on context, mood, or multi-attribute tradeoffs that cannot be collapsed into one number.

When the BT assumption is violated, DPO still optimizes its objective—the maximum likelihood estimator under the reparametrized BT model—but this objective no longer corresponds to the true preference distribution. Concretely, DPO will converge to the policy $\pi_\theta$ that best fits the observed preference data *as if* those preferences were BT-compatible. The paper itself notes that DPO "enjoys certain theoretical properties, such as consistencies under suitable assumption of the preference data distribution," meaning the consistency guarantees hold only when the preference data actually follows the assumed model. Under misspecification, the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ will be the projection of the true preference structure onto the nearest BT-compatible reward, in the maximum-likelihood sense. The resulting policy may still show *some* improvement over the reference policy, since the gradient still increases the probability of preferred completions and decreases that of dispreferred ones, but the policy will be optimal only for this approximate reward, not for the true human preferences.

The paper's theoretical analysis (Theorem 1 and the surrounding lemmas) is worth examining here. The theorem establishes that the DPO reparametrization $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ can represent *any* reward class consistent with the Plackett-Luce family. But this is a statement about the expressiveness of the reparametrization *within* the BT/Plackett-Luce framework—it does not address what happens when preferences fall outside that framework entirely. The lemmas about equivalence classes of rewards (where $r$ and $r'$ are equivalent if they differ only by a function of $x$) are also internal to the BT framework.

Regarding detection, the paper does not directly address model misspecification diagnostics, but several approaches follow naturally from the framework. One could test for **transitivity violations** in the preference dataset: if the dataset contains comparisons forming cycles (A preferred to B, B to C, C to A), this is direct evidence against BT. More generally, one can assess **goodness-of-fit** by comparing the BT model's predicted preference probabilities against observed empirical frequencies. If the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta$ assigns similar rewards to two completions that humans consistently distinguish, or if it systematically mispredicts observed preference rates, this signals misspecification. Another practical signal is **inconsistency across data subsets**: if the same pair $(y_1, y_2)$ is preferred differently depending on context not captured in $x$, this violates the BT assumption that preferences depend only on $x$ and the scalar reward difference.

It is also worth connecting this to the broader concept the paper mentions: the **von Neumann winner** from the contextual dueling bandits literature. When no scalar reward exists, one can still define an optimal policy as one whose expected win rate against any other policy is at least $50\%$. This suggests that even under preference intransitivity, there may be a meaningful notion of "best" policy—but DPO, being tied to the BT model, is not designed to find it. Alternative approaches that relax the scalar-reward assumption, such as preference-based RL methods that estimate a non-transitive preference function directly, would be needed.

In summary, DPO is not robust to arbitrary violations of the BT assumption. It will produce a policy that is optimal for the best BT-compatible approximation of the preferences, which may or may not be meaningful depending on how severely the true preferences deviate from transitivity and scalar-reducibility. Detecting such deviations requires examining the preference data for transitivity violations and measuring how well the BT model's predictions match observed preferences.

### How is the reward-KL frontier used to compare DPO and PPO in the sentiment experiments?
Question:
In the controlled sentiment generation experiment, the paper evaluates algorithms by their "frontier of achieved reward and KL-divergence from the reference policy." I understand that higher reward and lower KL are both desirable, but how exactly is this frontier computed and compared? The paper runs experiments with different hyperparameters ($\beta$ for DPO, target KL for PPO) and plots reward vs. KL. Is the comparison based on Pareto dominance, or is there a specific metric? Also, what does it mean that "DPO's reward/KL tradeoff strictly dominates PPO" — does this mean DPO is better at every point on the frontier?
Answer:
The reward-KL frontier in the sentiment experiment is a Pareto frontier constructed from multiple training runs, each using a different value of the hyperparameter that controls the reward-KL tradeoff. For PPO, the paper sweeps over target KL values in $\{3, 6, 9, 12\}$; for DPO, it sweeps over $\beta \in \{0.05, 0.1, 1, 5\}$; and for Unlikelihood, it sweeps over $\alpha \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1\}$. After every 100 training steps until convergence, each resulting policy is evaluated on a set of test prompts, and two quantities are computed: the average reward under the ground-truth sentiment classifier and the average sequence-level KL divergence from the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (which is the sum of per-timestep KL divergences, $\text{KL}(\pi \| \pi_{\text{ref}})$). Each evaluated checkpoint thus yields a single $(\text{KL}, \text{reward})$ point, and the collection of points across all hyperparameter settings and checkpoints forms the scatter plot shown in Figure 2 of the paper.

The comparison is indeed based on Pareto dominance. The idea is that both higher reward and lower KL are desirable — you want a policy that achieves high reward (good sentiment control) while staying close to the reference policy (preserving fluency and diversity). The frontier for each algorithm is the upper-left envelope of its set of points, representing the best reward achievable at each level of KL (or equivalently, the lowest KL needed for each reward level). An algorithm's frontier "dominates" another's if, for every point on the dominated frontier, the dominating algorithm has a point that is at least as good in both dimensions and strictly better in at least one.

When the paper states that "DPO's reward/KL tradeoff strictly dominates PPO," it means that across the entire range of KL values examined, DPO achieves higher reward than PPO at the same KL, or equivalently, achieves the same reward at a lower KL. In other words, no matter how you set PPO's target KL, DPO (with an appropriately chosen $\beta$) can achieve a better or equal reward-KL combination. This is particularly noteworthy because the comparison holds even against PPO-GT, which has access to the ground-truth reward function rather than a learned approximation, suggesting that DPO's advantage comes from optimization efficiency rather than reward modeling quality.

The intuition behind this comparison is that both DPO and PPO are optimizing the same underlying objective — the KL-constrained reward maximization in Equation 3, $\max_{\pi_\theta} \mathbb{E}[r_\phi(x,y)] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}}]$ — so the frontier directly measures how efficiently each algorithm navigates this tradeoff. DPO's dominance indicates that it finds better solutions to the same optimization problem, likely because it avoids the instabilities of the actor-critic loop (as discussed in Section 4.2 of the paper, where the normalization term in the PPO objective can cause high-variance gradients), and instead directly optimizes the policy through a simple classification loss.
