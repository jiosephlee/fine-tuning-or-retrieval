\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why does the naive probability ratio approach cause model degeneration without the DPO weighting?
Question:
The paper mentions that a "naive probability ratio objective" without the weighting coefficient causes model degeneration (referencing Appendix Table 5). The DPO gradient includes an importance weight $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))$ that gives "higher weight when reward estimate is wrong." What exactly happens when this weight is removed? Does the model collapse, or does it just overfit? What is the mechanistic explanation for why the weighting prevents degeneration?
Answer:
## Answer

The weighting coefficient $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))$ acts as a **self-limiting feedback mechanism** that prevents the model from over-optimizing on examples it has already learned to rank correctly. Removing it doesn't cause an immediate collapse, but rather a gradual degeneration through unbounded over-optimization — the model keeps pushing probability mass toward $y_w$ and away from $y_l$ even after the correct ordering has been achieved.

To understand why, consider what happens at different stages of training. Early in training, the model's implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta$ may incorrectly rate $y_l$ above $y_w$, making $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w) > 0$ and thus the weight $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w)) \approx 1$. The gradient strongly pushes to correct this. But once the model has learned the correct ordering — say $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w) \gg \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l)$ — the weight shrinks toward zero because $\sigma$ of a large negative number is near zero. The gradient essentially vanishes on that example. The model stops pushing.

Without the weight, this natural saturation disappears. The gradient becomes a constant-magnitude push: always increase $\log \pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)$ and always decrease $\log \pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)$, regardless of how extreme the probability ratio has already become. There is no signal telling the model "you've learned this example, stop here." This is essentially the **Unlikelihood** training objective described in the paper's experiments, which optimizes to maximize $y_w$ probability and minimize $y_l$ probability without any moderation.

The mechanistic consequence is that the model drifts arbitrarily far from $\pi_\text{ref}$. Recall that DPO is derived from the KL-constrained RL objective, where the optimal policy satisfies $\pi^*(y \mid x) \propto \pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$. The KL constraint is what keeps the policy from degenerating — it prevents mode collapse and maintains generation diversity. In DPO, this constraint is encoded implicitly through the logistic function in the loss. The sigmoid $\sigma(\cdot)$ in $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ creates a natural equilibrium: when the implicit reward difference $\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l \mid x)}$ is large and positive, the loss is near zero and gradients are small. Strip away the weighting and you strip away this equilibrium — the model is free to push log-probability ratios to extreme values, which manifests as degeneration of generation quality (as reported in Appendix Table 5).

The broader concept here connects to the difference between **contrastive learning with a proper probabilistic model** versus **unregularized contrastive objectives**. DPO's loss is a Bradley-Terry maximum likelihood objective — it models the *probability* that a human would prefer $y_w$ over $y_l$, and the logistic link function ensures the loss saturates once the model's predictions match the data. A naive probability ratio objective discards this probabilistic interpretation and becomes an unbounded push-pull on log-probabilities. The weighting is not a minor numerical detail; it is the mechanism by which DPO enforces the KL constraint that the original RLHF problem requires, without which the policy degenerates just as it would in standard RLHF if you removed the KL penalty term.
