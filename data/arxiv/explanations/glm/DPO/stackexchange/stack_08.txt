\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Does DPO truly optimize the same objective as RLHF, or is there an approximation?
Question:
The paper claims DPO "implicitly optimizes the same objective as existing RLHF algorithms (reward maximization with a KL-divergence constraint)." But DPO uses a completely different optimization procedure — a classification loss rather than reinforcement learning. Is the DPO objective mathematically equivalent to the RLHF objective in Equation 3, or is there some approximation being made? If they are truly equivalent, why does DPO achieve better empirical results than PPO in some experiments — shouldn't they converge to the same solution?
Answer:
# Answer

The short answer is that DPO is **mathematically equivalent** to the RLHF objective at the level of the optimal solution — no approximation is made in the derivation. The empirical gap between DPO and PPO arises because PPO is itself an *approximate* solver for Eq. 3, while DPO is an exact maximum-likelihood estimator for an equivalent reformulation. Let me walk through why.

## The derivation contains no approximation

The RLHF objective (Eq. 3) is the KL-constrained reward maximization:

$$\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr].$$

The first key step is that this objective has a **closed-form optimal policy** (Eq. 4):

$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),$$

where $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ is the partition function. Rearranging gives the reward in terms of the policy (Eq. 5):

$$r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).$$

Now comes the crucial, non-approximate step. The Bradley-Terry preference model depends only on the **difference** of rewards, $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))$. Since $Z(x)$ depends only on $x$ (not on $y$), it appears identically in both $r(x, y_1)$ and $r(x, y_2)$ and **cancels exactly** when we take the difference. This yields Eq. 6, which expresses the preference probability purely in terms of $\pi^*$ and $\pi_\text{ref}$, with no partition function and no approximation:

$$p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.$$

DPO then simply performs maximum likelihood on this expression (Eq. 7). Theorem 1 further establishes that the reparameterization $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$ does not lose any generality — every reward equivalence class consistent with the Bradley-Terry (or more general Plackett-Luce) model can be represented this way. Combined with Lemma 2 (equivalent reward functions induce the same optimal policy), this means DPO's optimum coincides with RLHF's optimum, assuming both are solved exactly and the preference data is generated by a reward compatible with the BT model.

## Why, then, does DPO outperform PPO?

The equivalence is at the level of the **global optimum**. PPO does not find the global optimum of Eq. 3 — it is a stochastic, sampling-based approximation. The paper diagnoses the specific sources of PPO's suboptimality in Section 4.2. When you expand the actor-critic objective that PPO implicitly targets (Eq. 10), the normalization term $f(r_\phi, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta) = r_\phi(x,y) - \beta \log \sum_y \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_\phi(x,y)\right)$ acts as a soft value function. This term does not affect the optimal solution, but without it, the policy gradient has high variance, making learning unstable. Prior work approximates this term with a learned value function or a single-sample Monte Carlo baseline, both of which introduce error. DPO's reparameterization **absorbs** this normalization term into the reward definition itself, so no baseline or value function is needed at all — the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ is exact and low-variance.

Additionally, PPO requires sampling completions from the current policy $\pi_\theta$ during training (the "rollout" step), which adds further variance and computational overhead. DPO operates entirely on the fixed offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D}$, computing gradients directly through the policy's log-probabilities. This makes DPO's optimization landscape smoother and its gradients more reliable.

So the situation is analogous to having two algorithms that target the same maximum likelihood estimate: one computes exact gradients of a smooth objective, while the other uses a high-variance stochastic approximation with auxiliary networks that must also be learned. Even though both target the same optimum, the exact method will typically converge more reliably and reach a better solution in practice — which is exactly what the paper observes. DPO's reward-KL frontier strictly dominates PPO's, and notably even dominates PPO-GT (which has access to ground-truth rewards), precisely because PPO's approximation errors persist regardless of reward quality.

## Summary

DPO is not an approximation of RLHF; it is an **exact reformulation**. The KL-constrained objective (Eq. 3) is solved in closed form, the reward is reparameterized in terms of the policy (Eq. 5), the partition function cancels exactly under the Bradley-Terry model, and the resulting maximum-likelihood objective (Eq. 7) has the same global optimum as RLHF. The empirical superiority of DPO over PPO reflects the fact that PPO is an approximate, high-variance solver for Eq. 3, while DPO optimizes an equivalent objective with exact, low-variance gradients and no need for value function estimation or on-policy sampling.
