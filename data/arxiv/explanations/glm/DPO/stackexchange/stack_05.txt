\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What are the 'mild assumptions' in Theorem 1 and does it guarantee DPO can represent any reward?
Question:
Theorem 1 states: "Under mild assumptions, all reward classes consistent with the Plackett-Luce (and Bradley-Terry in particular) models can be represented with the reparameterization $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$." What exactly are these "mild assumptions"? The proof sketch mentions a projection operator $f$ that normalizes the reward, but I'm unclear on what conditions are needed for this to work. Does this theorem mean DPO can represent any possible reward function, or are there restrictions on what rewards can be learned?
Answer:
The "mild assumptions" in Theorem 1 are primarily that (1) the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)$ has full support (i.e., $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) > 0$ for all $(x, y)$ of interest) and (2) the partition function $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)$ is finite for all $x$. These ensure the reparameterization is well-defined and produces a valid probability distribution.

To understand why these are the needed conditions, consider the proof's construction. Given any reward function $r(x, y)$, the optimal policy under the KL-constrained objective is $\pi_r(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)$. The proof defines a projection operator $f$ that subtracts $\beta \log Z(x)$ from the reward, yielding a reward in the same equivalence class (since $Z(x)$ depends only on $x$). After this subtraction, the reward takes the form $r'(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}$, which is exactly the DPO reparameterization. For this to work, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ must be positive everywhere (otherwise the log ratio is undefined) and $Z(x)$ must be finite (otherwise $\pi_r$ is not a valid distribution).

Regarding your second question: the theorem does **not** say DPO can represent any arbitrary reward function exactly. Rather, it says that for every equivalence class of rewards (where two rewards are equivalent if they differ by a function of $x$ alone), there exists a representative of that class expressible via the DPO reparameterization. Since rewards in the same equivalence class induce identical preference distributions under the Bradley-Terry/Plackett-Luce models (Lemma 1) and identical optimal policies (Lemma 2), this is sufficient for DPO's purposes. The practical restriction is that DPO can only learn rewards that are consistent with the preference model and where the corresponding optimal policy is a proper distribution given $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. If $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ assigns zero probability to some response $y$, for instance, DPO cannot assign a finite reward to that response, which could be a limitation in theory (though in practice language models assign nonzero probability to all token sequences).

Intuitively, the theorem tells us that the DPO reparameterization doesn't restrict the expressiveness of the reward model in any meaningful way—any reward that could be learned by explicitly fitting a reward model and then optimizing it with RL can instead be captured directly through the policy parameterization, provided the support and finiteness conditions hold.
