\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Is DPO just supervised learning on preferred responses?
Question:
The DPO paper states that the method uses "a simple binary cross entropy objective" and eliminates reinforcement learning. Given that binary cross-entropy is a standard supervised learning loss, how is DPO fundamentally different from just doing supervised fine-tuning on the preferred completions $y_w$? The loss in Equation 7 looks like a classification loss, but what exactly is being classified, and why isn't this just supervised learning?
Answer:
# Direct Preference Optimization Is Preference Learning, Not Imitation Learning

The short answer is that DPO is doing supervised learning on *preferences* (relative judgments between two completions), not on preferred completions themselves. This distinction matters substantially and explains both what is being "classified" and why DPO behaves differently from supervised fine-tuning on $y_w$.

## What is actually being classified

The DPO loss in Equation 7 is a binary cross-entropy objective, but the binary variable being predicted is not "is this response good" — it is **"is $y_w$ preferred over $y_l$ given $x$"**. The "classifier" here is parameterized through the implicit reward

$$\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)},$$

and the logit of the binary classifier is the reward difference $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l)$. So DPO trains $\pi_\theta$ such that the Bradley-Terry probability $p(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l))$ matches the empirical preference distribution in the dataset.

In contrast, supervised fine-tuning on preferred completions (the "Preferred-FT" baseline the paper evaluates) would simply minimize $-\log \pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)$. That objective is purely an imitation objective: it makes the model assign higher probability to $y_w$ but never references $y_l$, never references $\pi_\text{ref}$, and has no notion of *relative* preference.

## Why this is not just supervised learning on $y_w$

There are three structural differences, all visible in the loss and its gradient.

**It is contrastive.** The gradient in the paper shows that DPO simultaneously increases $\log \pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)$ *and* decreases $\log \pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)$:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} \propto -\beta\, \mathbb{E}\left[\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w)) \cdot \bigl(\nabla_\theta \log \pi(y_w \mid x) - \nabla_\theta \log \pi(y_l \mid x)\bigr)\right].$$

Preferred-FT only has the first term; it has no signal at all from the dispreferred response. The information contained in "$y_w$ is better than $y_l$" is fundamentally different from the information in "$y_w$ is a good response," and DPO uses the former.

**The logits are log-ratios against a reference policy, not raw log-probabilities.** The implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) - \beta \log \pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)$ bakes in the KL constraint from the RLHF objective in Equation 3. This is what makes DPO equivalent, under the Bradley-Terry model, to *solving* the constrained RL problem in closed form rather than merely imitating good responses. The reference model $\pi_\text{ref}$ (typically the SFT model) acts as an anchor: the "reward" of a response is not its raw probability under $\pi_\theta$, but how much more probable it is *relative to what the SFT model would assign*. This is what prevents the model from drifting arbitrarily far from the SFT policy — exactly the role the KL divergence plays in PPO-based RLHF.

**There is a dynamic, per-example importance weight.** The coefficient $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))$ in the gradient down-weights examples where the implicit reward model already ranks $y_w$ above $y_l$ correctly, and up-weights examples where it is wrong. The paper explicitly notes that removing this weighting — i.e., a naive "increase $y_w$, decrease $y_l$" unlikelihood-style objective — causes the model to degenerate (Appendix Table 6). Preferred-FT has no such mechanism because it has no notion of a current reward estimate.

## Putting it together

A useful way to think about it: DPO *is* supervised learning, but the thing being supervised is a **preference classifier**, and the classifier happens to be parameterized in such a way that, once trained, its *induced optimal policy* under the standard RLHF KL-constrained objective is exactly $\pi_\theta$. The paper's main theorem (Theorem 4.1) establishes that this reparameterization $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ does not restrict the class of representable reward functions, so DPO is not a weaker approximation of RLHF — it is solving the same problem, just with the reward model and the policy tied together through the change of variables.

So the answer to "is DPO just supervised learning on preferred responses?" is: it is supervised learning, but on a fundamentally different target (pairwise preferences under a Bradley-Terry model with a reference-anchored reward parameterization), and that difference is what makes it equivalent to RLHF rather than to imitation. The cross-entropy form is shared with classification, but the underlying probabilistic model, the contrastive structure, the implicit reward parameterization, and the dynamic weighting are what distinguish it from naive supervised fine-tuning.
