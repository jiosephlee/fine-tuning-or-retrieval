\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Unraveling the RL Puzzle: How DeepSeekMath Unifies LLM Training Methods

## The Unified Paradigm: One Formula to Rule Them All

When we look at training methods like Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), they can seem like entirely different beasts. However, the DeepSeekMath paper provides a unified paradigm to understand them all through a single gradient equation:

$$ \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[(q,o) \sim \mathcal{D}\right] \left( \frac{1}{|o|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o|} GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q, o, t, \pi_{rf}) \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t}) \right) $$

This equation reveals three key components that define any training method:

- **Data Source ($\mathcal{D}$)**: Where the training data comes from. Is it sampled offline from the initial SFT model, or online from the real-time training policy?
- **Reward Function ($\pi_{rf}$)**: What provides the training signal? This could be a simple rule checking answer correctness or a trained neural reward model.
- **Algorithm ($\mathcal{A}$)**: How the method processes the data and reward signal to calculate the gradient coefficient ($GC$), which dictates how much to penalize or reinforce certain outputs.

By analyzing these components, DeepSeekMath highlights some crucial differences between methods. For instance, standard RFT and DPO rely on offline sampling, while Online RFT and GRPO use online sampling. The researchers found that online sampling gains a significant advantage in later stages of training because the real-time policy model generates more relevant and challenging data over time.

Furthermore, the choice of algorithm drastically changes how rewards are applied. Online RFT does not penalize incorrect responses and uniformly reinforces all correct ones. In contrast, GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) adjusts its gradient coefficient based on the actual reward value. This allows for differential reinforcement, meaning it can penalize wrong answers and reward right ones to varying degrees. The paper demonstrates that GRPO's approach of altering positive and negative gradient coefficients is far more efficient. Additionally, incorporating process supervision (GRPO+PS) over outcome supervision (GRPO+OS) yields even better performance, highlighting the value of fine-grained, step-aware gradient coefficients.

## Why Does Reinforcement Learning Actually Work?

DeepSeekMath's GRPO achieved impressive performance gains over its instruction-tuned baseline. But why does RL work so well? 

To answer this, the researchers evaluated the models using $Pass@K$ and $Maj@K$ metrics. $Pass@K$ measures the fundamental capability of the model (the probability that at least one of $K$ samples is correct), while $Maj@K$ measures the robustness of the output distribution (the accuracy of the majority vote among $K$ samples). 

The results showed that RL significantly enhances $Maj@K$ but does not improve $Pass@K$. This indicates that reinforcement learning doesn't necessarily teach the model new fundamental problem-solving skills. Instead, it makes the model's output distribution more robust by aligning it to favor correct responses. The paper notes this addresses a "misalignment problem" in reasoning tasks within SFT models, where the model knows the answer but doesn't always surface it as the top response. RL effectively boosts the correct response from the top-$K$ possibilities.

## The Road Ahead: Achieving More Effective RL

Based on their unified paradigm, the DeepSeekMath team outlines several promising future directions for the three core components of training:

### Data Source

Currently, RL relies on naive nucleus sampling from in-distribution prompts. To push boundaries, future work should explore out-of-distribution question prompts. Pairing this with advanced decoding strategies like tree-search, and leveraging efficient inference techniques, could drastically improve exploration efficiency.

### Algorithms

Today's methods blindly trust the reward signal to adjust token probabilities. However, reward signals are often noisy—even meticulously annotated datasets can contain a significant percentage of incorrect labels. Future algorithms need to be robust against this noise, and the researchers suggest that "weak-to-strong" alignment methods could fundamentally change how we handle unreliable reward signals.

### Reward Function

Since the reward function is the source of all training signals, improving it is critical. The authors highlight three goals:

- Enhancing the generalization ability of reward models to handle out-of-distribution questions.
- Finding ways for reward models to reflect their own uncertainty.
- Efficiently building high-quality process reward models that can provide fine-grained, step-by-step training signals.

By unifying these disparate training methods into a single framework, DeepSeekMath not only clarifies our understanding of how language models learn to reason but also charts a clear, structured path for the future of AI alignment.
