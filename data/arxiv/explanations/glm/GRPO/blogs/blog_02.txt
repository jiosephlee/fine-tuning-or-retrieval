\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Mining the Web for Math: The Secret Behind DeepSeekMath's Training Data

Building a language model that can reason through complex mathematical problems is notoriously difficult. Historically, the best-performing models have been closed-source, leaving the open-source community trailing behind. DeepSeekMath 7B changes this narrative, achieving a score of 51.7% on the competition-level MATH benchmark and approaching the performance of frontier models like GPT-4. 

The secret to DeepSeekMath's success doesn't lie in an unprecedented number of parameters, but rather in the quality and composition of its training data. By meticulously mining the web for mathematical content and questioning conventional wisdom about what makes good math training data, the DeepSeekMath team uncovered some surprising insights.

## An Iterative Pipeline for Web-Scale Math Data

The foundation of DeepSeekMath is the DeepSeekMath Corpus, a massive 120-billion-token dataset sourced entirely from Common Crawl. Creating a dataset of this scale and quality required an iterative data collection pipeline that balances automated classification with human curation.

The process starts with a seed corpus—in this case, OpenWebMath, a collection of high-quality mathematical web texts. Using this seed, the team trained a fastText classifier to distinguish between math-related and non-math-related web pages. They randomly selected 500,000 data points from the seed corpus as positive examples and 500,000 random Common Crawl pages as negative examples, training the classifier to recognize the mathematical patterns in the seed data. 

Once trained, the classifier was unleashed on a deduplicated set of 40 billion HTML web pages from Common Crawl. To ensure quality, the team ranked the collected pages by their fastText scores and only kept the top-ranking ones. After the first iteration, the classifier had identified 40 billion tokens of math data, but plenty of valuable mathematical content was still slipping through the cracks because the initial seed lacked diversity.

To fix this, the team took a domain-based approach to expand their seed data. They organized Common Crawl into domains (defined by base URLs) and calculated the percentage of collected math pages within each domain. If a domain had more than 10% of its pages collected, it was classified as math-related. Researchers then manually annotated the specific URLs within these domains that hosted mathematical content, adding the uncollected pages from these URLs back into the seed corpus to retrain and improve the fastText model. 

After four iterations of this expanding pipeline, the team had identified 35.5 million mathematical web pages, totaling 120 billion tokens. By the fourth iteration, 98% of the recoverable data had already been collected, signaling diminishing returns and prompting the team to stop. Finally, to avoid benchmark contamination, they aggressively filtered the data, removing any web pages containing exact 10-gram matches with evaluation datasets like GSM8K and MATH.

## The ArXiv Surprise: Scientific Papers Aren't a Silver Bullet

A common practice in training math-focused language models is to include a large volume of scientific papers from arXiv. Intuitively, one would expect that feeding a model dense, academic mathematics would directly boost its reasoning abilities. Surprisingly, the DeepSeekMath team found that arXiv papers seem ineffective in improving mathematical reasoning.

The researchers tested this by training models on different arXiv corpora, including MathPile (which is over 85% arXiv) and ArXiv-RedPajama. They experimented with both a 1.3B parameter model (trained for 150 billion tokens) and a 7B model (trained for 40 billion tokens). Across the board, training on arXiv-only corpora yielded no notable improvements, and sometimes even deteriorated performance on benchmarks like GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-STEM, and the formal mathematics benchmark miniF2F.

The authors note that this conclusion has limits. They have not yet explored whether arXiv data helps with specific tasks not included in their benchmarks, whether it helps when mixed with other data types, or if its benefits only manifest in much larger models. Nonetheless, for the core task of mathematical reasoning, their findings suggest that the open web contains richer, more effective training data than the formal, highly-structured text of academic papers.

## Code Training: The Unexpected Catalyst for Math Reasoning

Another long-standing question in the AI community is whether training models on code improves their general reasoning abilities. DeepSeekMath provides a compelling answer: at least for mathematical reasoning, prior code training significantly helps. 

DeepSeekMath-Base 7B was initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B rather than a general language model. The researchers validated this choice by comparing two-stage training (code training followed by math training) against a baseline of general training followed by math training. 

They found that code training enhances a model's ability to solve math problems both with and without the use of tools. When models are allowed to write Python programs to solve math problems, prior code training naturally provides a huge boost. But even when tools are disallowed and the model must rely purely on step-by-step text reasoning, code training still improves performance. It primes the model with a structured, logical foundation that makes the subsequent math training more efficient and effective.

The experiments also revealed a trade-off in how the data is mixed. If code and math tokens are mixed together in a single training stage, it effectively prevents the model from forgetting how to code (a problem known as catastrophic forgetting). However, in a smaller 1.3B model, this mixed approach slightly compromised pure mathematical reasoning without tools. The best mathematical performance was still achieved by the two-stage approach: training on code first, and then focusing on math. 

## Conclusion

DeepSeekMath demonstrates that open language models can achieve elite mathematical reasoning without scaling up to massive parameter counts. By carefully engineering a data selection pipeline to extract 120 billion tokens from Common Crawl, the team proved that the public web is an untapped goldmine for mathematical data. Along the way, they debunked the assumption that arXiv papers are inherently beneficial for math reasoning and confirmed that a foundation in code provides a surprising but powerful boost to a model's mathematical capabilities. These insights offer a clear roadmap for the open-source community: when it comes to training math models, the quality, composition, and structure of the data matter far more than the sheer size of the model.
