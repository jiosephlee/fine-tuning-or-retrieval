\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why does the iterative GRPO algorithm update the reference model to the current policy at each iteration?
Question:
In Algorithm 1 of the DeepSeekMath paper (Iterative GRPO), line 3 states: 'reference model $\pi_{ref} \leftarrow \pi_{\theta}$' at the beginning of each iteration. This means the reference model used for KL regularization is updated to the current policy model at the start of each iteration.

In standard PPO/RLHF, the reference model is typically kept fixed as the initial SFT model throughout training, serving as an anchor to prevent the policy from drifting too far. If GRPO updates the reference model to the current policy at each iteration, doesn't this weaken the KL regularization over time, allowing the policy to drift further from the original SFT model? Why is this design choice made, and doesn't it risk the model diverging from its original capabilities?
Answer:
The key to understanding this design choice lies in the purpose of the iterative GRPO framework and the role the reference model plays within it. The paper explains the motivation in the "Iterative RL with GRPO" subsection: "As the reinforcement learning training process progresses, the old reward model may not be sufficient to supervise the current policy model." The iterative scheme addresses this by updating both the reward model and the reference model in tandem at the start of each iteration.

In standard PPO-based RLHF, the reference model (typically the SFT model) acts as a global anchor, and the KL penalty $\beta \mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_\theta \| \pi_{ref}]$ prevents the policy from drifting too far from the initial aligned model. This makes sense when the reward model is fixed and the primary concern is reward over-optimization—i.e., the policy exploiting the reward model in ways that degrade general behavior.

In iterative GRPO, however, the situation is different. The reward model itself is continually retrained on new samples from the current policy, using a replay mechanism with 10% historical data. This means the reward model adapts to the evolving policy distribution. If the reference model remained frozen at the original SFT model while the reward model moved forward, there would be a mismatch: the KL penalty would pull the policy back toward the SFT distribution, while the reward model—now trained on outputs from a more advanced policy—would be pushing it forward. This tension would make optimization harder and potentially less stable.

By setting $\pi_{ref} \leftarrow \pi_\theta$ at each iteration, the KL penalty becomes a **local regularizer** rather than a global anchor. Within each iteration, it prevents the policy from making overly drastic updates relative to its current state, which is consistent with the trust-region philosophy underlying PPO's clipping mechanism. The regularization still functions—it constrains how far $\pi_\theta$ can move during the inner optimization loop (steps 1 through $M$ in Algorithm 1)—but it does so relative to the policy's position at the start of that iteration, not relative to the original SFT model from many iterations ago.

Regarding the concern about divergence from original capabilities: the paper's analysis in "Why RL Works?" provides relevant context. The authors find that "RL enhances the model's overall performance by rendering the output distribution more robust" and that "the improvement is attributed to boosting the correct response from TopK rather than the enhancement of fundamental capabilities." This suggests the RL process is reshaping the output distribution to favor correct responses, not fundamentally rewriting the model's knowledge. The KL regularization at each iteration, combined with the reward model replay mechanism (which retains 10% historical data), provides sufficient stabilization. The empirical results support this: iterative RL "significantly improves the performance, especially at the first iteration," without reported degradation of the model's foundational abilities.

In essence, the reference model update is a deliberate design choice that matches the KL regularization's anchor point to the iterative training dynamics. It trades global anchoring for local stability, which is appropriate when the reward model itself is co-evolving with the policy.
