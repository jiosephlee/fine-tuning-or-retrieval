\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How is the advantage computed in process supervision GRPO and why use a sum of future rewards?
Question:
In the DeepSeekMath paper, for process supervision with GRPO, the advantage of each token is calculated as:
$$\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{index(j) \ge t} \tilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$$

where $\tilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$ is the normalized reward at the end of the $j$-th reasoning step. This means the advantage for token $t$ is the sum of all future step-level normalized rewards.

I understand that in outcome supervision, all tokens in an output get the same advantage (the final normalized reward). But for process supervision, I'm confused about why the advantage is a *sum* of future rewards rather than, say, the discounted sum or just the next step's reward. Is this related to the return in reinforcement learning? In standard RL, the return is typically $\sum_{k=t}^{T} \gamma^{k-t} r_k$, but here there's no discount factor $\gamma$. Why is there no discounting, and why is it a sum rather than something like GAE?
Answer:
The advantage formula $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{\mathrm{index}(j) \ge t} \tilde{r}_i^{\mathrm{index}(j)}$ in process supervision GRPO is indeed directly analogous to the return in reinforcement learning — specifically, it is the **Monte Carlo return** (undiscounted) computed from step-level rewards. Let me unpack why this particular form is used and why alternatives like discounting or GAE are absent.

**The sum of future rewards is the Monte Carlo return.** In standard RL, the return from time step $t$ is $G_t = \sum_{k=t}^{T} \gamma^{k-t} r_k$. When $\gamma = 1$, this becomes the undiscounted return $G_t = \sum_{k=t}^{T} r_k$. What GRPO computes for process supervision is exactly this, but with rewards placed at the end of each reasoning step rather than at every token. A token at position $t$ receives credit for all step-level rewards that occur at or after its position, which is the natural credit assignment: if a token contributes to a correct reasoning step downstream, it should share in that step's reward.

**Why no discount factor $\gamma$?** The reasoning chains in mathematical problem-solving are relatively short (the paper sets a max length of 1024 tokens), so the need to discount distant rewards — which in standard RL primarily addresses infinite horizons and variance reduction — is less pressing. More importantly, in this setting there is no clear notion of "temporal distance" between steps in a way that would make a step three positions later inherently less relevant than the next step. Each step is a logical unit of reasoning, and all subsequent step rewards are equally informative about whether the token at position $t$ was a good choice. Using $\gamma = 1$ (i.e., no discounting) keeps the credit assignment straightforward: every token is held accountable for all future step outcomes it influences.

**Why not GAE?** This is the crux. GAE requires a learned value function $V_\psi$ to compute TD errors $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\psi(s_{t+1}) - V_\psi(s_t)$, and then exponentially weights them: $\hat{A}_t = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma\lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}$. But the entire premise of GRPO, as the paper emphasizes, is to **forego the critic model** to reduce memory and computational burden. The paper notes that in the LLM setting, typically only the last token receives a reward from the reward model, which "may complicate the training of a value function that is accurate at each token." Without a value function, GAE is simply not available — you cannot compute TD errors without a baseline estimate. The Monte Carlo return (the sum of future rewards) is the natural alternative: it requires no bootstrapping from a learned value function, only the actual rewards observed along the trajectory.

**This generalizes outcome supervision naturally.** In outcome supervision, there is a single reward at the end of the output, and every token gets the same advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \tilde{r}_i$. Process supervision replaces this single terminal reward with multiple step-level rewards, and the advantage at each token becomes the sum of all future step rewards. If the output had only one step (the final one), the process supervision formula would reduce exactly to the outcome supervision case. So the formula is a principled generalization: outcome supervision is the special case where the return consists of a single reward.

**The normalization is across the entire group of step rewards.** Note that the paper normalizes all step rewards together: $\tilde{r}_i^{\mathrm{index}(j)} = \frac{r_i^{\mathrm{index}(j)} - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{R})}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{R})}$, where $\mathbf{R}$ includes rewards from all steps of all outputs in the group. This group-relative normalization serves the same variance-reduction purpose that a value function baseline would in PPO — but it does so without requiring a learned critic. The advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ then inherits this normalization, keeping gradient magnitudes stable across different questions and output lengths.

In summary, the sum-of-future-rewards formula is the Monte Carlo return applied to step-level rewards, chosen because GRPO's design eliminates the value function that GAE depends on. The lack of discounting reflects both the short horizon of math reasoning episodes and the logical (rather than temporal) relationship between tokens and subsequent steps. This keeps the method simple, memory-efficient, and consistent with the group-relative baseline strategy that defines GRPO.
