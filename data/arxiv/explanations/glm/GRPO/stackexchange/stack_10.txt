\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does GRPO's group-relative advantage align with reward model training on comparisons?
Question:
The DeepSeekMath paper states that 'The group relative way that GRPO leverages to calculate the advantages aligns well with the comparative nature of reward models, as reward models are typically trained on datasets of comparisons between outputs on the same question.'

I don't fully understand this claim. Reward models in RLHF are typically trained on pairwise comparisons using a Bradley-Terry model, which learns to assign higher scores to preferred outputs. But the advantage in GRPO is computed as $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{r})}$, which is a normalized scalar. How does this normalization 'align' with the comparative training of reward models? Is the paper saying that because the reward model was trained to compare outputs on the same question, the relative ranking within a group is more reliable than absolute reward values? Or is there a deeper connection I'm missing?
Answer:
Your intuition is essentially correct, and the connection is indeed about the reliability of relative versus absolute reward values. Let me elaborate on why this alignment is both intuitive and somewhat deeper than it might first appear.

Reward models in RLHF are typically trained with a Bradley-Terry (or similar) objective, which models the probability that one output is preferred over another as a function of the *difference* in their reward scores:

$$P(o_i \succ o_j \mid q) = \sigma\!\bigl(r_\varphi(q, o_i) - r_\varphi(q, o_j)\bigr)$$

The crucial property of this formulation is that the training objective is invariant to both constant shifts and positive scaling of the reward function. Adding a constant $c$ to all rewards leaves the difference $r_\varphi(q, o_i) - r_\varphi(q, o_j)$ unchanged, and scaling all rewards by a positive constant merely rescales the log-odds. What the reward model actually learns, then, is to produce correct *relative orderings and differences* between outputs on the same question—not well-calibrated absolute scores. The absolute magnitude of any single reward value is, in a meaningful sense, arbitrary.

GRPO's advantage computation directly exploits this. For a group of $G$ outputs sampled for the same question $q$, the advantage is:

$$\hat{A}_{i,t} = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})}$$

The centering operation $r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r})$ removes any constant offset in the rewards, and dividing by $\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})$ normalizes the scale. Together, these two operations extract precisely the information the reward model was optimized to produce: the relative differences among outputs responding to the same question, with the arbitrary offset and scale stripped away. This is the "alignment" the paper refers to—GRPO queries the reward model in exactly the mode for which it was trained, namely comparison between outputs on the same prompt.

This stands in contrast to standard PPO, where the advantage is computed as $A_t = r_t - V_\psi(q, o_{<t})$. Here, the absolute reward value $r_t$ carries the full signal, and the value function $V_\psi$ must independently learn to predict the expected reward scale. This is problematic for at least two reasons beyond the computational burden of training a separate value network. First, since the reward model's absolute scale is not a well-defined quantity (given the shift and scale invariances above), the value function is trying to predict something the reward model itself was never optimized to calibrate. Second, as the paper notes, in the LLM setting typically only the last token receives a reward, making it difficult to train a value function that is accurate at each intermediate token position.

There is also a practical dimension that reinforces this alignment. The paper describes an iterative RL scheme where the reward model is continually retrained on new preference data. As the reward model is updated, its absolute scale may drift, but its *comparative* judgments—what it was trained to produce—remain the reliable signal. GRPO's group-relative normalization is robust to such drift in a way that absolute-reward-based advantage computation would not be.

So the connection is not merely that "rankings within a group are more reliable than absolute values" (though that is true), but more precisely that the mathematical form of GRPO's advantage—centering and scaling within a group—mirrors the invariances of the Bradley-Terry training objective, ensuring that the policy optimization depends only on the reward model's comparative judgments and discards the uncalibrated absolute component.
