\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why does training on arXiv papers not improve mathematical reasoning, and is this conclusion trustworthy?
Question:
The DeepSeekMath paper claims that 'arXiv papers seem ineffective in improving mathematical reasoning.' They tested this by training on MathPile (85%+ arXiv) and ArXiv-RedPajama, and found no improvements or even deterioration on benchmarks like GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-STEM, and miniF2F.

This seems counterintuitive since arXiv papers contain a lot of mathematical content. The paper itself acknowledges limitations: they haven't tested the effect on tasks like theorem informalization, the effect when combined with other data types, or whether benefits would appear at larger model scales.

Given these caveats, is the conclusion that arXiv is ineffective actually well-supported? Could it be that the issue is with how they processed arXiv data (removing LaTeX preambles, comments, macros, bibliographies) rather than the content itself? Or could the problem be that arXiv papers are too dense/advanced compared to what's tested on benchmarks like GSM8K (grade-school math)? What are the possible explanations for this counterintuitive finding?
Answer:
The conclusion that arXiv papers are ineffective for mathematical reasoning is tentatively supported by the paper's experiments, but the authors themselves explicitly caution that it "should be taken with a grain of salt." The finding is based on training DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B for 150B tokens and DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B for 40B tokens on arXiv-only corpora (MathPile and ArXiv-RedPajama), with no improvements or even deterioration on GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-STEM, and miniF2F. However, the scope of this conclusion is genuinely narrow, and several plausible explanations exist for why arXiv content might underperform despite containing substantial mathematical material.

The most likely explanation is a distribution mismatch between arXiv papers and the benchmark tasks. arXiv papers contain advanced, specialized research mathematics written in dense prose for experts, while benchmarks like GSM8K test grade-school word problems and MATH tests competition-style problems requiring step-by-step procedural reasoning. These are fundamentally different cognitive tasks: reading and understanding a research paper's proof is not the same as generating a chain of reasoning to solve a novel problem. The paper notes that arXiv content didn't help on miniF2F (formal theorem proving) either, which is somewhat more surprising given that arXiv contains formal mathematics, but even here the task requires translating informal proofs into formal language—a specific skill that dense arXiv prose may not directly teach.

A second plausible explanation concerns data processing. ArXiv-RedPajama removes LaTeX preambles, comments, macros, and bibliographies. While this cleanup is reasonable, LaTeX macros often encode meaningful mathematical notation conventions, and removing them may degrade the structural coherence of mathematical expressions. MathPile uses different processing heuristics but is still $85\%+$ arXiv. The paper does not experiment with alternative arXiv processing pipelines, so we cannot rule out that better-preserved arXiv content would perform differently.

A third explanation is that arXiv papers primarily provide mathematical *knowledge* (definitions, theorems, proofs of established results) rather than the *problem-solving patterns* that benchmarks reward. A model trained on arXiv may learn to recognize and continue mathematical discourse but not necessarily to strategically decompose a novel problem into solvable steps—a skill better learned from the kind of instructional and problem-solution data that dominates the DeepSeekMath Corpus from Common Crawl.

Importantly, the paper acknowledges three specific limitations: they have not tested whether arXiv helps with tasks like theorem *informalization* (converting formal statements to informal versions), whether benefits emerge when arXiv is combined with other data types, or whether larger models might extract more value from arXiv's dense content. Notably, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B itself includes $10\%$ arXiv in its final training mixture, suggesting the authors did not entirely abandon arXiv but rather found it insufficient as a standalone training source.

In summary, the finding is well-supported within the specific experimental conditions tested, but it should be interpreted as "arXiv-only training on 1.3B and 7B models does not improve performance on these particular benchmarks" rather than the broader claim that arXiv content is universally unhelpful for mathematical reasoning. The distribution mismatch between research papers and benchmark tasks is the most compelling explanation, and the authors' own caveats suggest this question remains open for future work.
