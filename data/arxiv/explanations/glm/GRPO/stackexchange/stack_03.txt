\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why does RL improve Maj@K but not Pass@K, and what does this imply about what RL actually does?
Question:
The DeepSeekMath paper observes that after reinforcement learning, the model's Maj@K (majority voting over K samples) accuracy improves, but its Pass@K (at least one correct among K samples) accuracy does not. The paper concludes that 'the improvement is attributed to boosting the correct response from TopK rather than the enhancement of fundamental capabilities.'

I find this confusing. If Pass@K doesn't improve, that means the model isn't generating any new correct answers it couldn't before. But if Maj@K improves, the correct answers are being generated more frequently. So RL is essentially redistributing probability mass to favor correct answers that were already possible, without making the model capable of solving problems it couldn't solve before? Is this saying that RL is just doing a form of preference alignment on the output distribution, rather than improving the model's reasoning ability? If so, why does the paper frame this as a significant finding rather than a limitation?
Answer:
Your interpretation is essentially correct, and the paper itself acknowledges this framing rather than trying to oversell RL as fundamentally expanding reasoning capability.

Pass@$K$ measures whether *at least one* of $K$ sampled responses is correct. If Pass@$K$ doesn't improve after RL, the model hasn't become able to solve any problems it couldn't already solve—its "repertoire" of solvable problems is unchanged. Maj@$K$ measures whether the majority vote across $K$ samples yields the correct answer. If Maj@$K$ improves while Pass@$K$ stays flat, the correct answers (which were already occasionally generated) are now being produced *more frequently*, so they dominate the vote more often. In probability terms, RL is shifting mass onto already-reachable correct solutions rather than expanding the support of the distribution to cover new correct solutions.

The paper states this explicitly: "the improvement is attributed to boosting the correct response from Top$K$ rather than the enhancement of fundamental capabilities." It also connects this to prior work on the "misalignment problem" in SFT models (citing Wang et al., 2023), where the model's underlying reasoning ability exceeds what its output distribution reflects—SFT can cause the model to "misalign" by not consistently favoring its own correct reasoning paths. RL, in this view, acts as a form of preference alignment that corrects this misalignment, making the model more reliably produce the correct solutions it was already capable of producing.

The paper does not frame this purely as a positive finding—it is quite candid about the limitation. In Section 5.2.3 ("How to Achieve More Effective RL?"), the authors identify the likely cause: their RL pipeline only uses questions from the instruction tuning stage with naive nucleus sampling for exploration. They suggest this is "a potential reason that our RL pipeline only improves the Maj@$K$ performance" and propose that out-of-distribution question prompts combined with advanced decoding strategies (like tree search) could push RL to expand Pass@$K$ as well. They further warn that without better reward model generalization, "reinforcement learning may merely stabilize the distribution of LLMs rather than improve their fundamental capabilities."

So the significance of the finding is diagnostic rather than celebratory: it pinpoints exactly what current RL is doing (distribution sharpening over existing capability) and what it is not doing (capability expansion), which directly motivates the paper's proposed future directions around better data sources, robust algorithms, and stronger reward models. The finding is significant because it clarifies the mechanism behind RL gains in mathematical reasoning and identifies a concrete gap that future work needs to close.
