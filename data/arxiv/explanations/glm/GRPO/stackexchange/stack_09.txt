\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### What is the difference between online and offline sampling in the unified paradigm, and why does online win in later training?
Question:
In the DeepSeekMath paper's discussion of the unified paradigm, they distinguish between 'online sampling' (training data from the real-time policy model) and 'offline sampling' (training data from the initial SFT model). RFT and DPO are offline, while Online RFT and GRPO are online.

The paper finds that Online RFT significantly outperforms RFT, especially in the later stages of training. The explanation given is that 'in the initial stage, the actor and the SFT model exhibit close resemblance, with the sampled data revealing only minor differences. In the later stage, the data sampled from the actor will exhibit more significant differences, and real-time data sampling will offer greater advantages.'

I understand the intuition, but I'm unclear on the mechanism. If the policy model has already improved from the SFT model, wouldn't sampling from it just produce better outputs? How does training on better outputs from the current model lead to *more* improvement compared to training on outputs from the SFT model? Is it about the distribution mismatch between what the model currently generates and what the SFT model generated?
Answer:
The distinction between online and offline sampling is fundamentally about **distribution match** between the training data and the current policy, not about the quality of the sampled outputs per se.

In the unified paradigm, the gradient of any training method takes the form:

$$\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[(q,o) \sim \mathcal{D}\right]\left( \frac{1}{|o|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o|} GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q, o, t, \pi_{rf}) \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t})\right)$$

The gradient $\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t})$ pushes the model to increase the probability of tokens in the sampled output $o$. The question is: where does $o$ come from? With offline sampling (RFT, DPO), $o$ is drawn once from the initial SFT model $\pi_{\text{SFT}}$ and reused throughout training. With online sampling (Online RFT, GRPO), $o$ is drawn from the current policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ at each training step.

Early in training, $\pi_{\theta} \approx \pi_{\text{SFT}}$, so the two data sources yield similar outputs and the distinction barely matters — the paper confirms that Online RFT and RFT are "comparable in the early stage." But as training progresses, $\pi_{\theta}$ diverges from $\pi_{\text{SFT}}$. The SFT-sampled outputs increasingly represent a distribution that the current model no longer generates. This is where the mechanism kicks in.

The key insight is that training on data sampled from a *different* distribution than the current policy is **off-policy**, and the gradients become increasingly misaligned. You are reinforcing reasoning paths and token sequences that the current model would not naturally produce. Some of these may correspond to modes the model has already moved away from; the gradient updates are spent pushing the model back toward patterns it has already abandoned, rather than sharpening its current correct behaviors. In contrast, when you sample online, the correct outputs you filter for are ones the model actually generates now — reinforcing them directly increases the probability of correct outputs within the model's current support.

There is also an **exploration** dimension. As the policy improves, it may discover correct solution strategies that the SFT model never produced. Offline sampling is frozen and can never capture these new correct patterns. Online sampling continuously reflects the model's evolving capability, including newly discovered correct approaches, and can reinforce them.

This is analogous to the classic on-policy vs. off-policy distinction in reinforcement learning. Off-policy data becomes stale as the policy diverges from the behavior policy that generated it, and the importance sampling ratio $\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{SFT}}(o_t | q, o_{<t})}$ deviates further from $1$. While RFT does not explicitly use this ratio (it simply fine-tunes on filtered correct outputs), the same distributional mismatch degrades the usefulness of the gradient signal. The model is being trained to produce outputs that are increasingly out-of-distribution relative to what it actually generates, making the updates less efficient and potentially pushing the model in suboptimal directions.

So to directly answer your question: it is not that the current model produces "better" outputs that somehow lead to more improvement. Both methods filter for correctness, so quality is controlled. The advantage of online sampling is that the training data remains **on-policy** — it reflects what the model currently generates, so the gradient updates are directly reinforcing and sharpening the model's own correct output distribution rather than imposing an increasingly foreign distribution on it. The growing distribution mismatch you suspected is exactly the mechanism at work.
