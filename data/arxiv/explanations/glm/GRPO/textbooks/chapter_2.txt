\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 2: Data Collection and the DeepSeekMath Corpus

## The Need for High-Quality Math Web Data

Mathematical reasoning poses a unique challenge for large language models due to its structured and complex nature. Unlike general language tasks, mathematical reasoning requires precise logical steps, an understanding of abstract concepts, and the ability to perform reliable computations. To build language models capable of sophisticated mathematical reasoning, the quantity and quality of the training data become paramount. The DeepSeekMath project recognized that the publicly accessible Common Crawl contains valuable mathematical content, but this content is buried within an enormous sea of web pages spanning every conceivable topic. The challenge, then, is not merely one of data collection but of data selection—identifying and extracting the mathematically relevant content from the broader web while maintaining high quality standards.

Prior efforts to construct mathematical training corpora existed but were limited in scale. Minerva, a closed-source model built on PaLM, used mathematical web pages that were significantly smaller in scale than what DeepSeekMath aimed to collect. OpenWebMath, a recently released dataset, contained 13.6 billion tokens of mathematical content filtered from Common Crawl. MathPile aggregated 8.9 billion tokens from multiple sources, with the majority (over 85\%) coming from arXiv papers. Proof-Pile-2 combined OpenWebMath with AlgebraicStack (10.3 billion tokens of mathematical code) and arXiv papers (28.0 billion tokens). The DeepSeekMath team sought to build a corpus that would be substantially larger than all of these—targeting 120 billion tokens—and of higher quality, sourced primarily from Common Crawl.

The motivation for this ambitious data collection effort was grounded in a key insight: the number of model parameters is not the only factor determining mathematical reasoning capability. If high-quality data is available in sufficient quantity, a smaller model can achieve performance comparable to a much larger one trained on less optimal data. The DeepSeekMath-Base 7B model, trained on this corpus, would eventually achieve 64.2\% on GSM8K and 36.2\% on the competition-level MATH dataset, rivaling Minerva 540B—a model 77 times larger. This result underscores the critical role that data quality and scale play in enabling mathematical reasoning, and it motivated the meticulous data selection pipeline that the DeepSeekMath team developed.

An additional consideration was linguistic diversity. Previous mathematical corpora were primarily English-centric, which limited improvements on non-English mathematical benchmarks. The DeepSeekMath team observed that by including high-quality non-English mathematical content—predominantly Chinese—they could achieve improvements on Chinese mathematical benchmarks such as CMATH and AGIEval, whereas existing English-centric corpora showed limited improvement or even hindered performance in Chinese. This multilingual dimension of the corpus became an important design goal from the outset.

## Initial Seed Corpus and fastText Classifier Training

The data collection pipeline begins with a seed corpus: a small but high-quality collection of mathematical text that serves as the starting point for identifying more mathematical content across the web. The DeepSeekMath team chose OpenWebMath as their initial seed corpus. OpenWebMath is a collection of high-quality mathematical web texts that had already been curated for mathematical relevance, making it an ideal starting point for training a classifier that could identify similar content elsewhere in Common Crawl.

The core tool for this classification is fastText, an open-source library developed for efficient text classification and representation learning. The approach is conceptually elegant: train a fastText classifier to distinguish between mathematical and non-mathematical web pages, then use this classifier to scan the vast Common Crawl and recall pages that resemble the mathematical content in the seed corpus. The classifier learns to associate certain textual patterns, vocabulary, and structural features with mathematical content, and then applies this learned knowledge to identify new mathematical pages that were not in the original seed.

To train the fastText classifier, the team randomly selected 500,000 data points from the OpenWebMath seed corpus as positive training examples—examples the classifier should learn to recognize as mathematical. For negative examples, they selected 500,000 web pages from Common Crawl that were not specifically identified as mathematical, providing the classifier with contrastive examples to learn what non-mathematical content looks like. This balanced training set of one million examples allowed the classifier to learn meaningful discriminative features.

The training configuration was carefully specified. The vector dimension was set to 256, meaning each text was represented as a 256-dimensional vector in the classifier's embedding space. The learning rate was set to 0.1, controlling how quickly the model's parameters were updated during training. The maximum length of word n-grams was set to 3, allowing the classifier to capture local word order information (sequences of up to three consecutive words) rather than treating words as isolated units. The minimum number of word occurrences was set to 3, filtering out extremely rare words that might not provide useful signal. The number of training epochs was set to 3, meaning the classifier iterated over the full training data three times. These hyperparameters were chosen to balance training efficiency and classification accuracy, and they proved effective for the task of identifying mathematical content in web pages.

Once trained, the fastText classifier assigns a score to each web page, indicating the likelihood that the page contains mathematical content. Pages with higher scores are more likely to be mathematical, and this scoring mechanism becomes the basis for filtering and ranking collected pages in subsequent steps of the pipeline.

## The Iterative Data Collection Pipeline

The process of collecting mathematical data from Common Crawl is not a single-pass operation but an iterative pipeline that progressively expands both the classifier's capabilities and the resulting corpus. The iterative nature of the approach is essential because the initial seed corpus, while high-quality, may not capture the full diversity of mathematical content on the web. Each iteration refines the classifier using expanded seed data, allowing it to identify mathematical content that was missed in previous iterations.

The pipeline begins with the full Common Crawl, which contains an enormous number of web pages. To make this dataset more manageable, the team first applied URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication techniques, resulting in a reduced set of 40 billion HTML web pages. Deduplication is important because Common Crawl contains many duplicate or near-duplicate pages (due to content mirroring, syndication, or simple redundancy), and processing these duplicates would waste computational resources without adding value to the corpus.

With the deduplicated Common Crawl in hand, the trained fastText classifier is used to recall mathematical web pages. The classifier processes each page and assigns a mathematical relevance score. However, not all pages that the classifier identifies as mathematical are of equal quality. To filter out low-quality mathematical content, the team ranks the collected pages according to their fastText scores and only preserves the top-ranking ones. This ranking-based filtering ensures that the corpus contains the most confidently mathematical content while excluding pages that might only marginally resemble mathematical text.

The question of how much data to preserve is itself an empirical one. The team assessed the optimal volume of data to keep through pre-training experiments, testing the top 40B, 80B, 120B, and 160B tokens. These experiments involved training a language model on each subset and evaluating downstream performance on mathematical benchmarks. In the first iteration, the team chose to keep the top 40 billion tokens, a conservative choice that prioritized precision over recall—ensuring that the initial corpus was of the highest possible quality.

After the first iteration, a significant amount of mathematical content on the web remained uncollected. This was primarily because the fastText classifier was trained on a seed corpus (OpenWebMath) that, while high-quality, lacked sufficient diversity to capture all the different forms and styles in which mathematical content appears online. Mathematical content can be found in online forums, educational platforms, research repositories, and many other types of websites, each with different conventions and presentation styles. A classifier trained on a narrow set of examples will inevitably miss some of this diversity.

This limitation motivates the iterative approach: each iteration expands the seed corpus with new positive examples, retraining the classifier to recognize a broader range of mathematical content. The pipeline repeats this process of classifier training, data collection, ranking, and filtering across multiple iterations. After four iterations, the team had collected 35.5 million mathematical web pages totaling 120 billion tokens. The decision to stop at four iterations was data-driven: in the fourth iteration, the team noticed that nearly 98\% of the mathematical data had already been collected in the third iteration, indicating that the classifier had reached a point of diminishing returns where additional iterations would yield minimal new data.

The iterative pipeline demonstrates a powerful principle in data engineering for machine learning: rather than attempting to build a perfect classifier from the outset, one can progressively improve both the classifier and the dataset through repeated cycles of training, collection, and refinement. This approach is particularly well-suited to domains like mathematical text, where the space of relevant content is diverse and difficult to characterize comprehensively from a small initial sample.

## Domain-Based Expansion and Human Annotation

A critical innovation in the iterative pipeline is the domain-based expansion strategy, which systematically identifies new mathematical content sources to enrich the seed corpus between iterations. This approach addresses the core limitation of the initial seed corpus—its lack of diversity—by leveraging the structure of the web itself to discover new mathematical content.

The approach begins by organizing the entire Common Crawl into disjoint domains, where a domain is defined as the set of web pages sharing the same base URL. For example, all pages on "mathoverflow.net" would constitute one domain, while all pages on "wikipedia.org" would constitute another. This domain-level organization allows the team to analyze the web at a granularity that reveals which websites are likely to be sources of mathematical content.

For each domain, the team calculates the percentage of web pages that were collected in the first iteration of the pipeline. This percentage serves as a signal: if a high proportion of pages from a particular domain were identified as mathematical, it is likely that the domain as a whole is a mathematical content source. The threshold for classification was set at 10\%—domains where over 10\% of web pages had been collected in the first iteration were classified as math-related. For example, the domain "mathoverflow.net" would likely exceed this threshold, as a large fraction of its content is mathematical in nature.

Once math-related domains are identified, the next step is human annotation. The team manually annotates the URLs within these identified domains that are associated with mathematical content. For example, within the domain "mathoverflow.net," the URL path "mathoverflow.net/questions" leads to mathematical questions and would be annotated as mathematical. This human judgment is important because not all content within a math-related domain is necessarily mathematical—for instance, a website might have mathematical content in one section and administrative or general content in another. Human annotation ensures that only genuinely mathematical URLs are added to the seed corpus.

Web pages linked to these annotated URLs that were not collected in the previous iteration are then added to the seed corpus as new positive examples. This expanded seed corpus, now containing a more diverse set of mathematical content, is used to retrain the fastText classifier. The improved classifier, having learned from a broader range of examples, is capable of recalling more mathematical data in the subsequent iteration—capturing content that the previous version of the classifier missed.

This domain-based expansion strategy is notable for its efficiency and scalability. Rather than relying on manual browsing of the web to discover new mathematical sources, the approach uses the collection results from the first iteration as a signal to identify promising domains automatically. Human effort is then focused where it is most valuable: annotating specific URLs within already-identified math-related domains. This division of labor between automated classification and human judgment allows the pipeline to scale to the enormous size of Common Crawl while maintaining high quality standards.

The iterative refinement through domain-based expansion also illustrates a broader principle: the quality of a classifier depends critically on the diversity and representativeness of its training data. By systematically expanding the seed corpus to cover more of the mathematical web, the team ensured that the classifier learned to recognize mathematical content in its many forms, rather than overfitting to the particular characteristics of the initial seed corpus.

## Data Decontamination Techniques

A fundamental concern in building training corpora for language models is benchmark contamination: the inclusion of evaluation data in the training set. If a model is trained on the same questions or answers that appear in the benchmarks used to evaluate it, the reported performance may be artificially inflated and not representative of the model's true capabilities. This is particularly problematic in mathematical reasoning, where benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH contain specific problems that may appear in online educational resources, forum discussions, or other web pages.

To avoid benchmark contamination, the DeepSeekMath team applied filtering techniques following procedures established in prior work (specifically, the DeepSeek-Coder project). The filtering criteria are designed to remove web pages that contain questions or answers from English mathematical benchmarks (GSM8K and MATH) and Chinese benchmarks (CMATH and AGIEval).

The primary filtering technique is based on n-gram matching. An n-gram is a contiguous sequence of $n$ items (in this case, words or tokens) from a given text. The team uses 10-gram matching as the standard criterion: any text segment containing a 10-gram string that matches exactly with any substring from the evaluation benchmarks is removed from the math training corpus. This means that if a web page contains a sequence of 10 consecutive words that also appear consecutively in a benchmark question or answer, that web page is filtered out. The choice of 10 grams is a balance: a longer n-gram would be more specific (reducing false positives) but might miss shorter contaminated segments, while a shorter n-gram would catch more potential contamination but might also remove legitimately useful mathematical content that happens to share phrasing with benchmark items.

For benchmark texts that are shorter than 10 grams but have at least 3 grams, the team employs exact matching. This means that if a benchmark question or answer is between 3 and 9 grams in length, the entire text must match exactly for the web page to be filtered. This handles edge cases where benchmark items are too short for meaningful 10-gram matching but still need to be excluded.

The decontamination process is an essential quality assurance step. Without it, the impressive benchmark scores reported for models trained on the corpus could be misleading, as the model might have simply memorized the evaluation questions rather than learning genuine mathematical reasoning. By removing contaminated web pages, the team ensures that the benchmark performance reflects the model's ability to generalize to unseen mathematical problems, which is the true measure of mathematical reasoning capability.

It is worth noting that decontamination is not a perfect process—there may be paraphrased versions of benchmark questions that evade exact n-gram matching, or the benchmarks themselves might evolve over time. However, the n-gram-based approach provides a strong and practical safeguard against the most common and problematic form of contamination: the verbatim inclusion of benchmark questions and answers in training data.

## Validating Corpus Quality Against Existing Datasets

To assess the quality of the DeepSeekMath Corpus, the team conducted pre-training experiments comparing it against three recently released mathematical training corpora: MathPile, OpenWebMath, and Proof-Pile-2. This comparison was essential to demonstrate that the extensive data collection pipeline produced a corpus that was not merely larger but also of higher quality than existing alternatives.

The three comparison corpora represent different approaches to building mathematical training data. MathPile is a multi-source corpus of 8.9 billion tokens aggregated from textbooks, Wikipedia, ProofWiki, CommonCrawl, StackExchange, and arXiv, with the majority (over 85\%) sourced from arXiv. OpenWebMath consists of CommonCrawl data filtered for mathematical content, totaling 13.6 billion tokens. Proof-Pile-2 is a mathematical corpus combining OpenWebMath, AlgebraicStack (10.3 billion tokens of mathematical code), and arXiv papers (28.0 billion tokens). When experimenting with Proof-Pile-2, the team followed the recommended arXiv:Web:Code ratio of 2:4:1.

The experimental setup was designed for a fair comparison. The team applied math training to a general pre-trained language model with 1.3 billion parameters (DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B), which shares the same framework as the DeepSeek LLMs. Each mathematical corpus was used to train a separate model for 150 billion tokens, ensuring that all models were trained on the same volume of data regardless of the corpus's original size. If a corpus was smaller than 150 billion tokens, it was repeated during training. All experiments used the HAI-LLM training framework, the AdamW optimizer with $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.95$, and $\mathrm{weight\_decay}=0.1$, and a multi-step learning rate schedule. The learning rate reached its peak of $5.3 \times 10^{-4}$ after 2,000 warmup steps, decreased to 31.6\% of the peak after 80\% of training, and further decreased to 10.0\% of the peak after 90\% of training. A batch size of 4 million tokens with a 4K context length was used.

The evaluation results revealed three key properties of the DeepSeekMath Corpus. First, it is high-quality: the model trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus demonstrated a clear performance lead across 8 mathematical benchmarks using few-shot chain-of-thought prompting. Notably, the model trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus showed better performance than the model trained on Proof-Pile-2 at the 50-billion-token mark (which corresponds to one full epoch of Proof-Pile-2), indicating that the average quality per token of the DeepSeekMath Corpus is higher.

Second, the corpus is multilingual. The DeepSeekMath Corpus encompasses data in multiple languages, predominantly English and Chinese. Training on the DeepSeekMath Corpus enhanced mathematical reasoning performance in both languages. In contrast, existing corpora—being primarily English-centric—showed limited improvement and sometimes even hindered performance on Chinese mathematical benchmarks. This multilingual property is a direct result of the data collection pipeline, which did not restrict collection to English-only sources but instead identified mathematical content across the web regardless of language.

Third, the corpus is large-scale. At 120 billion tokens, the DeepSeekMath Corpus is several times larger than existing mathematical corpora. This scale advantage is reflected in the learning curves: the model trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus showed a steeper learning curve with more lasting improvements, while the baseline corpora—being much smaller—were repeated multiple times during training, causing model performance to quickly reach a plateau. This observation underscores the importance of data scale: even with high-quality data, a corpus that is too small will limit the model's learning potential. The DeepSeekMath Corpus, by combining high quality with large scale, provides the foundation for training models with strong mathematical reasoning capabilities.

These validation experiments confirm that the iterative data collection pipeline, with its careful classifier training, domain-based expansion, and decontamination procedures, produces a corpus that is superior to existing alternatives in terms of quality, linguistic coverage, and scale. The DeepSeekMath Corpus thus represents a significant contribution to the resources available for training mathematical language models, demonstrating that publicly available web data contains substantial valuable mathematical content when properly identified and filtered.
