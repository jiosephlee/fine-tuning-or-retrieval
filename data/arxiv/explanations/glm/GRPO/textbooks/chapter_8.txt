\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 8: A Unified Paradigm for Reinforcement Learning

## The Unified Paradigm: Data Source, Reward Function, and Algorithm

The paper presents a unified paradigm that conceptualizes a variety of training methods—Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), Online RFT, and PPO/GRPO—within a single analytical framework. The central idea is that the gradient of any training method's objective with respect to the model parameters $\theta$ can be decomposed into three interpretable components. Formally, the gradient is expressed as:

$$\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[\underbrace{(q,o) \sim \mathcal{D}}_{\text{Data Source}}\right]\left(\frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o|}\underbrace{GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q, o, t, \pi_{rf})}_{\text{Gradient Coefficient}}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t})\right).$$

This equation reveals three key components that distinguish one training method from another. The first is the **Data Source** $\mathcal{D}$, which determines where the training data—consisting of question-output pairs $(q, o)$—comes from. The second is the **Reward Function** $\pi_{rf}$, which is the source of the training reward signal that guides learning. The third is the **Algorithm** $\mathcal{A}$, which processes the training data and the reward signal to produce the gradient coefficient $GC$, a scalar that determines the magnitude of reinforcement or penalization applied to each data point during the parameter update.

To understand how different methods fit into this paradigm, consider each in turn. SFT fine-tunes a pretrained model on human-selected instruction-tuning data, where the data source is a fixed dataset of curated question-solution pairs and the gradient coefficients are uniform and positive. RFT extends SFT by sampling outputs from the already-trained SFT model, filtering them based on answer correctness, and then fine-tuning on the correct ones. DPO refines the SFT model by fine-tuning on augmented outputs sampled from the SFT model using a pairwise preference loss. Online RFT differs from RFT in that it samples outputs from the real-time policy model during training rather than from the fixed SFT model. PPO and GRPO initialize from the SFT model and reinforce the policy using outputs sampled from the real-time policy model, with gradient coefficients determined by a learned reward model rather than simple correctness filtering. The key insight of this paradigm is that all these methods can be understood as either direct or simplified reinforcement learning techniques, differing only in their choices of data source, reward function, and algorithm.

## Analysis of Data Source: Online vs. Offline Sampling

Within the unified paradigm, the data source component can be categorized into two styles: **online sampling** and **offline sampling**. Online sampling refers to generating training data from the real-time policy model as it is being trained—the exploration results come from the current state of the model itself. Offline sampling, in contrast, refers to generating training data from the initial SFT model, meaning the training data is fixed and does not evolve as the policy model improves. Among the methods analyzed, RFT and DPO follow the offline style, while Online RFT and GRPO follow the online style.

The paper provides experimental evidence comparing these two approaches. The results show that Online RFT significantly outperforms RFT on two benchmarks. In the early stages of training, Online RFT and RFT perform comparably, which is intuitive because at the beginning of training, the policy model and the SFT model are nearly identical, so the data they sample reveals only minor differences. However, as training progresses into later stages, the data sampled from the real-time policy model diverges more substantially from the data sampled from the SFT model. At this point, the online approach gains a clear and growing advantage because it exposes the model to data that reflects its current capabilities and weaknesses, allowing for more targeted and effective learning. This finding demonstrates the superiority of online training: as the model improves, it benefits from continuously refreshing the training data to match its evolving output distribution, something that offline methods cannot do.

## Analysis of Gradient Coefficients: Rule vs. Model Rewards

The gradient coefficient is the mechanism through which the algorithm translates reward signals into updates for the model parameters. The paper distinguishes between two types of reward functions used to compute gradient coefficients: **Rule** and **Model**. A rule-based reward function judges the quality of a response based on the correctness of the answer—essentially a binary signal indicating whether the output arrived at the right answer. A model-based reward function uses a trained reward model to assign a continuous score to each response, providing a more nuanced signal.

A critical difference between GRPO and Online RFT lies in how they use the reward signal to construct gradient coefficients. GRPO adjusts its gradient coefficient based on the actual reward value provided by the reward model, which allows it to differentially reinforce and penalize responses according to their varying magnitudes of quality. In other words, a response that is barely correct receives a smaller positive gradient coefficient than one that is clearly and elegantly correct, and an incorrect response receives a negative gradient coefficient. In contrast, Online RFT lacks this feature: it does not penalize incorrect responses at all, and it uniformly reinforces all correct responses with the same level of intensity, regardless of how good or marginal the response is.

The experimental results demonstrate that GRPO surpasses Online RFT, highlighting the efficiency of using gradient coefficients that can vary in both sign and magnitude. This finding suggests that the ability to apply differential reinforcement and penalization—rather than just filtering for correctness—is a key factor in the effectiveness of reinforcement learning for mathematical reasoning.

The paper further explores the distinction between **outcome supervision** and **process supervision** within GRPO. In outcome supervision, a reward is provided only at the end of each output, and the advantage for all tokens in that output is set to the normalized reward. In process supervision, a reward is provided at the end of each reasoning step, and the advantage for each token is computed as the sum of normalized rewards from all subsequent steps. The results show that GRPO with process supervision (GRPO+PS) outperforms GRPO with outcome supervision (GRPO+OS), indicating the benefits of using fine-grained, step-aware gradient coefficients that can provide more precise learning signals for multi-step reasoning tasks.

Additionally, the paper explores **iterative RL**, in which the reward model is continually updated using new training data generated from the policy model, combined with a replay mechanism that incorporates 10% of historical data. The reference model is reset to the current policy model at each iteration. Experiments with two rounds of iteration show that iterative RL significantly improves performance, with the largest gains occurring at the first iteration.

## Why RL Works: Pass@K vs. Maj@K Accuracy

To understand why reinforcement learning improves performance over instruction-tuned models, the paper evaluates two metrics: **Pass@K** and **Maj@K** accuracy. Pass@K measures the model's fundamental capability by sampling $K$ outputs and checking whether at least one of them is correct—it captures the best-case performance across multiple attempts. Maj@K, on the other hand, measures the model's robustness by sampling $K$ outputs and taking a majority vote—if the majority of outputs converge on the correct answer, it is counted as correct. Maj@K thus reflects the model's ability to consistently produce correct answers, not just occasionally stumble upon them.

The experimental results reveal a striking pattern: RL enhances Maj@K performance but does not improve Pass@K performance. This finding has a profound implication for understanding what reinforcement learning actually does to the model. If RL improved Pass@K, it would mean that the model's fundamental reasoning capabilities were being enhanced—it was gaining the ability to solve problems it previously could not. Instead, the improvement in Maj@K without improvement in Pass@K indicates that RL works by making the output distribution more robust: it boosts the probability of correct responses that were already in the model's Top-K outputs, making them appear more consistently and dominate over incorrect alternatives.

In other words, the improvement is attributed to boosting the correct response from the Top-K candidates rather than enhancing the model's fundamental capabilities. The model already "knows" how to solve many problems—evidenced by its Pass@K performance—but without RL, it does not reliably produce the correct solution as its most frequent output. RL shifts the probability mass toward those correct solutions, aligning the model's most probable outputs with its best outputs.

This finding also connects to what the paper identifies as a **misalignment problem** in reasoning tasks within SFT models. Prior work has shown that the reasoning performance of SFT models can be improved through preference alignment strategies, suggesting that SFT models suffer from a misalignment between their most likely outputs and their correct outputs. RL addresses this misalignment by reinforcing correct reasoning paths and suppressing incorrect ones, thereby improving the consistency and reliability of the model's responses.

## Future Directions: Advanced Sampling, Robust Algorithms, and Reward Models

The paper identifies several potential future directions for achieving more effective reinforcement learning, organized around the three key components of the unified paradigm: data source, algorithms, and reward functions.

Regarding the **data source**, the paper notes that its RL pipeline only uses questions from the instruction tuning stage and employs naive nucleus sampling to generate outputs. This limitation may explain why the RL pipeline only improves Maj@K performance rather than Pass@K—the model is never exposed to sufficiently novel or challenging data that would push the boundaries of its fundamental capabilities. The paper suggests two promising directions. First, exploring the RL pipeline on **out-of-distribution question prompts**—questions that differ from those seen during instruction tuning—could force the model to generalize beyond its training distribution. Second, combining this with **advanced sampling (decoding) strategies**, such as tree-search methods, could enable more systematic exploration of the solution space. Additionally, **efficient inference techniques**, such as speculative decoding, play a crucial role because they determine the exploration efficiency of policy models—how quickly and broadly the model can sample diverse outputs during training.

Regarding **algorithms**, the paper observes that current methods fully trust the signal from the reward function to increase or decrease the conditional probability of certain tokens. However, it is impossible to guarantee that the reward signal is always reliable, especially in extremely complex tasks. The paper cites the example of the PRM800K dataset, which, despite being carefully annotated by well-trained human annotators, still contains approximately 20% incorrect annotations. This unreliability of reward signals motivates the exploration of reinforcement learning algorithms that are **robust against noisy reward signals**. The paper suggests that **weak-to-strong alignment methods**—techniques that can learn effectively even from imperfect or noisy supervision—could bring a fundamental change to learning algorithms, allowing RL to remain effective even when the reward signal is unreliable.

Regarding **reward functions**, the paper identifies three important directions. First, **enhancing the generalization ability of the reward model** is critical. The reward model must generalize effectively to handle out-of-distribution questions and advanced decoding outputs. If it cannot, reinforcement learning may merely stabilize the output distribution of the language model rather than improve its fundamental capabilities—the model would learn to produce outputs that the reward model scores highly, but those outputs might not actually be better if the reward model itself is unreliable on novel data. Second, **reflecting the uncertainty of the reward model** could serve as a bridge between weak reward models and weak-to-strong learning algorithms. If the reward model can express how confident it is in its own scores, the learning algorithm can weight those scores accordingly, placing more trust in high-confidence signals and less in low-confidence ones. Third, **building high-quality process reward models** that can provide fine-grained, step-level training signals for the reasoning process remains an important challenge. Process supervision has already been shown to outperform outcome supervision, but constructing process reward models at scale with high accuracy is difficult and remains an open area of research.
