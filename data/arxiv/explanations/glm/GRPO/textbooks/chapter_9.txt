\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 9: Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Work

#
Summary of DeepSeekMath's Achievements

The DeepSeekMath project represents a significant milestone in the development of open-source language models capable of advanced mathematical reasoning. As detailed in the paper, DeepSeekMath is initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-v1.5 7B and undergoes continual training over 500 billion tokens. A cornerstone of this training is the inclusion of 120 billion math-related tokens meticulously sourced from Common Crawl, forming the DeepSeekMath Corpus. Through this rigorous pre-training and subsequent fine-tuning stages, DeepSeekMath outperforms all existing open-source models on the competition-level MATH benchmark and approaches the performance levels of proprietary, closed-source models such as GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra.

A crucial achievement of this research is the demonstration that publicly available web data holds immense, largely untapped potential for constructing high-quality mathematical training corpora. The authors' extensive ablation studies reveal that carefully filtered web pages provide superior mathematical training material, whereas arXiv papers—often assumed to be a gold standard for technical training data—do not significantly improve mathematical reasoning. This finding challenges existing assumptions in the field and highlights the effectiveness of the authors' data selection pipeline.

Furthermore, the paper introduces Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a novel reinforcement learning algorithm designed as a variant of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). GRPO enhances mathematical reasoning capabilities while significantly reducing memory consumption by eliminating the need for a critic model. Instead, it estimates a baseline from group scores. The experimental results show that GRPO is remarkably effective; it yields substantial performance gains even when applied to DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, a model that had already achieved high benchmark scores prior to reinforcement learning. By providing a unified paradigm to understand various training methods, the authors also lay the groundwork for conceptualizing these diverse techniques as either direct or simplified reinforcement learning strategies.

#
Limitations in Geometry, Theorem Proving, and Few-Shot Learning

Despite its impressive performance on quantitative reasoning benchmarks, DeepSeekMath exhibits notable limitations in specific mathematical domains, particularly in geometry and theorem proving. The authors acknowledge that the model's capabilities in these areas are relatively weaker when compared to leading closed-source models. For instance, during dry runs, DeepSeekMath struggled to handle problems specifically related to triangles and ellipses. This indicates a gap in the model's geometric reasoning skills, which are essential for a comprehensive mathematical understanding. The paper highlights this discrepancy to provide a transparent assessment of the model's boundaries and to guide future research efforts.

In addition to domain-specific limitations, DeepSeekMath faces constraints regarding its few-shot learning capabilities. Few-shot learning refers to the ability of a model to improve its performance when provided with a few examples in the prompt. The authors observed that DeepSeekMath's performance remains largely similar whether evaluated in a zero-shot or few-shot setting. In contrast, larger closed-source models like GPT-4 demonstrate a marked improvement in performance when given few-shot inputs. This discrepancy suggests that the scale of the model plays a critical role in its ability to leverage in-context examples effectively. Because DeepSeekMath is restricted by its 7B parameter scale, it lacks the capacity to fully utilize few-shot prompts to enhance its reasoning, putting it at a disadvantage compared to substantially larger state-of-the-art models.

#
Data Selection Bias and Model Scale Constraints

The limitations observed in DeepSeekMath can be traced back to two primary factors: data selection bias and model scale constraints. The inability to solve problems involving specific geometric concepts, such as triangles and ellipses, points directly to biases present in the training data. The data selection pipeline, while highly effective at gathering general mathematical web pages, may have inadvertently underrepresented or poorly filtered content related to these specific geometric topics. Consequently, the model did not acquire sufficient knowledge to tackle these problems during the pre-training and fine-tuning phases. This highlights a broader challenge in machine learning: the quality and composition of the training data fundamentally dictate the model's strengths and weaknesses.

Model scale constraints further compound these limitations. DeepSeekMath is built on a 7B parameter architecture, which is relatively modest compared to the massive scale of closed-source models like GPT-4. The restricted scale directly impacts the model's capacity to generalize and adapt to new information, as evidenced by its few-shot learning performance. Larger models typically possess greater representational capacity, allowing them to internalize complex patterns and adapt their behavior based on a few in-context examples. The authors' observations underscore the trade-offs in developing smaller, open-source models: while they achieve remarkable efficiency and accessibility, they may fall short in tasks that require the extensive flexibility and few-shot adaptability afforded by larger models.

#
Future Work in Data Engineering and RL Techniques

To address the current limitations and push the boundaries of mathematical reasoning in language models, the authors outline several promising directions for future work. A primary focus is the refinement of the engineered data selection pipeline. By improving the methods used to identify, filter, and process mathematical content from the web, researchers can construct more comprehensive and balanced pre-training corpora. This includes ensuring adequate representation of diverse mathematical fields, such as geometry, to mitigate data selection biases. Enhancing the quality and diversity of the training data is a fundamental step toward improving the model's baseline capabilities in all areas of mathematics.

In addition to data engineering, the authors identify significant opportunities for advancing reinforcement learning techniques. Within their unified paradigm, they emphasize three key areas for future RL research: data source, algorithms, and reward functions. For the data source, they suggest exploring out-of-distribution question prompts and integrating advanced sampling or decoding strategies, such as tree-search methods, alongside efficient inference techniques. This could help models explore a wider range of problem-solving paths. 

Regarding algorithms, the authors note that current methods place full trust in the reward signal to adjust the conditional probability of tokens. However, reward signals are often noisy and unreliable, especially in complex tasks. Future work should explore algorithms that are robust against noisy reward signals, potentially leveraging weak-to-strong alignment methods to improve learning reliability. Finally, for reward functions, the authors highlight the need to enhance the generalization ability of reward models so they can handle out-of-distribution questions. They also suggest exploring methods to reflect the uncertainty of the reward model and developing efficient, high-quality process reward models that can provide fine-grained training signals. By pursuing these directions, future iterations of mathematical language models can achieve more robust, accurate, and comprehensive reasoning capabilities.
