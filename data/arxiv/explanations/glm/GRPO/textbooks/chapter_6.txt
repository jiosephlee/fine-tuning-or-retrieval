\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 6: Reinforcement Learning Training and Evaluation

The application of reinforcement learning (RL) to large language models represents a critical stage in the development of DeepSeekMath, transforming an already capable instruction-tuned model into one that approaches the performance of frontier closed-source systems. In this chapter, we examine how Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), the algorithm introduced in the previous chapter, is concretely applied to produce DeepSeekMath-RL. We trace the entire pipeline from the construction and training of reward models, through the specific training configuration employed, to the evaluation results that validate the effectiveness of RL on both in-domain and out-of-domain mathematical reasoning tasks. The results reveal a striking phenomenon: training on a narrowly scoped subset of instruction-tuning data yields broad, generalized improvements across a wide spectrum of mathematical benchmarks, underscoring the power of RL to align and stabilize the output distribution of language models.

#

## Training Setup for DeepSeekMath-RL

The reinforcement learning phase of DeepSeekMath is built upon DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, the instruction-tuned model described in earlier chapters. This choice is deliberate: the instruction-tuned model already possesses strong mathematical reasoning capabilities, having been exposed to a diverse array of chain-of-thought (CoT), program-of-thought (PoT), and tool-integrated reasoning data during supervised fine-tuning. By initializing the RL phase from this strong starting point, the reinforcement learning algorithm can focus on refining and stabilizing the model's reasoning distribution rather than attempting to build mathematical competence from scratch.

The training data for RL consists exclusively of chain-of-thought-format questions drawn from the GSM8K and MATH datasets, which were part of the original supervised fine-tuning corpus. This amounts to approximately 144K questions. Critically, all other question types from the SFT data—including PoT and tool-integrated reasoning formats, as well as problems from other mathematical domains and languages—are deliberately excluded from the RL training set. This exclusion is a methodological choice designed to isolate and investigate the impact of reinforcement learning on benchmarks whose data never appears during the RL phase. In other words, any performance improvement on benchmarks beyond GSM8K and MATH can be attributed to the generalizing effect of RL rather than mere memorization or overfitting to the training distribution.

The GRPO algorithm is configured with several specific hyperparameters. The learning rate of the policy model is set to $1 \times 10^{-6}$, a relatively small value that ensures stable updates to the already well-trained model. The KL divergence coefficient $\beta$ is set to $0.04$, controlling the strength of the regularization that keeps the trained policy from deviating too far from the reference policy. For each question posed to the model, $G = 64$ outputs are sampled from the old policy, forming the group from which relative advantages are computed. This group size is substantial, providing a rich set of comparative samples for each question and enabling robust estimation of the baseline reward. The maximum sequence length during RL training is set to $1024$ tokens, and the training batch size is $1024$. Importantly, the policy model undergoes only a single gradient update following each exploration stage, a conservative strategy that prioritizes stability over speed of improvement.

The single-update-per-exploration design deserves further elaboration. In standard RL training loops, one might perform multiple gradient steps on the same batch of sampled data to extract more learning signal per exploration round. However, DeepSeekMath-RL restricts the update to a single step, meaning that the model's parameters are adjusted once based on the group of 64 sampled outputs before new outputs are generated for the next batch. This approach reduces the risk of the policy drifting too far from the data distribution on which the advantage estimates were computed, which would introduce bias into the training signal. The trade-off is that more exploration rounds are needed to achieve comparable improvement, but the resulting training trajectory is more stable and reliable.

## Reward Model Construction and Training

The reward model plays a central role in the GRPO framework, as it provides the scalar reward signals that are used to compute the group-relative advantages driving policy updates. Constructing a capable reward model is therefore essential to the success of the RL phase. The training set for the reward model is assembled following the methodology of prior work, which involves collecting comparisons between model-generated outputs on mathematical problems. These comparisons allow the reward model to learn to distinguish higher-quality solutions from lower-quality ones, ultimately producing scalar scores that reflect the relative merit of a given output.

The initial reward model is trained from DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, the pre-trained base model that demonstrated strong mathematical reasoning capabilities. This choice of initialization is significant: the base model already possesses deep mathematical knowledge from its extensive pre-training on the DeepSeekMath Corpus, giving the reward model a strong foundation from which to learn the additional task of solution quality assessment. The learning rate for reward model training is set to $2 \times 10^{-5}$, a moderate value appropriate for fine-tuning a large pre-trained model on a specialized task.

During RL training, the reward model is used to score each of the 64 outputs sampled per question. These scores form the vector $\mathbf{r} = \{r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_G\}$, which is then normalized by subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation. This normalization step is crucial because it transforms the absolute reward scores into relative measures that indicate how much better or worse a particular output is compared to the other outputs in the same group. The normalized rewards $\widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})}$ are then used to compute the advantages $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ that drive the policy gradient updates.

The paper also explores iterative RL, in which the reward model is continually updated as training progresses. The rationale for this is straightforward: as the policy model improves through RL training, the distribution of outputs it generates shifts, and the original reward model—trained on outputs from the initial SFT model—may become less calibrated for scoring the improved outputs. To address this, the iterative GRPO algorithm generates new training data for the reward model based on sampling results from the current policy model and continually trains the reward model using a replay mechanism. This replay mechanism incorporates 10% of historical data, ensuring that the reward model does not catastrophically forget its earlier training while adapting to the evolving policy distribution. After updating the reward model, the reference model is set to the current policy model, and the policy model is continually trained with the updated reward model. This iterative process, as described in Algorithm 1 of the paper, enables a co-evolution of the reward model and the policy model, maintaining the alignment between the reward signal and the model's current capabilities.

The paper also distinguishes between two modes of supervision: outcome supervision and process supervision. Under outcome supervision, the reward model provides a single reward score at the end of each output, and this normalized score is assigned as the advantage for all tokens in the output: $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i$ for all $t$. Under process supervision, a process reward model scores each reasoning step within an output, yielding rewards at multiple points: $\mathbf{R} = \{\{r_1^{index(1)}, \ldots, r_1^{index(K_1)}\}, \ldots, \{r_G^{index(1)}, \ldots, r_G^{index(K_G)}\}\}$, where $index(j)$ is the end token index of the $j$-th step and $K_i$ is the number of steps in the $i$-th output. These step-level rewards are similarly normalized, and the advantage for each token is computed as the sum of normalized rewards from all subsequent steps: $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{index(j) \geq t} \widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$. Process supervision provides a finer-grained training signal, allowing the model to learn not just whether a complete solution is good or bad, but which specific steps contribute to or detract from the solution's quality.

## Performance on In-Domain Tasks (GSM8K and MATH)

The most direct measure of the RL phase's effectiveness is performance on the in-domain tasks—GSM8K and MATH—since these are the datasets from which the RL training questions are drawn. The results are striking. DeepSeekMath-RL 7B achieves an accuracy of $88.2\%$ on GSM8K and $51.7\%$ on the competition-level MATH dataset when evaluated using chain-of-thought reasoning without external tools. These figures represent substantial improvements over the DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B model from which the RL phase was initialized, which scored $82.9\%$ on GSM8K and $46.8\%$ on MATH. The absolute improvements of $5.3$ and $4.9$ percentage points on these two benchmarks, respectively, demonstrate that GRPO is capable of extracting significant additional performance from an already strong instruction-tuned model.

The $51.7\%$ accuracy on MATH is particularly noteworthy. The MATH dataset contains competition-level mathematics problems that are substantially more challenging than the grade-school problems in GSM8K, requiring multi-step reasoning, creative problem-solving strategies, and mastery of diverse mathematical domains including algebra, number theory, combinatorics, geometry, and precalculus. Achieving over 50% accuracy on this benchmark represents, as the paper emphasizes, the first time an open-source model has surpassed this threshold without relying on external toolkits or voting techniques. This level of performance approaches that of frontier closed-source models such as GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra, narrowing the gap between open and closed systems in mathematical reasoning.

Furthermore, when self-consistency is applied—generating 64 samples and selecting the most common answer—DeepSeekMath-RL achieves $60.9\%$ on MATH. This substantial boost from the single-sample accuracy of $51.7\%$ indicates that the model frequently produces correct solutions but does not always rank them first in its output distribution. The gap between single-sample and self-consistency performance has important implications for understanding what RL accomplishes, a topic we revisit in the discussion of why RL works.

The in-domain results also surpass all open-source models in the $7$B to $70$B parameter range, including models that are substantially larger or have been specifically enhanced through math-focused reinforcement learning. For instance, WizardMath models, which use evolve-instruct and PPO training, and MetaMath 70B, which fine-tunes Llama-2 70B on augmented versions of GSM8K and MATH, are outperformed by the 7B DeepSeekMath-RL model. This demonstrates that the combination of high-quality pre-training data, effective instruction tuning, and the GRPO algorithm can compensate for a significant disadvantage in model size.

## Out-of-Domain Performance Enhancements

Perhaps the most remarkable finding from the RL evaluation is the improvement in out-of-domain performance. DeepSeekMath-RL is trained exclusively on chain-of-thought questions from GSM8K and MATH, yet it outperforms DeepSeekMath-Instruct across all evaluation metrics, including benchmarks that were entirely absent from the RL training data. This generalization effect is a strong indicator that RL is not merely improving the model's ability to solve the specific types of problems it was trained on, but is instead enhancing a more fundamental aspect of the model's reasoning behavior.

The out-of-domain improvements are observed on both English and Chinese benchmarks. On the Chinese mathematical benchmark CMATH, which assesses grade-school level mathematical reasoning in Chinese, the RL model improves from $84.6\%$ to $88.8\%$—an absolute gain of $4.2$ percentage points. This improvement is particularly significant because the RL training data consists entirely of English problems, yet the model's Chinese mathematical reasoning also benefits. This cross-lingual transfer suggests that RL is improving something deeper than language-specific problem-solving patterns, possibly related to the structure and coherence of the reasoning process itself.

The paper identifies GSM8K and MATH with chain-of-thought reasoning as in-domain tasks for DeepSeekMath-RL, while all other benchmarks—including those involving tool-integrated reasoning, Chinese mathematical problems, and multiple-choice formats—are considered out-of-domain. The fact that DeepSeekMath-RL outperforms DeepSeekMath-Instruct on all of these out-of-domain tasks, despite never being trained on them during the RL phase, demonstrates that the reinforcement learning signal generalizes beyond the narrow training distribution. This finding has profound implications for the understanding of what RL accomplishes in the context of language model alignment.

The breadth of out-of-domain improvement also addresses a natural concern: one might worry that RL on a narrow subset of data could lead to overfitting, degrading performance on tasks not included in the RL training set. The results conclusively show that this is not the case with GRPO. The KL regularization in the GRPO objective, which constrains the trained policy to remain close to the reference policy, likely plays a role in preventing such degradation. By keeping the policy anchored to the instruction-tuned model's distribution, GRPO ensures that the broad capabilities acquired during pre-training and SFT are preserved while the RL signal refines the model's reasoning behavior.

## Comparative Analysis with SFT and Other Models

The comparative analysis between DeepSeekMath-RL and DeepSeekMath-Instruct provides the clearest evidence for the value added by the RL phase. DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B is already a strong model, having undergone mathematical instruction tuning on a diverse dataset of 776K examples covering English and Chinese problems across multiple mathematical fields and solution formats. Yet, the RL phase—trained on only a subset of this instruction-tuning data, using the same questions but with the additional signal of reward-based optimization—produces consistent improvements across every benchmark evaluated. This means that the added value of RL cannot be attributed to exposure to new data or new problem types; it must arise from the learning signal itself.

The paper provides insight into the mechanism behind this improvement through analysis of Pass@K and Maj@K metrics. Pass@K measures whether at least one of the top-$K$ sampled outputs is correct, representing the model's fundamental capability to generate correct solutions. Maj@K, by contrast, selects the most common answer among $K$ samples and checks its correctness, representing the model's ability to reliably produce correct solutions at the top of its distribution. The analysis reveals that RL enhances Maj@K performance but not Pass@K. In other words, the model's fundamental ability to solve problems—its capacity to generate correct solutions given enough attempts—does not improve through RL. What improves is the reliability and consistency of correct solutions, moving them higher in the output distribution so that they are more likely to be selected.

This finding suggests that the SFT model suffers from what the paper terms a "misalignment problem": the model has the capability to solve problems correctly, but its output distribution does not always prioritize the correct reasoning path. RL, through the reward signal, aligns the distribution so that correct responses are boosted and incorrect responses are penalized, resulting in a more robust output distribution. The improvement is thus attributed to boosting the correct response from the TopK rather than enhancing fundamental capabilities. This is a crucial insight for understanding the role of RL in the language model training pipeline: RL does not teach the model new knowledge or reasoning strategies; it refines the model's preferences to better surface the capabilities it already has.

In the broader comparative landscape, DeepSeekMath-RL 7B outperforms all open-source models in the $7$B to $70$B range and the majority of closed-source models. Under the evaluation setting where tool use is disallowed, DeepSeekMath-RL surpasses models like Qwen 72B (which is ten times larger), WizardMath-v1.1 7B (which specifically underwent math-focused RL), and MetaMath 70B. On the MATH benchmark specifically, the model surpasses all open-source models and most proprietary models, including Inflection-2 and Gemini Pro, by at least $9\%$ absolute. While DeepSeekMath-RL rivals Chinese proprietary models like GLM-4 and Baichuan-3 on MATH, it still underperforms GPT-4 and Gemini Ultra, leaving room for future improvement.

Under the evaluation setting where models are allowed to integrate natural language reasoning with program-based tool use, DeepSeekMath-Instruct (and by extension, the RL model) approaches $60\%$ accuracy on MATH, surpassing all existing open-source models. This demonstrates that the model's capabilities extend beyond pure text-based reasoning to effective use of computational tools, a skill that is increasingly important for solving complex mathematical problems that require numerical or symbolic computation.

The comparative analysis also extends to the unified paradigm discussed in the paper, which conceptualizes various training methods—including SFT, Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), PPO, and GRPO—as either direct or simplified RL techniques. Within this paradigm, the key differentiators are the data source (online vs. offline sampling), the reward function (rule-based vs. model-based), and the algorithm (how the reward signal is processed into gradient coefficients). The experiments show that online sampling significantly outperforms offline sampling, particularly in later training stages, and that GRPO's ability to adjust gradient coefficients based on reward magnitude provides an advantage over methods like Online RFT, which uniformly reinforces all correct responses without penalizing incorrect ones. Process supervision, which provides step-level gradient coefficients, further improves upon outcome supervision, highlighting the value of fine-grained training signals.

These comparative results collectively paint a picture of GRPO as a highly effective RL algorithm for mathematical reasoning: it achieves substantial in-domain improvements, generalizes broadly to out-of-domain tasks, and does so with an efficient training setup that forgoes the computational burden of a separate value function model. The success of DeepSeekMath-RL demonstrates that careful data curation during pre-training, combined with effective instruction tuning and a well-designed RL algorithm, can produce a relatively small open-source model that rivals systems an order of magnitude larger.
