\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### What exactly is hyperspherical energy and why does it matter for finetuning diffusion models?
Question:
I'm reading the OFT paper and they keep mentioning "hyperspherical energy" as a key concept for preserving the pretrained model's generative ability during finetuning. The paper defines it as $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$ is the normalized neuron. But I don't have an intuitive understanding of what this quantity captures. Why is this specific measure of pairwise neuron relationships on a hypersphere important for diffusion models? How does it differ from simply measuring Euclidean distance between weight matrices?
Answer:
Hyperspherical energy measures how the neurons in a layer are distributed on the unit hypersphere in terms of their pairwise angular relationships. To understand it intuitively, consider what happens when you normalize each neuron $\bm{w}_i$ to unit length, projecting it onto a hypersphere. The Euclidean distance between two normalized neurons $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$ is directly related to the angle between them: when two neurons point in similar directions, this distance is small, and the inverse $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ becomes large. So the sum $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ effectively measures how "crowded" the neurons are on the hypersphere. A high energy means neurons are clustered together (many similar directions), while a low energy means they are spread out (diverse directions).

The key insight from the paper is that this angular structure encodes the semantic knowledge learned during pretraining. The authors support this with a toy autoencoder experiment showing that the angular information of neurons can almost perfectly reconstruct input images, while the magnitude information alone contains essentially no useful information. They draw an analogy to the 2D Fourier transform, where the phase spectrum (which is angular information) preserves the major part of an image's semantics—a phase spectrum combined with a random magnitude spectrum can still reconstruct the original image's semantic content. This suggests that the directions of neurons, not their lengths, are what store the meaningful representations.

This is where the distinction from Euclidean distance becomes important. Standard finetuning methods implicitly minimize $\|\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$, the Euclidean distance between finetuned and pretrained weights. But as the paper argues, this measure is insufficient because two weight matrices can be close in Euclidean distance yet have very different pairwise neuron relationships—small perturbations can accumulate across many neurons and fundamentally alter the angular structure. Conversely, an orthogonal transformation $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ can move neurons arbitrarily far in Euclidean distance while preserving every pairwise angle exactly, since orthogonal transformations are isometries that preserve inner products. The paper leverages this invariance: by constraining the finetuning update to be an orthogonal transformation, OFT guarantees $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$ exactly, with zero approximation error.

For diffusion models specifically, preserving this angular structure matters because the pretrained model has learned a rich semantic mapping from text to image space. The neurons' relative directions encode which concepts are similar, which are distinct, and how text prompts map to visual features. If finetuning disrupts these angular relationships—say, by making previously distinct neurons point in similar directions—the model loses its ability to generate the diverse, high-fidelity images it produced before finetuning. This manifests as mode collapse, loss of subject identity, or failure to follow text prompts, which the authors observe in methods like DreamBooth and LoRA when trained for too many iterations.

It is also worth noting why the goal is to *preserve* hyperspherical energy rather than *minimize* it. Prior work by Liu et al. (2021) on orthogonal over-parameterized training minimizes hyperspherical energy for classification, since uniformly spaced neurons reduce redundancy and improve generalization. But for finetuning a pretrained generative model, minimizing the energy would push all neurons toward uniform spacing on the hypersphere, destroying the specific semantic structure learned during pretraining. The paper explicitly states that "minimizing hyperspherical energy could destroy the original semantic structures." The objective is instead to maintain the pretrained angular relationships while allowing enough flexibility to adapt to the downstream task—striking what the authors call the "trade-off between flexibility and regularity."

### Why does the autoencoder toy experiment show that angular information recovers images but magnitude doesn't?
Question:
In the OFT paper, they present a toy experiment (Figure 1) where a convolutional autoencoder is trained on flower images using standard inner products. During testing, they compare three ways of generating feature maps: (a) inner product, (b) magnitude information, and (c) angular information. The paper claims that angular information "can almost perfectly recover the input images" while magnitude "contains no useful information." I'm confused about what exactly is being compared here. What does it mean to generate a feature map using only "magnitude information" or "angular information"? Is the angular version using cosine similarity instead of inner product? And how does this justify that neuron directions store semantic information?
Answer:
The key to understanding this experiment is the decomposition of the standard inner product into its magnitude and angular components. During training, the autoencoder uses the standard inner product $z = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}$ to compute feature maps, where $\mathbf{w}$ is the convolution kernel and $\mathbf{x}$ is the input patch. This inner product can be decomposed as $\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} = \|\mathbf{w}\| \cdot \|\mathbf{x}\| \cdot \cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf{w}$ and $\mathbf{x}$.

At test time, the paper compares three ways of generating feature maps:

- **(a) Inner product**: $z = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}$, exactly as in training.
- **(b) Magnitude information**: $z = \|\mathbf{w}\| \cdot \|\mathbf{x}\|$, which discards the angular term $\cos\theta$ and keeps only the product of the norms.
- **(c) Angular information**: $z = \cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{w}\| \|\mathbf{x}\|}$, which is the cosine similarity between the weight vector and the input patch, discarding the magnitude term.

So yes, the angular version is indeed using cosine similarity in place of the inner product. The critical detail is that the model was trained using the standard inner product — cosine similarity was never used during training. The experiment is testing which *component* of the learned inner product carries the information that the decoder relies on.

The result is that the angular component alone can almost perfectly reconstruct the flower images, while the magnitude component produces essentially garbage. This means the autoencoder's decoder learned to extract semantic content primarily from the directions (angles) of the feature representations, not from their magnitudes. The magnitudes are effectively redundant — the decoder does not depend on them.

The paper draws an analogy to the 2D Fourier transform, where the phase spectrum (angular information between the input and basis functions) carries the semantic structure of an image, while the magnitude spectrum does not. Swapping the magnitude spectrum with a random one still yields a recognizable image; swapping the phase spectrum destroys it. The toy experiment shows the same phenomenon in the learned feature space of a neural network.

This result motivates the central hypothesis of the paper: **neuron directions store semantic information**. If this is true, then to *modify* the semantics of generated images during finetuning, one should change neuron directions. However, to *preserve* the pretrained model's knowledge, one should preserve the pairwise angles between neurons — because those angles encode the relational structure that the pretrained model relies on. This is exactly what OFT does: it applies a layer-shared orthogonal transformation $\mathbf{R}$ to the weight matrix, changing neuron directions while preserving all pairwise angles (and thus the hyperspherical energy). The orthogonal constraint ensures the structural relationships among neurons are maintained, while still allowing enough flexibility to adapt to the downstream task.

### How does Cayley parameterization guarantee orthogonality, and why can't it produce reflections?
Question:
The OFT paper uses Cayley parameterization to construct orthogonal matrices: $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric ($\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$). The paper mentions this can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant 1 (the special orthogonal group), meaning it can produce rotations but not reflections. Can someone explain why this parameterization produces orthogonal matrices, and specifically why it's limited to determinant 1? Also, the paper says this limitation "does not affect the performance in practice" — is there an intuitive reason why reflections wouldn't be needed for finetuning?
Answer:
The Cayley parameterization $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ produces orthogonal matrices because of the algebraic properties of skew-symmetric matrices. To see this, we need to verify that $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$. Since $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$, we have $\bm{R}^\top = \big((\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}\big)^\top (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^\top = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$. The key observation is that $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$ and $(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})$ commute, since $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q}) = \bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2 = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$. Therefore, $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} = \bm{I}$, confirming orthogonality.

The reason the determinant is always 1 can be understood through a topological argument. The space of skew-symmetric matrices is a vector space and thus connected. The Cayley transform is a continuous map from this connected space into the orthogonal group $O(n)$, which has two connected components: $SO(n)$ (the special orthogonal group with determinant 1, containing rotations) and its complement (determinant $-1$, containing reflections). Since $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$ maps to $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, which has determinant 1, and the image of a connected set under a continuous map must be connected, the entire image must lie within $SO(n)$. Equivalently, one can verify algebraically: the eigenvalues of a real skew-symmetric matrix come in conjugate pairs $\pm i\lambda_k$, so $\det(\bm{I} + \bm{Q}) = \prod_k (1 + \lambda_k^2)$ and $\det(\bm{I} - \bm{Q}) = \prod_k (1 + \lambda_k^2)$, giving $\det(\bm{R}) = \det(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})/\det(\bm{I} - \bm{Q}) = 1$

As for why this limitation does not matter in practice, the key insight is that OFT initializes $\bm{R}$ to the identity matrix and finetuning proceeds through small, smooth perturbations. Since $\bm{I} \in SO(n)$ and $SO(n)$ is a connected manifold, any rotation can be reached through a continuous path from the identity. Reflections, by contrast, live in a disconnected component of $O(n)$ and cannot be reached by smooth deformation from $\bm{I}$. But for the purpose of finetuning, this is not a real restriction. Both rotations and reflections preserve the pairwise angles between neurons (and hence the hyperspherical energy that OFT is designed to maintain), so the structural preservation guarantee holds either way. What matters for finetuning is having enough flexibility to adjust neuron directions while maintaining their relative geometry, and the rotation group $SO(n)$ already provides a rich enough space to do this. Intuitively, in the high-dimensional spaces typical of neural network layers, the expressiveness of $SO(n)$ is more than sufficient for the relatively small adjustments needed during finetuning — one is not trying to achieve arbitrary coordinate transformations, but rather to gently redirect neurons while preserving their pairwise relationships.

### How does the COFT deviation constraint work with the Neumann series approximation?
Question:
In the COFT section, the paper constrains $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ and says they use the Neumann series to approximate $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ as $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$. From this, they claim the constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ becomes $\|\bm{Q} - \bm{0}\| \leq \epsilon'$. I have several confusions: (1) What is the Neumann series and when is it valid? (2) How exactly does $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ translate to $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'$ — what is the relationship between $\epsilon$ and $\epsilon'$? (3) How is this enforced via projected gradient descent in practice?
Answer:
The Neumann series is the matrix generalization of the geometric series. For any square matrix $\bm{A}$ with operator norm (spectral norm) $\|\bm{A}\|_{\text{op}} < 1$, the series $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \bm{A}^k = \bm{I} + \bm{A} + \bm{A}^2 + \cdots$ converges, and its sum equals $(\bm{I} - \bm{A})^{-1}$. This is the Neumann series, and it is valid precisely when $\|\bm{A}\|_{\text{op}} < 1$, which ensures that the higher-order terms decay geometrically.

In the paper's setting, the Cayley parameterization gives $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric. Applying the Neumann series to $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{A} = \bm{Q}$, we get $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} = \bm{I} + \bm{Q} + \bm{Q}^2 + \cdots$, provided $\|\bm{Q}\|_{\text{op}} < 1$. Multiplying through by $(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})$ and collecting terms yields $\bm{R} = \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + 2\bm{Q}^2 + \cdots = \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$. The key observation is that when $\|\bm{Q}\|$ is small, the higher-order terms $\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$ become negligible, so $\bm{R} - \bm{I} \approx 2\bm{Q}$.

This approximation directly establishes the relationship between $\epsilon$ and $\epsilon'$. Since $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \approx 2\|\bm{Q}\|$ when $\|\bm{Q}\|$ is small, the constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ translates to $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon/2$ to leading order, giving $\epsilon' \approx \epsilon/2$. More precisely, including the next-order term, we have $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq 2\|\bm{Q}\| + C\|\bm{Q}\|^2$ for some constant $C$ depending on the norm, so the exact relationship between $\epsilon$ and $\epsilon'$ is slightly nonlinear. However, the paper treats $\epsilon'$ as a separate tunable hyperparameter rather than fixing an exact mapping, which is reasonable because the approximation is only accurate for small $\|\bm{Q}\|$, and the practical choice of $\epsilon'$ will depend on the desired trade-off between flexibility and stability.

In practice, the constraint $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'$ is enforced via projected gradient descent. After each gradient update step on the unconstrained parameter $\bm{Q}$, the algorithm checks whether $\|\bm{Q}\|$ exceeds $\epsilon'$. If it does, $\bm{Q}$ is projected back onto the ball of radius $\epsilon'$. For the Frobenius norm, this projection is simply a rescaling: $\bm{Q} \leftarrow \frac{\epsilon'}{\|\bm{Q}\|_F} \cdot \bm{Q}$ when $\|\bm{Q}\|_F > \epsilon'$. For the operator (spectral) norm, the projection requires computing the SVD $\bm{Q} = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, clipping each singular value to $\epsilon'$ (i.e., $\sigma_i \leftarrow \min(\sigma_i, \epsilon')$), and reconstructing $\bm{Q} \leftarrow \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}'\bm{V}^\top$. Since $\bm{Q}$ is initialized as the zero matrix (which gives $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, the identity initialization), the constraint is satisfied at the start of training, and projected gradient descent ensures it remains satisfied throughout. This is much simpler than trying to enforce $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ directly on the Cayley-transformed matrix $\bm{R}$, because $\bm{R}$ lives on the orthogonal manifold where standard projections are computationally expensive. By moving the constraint to the unconstrained parameter $\bm{Q}$ via the Neumann approximation, COFT decouples the orthogonality constraint (handled by the Cayley parameterization) from the deviation constraint (handled by projected gradient descent on $\bm{Q}$), making both tractable.

### Why does the paper say OFT performs "low-rank weight update" when R-I is low-rank, and how does this compare to LoRA?
Question:
The OFT paper draws a connection to LoRA by rewriting the forward pass as $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ and saying $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ is "analogous to LoRA's low-rank weight update" when $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ is low-rank. But I'm confused: the block-diagonal $\bm{R}$ has complexity $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, and $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ is a $d \times d$ matrix. How can $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ be low-rank? Is the claim that because $\bm{R}$ is block-diagonal with $r$ blocks, $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ has rank related to the block structure? Also, LoRA explicitly parameterizes a low-rank update, while OFT parameterizes an orthogonal matrix — so how are they fundamentally different in approach?
Answer:
The paper's claim is **conditional**, not absolute: it states that OFT "performs low-rank weight update **when** $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ is low-rank." The key to understanding when this condition holds lies in the block-diagonal structure and the spectral properties of orthogonal matrices.

Since $\bm{R}$ is block-diagonal with $r$ blocks, $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ is also block-diagonal:

$$\bm{R} - \bm{I} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1 - \bm{I}_{d/r}, \ldots, \bm{R}_r - \bm{I}_{d/r})$$

The rank of this matrix is $\sum_{i=1}^{r} \text{rank}(\bm{R}_i - \bm{I}_{d/r})$. For an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}_i$, the rank of $\bm{R}_i - \bm{I}$ equals the number of eigenvalues of $\bm{R}_i$ that differ from $1$. If $\bm{R}_i$ represents a simple 2D rotation by angle $\theta$ (embedded in the $d/r$-dimensional block), only two eigenvalues differ from $1$ (namely $e^{i\theta}$ and $e^{-i\theta}$), giving $\text{rank}(\bm{R}_i - \bm{I}) = 2$. In that case, $\text{rank}(\bm{R} - \bm{I}) = 2r = \mathcal{O}(r)$, which is indeed low-rank when $r \ll d$.

This situation is especially relevant under COFT, where the constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ keeps $\bm{R}$ close to $\bm{I}$. Using the Cayley parameterization, $\bm{R} - \bm{I} = 2\bm{Q}(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, so $\text{rank}(\bm{R} - \bm{I}) = \text{rank}(\bm{Q})$. When $\epsilon$ is small, $\bm{Q}$ has small norm, and the resulting rotation in each block tends to involve few directions, yielding low effective rank.

It is important to distinguish the $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ figure from rank: this is the **parameter count** (the number of free variables needed to specify the skew-symmetric matrices parameterizing each block), not the rank of $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$. A matrix with $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ parameters can still be full-rank; the low-rank property of $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ depends on the spectral structure of the learned orthogonal blocks, not on the parameter count alone.

The analogy to LoRA is **structural**: LoRA's update has the factored form $\bm{B}\bm{A}$ with rank at most $r'$, while OFT's update has the factored form $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ with rank at most $\text{rank}(\bm{R} - \bm{I})$. Since $\bm{W}^0$ is typically full-rank, the rank of the OFT update equals $\text{rank}(\bm{R} - \bm{I})$. Both methods limit the degrees of freedom in the weight update, but through fundamentally different mechanisms.

The core differences are: LoRA applies an **additive** update $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{B}\bm{A}$ constrained to be low-rank, while OFT applies a **multiplicative** update $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ constrained to be orthogonal. LoRA achieves parameter efficiency through low-rank factorization; OFT achieves it through block-diagonal sparsity of the orthogonal matrix. Most importantly, OFT provably preserves the hyperspherical energy — the pairwise angles between neurons — because orthogonal transformations preserve inner products. LoRA makes no such structural guarantee about the pretrained weights' geometry. The paper's "low-rank" remark is simply noting that OFT's update *can* be low-rank in certain regimes, drawing a parallel to LoRA's explicit low-rank design, but the two methods remain fundamentally different in what structural property they preserve.

### What is the interpretation of block-diagonal R for convolution layers, and why doesn't LoRA have such an interpretation?
Question:
The paper states that for convolution layers, the block-diagonal structure of $\bm{R}$ has "interesting interpretations" unlike LoRA. Specifically, when the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, each block transforms a unique neuron channel, "similar to learning depth-wise convolution kernels." And when all blocks are shared, OFT transforms neurons with an orthogonal matrix shared across channels. Can someone explain this more concretely? How exactly does a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix correspond to depth-wise convolution? And why can't LoRA have a similar interpretation — is it because LoRA's low-rank additive update doesn't decompose nicely per-channel?
Answer:
# Understanding Block-Diagonal R for Convolution Layers

The key insight comes from how the block-diagonal structure of $\bm{R}$ interacts with the channel structure of convolution weights.

## Convolution Weights and the Role of $\bm{R}$

Consider a convolution layer with weights that can be reshaped as $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{out} \times (C_{in} \cdot k_h \cdot k_w)}$, where $C_{in}$ is the number of input channels and $k_h \times k_w$ is the kernel size. The OFT transformation applies $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ where $d = C_{in} \cdot k_h \cdot k_w$, so the forward pass becomes $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$.

When $\bm{R}$ is block-diagonal with $r = C_{in}$ blocks, each block $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{(k_h k_w) \times (k_h k_w)}$ acts exclusively on the kernel weights of a single input channel. In other words, input channel $i$ is transformed by $\bm{R}_i$ independently of all other channels. This is precisely what depth-wise convolution does: each input channel is processed by its own kernel without mixing information across channels. The orthogonal constraint means each such per-channel transformation is a rotation/reflection of that channel's kernel weights, preserving the angular structure within each channel.

When all blocks are shared ($\bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j, \forall i,j$), the same orthogonal transformation is applied uniformly across all input channels. This is analogous to a transformation that operates identically on every channel — a shared coordinate system change applied across the channel dimension.

## Why LoRA Lacks This Interpretation

LoRA performs an additive update $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}^\top$ where $\bm{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{out} \times r'}$ and $\bm{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r'}$. The rank constraint is global: it limits the total rank of the update matrix $\bm{A}\bm{B}^\top$ but does not impose any structure aligned with the channel decomposition of the convolution. The matrix $\bm{B}$ is a dense (low-rank) matrix that mixes information across all input channels simultaneously. There is no natural way to partition $\bm{A}\bm{B}^\top$ into per-channel transformations, because the low-rank factorization couples all channels together through the shared rank-$r'$ bottleneck.

In contrast, OFT's block-diagonal structure has an explicit sparsity pattern that can be aligned with the channel structure of convolutions. The blocks partition the input dimension $d$ into groups that correspond exactly to input channels, creating a natural per-channel interpretation. LoRA's low-rank structure is a constraint on the global rank of the update, not on its per-channel structure, so it cannot decompose into independent channel-wise operations in the same way.

This distinction reflects a deeper difference in philosophy: LoRA exploits low-rank structure to reduce parameters globally, while OFT exploits sparsity structure (block-diagonal orthogonality) that can be naturally aligned with architectural features like convolution channels.

### Why is preserving hyperspherical energy better than minimizing it for finetuning diffusion models?
Question:
The OFT paper distinguishes itself from prior work (Liu et al., 2021) by saying they preserve hyperspherical energy rather than minimize it. They argue that minimizing HE "could destroy the original semantic structures" because minimum HE means neurons are uniformly spaced on the hypersphere. For classification, uniform spacing is desirable to avoid redundancy. But for finetuning a pretrained diffusion model, the pretrained structure already encodes useful knowledge. I have two questions: (1) Why would uniform spacing destroy semantic information — doesn't it just remove redundancy? (2) How does OFT actually guarantee that HE is preserved, not just approximately? Is it exact or just close to zero?
Answer:
## Answer

Great questions. Let me address both.

### (1) Why uniform spacing destroys semantic information

The key insight is that "redundancy" and "semantic structure" are not the same thing. In a classification network trained from scratch, you want neurons to be uniformly spaced on the hypersphere because this maximizes their representational diversity for distinguishing classes — there is no pre-existing structure to preserve. But a pretrained diffusion model has already learned specific pairwise angular relationships between neurons that encode the knowledge needed for high-fidelity, diverse image generation. These angular relationships are the *actual content* of what was learned during pretraining, not redundancy to be pruned.

The paper makes this clear in the "Why not minimize hyperspherical energy" discussion: minimum hyperspherical energy forces all neurons into a uniform configuration, which would overwrite the specific learned angular structure. Think of it this way: if neuron A and neuron B have learned to be at a particular angle to each other because that angle encodes some useful semantic distinction (say, between fur and fabric textures), forcing them to be uniformly spaced would erase that distinction. The paper's toy autoencoder experiment (Figure 1) supports this intuition by showing that angular information — not magnitude — is what carries semantic content. So minimizing HE doesn't just "remove redundancy"; it actively destroys the learned knowledge representation.

### (2) How OFT guarantees HE preservation exactly

The preservation is **exact**, not approximate. The key mathematical fact is that orthogonal transformations preserve inner products and norms. When you transform all neurons in a layer by the same orthogonal matrix $R$, the normalized neurons become $\hat{w}_i = Rw_i^0 / \|Rw_i^0\| = Rw_i^0 / \|w_i^0\|$ (since $R$ preserves norms). Then the pairwise distance between any two transformed neurons satisfies:

$$\|R\hat{w}_i^0 - R\hat{w}_j^0\| = \|R(\hat{w}_i^0 - \hat{w}_j^0)\| = \|\hat{w}_i^0 - \hat{w}_j^0\|$$

because orthogonal matrices preserve norms. Since hyperspherical energy is defined as $\text{HE}(W) = \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{w}_i - \hat{w}_j\|^{-1}$, every term in the sum is unchanged, and therefore $\text{HE}(W) = \text{HE}(W^0)$ *exactly*.

This is precisely why OFT uses orthogonal transformation rather than a soft regularizer. As the paper notes in the introduction, a naive approach would be to add a regularizer penalizing $\|\text{HE}(W) - \text{HE}(W^0)\|$, but "there is no guarantee that the hyperspherical energy difference can be well minimized." Instead, OFT exploits the invariance property: by construction, if $W = RW^0$ with $R^\top R = I$, the HE difference is provably zero. The orthogonality of $R$ is itself guaranteed exactly through the Cayley parameterization $R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ with $Q$ skew-symmetric, which produces matrices in the special orthogonal group by construction. So the guarantee holds at every training step, not just at convergence.

### In re-scaled OFT, why is the diagonal matrix D applied on the right of W^0 rather than the left?
Question:
The paper proposes re-scaled OFT with forward pass $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}$ where $\bm{D} = \text{diag}(s_1, \dots, s_n)$ is a learnable diagonal matrix. There's an errata note saying the camera-ready version mistakenly wrote $\bm{z} = (\bm{D}\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. I want to understand the difference: if $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, then $\bm{D} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ scales the output neurons (columns of $\bm{W}^0$), whereas $\bm{D}$ on the left would require it to be $\mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and scale input dimensions. Is the correct version scaling each output neuron's magnitude independently? And why is this compatible with preserving hyperspherical energy — because scaling neuron magnitudes doesn't affect normalized neuron angles?
Answer:
You've essentially answered your own question correctly, but let me elaborate on why the right-multiplication is the intended and correct formulation, and why the left-multiplication version is not just a dimensional error but a fundamentally different (and problematic) operation.

In the correct formulation $\z = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}$, the matrix $\bm{D} = \operatorname{diag}(s_1, \dots, s_n) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ right-multiplies $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, which scales each column (i.e., each neuron) independently. The $i$-th neuron becomes $s_i \cdot (\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i)$, so $\bm{D}$ controls the magnitude of each output neuron separately while $\bm{R}$ controls the shared angular transformation. This is exactly the intended behavior: the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ rotates/reflects all neurons in the input space, and then $\bm{D}$ rescales each neuron's magnitude.

The erroneous version $\bm{z} = (\bm{D}\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ would require $\bm{D} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ to be dimensionally consistent. In that case, $\bm{D}$ would scale along input dimensions (rows of $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$), not individual output neurons. This is a categorically different operation—it changes the relative importance of input features rather than the magnitudes of output neurons. Moreover, the composite matrix $\bm{D}\bm{R}$ is generally **not orthogonal** (unless $\bm{D} = c\bm{I}$ for some scalar $c$), so the combined transformation $\bm{D}\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ would **not** preserve pairwise angles between neurons. This would break the core guarantee of OFT: that hyperspherical energy is preserved.

The compatibility with hyperspherical energy preservation is precisely as you described. The hyperspherical energy is defined in terms of *normalized* neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$, so it depends only on the *directions* of neurons, not their magnitudes. When we write the finetuned weight matrix as $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$, the $i$-th neuron is $s_i \bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i$. Its normalized version is:

$$\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \frac{s_i \bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i}{\|s_i \bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i\|} = \frac{\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i}{\|\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i\|} = \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i$$

where the last step uses the fact that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal (so $\|\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i\| = \|\bm{w}^0_i\|$). The positive scalar $s_i$ cancels out entirely. Since all normalized neurons are transformed by the same orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, the pairwise cosine similarities (and hence the hyperspherical energy) are identical to those of the original pretrained model. The diagonal scaling adds learnable per-neuron magnitudes as an extra degree of freedom *without* disturbing the angular structure that OFT is designed to protect, which is why the paper describes it as improving flexibility "with a negligible number of additional parameters" while still preserving hyperspherical energy.

### How does the 2D Fourier transform analogy justify using angular information for finetuning?
Question:
The paper draws an analogy to 2D Fourier transform, saying that the phase spectrum (angular information) preserves the major part of semantics, and that the phase spectrum along with a random magnitude spectrum can reconstruct an image without losing semantics. I find this analogy confusing in the context of neural network finetuning. The Fourier transform decomposes images into frequency components — what is the neural network analog of "phase" and "magnitude" here? Are neuron directions the analog of phase and neuron magnitudes the analog of magnitude spectrum? If so, this seems like a loose analogy. Can someone clarify whether this is meant as a rigorous connection or just intuition, and how it specifically motivates OFT?
Answer:
The 2D Fourier transform analogy in the paper is intended as intuition rather than a rigorous mathematical connection, and the parallel works at the level of "angular information preserves semantics" rather than through a direct isomorphism between Fourier components and neural network parameters.

In the 2D Fourier transform, an image is decomposed into sinusoidal basis functions, and each coefficient is a complex number with a magnitude and a phase. The well-known empirical observation is that if you keep the phase spectrum (the angular information between the input image and each basis function) but replace the magnitude spectrum with random values, the reconstructed image still recognizably preserves the semantic content of the original. The phase encodes "where things are" spatially, while the magnitude mainly encodes contrast and texture intensity.

The paper draws a loose but instructive parallel: in a neural network layer, the output $z = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{x}$ involves inner products between the input $\mathbf{x}$ and each neuron weight $\mathbf{w}_i$. If you decompose this inner product into a magnitude component $\|\mathbf{w}_i\| \cdot \|\mathbf{x}\|$ and an angular component $\cos\theta_i$ (where $\theta_i$ is the angle between $\mathbf{w}_i$ and $\mathbf{x}$), the paper argues that the angular component is what carries semantic meaning. This is supported by the toy autoencoder experiment (Figure 1 in the paper), where replacing the standard inner product with purely angular information (cosine similarity) during inference almost perfectly reconstructs the input images, while using only magnitude information produces no recognizable content. The paper also cites prior work (SphereNet, decoupled angular margins) showing that normalizing all neurons onto a unit hypersphere during training preserves model capacity and generalization, reinforcing that directions matter more than magnitudes.

So yes, your reading is essentially correct: neuron directions are the analog of phase, and neuron magnitudes are the analog of the magnitude spectrum. The analogy is deliberately loose — there is no claim that a neural network layer literally performs a Fourier transform. Rather, the paper uses this as conceptual motivation for the hypothesis that "the angles between neurons are crucial for representing the knowledge of neural networks," which is then empirically supported by the toy experiment and prior literature on hyperspherical learning.

This directly motivates OFT in two steps. First, if neuron directions encode semantics, then to semantically modify generated images (the goal of both subject-driven and controllable generation), finetuning should change neuron directions. Second, however, changing directions with too much freedom will destroy the pretrained model's existing semantic structure. OFT's solution is to apply a shared orthogonal transformation $\mathbf{R}$ to all neurons in a layer, which rotates or reflects the entire set of neurons together. This provably preserves all pairwise angles (and hence the hyperspherical energy) while still allowing enough flexibility to adapt to downstream tasks. The analogy thus provides the intuition for *why* one should focus on angular structure, and OFT provides the mechanism for *how* to modify it safely.

### Why does OFT converge faster than LoRA or ControlNet — is there a theoretical reason or just empirical observation?
Question:
The paper shows that OFT and COFT converge significantly faster than LoRA, ControlNet, and T2I-Adapter in both subject-driven and controllable generation tasks. They offer two explanations: (1) changing neuron directions is the most effective way to modify semantics, which is what OFT does; and (2) OFT can be viewed as finetuning on a smooth hypersphere manifold, yielding a better optimization landscape. The second point cites Liu et al. (2021). Can someone elaborate on why optimizing on a hypersphere manifold leads to better optimization landscapes? Is this related to the absence of saddle points, the curvature of the manifold, or something else? And is there any formal analysis, or is this purely empirical?
Answer:
The paper offers two complementary explanations for OFT's faster convergence, and the hypersphere manifold argument is indeed the more theoretically nuanced one. Let me elaborate.

**First, the semantic argument.** The paper demonstrates via a toy autoencoder experiment that angular information of neurons (their directions) almost perfectly reconstructs input images, while magnitude information contains essentially no useful semantic content. This is inspired by the 2D Fourier transform analogy: the phase spectrum (angular information) preserves most of an image's semantics, while the magnitude spectrum does not. Since OFT directly optimizes neuron directions via orthogonal transformations, it is modifying precisely the parameters that carry semantic information. LoRA, by contrast, performs additive low-rank weight updates that affect both direction and magnitude, while ControlNet adds an entirely new branch that must learn to interact with the pretrained model from scratch. Intuitively, OFT converges faster because it is searching in the right "subspace" of meaningful changes from the start.

**Second, the manifold optimization argument.** The key insight is that OFT parameterizes the weight update as $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ where $\bm{R} \in O(d)$ is an orthogonal matrix. Since the orthogonal group $O(d)$ is a compact, smooth Riemannian manifold, the optimization over $\bm{R}$ is effectively an optimization on a smooth manifold rather than in unconstrained Euclidean space. There are several reasons this improves the optimization landscape:

The orthogonal group is compact, meaning all parameters are inherently bounded. This eliminates issues like gradient explosion or weight collapse to zero, which can plague unconstrained finetuning. The optimization trajectory stays on a well-behaved surface rather than wandering through an unbounded space where pathological solutions can arise.

The group structure of $O(d)$ provides a natural geodesic structure for optimization. The Cayley parameterization $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ used in the paper maps the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ (which lives in a vector space) smoothly onto the orthogonal group. The Neumann series approximation $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$ shows that small updates in $\bm{Q}$ translate to small, well-controlled rotations of the neuron directions. This provides a principled analog to using a small learning rate, but with the guarantee that the update never destroys the angular structure between neurons.

The preservation of hyperspherical energy—meaning the pairwise angles between all neurons in a layer are invariant under orthogonal transformation—effectively constrains the optimization to a submanifold where the pretrained semantic structure is preserved. This dramatically reduces the effective search space. LoRA constrains the update to be low-rank, which is a different kind of constraint, but one that does not explicitly preserve the angular relationships that the paper argues are semantically critical.

**Is there formal analysis?** The paper itself acknowledges that the better optimization landscape claim is "empirically verified in [Liu et al., 2021]," referring to the Orthogonal Over-Parameterized Training work. That earlier work demonstrated empirically that training on orthogonal manifolds leads to faster convergence and better generalization in classification networks. The theoretical backing comes from the well-established literature on Riemannian optimization: optimization on compact smooth manifolds generally enjoys better convergence guarantees because the loss landscape is bounded and the geometry prevents many degenerate critical points. However, a rigorous formal proof that manifold optimization on $O(d)$ specifically yields fewer saddle points or better curvature properties for diffusion model finetuning appears to be an open question. The paper does not provide such a proof and positions the claim as empirically grounded.

In summary, OFT's faster convergence likely stems from both factors working in concert: it directly optimizes the semantically meaningful parameters (neuron directions) and does so on a geometrically well-behaved manifold that provides natural regularization through compactness and structure preservation. The first factor explains *what* to optimize for maximum semantic effect, and the second explains *why* the optimization process itself is more efficient and stable.

### What does the paper mean by "no additional inference overhead" and how does merging R into W^0 work?
Question:
The paper claims OFT introduces no additional inference overhead because "we can simply multiply the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ into the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ and obtain an equivalent weight matrix $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$." This makes sense in principle, but I'm confused about how this works with the block-diagonal structure. If $\bm{R}$ is block-diagonal with $r$ blocks, does merging still produce a regular weight matrix $\bm{W}$? Also, ControlNet introduces additional network branches, which is why it has overhead. LoRA also can be merged — so does LoRA also have no inference overhead? How does OFT's "no overhead" claim differ from LoRA's?
Answer:
The key insight is that the block-diagonal structure of $\bm{R}$ is a **parameterization strategy**, not a constraint on the matrix multiplication itself. Whether $\bm{R}$ is a full orthogonal matrix or a block-diagonal one composed of $r$ smaller orthogonal blocks $\bm{R}_1, \ldots, \bm{R}_r$, it is still a $d \times d$ matrix. The product $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ where $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ produces a dense $d \times n$ matrix — exactly the same shape as the pretrained weight matrix. The block-diagonal structure simply means that $\bm{R}$ mixes together groups of rows of $\bm{W}^0$ within each block, but rows belonging to different blocks do not interact. Once you compute $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, the result is an ordinary dense weight matrix with no special structure, and inference proceeds exactly as it would with the original pretrained weights: $\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x}$. The block-diagonal structure lives only in the trainable parameterization; after merging, it disappears entirely.

You are also correct that LoRA can similarly be merged — after training, the low-rank update $\bm{A}\bm{B}$ is added to $\bm{W}^0$ to form $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$, yielding a single dense matrix with no inference overhead. In this respect, OFT and LoRA are alike: both modify the pretrained weights in a structured, parameter-efficient way during training, and both can be collapsed into the base weights for deployment.

The paper's "no additional inference overhead" claim is primarily contrasted with **ControlNet**, not LoRA. ControlNet introduces an entirely separate trainable network branch — a copy of the encoder that processes the control signal — connected to the pretrained model via zero-convolution layers. At inference time, this auxiliary branch must remain active and cannot be folded into the original weights, because it implements a fundamentally different computation path (processing an additional input signal) rather than a reparameterization of existing weights. The paper explicitly states this distinction: "Unlike ControlNet, our OFT framework introduces no additional inference overhead to the finetuned model." So the claim is really about architectural overhead from extra inference-time computation paths, which ControlNet has and OFT (like LoRA) does not.

The paper also draws an explicit connection between OFT and LoRA by rewriting the OFT forward pass as $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, noting that $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ is "analogous to LoRA's low-rank weight update." The conceptual difference is that LoRA constrains the *additive* update to be low-rank, while OFT constrains the *multiplicative* transformation to be orthogonal (and uses block-diagonal sparsity for parameter efficiency). Both approaches are mergeable, but they enforce different structural priors on how the pretrained weights are allowed to change — LoRA limits rank, while OFT preserves pairwise neuron angles (hyperspherical energy).

### Why is the hyperspherical energy defined with inverse distance, and what happens when two neurons are identical?
Question:
The hyperspherical energy is defined as $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$. This uses inverse Euclidean distance between normalized neurons. I have several concerns: (1) What happens when two neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}_j$ are identical or nearly identical? The term would blow up to infinity. (2) Why inverse distance rather than, say, cosine similarity directly? (3) Does the paper address numerical stability of this computation? (4) Is this a standard definition from the hyperspherical energy literature, and if so, what's the motivation for this specific form?
Answer:
The hyperspherical energy definition used in this paper comes from the literature on learning with angular information, specifically cited to Liu et al. (2018) and Liu et al. (2021). The form $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ is indeed a standard definition from that line of work, and it is closely related to Riesz energy or potential energy concepts from physics and optimal point configuration theory on spheres. The intuition is that of a repulsive potential: neurons that are close together on the unit hypersphere contribute high energy, creating a "force" that encourages uniform spreading. The inverse distance creates a singularity at zero distance, which serves as a strong penalty against neuron redundancy — exactly the property you want when measuring how uniformly distributed neurons are.

Regarding your concern about identical or nearly identical neurons causing the energy to blow up: this is a valid observation in general, but it is largely sidestepped in this paper because OFT never actually computes the hyperspherical energy directly. The key insight is that the paper does not minimize $\|\text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\|$ as a loss term. Instead, it achieves this minimum by construction. As the paper states, the minimum of zero is attained whenever $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ for an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, because orthogonal transformations preserve all pairwise Euclidean distances between normalized neurons. So OFT simply learns an orthogonal transformation and the energy preservation is guaranteed mathematically, with no need to evaluate the energy sum numerically. The paper explicitly notes: "One can easily observe that the attainable minimum is zero... The minimum can be achieved as long as $\bm{W}$ and $\bm{W}^0$ differ only up to a rotation or reflection."

As for why inverse Euclidean distance rather than cosine similarity directly: for unit vectors, the two are monotonically related since $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\| = \sqrt{2(1 - \cos\theta_{ij})}$, so they encode the same angular information. However, the inverse distance form has the desirable property of creating a singularity at zero separation, which more sharply penalizes neuron collapse. Cosine similarity alone (without inversion) would not create this singularity — two identical neurons would simply contribute a value of $1$, which does not stand out dramatically from other terms. The inverse distance form makes the energy more sensitive to redundancy, which is the whole point of using it as a measure of uniformity.

The paper does not explicitly discuss numerical stability of the $\text{HE}$ computation, likely because, as explained above, the computation is avoided entirely. The practical stability concerns in the paper are instead addressed through the COFT variant, which adds an explicit constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ to limit how far the orthogonal transformation can deviate from the identity, and through the Cayley parameterization for ensuring orthogonality. These mechanisms handle stability at the level of the transformation itself rather than at the level of energy computation.
