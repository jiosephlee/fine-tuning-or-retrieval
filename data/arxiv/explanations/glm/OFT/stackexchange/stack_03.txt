\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### How does Cayley parameterization guarantee orthogonality, and why can't it produce reflections?
Question:
The OFT paper uses Cayley parameterization to construct orthogonal matrices: $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric ($\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$). The paper mentions this can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant 1 (the special orthogonal group), meaning it can produce rotations but not reflections. Can someone explain why this parameterization produces orthogonal matrices, and specifically why it's limited to determinant 1? Also, the paper says this limitation "does not affect the performance in practice" — is there an intuitive reason why reflections wouldn't be needed for finetuning?
Answer:
The Cayley parameterization $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ produces orthogonal matrices because of the algebraic properties of skew-symmetric matrices. To see this, we need to verify that $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$. Since $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$, we have $\bm{R}^\top = \big((\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}\big)^\top (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^\top = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$. The key observation is that $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$ and $(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})$ commute, since $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q}) = \bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2 = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$. Therefore, $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} = \bm{I}$, confirming orthogonality.

The reason the determinant is always 1 can be understood through a topological argument. The space of skew-symmetric matrices is a vector space and thus connected. The Cayley transform is a continuous map from this connected space into the orthogonal group $O(n)$, which has two connected components: $SO(n)$ (the special orthogonal group with determinant 1, containing rotations) and its complement (determinant $-1$, containing reflections). Since $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$ maps to $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, which has determinant 1, and the image of a connected set under a continuous map must be connected, the entire image must lie within $SO(n)$. Equivalently, one can verify algebraically: the eigenvalues of a real skew-symmetric matrix come in conjugate pairs $\pm i\lambda_k$, so $\det(\bm{I} + \bm{Q}) = \prod_k (1 + \lambda_k^2)$ and $\det(\bm{I} - \bm{Q}) = \prod_k (1 + \lambda_k^2)$, giving $\det(\bm{R}) = \det(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})/\det(\bm{I} - \bm{Q}) = 1$

As for why this limitation does not matter in practice, the key insight is that OFT initializes $\bm{R}$ to the identity matrix and finetuning proceeds through small, smooth perturbations. Since $\bm{I} \in SO(n)$ and $SO(n)$ is a connected manifold, any rotation can be reached through a continuous path from the identity. Reflections, by contrast, live in a disconnected component of $O(n)$ and cannot be reached by smooth deformation from $\bm{I}$. But for the purpose of finetuning, this is not a real restriction. Both rotations and reflections preserve the pairwise angles between neurons (and hence the hyperspherical energy that OFT is designed to maintain), so the structural preservation guarantee holds either way. What matters for finetuning is having enough flexibility to adjust neuron directions while maintaining their relative geometry, and the rotation group $SO(n)$ already provides a rich enough space to do this. Intuitively, in the high-dimensional spaces typical of neural network layers, the expressiveness of $SO(n)$ is more than sufficient for the relatively small adjustments needed during finetuning — one is not trying to achieve arbitrary coordinate transformations, but rather to gently redirect neurons while preserving their pairwise relationships.
