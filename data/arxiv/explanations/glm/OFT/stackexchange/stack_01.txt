\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### What exactly is hyperspherical energy and why does it matter for finetuning diffusion models?
Question:
I'm reading the OFT paper and they keep mentioning "hyperspherical energy" as a key concept for preserving the pretrained model's generative ability during finetuning. The paper defines it as $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$ is the normalized neuron. But I don't have an intuitive understanding of what this quantity captures. Why is this specific measure of pairwise neuron relationships on a hypersphere important for diffusion models? How does it differ from simply measuring Euclidean distance between weight matrices?
Answer:
Hyperspherical energy measures how the neurons in a layer are distributed on the unit hypersphere in terms of their pairwise angular relationships. To understand it intuitively, consider what happens when you normalize each neuron $\bm{w}_i$ to unit length, projecting it onto a hypersphere. The Euclidean distance between two normalized neurons $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$ is directly related to the angle between them: when two neurons point in similar directions, this distance is small, and the inverse $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ becomes large. So the sum $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ effectively measures how "crowded" the neurons are on the hypersphere. A high energy means neurons are clustered together (many similar directions), while a low energy means they are spread out (diverse directions).

The key insight from the paper is that this angular structure encodes the semantic knowledge learned during pretraining. The authors support this with a toy autoencoder experiment showing that the angular information of neurons can almost perfectly reconstruct input images, while the magnitude information alone contains essentially no useful information. They draw an analogy to the 2D Fourier transform, where the phase spectrum (which is angular information) preserves the major part of an image's semantics—a phase spectrum combined with a random magnitude spectrum can still reconstruct the original image's semantic content. This suggests that the directions of neurons, not their lengths, are what store the meaningful representations.

This is where the distinction from Euclidean distance becomes important. Standard finetuning methods implicitly minimize $\|\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$, the Euclidean distance between finetuned and pretrained weights. But as the paper argues, this measure is insufficient because two weight matrices can be close in Euclidean distance yet have very different pairwise neuron relationships—small perturbations can accumulate across many neurons and fundamentally alter the angular structure. Conversely, an orthogonal transformation $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ can move neurons arbitrarily far in Euclidean distance while preserving every pairwise angle exactly, since orthogonal transformations are isometries that preserve inner products. The paper leverages this invariance: by constraining the finetuning update to be an orthogonal transformation, OFT guarantees $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$ exactly, with zero approximation error.

For diffusion models specifically, preserving this angular structure matters because the pretrained model has learned a rich semantic mapping from text to image space. The neurons' relative directions encode which concepts are similar, which are distinct, and how text prompts map to visual features. If finetuning disrupts these angular relationships—say, by making previously distinct neurons point in similar directions—the model loses its ability to generate the diverse, high-fidelity images it produced before finetuning. This manifests as mode collapse, loss of subject identity, or failure to follow text prompts, which the authors observe in methods like DreamBooth and LoRA when trained for too many iterations.

It is also worth noting why the goal is to *preserve* hyperspherical energy rather than *minimize* it. Prior work by Liu et al. (2021) on orthogonal over-parameterized training minimizes hyperspherical energy for classification, since uniformly spaced neurons reduce redundancy and improve generalization. But for finetuning a pretrained generative model, minimizing the energy would push all neurons toward uniform spacing on the hypersphere, destroying the specific semantic structure learned during pretraining. The paper explicitly states that "minimizing hyperspherical energy could destroy the original semantic structures." The objective is instead to maintain the pretrained angular relationships while allowing enough flexibility to adapt to the downstream task—striking what the authors call the "trade-off between flexibility and regularity."
