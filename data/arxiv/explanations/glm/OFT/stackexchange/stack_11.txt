\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### How does the 2D Fourier transform analogy justify using angular information for finetuning?
Question:
The paper draws an analogy to 2D Fourier transform, saying that the phase spectrum (angular information) preserves the major part of semantics, and that the phase spectrum along with a random magnitude spectrum can reconstruct an image without losing semantics. I find this analogy confusing in the context of neural network finetuning. The Fourier transform decomposes images into frequency components — what is the neural network analog of "phase" and "magnitude" here? Are neuron directions the analog of phase and neuron magnitudes the analog of magnitude spectrum? If so, this seems like a loose analogy. Can someone clarify whether this is meant as a rigorous connection or just intuition, and how it specifically motivates OFT?
Answer:
The 2D Fourier transform analogy in the paper is intended as intuition rather than a rigorous mathematical connection, and the parallel works at the level of "angular information preserves semantics" rather than through a direct isomorphism between Fourier components and neural network parameters.

In the 2D Fourier transform, an image is decomposed into sinusoidal basis functions, and each coefficient is a complex number with a magnitude and a phase. The well-known empirical observation is that if you keep the phase spectrum (the angular information between the input image and each basis function) but replace the magnitude spectrum with random values, the reconstructed image still recognizably preserves the semantic content of the original. The phase encodes "where things are" spatially, while the magnitude mainly encodes contrast and texture intensity.

The paper draws a loose but instructive parallel: in a neural network layer, the output $z = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{x}$ involves inner products between the input $\mathbf{x}$ and each neuron weight $\mathbf{w}_i$. If you decompose this inner product into a magnitude component $\|\mathbf{w}_i\| \cdot \|\mathbf{x}\|$ and an angular component $\cos\theta_i$ (where $\theta_i$ is the angle between $\mathbf{w}_i$ and $\mathbf{x}$), the paper argues that the angular component is what carries semantic meaning. This is supported by the toy autoencoder experiment (Figure 1 in the paper), where replacing the standard inner product with purely angular information (cosine similarity) during inference almost perfectly reconstructs the input images, while using only magnitude information produces no recognizable content. The paper also cites prior work (SphereNet, decoupled angular margins) showing that normalizing all neurons onto a unit hypersphere during training preserves model capacity and generalization, reinforcing that directions matter more than magnitudes.

So yes, your reading is essentially correct: neuron directions are the analog of phase, and neuron magnitudes are the analog of the magnitude spectrum. The analogy is deliberately loose — there is no claim that a neural network layer literally performs a Fourier transform. Rather, the paper uses this as conceptual motivation for the hypothesis that "the angles between neurons are crucial for representing the knowledge of neural networks," which is then empirically supported by the toy experiment and prior literature on hyperspherical learning.

This directly motivates OFT in two steps. First, if neuron directions encode semantics, then to semantically modify generated images (the goal of both subject-driven and controllable generation), finetuning should change neuron directions. Second, however, changing directions with too much freedom will destroy the pretrained model's existing semantic structure. OFT's solution is to apply a shared orthogonal transformation $\mathbf{R}$ to all neurons in a layer, which rotates or reflects the entire set of neurons together. This provably preserves all pairwise angles (and hence the hyperspherical energy) while still allowing enough flexibility to adapt to downstream tasks. The analogy thus provides the intuition for *why* one should focus on angular structure, and OFT provides the mechanism for *how* to modify it safely.
