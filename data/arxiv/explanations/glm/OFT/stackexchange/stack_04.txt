\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### How does the COFT deviation constraint work with the Neumann series approximation?
Question:
In the COFT section, the paper constrains $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ and says they use the Neumann series to approximate $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ as $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$. From this, they claim the constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ becomes $\|\bm{Q} - \bm{0}\| \leq \epsilon'$. I have several confusions: (1) What is the Neumann series and when is it valid? (2) How exactly does $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ translate to $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'$ — what is the relationship between $\epsilon$ and $\epsilon'$? (3) How is this enforced via projected gradient descent in practice?
Answer:
The Neumann series is the matrix generalization of the geometric series. For any square matrix $\bm{A}$ with operator norm (spectral norm) $\|\bm{A}\|_{\text{op}} < 1$, the series $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \bm{A}^k = \bm{I} + \bm{A} + \bm{A}^2 + \cdots$ converges, and its sum equals $(\bm{I} - \bm{A})^{-1}$. This is the Neumann series, and it is valid precisely when $\|\bm{A}\|_{\text{op}} < 1$, which ensures that the higher-order terms decay geometrically.

In the paper's setting, the Cayley parameterization gives $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric. Applying the Neumann series to $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{A} = \bm{Q}$, we get $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} = \bm{I} + \bm{Q} + \bm{Q}^2 + \cdots$, provided $\|\bm{Q}\|_{\text{op}} < 1$. Multiplying through by $(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})$ and collecting terms yields $\bm{R} = \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + 2\bm{Q}^2 + \cdots = \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$. The key observation is that when $\|\bm{Q}\|$ is small, the higher-order terms $\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$ become negligible, so $\bm{R} - \bm{I} \approx 2\bm{Q}$.

This approximation directly establishes the relationship between $\epsilon$ and $\epsilon'$. Since $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \approx 2\|\bm{Q}\|$ when $\|\bm{Q}\|$ is small, the constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ translates to $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon/2$ to leading order, giving $\epsilon' \approx \epsilon/2$. More precisely, including the next-order term, we have $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq 2\|\bm{Q}\| + C\|\bm{Q}\|^2$ for some constant $C$ depending on the norm, so the exact relationship between $\epsilon$ and $\epsilon'$ is slightly nonlinear. However, the paper treats $\epsilon'$ as a separate tunable hyperparameter rather than fixing an exact mapping, which is reasonable because the approximation is only accurate for small $\|\bm{Q}\|$, and the practical choice of $\epsilon'$ will depend on the desired trade-off between flexibility and stability.

In practice, the constraint $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'$ is enforced via projected gradient descent. After each gradient update step on the unconstrained parameter $\bm{Q}$, the algorithm checks whether $\|\bm{Q}\|$ exceeds $\epsilon'$. If it does, $\bm{Q}$ is projected back onto the ball of radius $\epsilon'$. For the Frobenius norm, this projection is simply a rescaling: $\bm{Q} \leftarrow \frac{\epsilon'}{\|\bm{Q}\|_F} \cdot \bm{Q}$ when $\|\bm{Q}\|_F > \epsilon'$. For the operator (spectral) norm, the projection requires computing the SVD $\bm{Q} = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, clipping each singular value to $\epsilon'$ (i.e., $\sigma_i \leftarrow \min(\sigma_i, \epsilon')$), and reconstructing $\bm{Q} \leftarrow \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}'\bm{V}^\top$. Since $\bm{Q}$ is initialized as the zero matrix (which gives $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, the identity initialization), the constraint is satisfied at the start of training, and projected gradient descent ensures it remains satisfied throughout. This is much simpler than trying to enforce $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ directly on the Cayley-transformed matrix $\bm{R}$, because $\bm{R}$ lives on the orthogonal manifold where standard projections are computationally expensive. By moving the constraint to the unconstrained parameter $\bm{Q}$ via the Neumann approximation, COFT decouples the orthogonality constraint (handled by the Cayley parameterization) from the deviation constraint (handled by projected gradient descent on $\bm{Q}$), making both tractable.
