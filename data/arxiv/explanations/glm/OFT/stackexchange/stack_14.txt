\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Why is the hyperspherical energy defined with inverse distance, and what happens when two neurons are identical?
Question:
The hyperspherical energy is defined as $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$. This uses inverse Euclidean distance between normalized neurons. I have several concerns: (1) What happens when two neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}_j$ are identical or nearly identical? The term would blow up to infinity. (2) Why inverse distance rather than, say, cosine similarity directly? (3) Does the paper address numerical stability of this computation? (4) Is this a standard definition from the hyperspherical energy literature, and if so, what's the motivation for this specific form?
Answer:
The hyperspherical energy definition used in this paper comes from the literature on learning with angular information, specifically cited to Liu et al. (2018) and Liu et al. (2021). The form $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ is indeed a standard definition from that line of work, and it is closely related to Riesz energy or potential energy concepts from physics and optimal point configuration theory on spheres. The intuition is that of a repulsive potential: neurons that are close together on the unit hypersphere contribute high energy, creating a "force" that encourages uniform spreading. The inverse distance creates a singularity at zero distance, which serves as a strong penalty against neuron redundancy — exactly the property you want when measuring how uniformly distributed neurons are.

Regarding your concern about identical or nearly identical neurons causing the energy to blow up: this is a valid observation in general, but it is largely sidestepped in this paper because OFT never actually computes the hyperspherical energy directly. The key insight is that the paper does not minimize $\|\text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\|$ as a loss term. Instead, it achieves this minimum by construction. As the paper states, the minimum of zero is attained whenever $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ for an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, because orthogonal transformations preserve all pairwise Euclidean distances between normalized neurons. So OFT simply learns an orthogonal transformation and the energy preservation is guaranteed mathematically, with no need to evaluate the energy sum numerically. The paper explicitly notes: "One can easily observe that the attainable minimum is zero... The minimum can be achieved as long as $\bm{W}$ and $\bm{W}^0$ differ only up to a rotation or reflection."

As for why inverse Euclidean distance rather than cosine similarity directly: for unit vectors, the two are monotonically related since $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\| = \sqrt{2(1 - \cos\theta_{ij})}$, so they encode the same angular information. However, the inverse distance form has the desirable property of creating a singularity at zero separation, which more sharply penalizes neuron collapse. Cosine similarity alone (without inversion) would not create this singularity — two identical neurons would simply contribute a value of $1$, which does not stand out dramatically from other terms. The inverse distance form makes the energy more sensitive to redundancy, which is the whole point of using it as a measure of uniformity.

The paper does not explicitly discuss numerical stability of the $\text{HE}$ computation, likely because, as explained above, the computation is avoided entirely. The practical stability concerns in the paper are instead addressed through the COFT variant, which adds an explicit constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ to limit how far the orthogonal transformation can deviate from the identity, and through the Cayley parameterization for ensuring orthogonality. These mechanisms handle stability at the level of the transformation itself rather than at the level of energy computation.
