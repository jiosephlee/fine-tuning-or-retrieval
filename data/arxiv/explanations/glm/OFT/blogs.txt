# Teaching Old Dogs New Tricks: How Orthogonal Finetuning Keeps AI Models from Forgetting

## The Finetuning Dilemma

Large text-to-image diffusion models, like Stable Diffusion, are incredibly good at generating photorealistic images from text prompts. However, guiding them to perform specific downstream tasks—such as generating images of a particular subject or following a structural control signal like an edge map—requires finetuning. The challenge is that finetuning often causes the model to "forget" its pretraining knowledge. Existing methods try to prevent this by keeping the updated weights close to the original ones using a small learning rate or low-rank updates like LoRA. But simply minimizing the Euclidean distance between the old and new weights isn't enough to preserve the complex semantic relationships the model learned during pretraining.

## It's All About Angles

The authors of the paper introduce a new way to think about model knowledge: hyperspherical energy. This concept characterizes the pairwise relationships between neurons by looking at the angles between them on a unit hypersphere. The authors found that these angles are crucial for storing semantic information. They demonstrate this with a toy experiment showing that the angular information of neurons can almost perfectly recover input images, while magnitude information is largely useless. If finetuning disrupts these angles, the model's core knowledge is destroyed.

## The OFT Solution: Rotating Without Breaking

To preserve these critical angles, the authors propose Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). The core idea is to update the model's weights using orthogonal transformations. An orthogonal transformation is essentially a rotation or reflection. Think of it like adjusting the angle of a camera: you can change the viewpoint, but the fundamental structure and relationships of the objects in the scene remain intact. By applying the same orthogonal transformation to all neurons in a layer, OFT provably preserves the hyperspherical energy, ensuring the model's pretrained knowledge is maintained.

Formally, instead of directly updating the weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, OFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ and updates the weights as $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$, subject to the orthogonality constraint $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$. This is a multiplicative update, unlike the additive updates used in LoRA.

## Keeping Things Stable with COFT

While OFT is effective, sometimes you want to limit how much the model changes. For this, the authors introduce Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT). COFT adds an additional constraint that limits how far the orthogonal matrix can deviate from the identity matrix: $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. This ensures the finetuned model stays within a small neighborhood of the pretrained model, acting as an explicit brake on drastic changes. This makes finetuning even more stable and easier to tune.

## Efficiency and Inference

You might think applying matrix transformations would be slow or parameter-heavy. OFT addresses this using a block-diagonal structure for the orthogonal matrix, which significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters. The authors use Cayley parameterization to efficiently generate these orthogonal matrices. Importantly, because the orthogonal matrix can simply be multiplied into the pretrained weights, OFT introduces zero additional computational overhead during inference. The model runs just as fast as the original.

## Results That Speak for Themselves

The authors apply OFT and COFT to two challenging tasks:
*   **Subject-driven generation:** Generating images of a specific subject in new contexts.
*   **Controllable generation:** Generating images that follow additional control signals like edge maps or segmentation maps.

In both tasks, OFT and COFT outperform existing methods like DreamBooth, LoRA, and ControlNet. They converge significantly faster—sometimes needing less than half the training epochs—and are much more stable. The generated images are higher fidelity, and the control is more accurate. For example, in subject-driven generation, OFT perfectly preserves the subject's identity while accurately following the text prompt, whereas LoRA often loses the subject identity and DreamBooth fails to follow the prompt.

## Conclusion

Orthogonal Finetuning offers a principled way to adapt powerful AI models to new tasks without causing them to forget what they already know. By focusing on preserving the angular relationships between neurons, OFT finds a sweet spot between flexibility and regularity. It's a simple yet powerful idea that leads to faster convergence, better stability, and superior image quality, all without adding any cost at inference time. This makes OFT a highly practical tool for anyone looking to customize large diffusion models.



# Your Dog in Paris: How AI Learns to Generate Custom Subjects in New Contexts

Imagine having a few photos of your beloved dog and wanting to see what it would look like wearing a yellow shirt while strolling through the streets of Paris. Thanks to advances in text-to-image diffusion models, this is now possible through a task called **subject-driven generation**: given just a handful of images of a subject and a text prompt, the model generates images of that subject in entirely new contexts.

The challenge, however, is doing this well. How do you teach a powerful pretrained model about your specific dog without forgetting everything it already knows about generating high-quality, diverse images? As it turns out, existing methods struggle with this balance. In this post, we explore a new finetuning method called **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** that tackles this problem in a principled way, achieving better subject preservation and text adherence than prior approaches like DreamBooth and LoRA.

## The Problem: Finetuning Without Forgetting

Text-to-image diffusion models like Stable Diffusion are trained on massive datasets and can generate photorealistic images from text descriptions. But text alone is often ambiguous—you might want something more specific than what a generic prompt can produce.

Subject-driven generation addresses this by finetuning the model on a few images of your subject (say, your dog) along with a text prompt (say, "a photo of [your dog] wearing a yellow shirt in Paris"). The finetuned model should then generate images that:

- Preserve the identity and key features of your subject
- Follow the text prompt accurately
- Maintain the overall image quality and diversity of the original pretrained model

Existing finetuning methods typically either update the model weights with a small learning rate (DreamBooth) or add a small learnable component to the weights (LoRA). While these methods can work, they have notable limitations. DreamBooth can collapse after too many training iterations, producing distorted images. LoRA often fails to preserve the subject's identity, as we will see.

## A Geometric Perspective on Finetuning

The key insight behind OFT comes from looking at neural network weights through a geometric lens. In a neural network layer, each neuron can be thought of as a vector in a high-dimensional space. The angles between these neurons encode important semantic information—what the model "knows" about generating different types of images.

The paper provides a compelling demonstration of this: when training a convolutional autoencoder on flower images, the angular information of neurons alone can almost perfectly reconstruct the input images, while the magnitude information contains essentially no useful information. This suggests that the directions of neurons, not their magnitudes, carry the semantic content.

OFT builds on this insight. Instead of updating weights freely or adding low-rank perturbations, OFT learns a layer-shared **orthogonal transformation** that rotates or reflects all neurons in a layer while preserving the angles between them. This is achieved by multiplying the pretrained weight matrix $W^0$ by an orthogonal matrix $R$:

$$z = (R \cdot W^0)^\top x, \quad \text{s.t.} \quad R^\top R = R R^\top = I$$

Because orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles, the "hyperspherical energy"—a measure of how neurons are distributed on the unit hypersphere—remains unchanged. This means the pretrained model's semantic structure is preserved during finetuning, while still allowing enough flexibility to adapt to the new subject.

## Constrained OFT: Even More Stability

The paper also introduces a variant called **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)**, which adds an additional constraint limiting how far the orthogonal matrix can deviate from the identity matrix:

$$\|R - I\| \leq \epsilon$$

This keeps the finetuned model within a small neighborhood of the pretrained model, providing even greater stability. The parameter $\epsilon$ controls the trade-off: larger values allow more flexibility, while smaller values keep the model closer to its pretrained state.

## Putting It to the Test: Your Dog in a Yellow Shirt

The paper evaluates OFT on subject-driven generation using the DreamBooth framework, comparing against DreamBooth itself and LoRA. The results are striking.

### Stability Across Training Iterations

One of the most notable findings is how differently these methods behave as training progresses:

- **After 400 iterations**: Both DreamBooth and OFT achieve good control over the generated images, while LoRA fails to preserve the subject identity.
- **After 2000 iterations**: DreamBooth starts generating collapsed, distorted images. LoRA fails to follow the text prompt correctly—when asked to generate the dog wearing a yellow shirt, it produces a dog with yellow fur instead. Meanwhile, both OFT and COFT maintain stable and consistent control, generating the subject accurately while following the text prompt.

This stability matters in practice. With DreamBooth and LoRA, you need to carefully tune the number of training iterations for each subject—too few and the subject isn't captured, too many and the model degrades. With OFT, you can set a relatively large iteration number without worrying about collapse, making the method much easier to use.

### Quantitative Results

The paper measures subject fidelity using DINO and CLIP-I metrics (which compare generated images to real images of the subject), text prompt fidelity using CLIP-T, and sample diversity using LPIPS. Across 30 random seeds for each method, both OFT and COFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA in DINO and CLIP-I by a considerable margin, while achieving comparable or slightly better performance in prompt fidelity and diversity.

### Qualitative Results

The visual examples tell a clear story. In one example using a bowl as the subject, LoRA completely loses the subject identity, while OFT and COFT preserve it faithfully. DreamBooth, while preserving the subject, often fails to follow the text prompt accurately. OFT and COFT manage to do both simultaneously—preserving the subject while generating images that match the text description.

## Why OFT Works So Well

The paper offers several explanations for why OFT excels at subject-driven generation:

- **Targeting the right information**: Since angular information carries most of the semantic content, updating neuron directions (which OFT does) is the most effective way to modify the generated subject. LoRA's low-rank updates and DreamBooth's small learning rate updates are less targeted.

- **Smooth optimization landscape**: OFT effectively finetunes neurons on a smooth hypersphere manifold, which provides a better optimization landscape for gradient-based training.

- **Balanced flexibility and regularity**: OFT finds a sweet spot between the unconstrained flexibility of standard finetuning (which is unstable) and overly rigid constraints (which prevent adaptation). By preserving pairwise angles between neurons, OFT maintains enough structure to prevent collapse while allowing enough freedom to learn the new subject.

## Looking Ahead

Subject-driven generation is just one application of OFT. The paper also demonstrates its effectiveness on controllable generation tasks, where the model must follow additional control signals like edge maps or segmentation maps alongside text prompts.

What makes OFT particularly appealing is that it introduces **no additional inference overhead**—the learned orthogonal matrix can be multiplied into the pretrained weights ahead of time, so the deployed model runs at the same speed as the original.

For anyone who has struggled with finetuning text-to-image models—carefully tuning learning rates and iteration counts, watching models collapse after training too long—OFT offers a more stable, principled approach. Your dog can wear a yellow shirt in Paris, and it will actually look like your dog.



# Beyond Text Prompts: Giving AI Precise Visual Control with OFT

Text-to-image diffusion models like Stable Diffusion are incredibly good at generating photorealistic images from text prompts. However, text can often be ambiguous. If you want to generate an image with a very specific spatial layout, text alone usually falls short. You cannot easily describe the exact edge boundaries or the precise placement of every object. 

This is where controllable generation comes in. The goal is to guide the model using additional visual signals—like Canny edge maps, segmentation maps, or facial landmarks—alongside a text prompt. While methods like ControlNet and T2I-Adapter have made impressive strides in this area, they often require a large number of trainable parameters and extensive training data. A new finetuning method called Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) offers a more efficient and precise alternative.

## The OFT Approach: Preserving Structure with Orthogonality

When finetuning a large pretrained model, the biggest challenge is adapting it to a new task without destroying the general knowledge it learned during pretraining. Most finetuning methods, like LoRA, update weights by adding a low-rank matrix. However, this doesn't explicitly guarantee that the structural relationships between neurons are preserved.

OFT takes a different, more principled approach. It is built on the observation that the angles between neurons in a layer (their pairwise directions) are crucial for storing semantic information. To modify the model's behavior without losing its pretrained knowledge, OFT applies a layer-shared orthogonal transformation to the neurons. 

Mathematically, for a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, OFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ and updates the forward pass to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, subject to the orthogonality constraint $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R} \bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$. Because $\bm{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix, it acts as a rotation or reflection. This provably preserves the hyperspherical energy—which characterizes the pairwise relationships between neurons on a unit hypersphere—ensuring the model's core generative abilities remain intact.

## Efficiency and Stability

To make the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ parameter-efficient, OFT represents it as a block-diagonal matrix. This drastically reduces the number of trainable parameters compared to ControlNet and T2I-Adapter. 

The benefits of this approach are twofold:

*   **Faster and Smoother Convergence:** OFT converges much faster than existing methods. While ControlNet is known for a "sudden convergence" phenomenon where controllability abruptly emerges after many epochs, OFT converges smoothly and predictably. In facial landmark control tasks, OFT reaches in 8 epochs the same level of accuracy that LoRA takes 20 epochs to achieve.
*   **High Data Efficiency:** OFT is remarkably data-efficient. In controllable generation tasks, OFT can converge and achieve strong control using only 5% of the ADE20K dataset, making it highly practical when training data is scarce.

Furthermore, OFT introduces zero additional computational overhead during inference. Once training is complete, the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can be multiplied directly into the pretrained weights to get an equivalent weight matrix $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$. The resulting model runs just as fast as the original pretrained model.

## Measuring Success: The Control Consistency Metric

Evaluating how well a model follows a control signal is surprisingly difficult. A generated image might look good, but did it accurately follow the input edge map? To answer this, the paper introduces a novel control consistency metric.

Instead of relying on subjective human evaluation, this metric works by extracting the control signal directly from the generated image and comparing it to the original input control signal. For example:

*   For Canny edge-to-image generation, the metric extracts edges from the generated image and compares them to the input edges using Intersection over Union (IoU) and F1 score.
*   For segmentation map-to-image generation, it computes the mean IoU and overall accuracy between the input map and the segmentation extracted from the output image.
*   For landmark-to-face generation, it extracts facial landmarks from the generated face and computes the mean $\ell_2$ distance to the original input landmarks.

This provides an objective, quantifiable measure of a model's controllability.

## Results and Implications

When evaluated against ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA, OFT (and its constrained variant, COFT) consistently demonstrates superior control accuracy and image fidelity. Whether the task is generating images from rough Canny edges, precise segmentation maps, or challenging facial landmarks, OFT produces high-fidelity images that align much more closely with the input control signals.

By finding a sweet spot between flexibility and structural preservation, OFT enables precise visual control over text-to-image models, requiring fewer parameters and less data than existing state-of-the-art methods.



# Why Angles Matter More Than Magnitude: The Surprising Math Behind AI's Semantic Understanding

## The Problem with Finetuning Large Diffusion Models
Large text-to-image diffusion models have an impressive ability to generate photorealistic images from text prompts. However, guiding these powerful models for specific downstream tasks—like generating images of a particular subject or following an additional control signal like a segmentation map—requires careful finetuning. The goal is to adapt the model without destroying its pretrained generative abilities. 

Existing finetuning methods often assume that keeping the finetuned weights close in Euclidean distance to the pretrained weights is enough to preserve the model's knowledge. But is this the best way to think about semantic preservation? A recent paper introducing Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) argues that we should be looking at something more structural: the angles between neurons.

## The Core Insight: Neuron Directions Store Semantics
To understand why OFT works, we have to look at a fundamental question: what part of a neuron actually carries semantic meaning? Is it its magnitude or its direction? 

To answer this, the authors conducted a simple but illuminating toy experiment. They trained a standard convolutional autoencoder on images of flowers. During training, the network used the standard inner product to produce feature maps. Let $z$ denote the element output of a convolution kernel $\bm{w}$ and an input sliding window $\bm{x}$. 

During testing, they compared three different ways to generate the feature map:
*   The standard inner product used in training.
*   Using only the magnitude information of the neurons.
*   Using only the angular (directional) information of the neurons.

The results were striking. The angular information alone could almost perfectly recover the original input images, while the magnitude information contained virtually no useful semantic information. This suggests that the directions of neurons—not their magnitudes—are the primary carriers of semantic knowledge in a neural network.

## A Lesson from 2D Fourier Transforms
This phenomenon isn't entirely unprecedented. The authors draw a parallel to the 2D Fourier transform, where an image can be decomposed into a magnitude spectrum and a phase spectrum. The phase spectrum, which represents the angular information between the input and the basis functions, preserves the major part of an image's semantics. 

In fact, if you take the phase spectrum of an image and pair it with a completely random magnitude spectrum, you can still reconstruct the original image without losing its core semantics. The same principle seems to apply to neural networks: changing neuron directions is the key to semantically modifying the generated image. 

## Finetuning by Preserving Angles
If neuron directions carry the semantics, then finetuning a model to perform a new task means we need to change these directions. However, if we change them with too much freedom, we risk destroying the pretrained knowledge. 

This is where Orthogonal Finetuning comes in. OFT is built on the hypothesis that the angles *between* neurons are crucial for representing the network's knowledge. To constrain the degree of freedom during finetuning, OFT preserves the pairwise angles between all neurons in a layer.

How does it do this? By applying a layer-shared orthogonal transformation. Mathematically, an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ satisfies the constraint $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$. If we transform the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ by multiplying them by this orthogonal matrix to get the finetuned weights $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, the angles between all the neurons remain exactly the same. 

In the language of the paper, this preserves the "hyperspherical energy"—a measure of the pairwise relational structure among neurons on a unit hypersphere. By ensuring the hyperspherical energy of the finetuned model matches the pretrained model, OFT finds a sweet spot between flexibility and regularity. It can adjust the canonical coordinate system to modify semantics, but it does so safely without collapsing the pretrained structure. 

## Conclusion
The next time you think about finetuning a large generative model, remember the autoencoder experiment. Magnitudes are largely irrelevant; the semantic magic lies in the angles. By respecting this geometric structure through orthogonal transformations, OFT provides a principled, stable, and highly efficient way to guide powerful diffusion models into new creative territories.



# LoRA vs. OFT: Two Paths to Efficient AI Model Finetuning

Large text-to-image diffusion models are incredibly powerful, but guiding them for specific downstream tasks—like generating images of a particular subject or following an edge map—requires careful finetuning. The goal is to adapt the model without destroying its pretrained generative abilities. Two prominent paths to parameter-efficient finetuning have emerged: LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) and OFT (Orthogonal Finetuning). While both aim to preserve pretrained knowledge, they take fundamentally different approaches to how they update the model's weights. 

## The LoRA Approach: Low-Rank Additive Updates

LoRA tackles finetuning by adding a small, learnable low-rank component to the pretrained weights. Conceptually, if $\bm{M}^0$ is the pretrained model's weights and $\bm{M}$ is the finetuned model's weights, LoRA implicitly assumes that the weight update should have a low rank, meaning $\text{rank}(\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0)=r'$ where $r'$ is a small number. This additive update prevents the pretrained weights from shifting too dramatically.

However, LoRA relies on the implicit assumption that a smaller Euclidean distance between the finetuned model and the pretrained model equates to better preservation of the model's generative performance. While using a small learning rate and low-rank constraints helps, this Euclidean measure alone does not fully capture the semantic preservation of the model. It can still be too flexible, sometimes leading to model collapse or failing to preserve subject identity during generation.

## The OFT Approach: Preserving Neuronal Geometry

OFT takes a more structural approach. Instead of additive updates, OFT uses multiplicative orthogonal transformations. The core idea is based on the observation that the angles (or directions) between neurons in a layer play a major role in storing semantic information. To preserve this, OFT aims to maintain the "hyperspherical energy"—a measure of the pairwise relationships between neurons on a unit hypersphere.

The forward pass in OFT changes from $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, subject to the orthogonality constraint $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$, where $\bm{R}$ is a learnable layer-shared orthogonal matrix and $\bm{W}^0$ is the pretrained weight matrix. By applying the same orthogonal transformation to all neurons in a layer, OFT provably preserves the pairwise angles between them. This keeps the hyperspherical energy exactly the same as in the pretrained model, ensuring the intrinsic pretrained structure is not destroyed. 

To make this computationally efficient, OFT uses a block-diagonal structure for $\bm{R}$ and employs Cayley parameterization to guarantee orthogonality without expensive calculations like the Gram-Schmidt process. 

## COFT: Adding an Explicit Deviation Constraint

While OFT provides a strong structural guarantee, the authors further introduce Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) to improve stability even more. COFT adds an explicit constraint that limits how far the finetuned model can deviate from the pretrained model: $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$, where $\bm{I}$ is the identity matrix and $\epsilon$ is a small error hyperparameter. 

This constraint ensures that the orthogonal transformation stays within a small neighborhood of the identity matrix, keeping the finetuned weights close to the original ones. Enforcing this constraint is done by limiting the corresponding skew-symmetric matrix used in the Cayley parameterization, making COFT a robust combination of minimal hyperspherical energy difference and bounded deviation.

## Comparing the Trade-offs

When comparing LoRA and OFT, several trade-offs emerge across parameter efficiency, convergence, and stability.

**Parameter Efficiency:** LoRA exploits a low-rank structure to reduce trainable parameters, while OFT exploits the sparsity structure of block-diagonal orthogonality. For a weight matrix of size $128 \times 128$, LoRA with rank $r'=8$ uses $2,048$ parameters. In contrast, OFT with $8$ blocks uses only $960$ parameters. Generally, if both methods scale their parameters relative to the neuron dimension $d$, LoRA's complexity is $\mathcal{O}(d^2 + dn)$, whereas OFT's complexity drops to $\mathcal{O}(d)$. 

**Convergence Speed:** OFT converges significantly faster than LoRA. In controllable generation tasks like landmark-to-face translation, OFT reaches in 8 epochs the same level of performance that takes LoRA 20 epochs to achieve. This faster convergence is partly because changing neuron directions (which OFT directly targets) is the most effective way to modify semantics, and optimizing on a smooth hypersphere manifold yields a better optimization landscape.

**Finetuning Stability:** OFT and COFT offer much better stability. Standard finetuning is highly flexible but prone to model collapse. LoRA often struggles to preserve subject identity or follow control signals accurately. OFT strikes a balance by preserving pairwise neuron angles, leading to stable and predictable training dynamics. COFT further enhances this by allowing users to set a relatively large number of training iterations without the risk of degrading the model, removing the need to carefully tune the iteration count.

## When to Use Which?

LoRA remains a viable option for its simplicity and effectiveness in many language and vision tasks. However, if your primary concerns are finetuning stability, convergence speed, and preserving the rich generative abilities of text-to-image diffusion models, OFT and COFT offer a principled advantage. By preserving the angular geometry of neurons, OFT allows you to adapt powerful models to new tasks with fewer parameters, faster training, and a much lower risk of catastrophic forgetting. Plus, unlike some adapter methods, OFT introduces no additional computational overhead during inference, making it highly practical for deployment.
