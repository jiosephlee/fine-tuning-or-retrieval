\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 1: Introduction to Text-to-Image Diffusion Models and the Finetuning Problem

#

## Overview of Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

In recent years, text-to-image diffusion models have emerged as a powerful class of generative models capable of producing photorealistic images from textual descriptions. These models represent a convergence of two major lines of research: diffusion-based generative modeling and vision-language representation learning. The rapid development in diffusion models, which learn to generate data by progressively denoising random noise, combined with advances in learning joint representations of images and text, has enabled a leap forward in the quality and controllability of generated images.

Several landmark models have defined this landscape. GLIDE and Imagen train diffusion models directly in the pixel space. GLIDE jointly trains a text encoder alongside a diffusion prior using paired text-image data, allowing the model to learn the alignment between language and visual content during training. Imagen, by contrast, employs a frozen pretrained text encoder, leveraging the representational power of a language model that has already been trained on large-scale text corpora. Training in pixel space means that the diffusion process operates directly on image pixels, which can be computationally expensive but allows for direct supervision on the image signal.

Stable Diffusion and DALL-E2 take a different approach by training diffusion models in a latent space rather than directly in pixel space. Stable Diffusion uses VQ-GAN to learn a visual codebook as its latent space, effectively compressing images into a lower-dimensional representation where the diffusion process is more tractable. DALL-E2 adopts CLIP to construct a joint latent embedding space for representing both images and text, providing a shared semantic space that naturally aligns the two modalities. By operating in latent space, these models achieve greater computational efficiency during both training and inference.

Beyond diffusion models, other generative paradigms have also been explored for text-to-image generation, including generative adversarial networks and autoregressive models. However, diffusion-based approaches have come to dominate the field due to their superior image quality and training stability. It is worth emphasizing that the finetuning methods discussed in this chapter are model-agnostic in principle and can be applied to any text-to-image diffusion model, regardless of whether it operates in pixel space or latent space. This generality is an important property, as new diffusion architectures continue to emerge.

## The Two Downstream Tasks: Subject-driven Generation and Controllable Generation

Despite the impressive capabilities of text-to-image diffusion models, text guidance alone can be ambiguous and insufficient for providing fine-grained and accurate control over the generated images. A text prompt such as "a dog in a park" specifies the general content of the desired image but leaves enormous freedom in how the dog looks, its pose, the composition of the scene, and countless other details. To address this shortcoming, two important downstream tasks have been identified that require adapting a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model to exert more precise control.

The first task is subject-driven generation. Given just a few images of a specific subject—perhaps a particular dog, a unique vase, or a person's face—the goal is to generate images of that same subject in a different context, guided by a text prompt. For example, if a user provides several photos of their pet dog and the text prompt "a dog sitting on the beach," the model should generate an image of that specific dog, preserving its identifying features, situated on a beach. The challenge here is to capture the subject-specific visual identity from only a handful of images and to faithfully reproduce it in novel contexts without losing the subject's distinctive characteristics. This task was popularized by DreamBooth, which finetunes the text-to-image diffusion model using a reconstruction loss and a class-specific prior preservation loss, along with a customized token representing the subject.

The second task is controllable generation. Here, the model is provided with an additional control signal beyond the text prompt—such as Canny edge maps, segmentation maps, or facial landmarks—and the goal is to generate images that follow both the control signal and the text prompt. For instance, a user might provide a rough sketch (Canny edge map) of a landscape along with the text prompt "a mountain landscape at sunset," and the model should generate a photorealistic image whose structure matches the sketch while incorporating the colors and atmosphere described by the text. ControlNet and T2I-Adapter are prominent methods for this task, both of which finetune a pretrained diffusion model to accept additional control signals.

Both of these tasks fundamentally reduce to the same core problem: how to effectively finetune a text-to-image diffusion model so that it acquires new capabilities—whether recognizing a new subject or responding to a new control signal—without losing the generative performance it gained during pretraining. The model must adapt to the downstream task while retaining its ability to produce high-fidelity, diverse, and semantically meaningful images. This shared challenge motivates the development of principled finetuning methods that go beyond naive approaches.

## Desiderata for Effective Finetuning: Training Efficiency and Generalizability Preservation

Given that both subject-driven generation and controllable generation require finetuning a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model, it is essential to identify the properties that distinguish a good finetuning method from a poor one. Two key desiderata emerge from the practical demands of these tasks.

The first desideratum is training efficiency. Text-to-image diffusion models are large, often containing hundreds of millions or even billions of parameters. Finetuning such models from scratch or with a large number of trainable parameters can be prohibitively expensive in terms of both computation and memory. Moreover, the downstream tasks often involve limited training data: subject-driven generation may use only a few images of a subject, and controllable generation may benefit from rapid adaptation with small datasets. An effective finetuning method should therefore have fewer trainable parameters and require fewer training epochs to reach satisfactory performance. This efficiency is not merely a matter of convenience; it directly impacts the feasibility of deploying these models in real-world applications where resources are constrained and rapid adaptation is valued.

The second desideratum is generalizability preservation. A pretrained text-to-image diffusion model has learned a rich and diverse generative distribution over images, capable of producing high-fidelity outputs across a wide range of content and styles. When finetuning the model for a downstream task, it is crucial that this pretraining generative performance is preserved. The model should not forget how to generate realistic images, nor should it collapse to producing a narrow range of outputs. This is sometimes referred to as avoiding catastrophic forgetting, where the model's pretrained knowledge is overwritten by the finetuning objective. Preserving generalizability means that the finetuned model should still produce high-fidelity and diverse images, even as it acquires new task-specific capabilities. The tension between adaptation and preservation is at the heart of the finetuning problem: one wants to change the model enough to perform the downstream task well, but not so much that the pretrained generative ability is lost.

These two desiderata—training efficiency and generalizability preservation—are not independent. A method that uses very few trainable parameters may be efficient but could lack the flexibility to adapt to the downstream task. Conversely, a method that freely updates all model parameters may adapt quickly but risks destroying pretrained knowledge. An ideal finetuning method strikes a balance between these competing demands, achieving strong task performance with minimal training while preserving the generative capabilities of the pretrained model.

## Limitations of Existing Finetuning Strategies

Existing finetuning strategies for text-to-image diffusion models can be broadly categorized into two approaches, each with significant limitations.

The first approach is standard finetuning with a small learning rate. In this strategy, the pretrained model's neuron weights are directly updated via gradient descent, but the learning rate is kept small to limit the magnitude of the updates. DreamBooth exemplifies this approach for subject-driven generation. The intuition is straightforward: a small learning rate implicitly encourages the finetuned model to remain close to the pretrained model in parameter space, thereby preserving the pretrained generative ability to some extent. However, this approach has several drawbacks. First, even with a small learning rate, there is no formal guarantee that the pretrained generative performance will be preserved. The model can still drift away from its pretrained state in ways that degrade its generalization ability, especially after many training iterations. Second, the choice of learning rate and the number of training epochs become critical hyperparameters that must be carefully tuned for each task or even each subject. Too many iterations can cause the model to collapse, generating images that have lost the diversity and fidelity of the pretrained model. Too few iterations may result in insufficient adaptation to the downstream task. This sensitivity makes the approach fragile and difficult to use reliably in practice.

The second approach is re-parameterized finetuning, which adds a small learnable component to the pretrained model's weights rather than updating them directly. LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) is a prominent example, which assumes that the weight update during finetuning has a low-rank structure. Specifically, LoRA introduces an additive low-rank matrix to the pretrained weights, so the finetuned weight matrix is $\bm{M} = \bm{M}^0 + \Delta\bm{M}$ where $\bm{M}^0$ is the pretrained weight matrix, $\Delta\bm{M}$ is the low-rank update, and $\text{rank}(\Delta\bm{M}) = r'$ for some small $r'$. This reduces the number of trainable parameters while still allowing the model to adapt. However, LoRA also has limitations. The low-rank constraint restricts the form of the weight update, which may be insufficient to capture the transformations needed for effective adaptation. Moreover, like standard finetuning, LoRA does not provide any formal guarantee that the pretrained generative performance is preserved. The low-rank structure is an assumption about the nature of the finetuning update, but it is not grounded in a principled measure of what makes a finetuned model preserve the pretrained model's capabilities.

A fundamental difficulty shared by both approaches is the lack of a principled measure for quantifying the preservation of pretrained generative ability. Without such a measure, it is impossible to guarantee that a finetuning method will preserve the pretrained model's performance, and it becomes difficult to design finetuning strategies with formal guarantees. This gap motivates the search for a more structural and theoretically grounded approach to finetuning.

## The Problem with Euclidean Distance as a Measure of Semantic Preservation

A critical assumption underlying existing finetuning methods is that a smaller Euclidean distance between the finetuned model and the pretrained model indicates better preservation of the pretrained ability. In standard finetuning, the small learning rate implicitly minimizes $\|\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$, where $\bm{M}$ is the finetuned model's weights and $\bm{M}^0$ is the pretrained model's weights. In LoRA, the low-rank constraint $\text{rank}(\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0) = r'$ limits the complexity of the weight update, which indirectly keeps the Euclidean distance small when $r'$ is small.

While this assumption may occasionally hold, it has a fundamental limitation: the Euclidean difference between two sets of weights does not fully capture the degree of semantic preservation. Two models can have weights that are close in Euclidean distance but produce semantically very different outputs, and conversely, two models with larger Euclidean distance between their weights might still produce similar images. The reason is that Euclidean distance treats all weight coordinates independently and equally, ignoring the structural and relational properties of the weights that determine the model's behavior.

Consider, for example, two neurons in a layer. Their individual weight values might change by a small Euclidean amount, but if the angle between them changes significantly, the semantic information encoded by their pairwise relationship could be altered substantially. Conversely, a transformation that changes the absolute weight values but preserves the angles between neurons might maintain the model's semantic behavior. This suggests that the geometric relationships among neurons—particularly their angular relationships—are more relevant to semantic preservation than the raw Euclidean distance between weight matrices.

This observation is supported by empirical findings in the literature. Studies have shown that hyperspherical similarity, which is based on angular relationships, encodes semantic information well. Neural networks trained with neurons normalized onto a unit hypersphere yield comparable capacity and even better generalizability, implying that the direction of neurons captures the most important information from data. These findings suggest that a more structural measure of model difference—one that accounts for the relational geometry of neurons—would be a better predictor of semantic preservation than Euclidean distance alone.

The inadequacy of Euclidean distance also explains a practical pain point in finetuning: the need for very small learning rates. Because Euclidean distance is an imperfect proxy for semantic preservation, practitioners must be conservative and use tiny learning rates to avoid inadvertently destroying the pretrained model's capabilities. This conservatism slows down training and makes the finetuning process inefficient. A finetuning method that directly preserves the structurally relevant properties of the pretrained model would not need to rely on this blunt proxy and could potentially use larger learning rates or fewer training iterations while still maintaining semantic preservation.

## Motivation for a Structural Measure of Model Difference

The limitations of Euclidean distance as a measure of semantic preservation motivate the search for a more structural measure that captures the relational geometry of neurons. Such a measure should characterize the pairwise relationships among neurons in a way that reflects the semantic knowledge encoded in the pretrained model.

One such measure is hyperspherical energy, which is inspired by the empirical observation that hyperspherical similarity encodes semantic information. Hyperspherical energy is defined as the sum of hyperspherical similarities (such as cosine similarity) between all pairs of neurons in the same layer. For a weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1, \cdots, \bm{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where $\bm{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the $i$-th neuron, the hyperspherical energy is defined as:

$$\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$$

where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$ denotes the $i$-th normalized neuron. The hyperspherical energy captures the level of neuron uniformity on the unit hypersphere: when neurons are uniformly spaced, the energy is low; when neurons are clustered together, the energy is high. This measure goes beyond individual neuron values and instead characterizes the pairwise relational structure among all neurons in a layer.

The hypothesis is that a good finetuned model should have a minimal difference in hyperspherical energy compared to the pretrained model. In other words, the finetuning process should preserve the pairwise angular relationships among neurons, as these relationships encode the structural knowledge that underpins the pretrained model's generative ability. A naive approach would be to add a regularizer that penalizes differences in hyperspherical energy during finetuning. However, regularization alone cannot guarantee that the hyperspherical energy difference will be well minimized—the optimization may find solutions that approximately satisfy the regularizer but still deviate significantly from the pretrained energy.

A more principled approach takes advantage of an invariance property of hyperspherical energy: the pairwise hyperspherical similarity between neurons is provably preserved if the same orthogonal transformation is applied to all neurons in a layer. An orthogonal transformation—comprising rotations and reflections—preserves all pairwise angles between vectors, and therefore preserves the hyperspherical energy exactly. This invariance suggests a powerful finetuning strategy: instead of freely updating the model's weights, one can learn a layer-shared orthogonal transformation that adapts the model to the downstream task while guaranteeing that the hyperspherical energy remains unchanged. This is the central insight behind Orthogonal Finetuning, which will be the focus of the subsequent chapters.

The motivation for this structural approach can also be understood through an analogy with the two-dimensional Fourier transform. An image can be decomposed into a magnitude spectrum and a phase spectrum, where the phase spectrum encodes the angular information between the input and the basis functions. It is well known that the phase spectrum preserves the major part of the image's semantics: the phase spectrum of an image, combined with a random magnitude spectrum, can still reconstruct the original image without losing its semantic content. By analogy, the angles between neurons in a neural network may play a role analogous to the phase spectrum, encoding the crucial semantic information that determines the model's output. Changing neuron directions is therefore the key to semantically modifying the generated image, which is precisely the goal of both subject-driven and controllable generation. However, changing neuron directions with a large degree of freedom would inevitably destroy the pretrained generative performance. Constraining the transformation to be orthogonal preserves the angles between all pairs of neurons, maintaining the structural knowledge of the pretrained model while allowing enough flexibility to adapt to the downstream task.

This structural perspective also offers a way to think about the trade-off between flexibility and stability in finetuning. Standard finetuning is maximally flexible—it can update the model in any direction—but it offers no stability guarantees and can easily cause model collapse. At the other extreme, freezing the model entirely guarantees perfect preservation but offers no adaptation. Orthogonal transformation occupies a sweet spot: it is flexible enough to modify neuron directions and adapt to new tasks, yet it is constrained enough to preserve the pairwise angular structure that encodes the pretrained model's semantic knowledge. This balance between flexibility and regularity is the key to effective finetuning, and it provides a principled foundation that Euclidean distance-based approaches lack.
