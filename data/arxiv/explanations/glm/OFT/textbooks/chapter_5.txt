\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 5: Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization

## Limitations of Standard Orthogonalization for Large Matrices

The core mechanism of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) relies on learning a layer-shared orthogonal transformation matrix $\bm{R}$ that is applied to the pretrained neuron weights. For OFT to function correctly, $\bm{R}$ must satisfy the orthogonality constraint $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$ at every step of training. A natural first instinct would be to use a standard orthogonalization procedure, such as the Gram-Schmidt process, to take an arbitrary matrix and project it onto the orthogonal group. The Gram-Schmidt process is indeed differentiable, meaning gradients can flow through it during backpropagation, which in principle makes it a candidate for use in a learning framework.

However, the paper identifies a critical practical limitation: standard orthogonalization methods like Gram-Schmidt are too computationally expensive when applied to large matrices. Text-to-image diffusion models such as Stable Diffusion contain weight matrices with large hidden dimensions $d$. Applying a full Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization at every training step—and computing gradients through it—would impose prohibitive computational overhead. The cost of such procedures scales unfavorably with the dimension $d$, making them impractical for the scale of modern diffusion models. This computational bottleneck motivates the search for a more efficient way to parameterize orthogonal matrices, one that guarantees orthogonality by construction rather than enforcing it through an expensive post-hoc projection.

## Cayley Parameterization

To overcome the computational burden of standard orthogonalization, OFT adopts the Cayley parameterization. The key idea is to construct the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ from a simpler, unconstrained matrix in a way that orthogonality is guaranteed by the mathematical structure of the construction itself. Specifically, the orthogonal matrix is formed as $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. The skew-symmetry constraint on $\bm{Q}$ is much simpler to enforce than full orthogonality: it simply requires that the matrix equals the negation of its own transpose, meaning the diagonal entries are zero and the off-diagonal entries satisfy $Q_{ij} = -Q_{ji}$.

The Cayley parameterization is a well-known construction in matrix analysis and Lie group theory. Its appeal in the OFT framework is that it provides a differentiable mapping from the space of skew-symmetric matrices to the space of orthogonal matrices. Rather than performing an expensive orthogonalization procedure on an arbitrary matrix, one simply optimizes the entries of $\bm{Q}$ (subject to the skew-symmetry constraint) and obtains an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ through the Cayley transform. This is computationally more tractable because the structure of $\bm{Q}$ is simpler, and the matrix inverse $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ can be handled through standard linear algebra routines.

To ensure that OFT starts finetuning from the pretrained model, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is initialized as the identity matrix $\bm{I}$. Under the Cayley parameterization, this is achieved by initializing $\bm{Q}$ as an all-zero matrix, since $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{0})(\bm{I}-\bm{0})^{-1}=\bm{I}$. This initialization guarantees that at the start of finetuning, the forward pass $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ reduces to $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, which is exactly the forward pass of the pretrained model.

## Properties and Limitations of the Cayley Transform

The Cayley parameterization offers a computationally efficient route to generating orthogonal matrices, but it comes with a specific mathematical limitation. The Cayley transform can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$. In group-theoretic terms, these matrices belong to the special orthogonal group, denoted $\text{SO}(d)$, which is a subgroup of the full orthogonal group $\text{O}(d)$. The full orthogonal group includes both rotations (determinant $+1$) and reflections (determinant $-1$), but the Cayley transform excludes the reflection component.

This limitation means that OFT, when using the Cayley parameterization, cannot represent all possible orthogonal transformations—only those corresponding to rotations. A reflection, which flips the orientation of the coordinate system, is not achievable. One might worry that this restriction could limit the expressiveness of OFT and harm its performance on downstream tasks.

However, the paper reports that this limitation does not affect performance in practice. The rotation-only restriction of the special orthogonal group is found to be sufficiently expressive for the finetuning tasks considered, namely subject-driven generation and controllable generation. The intuition here is that rotations provide enough flexibility to adjust neuron directions in the ways needed for these downstream tasks, and the additional freedom afforded by reflections is not necessary. This empirical finding is reassuring, as it means the computational benefits of the Cayley parameterization can be enjoyed without practical cost to task performance.

## Block-Diagonal Orthogonal Matrix Structure for Parameter Efficiency

Even with the Cayley parameterization, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can still be parameter-inefficient when the dimension $d$ is large. A full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix parameterized via the Cayley transform requires a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ of the same size, and the number of free parameters in a $d \times d$ skew-symmetric matrix is $d(d-1)/2$, resulting in a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. For large-scale diffusion models where $d$ can be hundreds or thousands, this quadratic scaling becomes a significant burden.

To address this, OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure for the orthogonal matrix. Instead of learning a single dense $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix, the matrix $\bm{R}$ is decomposed into $r$ smaller orthogonal blocks along the diagonal:

$$\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{R}_1\in \text{O}(\frac{d}{r}) & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & \bm{R}_r\in \text{O}(\frac{d}{r})\end{bmatrix}\in \text{O}(d)$$

Here, $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is the full block-diagonal orthogonal matrix, and each block $\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d/r\times d/r}$ is itself an orthogonal matrix of size $d/r \times d/r$. The key insight is that the product of block-diagonal orthogonal matrices remains orthogonal, so the overall matrix $\bm{R}$ still satisfies the orthogonality constraint $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$.

The parameter $r$ controls the trade-off between flexibility and efficiency. When $r=1$, the block-diagonal structure reduces to a single block, meaning $\bm{R}$ is a standard unconstrained $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix with no structural simplification. As $r$ increases, each block becomes smaller, the number of parameters decreases, and the transformation becomes more constrained. Each block $\bm{R}_i$ is itself parameterized using the Cayley transform, so the same orthogonality guarantees apply at the block level.

The paper notes that the block-diagonal structure has an interesting interpretation when applied to convolution layers. When the number of blocks $r$ equals the number of input channels, each block transforms a unique neuron channel, analogous to learning depth-wise convolution kernels. When all blocks are shared (i.e., $\bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j$ for all $i \neq j$), OFT applies a single orthogonal transformation across all channels.

## Parameter Complexity Analysis

The block-diagonal structure yields significant reductions in the number of trainable parameters. For a standard unconstrained $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix, the number of free parameters is $d(d-1)/2$, giving a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. When the matrix is decomposed into $r$ blocks of size $d/r \times d/r$, each block has $(d/r)(d/r - 1)/2$ free parameters, and there are $r$ such blocks, giving a total of $r \cdot (d/r)(d/r - 1)/2 = d(d/r - 1)/2$ parameters. This results in a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, a factor of $r$ reduction compared to the unconstrained case.

The parameter efficiency can be pushed even further through block sharing. If all $r$ blocks share the same orthogonal matrix (i.e., $\bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j$ for all $i \neq j$), then only one block of size $d/r \times d/r$ needs to be learned. The total number of parameters becomes $(d/r)(d/r - 1)/2$, yielding a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. This quadratic reduction in $r$ makes it possible to drastically cut the number of trainable parameters while still maintaining the orthogonality property.

To make these complexity figures concrete, the paper provides a numerical example. For a weight matrix of size $128 \times 128$, LoRA with a low-rank parameter $r'=8$ requires $2{,}048$ trainable parameters. In contrast, OFT with $r=8$ blocks (and no block sharing) requires only $960$ trainable parameters—fewer than half of LoRA's parameter count. This demonstrates that the block-diagonal structure makes OFT highly competitive in terms of parameter efficiency.

## Comparison of Parameter Efficiency Between OFT and LoRA

The paper draws a detailed comparison between OFT and LoRA, which is the most relevant existing finetuning method. LoRA assumes a low-rank structure for the additive weight update during finetuning, adding a low-rank matrix to the pretrained weights. For a low-rank parameter $r'$, LoRA introduces $r'(d+n)$ trainable parameters, where $d$ is the input dimension and $n$ is the output dimension of the weight matrix.

To compare the two methods on equal footing, the paper considers the scenario where both the block parameter $r$ in OFT and the rank parameter $r'$ in LoRA are proportional to the neuron dimension $d$, say $r = r' = \alpha d$ for some constant $0 < \alpha \leq 1$. Under this assumption, LoRA's parameter complexity becomes $\mathcal{O}(d^2 + dn)$, while OFT's parameter complexity becomes $\mathcal{O}(d)$. This reveals a fundamental difference: when the finetuning budget scales with the model dimension, OFT's parameter count grows linearly in $d$, whereas LoRA's grows quadratically (due to the $d^2$ term) plus an additional term linear in $n$.

Beyond the raw parameter counts, the paper highlights a deeper conceptual distinction between the two approaches. LoRA and OFT represent two distinct strategies for achieving parameter efficiency. LoRA exploits a low-rank structure—it constrains the weight update to lie in a low-dimensional subspace, thereby reducing the number of free parameters. OFT, on the other hand, exploits a sparsity structure through block-diagonal orthogonality—it constrains the transformation to be orthogonal and block-diagonal, which is a different kind of structural restriction. Both approaches reduce the number of trainable parameters, but they do so through fundamentally different geometric and algebraic mechanisms.

An additional connection noted in the paper is that OFT's forward pass can be rewritten as $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}=(\bm{W}^0+(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where the term $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ is analogous to LoRA's low-rank weight update. Since the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ is typically full-rank, OFT also performs a low-rank weight update when $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ is low-rank. This shows that OFT can be viewed, in a certain sense, as performing a structured low-rank update, but one that is guaranteed to preserve the pairwise angular relationships among neurons.

## Preservation of Orthogonality Under Parameter-Efficient Strategies

A critical concern when introducing parameter-efficient approximations—such as the block-diagonal structure and block sharing—is whether the fundamental guarantee of OFT is maintained. The central promise of OFT is that the hyperspherical energy of the finetuned model equals that of the pretrained model, which is achieved by applying an orthogonal transformation to the neuron weights. If the block-diagonal or block-sharing strategies were to compromise orthogonality, the entire theoretical foundation of OFT would be undermined.

The paper explicitly addresses this concern and confirms that all the parameter-efficient strategies preserve orthogonality. The block-diagonal matrix $\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)$ is itself an orthogonal matrix because each block $\bm{R}_i$ is orthogonal, and the direct sum of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal. Similarly, when blocks are shared, the resulting matrix remains orthogonal because it is still a block-diagonal matrix with orthogonal blocks, just with repeated blocks. The Cayley parameterization guarantees that each block is orthogonal by construction, and this property propagates to the full matrix $\bm{R}$.

Therefore, despite all the strategies employed to improve parameter efficiency—Cayley parameterization, block-diagonal decomposition, and block sharing—the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal. There is no sacrifice in the preservation of hyperspherical energy. The pairwise angles between neurons in the same layer are provably preserved, and the semantic structure encoded by the pretrained model is maintained throughout finetuning. This is a key advantage of OFT: it achieves parameter efficiency through structural constraints that are compatible with the orthogonality requirement, rather than through approximations that might compromise it.

The paper also notes an important practical benefit related to inference. Unlike adapter-based methods such as ControlNet, OFT introduces no additional inference overhead. During inference, the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can be pre-multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ to obtain an equivalent weight matrix $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. This means the finetuned model has the same architecture and inference speed as the pretrained model, with no extra computational cost at test time. The parameter-efficient parameterization strategies thus not only reduce training costs but also ensure that the deployed model remains as efficient as the original.
