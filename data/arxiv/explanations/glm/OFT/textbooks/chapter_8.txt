Chapter 8: Insights: Connections to LoRA, Convergence, and Design Principles

# Rewriting OFT as a Low-Rank Weight Update Analogous to LoRA

To understand the relationship between Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), it is instructive to rewrite OFT's forward pass in a form that makes the comparison with LoRA more transparent. Recall that OFT modifies the forward pass of a pretrained layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by applying a learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, so that the finetuned weight becomes $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. The forward pass is then $\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top\bm{x} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$. The key insight comes from rewriting this expression by decomposing the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ into the identity matrix and a residual term: $\bm{R} = \bm{I} + (\bm{R} - \bm{I})$. Substituting this decomposition into the forward pass yields $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$. Here, $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ plays a role analogous to LoRA's low-rank weight update. In LoRA, the weight update takes the form $\Delta\bm{W} = \bm{A}\bm{B}$ where $\bm{A}$ and $\bm{B}$ are low-rank matrices, and the finetuned weight is $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$. In OFT, the effective weight update is $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0$, where the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ is multiplied by the deviation of the orthogonal matrix from identity.

This reformulation reveals a deeper structural parallel between the two methods. Both approaches add a correction term to the pretrained weights rather than replacing them entirely, and both seek to control the magnitude or structure of this correction to preserve pretrained knowledge. The distinction lies in how the correction is parameterized and constrained. LoRA directly parameterizes the update as a low-rank product $\bm{A}\bm{B}$, which by construction limits the rank of the weight change. OFT, by contrast, parameterizes the update through an orthogonal transformation that deviates from the identity, and the rank properties of the update $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ depend on the structure of $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$. When $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ is low-rank (for instance, when the block-diagonal parameterization is used with a small number of blocks), OFT also effectively performs a low-rank weight update. Since $\bm{W}^0$ is typically full-rank, the rank of the product $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ is bounded by the rank of $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$, meaning that OFT can achieve similar parameter efficiency to LoRA through this mechanism.

The analogy becomes even more precise when we consider the role of the rank parameter. LoRA has a rank parameter $r'$ that controls the expressiveness of the weight update, with larger $r'$ permitting more expressive updates at the cost of more trainable parameters. OFT has a diagonal block parameter $r$ that serves a similar purpose: with $r = 1$, the orthogonal matrix is unconstrained and fully expressive, while increasing $r$ constrains the structure of $\bm{R}$ and reduces the number of trainable parameters. In both cases, the parameter trades off flexibility against parameter efficiency, though the nature of the constraint differs fundamentally between the two approaches.

# LoRA Exploits Low-Rank Structure vs. OFT Exploits Sparsity (Block-Diagonal) Structure

Having established the formal analogy between OFT and LoRA, we can now articulate the deeper conceptual distinction: the two methods achieve parameter efficiency through fundamentally different structural assumptions about the weight update. LoRA exploits low-rank structure to reduce the number of trainable parameters. The assumption underlying LoRA is that the weight update $\Delta\bm{W}$ during finetuning can be well-approximated by a low-rank matrix, meaning that the update lies in a low-dimensional subspace. This is a direct constraint on the content of the update: the rank of $\Delta\bm{W}$ is at most $r'$, regardless of the structure of the pretrained weights. The low-rank assumption is motivated by the empirical observation that the changes needed for downstream adaptation often reside in a small number of directions in weight space.

OFT, on the other hand, takes a fundamentally different route to parameter efficiency. Rather than constraining the rank of the weight update, OFT constrains the weight update to be an orthogonal transformation of the pretrained weights, and then exploits the sparsity structure of block-diagonal orthogonality to reduce parameters. The block-diagonal structure means that the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is not a fully dense $d \times d$ matrix but rather a collection of $r$ smaller orthogonal matrices $\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \ldots, \bm{R}_r$ arranged along the diagonal, each of size $\frac{d}{r} \times \frac{d}{r}$. This is a sparsity constraint on the transformation itself: the off-diagonal blocks are constrained to be zero, meaning that certain dimensions are never mixed with others during the transformation. The key difference is that LoRA constrains the dimensionality of the update subspace, while OFT constrains the connectivity pattern of the transformation.

This distinction has practical consequences. In LoRA, the low-rank constraint means that the update can only move the pretrained weights within a low-dimensional subspace, which may be too restrictive for certain tasks that require changes spanning many directions. In OFT, the block-diagonal orthogonal transformation preserves all pairwise angles within each block, so the relational structure among neurons within a block is maintained while the relationships between blocks are fixed (since the off-diagonal elements are zero). The sparsity structure of OFT can also have interesting interpretations in specific architectures. For convolution layers, when the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, each block transforms a unique neuron channel, analogous to learning depth-wise convolution kernels. When all blocks are shared, OFT applies a single orthogonal transformation across all channels. This architectural interpretability is a unique advantage of OFT's sparsity-based approach, as the block-diagonal structure maps naturally onto the channel structure of convolution operations, a correspondence that has no direct analogue in LoRA's low-rank parameterization.

From a parameter complexity perspective, the two approaches scale differently with the dimensions of the weight matrix. For a weight matrix of size $d \times n$, LoRA with rank $r'$ requires $r'(d + n)$ trainable parameters. OFT with $r$ blocks (without block sharing) requires $\frac{d(d/r - 1)}{2}$ parameters, which simplifies to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. When block sharing is applied, meaning all blocks share the same orthogonal matrix, the complexity further reduces to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. If we consider both $r$ and $r'$ to scale linearly with $d$ (for example, $r = r' = \alpha d$ for some constant $0 < \alpha \leq 1$), the parameter complexity of LoRA becomes $\mathcal{O}(d^2 + dn)$ while OFT becomes $\mathcal{O}(d)$. This shows that OFT can be more parameter-efficient than LoRA in certain regimes, particularly when the output dimension $n$ is large. A concrete example illustrates this: for a weight matrix of size $128 \times 128$, LoRA with $r' = 8$ has $2{,}048$ trainable parameters, while OFT with $r = 8$ (without block sharing) has only $960$ parameters.

# Why OFT Converges Faster: Effective Semantic Updates and Smooth Optimization Landscape

One of the most striking empirical observations about OFT is its faster convergence compared to existing finetuning methods. The paper provides two complementary explanations for this phenomenon, one based on the nature of the updates OFT makes and the other based on the geometry of the optimization landscape.

The first explanation stems from the observation that angular information among neurons is the primary carrier of semantic meaning in neural networks. The paper demonstrates this through a toy experiment using a convolutional autoencoder trained on flower images. During training, the autoencoder uses standard inner products between convolutional kernels $\bm{w}$ and input patches $\bm{x}$ to produce feature maps. At test time, the experiment compares three ways of generating feature maps: (a) the standard inner product used in training, (b) using only the magnitude information, and (c) using only the angular (cosine) information. The remarkable finding is that the angular information alone can almost perfectly recover the input images, while the magnitude information contains essentially no useful information for reconstruction. This experiment powerfully demonstrates that the directions (angles) of neurons, not their magnitudes, encode the semantic content of the data.

Given this observation, it follows that the most effective way to modify the semantic output of a model during finetuning is to change the directions of its neurons. This is precisely what OFT is designed to do: by learning an orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$, OFT directly rotates and reflects the neurons in each layer, changing their directions while preserving the pairwise angles between them. LoRA, by contrast, adds a low-rank update to the weights, which changes both the magnitudes and directions of neurons in a coupled manner that may not optimally target the semantically relevant dimensions. Standard finetuning with a small learning rate also updates weights in an unstructured way, diluting the update across both magnitude and direction. Because OFT focuses its updates specifically on the angular (semantic) dimensions, it can achieve meaningful semantic changes more efficiently, leading to faster convergence.

The second explanation for OFT's fast convergence relates to the geometry of the optimization problem. OFT can be viewed as finetuning neurons on a smooth hypersphere manifold. The orthogonality constraint ensures that the neurons remain on the unit hypersphere (after normalization), and the optimization proceeds over the space of orthogonal transformations, which is a smooth manifold known as the orthogonal group $\text{O}(d)$. Optimization on smooth manifolds generally has better convergence properties than unconstrained optimization in Euclidean space, because the constraint eliminates degenerate directions and the geometry of the manifold provides a more regular optimization landscape. The empirical verification of this property is referenced in the paper through prior work on orthogonal over-parameterized training, which showed that optimizing over orthogonal transformations yields a smoother optimization landscape compared to standard training. The combination of targeting semantically relevant updates and operating on a smooth manifold explains why OFT converges significantly faster than methods like LoRA and ControlNet. For example, in the landmark-to-face task, it takes 20 epochs for LoRA to converge to the performance that OFT achieves at the 8th epoch, and OFT can converge well with only 5\% of the ADE20K dataset, demonstrating remarkable data efficiency.

# Why Not Minimize Hyperspherical Energy: Preserving vs. Minimizing for Finetuning

A natural question that arises when considering OFT is: if hyperspherical energy is such an important measure of neural network structure, why not actively minimize it during finetuning, as some prior work has done for classification? The answer reveals a crucial distinction between training a network from scratch and finetuning a pretrained model, and it highlights a key design principle of OFT.

Hyperspherical energy, defined as $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ denotes the $i$-th normalized neuron, measures how uniformly the neurons are distributed on the unit hypersphere. Minimizing hyperspherical energy pushes neurons to be as spread out as possible, approaching a uniform distribution. In the context of classification, this is desirable because uniformly distributed neurons minimize redundancy: each neuron covers a distinct region of the input space, and there is no wasted overlap between neurons. Prior work on orthogonal over-parameterized training exploits exactly this principle, training classification networks from scratch by applying orthogonal transformations to a randomly initialized network, which in expectation yields a small hyperspherical energy. The goal there is to keep the hyperspherical energy small throughout training, as small energy is associated with better generalization in classification.

However, the situation is fundamentally different for finetuning a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model. The pretrained model has already learned a rich semantic structure during pretraining, and this structure is encoded in the specific configuration of neuron angles — not in a uniform distribution of neurons. The hyperspherical energy of the pretrained model reflects the specific, non-uniform arrangement of neurons that was learned through extensive pretraining on large-scale data. This arrangement captures complex semantic relationships: some neurons may be intentionally correlated because they encode related visual concepts, while others may be spread apart because they represent distinct concepts. Actively minimizing hyperspherical energy during finetuning would push all neurons toward a uniform distribution, effectively destroying the carefully learned semantic structure. The non-uniform angular relationships among neurons in the pretrained model are not redundancy to be eliminated — they are knowledge to be preserved.

This is exactly why OFT aims to preserve hyperspherical energy rather than minimize it. By constraining the finetuning transformation to be orthogonal, OFT provably preserves the pairwise angles between all neurons in each layer, and therefore preserves the hyperspherical energy exactly. The pretrained model's semantic structure, encoded in its neuron angles, is maintained throughout finetuning, and the orthogonal transformation only adjusts the overall orientation of the neuron set to adapt to the downstream task. This distinction between preserving and minimizing hyperspherical energy is one of the key differences between OFT and prior work on orthogonal training. It reflects the fundamental difference between the goals of training from scratch (where one wants to learn a good structure) and finetuning (where one wants to adapt while keeping an existing good structure).

# The Flexibility-Regularity Trade-off in Finetuning Method Design

The design of OFT reveals a fundamental trade-off in finetuning method design: the trade-off between flexibility and regularity. Flexibility refers to the ability of a finetuning method to make arbitrary changes to the model, enabling it to adapt to a wide range of downstream tasks. Regularity refers to the constraints that prevent the finetuning from destroying the pretrained model's knowledge and that ensure stable training dynamics. A good finetuning method must balance these two competing objectives, and different methods fall at different points along this spectrum.

Standard finetuning, which simply updates the pretrained weights via gradient descent with a small learning rate, sits at the extreme of maximum flexibility. There are no structural constraints on the weight updates: the weights can move in any direction by any amount (controlled only by the learning rate). This maximum flexibility comes at a cost: poor stability and susceptibility to model collapse. The paper observes that DreamBooth, which uses standard finetuning, starts to generate collapsed images after 2000 iterations, losing the pretrained model's generative capability. The lack of any structural constraint means that the finetuning can easily move the model into a degenerate region of weight space where it no longer produces realistic images.

At the other extreme, one could imagine a finetuning method with maximum regularity that barely changes the model at all, but such a method would be too inflexible to adapt to downstream tasks. The challenge is to find a method that provides sufficient flexibility for adaptation while maintaining enough regularity to preserve the pretrained model's capabilities. OFT finds a compelling balance in this trade-off. The orthogonal transformation provides meaningful flexibility: it can rotate and reflect all neurons in a layer, which, as the paper's experiments show, is sufficient to achieve strong performance on both subject-driven and controllable generation tasks. At the same time, the orthogonality constraint provides strong regularity by preserving all pairwise neuron angles, which as argued above, is crucial for maintaining the pretrained semantic structure.

The block-diagonal parameterization of the orthogonal matrix introduces an additional knob for controlling the flexibility-regularity trade-off. With $r = 1$ (a single block), the orthogonal matrix is unconstrained within the space of orthogonal transformations, providing maximum flexibility within the orthogonality constraint. As $r$ increases, the block-diagonal structure becomes more restrictive: neurons in different blocks cannot be mixed, which reduces flexibility but also increases regularity by limiting the types of transformations that can be applied. The block-diagonal parameterization can thus be viewed as a stronger regularization of the orthogonal matrix, and the choice of $r$ allows practitioners to tune the flexibility-regularity balance for their specific task.

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) adds yet another layer of regularity through the deviation constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. This constraint limits how far the orthogonal transformation can deviate from the identity, directly controlling the magnitude of the change to the pretrained model. With larger $\epsilon$, COFT approaches the flexibility of standard OFT, while with smaller $\epsilon$, the finetuned model increasingly resembles the pretrained model. The paper demonstrates that COFT achieves even better finetuning stability than OFT due to this explicit deviation constraint, and that with a proper $\epsilon$, both the learning rate and the iteration number become effortless to set. This makes COFT particularly practical, as it reduces the need for careful hyperparameter tuning, which is a significant practical challenge in finetuning large diffusion models.

# No Additional Inference Overhead: Merging R into W⁰

A practically important property of OFT that distinguishes it from some other finetuning approaches is that it introduces no additional inference overhead. This property is a direct consequence of the multiplicative structure of OFT's weight update and has significant implications for deployment.

Recall that OFT modifies the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by left-multiplying it with a learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, producing the finetuned weight $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. During training, this is implemented as two separate operations: first computing $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, then using $\bm{W}$ in the forward pass. However, once training is complete, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ has been learned and is fixed. At this point, one can simply precompute the product $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ once and store the resulting weight matrix. During inference, the model then uses $\bm{W}$ exactly as the pretrained model would have used $\bm{W}^0$, with no additional computation. The forward pass remains $\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top\bm{x}$, which has the same computational cost as the original forward pass $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$.

This is in contrast to adapter-based methods like ControlNet, which introduce additional network components that must be executed during inference. ControlNet, for instance, adds a trainable copy of the encoder blocks that processes the control signal and injects it into the pretrained model. These additional components increase the inference time and memory footprint of the model, which can be a significant drawback in deployment scenarios where computational resources are limited or where real-time generation is required. Similarly, LoRA, while it can also be merged into the pretrained weights (since the update is additive: $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$), requires the computation of the low-rank product $\bm{A}\bm{B}$ during training, and the merged weight matrix may have different numerical properties than the pretrained one.

The ability to merge $\bm{R}$ into $\bm{W}^0$ without any residual computational overhead makes OFT particularly attractive for practical deployment. The finetuned model has exactly the same architecture, the same number of parameters (in terms of the weight matrix), and the same inference speed as the pretrained model. This means that OFT can be seamlessly integrated into existing inference pipelines without any modifications to the model architecture or the inference code — one simply replaces the pretrained weight matrix with the merged finetuned weight matrix. This property also means that OFT does not increase the memory footprint of the model during inference, which is important for serving models on resource-constrained devices or for serving multiple finetuned models simultaneously.

# Comparison to Orthogonal Over-Parameterized Training

OFT shares a conceptual foundation with orthogonal over-parameterized training, but the two methods differ in their goals, constraints, and applications in important ways. Understanding these differences illuminates the unique design principles behind OFT and why it is specifically suited for finetuning text-to-image diffusion models.

Orthogonal over-parameterized training, as developed in prior work, is a method for training classification neural networks from scratch. The approach begins with a randomly initialized neural network and trains it by applying orthogonal transformations to the weights. The motivation comes from the observation that a randomly initialized neural network yields a provably small hyperspherical energy in expectation, meaning the neurons are approximately uniformly distributed on the hypersphere. The goal of orthogonal over-parameterized training is to keep the hyperspherical energy small throughout training, since small energy is associated with better generalization in classification. This is achieved by constraining the weight updates to be orthogonal transformations, which preserve the pairwise angles among neurons and thus maintain the (approximately) uniform distribution established by random initialization.

OFT adopts the same mathematical tool — orthogonal transformation of neuron weights — but applies it with a fundamentally different objective. Instead of training a network from scratch, OFT finetunes a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model for downstream tasks. The pretrained model has a specific, learned neuron configuration that encodes rich semantic knowledge, and this configuration is generally not uniform (the hyperspherical energy is not necessarily small). The goal of OFT is to preserve this learned configuration while making targeted adjustments for the downstream task. This leads to three key differences from orthogonal over-parameterized training.

First, while orthogonal over-parameterized training aims to minimize hyperspherical energy, OFT aims to preserve the same hyperspherical energy as the pretrained model. Minimizing hyperspherical energy during finetuning would push the neurons toward a uniform distribution, destroying the non-uniform semantic structure learned during pretraining. OFT's preservation objective is captured by the constraint $\|\text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\| = 0$, which is provably satisfied by orthogonal transformation. This is not just a minor variation of the minimization objective — it is a fundamentally different goal that reflects the different context of finetuning versus training from scratch.

Second, OFT seeks to minimize the deviation from the pretrained model, which leads to the constrained variant COFT. In COFT, the orthogonal transformation is additionally constrained to stay within a small neighborhood of the identity matrix: $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. This constraint has no counterpart in orthogonal over-parameterized training, which imposes no such deviation constraint because there is no pretrained model to stay close to. The deviation constraint in COFT embodies the principle that finetuning should make the smallest change necessary to adapt to the downstream task, and it provides additional stability that is critical when working with large, expensive pretrained models.

Third, the application domains differ significantly. Orthogonal over-parameterized training was designed and evaluated for classification problems, where the goal is to learn a mapping from inputs to discrete class labels. OFT is designed for text-to-image diffusion models, where the goal is to generate high-fidelity, semantically meaningful images. The generative setting imposes different requirements: the finetuned model must not only perform well on the downstream task (e.g., subject-driven or controllable generation) but also maintain the high fidelity and diversity of the pretrained model's generative capabilities. This is a more delicate objective than classification accuracy, as it requires preserving the rich, continuous structure of the generative model's output space rather than just maintaining decision boundaries between classes.

Together, these differences highlight that OFT is not a straightforward adaptation of orthogonal over-parameterized training to a new domain, but rather a method that shares a core mathematical insight (the power and structure of orthogonal transformations) while being carefully redesigned for the specific challenges of finetuning large pretrained generative models. The key to finetuning, as the paper emphasizes, is finding a good trade-off between flexibility and stability, and the OFT framework achieves this through the combination of orthogonal transformation (for structured, semantic updates), hyperspherical energy preservation (for maintaining pretrained knowledge), and optional deviation constraints (for additional stability).
