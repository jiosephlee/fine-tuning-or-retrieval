\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 9: Subject-driven Generation: Experiments and Analysis

# Experimental Setup: Stable Diffusion v1.5, DreamBooth Framework, and Baseline Configurations

The subject-driven generation experiments are designed to rigorously evaluate the effectiveness of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT) against established baselines. The pretrained text-to-image model used throughout all experiments is Stable Diffusion v1.5, a latent diffusion model that has demonstrated strong capabilities in text-guided high-fidelity image generation. By using the same pretrained backbone across all methods, the experiments isolate the effect of the finetuning strategy itself, ensuring that any observed differences in performance are attributable to the finetuning method rather than the base model.

The two primary baselines for comparison are DreamBooth and LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation). DreamBooth represents the standard approach to subject-driven generation: it finetunes the entire text-to-image diffusion model using a reconstruction loss and a class-specific prior preservation loss, employing a small learning rate to implicitly constrain the deviation from the pretrained weights. LoRA, on the other hand, introduces a low-rank additive update to the weight matrices, reducing the number of trainable parameters while still allowing the model to adapt to the downstream task. Both baselines are configured according to their respective original papers, using the best hyperparameter setups reported by their authors. This ensures that the comparison is fair and that the baselines are performing at their intended capacity.

A critical aspect of the experimental design is that all methods—DreamBooth, LoRA, OFT, and COFT—adopt the same loss function as DreamBooth. This means that the training objectives are held constant, and the only variable being tested is the finetuning mechanism itself. OFT and COFT replace the standard weight update with a learned orthogonal transformation applied to the pretrained weights, while DreamBooth and LoRA use their respective update strategies. To ensure a fair comparison with LoRA, OFT and COFT are applied only to the same layers where LoRA is used—specifically, the attention weights in the transformer components of the diffusion model. This matching of applied layers prevents OFT from gaining an unfair advantage by finetuning a broader set of layers than LoRA.

To further ensure fairness in evaluation, generated images are randomly picked from each method, meaning that no method benefits from careful curation or cherry-picking of outputs. For each method, the model that achieves the best validation CLIP metrics is selected for reporting, providing a consistent and principled criterion for model selection across all approaches.

# Finetuning Stability and Convergence Analysis Across Iterations

One of the most striking findings from the subject-driven generation experiments is the difference in finetuning stability and convergence behavior among the four methods. The experiments reveal that both COFT and OFT are able to finetune the diffusion model in a remarkably stable manner, while DreamBooth and LoRA exhibit significant instability over the course of training.

At 400 training iterations, both DreamBooth and the OFT variants achieve good control over the generated subject, producing images that recognizable capture the subject identity. However, LoRA at this stage fails to preserve the subject identity, indicating a slower convergence to the desired subject representation. This early divergence in performance already highlights a convergence advantage for OFT and COFT over LoRA.

As training progresses to 2000 iterations, the differences become even more pronounced. DreamBooth begins to generate collapsed images—a phenomenon where the model's outputs degrade catastrophically, losing coherence and realism. This model collapse is a well-known failure mode of standard finetuning with small learning rates: while the approach can work for a limited number of iterations, it eventually destabilizes as the cumulative weight updates drift too far from the pretrained model. LoRA, at 2000 iterations, fails to generate the correct subject attributes—for example, when the subject is wearing a yellow shirt, LoRA instead generates yellow fur, indicating that the model has lost track of the fine-grained subject details.

In stark contrast, both OFT and COFT maintain stable and consistent control over the generated images even at 2000 iterations. The orthogonal transformation constraint ensures that the pairwise angular relationships among neurons are preserved, preventing the kind of semantic drift that causes DreamBooth to collapse and LoRA to lose subject fidelity. This stability is a direct consequence of the structural preservation guaranteed by OFT: by maintaining the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained model, OFT prevents the catastrophic forgetting that plagues less constrained finetuning approaches.

The practical significance of this stability cannot be overstated. In real-world applications of subject-driven generation, the number of finetuning iterations is a critical hyperparameter that must be carefully tuned for each subject. Too few iterations and the model fails to capture the subject; too many iterations and the model may collapse. The insensitivity of OFT and COFT to the iteration count effectively eliminates this tuning burden. For both OFT and COFT, one can directly set a relatively large iteration number without carefully tuning it for each new subject. For COFT specifically, with a properly chosen deviation constraint $\epsilon$, both the learning rate and the iteration number become nearly effortless to set, making the entire finetuning pipeline substantially more user-friendly.

# Quantitative Metrics: DINO, CLIP-I, CLIP-T, and LPIPS for Subject Fidelity and Diversity

To rigorously evaluate subject-driven generation, the paper adopts a suite of quantitative metrics originally proposed in the DreamBooth paper. These metrics are designed to measure different aspects of generation quality, capturing the tension between subject fidelity (how well the generated image matches the real subject), text prompt fidelity (how well the generated image follows the text prompt), and sample diversity (how varied the generated images are for the same subject and prompt).

The DINO metric measures subject fidelity using ViT S/16 DINO embeddings. DINO is a self-supervised vision transformer that produces rich visual representations known to capture object identity and structure. The DINO metric computes the average cosine similarity between the DINO embeddings of generated images and real images of the subject. A higher DINO score indicates that the generated images more faithfully preserve the visual identity of the subject—a crucial requirement for subject-driven generation, where the goal is to render the same subject in novel contexts.

The CLIP-I (CLIP-Image) metric also measures subject fidelity but uses CLIP image embeddings instead of DINO embeddings. CLIP-I computes the average pairwise cosine similarity of CLIP embeddings between generated and real images. While DINO is particularly strong at capturing structural and identity-related features, CLIP embeddings encode a broader range of semantic information learned from image-text pairs, making CLIP-I a complementary measure of subject preservation.

The CLIP-T (CLIP-Text) metric evaluates text prompt fidelity by computing the average cosine similarity of CLIP embeddings between the text prompt and the generated images. This metric assesses whether the finetuned model still follows text guidance effectively after adaptation. A finetuning method that achieves high subject fidelity but low CLIP-T would indicate that the model has overfit to the subject images at the expense of its text-guided generation ability—a failure mode that is particularly common in standard finetuning approaches.

Finally, the LPIPS (Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity) metric measures sample diversity. LPIPS computes the average perceptual distance between pairs of generated images of the same subject with the same text prompt. A higher LPIPS score indicates greater diversity in the generated outputs. This metric is important because a good subject-driven generation model should not simply memorize and reproduce a single image of the subject; it should be able to generate diverse images that all maintain subject fidelity while varying in pose, lighting, background, and other factors.

Together, these four metrics provide a comprehensive picture of generation quality. An ideal finetuning method should achieve high DINO and CLIP-I scores (strong subject fidelity), high CLIP-T scores (good text prompt following), and high LPIPS scores (good diversity). The tension among these metrics—particularly between subject fidelity and text prompt fidelity—makes subject-driven generation a challenging multi-objective problem.

# Quantitative Comparison Results and Statistical Significance

The quantitative comparison results demonstrate that both COFT and OFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA in the DINO and CLIP-I metrics by a considerable margin. This means that the images generated by OFT and COFT more faithfully preserve the visual identity of the subject compared to the baseline methods. The improvement in DINO and CLIP-I is particularly significant because subject fidelity is the primary objective of subject-driven generation: if the generated image does not look like the subject, the method has fundamentally failed regardless of how well it follows the text prompt.

In addition to the strong subject fidelity results, OFT and COFT achieve slightly better or comparable performance in the CLIP-T (text prompt fidelity) and LPIPS (diversity) metrics. This is a notable achievement because many finetuning methods that improve subject fidelity do so at the expense of text prompt following—the model becomes so specialized to the subject that it loses its ability to respond to text guidance. The fact that OFT and COFT maintain or improve text prompt fidelity while substantially improving subject fidelity suggests that the orthogonal transformation preserves the semantic structure needed for text-guided generation while providing enough flexibility to adapt to the new subject.

To ensure the statistical reliability of these results, each method is repeatedly finetuned on the same pretrained model using 30 different random seeds. This large number of repetitions minimizes the influence of randomness and provides a robust estimate of each method's expected performance. The use of 30 random seeds is a methodologically rigorous choice that allows for meaningful statistical comparisons and ensures that the observed improvements are not artifacts of a particular random initialization or data ordering. The consistent superiority of OFT and COFT across 30 seeds demonstrates that the performance gains are robust and reproducible, not contingent on lucky random outcomes.

The overall picture that emerges from the quantitative results is that the OFT framework not only achieves better convergence and stability—as demonstrated by the convergence analysis—but also yields consistently better final performance across all relevant metrics. The combination of faster convergence, greater stability, and superior final quality makes OFT and COFT compelling alternatives to existing finetuning methods for subject-driven generation.

# Qualitative Comparison: Subject Preservation and Text Prompt Following

Beyond the quantitative metrics, the paper provides qualitative comparisons that offer more intuitive insight into the differences among the methods. The qualitative examples are randomly picked from generated images, ensuring that the comparison is representative rather than selectively favorable. Importantly, all examples for a given method are generated from the same finetuned model, meaning that no text prompt is separately optimized or tuned for individual results. This design choice further reinforces the fairness of the comparison.

The qualitative results reveal that both OFT and COFT deliver excellent semantic subject preservation across a variety of subjects and contexts. The generated images clearly maintain the distinguishing features of the subject—whether it is a particular animal, object, or person—while successfully placing the subject in the novel context specified by the text prompt. This dual capability of preserving subject identity while following text guidance is the hallmark of successful subject-driven generation.

LoRA, in contrast, often fails to preserve the subject identity in the qualitative examples. A particularly illustrative case is the bowl example, where LoRA completely loses the subject identity: the generated image bears little resemblance to the original subject, demonstrating that LoRA's low-rank update strategy can be insufficient for capturing the fine-grained visual details needed for faithful subject reproduction. This failure aligns with the quantitative results showing lower DINO and CLIP-I scores for LoRA.

DreamBooth presents a different failure mode. While it manages to preserve the subject identity in many cases—generating images that are recognizably of the correct subject—it frequently fails to generate images that follow the text prompt. For example, in the first row of the bowl example, DreamBooth produces an image of the subject but in a context that does not match the specified text prompt. This limitation reflects the tension between subject fidelity and text prompt fidelity: DreamBooth's finetuning strategy, which updates the model weights directly with a small learning rate, can cause the model to overfit to the subject images at the expense of its pretrained text-guided generation ability.

The qualitative comparison thus demonstrates that the OFT framework achieves a better balance between controllability and subject preservation than either DreamBooth or LoRA. OFT and COFT generate images that simultaneously maintain the subject's visual identity and follow the text prompt's contextual guidance—a balance that the baselines struggle to achieve. Furthermore, the number of iterations is not a sensitive hyperparameter for OFT: it performs well even with a large number of iterations, whereas neither DreamBooth nor LoRA can maintain quality under the same conditions. This robustness to iteration count, combined with the superior quality of generated images, makes OFT a more practical and reliable choice for subject-driven generation applications.

# Robustness to Hyperparameter Settings and Iteration Count Insensitivity

A recurring theme throughout the subject-driven generation experiments is the robustness of OFT and COFT to hyperparameter settings, particularly the insensitivity to the number of finetuning iterations. This property has significant practical implications for the deployment of subject-driven generation systems.

In standard finetuning approaches like DreamBooth, the number of training iterations is a critical hyperparameter that must be carefully tuned for each new subject. Too few iterations result in insufficient adaptation—the model fails to capture the subject's distinctive features. Too many iterations lead to model collapse—the generated images degrade catastrophically, losing realism and coherence. This narrow window of acceptable iteration counts creates a practical burden: for each new subject, the user must experiment with different iteration numbers to find the sweet spot, a process that is time-consuming and subject-dependent.

The OFT framework largely eliminates this burden. Because the orthogonal transformation preserves the pairwise angular relationships among neurons, the pretrained model's semantic structure is maintained throughout finetuning. This structural preservation acts as a powerful regularizer that prevents the catastrophic forgetting and model collapse observed in DreamBooth. As a result, both OFT and COFT can be trained for a relatively large number of iterations without degrading output quality. The user can set a generous iteration count without worrying about overshooting the optimal training duration.

COFT provides an even greater degree of robustness through its additional deviation constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. This constraint explicitly limits how far the orthogonal transformation can deviate from the identity matrix, which corresponds to the pretrained model. With a properly chosen $\epsilon$, the COFT-finetuned model's behavior is bounded: it cannot drift too far from the pretrained model regardless of the number of iterations or the learning rate. This makes both the learning rate and the iteration count nearly effortless to set. The deviation constraint acts as a safety net that prevents overfitting and model collapse, even in the absence of careful hyperparameter tuning.

The practical value of this robustness is considerable. In real-world applications, subject-driven generation is often used by non-experts who may not have the expertise or resources to carefully tune hyperparameters for each new subject. The ability of OFT and COFT to perform well across a wide range of iteration counts and learning rates makes them far more accessible and reliable than existing methods, which require subject-specific tuning to achieve good results.

# Human Evaluation of Generation Quality

In addition to the quantitative metrics and qualitative comparisons, the paper conducts a human evaluation to further validate the superiority of OFT. Human evaluation provides an important complement to automated metrics because it captures subjective aspects of image quality—such as realism, aesthetic appeal, and perceived subject similarity—that may not be fully captured by metrics like DINO or CLIP-I.

Human evaluation is particularly valuable in text-to-image generation because the ultimate consumers of generated images are human users. A finetuning method that achieves high scores on automated metrics but produces images that humans find unconvincing or aesthetically unappealing would be of limited practical value. By directly assessing human judgments of generation quality, the evaluation provides a more holistic assessment of the method's effectiveness.

The human evaluation results further validate the superiority of OFT, consistent with the findings from both the quantitative metrics and the qualitative comparisons. Human evaluators prefer the images generated by OFT and COFT over those produced by DreamBooth and LoRA, confirming that the improvements observed in automated metrics translate to perceptually meaningful differences in image quality. This convergence of evidence—from quantitative metrics, qualitative examples, and human evaluation—provides a compelling and multi-faceted validation of the OFT framework's effectiveness for subject-driven generation.
