\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 3: Hyperspherical Energy: A Structural Measure of Neuron Relationships

## Definition of Hyperspherical Energy and Its Computation

When we finetune a large pretrained text-to-image diffusion model, one of the central challenges is knowing how to measure whether the finetuned model has preserved the knowledge of the pretrained model. Existing finetuning methods implicitly rely on the Euclidean distance between the finetuned weights and the pretrained weights, assuming that a smaller distance implies better preservation. However, the authors of this work argue that Euclidean distance alone cannot fully capture the degree of semantic preservation, and that a more structural measure is needed. This is where hyperspherical energy comes in.

To define hyperspherical energy, we first need to consider the neurons in a layer of a neural network. Consider a fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\cdots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, where $\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the $i$-th neuron. Each neuron can be normalized to unit length, giving us $\hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$, which lives on the unit hypersphere in $\mathbb{R}^d$. The hyperspherical energy of this layer is then defined as:

$$\text{HE}(\bm{W}):= \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$$

The computation proceeds as follows. For every pair of distinct neurons $(i, j)$ with $i \neq j$, we compute the Euclidean distance between their normalized versions $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}_j$ on the unit hypersphere. We then take the reciprocal of this distance and sum over all such pairs. The result is a single scalar value that characterizes the overall relational structure of the neuron set. The key insight is that this energy depends only on the directions of the neurons, not their magnitudes, since normalization removes magnitude information before the pairwise distances are computed.

This formulation is grounded in prior work on hyperspherical learning, where it has been shown that the arrangement of neurons on a unit hypersphere encodes important structural information about a neural network. The hyperspherical energy is a global measure: it does not look at any single neuron in isolation, but rather at the entire constellation of neurons within a layer. This global, relational perspective is what makes it a more structural measure compared to simply computing the Euclidean distance between weight matrices.

## Pairwise Neuron Similarity on the Unit Hypersphere

To understand why hyperspherical energy is meaningful, we need to think about what it means for two neurons to be similar on the unit hypersphere. Once neurons are normalized to unit length, their similarity can be characterized by the angular relationship between them. Two neurons that point in similar directions will be close together on the unit hypersphere, while two neurons that point in very different directions will be far apart.

The hyperspherical energy uses the Euclidean distance between normalized neurons, $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$, as a measure of pairwise dissimilarity. On the unit hypersphere, this Euclidean distance is directly related to the angle between the two neurons. If two normalized neurons are identical, the distance is zero (and the energy term would be infinite, signaling maximal redundancy). If two neurons are maximally separated (pointing in opposite directions), the distance is 2 (the maximum possible distance on the unit hypersphere), and the energy contribution is minimized at $1/2$.

The paper draws on empirical observations from prior work that hyperspherical similarity—such as cosine similarity—encodes semantic information well. In particular, angular feature differences have been shown to characterize the semantic gap between features. This means that when two neurons have a small angular difference, they tend to encode similar semantic concepts, and when they have a large angular difference, they encode different concepts. The pairwise similarity structure across all neurons in a layer thus reflects the semantic organization of the knowledge stored in that layer.

By summing the reciprocal of pairwise distances over all neuron pairs, hyperspherical energy aggregates all of these pairwise angular relationships into a single quantity. A layer where neurons are well-spread on the hypersphere (many large pairwise distances) will have low hyperspherical energy, while a layer where neurons cluster together (many small pairwise distances) will have high hyperspherical energy. The paper identifies this quantity as capturing the "pairwise relational structure among neurons," which is precisely the structure that encodes the semantic knowledge of the pretrained model.

## Connection Between Hyperspherical Energy and Neuron Uniformity

A crucial property of hyperspherical energy is that it captures the level of neuron uniformity on the unit hypersphere. The connection is intuitive when we think about what the energy formula is doing. The term $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ is large when two normalized neurons are close together and small when they are far apart. Summing over all pairs means that if many neurons are clustered in the same region of the hypersphere, many terms in the sum will be large, yielding high overall energy. Conversely, if neurons are uniformly distributed across the hypersphere—maximally spread out—then most pairwise distances will be large, and the energy will be low.

The paper explicitly states that hyperspherical energy "captures the level of neuron uniformity on the unit hypersphere." This connection is important for two reasons. First, in the context of classification, prior work has shown that minimum hyperspherical energy (i.e., maximum uniformity) leads to better generalization because it reduces neuron redundancy. When neurons are uniformly spread, each neuron covers a distinct region of the feature space, and there is less overlap in what they represent. Second, in the context of finetuning, the specific level of uniformity achieved by the pretrained model reflects the specific semantic structure it has learned. This structure is what we want to preserve.

The paper provides a concrete demonstration of why the angular structure matters. In a toy experiment with a convolutional autoencoder trained on flower images, the authors show that the angular information of neurons can almost perfectly recover the input images, while the magnitude information contains no useful information. This experiment illustrates that the directions of neurons—which determine their positions on the unit hypersphere and thus their contribution to hyperspherical energy—are the primary carriers of semantic information. The uniformity of these directions, as captured by hyperspherical energy, therefore reflects the organization of semantic knowledge in the model.

## Hypothesis: Minimal Hyperspherical Energy Difference Indicates Better Semantic Preservation

Given that hyperspherical energy captures the semantic structure of neurons, the authors propose a specific hypothesis for finetuning: a good finetuned model should have a minimal difference in hyperspherical energy compared to the pretrained model. Formally, for a finetuned weight matrix $\bm{W}$ and pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, the goal is to minimize:

$$\left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\right\|$$

which expands to:

$$\bigg{\|} \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}-\sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i-\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|^{-1}\bigg{\|}$$

where $\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i$ denotes the $i$-th normalized neuron of the pretrained model.

The reasoning behind this hypothesis is as follows. The pretrained model has already learned a rich semantic structure through extensive pretraining on large-scale data. This structure is encoded in the angular relationships among neurons, which hyperspherical energy summarizes. If finetuning disrupts these angular relationships too much, the semantic knowledge of the pretrained model is degraded, and the finetuned model may lose its ability to generate high-fidelity, diverse images. By requiring the hyperspherical energy to remain close to that of the pretrained model, we are requiring that the overall relational structure among neurons be preserved.

This hypothesis stands in contrast to the implicit assumption of existing finetuning methods, which only minimize the Euclidean distance $\|\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$ between finetuned and pretrained weights. The authors argue that Euclidean distance alone is insufficient because it does not capture the structural relationships among neurons. Two weight matrices could be close in Euclidean distance but have very different neuron angular structures, leading to different semantic behaviors. Conversely, preserving the hyperspherical energy directly targets the structural property that matters for semantic generation.

The paper finds this property to be "crucial for preserving the semantic generation ability of text-to-image diffusion models," and the empirical results strongly support this hypothesis. Models finetuned with methods that preserve hyperspherical energy (OFT and COFT) demonstrate better subject fidelity, text prompt fidelity, and controllability compared to methods that do not.

## Why a Naive Regularizer Approach Is Insufficient

Given the hypothesis that we want to minimize the hyperspherical energy difference, one might consider simply adding a regularization term to the finetuning loss that penalizes deviations in hyperspherical energy. This would mean optimizing the downstream task loss while simultaneously minimizing $\|\text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\|$ as a soft penalty.

The authors explicitly note that this naive approach is problematic. The key issue is that "there is no guarantee that the hyperspherical energy difference can be well minimized" when it is only a soft regularization term. In other words, the optimizer might find a compromise between the task loss and the regularization penalty that still leaves a significant gap in hyperspherical energy. The regularization approach treats the preservation of hyperspherical energy as a preference rather than a hard constraint, and depending on the optimization landscape, the actual energy difference achieved at convergence could be far from zero.

This is a fundamental limitation because the goal is exact preservation of the pairwise angular structure. A soft penalty cannot guarantee exactness—it can only encourage the model to move toward the desired property. In practice, the interplay between the task loss gradients and the regularization gradients might lead to a solution where the hyperspherical energy is partially but not fully preserved, leaving the semantic structure in a degraded state.

The authors therefore reject the regularization approach and instead seek a method that can provably guarantee the preservation of hyperspherical energy. This leads them to look for a transformation that, by its mathematical nature, leaves hyperspherical energy invariant. Rather than trying to minimize the energy difference after the fact through a penalty, they want to restrict the finetuning to a family of transformations where the energy difference is guaranteed to be exactly zero by construction.

## The Invariance Property: Orthogonal Transformations Preserve Pairwise Hyperspherical Similarity

The solution to the insufficiency of the regularization approach comes from a key mathematical property: hyperspherical energy is invariant under orthogonal transformations. Specifically, if the same orthogonal transformation is applied to all neurons in a layer, the pairwise hyperspherical similarities between all neurons remain unchanged.

To see why, consider an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ satisfying $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$. If we transform every neuron in the layer by this matrix, the new weight matrix becomes $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. The normalized neurons after transformation are $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i / \|\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i\|$. Since orthogonal transformations preserve vector norms (i.e., $\|\bm{R}\bm{v}\| = \|\bm{v}\|$ for any vector $\bm{v}$), we have $\|\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i\| = \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i\| = 1$, so $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i$. Now, the pairwise distance between any two transformed neurons is:

$$\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\| = \|\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\| = \|\bm{R}(\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \hat{\bm{w}}^0_j)\| = \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|$$

where the last equality uses the norm-preserving property of orthogonal matrices. Since every pairwise distance is unchanged, the hyperspherical energy $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$ exactly. The energy difference is provably zero, not approximately zero.

This invariance property is the mathematical foundation of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). Instead of adding a regularizer and hoping for the best, OFT constrains the finetuning to only learn an orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ for each layer, applied as $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because the transformation is orthogonal, the hyperspherical energy is preserved by construction, with no need for a regularization term.

The authors provide additional intuition for why orthogonal transformation is the right choice. They draw an analogy to 2D Fourier transform, where an image can be decomposed into magnitude and phase spectra. The phase spectrum, which encodes angular information, preserves the major part of the image's semantics. Similarly, in neural networks, the angles between neurons carry the semantic information. Orthogonal transformations rotate or reflect all neurons together, preserving all pairwise angles while still allowing the model to adapt to the downstream task. The transformation can be viewed as "adjusting the canonical coordinate system for the neurons in the same layer"—a change of perspective that preserves the relational structure.

The authors also note that this approach is inspired by orthogonal over-parameterized training, where neural networks are trained from scratch by orthogonally transforming a randomly initialized network. However, there is a critical difference: while that prior work aims to minimize hyperspherical energy (for better classification generalization), OFT aims to preserve the existing hyperspherical energy of the pretrained model. Minimizing the energy would make neurons uniformly spread, which could destroy the specific semantic structure learned during pretraining. The goal in finetuning is not uniformity but preservation, and the invariance property of orthogonal transformations provides exactly the guarantee needed.
