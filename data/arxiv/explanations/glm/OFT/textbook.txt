\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# Introduction to Text-to-Image Diffusion Models and the Finetuning Problem

#

## Overview of Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

In recent years, text-to-image diffusion models have emerged as a powerful class of generative models capable of producing photorealistic images from textual descriptions. These models represent a convergence of two major lines of research: diffusion-based generative modeling and vision-language representation learning. The rapid development in diffusion models, which learn to generate data by progressively denoising random noise, combined with advances in learning joint representations of images and text, has enabled a leap forward in the quality and controllability of generated images.

Several landmark models have defined this landscape. GLIDE and Imagen train diffusion models directly in the pixel space. GLIDE jointly trains a text encoder alongside a diffusion prior using paired text-image data, allowing the model to learn the alignment between language and visual content during training. Imagen, by contrast, employs a frozen pretrained text encoder, leveraging the representational power of a language model that has already been trained on large-scale text corpora. Training in pixel space means that the diffusion process operates directly on image pixels, which can be computationally expensive but allows for direct supervision on the image signal.

Stable Diffusion and DALL-E2 take a different approach by training diffusion models in a latent space rather than directly in pixel space. Stable Diffusion uses VQ-GAN to learn a visual codebook as its latent space, effectively compressing images into a lower-dimensional representation where the diffusion process is more tractable. DALL-E2 adopts CLIP to construct a joint latent embedding space for representing both images and text, providing a shared semantic space that naturally aligns the two modalities. By operating in latent space, these models achieve greater computational efficiency during both training and inference.

Beyond diffusion models, other generative paradigms have also been explored for text-to-image generation, including generative adversarial networks and autoregressive models. However, diffusion-based approaches have come to dominate the field due to their superior image quality and training stability. It is worth emphasizing that the finetuning methods discussed in this chapter are model-agnostic in principle and can be applied to any text-to-image diffusion model, regardless of whether it operates in pixel space or latent space. This generality is an important property, as new diffusion architectures continue to emerge.

#

## The Two Downstream Tasks: Subject-driven Generation and Controllable Generation

Despite the impressive capabilities of text-to-image diffusion models, text guidance alone can be ambiguous and insufficient for providing fine-grained and accurate control over the generated images. A text prompt such as "a dog in a park" specifies the general content of the desired image but leaves enormous freedom in how the dog looks, its pose, the composition of the scene, and countless other details. To address this shortcoming, two important downstream tasks have been identified that require adapting a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model to exert more precise control.

The first task is subject-driven generation. Given just a few images of a specific subject—perhaps a particular dog, a unique vase, or a person's face—the goal is to generate images of that same subject in a different context, guided by a text prompt. For example, if a user provides several photos of their pet dog and the text prompt "a dog sitting on the beach," the model should generate an image of that specific dog, preserving its identifying features, situated on a beach. The challenge here is to capture the subject-specific visual identity from only a handful of images and to faithfully reproduce it in novel contexts without losing the subject's distinctive characteristics. This task was popularized by DreamBooth, which finetunes the text-to-image diffusion model using a reconstruction loss and a class-specific prior preservation loss, along with a customized token representing the subject.

The second task is controllable generation. Here, the model is provided with an additional control signal beyond the text prompt—such as Canny edge maps, segmentation maps, or facial landmarks—and the goal is to generate images that follow both the control signal and the text prompt. For instance, a user might provide a rough sketch (Canny edge map) of a landscape along with the text prompt "a mountain landscape at sunset," and the model should generate a photorealistic image whose structure matches the sketch while incorporating the colors and atmosphere described by the text. ControlNet and T2I-Adapter are prominent methods for this task, both of which finetune a pretrained diffusion model to accept additional control signals.

Both of these tasks fundamentally reduce to the same core problem: how to effectively finetune a text-to-image diffusion model so that it acquires new capabilities—whether recognizing a new subject or responding to a new control signal—without losing the generative performance it gained during pretraining. The model must adapt to the downstream task while retaining its ability to produce high-fidelity, diverse, and semantically meaningful images. This shared challenge motivates the development of principled finetuning methods that go beyond naive approaches.

#

## Desiderata for Effective Finetuning: Training Efficiency and Generalizability Preservation

Given that both subject-driven generation and controllable generation require finetuning a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model, it is essential to identify the properties that distinguish a good finetuning method from a poor one. Two key desiderata emerge from the practical demands of these tasks.

The first desideratum is training efficiency. Text-to-image diffusion models are large, often containing hundreds of millions or even billions of parameters. Finetuning such models from scratch or with a large number of trainable parameters can be prohibitively expensive in terms of both computation and memory. Moreover, the downstream tasks often involve limited training data: subject-driven generation may use only a few images of a subject, and controllable generation may benefit from rapid adaptation with small datasets. An effective finetuning method should therefore have fewer trainable parameters and require fewer training epochs to reach satisfactory performance. This efficiency is not merely a matter of convenience; it directly impacts the feasibility of deploying these models in real-world applications where resources are constrained and rapid adaptation is valued.

The second desideratum is generalizability preservation. A pretrained text-to-image diffusion model has learned a rich and diverse generative distribution over images, capable of producing high-fidelity outputs across a wide range of content and styles. When finetuning the model for a downstream task, it is crucial that this pretraining generative performance is preserved. The model should not forget how to generate realistic images, nor should it collapse to producing a narrow range of outputs. This is sometimes referred to as avoiding catastrophic forgetting, where the model's pretrained knowledge is overwritten by the finetuning objective. Preserving generalizability means that the finetuned model should still produce high-fidelity and diverse images, even as it acquires new task-specific capabilities. The tension between adaptation and preservation is at the heart of the finetuning problem: one wants to change the model enough to perform the downstream task well, but not so much that the pretrained generative ability is lost.

These two desiderata—training efficiency and generalizability preservation—are not independent. A method that uses very few trainable parameters may be efficient but could lack the flexibility to adapt to the downstream task. Conversely, a method that freely updates all model parameters may adapt quickly but risks destroying pretrained knowledge. An ideal finetuning method strikes a balance between these competing demands, achieving strong task performance with minimal training while preserving the generative capabilities of the pretrained model.

#

## Limitations of Existing Finetuning Strategies

Existing finetuning strategies for text-to-image diffusion models can be broadly categorized into two approaches, each with significant limitations.

The first approach is standard finetuning with a small learning rate. In this strategy, the pretrained model's neuron weights are directly updated via gradient descent, but the learning rate is kept small to limit the magnitude of the updates. DreamBooth exemplifies this approach for subject-driven generation. The intuition is straightforward: a small learning rate implicitly encourages the finetuned model to remain close to the pretrained model in parameter space, thereby preserving the pretrained generative ability to some extent. However, this approach has several drawbacks. First, even with a small learning rate, there is no formal guarantee that the pretrained generative performance will be preserved. The model can still drift away from its pretrained state in ways that degrade its generalization ability, especially after many training iterations. Second, the choice of learning rate and the number of training epochs become critical hyperparameters that must be carefully tuned for each task or even each subject. Too many iterations can cause the model to collapse, generating images that have lost the diversity and fidelity of the pretrained model. Too few iterations may result in insufficient adaptation to the downstream task. This sensitivity makes the approach fragile and difficult to use reliably in practice.

The second approach is re-parameterized finetuning, which adds a small learnable component to the pretrained model's weights rather than updating them directly. LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) is a prominent example, which assumes that the weight update during finetuning has a low-rank structure. Specifically, LoRA introduces an additive low-rank matrix to the pretrained weights, so the finetuned weight matrix is $\bm{M} = \bm{M}^0 + \Delta\bm{M}$ where $\bm{M}^0$ is the pretrained weight matrix, $\Delta\bm{M}$ is the low-rank update, and $\text{rank}(\Delta\bm{M}) = r'$ for some small $r'$. This reduces the number of trainable parameters while still allowing the model to adapt. However, LoRA also has limitations. The low-rank constraint restricts the form of the weight update, which may be insufficient to capture the transformations needed for effective adaptation. Moreover, like standard finetuning, LoRA does not provide any formal guarantee that the pretrained generative performance is preserved. The low-rank structure is an assumption about the nature of the finetuning update, but it is not grounded in a principled measure of what makes a finetuned model preserve the pretrained model's capabilities.

A fundamental difficulty shared by both approaches is the lack of a principled measure for quantifying the preservation of pretrained generative ability. Without such a measure, it is impossible to guarantee that a finetuning method will preserve the pretrained model's performance, and it becomes difficult to design finetuning strategies with formal guarantees. This gap motivates the search for a more structural and theoretically grounded approach to finetuning.

#

## The Problem with Euclidean Distance as a Measure of Semantic Preservation

A critical assumption underlying existing finetuning methods is that a smaller Euclidean distance between the finetuned model and the pretrained model indicates better preservation of the pretrained ability. In standard finetuning, the small learning rate implicitly minimizes $\|\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$, where $\bm{M}$ is the finetuned model's weights and $\bm{M}^0$ is the pretrained model's weights. In LoRA, the low-rank constraint $\text{rank}(\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0) = r'$ limits the complexity of the weight update, which indirectly keeps the Euclidean distance small when $r'$ is small.

While this assumption may occasionally hold, it has a fundamental limitation: the Euclidean difference between two sets of weights does not fully capture the degree of semantic preservation. Two models can have weights that are close in Euclidean distance but produce semantically very different outputs, and conversely, two models with larger Euclidean distance between their weights might still produce similar images. The reason is that Euclidean distance treats all weight coordinates independently and equally, ignoring the structural and relational properties of the weights that determine the model's behavior.

Consider, for example, two neurons in a layer. Their individual weight values might change by a small Euclidean amount, but if the angle between them changes significantly, the semantic information encoded by their pairwise relationship could be altered substantially. Conversely, a transformation that changes the absolute weight values but preserves the angles between neurons might maintain the model's semantic behavior. This suggests that the geometric relationships among neurons—particularly their angular relationships—are more relevant to semantic preservation than the raw Euclidean distance between weight matrices.

This observation is supported by empirical findings in the literature. Studies have shown that hyperspherical similarity, which is based on angular relationships, encodes semantic information well. Neural networks trained with neurons normalized onto a unit hypersphere yield comparable capacity and even better generalizability, implying that the direction of neurons captures the most important information from data. These findings suggest that a more structural measure of model difference—one that accounts for the relational geometry of neurons—would be a better predictor of semantic preservation than Euclidean distance alone.

The inadequacy of Euclidean distance also explains a practical pain point in finetuning: the need for very small learning rates. Because Euclidean distance is an imperfect proxy for semantic preservation, practitioners must be conservative and use tiny learning rates to avoid inadvertently destroying the pretrained model's capabilities. This conservatism slows down training and makes the finetuning process inefficient. A finetuning method that directly preserves the structurally relevant properties of the pretrained model would not need to rely on this blunt proxy and could potentially use larger learning rates or fewer training iterations while still maintaining semantic preservation.

## Motivation for a Structural Measure of Model Difference

The limitations of Euclidean distance as a measure of semantic preservation motivate the search for a more structural measure that captures the relational geometry of neurons. Such a measure should characterize the pairwise relationships among neurons in a way that reflects the semantic knowledge encoded in the pretrained model.

One such measure is hyperspherical energy, which is inspired by the empirical observation that hyperspherical similarity encodes semantic information. Hyperspherical energy is defined as the sum of hyperspherical similarities (such as cosine similarity) between all pairs of neurons in the same layer. For a weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1, \cdots, \bm{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where $\bm{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the $i$-th neuron, the hyperspherical energy is defined as:

$$\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$$

where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$ denotes the $i$-th normalized neuron. The hyperspherical energy captures the level of neuron uniformity on the unit hypersphere: when neurons are uniformly spaced, the energy is low; when neurons are clustered together, the energy is high. This measure goes beyond individual neuron values and instead characterizes the pairwise relational structure among all neurons in a layer.

The hypothesis is that a good finetuned model should have a minimal difference in hyperspherical energy compared to the pretrained model. In other words, the finetuning process should preserve the pairwise angular relationships among neurons, as these relationships encode the structural knowledge that underpins the pretrained model's generative ability. A naive approach would be to add a regularizer that penalizes differences in hyperspherical energy during finetuning. However, regularization alone cannot guarantee that the hyperspherical energy difference will be well minimized—the optimization may find solutions that approximately satisfy the regularizer but still deviate significantly from the pretrained energy.

A more principled approach takes advantage of an invariance property of hyperspherical energy: the pairwise hyperspherical similarity between neurons is provably preserved if the same orthogonal transformation is applied to all neurons in a layer. An orthogonal transformation—comprising rotations and reflections—preserves all pairwise angles between vectors, and therefore preserves the hyperspherical energy exactly. This invariance suggests a powerful finetuning strategy: instead of freely updating the model's weights, one can learn a layer-shared orthogonal transformation that adapts the model to the downstream task while guaranteeing that the hyperspherical energy remains unchanged. This is the central insight behind Orthogonal Finetuning, which will be the focus of the subsequent chapters.

The motivation for this structural approach can also be understood through an analogy with the two-dimensional Fourier transform. An image can be decomposed into a magnitude spectrum and a phase spectrum, where the phase spectrum encodes the angular information between the input and the basis functions. It is well known that the phase spectrum preserves the major part of the image's semantics: the phase spectrum of an image, combined with a random magnitude spectrum, can still reconstruct the original image without losing its semantic content. By analogy, the angles between neurons in a neural network may play a role analogous to the phase spectrum, encoding the crucial semantic information that determines the model's output. Changing neuron directions is therefore the key to semantically modifying the generated image, which is precisely the goal of both subject-driven and controllable generation. However, changing neuron directions with a large degree of freedom would inevitably destroy the pretrained generative performance. Constraining the transformation to be orthogonal preserves the angles between all pairs of neurons, maintaining the structural knowledge of the pretrained model while allowing enough flexibility to adapt to the downstream task.

This structural perspective also offers a way to think about the trade-off between flexibility and stability in finetuning. Standard finetuning is maximally flexible—it can update the model in any direction—but it offers no stability guarantees and can easily cause model collapse. At the other extreme, freezing the model entirely guarantees perfect preservation but offers no adaptation. Orthogonal transformation occupies a sweet spot: it is flexible enough to modify neuron directions and adapt to new tasks, yet it is constrained enough to preserve the pairwise angular structure that encodes the pretrained model's semantic knowledge. This balance between flexibility and regularity is the key to effective finetuning, and it provides a principled foundation that Euclidean distance-based approaches lack.

# Neuron Angular Information and Semantic Representation

Understanding how neural networks encode and preserve semantic information is fundamental to designing effective finetuning strategies. This chapter delves into the central insight that motivates Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): the directions (angles) of neurons, rather than their magnitudes, are the primary carriers of semantic information in neural networks. By exploring this principle through multiple lenses—hyperspherical geometry, prior empirical evidence, controlled experiments, and an illuminating analogy from signal processing—we build an intuition for why finetuning methods that modify neuron directions while preserving their pairwise angular relationships can achieve both effective semantic adaptation and preservation of pretrained knowledge.

# Hyperspherical Similarity and Its Role in Encoding Semantic Information

At the heart of the OFT framework lies the concept of hyperspherical similarity, a geometric perspective on how neurons relate to one another. When we speak of neurons in a neural network layer, we can view each neuron as a vector in a high-dimensional space. If we normalize these vectors onto a unit hypersphere—a generalization of the sphere to arbitrary dimensions—we can characterize the relationships between neurons purely in terms of the angles between them. This angular relationship is what we call hyperspherical similarity, and it is commonly measured using cosine similarity between pairs of neuron vectors.

The concept of hyperspherical energy formalizes this idea further. For a weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1, \cdots, \bm{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where $\bm{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the $i$-th neuron, the hyperspherical energy is defined as $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$, where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$ denotes the $i$-th normalized neuron. This quantity sums the inverse distances between all pairs of normalized neurons on the unit hypersphere. When neurons are uniformly spread across the hypersphere—meaning they point in diverse directions with minimal redundancy—the hyperspherical energy is low. When neurons cluster together, pointing in similar directions, the energy increases because pairs of neurons become closer on the hypersphere.

The key insight from prior research is that this hyperspherical structure encodes semantic information. The pairwise angles between neurons capture how the network has organized its internal representations to distinguish between different semantic concepts. A well-trained network has neurons that are strategically positioned to respond to meaningful features in the data, and the geometric arrangement of these neurons on the hypersphere reflects the semantic structure learned during pretraining. When we finetune a model, we want to adapt it to new tasks while preserving this learned semantic structure as much as possible. This is precisely why the authors of OFT hypothesize that a good finetuned model should have a minimal difference in hyperspherical energy compared to the pretrained model. If the hyperspherical energy changes significantly during finetuning, the internal semantic organization of the network may be disrupted, leading to degradation of the generative performance that was carefully learned during pretraining.

# Evidence from Prior Work: SphereNet and Angular Feature Differences

The hypothesis that angular information encodes semantics is not merely a theoretical conjecture; it is supported by a body of empirical evidence from prior work. One of the most compelling pieces of evidence comes from SphereNet, a neural network architecture that normalizes all neurons onto a unit hypersphere during training. In SphereNet, every neuron weight vector is constrained to have unit norm, meaning that the network operates entirely in the angular domain, with no magnitude information. Despite this constraint, SphereNet achieves comparable capacity and even better generalizability than conventional networks. This result is profound because it demonstrates that the direction of neurons—where they point on the hypersphere—is sufficient to capture the most important information from data. The magnitude, which is discarded in SphereNet, turns out to be less critical for representing learned knowledge.

Additional evidence comes from studies on angular feature differences. Researchers have observed that the angular difference between feature vectors correlates strongly with the semantic gap between the corresponding concepts. In other words, when two feature vectors point in similar directions (small angular difference), they tend to represent semantically similar concepts, and when they point in different directions (large angular difference), they represent semantically distinct concepts. This angular characterization of semantic relationships has been shown to be more informative than magnitude-based measures, particularly in tasks involving recognition and classification.

Together, these findings establish a strong foundation for the OFT approach. If neuron directions encode semantic information, then finetuning a model to adapt to new tasks should primarily involve modifying neuron directions rather than magnitudes. Moreover, if the pairwise angles between neurons reflect the learned semantic structure, then preserving these angles during finetuning should help maintain the pretrained model's generative capabilities while allowing for task-specific adaptation.

# Toy Experiment: Convolutional Autoencoder Demonstrating Angular vs. Magnitude Information

To provide a concrete and intuitive demonstration that neuron directions encode semantic information, the authors of OFT conduct a carefully designed toy experiment using a standard convolutional autoencoder trained on flower images. The experiment is structured to isolate the contributions of angular and magnitude information in a controlled setting, making the distinction between the two starkly visible.

During training, the autoencoder uses the standard inner product to compute feature maps. For a convolution kernel $\bm{w}$ and input $\bm{x}$ in a sliding window, the element output is $z = \bm{w}^\top \bm{x}$. This inner product inherently combines both magnitude and angular information, as it can be decomposed as $z = \|\bm{w}\| \|\bm{x}\| \cos(\theta)$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $\bm{w}$ and $\bm{x}$. The network learns to reconstruct flower images by adjusting both the directions and magnitudes of its kernels through standard backpropagation.

At test time, the experiment introduces three different ways to generate the feature map, each isolating a different component of the neuron computation. The first method uses the standard inner product, exactly as in training—this serves as the baseline. The second method uses only the magnitude information, effectively discarding the angular relationship between the neuron and the input. The third method uses only the angular information (cosine similarity), discarding the magnitude information. Critically, the cosine activation is not used during training; the network is trained exclusively with the inner product. This ensures that any performance difference at test time reflects the intrinsic information content of angular versus magnitude information, rather than an artifact of the training procedure.

The results are striking. The angular information alone can almost perfectly recover the input flower images, producing reconstructions that are visually indistinguishable from those produced by the full inner product. In contrast, the magnitude information alone produces reconstructions that contain no useful semantic content—the reconstructed images bear no resemblance to the original flowers. This experiment provides compelling evidence that the angles (directions) of neurons play the dominant role in storing the semantic information of the input images. The magnitudes, while present in the trained network, appear to carry little or no information about the visual content being processed.

This finding has direct implications for finetuning. If we want to modify the semantic output of a neural network—for instance, to generate images of a specific subject in a new context or to follow a new control signal—adjusting the directions of neurons is likely to be far more effective than adjusting their magnitudes. The autoencoder experiment suggests that the network's "knowledge" is encoded primarily in the angular structure of its neurons, and that modifying this structure is the key to meaningful semantic adaptation.

# Inspiration from 2D Fourier Transform: Phase Spectrum Preserves Semantics

The insight that angular information carries semantic content finds an elegant parallel in signal processing, specifically in the 2D Fourier transform. The 2D Fourier transform decomposes an image into two components: a magnitude spectrum and a phase spectrum. The magnitude spectrum encodes the amplitude of each frequency component, while the phase spectrum encodes the angular relationship between the input signal and the Fourier basis functions. In other words, the phase spectrum captures how the input aligns directionally with each basis element.

A well-known phenomenon in image processing is that the phase spectrum preserves the major part of an image's semantics. This can be demonstrated through a simple experiment: if one takes the phase spectrum of an image and pairs it with a random magnitude spectrum, the resulting reconstruction still resembles the original image. The structural layout, the positions of objects, and the overall visual semantics are largely preserved. Conversely, if one takes the magnitude spectrum of an image and pairs it with a random phase spectrum, the reconstruction bears no resemblance to the original. The image's semantics are entirely lost.

This phenomenon provides an intuitive analogy for why neuron directions matter in neural networks. Just as the phase spectrum—which represents angular information between the input and the Fourier basis—preserves the semantics of an image, the directions of neurons—which represent angular relationships between inputs and learned feature detectors—preserve the semantics of the network's learned representations. The magnitude of neurons, like the magnitude spectrum of a Fourier transform, plays a supporting role but does not carry the primary semantic content.

The analogy also suggests a principled approach to finetuning. If changing the phase of a Fourier representation allows us to semantically modify an image, then changing the directions of neurons should allow us to semantically modify the output of a neural network. Both subject-driven generation (where the goal is to generate images of a specific subject in new contexts) and controllable generation (where the goal is to follow additional control signals) require semantic modification of the generated images. The Fourier analogy tells us that the most effective way to achieve this modification is through angular changes—through the directions of neurons.

# Implications for Finetuning: Why Changing Neuron Directions Is the Key to Semantic Modification

The convergence of evidence from hyperspherical similarity, SphereNet, angular feature differences, the autoencoder experiment, and the Fourier transform analogy leads to a clear conclusion: finetuning neuron directions is the most effective way to achieve semantic modification in neural networks. This conclusion has profound implications for how we should design finetuning methods for text-to-image diffusion models.

When we finetune a text-to-image diffusion model for a downstream task, we want the model to adapt its behavior—to generate images of a new subject or to follow a new control signal—while retaining the broad generative capabilities acquired during pretraining. The standard approach to finetuning, which uses gradient descent with a small learning rate to update all neuron weights, implicitly treats both magnitude and direction as equally important. However, the evidence suggests that this approach is suboptimal. Much of the update may go toward modifying magnitudes that carry little semantic information, while the critical angular modifications may be insufficiently controlled.

A more principled approach would focus finetuning efforts on modifying neuron directions. This is precisely what OFT does: instead of directly updating the weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, OFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ and applies it to transform the pretrained weights, yielding $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0$. An orthogonal transformation is a rotation or reflection, which changes the directions of all neurons in a layer while preserving the angles between any pair of neurons. This means that the overall angular structure—the hyperspherical energy—of the layer is preserved, but individual neuron directions are modified.

The forward pass changes from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, subject to the orthogonality constraint $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R} \bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$. By initializing $\bm{R}$ as the identity matrix, OFT starts from the pretrained model and gradually learns an orthogonal transformation that adapts the model to the downstream task. The transformation rotates the entire set of neurons in a layer as a rigid body, modifying their individual directions to achieve task-specific adaptation while preserving their pairwise relationships.

This approach can also be understood as adjusting the canonical coordinate system for the neurons in the same layer. Rather than independently modifying each neuron—which would risk destroying the pretrained semantic structure—OFT applies a single, coordinated transformation that maintains the internal geometry of the layer. The result is a finetuning method that is both effective at semantic modification (because it changes directions) and protective of pretrained knowledge (because it preserves angles).

# The Challenge of Constraining the Degree of Freedom When Modifying Neuron Directions

While modifying neuron directions is essential for semantic adaptation, doing so without constraints poses significant risks. If we allow neuron directions to change with a large degree of freedom, the finetuning process can easily destroy the pretrained generative performance. The pairwise angles between neurons, which encode the semantic structure learned during pretraining, could be disrupted in arbitrary ways. Neurons that were carefully positioned to respond to distinct features might collapse onto one another, creating redundancy. Other neurons might drift to directions that produce incoherent or degraded outputs. The result is a finetuned model that has adapted to the downstream task at the cost of its general generative ability—a phenomenon often observed in standard finetuning, where too many training iterations or too high a learning rate leads to model collapse.

The challenge, then, is to find a way to modify neuron directions that is expressive enough to achieve meaningful semantic adaptation but constrained enough to preserve the pretrained structure. OFT addresses this challenge by preserving the angle between any pair of neurons through the use of orthogonal transformations. The hypothesis underlying this approach is that the angles between neurons are crucial for representing the knowledge of neural networks. By learning a layer-shared orthogonal transformation that rotates or reflects all neurons together, OFT ensures that every pairwise angle remains exactly the same as in the pretrained model. The hyperspherical energy, which characterizes the overall angular structure, stays unchanged.

However, even within the space of orthogonal transformations, there is still considerable flexibility. An orthogonal matrix in dimension $d$ has $d(d-1)/2$ degrees of freedom, which can be large for high-dimensional layers. To further constrain the degree of freedom and improve parameter efficiency, OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure for the orthogonal matrix. Instead of learning a single $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix, OFT learns $r$ smaller orthogonal matrices of size $d/r \times d/r$, arranged block-diagonally. This reduces the number of trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ while still guaranteeing orthogonality and, consequently, preservation of hyperspherical energy.

The Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) variant goes one step further by imposing an additional constraint: the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ must remain within an $\epsilon$-neighborhood of the identity matrix, i.e., $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. This constraint limits how far the finetuned model can deviate from the pretrained model, providing an explicit control over the trade-off between flexibility and stability. With larger $\epsilon$, COFT approaches the behavior of unconstrained OFT, allowing more aggressive adaptation. With smaller $\epsilon$, COFT increasingly resembles the pretrained model, prioritizing preservation over adaptation. This explicit deviation constraint is typically only implicit in standard finetuning methods, achieved through small learning rates. COFT makes it explicit and controllable.

The discovery of this trade-off between flexibility and regularity is one of the key insights of the OFT framework. Standard finetuning, which updates all weights with minimal constraints, is the most flexible approach but yields poor stability and is prone to model collapse. On the other extreme, freezing the model entirely is maximally stable but offers no adaptation. OFT finds a principled middle ground by preserving pairwise neuron angles, allowing meaningful semantic modification while protecting the pretrained structure. The block-diagonal parameterization and the COFT deviation constraint provide additional knobs to adjust this balance, making the finetuning process both effective and stable across a range of downstream tasks.

# Hyperspherical Energy: A Structural Measure of Neuron Relationships

## Definition of Hyperspherical Energy and Its Computation

When we finetune a large pretrained text-to-image diffusion model, one of the central challenges is knowing how to measure whether the finetuned model has preserved the knowledge of the pretrained model. Existing finetuning methods implicitly rely on the Euclidean distance between the finetuned weights and the pretrained weights, assuming that a smaller distance implies better preservation. However, the authors of this work argue that Euclidean distance alone cannot fully capture the degree of semantic preservation, and that a more structural measure is needed. This is where hyperspherical energy comes in.

To define hyperspherical energy, we first need to consider the neurons in a layer of a neural network. Consider a fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\cdots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, where $\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the $i$-th neuron. Each neuron can be normalized to unit length, giving us $\hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$, which lives on the unit hypersphere in $\mathbb{R}^d$. The hyperspherical energy of this layer is then defined as:

$$\text{HE}(\bm{W}):= \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$$

The computation proceeds as follows. For every pair of distinct neurons $(i, j)$ with $i \neq j$, we compute the Euclidean distance between their normalized versions $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}_j$ on the unit hypersphere. We then take the reciprocal of this distance and sum over all such pairs. The result is a single scalar value that characterizes the overall relational structure of the neuron set. The key insight is that this energy depends only on the directions of the neurons, not their magnitudes, since normalization removes magnitude information before the pairwise distances are computed.

This formulation is grounded in prior work on hyperspherical learning, where it has been shown that the arrangement of neurons on a unit hypersphere encodes important structural information about a neural network. The hyperspherical energy is a global measure: it does not look at any single neuron in isolation, but rather at the entire constellation of neurons within a layer. This global, relational perspective is what makes it a more structural measure compared to simply computing the Euclidean distance between weight matrices.

## Pairwise Neuron Similarity on the Unit Hypersphere

To understand why hyperspherical energy is meaningful, we need to think about what it means for two neurons to be similar on the unit hypersphere. Once neurons are normalized to unit length, their similarity can be characterized by the angular relationship between them. Two neurons that point in similar directions will be close together on the unit hypersphere, while two neurons that point in very different directions will be far apart.

The hyperspherical energy uses the Euclidean distance between normalized neurons, $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$, as a measure of pairwise dissimilarity. On the unit hypersphere, this Euclidean distance is directly related to the angle between the two neurons. If two normalized neurons are identical, the distance is zero (and the energy term would be infinite, signaling maximal redundancy). If two neurons are maximally separated (pointing in opposite directions), the distance is 2 (the maximum possible distance on the unit hypersphere), and the energy contribution is minimized at $1/2$.

The paper draws on empirical observations from prior work that hyperspherical similarity—such as cosine similarity—encodes semantic information well. In particular, angular feature differences have been shown to characterize the semantic gap between features. This means that when two neurons have a small angular difference, they tend to encode similar semantic concepts, and when they have a large angular difference, they encode different concepts. The pairwise similarity structure across all neurons in a layer thus reflects the semantic organization of the knowledge stored in that layer.

By summing the reciprocal of pairwise distances over all neuron pairs, hyperspherical energy aggregates all of these pairwise angular relationships into a single quantity. A layer where neurons are well-spread on the hypersphere (many large pairwise distances) will have low hyperspherical energy, while a layer where neurons cluster together (many small pairwise distances) will have high hyperspherical energy. The paper identifies this quantity as capturing the "pairwise relational structure among neurons," which is precisely the structure that encodes the semantic knowledge of the pretrained model.

## Connection Between Hyperspherical Energy and Neuron Uniformity

A crucial property of hyperspherical energy is that it captures the level of neuron uniformity on the unit hypersphere. The connection is intuitive when we think about what the energy formula is doing. The term $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ is large when two normalized neurons are close together and small when they are far apart. Summing over all pairs means that if many neurons are clustered in the same region of the hypersphere, many terms in the sum will be large, yielding high overall energy. Conversely, if neurons are uniformly distributed across the hypersphere—maximally spread out—then most pairwise distances will be large, and the energy will be low.

The paper explicitly states that hyperspherical energy "captures the level of neuron uniformity on the unit hypersphere." This connection is important for two reasons. First, in the context of classification, prior work has shown that minimum hyperspherical energy (i.e., maximum uniformity) leads to better generalization because it reduces neuron redundancy. When neurons are uniformly spread, each neuron covers a distinct region of the feature space, and there is less overlap in what they represent. Second, in the context of finetuning, the specific level of uniformity achieved by the pretrained model reflects the specific semantic structure it has learned. This structure is what we want to preserve.

The paper provides a concrete demonstration of why the angular structure matters. In a toy experiment with a convolutional autoencoder trained on flower images, the authors show that the angular information of neurons can almost perfectly recover the input images, while the magnitude information contains no useful information. This experiment illustrates that the directions of neurons—which determine their positions on the unit hypersphere and thus their contribution to hyperspherical energy—are the primary carriers of semantic information. The uniformity of these directions, as captured by hyperspherical energy, therefore reflects the organization of semantic knowledge in the model.

## Hypothesis: Minimal Hyperspherical Energy Difference Indicates Better Semantic Preservation

Given that hyperspherical energy captures the semantic structure of neurons, the authors propose a specific hypothesis for finetuning: a good finetuned model should have a minimal difference in hyperspherical energy compared to the pretrained model. Formally, for a finetuned weight matrix $\bm{W}$ and pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, the goal is to minimize:

$$\left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\right\|$$

which expands to:

$$\bigg{\|} \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}-\sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i-\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|^{-1}\bigg{\|}$$

where $\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i$ denotes the $i$-th normalized neuron of the pretrained model.

The reasoning behind this hypothesis is as follows. The pretrained model has already learned a rich semantic structure through extensive pretraining on large-scale data. This structure is encoded in the angular relationships among neurons, which hyperspherical energy summarizes. If finetuning disrupts these angular relationships too much, the semantic knowledge of the pretrained model is degraded, and the finetuned model may lose its ability to generate high-fidelity, diverse images. By requiring the hyperspherical energy to remain close to that of the pretrained model, we are requiring that the overall relational structure among neurons be preserved.

This hypothesis stands in contrast to the implicit assumption of existing finetuning methods, which only minimize the Euclidean distance $\|\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$ between finetuned and pretrained weights. The authors argue that Euclidean distance alone is insufficient because it does not capture the structural relationships among neurons. Two weight matrices could be close in Euclidean distance but have very different neuron angular structures, leading to different semantic behaviors. Conversely, preserving the hyperspherical energy directly targets the structural property that matters for semantic generation.

The paper finds this property to be "crucial for preserving the semantic generation ability of text-to-image diffusion models," and the empirical results strongly support this hypothesis. Models finetuned with methods that preserve hyperspherical energy (OFT and COFT) demonstrate better subject fidelity, text prompt fidelity, and controllability compared to methods that do not.

## Why a Naive Regularizer Approach Is Insufficient

Given the hypothesis that we want to minimize the hyperspherical energy difference, one might consider simply adding a regularization term to the finetuning loss that penalizes deviations in hyperspherical energy. This would mean optimizing the downstream task loss while simultaneously minimizing $\|\text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\|$ as a soft penalty.

The authors explicitly note that this naive approach is problematic. The key issue is that "there is no guarantee that the hyperspherical energy difference can be well minimized" when it is only a soft regularization term. In other words, the optimizer might find a compromise between the task loss and the regularization penalty that still leaves a significant gap in hyperspherical energy. The regularization approach treats the preservation of hyperspherical energy as a preference rather than a hard constraint, and depending on the optimization landscape, the actual energy difference achieved at convergence could be far from zero.

This is a fundamental limitation because the goal is exact preservation of the pairwise angular structure. A soft penalty cannot guarantee exactness—it can only encourage the model to move toward the desired property. In practice, the interplay between the task loss gradients and the regularization gradients might lead to a solution where the hyperspherical energy is partially but not fully preserved, leaving the semantic structure in a degraded state.

The authors therefore reject the regularization approach and instead seek a method that can provably guarantee the preservation of hyperspherical energy. This leads them to look for a transformation that, by its mathematical nature, leaves hyperspherical energy invariant. Rather than trying to minimize the energy difference after the fact through a penalty, they want to restrict the finetuning to a family of transformations where the energy difference is guaranteed to be exactly zero by construction.

## The Invariance Property: Orthogonal Transformations Preserve Pairwise Hyperspherical Similarity

The solution to the insufficiency of the regularization approach comes from a key mathematical property: hyperspherical energy is invariant under orthogonal transformations. Specifically, if the same orthogonal transformation is applied to all neurons in a layer, the pairwise hyperspherical similarities between all neurons remain unchanged.

To see why, consider an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ satisfying $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$. If we transform every neuron in the layer by this matrix, the new weight matrix becomes $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. The normalized neurons after transformation are $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i / \|\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i\|$. Since orthogonal transformations preserve vector norms (i.e., $\|\bm{R}\bm{v}\| = \|\bm{v}\|$ for any vector $\bm{v}$), we have $\|\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i\| = \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i\| = 1$, so $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i$. Now, the pairwise distance between any two transformed neurons is:

$$\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\| = \|\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\| = \|\bm{R}(\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \hat{\bm{w}}^0_j)\| = \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|$$

where the last equality uses the norm-preserving property of orthogonal matrices. Since every pairwise distance is unchanged, the hyperspherical energy $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$ exactly. The energy difference is provably zero, not approximately zero.

This invariance property is the mathematical foundation of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). Instead of adding a regularizer and hoping for the best, OFT constrains the finetuning to only learn an orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ for each layer, applied as $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because the transformation is orthogonal, the hyperspherical energy is preserved by construction, with no need for a regularization term.

The authors provide additional intuition for why orthogonal transformation is the right choice. They draw an analogy to 2D Fourier transform, where an image can be decomposed into magnitude and phase spectra. The phase spectrum, which encodes angular information, preserves the major part of the image's semantics. Similarly, in neural networks, the angles between neurons carry the semantic information. Orthogonal transformations rotate or reflect all neurons together, preserving all pairwise angles while still allowing the model to adapt to the downstream task. The transformation can be viewed as "adjusting the canonical coordinate system for the neurons in the same layer"—a change of perspective that preserves the relational structure.

The authors also note that this approach is inspired by orthogonal over-parameterized training, where neural networks are trained from scratch by orthogonally transforming a randomly initialized network. However, there is a critical difference: while that prior work aims to minimize hyperspherical energy (for better classification generalization), OFT aims to preserve the existing hyperspherical energy of the pretrained model. Minimizing the energy would make neurons uniformly spread, which could destroy the specific semantic structure learned during pretraining. The goal in finetuning is not uniformity but preservation, and the invariance property of orthogonal transformations provides exactly the guarantee needed.

# Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): General Framework

# From Hyperspherical Energy Preservation to Orthogonal Transformation

To understand why orthogonal finetuning is a principled approach to adapting text-to-image diffusion models, we must first appreciate the role that neuron geometry plays in encoding semantic information. The key insight motivating OFT comes from the empirical observation that hyperspherical similarity—meaning the angular relationships between vectors on a unit hypersphere—encodes semantic information remarkably well. Prior work has shown that training neural networks with all neurons normalized onto a unit hypersphere yields comparable capacity and even better generalizability, which implies that the directions of neurons, rather than their magnitudes, carry the most important information learned from data.

The paper provides a compelling toy experiment to illustrate this point. A standard convolutional autoencoder is trained on flower images using the standard inner product to produce feature maps. At test time, the feature maps are generated in three different ways: using the inner product (as in training), using only the magnitude information of the neurons, and using only the angular information of the neurons. The results show that the angular information can almost perfectly recover the input images, while the magnitude information contains essentially no useful information. Critically, this angular recovery works even though the network was trained with inner products, not cosine activations. This experiment demonstrates that the angles (directions) of neurons play the dominant role in storing the semantic information of input images.

This insight leads to a natural question: if we want to modify the semantic content of generated images—which is the goal of both subject-driven and controllable generation—then finetuning the directions of neurons should be more effective than finetuning their magnitudes. However, changing neuron directions with a large degree of freedom will inevitably destroy the pretrained generative performance. We need a principled way to modify directions while preserving the structural relationships among neurons.

This is where hyperspherical energy comes in. Hyperspherical energy characterizes the pairwise relational structure among neurons on the unit hypersphere. It is defined as the sum of hyperspherical similarities between all pairs of neurons in the same layer, capturing the level of neuron uniformity. The central hypothesis of OFT is that a good finetuned model should have a minimal difference in hyperspherical energy compared to the pretrained model.

A crucial property of hyperspherical energy is its invariance under orthogonal transformations. Specifically, the pairwise hyperspherical similarity between neurons is provably preserved if we apply the same orthogonal transformation to all neurons in a layer. An orthogonal transformation—meaning a rotation or reflection—preserves all pairwise angles between vectors. This invariance property is the mathematical foundation of OFT: by learning a layer-shared orthogonal transformation, we can adapt the model to downstream tasks while guaranteeing that the hyperspherical energy, and hence the pairwise neuron relationships, remain exactly unchanged.

The intuition can also be understood through an analogy with the 2D Fourier transform. An image can be decomposed into magnitude and phase spectra. The phase spectrum, which encodes angular information, preserves the major part of the image's semantics. For example, the phase spectrum of an image combined with a random magnitude spectrum can still reconstruct the original image without losing its semantic content. This phenomenon suggests that angular information is the carrier of semantics, and that modifying the directions of neurons—while preserving their relative angular relationships—is the key to semantically modifying generated images without destroying the pretrained knowledge.

# Mathematical Formulation of the OFT Optimization Problem

The mathematical formulation of OFT begins by contrasting it with existing finetuning strategies. Conventional finetuning uses gradient descent with a small learning rate to update model weights. This small learning rate implicitly encourages a small deviation from the pretrained model, and standard finetuning can be understood as implicitly minimizing $\|\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$, where $\bm{M}$ represents the finetuned model weights and $\bm{M}^0$ represents the pretrained model weights. LoRA, on the other hand, introduces an additional low-rank constraint on the weight update: $\text{rank}(\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0) = r'$, where $r'$ is set to a small number.

OFT takes a fundamentally different approach by introducing a constraint on the pairwise neuron similarity rather than on the weight update directly. Specifically, OFT imposes the constraint $\|\text{HE}(\bm{M}) - \text{HE}(\bm{M}^0)\| = 0$, where $\text{HE}(\cdot)$ denotes the hyperspherical energy of a weight matrix. To make this concrete, consider a fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1, \cdots, \bm{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where $\bm{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the $i$-th neuron and $\bm{W}^0$ is the pretrained weight matrix. The hyperspherical energy of this layer is defined as:

$$\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$$

where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$ denotes the $i$-th normalized neuron. The hyperspherical energy sums the inverse Euclidean distances between all pairs of normalized neurons, which is equivalent to summing a function of the cosine similarities between all neuron pairs. This quantity captures how uniformly the neurons are distributed on the unit hypersphere.

The OFT optimization problem can then be written as:

$$\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \right\| \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad \min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1} - \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i - \hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|^{-1} \right\|$$

One can observe that the attainable minimum of this objective is zero. This minimum is achieved whenever $\bm{W}$ and $\bm{W}^0$ differ only by a rotation or reflection—that is, when $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix. The determinant of $\bm{R}$ being $1$ or $-1$ corresponds to a rotation or reflection, respectively. This is the key mathematical insight: rather than trying to minimize the hyperspherical energy difference through a soft regularizer (which provides no guarantee of actually achieving zero difference), OFT directly parameterizes the weight update as an orthogonal transformation, which provably achieves the global minimum of zero hyperspherical energy difference.

# The Layer-Shared Orthogonal Transformation: R · W⁰

The practical implementation of OFT is elegantly simple. Given a pretrained fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, OFT does not directly optimize the weight matrix $\bm{W}$. Instead, it optimizes an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ that transforms the pretrained weights. The forward pass of the layer changes from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to:

$$\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}, \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R} \bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$$

where $\bm{W}$ denotes the OFT-finetuned weight matrix, $\bm{I}$ is the identity matrix, and $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the input vector. The orthogonality constraint $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R} \bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$ ensures that $\bm{R}$ is a proper orthogonal transformation (rotation or reflection), which guarantees that all pairwise angles between neurons are preserved.

The term "layer-shared" is important here: the same orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is applied to all neurons within a given layer. This means that the transformation is a global rotation or reflection of the entire neuron set, not a per-neuron adjustment. This is crucial because the preservation of hyperspherical energy relies on applying the same orthogonal transformation to all neurons—applying different transformations to different neurons would change the pairwise angles and thus alter the hyperspherical energy.

To guarantee the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ in a computationally efficient and differentiable manner, OFT adopts the Cayley parameterization. The orthogonal matrix is constructed as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. The Cayley parameterization ensures that any matrix $\bm{R}$ produced this way is automatically orthogonal. One limitation is that the Cayley parameterization can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$ (belonging to the special orthogonal group), meaning it can produce rotations but not reflections. However, the paper finds that this limitation does not affect performance in practice.

To further improve parameter efficiency, especially when the neuron dimension $d$ is large, OFT represents $\bm{R}$ as a block-diagonal matrix with $r$ blocks:

$$\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \cdots, \bm{R}_r) = \begin{bmatrix} \bm{R}_1 \in \text{O}(\frac{d}{r}) & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & \bm{R}_r \in \text{O}(\frac{d}{r}) \end{bmatrix} \in \text{O}(d)$$

where $\text{O}(d)$ denotes the orthogonal group in dimension $d$, and each $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d/r \times d/r}$ is itself an orthogonal matrix. When $r = 1$, this reduces to a standard unconstrained orthogonal matrix. For a full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix, the number of parameters is $d(d-1)/2$, giving complexity $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. With $r$ blocks, the parameter count becomes $d(d/r - 1)/2$, yielding complexity $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. The blocks can optionally be shared (i.e., $\bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j$ for all $i \neq j$), further reducing parameter complexity to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. Despite all these efficiency strategies, the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal, so there is no sacrifice in the preservation of hyperspherical energy.

# Identity Initialization for Starting from Pretrained Weights

A critical design choice in OFT is the initialization of the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. To ensure that OFT begins finetuning from exactly the pretrained model, $\bm{R}$ is initialized as the identity matrix $\bm{I}$. This means that at the start of training, the forward pass is $\bm{z} = (\bm{I} \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, which is identical to the pretrained model's forward pass. This initialization is analogous to the zero initialization strategy used in LoRA, where the low-rank update matrix is initialized to zero so that finetuning starts from the pretrained weights.

Under the Cayley parameterization, the identity initialization of $\bm{R}$ corresponds to initializing the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ as an all-zero matrix. When $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$, the Cayley transform gives $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{0})(\bm{I} - \bm{0})^{-1} = \bm{I}$, which is exactly the identity matrix. This provides a clean and principled starting point for finetuning.

The identity initialization is important for several reasons. First, it ensures that the finetuning process begins with the full generative capability of the pretrained model intact. Second, it means that any deviation from the pretrained behavior is a result of the learning process, not an artifact of initialization. Third, it makes the finetuning dynamics more predictable and interpretable: the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ starts at identity and gradually moves away from it as the model adapts to the downstream task, with the degree of deviation controlled by the training data and optimization process.

# Interpretation as Adjusting the Canonical Coordinate System for Neurons

OFT admits an elegant geometric interpretation: it can be viewed as adjusting the canonical coordinate system for the neurons in the same layer. To understand this, consider that a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ defines a set of neuron vectors in a $d$-dimensional space. Each neuron $\bm{w}_i$ is a vector in this space, and the forward pass computes inner products between the input $\bm{x}$ and each neuron. The set of inner products $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ can be interpreted as the coordinates of the input $\bm{x}$ in the coordinate system defined by the neurons.

When we apply an orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ to get $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$, we are effectively rotating (or reflecting) the entire coordinate system in which the neurons live. The neurons themselves maintain their relative positions to each other—all pairwise angles are preserved—but the coordinate axes are reoriented. This is analogous to rotating a camera while keeping the relative positions of all objects in the scene fixed: the view changes, but the internal structure of the scene is preserved.

This interpretation highlights the difference between OFT and unconstrained finetuning. In unconstrained finetuning, each neuron can move independently, which means both the coordinate system and the relative structure of the neurons can change. This provides maximum flexibility but risks destroying the pretrained knowledge. In OFT, only the coordinate system changes while the relative structure is preserved, which provides sufficient flexibility for adaptation while maintaining the essential geometric relationships encoded in the pretrained model.

The block-diagonal structure of $\bm{R}$ adds another layer of interpretation. When the number of blocks $r$ equals the number of input channels (in the case of convolutional layers), each block transforms only a unique neuron channel, which is similar to learning depth-wise convolution kernels. When all blocks are shared, OFT transforms the neurons with a single orthogonal matrix shared across all channels. This flexibility in the block structure allows OFT to trade off between expressiveness and regularization.

# Comparison to Standard Finetuning and LoRA Constraints

To fully appreciate the novelty of OFT, it is instructive to compare it with existing finetuning methods in terms of the constraints they impose. Standard finetuning, as used in DreamBooth, updates the neuron weights directly using gradient descent with a small learning rate. The implicit constraint is a small Euclidean distance between the finetuned and pretrained models, i.e., minimizing $\|\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$. While this makes intuitive sense—a smaller distance from the pretrained model should mean better preservation of pretrained capabilities—it can still be too flexible for finetuning large models. The Euclidean distance alone is unable to fully capture the degree of semantic preservation, as it does not account for the structural relationships among neurons.

LoRA introduces a low-rank constraint on the weight update: $\text{rank}(\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0) = r'$, where $r'$ is small. This means the weight update $\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0$ is decomposed into the product of two low-rank matrices, reducing the number of trainable parameters. LoRA prevents the information in the pretrained weight matrix from shifting dramatically by constraining the update to a low-rank subspace. In contrast, OFT controls the transform applied to the pretrained weight matrix to be orthogonal (full-rank), which prevents the transform from destroying the pretraining information through a different mechanism—by preserving pairwise angles.

An interesting connection can be drawn by rewriting OFT's forward pass. We have $\bm{z} = (\bm{R} \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R} - \bm{I}) \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $(\bm{R} - \bm{I}) \bm{W}^0$ is analogous to LoRA's low-rank weight update. Since $\bm{W}^0$ is typically full-rank, OFT also performs a low-rank weight update when $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ is low-rank. However, the structural constraints are different: LoRA exploits the low-rank structure of the weight update, while OFT exploits the block-diagonal orthogonality (a sparsity structure) of the transformation matrix.

In terms of parameter efficiency, for LoRA with a low-rank parameter $r'$, the number of trainable parameters is $r'(d + n)$. For OFT with $r$ blocks (no block sharing), the number of parameters is $d(d/r - 1)/2$. If both $r$ and $r'$ scale with $d$ (e.g., $r = r' = \alpha d$ for some constant $0 < \alpha \leq 1$), then LoRA's parameter complexity becomes $\mathcal{O}(d^2 + dn)$ while OFT's becomes $\mathcal{O}(d)$. As a concrete example, for a weight matrix of size $128 \times 128$, LoRA with $r' = 8$ has $2{,}048$ trainable parameters, while OFT with $r = 8$ (no block sharing) has $960$ trainable parameters—fewer than half of LoRA's count.

A practically important advantage of OFT over adapter-based methods like ControlNet is that OFT introduces no additional inference overhead. During inference, the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can be simply multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ to obtain an equivalent weight matrix $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$. The resulting model has the same architecture and inference speed as the original pretrained model.

# OFT as a Sweet Spot Between Flexibility and Regularity

The design of OFT reflects a fundamental trade-off between flexibility and regularity in finetuning. Standard finetuning is the most flexible method, as it allows every parameter to change freely. However, this flexibility comes at a cost: poor stability and susceptibility to model collapse, especially when the model is large and the training data for the downstream task is limited. The paper demonstrates this empirically, showing that DreamBooth (which uses standard finetuning) starts generating collapsed images after $2{,}000$ iterations, while OFT and COFT maintain stable and consistent control.

At the other extreme, one could imagine a very rigid finetuning method that barely changes the model at all, ensuring perfect preservation but failing to adapt to the downstream task. OFT finds a balance between these extremes by preserving the pairwise neuron angles—which are hypothesized to be crucial for representing the knowledge of neural networks—while allowing the coordinate system to rotate. This provides sufficient flexibility to modify the semantic output of the model (as required by both subject-driven and controllable generation) while maintaining the structural integrity of the pretrained knowledge.

The block-diagonal parameterization of $\bm{R}$ serves as an additional regularizer. By constraining $\bm{R}$ to be block-diagonal, we further restrict the space of possible transformations, which helps prevent overfitting to the downstream task data. The number of blocks $r$ acts as a hyperparameter that controls the flexibility-regularity trade-off: fewer blocks mean more flexibility, while more blocks mean more regularization.

The paper also provides insight into why OFT converges faster than existing methods. First, since the most effective way to modify semantics is to change neuron directions (as demonstrated by the toy autoencoder experiment), OFT is directly optimizing the most relevant parameters for semantic modification. Second, OFT can be viewed as finetuning neurons on a smooth hypersphere manifold, which yields a better optimization landscape compared to unconstrained optimization in Euclidean space. This smoother landscape leads to faster and more stable convergence.

An important distinction from prior work on orthogonal over-parameterized training is that OFT does not aim to minimize hyperspherical energy. In classification problems, minimizing hyperspherical energy is desirable because it makes neurons uniformly spaced on the hypersphere, reducing redundancy. However, in the context of finetuning, minimizing hyperspherical energy would destroy the pretrained semantic structures that we wish to preserve. Instead, OFT aims to maintain the same hyperspherical energy as the pretrained model, ensuring that the intrinsic pretrained structure is not destroyed by the finetuning process. This distinction is crucial: the goal is preservation, not optimization, of the neuron geometry.

The paper also proposes Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which adds an explicit deviation constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ to the orthogonal transformation. This constraint limits how far the orthogonal matrix can deviate from the identity, providing an additional knob to balance flexibility and regularity. With larger $\epsilon$, COFT approaches the behavior of standard OFT; with smaller $\epsilon$, COFT increasingly resembles the pretrained model. The paper finds that COFT yields even better finetuning stability than OFT due to this explicit deviation constraint, making both the learning rate and the number of training iterations easier to set without careful tuning.

# Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization

## Limitations of Standard Orthogonalization for Large Matrices

The core mechanism of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) relies on learning a layer-shared orthogonal transformation matrix $\bm{R}$ that is applied to the pretrained neuron weights. For OFT to function correctly, $\bm{R}$ must satisfy the orthogonality constraint $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$ at every step of training. A natural first instinct would be to use a standard orthogonalization procedure, such as the Gram-Schmidt process, to take an arbitrary matrix and project it onto the orthogonal group. The Gram-Schmidt process is indeed differentiable, meaning gradients can flow through it during backpropagation, which in principle makes it a candidate for use in a learning framework.

However, the paper identifies a critical practical limitation: standard orthogonalization methods like Gram-Schmidt are too computationally expensive when applied to large matrices. Text-to-image diffusion models such as Stable Diffusion contain weight matrices with large hidden dimensions $d$. Applying a full Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization at every training step—and computing gradients through it—would impose prohibitive computational overhead. The cost of such procedures scales unfavorably with the dimension $d$, making them impractical for the scale of modern diffusion models. This computational bottleneck motivates the search for a more efficient way to parameterize orthogonal matrices, one that guarantees orthogonality by construction rather than enforcing it through an expensive post-hoc projection.

## Cayley Parameterization

To overcome the computational burden of standard orthogonalization, OFT adopts the Cayley parameterization. The key idea is to construct the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ from a simpler, unconstrained matrix in a way that orthogonality is guaranteed by the mathematical structure of the construction itself. Specifically, the orthogonal matrix is formed as $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. The skew-symmetry constraint on $\bm{Q}$ is much simpler to enforce than full orthogonality: it simply requires that the matrix equals the negation of its own transpose, meaning the diagonal entries are zero and the off-diagonal entries satisfy $Q_{ij} = -Q_{ji}$.

The Cayley parameterization is a well-known construction in matrix analysis and Lie group theory. Its appeal in the OFT framework is that it provides a differentiable mapping from the space of skew-symmetric matrices to the space of orthogonal matrices. Rather than performing an expensive orthogonalization procedure on an arbitrary matrix, one simply optimizes the entries of $\bm{Q}$ (subject to the skew-symmetry constraint) and obtains an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ through the Cayley transform. This is computationally more tractable because the structure of $\bm{Q}$ is simpler, and the matrix inverse $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ can be handled through standard linear algebra routines.

To ensure that OFT starts finetuning from the pretrained model, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is initialized as the identity matrix $\bm{I}$. Under the Cayley parameterization, this is achieved by initializing $\bm{Q}$ as an all-zero matrix, since $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{0})(\bm{I}-\bm{0})^{-1}=\bm{I}$. This initialization guarantees that at the start of finetuning, the forward pass $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ reduces to $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, which is exactly the forward pass of the pretrained model.

## Properties and Limitations of the Cayley Transform

The Cayley parameterization offers a computationally efficient route to generating orthogonal matrices, but it comes with a specific mathematical limitation. The Cayley transform can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$. In group-theoretic terms, these matrices belong to the special orthogonal group, denoted $\text{SO}(d)$, which is a subgroup of the full orthogonal group $\text{O}(d)$. The full orthogonal group includes both rotations (determinant $+1$) and reflections (determinant $-1$), but the Cayley transform excludes the reflection component.

This limitation means that OFT, when using the Cayley parameterization, cannot represent all possible orthogonal transformations—only those corresponding to rotations. A reflection, which flips the orientation of the coordinate system, is not achievable. One might worry that this restriction could limit the expressiveness of OFT and harm its performance on downstream tasks.

However, the paper reports that this limitation does not affect performance in practice. The rotation-only restriction of the special orthogonal group is found to be sufficiently expressive for the finetuning tasks considered, namely subject-driven generation and controllable generation. The intuition here is that rotations provide enough flexibility to adjust neuron directions in the ways needed for these downstream tasks, and the additional freedom afforded by reflections is not necessary. This empirical finding is reassuring, as it means the computational benefits of the Cayley parameterization can be enjoyed without practical cost to task performance.

## Block-Diagonal Orthogonal Matrix Structure for Parameter Efficiency

Even with the Cayley parameterization, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can still be parameter-inefficient when the dimension $d$ is large. A full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix parameterized via the Cayley transform requires a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ of the same size, and the number of free parameters in a $d \times d$ skew-symmetric matrix is $d(d-1)/2$, resulting in a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. For large-scale diffusion models where $d$ can be hundreds or thousands, this quadratic scaling becomes a significant burden.

To address this, OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure for the orthogonal matrix. Instead of learning a single dense $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix, the matrix $\bm{R}$ is decomposed into $r$ smaller orthogonal blocks along the diagonal:

$$\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{R}_1\in \text{O}(\frac{d}{r}) & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & \bm{R}_r\in \text{O}(\frac{d}{r})\end{bmatrix}\in \text{O}(d)$$

Here, $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is the full block-diagonal orthogonal matrix, and each block $\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d/r\times d/r}$ is itself an orthogonal matrix of size $d/r \times d/r$. The key insight is that the product of block-diagonal orthogonal matrices remains orthogonal, so the overall matrix $\bm{R}$ still satisfies the orthogonality constraint $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$.

The parameter $r$ controls the trade-off between flexibility and efficiency. When $r=1$, the block-diagonal structure reduces to a single block, meaning $\bm{R}$ is a standard unconstrained $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix with no structural simplification. As $r$ increases, each block becomes smaller, the number of parameters decreases, and the transformation becomes more constrained. Each block $\bm{R}_i$ is itself parameterized using the Cayley transform, so the same orthogonality guarantees apply at the block level.

The paper notes that the block-diagonal structure has an interesting interpretation when applied to convolution layers. When the number of blocks $r$ equals the number of input channels, each block transforms a unique neuron channel, analogous to learning depth-wise convolution kernels. When all blocks are shared (i.e., $\bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j$ for all $i \neq j$), OFT applies a single orthogonal transformation across all channels.

## Parameter Complexity Analysis

The block-diagonal structure yields significant reductions in the number of trainable parameters. For a standard unconstrained $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix, the number of free parameters is $d(d-1)/2$, giving a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. When the matrix is decomposed into $r$ blocks of size $d/r \times d/r$, each block has $(d/r)(d/r - 1)/2$ free parameters, and there are $r$ such blocks, giving a total of $r \cdot (d/r)(d/r - 1)/2 = d(d/r - 1)/2$ parameters. This results in a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, a factor of $r$ reduction compared to the unconstrained case.

The parameter efficiency can be pushed even further through block sharing. If all $r$ blocks share the same orthogonal matrix (i.e., $\bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j$ for all $i \neq j$), then only one block of size $d/r \times d/r$ needs to be learned. The total number of parameters becomes $(d/r)(d/r - 1)/2$, yielding a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. This quadratic reduction in $r$ makes it possible to drastically cut the number of trainable parameters while still maintaining the orthogonality property.

To make these complexity figures concrete, the paper provides a numerical example. For a weight matrix of size $128 \times 128$, LoRA with a low-rank parameter $r'=8$ requires $2{,}048$ trainable parameters. In contrast, OFT with $r=8$ blocks (and no block sharing) requires only $960$ trainable parameters—fewer than half of LoRA's parameter count. This demonstrates that the block-diagonal structure makes OFT highly competitive in terms of parameter efficiency.

## Comparison of Parameter Efficiency Between OFT and LoRA

The paper draws a detailed comparison between OFT and LoRA, which is the most relevant existing finetuning method. LoRA assumes a low-rank structure for the additive weight update during finetuning, adding a low-rank matrix to the pretrained weights. For a low-rank parameter $r'$, LoRA introduces $r'(d+n)$ trainable parameters, where $d$ is the input dimension and $n$ is the output dimension of the weight matrix.

To compare the two methods on equal footing, the paper considers the scenario where both the block parameter $r$ in OFT and the rank parameter $r'$ in LoRA are proportional to the neuron dimension $d$, say $r = r' = \alpha d$ for some constant $0 < \alpha \leq 1$. Under this assumption, LoRA's parameter complexity becomes $\mathcal{O}(d^2 + dn)$, while OFT's parameter complexity becomes $\mathcal{O}(d)$. This reveals a fundamental difference: when the finetuning budget scales with the model dimension, OFT's parameter count grows linearly in $d$, whereas LoRA's grows quadratically (due to the $d^2$ term) plus an additional term linear in $n$.

Beyond the raw parameter counts, the paper highlights a deeper conceptual distinction between the two approaches. LoRA and OFT represent two distinct strategies for achieving parameter efficiency. LoRA exploits a low-rank structure—it constrains the weight update to lie in a low-dimensional subspace, thereby reducing the number of free parameters. OFT, on the other hand, exploits a sparsity structure through block-diagonal orthogonality—it constrains the transformation to be orthogonal and block-diagonal, which is a different kind of structural restriction. Both approaches reduce the number of trainable parameters, but they do so through fundamentally different geometric and algebraic mechanisms.

An additional connection noted in the paper is that OFT's forward pass can be rewritten as $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}=(\bm{W}^0+(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where the term $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ is analogous to LoRA's low-rank weight update. Since the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ is typically full-rank, OFT also performs a low-rank weight update when $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ is low-rank. This shows that OFT can be viewed, in a certain sense, as performing a structured low-rank update, but one that is guaranteed to preserve the pairwise angular relationships among neurons.

## Preservation of Orthogonality Under Parameter-Efficient Strategies

A critical concern when introducing parameter-efficient approximations—such as the block-diagonal structure and block sharing—is whether the fundamental guarantee of OFT is maintained. The central promise of OFT is that the hyperspherical energy of the finetuned model equals that of the pretrained model, which is achieved by applying an orthogonal transformation to the neuron weights. If the block-diagonal or block-sharing strategies were to compromise orthogonality, the entire theoretical foundation of OFT would be undermined.

The paper explicitly addresses this concern and confirms that all the parameter-efficient strategies preserve orthogonality. The block-diagonal matrix $\bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)$ is itself an orthogonal matrix because each block $\bm{R}_i$ is orthogonal, and the direct sum of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal. Similarly, when blocks are shared, the resulting matrix remains orthogonal because it is still a block-diagonal matrix with orthogonal blocks, just with repeated blocks. The Cayley parameterization guarantees that each block is orthogonal by construction, and this property propagates to the full matrix $\bm{R}$.

Therefore, despite all the strategies employed to improve parameter efficiency—Cayley parameterization, block-diagonal decomposition, and block sharing—the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal. There is no sacrifice in the preservation of hyperspherical energy. The pairwise angles between neurons in the same layer are provably preserved, and the semantic structure encoded by the pretrained model is maintained throughout finetuning. This is a key advantage of OFT: it achieves parameter efficiency through structural constraints that are compatible with the orthogonality requirement, rather than through approximations that might compromise it.

The paper also notes an important practical benefit related to inference. Unlike adapter-based methods such as ControlNet, OFT introduces no additional inference overhead. During inference, the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can be pre-multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ to obtain an equivalent weight matrix $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. This means the finetuned model has the same architecture and inference speed as the pretrained model, with no extra computational cost at test time. The parameter-efficient parameterization strategies thus not only reduce training costs but also ensure that the deployed model remains as efficient as the original.

# Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)

# Motivation: Combining Hyperspherical Energy Preservation with Deviation Constraint

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) introduces a principled approach to adapting text-to-image diffusion models by learning layer-shared orthogonal transformations that provably preserve the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained model. This preservation of pairwise neuron angles ensures that the semantic structure encoded in the pretrained model is not destroyed during finetuning. However, OFT alone, while powerful, still permits a wide range of possible orthogonal transformations. The orthogonal group is a rich and high-dimensional manifold, and even though every orthogonal matrix preserves hyperspherical energy, some orthogonal transformations may rotate the neurons so far from their original directions that the pretrained generative performance is degraded in practice. In other words, OFT guarantees a structural invariant—preserved pairwise angles—but it does not directly constrain how far individual neuron directions can move from their pretrained positions.

This observation motivates the development of Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which augments OFT with an additional explicit constraint on the deviation of the finetuned model from the pretrained model. The key insight is that effective finetuning requires balancing two competing objectives: flexibility to adapt to the downstream task, and stability to preserve the generative capabilities of the pretrained model. OFT addresses stability by preserving the hyperspherical energy, but this alone may not be sufficient. Existing finetuning methods, such as standard gradient descent with a small learning rate or LoRA with a low-rank constraint, implicitly encourage the finetuned model to remain close to the pretrained model in Euclidean distance. This implicit constraint makes intuitive sense—if the finetuned model deviates too far from the pretrained model, the rich generative knowledge acquired during pretraining is likely to be lost.

COFT makes this deviation constraint explicit rather than implicit. By constraining the orthogonal transformation matrix $\bm{R}$ to remain within a small neighborhood of the identity matrix, COFT ensures that the finetuned model does not stray too far from the pretrained model while still retaining the ability to adapt. COFT can thus be understood as a combination of two explicit constraints working in tandem: the hyperspherical energy preservation constraint, inherited from OFT, which ensures that the pairwise relational structure among neurons is maintained, and the deviation constraint, which ensures that the overall transformation remains close to the identity. The first constraint controls the structural relationships, while the second controls the magnitude of the transformation. Together, they provide a principled and robust framework for finetuning that achieves an effective trade-off between flexibility and regularity.

The motivation for making the deviation constraint explicit, rather than relying on the implicit encouragement provided by a small learning rate, is rooted in the observation that implicit constraints are unreliable. A small learning rate does not guarantee that the model remains close to the pretrained model after many iterations of training, especially when the loss landscape is complex. By contrast, COFT enforces the deviation constraint as a hard constraint, guaranteeing that the finetuned model remains within a specified neighborhood of the pretrained model regardless of the number of training iterations or the learning rate used. This leads to significantly improved finetuning stability, as the model is provably prevented from collapsing or drifting too far from its pretrained state.

# Mathematical Formulation of COFT with the $\epsilon$-Deviation Constraint

COFT modifies the forward pass of OFT by adding an additional constraint on the orthogonal transformation matrix. Recall that in OFT, the forward pass for a fully connected layer with pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ is given by $\bm{z} = (\bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix satisfying $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R} \bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$, and $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the input vector. COFT retains this forward pass but introduces an additional constraint:

$$
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}, \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R} \bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}, \quad \|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon
$$

Here, $\|\cdot\|$ denotes a matrix norm (typically the operator norm or Frobenius norm), $\bm{I}$ is the identity matrix of appropriate dimension, and $\epsilon > 0$ is a hyperparameter that controls the maximum allowed deviation of the orthogonal transformation from the identity. The first constraint, $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R} \bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$, ensures that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, which guarantees the preservation of hyperspherical energy as in OFT. The second constraint, $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$, ensures that the orthogonal transformation does not deviate too far from the identity transformation.

The deviation constraint has a clear geometric interpretation. The identity matrix $\bm{I}$ corresponds to no transformation at all—applying $\bm{I}$ to the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ leaves them unchanged. The constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ defines a ball of radius $\epsilon$ centered at the identity matrix in the space of orthogonal matrices. The finetuning process is restricted to searching for an optimal orthogonal transformation within this ball. When $\epsilon$ is small, the search space is tightly constrained around the identity, meaning the finetuned model will be very close to the pretrained model. When $\epsilon$ is large, the constraint becomes loose, and COFT approaches the behavior of unconstrained OFT, allowing the orthogonal transformation to take any value in the orthogonal group.

It is important to emphasize that the deviation constraint operates on the transformation matrix $\bm{R}$, not directly on the weight matrix $\bm{W}$. This is a subtle but crucial distinction. The weight matrix $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$ is a product of the orthogonal transformation and the pretrained weights. Even if $\bm{R}$ is close to the identity, the effect on $\bm{W}$ depends on the structure of $\bm{W}^0$. However, because $\bm{R}$ is close to $\bm{I}$, the product $\bm{R} \bm{W}^0$ will be close to $\bm{W}^0$ in an appropriate sense. Specifically, $\|\bm{W} - \bm{W}^0\| = \|(\bm{R} - \bm{I}) \bm{W}^0\| \leq \|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \cdot \|\bm{W}^0\| \leq \epsilon \cdot \|\bm{W}^0\|$, which shows that the deviation of the finetuned weights from the pretrained weights is controlled by $\epsilon$ and the norm of the pretrained weights.

# Neumann Series Approximation of the Cayley Transform

To enforce the orthogonality constraint on $\bm{R}$, COFT adopts the Cayley parameterization, which constructs an orthogonal matrix from a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$. The Cayley transform is defined as:

$$
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}
$$

where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. This parameterization guarantees that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal for any skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$, as long as $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$ is invertible. The Cayley transform is a well-known mapping between skew-symmetric matrices and orthogonal matrices, and it produces orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$ (i.e., rotation matrices belonging to the special orthogonal group).

While the Cayley parameterization elegantly resolves the orthogonality constraint, it introduces a new challenge: the deviation constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ is expressed in terms of $\bm{R}$, but the learnable parameters are in $\bm{Q}$. Directly enforcing a constraint on $\bm{R}$ through the Cayley transform is nontrivial because the relationship between $\bm{R}$ and $\bm{Q}$ involves a matrix inverse, which complicates the analysis. To address this, COFT employs the Neumann series approximation to establish a simpler relationship between $\bm{R}$ and $\bm{Q}$.

The Neumann series is the matrix analogue of the geometric series. For a matrix $\bm{A}$ whose spectral radius (the largest absolute value of its eigenvalues) is less than $1$, the inverse $(\bm{I} - \bm{A})^{-1}$ can be expressed as the convergent series:

$$
(\bm{I} - \bm{A})^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \bm{A}^k = \bm{I} + \bm{A} + \bm{A}^2 + \bm{A}^3 + \cdots
$$

Applying this to the Cayley transform with $\bm{A} = \bm{Q}$ (under the assumption that the Neumann series converges in the operator norm, which requires the spectral radius of $\bm{Q}$ to be less than $1$), we obtain:

$$
(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} = \bm{I} + \bm{Q} + \bm{Q}^2 + \bm{Q}^3 + \cdots
$$

Substituting this into the Cayley transform:

$$
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q} + \bm{Q}^2 + \cdots) = \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + 2\bm{Q}^2 + \cdots
$$

Keeping only the first-order terms in $\bm{Q}$ (i.e., truncating at the linear approximation), we get:

$$
\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)
$$

This approximation reveals that, to first order, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ deviates from the identity by approximately $2\bm{Q}$. The higher-order terms, denoted by $\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$, become negligible when $\bm{Q}$ is small, which is precisely the regime of interest for COFT since the deviation constraint is designed to keep the transformation close to the identity. This linear approximation is the key insight that allows us to translate the deviation constraint from the space of orthogonal matrices to the space of skew-symmetric matrices, where it can be more easily enforced during optimization.

# Translating the Constraint on $\bm{R}$ to a Constraint on $\bm{Q}$

The Neumann series approximation $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$ provides the crucial link needed to translate the deviation constraint on $\bm{R}$ into a constraint on $\bm{Q}$. Substituting the approximation into the deviation constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$, we obtain:

$$
\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| = \|2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)\| \approx 2\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon
$$

where the approximation holds when $\bm{Q}$ is sufficiently small, so that the higher-order terms $\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$ are negligible compared to the linear term $2\bm{Q}$. This yields the equivalent constraint on $\bm{Q}$:

$$
\|\bm{Q} - \bm{0}\| \leq \epsilon'
$$

where $\bm{0}$ denotes the all-zero matrix and $\epsilon'$ is a new error hyperparameter related to $\epsilon$ by $\epsilon' \approx \epsilon / 2$ (up to the approximation error from the higher-order terms). In practice, $\epsilon'$ is treated as a tunable hyperparameter, and the exact relationship between $\epsilon$ and $\epsilon'$ depends on the norm used and the magnitude of $\bm{Q}$.

This translation is significant because it converts a constraint on the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, which is difficult to enforce directly due to the nonlinear Cayley transform, into a simple norm constraint on the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$, which is the actual learnable parameter. The constraint $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'$ defines a ball of radius $\epsilon'$ centered at the origin in the space of skew-symmetric matrices. This is a convex constraint, which makes it particularly amenable to standard constrained optimization techniques.

The intuition behind this translation is clear. The skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ parameterizes the "rotation" applied by the orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$. When $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$, we have $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, corresponding to no rotation at all. As $\bm{Q}$ grows in magnitude, the rotation applied by $\bm{R}$ becomes larger. By constraining $\bm{Q}$ to remain within a ball of radius $\epsilon'$, we are effectively limiting the magnitude of the rotation, ensuring that the finetuned model does not deviate too far from the pretrained model. The approximation $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q}$ tells us that the deviation of $\bm{R}$ from the identity is roughly proportional to the magnitude of $\bm{Q}$, so constraining $\bm{Q}$ directly controls the deviation.

It is worth noting that this translation relies on the validity of the Neumann series approximation, which requires the spectral radius of $\bm{Q}$ to be less than $1$. In practice, since COFT is designed to keep $\bm{Q}$ small (via the deviation constraint), this condition is typically satisfied, and the approximation is accurate. For larger values of $\bm{Q}$, the higher-order terms in the Neumann series become more significant, and the relationship between $\epsilon$ and $\epsilon'$ becomes less precise. However, in the regime where COFT is intended to operate—namely, small deviations from the pretrained model—the approximation is reliable and the translated constraint is an effective proxy for the original deviation constraint on $\bm{R}$.

# Projected Gradient Descent for Enforcing the Constraint

Once the deviation constraint has been translated to $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'$ on the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$, the next challenge is to enforce this constraint during training. The paper states that this constraint "can be easily enforced by projected gradient descent." Projected gradient descent is a standard technique in constrained optimization that extends ordinary gradient descent to handle constraints by projecting the updated parameters back onto the feasible set after each gradient step.

The procedure works as follows. At each training step $t$, the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ is updated using a standard gradient step based on the loss function $\mathcal{L}$:

$$
\bm{Q}^{(t+1/2)} = \bm{Q}^{(t)} - \eta \nabla_{\bm{Q}} \mathcal{L}
$$

where $\eta$ is the learning rate and $\nabla_{\bm{Q}} \mathcal{L}$ is the gradient of the loss with respect to $\bm{Q}$. This intermediate update $\bm{Q}^{(t+1/2)}$ may violate the constraint $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'$, since the gradient step could move $\bm{Q}$ outside the feasible ball. To enforce the constraint, a projection step is applied:

$$
\bm{Q}^{(t+1)} = \text{Proj}_{\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'} \left( \bm{Q}^{(t+1/2)} \right)
$$

where $\text{Proj}_{\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'}(\cdot)$ denotes the projection onto the ball of radius $\epsilon'$ centered at the origin. The projection is straightforward to compute. If $\|\bm{Q}^{(t+1/2)}\| \leq \epsilon'$, then the intermediate update already satisfies the constraint, and no projection is needed: $\bm{Q}^{(t+1)} = \bm{Q}^{(t+1/2)}$. If $\|\bm{Q}^{(t+1/2)}\| > \epsilon'$, then the update is scaled back to lie on the boundary of the ball:

$$
\bm{Q}^{(t+1)} = \frac{\epsilon'}{\|\bm{Q}^{(t+1/2)}\|} \cdot \bm{Q}^{(t+1/2)}
$$

This projection ensures that $\bm{Q}^{(t+1)}$ always lies within (or on the boundary of) the feasible set. The projection onto a ball is one of the simplest and most computationally efficient projections, requiring only a matrix norm computation and, if necessary, a scalar multiplication. This makes the enforcement of the deviation constraint computationally cheap, adding negligible overhead to the training process.

An important aspect of this approach is that the skew-symmetry of $\bm{Q}$ must also be maintained during training. Since the Cayley transform requires $\bm{Q}$ to be skew-symmetric, the gradient updates must preserve this property. In practice, this can be handled by parameterizing $\bm{Q}$ in terms of its upper-triangular elements (which fully determine a skew-symmetric matrix) and performing gradient updates only on these free parameters. Alternatively, the gradient can be projected onto the space of skew-symmetric matrices at each step, which involves symmetrizing the gradient and negating it: the skew-symmetric projection of a matrix $\bm{G}$ is $(\bm{G} - \bm{G}^\top) / 2$. Both approaches ensure that $\bm{Q}$ remains skew-symmetric throughout training.

The combination of gradient descent, projection onto the skew-symmetric subspace, and projection onto the $\epsilon'$-ball constitutes the complete optimization procedure for COFT. This procedure is simple, efficient, and provably maintains both the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ (through the Cayley parameterization) and the deviation constraint (through the projected gradient descent on $\bm{Q}$). The result is a finetuning method that is both principled and practical, with strong stability guarantees arising from the explicit constraints.

# Effect of $\epsilon$ on Finetuning Flexibility and Stability

The hyperparameter $\epsilon$ (or equivalently $\epsilon'$, the translated constraint on $\bm{Q}$) plays a central role in COFT, directly governing the trade-off between finetuning flexibility and stability. Understanding the effect of $\epsilon$ is crucial for effectively applying COFT in practice, as it determines how much the finetuned model is allowed to deviate from the pretrained model.

When $\epsilon$ is large, the deviation constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ becomes loose, allowing the orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ to take values far from the identity matrix. In this regime, COFT behaves similarly to unconstrained OFT, as the deviation constraint is effectively inactive. The finetuning process has maximum flexibility to adapt the model to the downstream task, but the risk of degrading the pretrained generative performance increases. The model can undergo large rotations of the neuron directions, which, while preserving the pairwise angles, may move the neurons far from their pretrained positions and disrupt the delicate balance of features learned during pretraining.

When $\epsilon$ is small, the deviation constraint becomes tight, restricting $\bm{R}$ to remain close to the identity matrix. In this regime, the finetuned model remains very close to the pretrained model, and the risk of degrading pretrained performance is minimized. However, the flexibility to adapt to the downstream task is also reduced, as the orthogonal transformation is only allowed to make small adjustments to the neuron directions. If $\epsilon$ is too small, the model may not have sufficient capacity to learn the downstream task effectively.

The paper provides empirical evidence for this trade-off. With larger $\epsilon$, the COFT-finetuned model resembles the OFT-finetuned model, achieving the full flexibility of OFT but potentially at the cost of stability. With smaller $\epsilon$, the COFT-finetuned model behaves increasingly similar to the pretrained text-to-image diffusion model, prioritizing stability over task-specific adaptation. The paper demonstrates that COFT with an appropriate $\epsilon$ yields even better finetuning stability than OFT, validating the importance of the explicit deviation constraint.

A particularly notable practical benefit of COFT, as reported in the paper, is that with a properly chosen $\epsilon$, both the learning rate and the number of training iterations become effortless to set. This is because the deviation constraint provides a hard bound on how far the model can deviate from the pretrained model, regardless of the learning rate or the number of iterations. In standard finetuning, one must carefully tune the learning rate and the number of iterations to prevent the model from overfitting or collapsing. With COFT, the deviation constraint acts as a safety net, preventing model collapse even if the learning rate is too large or the training runs for too many iterations. This makes COFT significantly easier to use in practice, as it reduces the sensitivity of the finetuning process to hyperparameter choices. The insensitivity to the number of finetuning iterations is especially important, as it alleviates the need to tune the iteration count for different subjects or tasks—a common practical challenge in finetuning text-to-image diffusion models.

# Zero-Initialization of $\bm{Q}$ for Identity Initialization of $\bm{R}$

A critical requirement for any finetuning method is that the finetuned model must start from the pretrained model at the beginning of training. This ensures that the finetuning process builds upon the knowledge already encoded in the pretrained model, rather than starting from a random or arbitrary state. In OFT and COFT, this requirement translates to initializing the orthogonal transformation matrix $\bm{R}$ to be the identity matrix $\bm{I}$, so that the initial forward pass is $\bm{z} = (\bm{I} \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, which is exactly the forward pass of the pretrained model.

The Cayley parameterization provides a natural way to achieve this identity initialization. Recall that the Cayley transform relates the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ via:

$$
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}
$$

When $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$ (the all-zero matrix), this expression becomes:

$$
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{0})(\bm{I} - \bm{0})^{-1} = \bm{I} \cdot \bm{I}^{-1} = \bm{I}
$$

Thus, initializing $\bm{Q}$ as the all-zero matrix automatically yields $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ at the start of training, ensuring that the finetuned model begins from exactly the pretrained weights. This is analogous to the zero initialization strategy used in LoRA, where the low-rank update is initialized to zero so that the finetuned model starts from the pretrained weights. The zero initialization of $\bm{Q}$ serves the same purpose in COFT: it guarantees that no transformation is applied initially, and the model begins in the pretrained state.

The zero initialization also has an important connection to the deviation constraint. At initialization, $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$ satisfies the constraint $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'$ trivially, since $\|\bm{0}\| = 0 \leq \epsilon'$ for any positive $\epsilon'$. This means that the initial point is always feasible, regardless of the choice of $\epsilon'$. As training progresses, the gradient updates push $\bm{Q}$ away from zero, allowing the orthogonal transformation to deviate from the identity. The deviation constraint $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'$ ensures that this deviation remains bounded, preventing the model from straying too far from the pretrained state. The interplay between the gradient-driven exploration and the constraint-driven regularization is what gives COFT its characteristic balance of flexibility and stability.

From an optimization perspective, the zero initialization of $\bm{Q}$ means that the initial gradient of the loss with respect to $\bm{Q}$ determines the initial direction of movement on the manifold of orthogonal transformations. Since $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, the initial gradient (after projection onto the skew-symmetric subspace) encodes the direction in which the neuron directions should be rotated to best adapt to the downstream task. The deviation constraint then limits how far this rotation can proceed, ensuring that the adaptation remains within a controlled neighborhood of the pretrained model. This controlled adaptation is the essence of COFT: the model is free to explore the neighborhood of the pretrained model, but it cannot escape the bounds set by the deviation constraint.

# Re-scaled Orthogonal Finetuning and Extensions

# Motivation for Re-scaled OFT: Magnitude Does Not Affect Hyperspherical Energy

The original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) framework is built upon the central idea of preserving the pairwise angles between neurons in a layer, as these angular relationships are what encode the semantic information learned during pretraining. The hyperspherical energy, which sums the inverse pairwise distances between normalized neurons on the unit hypersphere, depends exclusively on the directions of the neurons and is completely invariant to their magnitudes. This is because the definition of hyperspherical energy uses normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$, which project each neuron onto the unit hypersphere regardless of its original length. Consequently, scaling any neuron by a positive scalar does not alter its normalized direction and therefore does not change the hyperspherical energy of the layer.

This observation opens up an important opportunity. While OFT strictly preserves the angular structure by applying orthogonal transformations, it deliberately leaves the magnitudes of the neurons untouched. However, the magnitudes of neurons can carry useful information for downstream tasks. For instance, in a trained neural network, the magnitude of a neuron often controls the strength or prominence of the feature it detects. When finetuning for a new task, it may be beneficial to amplify certain features while suppressing others, and adjusting neuron magnitudes provides a natural mechanism for doing so. The key insight is that this adjustment can be made without any risk of disrupting the angular relationships that OFT is designed to protect.

This motivates the re-scaled extension of OFT. Because rescaling neurons does not affect hyperspherical energy, we can safely introduce learnable magnitude scaling coefficients alongside the orthogonal transformation. The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ continues to handle the angular adjustments needed for the downstream task, while a separate set of parameters adjusts the magnitudes. The two components operate in complementary regimes: the orthogonal transformation modifies directions on the hypersphere, and the magnitude scaling modifies radii. Importantly, the magnitude scaling does not compromise the hyperspherical energy preservation guarantee that is the hallmark of OFT. This means that re-scaled OFT inherits all the stability and generalization preservation properties of standard OFT while gaining additional expressive power.

The motivation also connects back to the toy autoencoder experiment discussed in the paper, which demonstrated that angular information of neurons is primarily responsible for storing semantic content, while magnitude information alone contains little useful information for image reconstruction. This finding justifies why OFT focuses on angular preservation as its primary mechanism. However, the re-scaled extension recognizes that while magnitude may not carry primary semantic content, it can still play a supporting role in fine-tuning by modulating the influence of different neurons. The combination of angular transformation via orthogonal matrices and magnitude adjustment via diagonal scaling thus provides a more complete toolkit for adapting a pretrained model to a new task.

# Mathematical Formulation: The Re-scaled OFT Forward Pass

The mathematical formulation of re-scaled OFT extends the original OFT forward pass in a clean and modular way. Recall that in standard OFT, the forward pass for a fully connected layer with pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ is given by $\bm{z} = (\bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is a learnable orthogonal matrix satisfying $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R} \bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$, and $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the input vector. The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is initialized as the identity matrix so that finetuning begins from the pretrained model.

In re-scaled OFT, we introduce an additional learnable diagonal matrix $\bm{D} = \text{diag}(s_1, \cdots, s_n) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, where each diagonal element $s_1, \cdots, s_n$ is constrained to be positive. The forward pass becomes:

$$\bm{z} = (\bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0 \cdot \bm{D})^\top \bm{x}$$

Here, $\bm{R}$ operates on the left side of $\bm{W}^0$, transforming the $d$-dimensional input space, while $\bm{D}$ operates on the right side, scaling the $n$ output neurons individually. The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is responsible for rotating or reflecting the neuron directions, preserving all pairwise angles. The diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$ independently scales each neuron's magnitude, allowing the model to adjust the relative importance of different output features.

It is worth noting the order of operations carefully. The paper includes an errata noting that in the NeurIPS camera-ready version, the forward pass was mistakenly written as $\bm{z} = (\bm{D} \bm{R} \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, but the correct formulation, matching the original implementation, is $\bm{z} = (\bm{R} \bm{W}^0 \bm{D})^\top \bm{x}$. In this correct formulation, the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ is first transformed by the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ in the input dimension, and then the result is scaled by $\bm{D}$ in the output dimension. This ordering ensures that the angular structure of the transformed neurons $\bm{R} \bm{W}^0$ is preserved before the magnitude scaling is applied.

Both $\bm{R}$ and $\bm{D}$ are learnable, in contrast to standard OFT where only $\bm{R}$ is learnable. The diagonal elements $s_i$ are constrained to be positive to ensure that the scaling does not flip the sign of any neuron, which would effectively be equivalent to a reflection and could interfere with the angular structure managed by $\bm{R}$. The identity initialization for $\bm{R}$ is maintained, and $\bm{D}$ is initialized to the identity matrix (i.e., $s_i = 1$ for all $i$) so that the re-scaled OFT also starts finetuning from the pretrained model exactly.

The mathematical elegance of this formulation lies in its clean separation of concerns. The orthogonal matrix handles direction, and the diagonal matrix handles magnitude. Because the hyperspherical energy is computed on normalized neurons, the introduction of $\bm{D}$ has zero effect on the hyperspherical energy difference between the finetuned and pretrained models. The preservation guarantee $\|\text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\| = 0$ still holds, where $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0 \bm{D}$, because the normalization in the hyperspherical energy computation cancels out the scaling factors from $\bm{D}$.

# Additional Flexibility with Negligible Parameter Overhead

One of the most attractive features of re-scaled OFT is that the additional flexibility it provides comes at a negligible cost in terms of trainable parameters. The diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$ introduces exactly $n$ additional parameters for a layer with $n$ output neurons, which is trivially small compared to the number of parameters in the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ or the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. For context, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ with block-diagonal structure using $r$ blocks requires $d(d/r - 1)/2$ parameters, which is $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ in complexity. The diagonal matrix adds only $\mathcal{O}(n)$ parameters, which is a lower-order term.

To make this concrete with the example from the paper, consider a weight matrix of size $128 \times 128$. Standard OFT with $r = 8$ blocks and no block sharing requires $960$ trainable parameters for the orthogonal matrix. The re-scaled extension would add only $128$ additional parameters for the diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$, bringing the total to $1,088$. This represents roughly a $13\%$ increase in parameter count, which is remarkably small given that the diagonal matrix provides an entirely new dimension of control over the finetuned model's behavior.

The paper reports that re-scaled OFT is generally better than standard OFT, though the main experiments in the paper stick to standard OFT to demonstrate the effectiveness of orthogonal transformation alone. This is an important methodological choice: by showing that OFT alone, without any magnitude adjustment, already outperforms existing methods, the paper establishes that the angular preservation mechanism is the primary driver of OFT's success. The re-scaled extension then serves as a further improvement that can be adopted in practice.

The negligible parameter overhead also means that re-scaled OFT maintains the parameter efficiency that is one of the key advantages of OFT over competing methods. The paper emphasizes that OFT achieves better or comparable parameter efficiency to LoRA, which is one of the most parameter-efficient finetuning methods. The addition of $n$ parameters for the diagonal matrix does not change this comparative advantage in any meaningful way. Moreover, like standard OFT, re-scaled OFT introduces no additional inference overhead: at test time, the learned matrices $\bm{R}$ and $\bm{D}$ can be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ to obtain an equivalent weight matrix $\bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0 \bm{D}$, so the inference speed is identical to that of the pretrained model.

# Architecture Agnosticism: Applying OFT to Transformers and CNNs

A key property of OFT that makes it broadly applicable is its architecture-agnostic nature. The core principle of OFT—learning a layer-shared orthogonal transformation to preserve pairwise neuron angles—does not depend on any specific architectural assumptions. In principle, OFT can be applied to any type of neural network layer where neuron weights can be organized into a matrix and transformed by an orthogonal matrix. This includes fully connected layers, attention layers in Transformers, and convolutional layers in CNNs.

For Transformer architectures, LoRA is typically applied to the attention weights, which are the key, query, and value projection matrices. To ensure a fair comparison with LoRA, the paper applies OFT only to the same attention layers where LoRA is used. The attention mechanism computes queries, keys, and values through linear projections of the input, and these projections are represented as matrix multiplications. OFT can be directly applied to these projection matrices by learning an orthogonal transformation for each. The orthogonal matrix transforms the input space of each projection, adjusting the directions of the projection neurons while preserving their pairwise angles. This is particularly relevant for attention layers, where the relationships between different attention heads and their projections are important for maintaining the model's ability to attend to relevant features.

The application to Transformers also highlights an interesting connection between OFT and the structure of attention. In multi-head attention, each head has its own set of projection matrices, and the outputs of different heads are concatenated and projected. The pairwise angles between neurons in these projection matrices encode the diversity and complementarity of different attention heads. By preserving these angles, OFT ensures that the attention mechanism retains its pretrained ability to capture diverse features while allowing the model to adjust to new tasks through controlled angular transformations.

For convolutional neural networks, OFT is equally applicable and in some ways even more natural, as the block-diagonal structure of the orthogonal matrix has interesting interpretations in the context of convolution operations. The paper notes that OFT is well suited for finetuning convolution layers, and the block-diagonal parameterization provides a meaningful decomposition of the transformation that aligns with the structure of convolutional kernels. The paper also mentions a preliminary study on finetuning convolution layers with OFT, suggesting that this is a promising direction for future exploration.

The architecture-agnostic property of OFT is significant because text-to-image diffusion models, the primary focus of the paper, often incorporate both Transformer-based components (such as cross-attention layers for text conditioning) and convolutional components (such as the U-Net architecture used in Stable Diffusion). The ability to apply OFT uniformly across these different architectural components makes it a versatile finetuning tool that does not require task-specific or architecture-specific modifications.

# OFT for Convolution Layers: Block-Diagonal Structure as Depth-wise Transformations

The application of OFT to convolutional layers reveals one of the most interesting structural properties of the method. The block-diagonal parameterization of the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, which was introduced primarily for parameter efficiency, takes on a meaningful interpretation when applied to convolution operations. This interpretation provides additional insight into why the block-diagonal structure is not merely a computational convenience but also a conceptually motivated design choice.

In a convolutional layer, the weight tensor has shape $n \times c \times k \times k$, where $n$ is the number of output channels, $c$ is the number of input channels, and $k \times k$ is the kernel size. Each output channel can be thought of as a neuron, and the weights connecting input channels to a given output channel form a vector in a $c \cdot k^2$-dimensional space. When we apply OFT with a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix, the structure of the blocks can be aligned with the channel structure of the convolution.

The paper describes two interesting configurations. First, when the number of blocks $r$ is set equal to the number of input channels $c$, each block in the diagonal matrix corresponds to a unique input channel. In this case, each block transforms only the weights associated with a single input channel, which is analogous to learning a depth-wise convolution. Depth-wise convolutions, as used in architectures like Xception, apply a separate filter to each input channel independently, and the block-diagonal OFT with per-channel blocks performs a similar function: it independently rotates or reflects the neuron weights for each input channel. This configuration is particularly interpretable because each input channel's contribution to the output is adjusted independently, and the orthogonal transformation ensures that the angular relationships within each channel's weights are preserved.

Second, when all blocks in $\bm{R}$ are shared (i.e., $\bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j$ for all $i \neq j$), the same orthogonal transformation is applied across all channels. This configuration transforms the neurons with an orthogonal matrix that is shared across channels, providing a unified rotation or reflection of the entire weight space. This is analogous to a global coordinate system adjustment that applies uniformly across all input channels, which can be useful when the downstream task requires a consistent transformation of the feature space regardless of which channel the features come from.

The contrast between these two configurations highlights a trade-off between specialization and generality. Per-channel blocks (no sharing) provide more specialized transformations that can adapt each input channel independently, at the cost of more parameters. Full sharing provides a single, general transformation that is more parameter-efficient but less flexible. Intermediate configurations, where some but not all blocks are shared, offer a continuum between these two extremes. The paper notes that unlike LoRA, which does not have a natural interpretation for convolutional layers, the block-diagonal structure of OFT's orthogonal matrix maps naturally onto the channel structure of convolutions, making OFT particularly well-suited for convolutional finetuning.

This structural alignment is not accidental. The block-diagonal matrix $\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \cdots, \bm{R}_r)$ partitions the input dimension $d$ into $r$ groups and applies an independent orthogonal transformation to each group. In the context of convolution, the input dimension corresponds to the flattened kernel weights across all input channels, and partitioning by channels is a natural choice that respects the structure of the convolution operation. The orthogonality of each block ensures that within each channel's weight subspace, the pairwise angles between neuron weights are preserved, maintaining the semantic relationships encoded in the pretrained convolutional kernels.

# Block Sharing Across Channels for Parameter Reduction

The parameter efficiency of OFT is a critical practical concern, especially when finetuning large models with high-dimensional layers. The block-diagonal parameterization of the orthogonal matrix is the primary mechanism for reducing the number of trainable parameters, and block sharing provides an additional layer of efficiency. Understanding both strategies and their trade-offs is essential for effectively applying OFT in practice.

Without any block sharing, the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \cdots, \bm{R}_r)$ with $r$ blocks of size $d/r \times d/r$ requires $r \cdot \frac{(d/r)(d/r - 1)}{2} = \frac{d(d/r - 1)}{2}$ parameters, giving a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. This is already a significant reduction from the full orthogonal matrix, which requires $d(d-1)/2$ parameters with complexity $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. When $r = 1$, the block-diagonal matrix reduces to a single full orthogonal matrix, which is the most flexible but also the most parameter-intensive configuration.

Block sharing further reduces the parameter count by using the same block matrix across multiple positions in the diagonal. Specifically, if we set $\bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j$ for all $i \neq j$, then all $r$ blocks are identical, and we only need to learn a single block $\bm{R}_1$ of size $d/r \times d/r$. The number of parameters becomes $\frac{(d/r)(d/r - 1)}{2}$, giving a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. This is a dramatic reduction, especially when $r$ is large. For example, with $d = 128$ and $r = 8$, the unshared block-diagonal configuration requires $960$ parameters, while the fully shared configuration requires only $120$ parameters.

The key property that makes block sharing viable is that the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal regardless of whether blocks are shared. Each block is individually orthogonal, and the block-diagonal composition of orthogonal matrices is itself orthogonal. Therefore, block sharing does not compromise the hyperspherical energy preservation guarantee. The trade-off is in flexibility: sharing blocks reduces the degrees of freedom available for the orthogonal transformation, which may limit the model's ability to adapt to certain downstream tasks. However, the paper's results suggest that even with significant parameter reduction through block sharing, OFT maintains strong performance, indicating that the angular structure of pretrained models can be effectively adjusted with relatively few parameters.

The paper also draws an interesting comparison between the parameter efficiency strategies of OFT and LoRA. LoRA achieves parameter efficiency by exploiting a low-rank structure for the additive weight update, assuming that the change in weights during finetuning can be well-approximated by a low-rank matrix. OFT takes a fundamentally different route by exploiting the sparsity structure of block-diagonal orthogonality. While LoRA's low-rank assumption may not always hold—especially for tasks that require substantial changes to the pretrained weights—OFT's block-diagonal structure provides a different inductive bias that controls the complexity of the transformation through the number and sharing of blocks. The two methods thus represent distinct philosophical approaches to parameter-efficient finetuning: LoRA constrains the rank of the update, while OFT constrains the structure of the transformation.

It is also worth emphasizing that the parameter reduction strategies of OFT—block-diagonal structure and block sharing—are orthogonal to the re-scaled extension. One can combine re-scaled OFT with any block-diagonal configuration and any level of block sharing. The diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$ adds $n$ parameters regardless of the block structure, and its effect on the total parameter count remains negligible in all configurations. This composability makes OFT a highly flexible framework that can be adapted to different computational budgets and task requirements.

# Insights: Connections to LoRA, Convergence, and Design Principles

# Rewriting OFT as a Low-Rank Weight Update Analogous to LoRA

To understand the relationship between Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), it is instructive to rewrite OFT's forward pass in a form that makes the comparison with LoRA more transparent. Recall that OFT modifies the forward pass of a pretrained layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by applying a learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, so that the finetuned weight becomes $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. The forward pass is then $\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top\bm{x} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$. The key insight comes from rewriting this expression by decomposing the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ into the identity matrix and a residual term: $\bm{R} = \bm{I} + (\bm{R} - \bm{I})$. Substituting this decomposition into the forward pass yields $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$. Here, $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ plays a role analogous to LoRA's low-rank weight update. In LoRA, the weight update takes the form $\Delta\bm{W} = \bm{A}\bm{B}$ where $\bm{A}$ and $\bm{B}$ are low-rank matrices, and the finetuned weight is $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$. In OFT, the effective weight update is $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0$, where the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ is multiplied by the deviation of the orthogonal matrix from identity.

This reformulation reveals a deeper structural parallel between the two methods. Both approaches add a correction term to the pretrained weights rather than replacing them entirely, and both seek to control the magnitude or structure of this correction to preserve pretrained knowledge. The distinction lies in how the correction is parameterized and constrained. LoRA directly parameterizes the update as a low-rank product $\bm{A}\bm{B}$, which by construction limits the rank of the weight change. OFT, by contrast, parameterizes the update through an orthogonal transformation that deviates from the identity, and the rank properties of the update $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ depend on the structure of $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$. When $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$ is low-rank (for instance, when the block-diagonal parameterization is used with a small number of blocks), OFT also effectively performs a low-rank weight update. Since $\bm{W}^0$ is typically full-rank, the rank of the product $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ is bounded by the rank of $\bm{R} - \bm{I}$, meaning that OFT can achieve similar parameter efficiency to LoRA through this mechanism.

The analogy becomes even more precise when we consider the role of the rank parameter. LoRA has a rank parameter $r'$ that controls the expressiveness of the weight update, with larger $r'$ permitting more expressive updates at the cost of more trainable parameters. OFT has a diagonal block parameter $r$ that serves a similar purpose: with $r = 1$, the orthogonal matrix is unconstrained and fully expressive, while increasing $r$ constrains the structure of $\bm{R}$ and reduces the number of trainable parameters. In both cases, the parameter trades off flexibility against parameter efficiency, though the nature of the constraint differs fundamentally between the two approaches.

# LoRA Exploits Low-Rank Structure vs. OFT Exploits Sparsity (Block-Diagonal) Structure

Having established the formal analogy between OFT and LoRA, we can now articulate the deeper conceptual distinction: the two methods achieve parameter efficiency through fundamentally different structural assumptions about the weight update. LoRA exploits low-rank structure to reduce the number of trainable parameters. The assumption underlying LoRA is that the weight update $\Delta\bm{W}$ during finetuning can be well-approximated by a low-rank matrix, meaning that the update lies in a low-dimensional subspace. This is a direct constraint on the content of the update: the rank of $\Delta\bm{W}$ is at most $r'$, regardless of the structure of the pretrained weights. The low-rank assumption is motivated by the empirical observation that the changes needed for downstream adaptation often reside in a small number of directions in weight space.

OFT, on the other hand, takes a fundamentally different route to parameter efficiency. Rather than constraining the rank of the weight update, OFT constrains the weight update to be an orthogonal transformation of the pretrained weights, and then exploits the sparsity structure of block-diagonal orthogonality to reduce parameters. The block-diagonal structure means that the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is not a fully dense $d \times d$ matrix but rather a collection of $r$ smaller orthogonal matrices $\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \ldots, \bm{R}_r$ arranged along the diagonal, each of size $\frac{d}{r} \times \frac{d}{r}$. This is a sparsity constraint on the transformation itself: the off-diagonal blocks are constrained to be zero, meaning that certain dimensions are never mixed with others during the transformation. The key difference is that LoRA constrains the dimensionality of the update subspace, while OFT constrains the connectivity pattern of the transformation.

This distinction has practical consequences. In LoRA, the low-rank constraint means that the update can only move the pretrained weights within a low-dimensional subspace, which may be too restrictive for certain tasks that require changes spanning many directions. In OFT, the block-diagonal orthogonal transformation preserves all pairwise angles within each block, so the relational structure among neurons within a block is maintained while the relationships between blocks are fixed (since the off-diagonal elements are zero). The sparsity structure of OFT can also have interesting interpretations in specific architectures. For convolution layers, when the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, each block transforms a unique neuron channel, analogous to learning depth-wise convolution kernels. When all blocks are shared, OFT applies a single orthogonal transformation across all channels. This architectural interpretability is a unique advantage of OFT's sparsity-based approach, as the block-diagonal structure maps naturally onto the channel structure of convolution operations, a correspondence that has no direct analogue in LoRA's low-rank parameterization.

From a parameter complexity perspective, the two approaches scale differently with the dimensions of the weight matrix. For a weight matrix of size $d \times n$, LoRA with rank $r'$ requires $r'(d + n)$ trainable parameters. OFT with $r$ blocks (without block sharing) requires $\frac{d(d/r - 1)}{2}$ parameters, which simplifies to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. When block sharing is applied, meaning all blocks share the same orthogonal matrix, the complexity further reduces to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. If we consider both $r$ and $r'$ to scale linearly with $d$ (for example, $r = r' = \alpha d$ for some constant $0 < \alpha \leq 1$), the parameter complexity of LoRA becomes $\mathcal{O}(d^2 + dn)$ while OFT becomes $\mathcal{O}(d)$. This shows that OFT can be more parameter-efficient than LoRA in certain regimes, particularly when the output dimension $n$ is large. A concrete example illustrates this: for a weight matrix of size $128 \times 128$, LoRA with $r' = 8$ has $2{,}048$ trainable parameters, while OFT with $r = 8$ (without block sharing) has only $960$ parameters.

# Why OFT Converges Faster: Effective Semantic Updates and Smooth Optimization Landscape

One of the most striking empirical observations about OFT is its faster convergence compared to existing finetuning methods. The paper provides two complementary explanations for this phenomenon, one based on the nature of the updates OFT makes and the other based on the geometry of the optimization landscape.

The first explanation stems from the observation that angular information among neurons is the primary carrier of semantic meaning in neural networks. The paper demonstrates this through a toy experiment using a convolutional autoencoder trained on flower images. During training, the autoencoder uses standard inner products between convolutional kernels $\bm{w}$ and input patches $\bm{x}$ to produce feature maps. At test time, the experiment compares three ways of generating feature maps: (a) the standard inner product used in training, (b) using only the magnitude information, and (c) using only the angular (cosine) information. The remarkable finding is that the angular information alone can almost perfectly recover the input images, while the magnitude information contains essentially no useful information for reconstruction. This experiment powerfully demonstrates that the directions (angles) of neurons, not their magnitudes, encode the semantic content of the data.

Given this observation, it follows that the most effective way to modify the semantic output of a model during finetuning is to change the directions of its neurons. This is precisely what OFT is designed to do: by learning an orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$, OFT directly rotates and reflects the neurons in each layer, changing their directions while preserving the pairwise angles between them. LoRA, by contrast, adds a low-rank update to the weights, which changes both the magnitudes and directions of neurons in a coupled manner that may not optimally target the semantically relevant dimensions. Standard finetuning with a small learning rate also updates weights in an unstructured way, diluting the update across both magnitude and direction. Because OFT focuses its updates specifically on the angular (semantic) dimensions, it can achieve meaningful semantic changes more efficiently, leading to faster convergence.

The second explanation for OFT's fast convergence relates to the geometry of the optimization problem. OFT can be viewed as finetuning neurons on a smooth hypersphere manifold. The orthogonality constraint ensures that the neurons remain on the unit hypersphere (after normalization), and the optimization proceeds over the space of orthogonal transformations, which is a smooth manifold known as the orthogonal group $\text{O}(d)$. Optimization on smooth manifolds generally has better convergence properties than unconstrained optimization in Euclidean space, because the constraint eliminates degenerate directions and the geometry of the manifold provides a more regular optimization landscape. The empirical verification of this property is referenced in the paper through prior work on orthogonal over-parameterized training, which showed that optimizing over orthogonal transformations yields a smoother optimization landscape compared to standard training. The combination of targeting semantically relevant updates and operating on a smooth manifold explains why OFT converges significantly faster than methods like LoRA and ControlNet. For example, in the landmark-to-face task, it takes 20 epochs for LoRA to converge to the performance that OFT achieves at the 8th epoch, and OFT can converge well with only 5\% of the ADE20K dataset, demonstrating remarkable data efficiency.

# Why Not Minimize Hyperspherical Energy: Preserving vs. Minimizing for Finetuning

A natural question that arises when considering OFT is: if hyperspherical energy is such an important measure of neural network structure, why not actively minimize it during finetuning, as some prior work has done for classification? The answer reveals a crucial distinction between training a network from scratch and finetuning a pretrained model, and it highlights a key design principle of OFT.

Hyperspherical energy, defined as $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ denotes the $i$-th normalized neuron, measures how uniformly the neurons are distributed on the unit hypersphere. Minimizing hyperspherical energy pushes neurons to be as spread out as possible, approaching a uniform distribution. In the context of classification, this is desirable because uniformly distributed neurons minimize redundancy: each neuron covers a distinct region of the input space, and there is no wasted overlap between neurons. Prior work on orthogonal over-parameterized training exploits exactly this principle, training classification networks from scratch by applying orthogonal transformations to a randomly initialized network, which in expectation yields a small hyperspherical energy. The goal there is to keep the hyperspherical energy small throughout training, as small energy is associated with better generalization in classification.

However, the situation is fundamentally different for finetuning a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model. The pretrained model has already learned a rich semantic structure during pretraining, and this structure is encoded in the specific configuration of neuron angles — not in a uniform distribution of neurons. The hyperspherical energy of the pretrained model reflects the specific, non-uniform arrangement of neurons that was learned through extensive pretraining on large-scale data. This arrangement captures complex semantic relationships: some neurons may be intentionally correlated because they encode related visual concepts, while others may be spread apart because they represent distinct concepts. Actively minimizing hyperspherical energy during finetuning would push all neurons toward a uniform distribution, effectively destroying the carefully learned semantic structure. The non-uniform angular relationships among neurons in the pretrained model are not redundancy to be eliminated — they are knowledge to be preserved.

This is exactly why OFT aims to preserve hyperspherical energy rather than minimize it. By constraining the finetuning transformation to be orthogonal, OFT provably preserves the pairwise angles between all neurons in each layer, and therefore preserves the hyperspherical energy exactly. The pretrained model's semantic structure, encoded in its neuron angles, is maintained throughout finetuning, and the orthogonal transformation only adjusts the overall orientation of the neuron set to adapt to the downstream task. This distinction between preserving and minimizing hyperspherical energy is one of the key differences between OFT and prior work on orthogonal training. It reflects the fundamental difference between the goals of training from scratch (where one wants to learn a good structure) and finetuning (where one wants to adapt while keeping an existing good structure).

# The Flexibility-Regularity Trade-off in Finetuning Method Design

The design of OFT reveals a fundamental trade-off in finetuning method design: the trade-off between flexibility and regularity. Flexibility refers to the ability of a finetuning method to make arbitrary changes to the model, enabling it to adapt to a wide range of downstream tasks. Regularity refers to the constraints that prevent the finetuning from destroying the pretrained model's knowledge and that ensure stable training dynamics. A good finetuning method must balance these two competing objectives, and different methods fall at different points along this spectrum.

Standard finetuning, which simply updates the pretrained weights via gradient descent with a small learning rate, sits at the extreme of maximum flexibility. There are no structural constraints on the weight updates: the weights can move in any direction by any amount (controlled only by the learning rate). This maximum flexibility comes at a cost: poor stability and susceptibility to model collapse. The paper observes that DreamBooth, which uses standard finetuning, starts to generate collapsed images after 2000 iterations, losing the pretrained model's generative capability. The lack of any structural constraint means that the finetuning can easily move the model into a degenerate region of weight space where it no longer produces realistic images.

At the other extreme, one could imagine a finetuning method with maximum regularity that barely changes the model at all, but such a method would be too inflexible to adapt to downstream tasks. The challenge is to find a method that provides sufficient flexibility for adaptation while maintaining enough regularity to preserve the pretrained model's capabilities. OFT finds a compelling balance in this trade-off. The orthogonal transformation provides meaningful flexibility: it can rotate and reflect all neurons in a layer, which, as the paper's experiments show, is sufficient to achieve strong performance on both subject-driven and controllable generation tasks. At the same time, the orthogonality constraint provides strong regularity by preserving all pairwise neuron angles, which as argued above, is crucial for maintaining the pretrained semantic structure.

The block-diagonal parameterization of the orthogonal matrix introduces an additional knob for controlling the flexibility-regularity trade-off. With $r = 1$ (a single block), the orthogonal matrix is unconstrained within the space of orthogonal transformations, providing maximum flexibility within the orthogonality constraint. As $r$ increases, the block-diagonal structure becomes more restrictive: neurons in different blocks cannot be mixed, which reduces flexibility but also increases regularity by limiting the types of transformations that can be applied. The block-diagonal parameterization can thus be viewed as a stronger regularization of the orthogonal matrix, and the choice of $r$ allows practitioners to tune the flexibility-regularity balance for their specific task.

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) adds yet another layer of regularity through the deviation constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. This constraint limits how far the orthogonal transformation can deviate from the identity, directly controlling the magnitude of the change to the pretrained model. With larger $\epsilon$, COFT approaches the flexibility of standard OFT, while with smaller $\epsilon$, the finetuned model increasingly resembles the pretrained model. The paper demonstrates that COFT achieves even better finetuning stability than OFT due to this explicit deviation constraint, and that with a proper $\epsilon$, both the learning rate and the iteration number become effortless to set. This makes COFT particularly practical, as it reduces the need for careful hyperparameter tuning, which is a significant practical challenge in finetuning large diffusion models.

# No Additional Inference Overhead: Merging R into W⁰

A practically important property of OFT that distinguishes it from some other finetuning approaches is that it introduces no additional inference overhead. This property is a direct consequence of the multiplicative structure of OFT's weight update and has significant implications for deployment.

Recall that OFT modifies the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by left-multiplying it with a learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, producing the finetuned weight $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. During training, this is implemented as two separate operations: first computing $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, then using $\bm{W}$ in the forward pass. However, once training is complete, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ has been learned and is fixed. At this point, one can simply precompute the product $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ once and store the resulting weight matrix. During inference, the model then uses $\bm{W}$ exactly as the pretrained model would have used $\bm{W}^0$, with no additional computation. The forward pass remains $\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top\bm{x}$, which has the same computational cost as the original forward pass $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$.

This is in contrast to adapter-based methods like ControlNet, which introduce additional network components that must be executed during inference. ControlNet, for instance, adds a trainable copy of the encoder blocks that processes the control signal and injects it into the pretrained model. These additional components increase the inference time and memory footprint of the model, which can be a significant drawback in deployment scenarios where computational resources are limited or where real-time generation is required. Similarly, LoRA, while it can also be merged into the pretrained weights (since the update is additive: $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$), requires the computation of the low-rank product $\bm{A}\bm{B}$ during training, and the merged weight matrix may have different numerical properties than the pretrained one.

The ability to merge $\bm{R}$ into $\bm{W}^0$ without any residual computational overhead makes OFT particularly attractive for practical deployment. The finetuned model has exactly the same architecture, the same number of parameters (in terms of the weight matrix), and the same inference speed as the pretrained model. This means that OFT can be seamlessly integrated into existing inference pipelines without any modifications to the model architecture or the inference code — one simply replaces the pretrained weight matrix with the merged finetuned weight matrix. This property also means that OFT does not increase the memory footprint of the model during inference, which is important for serving models on resource-constrained devices or for serving multiple finetuned models simultaneously.

# Comparison to Orthogonal Over-Parameterized Training

OFT shares a conceptual foundation with orthogonal over-parameterized training, but the two methods differ in their goals, constraints, and applications in important ways. Understanding these differences illuminates the unique design principles behind OFT and why it is specifically suited for finetuning text-to-image diffusion models.

Orthogonal over-parameterized training, as developed in prior work, is a method for training classification neural networks from scratch. The approach begins with a randomly initialized neural network and trains it by applying orthogonal transformations to the weights. The motivation comes from the observation that a randomly initialized neural network yields a provably small hyperspherical energy in expectation, meaning the neurons are approximately uniformly distributed on the hypersphere. The goal of orthogonal over-parameterized training is to keep the hyperspherical energy small throughout training, since small energy is associated with better generalization in classification. This is achieved by constraining the weight updates to be orthogonal transformations, which preserve the pairwise angles among neurons and thus maintain the (approximately) uniform distribution established by random initialization.

OFT adopts the same mathematical tool — orthogonal transformation of neuron weights — but applies it with a fundamentally different objective. Instead of training a network from scratch, OFT finetunes a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model for downstream tasks. The pretrained model has a specific, learned neuron configuration that encodes rich semantic knowledge, and this configuration is generally not uniform (the hyperspherical energy is not necessarily small). The goal of OFT is to preserve this learned configuration while making targeted adjustments for the downstream task. This leads to three key differences from orthogonal over-parameterized training.

First, while orthogonal over-parameterized training aims to minimize hyperspherical energy, OFT aims to preserve the same hyperspherical energy as the pretrained model. Minimizing hyperspherical energy during finetuning would push the neurons toward a uniform distribution, destroying the non-uniform semantic structure learned during pretraining. OFT's preservation objective is captured by the constraint $\|\text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\| = 0$, which is provably satisfied by orthogonal transformation. This is not just a minor variation of the minimization objective — it is a fundamentally different goal that reflects the different context of finetuning versus training from scratch.

Second, OFT seeks to minimize the deviation from the pretrained model, which leads to the constrained variant COFT. In COFT, the orthogonal transformation is additionally constrained to stay within a small neighborhood of the identity matrix: $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. This constraint has no counterpart in orthogonal over-parameterized training, which imposes no such deviation constraint because there is no pretrained model to stay close to. The deviation constraint in COFT embodies the principle that finetuning should make the smallest change necessary to adapt to the downstream task, and it provides additional stability that is critical when working with large, expensive pretrained models.

Third, the application domains differ significantly. Orthogonal over-parameterized training was designed and evaluated for classification problems, where the goal is to learn a mapping from inputs to discrete class labels. OFT is designed for text-to-image diffusion models, where the goal is to generate high-fidelity, semantically meaningful images. The generative setting imposes different requirements: the finetuned model must not only perform well on the downstream task (e.g., subject-driven or controllable generation) but also maintain the high fidelity and diversity of the pretrained model's generative capabilities. This is a more delicate objective than classification accuracy, as it requires preserving the rich, continuous structure of the generative model's output space rather than just maintaining decision boundaries between classes.

Together, these differences highlight that OFT is not a straightforward adaptation of orthogonal over-parameterized training to a new domain, but rather a method that shares a core mathematical insight (the power and structure of orthogonal transformations) while being carefully redesigned for the specific challenges of finetuning large pretrained generative models. The key to finetuning, as the paper emphasizes, is finding a good trade-off between flexibility and stability, and the OFT framework achieves this through the combination of orthogonal transformation (for structured, semantic updates), hyperspherical energy preservation (for maintaining pretrained knowledge), and optional deviation constraints (for additional stability).

# Subject-driven Generation: Experiments and Analysis

# Experimental Setup: Stable Diffusion v1.5, DreamBooth Framework, and Baseline Configurations

The subject-driven generation experiments are designed to rigorously evaluate the effectiveness of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT) against established baselines. The pretrained text-to-image model used throughout all experiments is Stable Diffusion v1.5, a latent diffusion model that has demonstrated strong capabilities in text-guided high-fidelity image generation. By using the same pretrained backbone across all methods, the experiments isolate the effect of the finetuning strategy itself, ensuring that any observed differences in performance are attributable to the finetuning method rather than the base model.

The two primary baselines for comparison are DreamBooth and LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation). DreamBooth represents the standard approach to subject-driven generation: it finetunes the entire text-to-image diffusion model using a reconstruction loss and a class-specific prior preservation loss, employing a small learning rate to implicitly constrain the deviation from the pretrained weights. LoRA, on the other hand, introduces a low-rank additive update to the weight matrices, reducing the number of trainable parameters while still allowing the model to adapt to the downstream task. Both baselines are configured according to their respective original papers, using the best hyperparameter setups reported by their authors. This ensures that the comparison is fair and that the baselines are performing at their intended capacity.

A critical aspect of the experimental design is that all methods—DreamBooth, LoRA, OFT, and COFT—adopt the same loss function as DreamBooth. This means that the training objectives are held constant, and the only variable being tested is the finetuning mechanism itself. OFT and COFT replace the standard weight update with a learned orthogonal transformation applied to the pretrained weights, while DreamBooth and LoRA use their respective update strategies. To ensure a fair comparison with LoRA, OFT and COFT are applied only to the same layers where LoRA is used—specifically, the attention weights in the transformer components of the diffusion model. This matching of applied layers prevents OFT from gaining an unfair advantage by finetuning a broader set of layers than LoRA.

To further ensure fairness in evaluation, generated images are randomly picked from each method, meaning that no method benefits from careful curation or cherry-picking of outputs. For each method, the model that achieves the best validation CLIP metrics is selected for reporting, providing a consistent and principled criterion for model selection across all approaches.

# Finetuning Stability and Convergence Analysis Across Iterations

One of the most striking findings from the subject-driven generation experiments is the difference in finetuning stability and convergence behavior among the four methods. The experiments reveal that both COFT and OFT are able to finetune the diffusion model in a remarkably stable manner, while DreamBooth and LoRA exhibit significant instability over the course of training.

At 400 training iterations, both DreamBooth and the OFT variants achieve good control over the generated subject, producing images that recognizable capture the subject identity. However, LoRA at this stage fails to preserve the subject identity, indicating a slower convergence to the desired subject representation. This early divergence in performance already highlights a convergence advantage for OFT and COFT over LoRA.

As training progresses to 2000 iterations, the differences become even more pronounced. DreamBooth begins to generate collapsed images—a phenomenon where the model's outputs degrade catastrophically, losing coherence and realism. This model collapse is a well-known failure mode of standard finetuning with small learning rates: while the approach can work for a limited number of iterations, it eventually destabilizes as the cumulative weight updates drift too far from the pretrained model. LoRA, at 2000 iterations, fails to generate the correct subject attributes—for example, when the subject is wearing a yellow shirt, LoRA instead generates yellow fur, indicating that the model has lost track of the fine-grained subject details.

In stark contrast, both OFT and COFT maintain stable and consistent control over the generated images even at 2000 iterations. The orthogonal transformation constraint ensures that the pairwise angular relationships among neurons are preserved, preventing the kind of semantic drift that causes DreamBooth to collapse and LoRA to lose subject fidelity. This stability is a direct consequence of the structural preservation guaranteed by OFT: by maintaining the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained model, OFT prevents the catastrophic forgetting that plagues less constrained finetuning approaches.

The practical significance of this stability cannot be overstated. In real-world applications of subject-driven generation, the number of finetuning iterations is a critical hyperparameter that must be carefully tuned for each subject. Too few iterations and the model fails to capture the subject; too many iterations and the model may collapse. The insensitivity of OFT and COFT to the iteration count effectively eliminates this tuning burden. For both OFT and COFT, one can directly set a relatively large iteration number without carefully tuning it for each new subject. For COFT specifically, with a properly chosen deviation constraint $\epsilon$, both the learning rate and the iteration number become nearly effortless to set, making the entire finetuning pipeline substantially more user-friendly.

# Quantitative Metrics: DINO, CLIP-I, CLIP-T, and LPIPS for Subject Fidelity and Diversity

To rigorously evaluate subject-driven generation, the paper adopts a suite of quantitative metrics originally proposed in the DreamBooth paper. These metrics are designed to measure different aspects of generation quality, capturing the tension between subject fidelity (how well the generated image matches the real subject), text prompt fidelity (how well the generated image follows the text prompt), and sample diversity (how varied the generated images are for the same subject and prompt).

The DINO metric measures subject fidelity using ViT S/16 DINO embeddings. DINO is a self-supervised vision transformer that produces rich visual representations known to capture object identity and structure. The DINO metric computes the average cosine similarity between the DINO embeddings of generated images and real images of the subject. A higher DINO score indicates that the generated images more faithfully preserve the visual identity of the subject—a crucial requirement for subject-driven generation, where the goal is to render the same subject in novel contexts.

The CLIP-I (CLIP-Image) metric also measures subject fidelity but uses CLIP image embeddings instead of DINO embeddings. CLIP-I computes the average pairwise cosine similarity of CLIP embeddings between generated and real images. While DINO is particularly strong at capturing structural and identity-related features, CLIP embeddings encode a broader range of semantic information learned from image-text pairs, making CLIP-I a complementary measure of subject preservation.

The CLIP-T (CLIP-Text) metric evaluates text prompt fidelity by computing the average cosine similarity of CLIP embeddings between the text prompt and the generated images. This metric assesses whether the finetuned model still follows text guidance effectively after adaptation. A finetuning method that achieves high subject fidelity but low CLIP-T would indicate that the model has overfit to the subject images at the expense of its text-guided generation ability—a failure mode that is particularly common in standard finetuning approaches.

Finally, the LPIPS (Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity) metric measures sample diversity. LPIPS computes the average perceptual distance between pairs of generated images of the same subject with the same text prompt. A higher LPIPS score indicates greater diversity in the generated outputs. This metric is important because a good subject-driven generation model should not simply memorize and reproduce a single image of the subject; it should be able to generate diverse images that all maintain subject fidelity while varying in pose, lighting, background, and other factors.

Together, these four metrics provide a comprehensive picture of generation quality. An ideal finetuning method should achieve high DINO and CLIP-I scores (strong subject fidelity), high CLIP-T scores (good text prompt following), and high LPIPS scores (good diversity). The tension among these metrics—particularly between subject fidelity and text prompt fidelity—makes subject-driven generation a challenging multi-objective problem.

# Quantitative Comparison Results and Statistical Significance

The quantitative comparison results demonstrate that both COFT and OFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA in the DINO and CLIP-I metrics by a considerable margin. This means that the images generated by OFT and COFT more faithfully preserve the visual identity of the subject compared to the baseline methods. The improvement in DINO and CLIP-I is particularly significant because subject fidelity is the primary objective of subject-driven generation: if the generated image does not look like the subject, the method has fundamentally failed regardless of how well it follows the text prompt.

In addition to the strong subject fidelity results, OFT and COFT achieve slightly better or comparable performance in the CLIP-T (text prompt fidelity) and LPIPS (diversity) metrics. This is a notable achievement because many finetuning methods that improve subject fidelity do so at the expense of text prompt following—the model becomes so specialized to the subject that it loses its ability to respond to text guidance. The fact that OFT and COFT maintain or improve text prompt fidelity while substantially improving subject fidelity suggests that the orthogonal transformation preserves the semantic structure needed for text-guided generation while providing enough flexibility to adapt to the new subject.

To ensure the statistical reliability of these results, each method is repeatedly finetuned on the same pretrained model using 30 different random seeds. This large number of repetitions minimizes the influence of randomness and provides a robust estimate of each method's expected performance. The use of 30 random seeds is a methodologically rigorous choice that allows for meaningful statistical comparisons and ensures that the observed improvements are not artifacts of a particular random initialization or data ordering. The consistent superiority of OFT and COFT across 30 seeds demonstrates that the performance gains are robust and reproducible, not contingent on lucky random outcomes.

The overall picture that emerges from the quantitative results is that the OFT framework not only achieves better convergence and stability—as demonstrated by the convergence analysis—but also yields consistently better final performance across all relevant metrics. The combination of faster convergence, greater stability, and superior final quality makes OFT and COFT compelling alternatives to existing finetuning methods for subject-driven generation.

# Qualitative Comparison: Subject Preservation and Text Prompt Following

Beyond the quantitative metrics, the paper provides qualitative comparisons that offer more intuitive insight into the differences among the methods. The qualitative examples are randomly picked from generated images, ensuring that the comparison is representative rather than selectively favorable. Importantly, all examples for a given method are generated from the same finetuned model, meaning that no text prompt is separately optimized or tuned for individual results. This design choice further reinforces the fairness of the comparison.

The qualitative results reveal that both OFT and COFT deliver excellent semantic subject preservation across a variety of subjects and contexts. The generated images clearly maintain the distinguishing features of the subject—whether it is a particular animal, object, or person—while successfully placing the subject in the novel context specified by the text prompt. This dual capability of preserving subject identity while following text guidance is the hallmark of successful subject-driven generation.

LoRA, in contrast, often fails to preserve the subject identity in the qualitative examples. A particularly illustrative case is the bowl example, where LoRA completely loses the subject identity: the generated image bears little resemblance to the original subject, demonstrating that LoRA's low-rank update strategy can be insufficient for capturing the fine-grained visual details needed for faithful subject reproduction. This failure aligns with the quantitative results showing lower DINO and CLIP-I scores for LoRA.

DreamBooth presents a different failure mode. While it manages to preserve the subject identity in many cases—generating images that are recognizably of the correct subject—it frequently fails to generate images that follow the text prompt. For example, in the first row of the bowl example, DreamBooth produces an image of the subject but in a context that does not match the specified text prompt. This limitation reflects the tension between subject fidelity and text prompt fidelity: DreamBooth's finetuning strategy, which updates the model weights directly with a small learning rate, can cause the model to overfit to the subject images at the expense of its pretrained text-guided generation ability.

The qualitative comparison thus demonstrates that the OFT framework achieves a better balance between controllability and subject preservation than either DreamBooth or LoRA. OFT and COFT generate images that simultaneously maintain the subject's visual identity and follow the text prompt's contextual guidance—a balance that the baselines struggle to achieve. Furthermore, the number of iterations is not a sensitive hyperparameter for OFT: it performs well even with a large number of iterations, whereas neither DreamBooth nor LoRA can maintain quality under the same conditions. This robustness to iteration count, combined with the superior quality of generated images, makes OFT a more practical and reliable choice for subject-driven generation applications.

# Robustness to Hyperparameter Settings and Iteration Count Insensitivity

A recurring theme throughout the subject-driven generation experiments is the robustness of OFT and COFT to hyperparameter settings, particularly the insensitivity to the number of finetuning iterations. This property has significant practical implications for the deployment of subject-driven generation systems.

In standard finetuning approaches like DreamBooth, the number of training iterations is a critical hyperparameter that must be carefully tuned for each new subject. Too few iterations result in insufficient adaptation—the model fails to capture the subject's distinctive features. Too many iterations lead to model collapse—the generated images degrade catastrophically, losing realism and coherence. This narrow window of acceptable iteration counts creates a practical burden: for each new subject, the user must experiment with different iteration numbers to find the sweet spot, a process that is time-consuming and subject-dependent.

The OFT framework largely eliminates this burden. Because the orthogonal transformation preserves the pairwise angular relationships among neurons, the pretrained model's semantic structure is maintained throughout finetuning. This structural preservation acts as a powerful regularizer that prevents the catastrophic forgetting and model collapse observed in DreamBooth. As a result, both OFT and COFT can be trained for a relatively large number of iterations without degrading output quality. The user can set a generous iteration count without worrying about overshooting the optimal training duration.

COFT provides an even greater degree of robustness through its additional deviation constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. This constraint explicitly limits how far the orthogonal transformation can deviate from the identity matrix, which corresponds to the pretrained model. With a properly chosen $\epsilon$, the COFT-finetuned model's behavior is bounded: it cannot drift too far from the pretrained model regardless of the number of iterations or the learning rate. This makes both the learning rate and the iteration count nearly effortless to set. The deviation constraint acts as a safety net that prevents overfitting and model collapse, even in the absence of careful hyperparameter tuning.

The practical value of this robustness is considerable. In real-world applications, subject-driven generation is often used by non-experts who may not have the expertise or resources to carefully tune hyperparameters for each new subject. The ability of OFT and COFT to perform well across a wide range of iteration counts and learning rates makes them far more accessible and reliable than existing methods, which require subject-specific tuning to achieve good results.

# Human Evaluation of Generation Quality

In addition to the quantitative metrics and qualitative comparisons, the paper conducts a human evaluation to further validate the superiority of OFT. Human evaluation provides an important complement to automated metrics because it captures subjective aspects of image quality—such as realism, aesthetic appeal, and perceived subject similarity—that may not be fully captured by metrics like DINO or CLIP-I.

Human evaluation is particularly valuable in text-to-image generation because the ultimate consumers of generated images are human users. A finetuning method that achieves high scores on automated metrics but produces images that humans find unconvincing or aesthetically unappealing would be of limited practical value. By directly assessing human judgments of generation quality, the evaluation provides a more holistic assessment of the method's effectiveness.

The human evaluation results further validate the superiority of OFT, consistent with the findings from both the quantitative metrics and the qualitative comparisons. Human evaluators prefer the images generated by OFT and COFT over those produced by DreamBooth and LoRA, confirming that the improvements observed in automated metrics translate to perceptually meaningful differences in image quality. This convergence of evidence—from quantitative metrics, qualitative examples, and human evaluation—provides a compelling and multi-faceted validation of the OFT framework's effectiveness for subject-driven generation.

# Controllable Generation: Experiments and Analysis

## Task Setup: C2I on COCO, S2I on ADE20K, L2F on CelebA-HQ

The controllable generation experiments are designed to evaluate how effectively the Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) framework can adapt a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model to accept additional control signals beyond text prompts. The general task setting follows the paradigm established by ControlNet and T2I-Adapter, where the goal is to finetune a pretrained diffusion model so that it can generate images guided by both a text prompt and an additional spatial control signal, such as edge maps, segmentation maps, or facial landmarks. Three distinct controllable generation tasks are considered in the main experiments, each chosen to challenge different aspects of controllability and generation fidelity.

The first task is Canny edge-to-image (C2I) generation, evaluated on the COCO dataset. In this task, the control signal is a Canny edge map extracted from a source image. The finetuned model must generate a photorealistic image that is semantically consistent with the text prompt while simultaneously respecting the structural boundaries specified by the edge map. This task is particularly challenging because Canny edges provide only sparse structural information, leaving the model to hallucinate textures, colors, and fine details that are not explicitly specified by the control signal. The COCO dataset, with its diverse collection of natural scene images, provides a broad testbed for evaluating how well different finetuning methods can balance structural adherence with generation fidelity.

The second task is segmentation map-to-image (S2I) generation, evaluated on the ADE20K dataset. Here, the control signal is a semantic segmentation map, where each pixel is assigned a category label indicating the object or region it belongs to. The finetuned model must generate an image where the spatial layout of objects and regions matches the segmentation map. This task demands a higher degree of spatial precision than C2I, as the segmentation map provides explicit categorical and spatial information that the generated image must respect. The ADE20K dataset is particularly well-suited for this evaluation because it contains a rich variety of indoor and outdoor scenes with complex spatial arrangements and a large number of semantic categories.

The third task is landmark-to-face (L2F) generation, evaluated on the CelebA-HQ dataset. In this task, the control signal consists of facial landmarks specifying the locations of key facial features such as eyes, nose, mouth, and face contour. The finetuned model must generate a face image where the facial features align precisely with the given landmarks, which may specify challenging or unusual face poses. This task tests the model's ability to perform fine-grained geometric control, as even small misalignments in facial landmarks can produce visually implausible results. The CelebA-HQ dataset provides high-quality face images that serve as training data for learning this precise spatial correspondence.

Across all three tasks, the base pretrained model used is Stable Diffusion v1.5. All finetuning methods are trained for 20 epochs on each dataset, ensuring a consistent training budget for fair comparison. The control signals are processed and provided as additional conditioning inputs to the diffusion model, and the finetuning objective is to learn how to incorporate these spatial control signals into the image generation process while maintaining the high-fidelity generation capabilities of the pretrained model.

## Baselines: ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, LoRA

To provide a comprehensive evaluation, the OFT framework is compared against three state-of-the-art baselines that represent different philosophies for adapting pretrained diffusion models to controllable generation tasks.

ControlNet is a prominent method that finetunes a pretrained diffusion model by creating a trainable copy of the model's encoding layers, which processes the additional control signal. The trainable copy is connected to the frozen pretrained model through zero-convolution layers that are initialized to produce zero outputs, ensuring that finetuning starts from the pretrained model's behavior. ControlNet has demonstrated impressive controllable generation performance, but it introduces a significant number of additional trainable parameters and adds computational overhead during inference due to the extra network components. The ControlNet architecture processes control signals through a parallel network branch that interacts with the main diffusion model at multiple layers, enabling rich integration of spatial information but at the cost of increased model complexity.

T2I-Adapter is a concurrent approach that similarly finetunes a pretrained diffusion model to accept additional control signals. Like ControlNet, T2I-Adapter introduces additional network components to process control signals and integrate them with the pretrained model. However, T2I-Adapter is designed to be somewhat lighter than ControlNet, using adapter modules that insert control signal information into the diffusion model's intermediate representations. Despite this architectural difference, T2I-Adapter still introduces additional parameters and computational overhead, and the paper's results show that it converges slowly and yields worse final results compared to OFT.

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) represents a fundamentally different approach. Rather than adding new network components, LoRA finetunes the pretrained model by learning low-rank additive updates to the existing weight matrices. Specifically, LoRA assumes that the weight update during finetuning has a low-rank structure and parameterizes the update as the product of two small matrices. This approach is parameter-efficient and introduces no additional inference overhead, as the low-rank updates can be merged into the original weights. However, the low-rank constraint may limit the expressiveness of the finetuning update. In the context of controllable generation, LoRA is applied to the attention weights of the diffusion model, following the same layers where OFT is applied to ensure a fair comparison.

For fairness across all comparisons, OFT and COFT are applied to the same layers where LoRA is used, specifically the attention weights of the diffusion model. This ensures that any performance differences between OFT and LoRA can be attributed to the finetuning strategy rather than differences in which layers are being adapted. All methods use the best possible hyperparameter settings identified through careful tuning, and the same pretrained Stable Diffusion v1.5 model serves as the starting point for all finetuning experiments.

## Convergence Analysis: Faster and Smoother Convergence Compared to Baselines

One of the most striking empirical findings of the OFT framework is its convergence behavior, which is both faster and smoother than existing methods. This is demonstrated through both quantitative and qualitative evaluations, with a particular focus on the landmark-to-face (L2F) task as a representative benchmark.

For the quantitative convergence analysis, the evaluation metric used is the mean $\ell_2$ distance between the control face landmarks (the input control signal) and the predicted face landmarks extracted from the generated images. This metric directly measures how accurately the generated faces align with the specified landmark positions. The face landmark error is plotted for models finetuned with different numbers of training epochs, providing a clear picture of how quickly each method achieves accurate control.

The results reveal that both COFT and OFT achieve significantly faster convergence compared to all baselines. Most notably, it takes 20 epochs for LoRA to converge to the performance level that the OFT framework achieves at only the 8th epoch. This represents a substantial reduction in training time and computational cost. Furthermore, OFT and COFT achieve this faster convergence while using a similar number of trainable parameters as LoRA and far fewer parameters than ControlNet, demonstrating that the efficiency gains come from the principled structure of orthogonal transformation rather than from increased model capacity.

The convergence behavior of ControlNet exhibits a qualitatively different pattern that the paper describes as a "sudden convergence phenomenon." Specifically, ControlNet shows minimal controllability improvement during the early epochs, and then its controllability suddenly emerges after approximately the 8th epoch. This abrupt transition in training dynamics makes it difficult to predict when the model will start performing well and can lead to wasted training resources if the training is stopped prematurely. In contrast, the convergence of OFT and COFT is smooth and gradual, with consistent improvement in control accuracy throughout training.

The qualitative convergence results further illustrate this difference. For OFT and COFT, the generated face poses gradually and consistently align with the control landmarks as training progresses, producing a smooth transition from poor control to accurate control. The training dynamics are predictable and monotonic, making it straightforward to determine when training has reached a satisfactory level. For ControlNet, the controllability appears abruptly between epochs, which matches the sudden convergence phenomenon described in the original ControlNet work. This qualitative observation reinforces the practical advantage of OFT: the smooth and predictable training dynamics make it much easier to find good hyperparameters and determine when to stop training.

The practical implications of this convergence behavior are significant. In real-world applications, the number of training epochs is a critical hyperparameter that must be tuned for each task and dataset. Methods with smooth and predictable convergence, such as OFT and COFT, reduce the burden of hyperparameter tuning because the training trajectory is monotonic and stable. Methods with sudden or unpredictable convergence, such as ControlNet, require more careful monitoring and tuning to achieve optimal results. The insensitivity of OFT and COFT to the exact number of finetuning iterations is a particularly valuable property, as it effectively alleviates the need to tune the iteration count for different control tasks.

## Data Efficiency: OFT Converges with 5% of Training Data

Beyond convergence speed in terms of training epochs, the OFT framework demonstrates remarkable data efficiency, meaning it can achieve good performance with significantly fewer training images than existing methods. This property is especially important in practical scenarios where collecting large-scale training data for specific control tasks may be costly or impractical.

The data efficiency of OFT is demonstrated through experiments showing that OFT can converge well with only 5% of the ADE20K dataset for the segmentation-to-image (S2I) task. This is a dramatic reduction in training data requirements compared to baselines such as ControlNet and LoRA, which require substantially more data to achieve comparable performance. The ability to converge with such a small fraction of the training data suggests that the orthogonal transformation approach effectively leverages the knowledge encoded in the pretrained model, requiring only minimal additional information from the control task training data to learn the new control behavior.

This data efficiency can be understood through the lens of the OFT design philosophy. Because OFT preserves the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained model, it maintains the pretrained model's generative capabilities while learning a relatively constrained transformation to adapt to the new control task. The orthogonal transformation has fewer degrees of freedom compared to unconstrained finetuning, which means the model has less flexibility to deviate from the pretrained behavior. While this might seem like a limitation, it actually serves as a strong inductive bias that prevents overfitting to small training datasets. The pretrained model already contains rich knowledge about image generation, and OFT only needs to learn how to incorporate the control signal into this existing knowledge framework.

In contrast, methods like ControlNet introduce large additional network components with many trainable parameters. These additional parameters provide greater flexibility but also require more training data to be properly tuned, as there is more capacity that needs to be filled with meaningful information. LoRA, while more parameter-efficient than ControlNet, still faces challenges with data efficiency because its low-rank update structure may not align as effectively with the types of changes needed for controllable generation.

The data efficiency of OFT has important practical implications. For practitioners working on new controllable generation tasks, the ability to achieve good performance with limited training data means faster development cycles, lower data collection costs, and broader applicability to specialized domains where large-scale datasets may not be available. This property also suggests that OFT may be particularly well-suited for fine-grained or domain-specific control tasks where the available training data is inherently limited.

## Control Consistency Metric: Estimating Control Signals from Generated Images

A key methodological contribution of the paper is the introduction of a novel control consistency metric for evaluating controllable generation performance. Traditional evaluation of controllable generation has relied primarily on qualitative visual assessment or indirect metrics that do not directly measure how well the generated images follow the input control signals. The control consistency metric addresses this limitation by providing a quantitative measure of control accuracy.

The fundamental idea behind the control consistency metric is to estimate the control signal from the generated image and then compare this estimated control signal with the original input control signal. If the finetuned model has successfully learned to follow the control signal, then extracting the same type of control signal from the generated image should yield a result that closely matches the original input. This approach provides a direct and intuitive measure of control fidelity, as it evaluates whether the structural information specified by the control signal is actually present in the generated image.

For the Canny edge-to-image (C2I) task, the control consistency metric is computed using Intersection over Union (IoU) and F1 score. After generating an image from a given Canny edge map, a Canny edge detector is applied to the generated image to extract its edge map. This extracted edge map is then compared with the original input edge map using IoU, which measures the overlap between the two edge maps, and F1 score, which balances precision and recall of edge detection. A high IoU and F1 score indicate that the generated image contains edges that closely match the input control signal, demonstrating strong control fidelity.

For the segmentation-to-image (S2I) task, the control consistency metric is computed using mean IoU, mean accuracy, and overall accuracy. After generating an image from a given segmentation map, a segmentation model is applied to the generated image to produce a predicted segmentation map. This predicted segmentation map is then compared with the original input segmentation map. Mean IoU computes the average IoU across all semantic categories, providing a per-category measure of segmentation fidelity. Mean accuracy computes the average pixel-wise classification accuracy per category, while overall accuracy computes the global pixel-wise classification accuracy across the entire image. These metrics collectively capture how well the spatial and categorical structure of the segmentation map is preserved in the generated image.

For the landmark-to-face (L2F) task, the control consistency metric is computed using the mean $\ell_2$ distance between the control landmarks and predicted landmarks. After generating a face image from a given set of facial landmarks, a landmark detection model is applied to the generated face to extract predicted landmarks. The mean $\ell_2$ distance between the original control landmarks and these predicted landmarks is then computed, with a smaller distance indicating more accurate control. This metric directly measures the geometric fidelity of the generated face with respect to the specified landmark positions.

The control consistency metric represents a significant contribution to the evaluation methodology for controllable generation. By using off-the-shelf detection and segmentation models to estimate control signals from generated images, this metric provides an objective, reproducible measure of control performance that does not rely on subjective human evaluation. The accuracy of this metric is, of course, bounded by the accuracy of the off-the-shelf models used for control signal estimation, but these models are sufficiently accurate to provide meaningful comparisons between different finetuning methods. This metric enables rigorous quantitative evaluation of controllable generation and can be applied to any method that generates images from spatial control signals.

## Quantitative Comparison: IoU, F1, Mean IoU, Accuracy, $\ell_2$ Distance

The quantitative comparison using the control consistency metric provides a comprehensive evaluation of how OFT and COFT compare to existing methods across all three controllable generation tasks. The results demonstrate that both OFT and COFT yield significantly stronger and more accurate control than the baseline methods.

For all compared methods, the best possible hyperparameter settings are used to ensure a fair comparison. This means that each baseline is given every opportunity to achieve its best performance, and any superiority of OFT or COFT can be attributed to the finetuning strategy itself rather than suboptimal hyperparameter choices. The quantitative results consistently show that OFT and COFT outperform the baselines across multiple metrics and tasks.

The adapter-based approaches, specifically T2I-Adapter and ControlNet, are observed to converge slowly and also yield worse final results compared to OFT and COFT. This is a notable finding, as ControlNet in particular has been widely regarded as a strong method for controllable generation. The slower convergence of these methods means they require more training time and computational resources to reach their final performance, and even after full training, their control accuracy falls short of what OFT achieves. The additional network components introduced by these methods apparently do not translate to better control performance, suggesting that the key bottleneck is not model capacity but rather the finetuning strategy's ability to effectively adapt the pretrained model.

The comparison with LoRA reveals an interesting pattern of task-dependent performance. LoRA performs better than ControlNet in the S2I task but worse in both the C2I and L2F tasks. This variable performance across tasks suggests that LoRA's low-rank adaptation strategy has an inconsistent ability to capture the types of transformations needed for different control tasks. In the S2I task, the relatively structured nature of segmentation maps may align well with low-rank updates, while the sparse structural information in Canny edges and the precise geometric requirements of facial landmarks may demand more expressive transformations that LoRA's low-rank constraint cannot adequately provide. In general, the paper finds that the performance ceiling of LoRA is relatively low, meaning that even with careful hyperparameter tuning, LoRA is unable to match the performance of OFT and COFT.

In contrast, the performance of the OFT framework shows that it has not yet saturated at the level of performance reported. The authors empirically find that OFT continues to improve as the number of trainable parameters increases, suggesting that the orthogonal transformation approach has a higher performance ceiling than the compared baselines. This is an important finding, as it indicates that OFT has greater potential for further improvement through scaling, whereas the baseline methods may have already reached their performance limits within the experimental settings.

The quantitative evaluation using the control consistency metric is highlighted as one of the novel contributions of the paper, as it provides a rigorous and objective way to evaluate control performance. Previous evaluations of controllable generation have often relied on qualitative visual comparisons or indirect metrics that do not directly measure control fidelity. The control consistency metric, by directly estimating and comparing control signals, provides a more meaningful assessment of how well each method achieves the fundamental goal of controllable generation: producing images that faithfully follow the specified control signals.

## Qualitative Comparison Across All Three Control Tasks

Beyond the quantitative metrics, the paper provides extensive qualitative comparisons that offer intuitive insights into the strengths and weaknesses of each method. Randomly generated images are used for all qualitative comparisons, ensuring that the examples are representative of typical performance rather than being cherry-picked.

In the segmentation-to-image (S2I) task, the qualitative comparison reveals stark differences between methods. LoRA completely fails to generate images that follow the input segmentation map, producing images that bear little structural resemblance to the specified spatial layout. This aligns with the quantitative findings where LoRA, despite performing better than ControlNet on S2I metrics, still falls significantly short of OFT and COFT. ControlNet, OFT, and COFT are all able to control the generated images according to the segmentation map, but the quality of the generated images differs substantially. Both OFT and COFT are able to generate high-fidelity images with more vivid details and more reasonable geometric structures compared to ControlNet. This is particularly noteworthy because OFT and COFT achieve this superior quality with far fewer model parameters than ControlNet, demonstrating that the orthogonal transformation approach is more parameter-efficient while also producing better visual results.

In the Canny edge-to-image (C2I) task, both OFT and COFT are able to hallucinate semantically similar images based on the rough Canny edge inputs. The term "hallucinate" here refers to the model's ability to generate plausible textures, colors, and fine details that are not explicitly specified by the sparse edge map but are consistent with the structural information it provides. T2I-Adapter and LoRA perform much worse in this task, struggling to produce images that are both visually coherent and structurally consistent with the input edges. The superior performance of OFT and COFT in this task can be attributed to their ability to effectively modify the semantic generation behavior of the pretrained model while preserving its high-fidelity generation capabilities. The orthogonal transformation adjusts the directions of neurons to incorporate the control signal information, while the preservation of hyperspherical energy ensures that the pretrained model's ability to generate realistic images is not destroyed.

In the landmark-to-face (L2F) task, OFT and COFT produce the most accurate pose control for generated faces, even under challenging face poses. The ability to handle challenging poses is particularly important, as it tests the model's ability to generalize beyond the range of poses commonly seen in training data. The precise geometric control achieved by OFT and COFT is consistent with the quantitative findings showing low $\ell_2$ landmark distances. The qualitative results show that the generated faces not only align with the specified landmark positions but also maintain realistic facial features and expressions, demonstrating that the control is achieved without sacrificing generation quality.

Across all three control tasks, the qualitative comparisons consistently show that both OFT and COFT produce qualitatively better images than the state-of-the-art baselines. This consistent superiority across diverse tasks—ranging from sparse edge control to dense semantic segmentation to precise geometric landmark control—demonstrates the robustness and versatility of the OFT framework. The orthogonal transformation approach appears to be well-suited to a wide range of controllable generation scenarios, as it provides a principled way to adapt the pretrained model's behavior without compromising its generative capabilities.

The qualitative results also illustrate the practical benefits of OFT's stable convergence. Because OFT and COFT converge smoothly and predictably, the final models consistently produce high-quality results without the variance and instability that can affect methods with less predictable training dynamics. This reliability is important for practical deployment, where consistent performance across different inputs and conditions is essential.

## Additional Control Tasks: Dense Pose, Sketch, Depth to Image

Beyond the three main controllable generation tasks evaluated in the main paper, the OFT framework is also demonstrated on additional control tasks to showcase its versatility. These additional experiments, presented in the appendix, include dense pose-to-human-body generation, sketch-to-image generation, and depth-to-image generation. Each of these tasks represents a different type of spatial control signal, further testing the generality of the OFT approach.

Dense pose-to-human-body generation uses dense pose maps as control signals, which specify detailed body surface keypoints and their correspondences to a 3D human body model. This task requires the model to generate human body images that match the specified pose, which is a more complex form of geometric control compared to facial landmarks. The dense pose representation provides rich information about body configuration, and the model must translate this abstract representation into a photorealistic human body image. The ability of OFT to handle this task demonstrates that the orthogonal transformation approach can scale to control signals with higher-dimensional and more complex spatial structures.

Sketch-to-image generation uses free-form sketches as control signals. Unlike Canny edges, which are automatically extracted from images using edge detection algorithms, sketches are typically hand-drawn and may not correspond to any specific source image. This task requires the model to interpret artistic or approximate sketches and generate images that match the intended content while maintaining realistic appearance. The challenge here is that sketches can be highly variable in style and precision, and the model must be robust to this variability while maintaining control fidelity. The application of OFT to this task shows that the framework can handle control signals that are qualitatively different from the structured signals used in the main experiments.

Depth-to-image generation uses depth maps as control signals, specifying the 3D structure of the scene from a particular viewpoint. The model must generate an image that is consistent with the depth information, which requires understanding of 3D geometry and its projection onto 2D image space. Depth maps provide complementary information to segmentation maps and edge maps, focusing on the geometric structure rather than semantic content or boundary information. The successful application of OFT to depth-to-image generation further confirms that the framework is not limited to a specific type of control signal but can be broadly applied to various forms of spatial conditioning.

The demonstration of OFT on these additional control tasks serves multiple purposes. First, it validates the generality of the OFT framework, showing that it is not specialized for a particular type of control signal but can be applied across a wide range of controllable generation scenarios. Second, it provides practical evidence that the orthogonal transformation approach effectively preserves the pretrained model's generative capabilities while learning to incorporate new types of spatial information. Third, it suggests that OFT can be a drop-in replacement for existing finetuning methods in controllable generation pipelines, offering better performance and stability without requiring task-specific modifications to the finetuning strategy.

The breadth of tasks to which OFT can be successfully applied also speaks to the fundamental soundness of the approach. By preserving the pairwise angles between neurons through orthogonal transformation, OFT maintains the structural knowledge of the pretrained model across diverse control scenarios. This structural preservation appears to be a universal requirement for effective finetuning, regardless of the specific type of control signal being used. The consistent success of OFT across all tested tasks supports the paper's central hypothesis that hyperspherical energy preservation is a crucial property for maintaining the semantic generation ability of text-to-image diffusion models during finetuning.

# Conclusions, Open Problems, and Future Directions

The development of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) represents a principled approach to adapting large text-to-image diffusion models for downstream tasks while preserving the semantic knowledge embedded in pretrained weights. Throughout this text, we have explored the theoretical motivation, mathematical formulation, efficient parameterization, and empirical validation of OFT and its constrained variant, Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT). In this concluding chapter, we synthesize the key contributions of the OFT framework, examine the open problems that remain to be addressed, and consider the broader implications of this work for the finetuning of large generative models more generally.

## Summary of Contributions

The central contribution of the OFT framework is the introduction of a finetuning method that provably preserves the hyperspherical energy of pretrained neural network weights. Unlike existing finetuning strategies—such as standard finetuning with a small learning rate or low-rank adaptation (LoRA)—which implicitly rely on the assumption that a smaller Euclidean distance between the finetuned and pretrained models implies better preservation of generative ability, OFT introduces a structural measure based on hyperspherical energy. Hyperspherical energy, defined as the sum of inverse pairwise distances between normalized neurons on the unit hypersphere, characterizes the pairwise relational structure among neurons in a layer. By constraining the finetuned weights to differ from the pretrained weights only by an orthogonal transformation, OFT guarantees that the pairwise angles between all neurons remain unchanged, and therefore the hyperspherical energy is exactly preserved.

This guarantee is not merely a mathematical curiosity. The empirical motivation for preserving hyperspherical energy comes from the observation that angular information among neurons is the primary carrier of semantic information in neural networks. The toy autoencoder experiment described in the paper demonstrated that angular information of convolutional neurons can almost perfectly recover input images, while magnitude information alone contains essentially no useful semantic content. This finding aligns with prior work showing that hyperspherical similarity encodes semantic information well, and that neural networks trained with neurons normalized onto a unit hypersphere yield comparable capacity and even better generalizability. From this perspective, OFT is designed to modify neuron directions—the key to changing visual semantics—while preserving the relational structure among neurons that encodes pretrained knowledge.

The COFT variant extends OFT by adding an explicit deviation constraint, requiring that the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ remain within an $\epsilon$-neighborhood of the identity matrix, i.e., $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq\epsilon$. This constraint is motivated by the complementary principle that smaller Euclidean deviation from the pretrained model also contributes to preserving generative performance. COFT thus combines two explicit constraints: minimal hyperspherical energy difference (achieved by orthogonality) and constrained deviation from the pretrained model (achieved by the $\epsilon$ constraint). The paper demonstrated that COFT yields even better finetuning stability than standard OFT, particularly in controlling the flexibility of the finetuning process through the hyperparameter $\epsilon$.

The empirical validation of OFT and COFT was conducted across two important downstream tasks: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. For subject-driven generation, following the DreamBooth framework, OFT and COFT were compared against DreamBooth and LoRA. The results showed that both OFT variants achieve better subject fidelity (measured by DINO and CLIP-I metrics) by a considerable margin, while maintaining comparable or better text prompt fidelity and sample diversity. Critically, OFT and COFT exhibited superior finetuning stability: after 2,000 iterations, DreamBooth began generating collapsed images and LoRA failed to preserve subject identity, while OFT and COFT maintained stable and consistent control. This insensitivity to the number of finetuning iterations is practically significant, as it alleviates the need to carefully tune iteration counts for different subjects.

For controllable generation, OFT and COFT were compared against ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA across three tasks: Canny edge to image, segmentation map to image, and landmark to face. The paper introduced a novel control consistency metric that estimates the control signal from the generated image and compares it with the original input control signal, providing an objective measure of controllability. The results demonstrated that OFT and COFT yield stronger and more accurate control than competing methods, with significantly faster convergence. For instance, in the landmark-to-face task, OFT at the 8th epoch achieved performance comparable to LoRA at 20 epochs. Furthermore, OFT demonstrated remarkable data efficiency, converging well with only 5% of the ADE20K dataset. Notably, OFT introduces no additional inference overhead, as the learned orthogonal matrix can be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix to produce an equivalent weight matrix $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, resulting in inference speed identical to the pretrained model.

## Open Problem 1: Scalability of Cayley Parameterization and Matrix Inverse

A key technical component of OFT is the parameterization of orthogonal matrices. To guarantee orthogonality in a differentiable manner, the paper adopts Cayley parameterization, which constructs the orthogonal matrix as $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. While this parameterization elegantly ensures that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal by construction, it comes with a computational cost: the matrix inverse $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ must be computed during both the forward and backward passes of training. Matrix inversion is a fundamentally expensive operation, with a time complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^3)$ for a $d\times d$ matrix, and this cost grows rapidly as the dimension of the neural network layers increases.

This scalability challenge is particularly relevant for modern text-to-image diffusion models, which often contain layers with large hidden dimensions. For example, the attention layers in Stable Diffusion v1.5 operate in spaces where $d$ can be on the order of hundreds or more. While the block-diagonal parameterization strategy—where $\bm{R}$ is decomposed into $r$ smaller orthogonal blocks of size $d/r \times d/r$—reduces the per-block inversion cost to $\mathcal{O}((d/r)^3)$, the overall computation still involves $r$ such inversions, and the matrix inverse remains a bottleneck. Moreover, the block-diagonal approach introduces a trade-off between parameter efficiency and the expressiveness of the transformation, a tension we discuss further in a subsequent section.

The paper notes that alternative orthogonalization strategies, such as the Gram-Schmidt method, are also differentiable but are even more expensive in practice. The Cayley parameterization was chosen as a practical compromise, but it does have a limitation beyond computational cost: it can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$, belonging to the special orthogonal group. While the paper reports that this limitation does not affect performance in practice, it does mean that reflections are excluded from the space of possible transformations.

The challenge of speeding up the matrix inverse in a differentiable way remains an open problem. Potential directions might include approximations to the matrix inverse, structured parameterizations that avoid explicit inversion altogether, or leveraging hardware-specific optimizations for small matrix operations. However, any approximation must be carefully validated to ensure that the orthogonality guarantee—and therefore the hyperspherical energy preservation property—is not compromised. This tension between computational efficiency and theoretical guarantees is a recurring theme in the design of finetuning methods and represents an important area for future research.

## Open Problem 2: Compositionality of Orthogonal Matrices from Multiple Tasks

One of the most intriguing properties of orthogonal matrices is their closure under multiplication: the product of two orthogonal matrices is itself an orthogonal matrix. This mathematical property suggests a unique potential for compositionality in the context of multi-task finetuning. Specifically, if one were to finetune a pretrained model on task A using OFT, producing an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}_A$, and separately finetune the same pretrained model on task B, producing $\bm{R}_B$, then the composed matrix $\bm{R}_B \cdot \bm{R}_A$ would also be orthogonal and would therefore preserve hyperspherical energy.

This raises a compelling question: whether the composition of orthogonal matrices from multiple OFT finetuning tasks could preserve the knowledge of all the downstream tasks simultaneously. In other words, could one construct a single model that incorporates the capabilities learned across multiple finetuning tasks by simply multiplying their respective orthogonal transformations? This would be a powerful capability, as it would enable a modular approach to building multi-task models without requiring joint training on all tasks at once.

The practical implications of such compositionality are significant. Consider a scenario where a user has finetuned a text-to-image diffusion model to generate images of a specific subject (e.g., a particular dog) and separately finetuned the same model to respond to spatial control signals (e.g., Canny edges). If the orthogonal matrices from these two finetuning tasks could be composed, the resulting model might be able to generate images of the specific subject following the spatial control signal, all without requiring a separate joint finetuning stage. This would greatly reduce the computational and data requirements for building multi-capable generative models.

However, several challenges and questions remain. First, it is unclear whether the semantic modifications encoded by different orthogonal transformations are compatible when composed. While the orthogonality of the product is guaranteed, the resulting transformation might not correspond to a meaningful combination of the two tasks' capabilities. The hyperspherical energy would be preserved, but the specific directions chosen by each transformation might interact in unexpected ways. Second, the order of composition might matter: $\bm{R}_B \cdot \bm{R}_A$ could yield different generative behavior than $\bm{R}_A \cdot \bm{R}_B$, since matrix multiplication is generally non-commutative. Third, the block-diagonal structure used for parameter efficiency might complicate composition, as the block structures of different tasks might not align. Whether a principled approach to composing OFT transformations can be developed, and under what conditions composition preserves task-relevant knowledge, remains an exciting direction for future investigation.

## Open Problem 3: Parameter Efficiency and Bias in Block-Diagonal Structure

The parameter efficiency of OFT is largely dependent on the block-diagonal structure of the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. By decomposing $\bm{R}$ into $r$ blocks of size $d/r \times d/r$, the number of trainable parameters is reduced from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. If the blocks are further shared—i.e., $\bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j$ for all $i \neq j$—the parameter complexity drops to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. This block-diagonal parameterization is crucial for making OFT practical, as a full unconstrained orthogonal matrix of size $d \times d$ would require $d(d-1)/2$ parameters, which can be prohibitively large for the high-dimensional layers characteristic of modern diffusion models.

However, the block-diagonal structure introduces an important limitation: it restricts the space of possible orthogonal transformations. A block-diagonal matrix can only rotate or reflect neurons within their respective blocks; it cannot mix or transform neurons across different blocks. This means that if the neuron dimension $d$ is divided into $r$ blocks of size $d/r$, the transformation applied to any neuron in a given block is independent of the neurons in other blocks. While this reduces the number of parameters, it also limits the flexibility of the finetuning process. The paper notes that this structure has an interesting interpretation in convolution layers—when the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, each block transforms a unique neuron channel, similar to depth-wise convolution—but it remains a constraint that may not always be desirable.

The bias introduced by the block-diagonal structure is a subtle but important concern. By preventing cross-block interactions, the block-diagonal parameterization implicitly assumes that the most important transformations for finetuning can be decomposed into independent sub-transformations within blocks. This assumption may not hold in general, particularly for tasks that require coordinated changes across many neuron dimensions. For instance, if a downstream task requires adjusting the relationship between neurons that happen to fall in different blocks, the block-diagonal structure would prevent the model from making such adjustments. The paper acknowledges that the performance of OFT had not yet saturated with respect to the number of trainable parameters, suggesting that increasing the block size (i.e., decreasing $r$) could improve performance at the cost of more parameters. However, this comes at the expense of the computational challenges associated with larger matrix inversions, as discussed in the first open problem.

Finding a more effective and less biased way to improve parameter efficiency remains an important open problem. Ideally, one would like a parameterization that achieves the computational efficiency of the block-diagonal approach while allowing for richer interactions across the full neuron dimension. Possible directions might include structured sparse orthogonal matrices, hierarchical block structures that allow limited cross-block interactions, or learned parameterizations that adaptively determine which neuron dimensions should be coupled. The fundamental tension here is between parameter efficiency, computational tractability, and the expressiveness of the transformation space, and resolving this tension in a principled manner is a key challenge for future work.

## Broader Implications for Finetuning Large Generative Models

The OFT framework carries several broader implications for the practice of finetuning large generative models that extend beyond the specific tasks of subject-driven and controllable generation explored in the paper. At its core, OFT embodies a shift in perspective from Euclidean to structural measures of model preservation. Existing finetuning methods have predominantly relied on the implicit assumption that a smaller Euclidean distance between the finetuned and pretrained models translates to better preservation of pretrained capabilities. While this assumption is not without merit, it fails to capture the relational structure among neurons that encodes semantic knowledge. OFT demonstrates that by explicitly preserving a structural property—hyperspherical energy—one can achieve superior finetuning stability and generative performance, even with fewer trainable parameters and faster convergence.

This structural perspective suggests that the design of finetuning methods should consider not only how close the finetuned model is to the pretrained model in a normative sense, but also how the internal relationships among model parameters are preserved or modified. The success of OFT in preserving pairwise neuron angles while still allowing sufficient flexibility for downstream adaptation hints at a more general principle: effective finetuning methods should identify the structural properties that are most important for preserving the pretrained model's capabilities and constrain the finetuning process to preserve those properties. For text-to-image diffusion models, the paper provides evidence that the angular structure of neurons is such a property, but for other types of models—such as large language models or audio generation models—the critical structural properties might be different and would need to be identified through similar empirical and theoretical investigations.

The trade-off between flexibility and regularity in finetuning, highlighted by the paper, is another broadly relevant insight. Standard finetuning, which updates model weights with minimal constraints, offers maximum flexibility but is prone to instability and model collapse, as demonstrated by the DreamBooth results showing image collapse after 2,000 iterations. On the other end of the spectrum, overly rigid constraints would prevent the model from adapting to the downstream task at all. OFT's approach of preserving pairwise neuron angles through orthogonal transformation represents a well-calibrated balance point: it is flexible enough to modify neuron directions for downstream adaptation, yet regular enough to prevent the destruction of pretrained knowledge. The COFT variant further refines this balance by introducing an explicit deviation constraint, allowing practitioners to tune the flexibility-regularity trade-off through a single hyperparameter $\epsilon$.

The observation that OFT converges significantly faster than competing methods also has practical implications. Faster convergence reduces the computational cost of finetuning, which is particularly important for large diffusion models that are expensive to train. The paper's finding that OFT can converge well with only 5% of the ADE20K dataset for controllable generation suggests that the structural preservation provided by OFT enables more efficient learning from limited data. This data efficiency is especially valuable in real-world applications where training data for downstream tasks may be scarce. Moreover, the stability of OFT's training dynamics—characterized by smooth and predictable convergence—makes the method easier to use in practice, as it reduces the need for extensive hyperparameter tuning. This stands in contrast to the sudden convergence phenomenon observed in ControlNet, where controllability emerges abruptly after a certain number of epochs, making it difficult to predict when training should be stopped.

Finally, the property that OFT introduces no additional inference overhead is of considerable practical importance. Unlike adapter-based methods such as ControlNet, which introduce additional network components that must be evaluated at inference time, OFT's learned orthogonal matrix can be absorbed into the pretrained weight matrix, resulting in a model with the same architecture and inference cost as the original. This makes OFT-finetuned models immediately deployable in production systems without any modifications to the inference pipeline, a significant advantage for real-world applications where inference latency and computational cost are critical concerns.

## Potential Applications Beyond Text-to-Image Generation

While the paper focuses on text-to-image diffusion models, the OFT framework is inherently model-agnostic and can, in principle, be applied to any neural network that benefits from finetuning. The core idea—preserving the pairwise angular structure among neurons through orthogonal transformation—does not depend on the specific architecture or modality of the model, making OFT a potentially valuable tool across a wide range of domains.

One natural extension is to other types of generative models. Text-to-video diffusion models, which generate video sequences from text prompts, share the same fundamental architecture as text-to-image diffusion models but operate in a higher-dimensional space. The challenges of finetuning these models—for example, to generate videos of specific subjects or to follow spatial-temporal control signals—are analogous to those addressed in the paper. OFT's guarantee of hyperspherical energy preservation could be equally valuable in this context, ensuring that the pretrained model's ability to generate temporally coherent and visually realistic video is not destroyed during finetuning. Similarly, audio generation models, such as those that produce music or speech from text descriptions, could benefit from OFT's structured finetuning approach. The semantic information encoded in neuron directions is not specific to visual data, and the principle of preserving angular structure during finetuning should generalize.

Beyond diffusion models, OFT could be applied to other generative architectures. Generative adversarial networks (GANs), which have been extensively studied for text-to-image generation, consist of generator and discriminator networks that could be finetuned using orthogonal transformations. Autoregressive models, such as those used for text generation and, more recently, for image generation in a token-based framework, represent another potential application domain. In the context of large language models (LLMs), finetuning for downstream tasks such as instruction following, code generation, or domain adaptation is a widespread practice, and the structural preservation offered by OFT could help mitigate the catastrophic forgetting that often accompanies standard finetuning of these models.

The paper also notes that OFT is well suited for finetuning convolution layers, with the block-diagonal structure of $\bm{R}$ having interesting interpretations. When the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, each block transforms a unique neuron channel, similar to learning depth-wise convolution kernels. When all blocks are shared, OFT applies a single orthogonal transformation across all channels. This versatility suggests that OFT could be applied to a wide range of computer vision architectures beyond diffusion models, including classification networks, segmentation models, and detection systems, wherever finetuning of convolutional layers is needed.

The compositional property of orthogonal matrices, discussed as an open problem, also hints at applications in multi-modal and multi-task learning. If the orthogonal transformations from different finetuning tasks can be composed meaningfully, OFT could enable the construction of models that simultaneously possess multiple capabilities without requiring joint training. This could be particularly valuable in scenarios where a single model needs to serve multiple purposes—for example, a text-to-image model that can simultaneously follow different types of control signals (edges, segmentation maps, depth maps) or generate images of multiple different subjects. The modular nature of OFT finetuning, where each task produces a separate orthogonal matrix, could make it feasible to build such multi-capable models incrementally, adding new capabilities as needed without retraining from scratch.

In conclusion, the OFT framework represents a principled and empirically validated approach to finetuning large generative models, with contributions that span theory, method, and application. While several open problems remain—particularly regarding the scalability of the Cayley parameterization, the compositionality of orthogonal matrices, and the bias introduced by block-diagonal parameterization—the framework's success in preserving pretrained knowledge while enabling effective downstream adaptation suggests a promising direction for the broader field of model finetuning. As large generative models continue to grow in scale and capability, the need for finetuning methods that are both efficient and structurally informed will only become more pressing, and the principles underlying OFT—preserving critical structural properties, balancing flexibility with regularity, and avoiding inference overhead—will likely remain central to meeting this need.
