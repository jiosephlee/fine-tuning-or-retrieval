\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Chapter 8: Related Work and Historical Context

The development of the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) did not emerge in a vacuum. Rather, it represents the culmination of years of research aimed at building language models that operate directly on raw bytes or characters, thereby sidestepping the limitations of fixed-vocabulary tokenization. To understand the significance of BLT's contributions, it is essential to trace the historical trajectory of byte- and character-level language modeling, from the earliest recurrent neural network approaches to the modern patching-based transformer architectures that paved the way for BLT. This chapter surveys that landscape, highlighting the motivations, successes, and limitations of each generation of approaches, and ultimately situating BLT as a synthesis and extension of these prior efforts.

#

## Character-Level RNNs: Early Motivations and Advantages on Morphologically Rich Languages

Character-level language modeling has been a vibrant area of research since the early days of neural language models. The fundamental motivation was elegantly simple: by modeling text at the character (or byte) level, a model could organically handle out-of-vocabulary words without resorting to back-off methods or special tokens. This was particularly valuable for languages with rich morphology, where the number of distinct word forms can be extremely large due to productive affixation, compounding, and other morphological processes. In such languages, a word-level model would necessarily encounter many rare or unseen word forms, whereas a character-level model could decompose any word into its constituent characters and leverage shared sub-word patterns.

Early work by Sutskever, Mikolov, and others demonstrated the viability of character-level recurrent neural networks (RNNs) for language modeling. These models processed text one character at a time, maintaining a hidden state that accumulated information across the sequence. Graves extended this line of work with generative models that could produce coherent text character by character, showcasing the creative potential of character-level modeling. A significant advance came from Kim et al., who trained a model that processed characters on the input side using convolutional and highway networks, which then fed into LSTM-based RNNs. This architecture was able to match the performance of the then-state-of-the-art RNN-based language models on English and, crucially, outperform them on morphologically rich languages, validating the long-held hypothesis that character-level representations provide a natural advantage when word forms are highly variable.

The advantages of character-level modeling extended beyond language modeling proper. Kenter and Zweig applied byte-level LSTM models to machine comprehension tasks and found that they outperformed word-level models on morphologically rich Turkish and Russian, further underscoring the utility of fine-grained input representations for understanding languages with complex morphology. Along similar lines, Zhang et al. used character-based convolutional models for classification tasks and found that they outperformed word-level models on certain tasks, suggesting that the benefits of character-level modeling were not confined to language modeling alone. Chung et al. further advanced the field with hierarchical LSTM models that used boundary detectors at each level to discover latent hierarchical structure in text, improving performance on character-level language modeling by explicitly modeling the multi-level nature of language. ByteNet, developed by Kalchbrenner et al., took a different architectural approach, using CNN-based layers on characters rather than attention for machine translation, demonstrating that convolutional architectures could also be effectively applied at the character level.

Despite these successes, character-level RNNs and convolutional models faced fundamental limitations in scalability. The sequential nature of RNNs made parallelization difficult, and the sheer length of character sequences meant that training on large datasets was slow. These limitations would only be partially addressed by the advent of the transformer architecture, which, as we shall see, brought its own set of trade-offs to the character-level setting.

## Character-Level Transformers: Challenges of Scaling and the Gap from Word-Level Models

The introduction of the transformer architecture by Vaswani et al., with its self-attention mechanism enabling parallel computation over sequences, marked a paradigm shift in natural language processing. When combined with subword tokenization techniques such as Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE), as introduced by Sennrich et al., transformers achieved dramatic improvements on language modeling and benchmark tasks. However, the success of word- and subword-level transformers also implicitly defined an inductive bias about the level of abstraction at which language models should operate, raising the question of whether character-level transformers could match the performance of their subword-level counterparts.

Al-Rfou et al. took up this challenge by training very deep transformers directly on character sequences. To facilitate training of these deep models, they employed auxiliary losses, and their models were able to outperform previous LSTM-based character language models. However, a significant gap remained between character-level and word-level language models in terms of performance, indicating that the transformer architecture alone was not sufficient to close the gap at the character level. This gap was further confirmed by GPT-2, which observed that on large-scale datasets such as the one-billion-word benchmark, byte-level language models were not competitive with word-level models, suggesting that the challenge of scaling character-level models was not merely a matter of architectural capacity but also of computational efficiency.

Choe and Choe demonstrated an important proof of concept: byte-level language models based on transformers could in fact outperform subword-level language models with comparable parameters. However, this came at a significant cost—the models required much more compute and took much longer to train. This tension between performance and efficiency would become a central theme in the development of byte-level models. El-Bayeh et al. trained a BERT model called CharFormer that built word representations by applying convolutions on character embeddings, demonstrating improvements in the medical domain, but again at the cost of substantially more compute. Clark et al. developed CANINE, a 150-million-parameter encoder-only model that operated directly on character sequences, using a deep transformer stack at its core (similar in spirit to BLT's global model) combined with a local transformer and strided convolutions to downsample input characters. CANINE outperformed the equivalent token-level encoder-only model (mBERT) on downstream multilingual tasks, providing further evidence that character-level models could achieve strong performance when equipped with appropriate architectural mechanisms for managing sequence length.

A key insight that emerged from this body of work was that directly modeling bytes or characters, rather than subword units, inevitably increases the sequence length of the input. For a transformer, longer sequences mean more expensive attention computations, scaling quadratically with sequence length. This made it challenging to efficiently scale byte-level transformer models to the same data and parameter regimes as token-based models. As BLT's authors would later observe, prior works attempted to mitigate this cost through more efficient self-attention mechanisms or attention-free architectures, but these approaches primarily helped train small models. At scale, the computational cost of a transformer is dominated by the large feed-forward network layers that run on every byte, not the cost of the attention mechanism itself—a crucial observation that would motivate the patching-based approaches discussed in subsequent sections.

## Byte-Level Models Without Patching: ByT5, CANINE, CharFormer, and MambaByte

Several notable approaches attempted to build byte-level models without any form of patching, instead processing every byte directly through the full model. While these models demonstrated impressive capabilities in terms of robustness and character-level understanding, they all suffered from the fundamental inefficiency of processing long byte sequences, which made scaling to large model and data sizes prohibitively expensive.

CANINE, developed by Clark et al., was a 150-million-parameter encoder-only model that operated directly on character sequences. Its architecture featured a deep transformer stack at its core, conceptually similar to BLT's global model, combined with a local transformer and strided convolutions to downsample the input characters. By downsampling characters before applying the full transformer, CANINE partially addressed the sequence length problem, though it did not employ dynamic patching and remained limited to encoder-only tasks. CANINE's success in outperforming mBERT on downstream multilingual tasks demonstrated the potential of character-level processing, but its relatively small scale and encoder-only design left open the question of whether such approaches could scale to decoder-based generative models at the scale of modern large language models.

CharFormer, introduced by El-Bayeh et al., took a different approach to building word representations from characters. Rather than using attention to pool character representations, CharFormer applied convolutions on character embeddings to construct higher-level representations. While this approach demonstrated improvements in the medical domain—where domain-specific terminology and out-of-vocabulary words are common—it also expended substantially more compute than token-based models, limiting its scalability.

ByT5, developed by Xue et al., was perhaps the most comprehensive exploration of byte-level encoder-decoder models without patching. ByT5 operated directly on byte sequences without any form of grouping or downsampling, applying full transformer attention over every byte. The model exhibited improved robustness to noise and was competitive with tokenizer-based models when trained on four times less data. However, the lack of patching meant that the model needed to compute expensive attention operations over every byte, making it extremely compute-heavy. ByT5's findings were important in demonstrating the robustness advantages of byte-level modeling, but its computational inefficiency precluded scaling to the levels of modern large language models. The authors of BLT would later cite the prohibitively costly nature of directly training on bytes at scale as a key motivation for their patching-based approach.

More recently, Wang et al. developed MambaByte, which applied the Mamba architecture—a state-space model capable of maintaining a fixed-size memory state over very large context lengths—to byte-level language modeling. Unlike transformer-based approaches, MambaByte did not use patching and instead processed every byte through the Mamba architecture. At the 350-million-parameter scale, MambaByte was able to outperform byte-level transformer models in a flop-controlled setting in terms of bits-per-byte on several datasets. This result was significant because it demonstrated that attention-free architectures could offer a path to efficient byte-level modeling. However, as BLT's authors noted, the computational savings from attention-free architectures primarily benefit small models. At scale, the dominant cost is the feed-forward network layers applied at every byte, which patching-based approaches address by reducing the number of times these expensive layers are invoked.

Together, these approaches demonstrated clear advantages of byte-level modeling—robustness to noise, character-level understanding, and improved handling of morphologically rich and low-resource languages—but they also highlighted the fundamental scalability challenge that patching-based approaches would seek to address.

## Patching-Based Approaches: Hourglass Transformer, Dynamic Patching, and Arithmetic Coding

The core idea behind patching is to group sequences of bytes into larger units called patches, which then serve as the primary units of computation for the main transformer model. By reducing the number of steps at which the expensive global transformer operates, patching can dramatically reduce computational cost while potentially retaining the benefits of byte-level modeling. Several approaches explored this idea with varying degrees of sophistication.

The Hourglass Transformer, developed by Nawrot et al., experimented with static patching-based downsampling and upsampling. The architecture featured a transformer that first processed individual bytes, then downsampled them into patches by grouping fixed numbers of adjacent bytes, applied a deeper transformer stack on the patch representations, and finally upsampled back to the byte level. This hierarchical structure, resembling the shape of an hourglass, allowed the model to capture both fine-grained byte-level information and higher-level patterns at the patch level. The Hourglass Transformer outperformed other byte-level baselines at the 150-million-parameter scale, demonstrating the viability of patching as a mechanism for efficient byte-level modeling.

Nawrot et al. subsequently extended this work with dynamic patching schemes, moving beyond fixed-size patches to context-dependent groupings. They explored several approaches to determining patch boundaries, including a boundary predictor that was learned in an end-to-end fashion, a boundary predictor supervised using certain tokenizers, and an entropy-based patching model similar to the approach BLT would later adopt. The entropy-based approach used the entropy of next-byte predictions from a small language model to identify points of high uncertainty, placing patch boundaries at these points. This approach was shown to outperform vanilla transformers on language modeling tasks at the 40-million-parameter scale on approximately 400 million tokens. This work was particularly significant for BLT, as it introduced the concept of entropy-based patching that BLT would adopt and scale to models of up to 8 billion parameters and 4 trillion training bytes.

Lester et al. took a different approach, investigating training on sequences compressed using arithmetic coding. Arithmetic coding is a form of entropy coding that can achieve compression rates beyond what BPE can offer, as it is not constrained by a fixed vocabulary. They used an equal-info windows technique to segment the compressed stream into windows of approximately equal information content, and were able to outperform byte-level baselines on language modeling tasks. However, their approach underperformed subword baselines, suggesting that compression-based approaches, while innovative, had not yet reached the level of performance needed to compete with established tokenization methods.

A common theme across these patching-based approaches was the recognition that not all bytes require the same amount of computational effort. Some bytes, such as the continuation of a common word, are highly predictable and require little computation, while others, such as the first byte of a new word or a character in a complex mathematical expression, are much less predictable and benefit from additional computational resources. The patching approaches differed in how they identified these boundaries—static methods used fixed positions, dynamic methods used learned predictors, and entropy-based methods used information-theoretic measures—but all shared the goal of reducing the number of expensive global transformer steps while maintaining model quality.

## MegaByte: Fixed Static Patching and Concatenation, and Its Limitations at Scale

MegaByte, introduced by Yu et al., was a decoder-only causal language model that represented a significant step forward in patching-based byte-level modeling. The architecture used fixed static patching, grouping bytes into patches of a predetermined size $k$, and employed concatenation of byte representations to convert them into patch representations. A local model on the decoder side was then used to convert patch representations back into individual bytes. The authors demonstrated that MegaByte could match tokenizer-based models at the 1-billion-parameter scale on a dataset of approximately 400 billion bytes, a notable achievement that suggested patching-based approaches could be competitive with tokenization-based methods.

The MegaByte architecture was conceptually similar to BLT in its use of a global model operating on patches and a local model for byte-level processing. However, it differed in several crucial respects. First, the patching was fixed and static, with bytes grouped into patches of a constant size $k$. As BLT's authors would later observe, this strided patching approach comes with significant downsides: compute is not dynamically allocated to where it is needed most, and it leads to inconsistent and non-contextual patching of similar byte sequences, such as the same word being split differently depending on its position. Second, MegaByte did not include a local encoder model; the patch representations were formed by simple concatenation of byte embeddings, without the benefit of learned encoding or cross-attention mechanisms. Third, MegaByte was evaluated at a scale and in a compute regime that did not fully reflect the compute-optimal training setups used by state-of-the-art token-based models such as Llama 3.

When BLT's authors ablated MegaByte in their experiments, they found that static patching lagged behind current state-of-the-art compute-optimally trained tokenizer-based models in a flop-controlled setting. Specifically, in scaling law experiments, they approximated SpaceByte using BLT's architecture with space patching but without n-gram embeddings and cross-attention, and found that while SpaceByte improved over MegaByte, it remained far from Llama 3 performance. This finding highlighted that simply adopting a patching-based approach was not sufficient to match the performance of modern token-based models—architectural innovations and dynamic patching were needed to bridge the gap.

The limitations of MegaByte at scale can be attributed to several factors. The fixed patching scheme fails to account for the varying information density of different parts of the input, wasting computation on predictable bytes while potentially under-allocating compute for information-dense regions. The lack of a learned encoder means that the patch representations are less expressive than they could be, as they do not benefit from the kind of cross-attention-based pooling that BLT employs. Furthermore, the concatenation approach to forming patch representations does not provide a mechanism for the model to learn how to best aggregate byte-level information into patch-level representations, a capability that BLT's cross-attention modules are specifically designed to enable.

## SpaceByte: Whitespace-Based Patching with a Local Encoder Model

SpaceByte, developed by Slagle and Listerman, addressed some of the limitations of MegaByte by introducing a simple yet effective improvement: rather than using fixed-size patches, SpaceByte created new patches after any space-like bytes, which serve as natural boundaries for linguistic units in many languages. Space-like bytes were defined as any byte that is not a Latin character, digit, or UTF-8 continuation byte, with the additional constraint that each patch must contain at least one non-space-like byte. This ensured that words were patched in the same way across sequences and that computational effort was allocated for hard predictions, which often follow spaces.

The key insight behind SpaceByte was that predicting the first byte of a word is typically much harder than predicting the remaining bytes. For example, predicting the first byte of the answer to the question "Who composed the Magic Flute?" is much harder than predicting the remaining bytes once the first character "M" is known, since the first character significantly reduces the number of likely choices, making the completion "Mozart" comparatively easy. By placing patch boundaries at word boundaries, SpaceByte ensured that the global transformer was invoked precisely at the points where its computational power was most needed.

SpaceByte also introduced a local encoder model, a lightweight transformer that processed byte-level information before it was fed to the global model. This was a significant architectural improvement over MegaByte, which relied on simple concatenation. The local encoder allowed the model to build more expressive byte representations before they were pooled into patch representations, though it did not employ the cross-attention mechanism that BLT would later introduce.

Slagle and Listerman found that SpaceByte improved over MegaByte and, in some domains such as GitHub and arXiv, even outperformed tokenized-based transformer models in a compute-controlled setting at the 1-billion-parameter scale. This was an encouraging result, suggesting that dynamic patching based on linguistic boundaries could be a viable path to competitive byte-level modeling. However, SpaceByte had its own limitations. Most fundamentally, whitespace-based patching cannot gracefully handle all languages and domains. Many languages do not use spaces between words, and in domains such as code or mathematics, whitespace does not consistently align with boundaries between linguistic or semantic units. Moreover, space patching cannot vary the patch size—the patch size is determined by the length of words in the input, with no mechanism for controlling the average patch size or adapting it to different computational budgets.

When BLT's authors evaluated SpaceByte in their experiments, they found that while it improved over MegaByte, it remained far from the performance of Llama 3. In their downstream task evaluations, the BLT-Space variant underperformed the Llama 3 tokenizer-based model on all but one task. However, it did achieve a significant reduction in inference flops due to its larger average patch size of 6 bytes, compared to approximately 4.5 bytes for BPE and entropy-patching-based models. This highlighted a key trade-off: space patching could achieve computational efficiency but at the cost of task performance, suggesting that more sophisticated patching methods were needed to achieve both efficiency and quality simultaneously.

## How BLT Advances Beyond Prior Patching-Based and Byte-Level Work

The Byte Latent Transformer represents a convergence of the insights and innovations from prior byte-level and patching-based approaches, combined with several novel architectural and methodological advances that enable it to match the performance of state-of-the-art tokenization-based models at scale for the first time. BLT's contributions can be understood as addressing the specific limitations of each prior approach while introducing new capabilities that were not previously available.

The most fundamental advance of BLT over prior patching-based approaches is its use of entropy-based dynamic patching. Unlike MegaByte's fixed static patching, which allocates the same amount of compute to every patch regardless of its complexity, and unlike SpaceByte's whitespace-based patching, which is limited to languages and domains where spaces serve as natural boundaries, BLT's entropy patching uses a data-driven approach to identify points of high uncertainty in the next-byte prediction. A small byte-level auto-regressive language model is trained on the training data, and next-byte entropies are computed under this model's distribution. Patch boundaries are then placed at points where the entropy exceeds a threshold, ensuring that the global transformer is invoked precisely where the data complexity demands it. This approach generalizes across all languages and domains, as it does not rely on any language-specific heuristics, and it provides a natural mechanism for controlling the average patch size by adjusting the entropy threshold.

BLT also introduces significant architectural innovations over prior approaches. Unlike MegaByte, which lacks a learned encoder and relies on concatenation, BLT employs a lightweight local encoder model with cross-attention layers that efficiently map sequences of bytes into expressive patch representations. The encoder uses hash n-gram embeddings to incorporate information about preceding bytes, allowing the model to capture local context that would be lost in a simple pooling operation. The cross-attention module, inspired by the Perceiver architecture but adapted for variable-length patch representations, allows each patch representation to attend specifically to the bytes that constitute it, ensuring that the patch representation captures the relevant byte-level information. Similarly, the local decoder uses cross-attention in the reverse direction, with byte representations as queries attending to patch representations as keys and values, allowing the model to effectively decode patch-level information back into individual bytes.

A crucial architectural insight in BLT is the separation of the model into three components: a large global latent transformer that operates on patch representations and consumes the bulk of the computational budget, and two small local models (encoder and decoder) that handle byte-level processing. This separation allows the model to dynamically allocate compute by choosing when to invoke the expensive global transformer. The local models, being much smaller, can afford to process every byte without incurring prohibitive computational cost. This design directly addresses the observation that at scale, the cost of a transformer is dominated by feed-forward network layers, not attention, and that prior approaches to efficient byte-level modeling focused primarily on reducing attention costs rather than reducing the number of times the expensive feed-forward layers are invoked.

BLT also breaks free of the efficiency trade-offs that constrain tokenization-based models. In standard LLMs, increasing the vocabulary size means larger tokens on average (and therefore fewer model steps) but also a larger output dimension for the final projection layer and embedding table. For example, Llama 3 increases the average token size from 3.7 to 4.4 bytes at the cost of increasing the size of its embedding table fourfold compared to Llama 2. This trade-off leaves little room for tokenization-based approaches to achieve significant variations in token size and inference cost. BLT, by contrast, can arbitrarily increase the patch size without increasing the vocabulary, because patches do not correspond to a fixed vocabulary. This unlocks a new dimension for scaling: model size and patch size can be increased simultaneously while maintaining the same inference flop budget. Longer patch sizes save compute, which can be reallocated to grow the global latent transformer, as it is run less often. BLT's fixed inference scaling experiments demonstrated that this approach yields significantly better scaling trends than tokenization-based architectures.

The empirical results of BLT at scale represent a milestone for byte-level modeling. Prior byte-level models either achieved competitive performance only at small scales or were prohibitively expensive to scale up. BLT, for the first time, matched the training flop-controlled performance of Llama 3 at scales up to 8 billion parameters and 4 trillion training bytes, while using up to 50% fewer flops at inference. In downstream task evaluations, BLT-Entropy outperformed the Llama 3 model on 4 out of 7 tasks while being trained on the same number of bytes. Beyond performance, BLT demonstrated significant qualitative improvements over tokenization-based models, including robustness to input noise (outperforming Llama 3 by an average of 8 points on noised HellaSwag), character-level understanding (outperforming BPE Llama 3 models by more than 25 points on the CUTE benchmark), phonology (outperforming Llama 3 on grapheme-to-phoneme conversion), and low-resource machine translation (achieving a 2-point overall advantage in translating into English on the FLORES-101 benchmark).

BLT also demonstrated the ability to leverage existing pre-trained tokenizer-based models, opening a path for "byte-ifying" models like Llama 3.1 that have been trained on more than 15 trillion tokens. By initializing the global transformer parameters of BLT with those of a pre-trained Llama 3.1 model and updating them at a reduced learning rate while training the local encoder and decoder from scratch, BLT from Llama 3.1 significantly outperformed both Llama 3 and BLT baselines trained with the same number of flops. This approach suggests a practical path to combining the benefits of byte-level modeling with the vast investments that have been made in training token-based models.

In summary, BLT advances beyond prior work by combining entropy-based dynamic patching for compute allocation, cross-attention-based encoder and decoder architectures for expressive byte-to-patch and patch-to-byte mappings, hash n-gram embeddings for local context modeling, and the ability to scale model and patch size simultaneously within a fixed inference budget. These innovations, grounded in the insights and lessons of a decade of research on character- and byte-level language modeling, enable BLT to achieve what no prior byte-level model has achieved: matching the performance of state-of-the-art tokenization-based models at scale, with significant improvements in efficiency, robustness, and character-level understanding.
