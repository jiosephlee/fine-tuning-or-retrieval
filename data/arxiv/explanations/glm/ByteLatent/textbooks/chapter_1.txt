\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Chapter 1: Introduction: The Problem with Tokenization and the BLT Paradigm

Modern large language models have achieved remarkable success across a wide range of tasks, from natural language understanding to code generation and reasoning. Yet despite the end-to-end training paradigm that characterizes contemporary LLM development, there remains one critical component that is not learned end-to-end: tokenization. Tokenization is a heuristic pre-processing step that groups raw bytes into a static set of tokens before the model ever sees the data. While this step has long been considered essential for practical scalability, it introduces a set of limitations that grow more consequential as models are deployed in increasingly diverse and multilingual settings. The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) represents a fundamentally different approach: a tokenizer-free architecture that learns directly from raw byte data and dynamically allocates computational resources based on the complexity of the data being processed. This chapter introduces the motivation behind BLT, the shortcomings of tokenization it seeks to overcome, and the high-level design principles that enable it to match the performance of tokenization-based models like Llama 3 at scale while offering significant improvements in efficiency and robustness.

#

## The Role of Tokenization in Existing LLMs and Its Heuristic Nature

To understand why BLT represents a paradigm shift, it is essential to first understand what tokenization does and why it has been so widely adopted. At the lowest level, all digital text is represented as a sequence of bytes. A byte is an 8-bit value, and there are only $256$ possible byte values. This means that any text—whether English prose, Python code, or Unicode characters encoded in UTF-8—can ultimately be reduced to a sequence of integers in the range $\{0, 1, \ldots, 255\}$. In principle, a language model could be trained directly on these byte sequences without any pre-processing.

However, training transformers directly on bytes has historically been prohibitively expensive. The reason is computational: the cost of a transformer scales with the sequence length it must process. A typical English sentence might contain dozens of bytes, and a long document could contain tens of thousands. If the transformer must run its full computation on every single byte, the cost becomes overwhelming, particularly because the feed-forward network layers, which dominate the computational budget of large transformers, are applied at every position in the sequence.

Tokenization solves this problem by grouping bytes into larger units called tokens before the model processes them. For example, the word "transformer" might be a single token, or it might be split into two tokens such as "trans" and "former." The set of possible tokens is determined by a fixed vocabulary that is constructed before training begins, typically using algorithms like Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE). With tokenization, the sequence length is dramatically reduced: instead of processing every byte, the transformer processes one token per several bytes, with the average token size being roughly 3.7 bytes for Llama 2 and 4.4 bytes for Llama 3.

The crucial point is that this grouping is heuristic. The tokenizer is not learned as part of the model's training process; it is determined by a separate algorithm that operates on the training data in advance. This means that the way a string is compressed into tokens is fixed and static, regardless of the context in which the string appears. The tokenizer does not know which parts of the input are easy to predict and which are hard. It treats every token as requiring the same amount of computation, even though the actual complexity of predicting different parts of the sequence varies enormously. This uniform allocation of compute is a fundamental inefficiency: a large transformer is not needed to predict the ending of most words, since these are comparatively easy, low-entropy decisions, but the tokenizer gives these easy predictions the same computational budget as the difficult decision of choosing the first word of a new sentence.

## Limitations of Fixed-Vocabulary Tokenization

The heuristic nature of tokenization leads to several well-documented shortcomings that motivate the development of a tokenizer-free alternative. The paper identifies four major categories of limitations: domain and modality sensitivity, sensitivity to input noise, lack of orthographic knowledge, and multilingual inequity.

Domain and modality sensitivity arises because tokenizers are typically optimized for a particular distribution of data. A tokenizer trained primarily on English web text will produce different token boundaries when applied to code, mathematical notation, or less common languages. This means that the compression efficiency of the tokenizer—and therefore the effective sequence length and computational cost—varies across domains in ways that are not under the model's control. A string that is efficiently compressed in one domain may be poorly compressed in another, leading to inconsistent treatment of similar content.

Sensitivity to input noise is a related but distinct problem. Because tokenizers operate on fixed rules applied before the model sees the data, they cannot adapt to small perturbations in the input. For example, if a character is dropped, repeated, or changed in case, the tokenizer may produce a completely different sequence of tokens, even though the semantic content of the text has barely changed. This makes tokenization-based models fragile when confronted with noisy inputs, which are common in real-world applications.

The lack of orthographic knowledge is perhaps the most subtle limitation. When a tokenizer groups bytes into a token, the model receives only the token's identity as input; it does not have direct access to the individual bytes that compose the token. This means that the model must separately learn the spelling of each token, and it has no built-in mechanism for manipulating characters within a token. Tasks that require character-level understanding—such as spelling, phonology, or simple character manipulation—become surprisingly difficult for tokenization-based models, even very large ones. The model may appear to know the spelling of common words, but it struggles to use this knowledge effectively for character-level tasks.

Multilingual inequity is a consequence of the fact that tokenizers are typically optimized for the dominant language in the training data, which is usually English. This means that English text is compressed more efficiently than text in other languages, particularly lower-resource languages with different scripts. A sentence in English might be represented by fewer tokens than a sentence of equivalent length in, say, Sinhala or Amharic. This not only makes multilingual training less efficient but also means that the model effectively has less computational capacity allocated to non-English content, creating a systematic bias.

## Why Directly Training on Bytes Has Been Prohibitively Expensive at Scale

Given the limitations of tokenization, one might ask why the field has not simply trained models directly on bytes. The answer is computational cost. The paper explains that directly training LLMs on bytes is prohibitively costly at scale due to the long sequence lengths involved. Since there are only $256$ possible byte values, representing text at the byte level requires far more positions than representing it at the token level, where the vocabulary might contain $128{,}000$ tokens (as in Llama 3).

Prior work has attempted to mitigate this cost through various strategies. Some approaches use more efficient self-attention mechanisms, such as strided convolutions or downsampling, to reduce the effective sequence length. Others employ attention-free architectures, such as the Mamba-based MambaByte model, which can maintain a fixed-size memory state over very large context lengths. However, the paper notes that these approaches primarily help train small models. At scale, the computational cost of a transformer is dominated not by the attention mechanism but by the large feed-forward network layers that run on every position in the sequence. This means that even if attention is made more efficient, the cost of applying the feed-forward network to every byte remains overwhelming.

This observation is critical because it reframes the problem. The issue is not merely that attention is expensive on long sequences; it is that the entire transformer computation—including the feed-forward layers, which constitute the bulk of the parameters and flops—is applied at every position. Reducing the number of positions at which the expensive computation is performed is therefore the key to making byte-level models feasible at scale. This is precisely what BLT's patching mechanism accomplishes: it reduces the number of positions at which the large, expensive transformer operates, while still processing every byte through smaller, cheaper models.

## The Core BLT Idea: Dynamic Patching Based on Next-Byte Entropy

The central innovation of BLT is the concept of dynamic patching based on the entropy of next-byte predictions. Instead of grouping bytes into fixed tokens determined by a heuristic pre-processing step, BLT groups bytes into patches whose boundaries are determined by the information content of the data. The key insight is that not all bytes are equally difficult to predict, and therefore not all bytes should receive the same amount of computational effort.

To understand this idea, consider the process of generating text one byte at a time. At each position, there is some distribution over the possible next bytes. If the next byte is highly predictable—say, the ending of a common word like "the"—then the entropy of the next-byte distribution is low, and little computation is needed to make the prediction. If the next byte is highly unpredictable—say, the first character of an answer to a question—then the entropy is high, and more computation is warranted.

BLT operationalizes this insight by training a small byte-level language model to estimate the entropy of the next-byte prediction at each position. The entropy $H(x_i)$ at position $i$ is computed as:

$$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i = v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i = v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i})$$

where $\mathcal{V}$ is the byte vocabulary (the set of $256$ possible byte values), $p_{e}$ is the distribution learned by the small entropy model, and $\mathbf{x}_{<i}$ is the sequence of preceding bytes. When this entropy exceeds a threshold, a new patch boundary is created. This means that patches are formed at points of high uncertainty—exactly the points where the model would benefit from additional computation.

The paper explores two specific methods for identifying patch boundaries from the entropy estimates. The first, called the global constraint, simply places a boundary whenever the entropy exceeds a global threshold $\theta_g$:

$$H(x_t) > \theta_g$$

The second, called the approximate monotonic constraint, places a boundary when the entropy increases relative to the previous position by more than a threshold $\theta_r$:

$$H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r$$

This second approach can be interpreted as identifying points that break the approximate monotonically decreasing entropy within a patch. The intuition is that within a patch, the entropy tends to decrease as more bytes are revealed: after the first byte of a word is known, the remaining bytes become easier to predict. A new patch is started when this trend is violated, indicating a new point of uncertainty.

This dynamic patching approach has several important properties. First, it allocates more compute and model capacity where the data is more complex, rather than uniformly across all positions. Second, it produces patches of variable size, adapting to the structure of the data. Third, it is data-driven rather than rule-based, learning the boundaries from the training distribution rather than imposing a fixed heuristic. Fourth, unlike BPE tokenization, which requires a fixed vocabulary determined before training, BLT patches have no fixed vocabulary—arbitrary groups of bytes can form patches, and the grouping is determined contextually based on the entropy of the data.

## Key Contributions of the Paper

The paper presents several key contributions that collectively demonstrate the viability and advantages of the BLT architecture.

First, BLT is the first byte-level LLM architecture to match the performance of tokenization-based models at scale. The paper presents a flop-controlled scaling study of byte-level models up to $8$ billion parameters and $4$ trillion training bytes, demonstrating that models trained on raw bytes without a fixed vocabulary can achieve performance comparable to tokenization-based baselines like Llama 3. This is a significant milestone, as prior byte-level models have consistently underperformed their tokenization-based counterparts at scale.

Second, BLT achieves significant improvements in inference efficiency. Because patches are dynamically sized, predictable data is grouped into longer patches, reducing the number of steps at which the expensive global transformer must operate. The paper reports that BLT can achieve up to $50\%$ fewer flops at inference compared to Llama 3 while maintaining comparable performance. This is a direct consequence of the entropy-based patching mechanism: when data is predictable, patches are longer, and the global transformer is invoked less frequently.

Third, BLT models demonstrate improved robustness to input noise and enhanced character-level understanding. Because BLT operates directly on bytes, it has access to the raw byte-level information that tokenization-based models lack. The paper shows that BLT outperforms tokenization-based models on character-level tasks such as orthographic manipulation, phonology (grapheme-to-phoneme conversion), and robustness to noisy inputs. BLT also shows improvements on low-resource machine translation, where the ability to model long-tail byte sequences is particularly valuable.

Fourth, BLT unlocks a new dimension for scaling LLMs. In tokenization-based models, increasing the vocabulary size to achieve longer tokens (and thus fewer transformer steps) requires a larger embedding table, which imposes a trade-off that limits the practical range of token sizes. BLT, by contrast, can arbitrarily increase the average patch size without a corresponding increase in model parameters, because patches are not drawn from a fixed vocabulary. This means that BLT can simultaneously increase both the model size and the patch size while maintaining a fixed inference flop budget. The paper demonstrates that this leads to better scaling trends than tokenization-based architectures, particularly in the regime where models are trained beyond the compute-optimal point—a common practice in real-world deployments.

Fifth, the paper introduces a method for "byte-ifying" pre-trained tokenization-based models. By initializing the global transformer of a BLT model with the weights of a pre-trained Llama 3.1 model, the authors show that BLT can leverage the knowledge encoded in existing tokenization-based models, achieving faster convergence and better performance than training from scratch with the same flop budget.

## High-Level Architecture Overview

The BLT architecture consists of three main components: a large global autoregressive transformer that operates on patch representations, and two smaller local models that encode bytes into patches and decode patches back into bytes.

The global model, denoted $\mathcal{G}$, is the primary computational engine of BLT. It is an autoregressive transformer with $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers that processes a sequence of latent patch representations $p_j$ and produces output patch representations $o_j$. The global model uses a block-causal attention mask, which restricts attention to be up to and including the current patch within the current document. This model consumes the bulk of the flops during both pre-training and inference, which is why the decision of when to invoke it—determined by the patching function—allows BLT to control and vary the amount of compute expended for different portions of the input and output.

The local encoder, denoted $\mathcal{E}$, is a lightweight transformer-based model with $l_{\mathcal{E}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers. Its role is to efficiently map a sequence of input bytes $b_i$ into expressive patch representations $p_j$. The encoder uses a local block-causal attention mask, where each byte attends to a fixed window of $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ preceding bytes. A key architectural innovation in the encoder is the addition of cross-attention layers that pool byte representations into patch representations. The encoder also incorporates hash n-gram embeddings, which augment the byte-level representations with information about preceding byte sequences, capturing local context that would otherwise be lost in the pooling process.

The local decoder, denoted $\mathcal{D}$, is similarly a lightweight transformer-based model with $l_{\mathcal{D}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers. It decodes the output patch representations $o_j$ from the global model back into raw bytes $y_i$. The decoder takes as input both the global patch representations and the hidden representations produced by the local encoder, allowing it to leverage information from the encoding process. Like the encoder, the decoder uses cross-attention layers, but with the roles reversed: byte representations serve as queries, and patch representations serve as keys and values. This allows the decoder to reconstruct the byte-level sequence from the patch-level representations produced by the global model.

The division of labor between the local and global models is central to BLT's efficiency. The local models handle the byte-level details—encoding bytes into patches and decoding patches back into bytes—at a low computational cost because they are small and operate with local attention. The global model handles the high-level reasoning and prediction at the patch level, where the expensive computation is performed but only at a fraction of the positions. This architecture embodies the principle that compute should be allocated dynamically based on data complexity: the small local models process every byte, while the large global model is invoked only at patch boundaries, where the data complexity warrants the additional computation.
