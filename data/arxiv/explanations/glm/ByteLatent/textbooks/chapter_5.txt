\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Chapter 5: Scaling Trends: Compute-Optimal and Beyond

The question of whether byte-level language models can scale as effectively as tokenization-based models has long been a central challenge in the field. Prior byte-level architectures, while demonstrating promising results at small scales, consistently fell short of matching the performance of tokenizer-based models when scaled up in both parameter count and training data. This chapter presents the core scaling results of the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT), demonstrating that BLT not only matches but in certain regimes exceeds the performance of BPE-based models such as Llama 3 across compute-optimal settings, downstream task evaluations, and fixed-inference-flop scaling regimes. These results represent a landmark achievement: for the first time, a byte-level architecture without fixed-vocabulary tokenization achieves parity with state-of-the-art tokenizer-based models at scales reaching 8B parameters and 4T training bytes.

# Parameter-Matched Compute-Optimal Scaling: BLT vs. BPE Llama 3 on Llama 2 Data

The foundational scaling comparison in this work is conducted under the compute-optimal training regime, which is theoretically designed to achieve the best performance on the training dataset within a given training budget. This regime follows the methodology established by Chinchilla scaling laws, which prescribe an optimal ratio of model parameters to training data for a given computational budget. For the baseline, the authors use BPE models trained with the Llama 3 tokenizer on the Llama 2 dataset, comprising 2 trillion tokens collected from publicly available sources. The compute-optimal ratio of parameters to training data is determined according to the guidelines established by Llama 3, providing a robust and well-calibrated baseline against which BLT can be evaluated.

The experiment proceeds by training compute-optimal BPE and BLT models across four different sizes, ranging from 1B to 8B parameters. For each BPE model, a corresponding BLT model is trained on the same data, using a Latent Global Transformer that matches the size and architecture of the corresponding BPE Transformer. This parameter-matching strategy ensures that the primary comparison isolates the effect of the byte-level patching architecture from confounding differences in model capacity. Training flops are plotted against language modeling performance, measured in Bits-Per-Byte (BPB) on a representative subset of the training data mixture. BPB is used rather than perplexity because perplexity depends on the specific tokenizer used; BPB normalizes the cross-entropy loss by the number of bytes, making it a tokenizer-independent metric that allows fair comparison between byte-level and token-level models.

The results reveal that BLT models either match or outperform their BPE counterparts across all model sizes and flop budgets. This trend holds consistently as model size and training flops are scaled up. To the best of the authors' knowledge, BLT is the first byte-level Transformer architecture to achieve matching scaling trends with BPE-based models in the compute-optimal regime. This finding validates the assumption that the optimal ratio of parameters to training compute, as determined for BPE-based models, also applies to BLT, or at least is not too far off from BLT's own optimum. This is an important practical result, as it means that the extensive scaling law research conducted for tokenizer-based models can be leveraged when designing and training BLT models, without requiring an entirely new scaling law framework from scratch.

# The Role of Architectural Improvements and Dynamic Patching in Matching BPE Scaling

Achieving parity with BPE-based models is not accomplished by any single design choice but rather through the combination of several architectural innovations and the dynamic patching scheme. The paper systematically isolates the contributions of each component to demonstrate that both architectural improvements and dynamic patching are crucial.

To understand the importance of these components, the authors compare several intermediate architectures against Llama 3. The first comparison involves SpaceByte, approximated using BLT with space-patching but without n-gram embeddings and without cross-attention. While SpaceByte improves over MegaByte (which uses static strided patching), it remains far from Llama 3 performance. This demonstrates that simply improving the patching scheme from fixed-size to whitespace-based, while beneficial, is insufficient on its own to close the gap with tokenizer-based models.

The full BLT architecture incorporates two key architectural improvements beyond patching: cross-attention layers in the local encoder and decoder, and hash n-gram embeddings. The cross-attention mechanism in the encoder pools byte representations into patch representations, allowing each patch query to attend only to the bytes within its corresponding patch. This is inspired by the Perceiver architecture but differs in that the latent representations correspond to variable-length patches rather than a fixed set of latent vectors. In the decoder, the roles are reversed: byte representations serve as queries attending to patch representations as keys and values, enabling the model to decode patch-level information back into byte-level predictions. The hash n-gram embeddings augment the byte embeddings with information about preceding byte sequences of lengths 3 through 8, using a rolling polynomial hash function to map n-grams to indices in fixed-size embedding tables. This provides the model with local context beyond what a single byte embedding can capture, analogous to how subword tokens implicitly encode multi-byte patterns.

When both architectural improvements and dynamic entropy-based patching are combined, BLT achieves performance on par with state-of-the-art tokenizer-based models such as Llama 3 at scale. The entropy-based patching scheme, which uses next-byte prediction entropies from a small byte-level language model to identify patch boundaries, ensures that computational resources are allocated where the data is most complex and unpredictable. This is a fundamental departure from tokenization, where the same amount of compute is allocated to every token regardless of prediction difficulty. Together, these innovations allow BLT to match BPE scaling trends, validating the hypothesis that intelligent, data-driven compute allocation combined with expressive byte-to-patch mapping can overcome the historical performance gap between byte-level and token-level models.

# Effect of Patch Size on Scaling: Patch Sizes of 6 and 8 Bytes vs. Llama 2/3 Token Sizes

One of the most striking advantages of BLT over tokenization-based models is the flexibility it provides in controlling the average patch size. In standard tokenization-based LLMs, the average token size is determined by the vocabulary size of the tokenizer, and changing it requires trade-offs that are difficult to navigate. For example, Llama 3 increases the average token size from 3.7 to 4.4 bytes compared to Llama 2, but does so at the cost of increasing the size of its embedding table by 4x. This trade-off between vocabulary size and compute leaves little room for tokenization-based approaches to achieve significant variations in token size and inference cost.

BLT, by contrast, has no fixed vocabulary for patches. The average patch size can be arbitrarily chosen by adjusting the entropy threshold used for patching, with significant implications for computational cost. Since inference flops are inversely proportional to the average patch size, larger patches mean the expensive global Latent Transformer is invoked less frequently, leading to substantial savings. The paper demonstrates that BLT can achieve similar scaling trends with average patch sizes of 6 and even 8 bytes, compared to the BPE tokenizers of Llama 2 and Llama 3, which have average token sizes of 3.7 and 4.4 bytes respectively.

The scaling behavior with different patch sizes reveals an intriguing pattern. At smaller model scales (e.g., 1B parameters), BLT with a patch size of 8 bytes starts at a significantly worse point compared to BPE Llama 2. However, as model size and training compute increase, the larger patch size model catches up and eventually surpasses BPE at around 7B scale. This suggests that larger patch sizes, which may initially seem disadvantageous due to coarser compute allocation, actually perform better at larger scales. The hypothesis is that as models grow, the relative cost of the byte-level local encoder and decoder modules decreases compared to the global Latent Transformer, making the savings from larger patches more impactful. Furthermore, it suggests that even larger patch sizes could be feasible as model size and training compute continue to grow.

A critical methodological detail in these experiments is the equalization of context length. To ensure fair comparison and prevent BLT models with larger patch sizes from gaining an unfair advantage by processing more bytes per context, the authors maintain the same average batch size in bytes across all models. This means that models with larger patch sizes have correspondingly shorter sequence lengths in terms of patches. On the Llama 2 data, an 8k byte context is used, while on the BLT-1T dataset, the context is increased to 16k bytes on average, with a constant batch size of 16M bytes on average.

# Downstream Task Evaluations: BLT-Entropy vs. BLT-Space vs. Llama 3 on Reasoning, Knowledge, and Coding Benchmarks

While language modeling performance measured in Bits-Per-Byte provides a fundamental comparison, the ultimate test of an architecture is its performance on downstream tasks that require reasoning, world knowledge, and code generation. To assess these capabilities, the authors train an 8B BLT model beyond the compute-optimal ratio on the BLT-1T dataset, a larger and higher-quality dataset comprising 1 trillion tokens gathered from various public sources including a subset of the Datacomp-LM pre-training data. Three models are compared on this dataset, all trained with an equivalent flop budget: a BPE Llama 3 tokenizer-based model, a BLT model employing space-patching (BLT-Space), and a BLT model utilizing entropy-based patching (BLT-Entropy).

The evaluation suite encompasses both classification and generation tasks. The classification tasks include ARC-Easy (0-shot), ARC-Challenge (0-shot), HellaSwag (0-shot), PIQA (0-shot), and MMLU (5-shot), covering common sense reasoning and world knowledge. For these tasks, a prompt-scoring method is employed, calculating the likelihood over choice characters and reporting average accuracy. The coding-related generation tasks include MBPP (3-shot) and HumanEval (0-shot), which evaluate the ability to generate Python code, with pass@1 scores reported.

The BLT-Entropy model outperforms the Llama 3 model on 4 out of 7 tasks, a notable achievement for a byte-level model trained on the same number of bytes. The authors attribute this improvement to two factors: better use of training compute via dynamic patching, which allocates more compute to harder predictions, and the direct modeling of byte-level information as opposed to tokens, which provides the model with finer-grained access to the data. On the other hand, BLT-Space underperforms the Llama 3 tokenizer on all but one task. However, it achieves a significant reduction in inference flops due to its larger average patch size of 6 bytes, compared to approximately 4.5 bytes for both the BPE and entropy-patching based models on the training data mix. This larger patch size means that, with the same training budget, the BLT-Space model covers 30% more data than the other two models, which may push it further from the compute-optimal point and partially explain its lower downstream performance. These results highlight the trade-off between inference efficiency and task performance, and suggest that entropy-based patching provides a better balance between the two.

# Inference-Time Entropy Threshold Adjustment for Improved Task Performance

A unique feature of entropy-based patching is that the entropy threshold, which determines when a new patch boundary is created, can be adjusted at inference time without retraining the model. A lower threshold means more patch boundaries, leading to more frequent invocation of the global Latent Transformer and thus more compute per byte, while a higher threshold means fewer boundaries and less compute. This provides a flexible mechanism for trading compute for performance at inference time, which is not available in tokenization-based models where the token size is fixed by the tokenizer.

For the BLT-Entropy model, the entropy threshold during training is set to 0.6 on the training data mix, yielding an average patch size of approximately 4.5 bytes. At inference time, the threshold is adjusted to 0.1, which is lower and thus creates more patch boundaries, effectively increasing the number of global transformer steps. This adjustment is found to improve task performance at the cost of more inference steps. The improvement is intuitive: by lowering the threshold, the model allocates more compute to predictions that are moderately uncertain, which may be particularly beneficial for structured tasks like multiple-choice questions where the model benefits from finer-grained processing.

This inference-time adjustment capability is a significant practical advantage of BLT. It allows practitioners to dynamically tune the compute-performance trade-off based on the specific application or task at hand, without needing to train separate models for different operating points. In contrast, a tokenizer-based model like Llama 3 has a fixed token size determined at training time, and the only way to change it is to retrain the model with a different tokenizer, which is expensive and may not be feasible in practice.

# Patches Scale Better Than Tokens: Simultaneously Increasing Model and Patch Size Under a Fixed Inference Budget

The most novel scaling insight of this work is the demonstration that patches scale better than tokens, in the sense that BLT models can simultaneously increase both model size and patch size while maintaining a fixed inference flop budget. This is a capability that is fundamentally unavailable to tokenization-based models due to the rigid trade-off between vocabulary size and compute that tokenization imposes.

The mechanism works as follows. Increasing the average patch size means that the global Latent Transformer, which consumes the bulk of the flops during both training and inference, is invoked less frequently. The compute saved by this reduction can be reallocated to grow the size of the global Latent Transformer itself, since it processes fewer patches per sequence and therefore can afford to have more parameters per patch. In other words, the model can be made larger (in terms of the global transformer parameters) while keeping the total inference flops constant, because the larger model processes fewer patches. This creates a new dimension for scaling that is orthogonal to the traditional approach of simply increasing model size and training data.

In tokenization-based models, the analogous operation would be to increase the vocabulary size to create larger tokens on average, thereby reducing the number of tokens processed. However, increasing the vocabulary size requires increasing the size of the embedding table and the output projection layer, which adds parameters that do not contribute proportionally to model capacity. The Llama 3 tokenizer, for instance, achieves an average token size of 4.4 bytes by using a vocabulary of 128K tokens, which requires an embedding table 4x larger than Llama 2's 32K vocabulary. This trade-off effectively limits the practical range of token sizes that can be explored. BLT, by contrast, can freely adjust the patch size by changing the entropy threshold, with no increase in embedding table size since the byte vocabulary is fixed at 256.

This new scaling dimension has significant practical implications. In real-world deployments, inference cost is often the binding constraint, and the ability to deploy a larger, more capable model within a fixed inference budget is highly valuable. The traditional approach to scaling models within a fixed inference budget is to use techniques like quantization or distillation, which reduce model size at some cost to performance. BLT offers a complementary approach: by increasing the patch size, the model can be made larger while keeping inference flops constant, potentially achieving better performance than a smaller model with more compute per token.

# Fixed Inference-Flop Scaling Study: Crossover Points Where BLT Surpasses BPE Models

To rigorously test the hypothesis that larger models taking fewer steps on larger patches perform better than smaller models taking more steps, the authors conduct a fixed inference-flop scaling study. This study compares models that are designed to have equivalent inference flop budgets but differ in their architecture and patch size. Starting from model sizes of 400M and 3.6B parameters with the Llama 2 tokenizer, flop-equivalent models are found using the Llama 3 tokenizer and BLT-Entropy models with average patch sizes of 6 and 8 bytes on the training data mix. For the patch size 8 models, 3 encoder layers are used instead of 1 to accommodate the larger patches. Each model is then trained for various training flop budgets.

The results demonstrate that BLT models achieve better scaling trends than tokenization-based architectures for both inference flop classes. A consistent pattern emerges across the experiments: BPE models perform better with small training budgets, but BLT models surpass them not far beyond the compute-optimal regime. The crossover point, defined as the training flop budget at which BLT begins to outperform the corresponding BPE model, is a key metric for understanding when the BLT architecture becomes advantageous.

For the smaller inference flop class (starting from 400M parameters), the crossover point occurs at approximately 3x the compute-optimal budget. For the larger inference flop class (starting from 3.6B parameters), the crossover point shifts closer to the compute-optimal point, at approximately 2.5x the compute-optimal budget. This shift indicates that the advantage of BLT becomes apparent sooner as the model scale increases, which is consistent with the observation that larger patch sizes perform better at larger model scales. In practice, it is common to train models well beyond the compute-optimal point; for example, Llama 3.1 has been trained on two orders of magnitude more data than what is compute-optimal for that model size. This means that the crossover points observed in the study fall well within the training regimes that are commonly used in practice.

The practical implication is that while BLT may not be the optimal choice for models trained with very limited compute (where the model has not yet been trained enough to benefit from the patch-based architecture), it becomes increasingly advantageous as training compute increases. For the large-scale models that are typically deployed in production, where training compute far exceeds the compute-optimal point, BLT offers a compelling alternative to tokenization-based models, providing better performance within a fixed inference budget.

# Observations on How Larger Patch Sizes Benefit from Larger Model Scales

The scaling study reveals a consistent and important pattern: larger patch sizes, which may seem disadvantageous at small model scales, become increasingly competitive and even superior as model scale grows. This observation has both practical and theoretical implications for the future of byte-level language modeling.

At the 1B parameter scale, BLT with a patch size of 8 bytes starts at a significantly worse performance point compared to BPE Llama 2. However, by the 7B scale, this same configuration surpasses BPE performance. This pattern is consistent across the fixed inference-flop scaling study as well, where the patch size 8 model shows a steeper scaling trend in the larger flop class, overtaking other models sooner. The authors attribute this phenomenon, in part, to the decreasing share of total flops used by the byte-level local encoder and decoder modules as the model scales.

When growing total parameters 20x from 400M to 8B, the authors only roughly double BLT's local model parameters. This is because the local models, which operate on individual bytes, do not need to scale as dramatically as the global Latent Transformer to maintain performance. Since larger patch sizes only reduce the flops of the global Latent Transformer and not the byte-level local modules, the relative benefit of larger patches increases as the global transformer's share of total flops grows. Concretely, the BLT-Entropy model with patch size 8 went from 1.6x to 1.7x of the Llama 2 model size when moving to the larger model scale, reflecting the fact that the local model parameters become a smaller fraction of the total as the model grows.

This observation suggests a promising direction for future scaling. As models continue to grow beyond 8B parameters, the local models will constitute an even smaller fraction of total parameters, making larger patch sizes even more beneficial. It is plausible that even larger patch sizes, beyond 8 bytes, could become feasible and advantageous at these scales. Furthermore, this finding implies that the optimal patch size is not a fixed quantity but rather a function of model scale, with larger models favoring larger patches. This is a dimension of scaling that is entirely absent from tokenization-based models, where the token size is fixed by the tokenizer and cannot be adjusted based on model scale without retraining.

In summary, the scaling trends presented in this chapter establish BLT as a viable and increasingly advantageous alternative to tokenization-based models as scale increases. The ability to dynamically allocate compute, to adjust patch size independently of model architecture, and to simultaneously grow model and patch size within a fixed inference budget represents a paradigm shift in how language models can be scaled. The consistent pattern of larger patch sizes becoming more competitive at larger scales points toward a future where byte-level models with large patches may become the dominant approach for training and deploying language models at scale.
