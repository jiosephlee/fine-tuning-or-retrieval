\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Chapter 4: Experimental Setup and Evaluation Metrics

## Pre-training Datasets

The experimental validation of the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) requires carefully curated training data to ensure that comparisons with tokenization-based baselines are both fair and informative. To this end, the authors utilize two distinct pre-training datasets, each serving a specific purpose within the broader experimental framework.

The first dataset is the Llama 2 dataset, which comprises approximately two trillion tokens collected from a variety of publicly available sources. These sources are subsequently cleaned and filtered to improve overall data quality. The Llama 2 dataset serves as the foundation for scaling law experiments, where the goal is to determine the optimal number of training tokens as prescribed by the Llama 3 scaling laws. This dataset is used to identify the best architectural choices for BLT by enabling controlled comparisons across different model sizes and patching strategies. By adhering to the compute-optimal training regime established for Llama 3, the authors ensure that BLT models are evaluated under the same theoretical optimality conditions that govern tokenization-based models, providing a robust and rigorous baseline.

The second dataset, referred to as BLT-1T, is a newly constructed dataset containing one trillion tokens gathered from various public sources, including a subset of the pre-training data released by Datacomp-LM. The BLT-1T dataset is used for a complete pre-training run that enables direct comparison with Llama 3 on downstream benchmark tasks. This dataset is designed to be of higher quality than the Llama 2 dataset and provides a larger and more diverse training corpus for evaluating the full capabilities of BLT models beyond the compute-optimal regime. The authors note that neither dataset includes any data gathered from Meta products or services, ensuring that the experimental results are reproducible and not dependent on proprietary data sources.

For baseline experiments involving tokenizer-based models, the authors use the Llama 3 tokenizer with a vocabulary size of 128,000 tokens. This choice is motivated by the observation that the Llama 3 tokenizer produces stronger baseline performance than the Llama 2 tokenizer, which has a smaller vocabulary of 32,000 tokens. By selecting the stronger tokenizer as the baseline, the authors ensure that any improvements demonstrated by BLT are not artifacts of a weak baseline but represent genuine architectural advantages.

## Entropy Model

A central innovation of BLT is entropy patching, which dynamically segments byte sequences into patches based on the predictability of the next byte. This patching strategy requires a mechanism for estimating the entropy of next-byte predictions, and the authors fulfill this role with a separately trained byte-level language model.

The entropy model used in the experiments is a byte-level autoregressive transformer trained on the same data distribution as the full BLT model. Unless otherwise specified, the entropy model consists of 100 million parameters, 14 transformer layers, and a hidden dimensionality of 512. It employs sliding window attention with a window size of 512 bytes, meaning that the model's predictions at any given byte position are conditioned on the preceding 512 bytes. The remaining hyperparameters of the entropy model, such as the activation function, layer normalization, and positional encoding strategy, are kept consistent with those used in the local and global transformers of the BLT architecture.

The entropy model computes, for each byte position $i$ in a sequence, the entropy of the next-byte prediction under its learned distribution $p_e$ over the byte vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$. Specifically, the entropy at position $i$ is calculated as:

$$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i})$$

This entropy value quantifies the uncertainty of the next-byte prediction: a high entropy indicates that the next byte is difficult to predict given the preceding context, while a low entropy indicates that the next byte is highly predictable. The patching mechanism uses these entropy values to identify natural boundaries where the model should allocate additional computational resources.

The authors explore different configurations of the entropy model to understand how its size and receptive field affect scaling performance. In ablation studies, they train byte-level entropy models with sizes ranging from 1 million to 100 million parameters and context window lengths from 64 to 512 bytes. Their findings indicate that scaling performance is positively correlated with both the size of the entropy model and the length of its context window, though with diminishing returns once the model exceeds approximately 50 million parameters. An important practical observation is that when the receptive field of the entropy model is sufficiently small, the trained model can be encoded as an efficient lookup table, substantially reducing the computational overhead of patching during both training and inference.

## Entropy Threshold Selection and Context Handling for Structured Content

Once the entropy model produces per-byte entropy values, the next step is to convert these values into actual patch boundaries. The authors experiment with two methods for identifying boundaries given the entropy sequence $H(x_i)$. The first method uses a global constraint, marking a patch boundary at any position where the entropy exceeds a fixed global threshold $\theta_g$:

$$H(x_t) > \theta_g$$

The second method uses an approximate monotonicity constraint, identifying boundaries at positions where the entropy increases relative to the previous position by more than a threshold $\theta_r$:

$$H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r$$

This second approach can be interpreted as identifying points that break the approximate monotonically decreasing entropy trend within a patch. The intuition is that within a patch, entropy tends to decrease as the model gains context about the current word or phrase, and a sudden increase signals the beginning of a new, harder prediction.

For models using entropy-based patching, the authors estimate a patching threshold that achieves a desired average patch size on the pre-training data mixture. This threshold is determined during a lightweight preprocessing step that is executed during data loading, making it distinct from prior approaches that train a separate classifier to predict entropy-based patch boundaries.

The authors observe an important empirical phenomenon when applying entropy patching to structured content such as multiple-choice tasks. In such content, which is often highly repetitive, entropy patching yields progressively larger patches. This occurs because the entropy model encounters lower entropy on repeated content within its context window, leading to an effect the authors term "entropy drift." For example, on MMLU benchmarks, the repeated formatting of multiple-choice questions causes the entropy model to assign low entropy to many byte positions, resulting in unusually large patches that may not be appropriate for the task.

To mitigate this issue, for the large-scale BLT-Entropy model with an average patch size of 4.5 bytes, the authors reset the entropy model's context at new lines. This means that the entropy model's context is cleared whenever a new line is encountered, preventing the accumulation of low-entropy context from repetitive structured content. Additionally, they use the approximate monotonicity constraint rather than the global threshold, as the monotonicity approach suffers less from entropy drift caused by changes in context length. Importantly, this adjustment only affects how entropies are computed; the same procedure is followed to identify the value of the entropy threshold from the training data.

## Equalizing Context Length for Fair Comparisons

A critical methodological concern in comparing BLT with tokenization-based models is ensuring that no model receives an unfair advantage from processing longer contexts. Since BLT's patch size can be arbitrarily chosen—unlike the fixed vocabulary of tokenization-based models—larger patch sizes naturally result in fewer patches for the same number of bytes, which could allow the global transformer to attend over a larger effective byte context. To prevent this confounding factor, the authors implement a careful context equalization procedure.

The key principle is that the number of bytes in each batch remains constant in expectation across all models, regardless of their patch size or tokenization scheme. This means that models with larger patch sizes have their sequence lengths proportionally reduced. On the Llama 2 dataset, the authors use an 8,000-byte context, while on the BLT-1T dataset, the context is increased to 16,000 bytes on average. The batch size is maintained at 16 million bytes on average across all experiments.

While the average batch size is held constant, the dynamic nature of patching means that different batches of data yield different ratios of bytes to patches. For implementation efficiency, the authors pack batches of patches to avoid padding steps in the more computationally expensive latent transformer, ensuring that every batch contains the same number of patches. During training, byte sequences are padded and potentially truncated to 12,000 bytes and 24,000 bytes for the Llama 2 and BLT-1T datasets, respectively. This prevents memory spikes that could arise from sequences with unusually large patches.

This equalization procedure is essential for drawing valid conclusions from the scaling experiments. Without it, models with larger patch sizes would effectively see more bytes per training example, confounding the effects of patch size with the effects of context length. By holding the byte-level context constant, the authors isolate the impact of the architectural choices—patching strategy, model size, and compute allocation—from any advantage that might accrue from simply processing longer contexts.

## FLOPs Estimation Methodology

To compare BLT with tokenization-based models in a compute-controlled manner, the authors develop a detailed FLOPs estimation methodology that accounts for the unique architectural components of BLT. The methodology largely follows the equations for transformer FLOPs computation established in the Chinchilla scaling laws, which include FLOPs for feed-forward layers, query-key-value-output (QKVO) projections in self-attention layers, and the computation of attention and output projections.

A notable departure from standard FLOPs accounting is the treatment of the input embedding layer. The authors assume that the input embedding layer is implemented as an efficient lookup operation rather than a dense matrix multiplication, rendering it a zero-FLOP operation. This assumption is reasonable for byte-level models, where the input vocabulary is only 256, making lookup-based embeddings trivially efficient. Following previous work, the backward pass is estimated to have twice the number of FLOPs as the forward pass.

The total FLOPs per byte for BLT models is computed by summing the FLOPs across all architectural components: the local encoder transformer, the global latent transformer, the local decoder transformer, and the cross-attention blocks in both the encoder and decoder. The formula is:

$$\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}} = \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{G}}, l_{\mathcal{G}}, m=n_{ctx}/n_p, V=0) / n_p + \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=w_{\mathcal{E}}, V=0) + \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=w_{\mathcal{D}}, V=256) + \text{Cross Attn. FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=n_p, r=n_p/k) \times k/n_p + \text{Cross Attn. FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=k, r=k/n_p)$$

Here, $n_{ctx}$ is the sequence length in bytes, $n_p$ is the patch size, $r$ is the ratio of queries to key/values in the cross-attention, and $k$ is the ratio of patch-dimension to byte-dimension (i.e., the number of local model splits that concatenate to form a global model representation, with $k=2$ in the experiments). The variable $V$ corresponds to the vocabulary size for the output projection, which is only used in the local decoder (set to 256, the size of the byte vocabulary). The parameter $m$ denotes the context length used for attention, which varies depending on whether a module operates on the byte sequence or the patch sequence.

Several important observations follow from this formulation. First, the global latent transformer's FLOPs are divided by the patch size $n_p$, reflecting the fact that the global transformer processes one representation per patch rather than per byte. This is the primary mechanism by which larger patch sizes reduce inference cost. Second, the local encoder and decoder operate with fixed window attention ($w_{\mathcal{E}}$ and $w_{\mathcal{D}}$), so their FLOPs scale linearly with the number of bytes rather than the number of patches. Third, the cross-attention FLOPs in the encoder are scaled by $k/n_p$, reflecting the ratio of byte-level to patch-level computation. This formulation makes explicit how the total computational budget is distributed across BLT's components and why larger patch sizes preferentially reduce the cost of the global transformer while leaving the byte-level modules largely unaffected.

## Bits-Per-Byte as a Tokenizer-Independent Metric

A fundamental challenge in comparing byte-level models with tokenization-based models is the choice of evaluation metric. Perplexity, the standard metric for language modeling, is inherently tied to the tokenization scheme used by the model. It measures the average uncertainty per token, but since different tokenizers produce different numbers of tokens for the same text, perplexity values are not directly comparable across tokenization schemes. A model with a tokenizer that produces fewer, longer tokens will naturally have lower perplexity, not because it models the data better, but because each prediction is easier when conditioned on more preceding bytes.

To address this incompatibility, the authors adopt Bits-Per-Byte (BPB) as a tokenizer-independent alternative to perplexity. BPB normalizes the cross-entropy loss by the total number of bytes in the data, rather than the number of tokens. Specifically, BPB is defined as:

$$\text{BPB}(x) = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\pmb{x})}{\ln(2) \cdot n_{\text{bytes}}}$$

where $\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\pmb{x})$ is the sum of cross-entropy losses over the data $\pmb{x}$, and $n_{\text{bytes}}$ is the total number of bytes in $\pmb{x}$. The division by $\ln(2)$ converts the natural logarithm-based cross-entropy (measured in nats) into bits, providing a consistent unit of information across all models.

BPB has several desirable properties for comparing byte-level and token-level models. First, it is defined in terms of bytes, which are a universal unit of digital text representation, independent of any particular tokenization scheme. Second, it provides a direct measure of how efficiently a model compresses the data: a lower BPB indicates that the model assigns higher probability to the observed data, corresponding to better compression. Third, BPB enables fair comparison between models with different average token or patch sizes, since the normalization is always with respect to the same byte-level unit.

The authors follow prior work in the byte-level modeling literature, including ByT5, MegaByte, and MambaByte, in adopting BPB as the primary language modeling metric. This choice is essential for the scaling law experiments, where BLT models with different patch sizes must be compared with BPE models with different vocabulary sizes. Without a tokenizer-independent metric, differences in perplexity could be attributed to the tokenization scheme rather than the model's actual predictive capabilities.

## Transformer Architecture Hyperparameters

To ensure that BLT models are directly comparable with state-of-the-art tokenization-based models, the authors design the transformer blocks within BLT to closely follow the architecture of Llama 3. This architectural alignment applies to all transformer blocks in BLT, including both the local models (encoder and decoder) and the global latent transformer.

The feed-forward layers in all transformer blocks use the SwiGLU activation function, which combines the Swish activation with a gating mechanism to improve the expressiveness and efficiency of the feed-forward computation. Rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) with a rotation base $\theta = 500,000$ are applied exclusively in the self-attention layers, providing a relative positional encoding scheme that generalizes better to longer sequences than absolute positional embeddings. Layer normalization is performed using RMSNorm, which is a computationally efficient variant of layer normalization that does not require mean subtraction.

For attention computation, the authors use Flash Attention for all self-attention layers that employ fixed attention masks, such as the block-causal mask used in the global transformer and the fixed-window block-causal mask used in the local models. The window size for fixed-width attention masks is set to 512. However, the cross-attention layers in the encoder and decoder involve dynamic, patch-dependent masks that cannot be handled by standard Flash Attention implementations. To address this, the authors use Flex Attention, a flexible attention implementation that produces fused kernels for arbitrary attention patterns, significantly speeding up training for the cross-attention modules.

This careful alignment with the Llama 3 architecture ensures that any performance differences between BLT and Llama 3 can be attributed to the novel aspects of BLT—namely, the patching mechanism, the byte-level local models, and the cross-attention modules—rather than to differences in the underlying transformer architecture. By holding the transformer design constant, the authors isolate the effects of their architectural innovations.

## BLT-Specific Hyperparameters

The authors conduct experiments across two principal directions: scaling trend analysis and downstream task evaluation. To cover these directions comprehensively, they train models at five different scales: 400 million, 1 billion, 2 billion, 4 billion, and 8 billion parameters. The architecture hyperparameters for each model scale are specified to ensure that the global latent transformer matches the size and architecture of the corresponding BPE transformer used as the baseline.

For the local encoder, the authors use max-pooling to initialize the queries for the first cross-attention layer. This pooling operation aggregates the byte representations within each patch into an initial patch representation, which is then refined through the cross-attention mechanism. The hash n-gram embeddings use 500,000 hash entries with a single hash function, covering n-gram sizes ranging from 3 to 8. These embeddings augment the byte representations with information about preceding byte sequences, compensating for the limited receptive field of the local encoder's fixed-window attention.

The learning rate is set to $4 \times 10^{-4}$ for all models. This choice follows a hyperparameter search conducted at the 400M and 1B model scales, which compared learning rates between $1 \times 10^{-3}$ and $1 \times 10^{-4}$ and found that the same learning rate is optimal for both token-based and BLT models. This finding simplifies the experimental setup, as no separate learning rate tuning is needed for BLT models relative to their BPE counterparts.

For the scaling trend experiments on the Llama 2 dataset, the authors use training batch sizes as recommended by the Llama 3 scaling laws, or their equivalent in bytes. This ensures that both BLT and BPE models are trained under compute-optimal conditions, as determined by the Chinchilla-style scaling analysis. For optimization, the authors use the AdamW optimizer with $\beta_1 = 0.9$ and $\beta_2 = 0.95$, and $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$. The learning rate schedule consists of a linear warm-up of 2,000 steps followed by cosine decay to zero. A weight decay of 0.1 is applied, and global gradient clipping is enforced at a threshold of 1.0 to ensure training stability.

An important BLT-specific consideration is the number of layers in the local models. Ablation studies reveal that when paired with hash n-gram embeddings, BLT performs well with an extremely lightweight encoder consisting of just one layer, while benefiting from a heavier decoder. This asymmetry reflects the different roles of the two local models: the encoder needs only to produce expressive patch representations, a task that is substantially aided by the n-gram embeddings, while the decoder must generate the full byte sequence from patch representations, requiring more computational capacity. For models with larger patch sizes (e.g., patch size 8), the authors use 3 encoder layers instead of 1, reflecting the greater demand on the encoder when patches contain more bytes.

The authors also investigate the effect of n-gram hash embedding vocabulary size, testing settings of 0, 100,000, 200,000, and 400,000 entries. Their findings indicate that hash embeddings improve performance across all domains, with particularly notable improvements on Wikipedia and GitHub data. At the 8B scale, increasing from 300,000 to 500,000 hashes changes performance by approximately 0.001 bits per byte over 15,000 steps, indicating diminishing returns beyond 300,000 hashes. Nevertheless, the hash embeddings are shown to be vital for bringing BLT's performance to match that of tokenizer-based models, and their gains appear largely complementary with the cross-attention improvements, as the two mechanisms provide benefits on different datasets.
