\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

# Introduction: The Problem with Tokenization and the BLT Paradigm

Modern large language models have achieved remarkable success across a wide range of tasks, from natural language understanding to code generation and reasoning. Yet despite the end-to-end training paradigm that characterizes contemporary LLM development, there remains one critical component that is not learned end-to-end: tokenization. Tokenization is a heuristic pre-processing step that groups raw bytes into a static set of tokens before the model ever sees the data. While this step has long been considered essential for practical scalability, it introduces a set of limitations that grow more consequential as models are deployed in increasingly diverse and multilingual settings. The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) represents a fundamentally different approach: a tokenizer-free architecture that learns directly from raw byte data and dynamically allocates computational resources based on the complexity of the data being processed. This chapter introduces the motivation behind BLT, the shortcomings of tokenization it seeks to overcome, and the high-level design principles that enable it to match the performance of tokenization-based models like Llama 3 at scale while offering significant improvements in efficiency and robustness.

#

## The Role of Tokenization in Existing LLMs and Its Heuristic Nature

To understand why BLT represents a paradigm shift, it is essential to first understand what tokenization does and why it has been so widely adopted. At the lowest level, all digital text is represented as a sequence of bytes. A byte is an 8-bit value, and there are only $256$ possible byte values. This means that any text—whether English prose, Python code, or Unicode characters encoded in UTF-8—can ultimately be reduced to a sequence of integers in the range $\{0, 1, \ldots, 255\}$. In principle, a language model could be trained directly on these byte sequences without any pre-processing.

However, training transformers directly on bytes has historically been prohibitively expensive. The reason is computational: the cost of a transformer scales with the sequence length it must process. A typical English sentence might contain dozens of bytes, and a long document could contain tens of thousands. If the transformer must run its full computation on every single byte, the cost becomes overwhelming, particularly because the feed-forward network layers, which dominate the computational budget of large transformers, are applied at every position in the sequence.

Tokenization solves this problem by grouping bytes into larger units called tokens before the model processes them. For example, the word "transformer" might be a single token, or it might be split into two tokens such as "trans" and "former." The set of possible tokens is determined by a fixed vocabulary that is constructed before training begins, typically using algorithms like Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE). With tokenization, the sequence length is dramatically reduced: instead of processing every byte, the transformer processes one token per several bytes, with the average token size being roughly 3.7 bytes for Llama 2 and 4.4 bytes for Llama 3.

The crucial point is that this grouping is heuristic. The tokenizer is not learned as part of the model's training process; it is determined by a separate algorithm that operates on the training data in advance. This means that the way a string is compressed into tokens is fixed and static, regardless of the context in which the string appears. The tokenizer does not know which parts of the input are easy to predict and which are hard. It treats every token as requiring the same amount of computation, even though the actual complexity of predicting different parts of the sequence varies enormously. This uniform allocation of compute is a fundamental inefficiency: a large transformer is not needed to predict the ending of most words, since these are comparatively easy, low-entropy decisions, but the tokenizer gives these easy predictions the same computational budget as the difficult decision of choosing the first word of a new sentence.

#

## Limitations of Fixed-Vocabulary Tokenization

The heuristic nature of tokenization leads to several well-documented shortcomings that motivate the development of a tokenizer-free alternative. The paper identifies four major categories of limitations: domain and modality sensitivity, sensitivity to input noise, lack of orthographic knowledge, and multilingual inequity.

Domain and modality sensitivity arises because tokenizers are typically optimized for a particular distribution of data. A tokenizer trained primarily on English web text will produce different token boundaries when applied to code, mathematical notation, or less common languages. This means that the compression efficiency of the tokenizer—and therefore the effective sequence length and computational cost—varies across domains in ways that are not under the model's control. A string that is efficiently compressed in one domain may be poorly compressed in another, leading to inconsistent treatment of similar content.

Sensitivity to input noise is a related but distinct problem. Because tokenizers operate on fixed rules applied before the model sees the data, they cannot adapt to small perturbations in the input. For example, if a character is dropped, repeated, or changed in case, the tokenizer may produce a completely different sequence of tokens, even though the semantic content of the text has barely changed. This makes tokenization-based models fragile when confronted with noisy inputs, which are common in real-world applications.

The lack of orthographic knowledge is perhaps the most subtle limitation. When a tokenizer groups bytes into a token, the model receives only the token's identity as input; it does not have direct access to the individual bytes that compose the token. This means that the model must separately learn the spelling of each token, and it has no built-in mechanism for manipulating characters within a token. Tasks that require character-level understanding—such as spelling, phonology, or simple character manipulation—become surprisingly difficult for tokenization-based models, even very large ones. The model may appear to know the spelling of common words, but it struggles to use this knowledge effectively for character-level tasks.

Multilingual inequity is a consequence of the fact that tokenizers are typically optimized for the dominant language in the training data, which is usually English. This means that English text is compressed more efficiently than text in other languages, particularly lower-resource languages with different scripts. A sentence in English might be represented by fewer tokens than a sentence of equivalent length in, say, Sinhala or Amharic. This not only makes multilingual training less efficient but also means that the model effectively has less computational capacity allocated to non-English content, creating a systematic bias.

#

## Why Directly Training on Bytes Has Been Prohibitively Expensive at Scale

Given the limitations of tokenization, one might ask why the field has not simply trained models directly on bytes. The answer is computational cost. The paper explains that directly training LLMs on bytes is prohibitively costly at scale due to the long sequence lengths involved. Since there are only $256$ possible byte values, representing text at the byte level requires far more positions than representing it at the token level, where the vocabulary might contain $128{,}000$ tokens (as in Llama 3).

Prior work has attempted to mitigate this cost through various strategies. Some approaches use more efficient self-attention mechanisms, such as strided convolutions or downsampling, to reduce the effective sequence length. Others employ attention-free architectures, such as the Mamba-based MambaByte model, which can maintain a fixed-size memory state over very large context lengths. However, the paper notes that these approaches primarily help train small models. At scale, the computational cost of a transformer is dominated not by the attention mechanism but by the large feed-forward network layers that run on every position in the sequence. This means that even if attention is made more efficient, the cost of applying the feed-forward network to every byte remains overwhelming.

This observation is critical because it reframes the problem. The issue is not merely that attention is expensive on long sequences; it is that the entire transformer computation—including the feed-forward layers, which constitute the bulk of the parameters and flops—is applied at every position. Reducing the number of positions at which the expensive computation is performed is therefore the key to making byte-level models feasible at scale. This is precisely what BLT's patching mechanism accomplishes: it reduces the number of positions at which the large, expensive transformer operates, while still processing every byte through smaller, cheaper models.

#

## The Core BLT Idea: Dynamic Patching Based on Next-Byte Entropy

The central innovation of BLT is the concept of dynamic patching based on the entropy of next-byte predictions. Instead of grouping bytes into fixed tokens determined by a heuristic pre-processing step, BLT groups bytes into patches whose boundaries are determined by the information content of the data. The key insight is that not all bytes are equally difficult to predict, and therefore not all bytes should receive the same amount of computational effort.

To understand this idea, consider the process of generating text one byte at a time. At each position, there is some distribution over the possible next bytes. If the next byte is highly predictable—say, the ending of a common word like "the"—then the entropy of the next-byte distribution is low, and little computation is needed to make the prediction. If the next byte is highly unpredictable—say, the first character of an answer to a question—then the entropy is high, and more computation is warranted.

BLT operationalizes this insight by training a small byte-level language model to estimate the entropy of the next-byte prediction at each position. The entropy $H(x_i)$ at position $i$ is computed as:

$$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i = v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i = v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i})$$

where $\mathcal{V}$ is the byte vocabulary (the set of $256$ possible byte values), $p_{e}$ is the distribution learned by the small entropy model, and $\mathbf{x}_{<i}$ is the sequence of preceding bytes. When this entropy exceeds a threshold, a new patch boundary is created. This means that patches are formed at points of high uncertainty—exactly the points where the model would benefit from additional computation.

The paper explores two specific methods for identifying patch boundaries from the entropy estimates. The first, called the global constraint, simply places a boundary whenever the entropy exceeds a global threshold $\theta_g$:

$$H(x_t) > \theta_g$$

The second, called the approximate monotonic constraint, places a boundary when the entropy increases relative to the previous position by more than a threshold $\theta_r$:

$$H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r$$

This second approach can be interpreted as identifying points that break the approximate monotonically decreasing entropy within a patch. The intuition is that within a patch, the entropy tends to decrease as more bytes are revealed: after the first byte of a word is known, the remaining bytes become easier to predict. A new patch is started when this trend is violated, indicating a new point of uncertainty.

This dynamic patching approach has several important properties. First, it allocates more compute and model capacity where the data is more complex, rather than uniformly across all positions. Second, it produces patches of variable size, adapting to the structure of the data. Third, it is data-driven rather than rule-based, learning the boundaries from the training distribution rather than imposing a fixed heuristic. Fourth, unlike BPE tokenization, which requires a fixed vocabulary determined before training, BLT patches have no fixed vocabulary—arbitrary groups of bytes can form patches, and the grouping is determined contextually based on the entropy of the data.

#

## Key Contributions of the Paper

The paper presents several key contributions that collectively demonstrate the viability and advantages of the BLT architecture.

First, BLT is the first byte-level LLM architecture to match the performance of tokenization-based models at scale. The paper presents a flop-controlled scaling study of byte-level models up to $8$ billion parameters and $4$ trillion training bytes, demonstrating that models trained on raw bytes without a fixed vocabulary can achieve performance comparable to tokenization-based baselines like Llama 3. This is a significant milestone, as prior byte-level models have consistently underperformed their tokenization-based counterparts at scale.

Second, BLT achieves significant improvements in inference efficiency. Because patches are dynamically sized, predictable data is grouped into longer patches, reducing the number of steps at which the expensive global transformer must operate. The paper reports that BLT can achieve up to $50\%$ fewer flops at inference compared to Llama 3 while maintaining comparable performance. This is a direct consequence of the entropy-based patching mechanism: when data is predictable, patches are longer, and the global transformer is invoked less frequently.

Third, BLT models demonstrate improved robustness to input noise and enhanced character-level understanding. Because BLT operates directly on bytes, it has access to the raw byte-level information that tokenization-based models lack. The paper shows that BLT outperforms tokenization-based models on character-level tasks such as orthographic manipulation, phonology (grapheme-to-phoneme conversion), and robustness to noisy inputs. BLT also shows improvements on low-resource machine translation, where the ability to model long-tail byte sequences is particularly valuable.

Fourth, BLT unlocks a new dimension for scaling LLMs. In tokenization-based models, increasing the vocabulary size to achieve longer tokens (and thus fewer transformer steps) requires a larger embedding table, which imposes a trade-off that limits the practical range of token sizes. BLT, by contrast, can arbitrarily increase the average patch size without a corresponding increase in model parameters, because patches are not drawn from a fixed vocabulary. This means that BLT can simultaneously increase both the model size and the patch size while maintaining a fixed inference flop budget. The paper demonstrates that this leads to better scaling trends than tokenization-based architectures, particularly in the regime where models are trained beyond the compute-optimal point—a common practice in real-world deployments.

Fifth, the paper introduces a method for "byte-ifying" pre-trained tokenization-based models. By initializing the global transformer of a BLT model with the weights of a pre-trained Llama 3.1 model, the authors show that BLT can leverage the knowledge encoded in existing tokenization-based models, achieving faster convergence and better performance than training from scratch with the same flop budget.

## High-Level Architecture Overview

The BLT architecture consists of three main components: a large global autoregressive transformer that operates on patch representations, and two smaller local models that encode bytes into patches and decode patches back into bytes.

The global model, denoted $\mathcal{G}$, is the primary computational engine of BLT. It is an autoregressive transformer with $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers that processes a sequence of latent patch representations $p_j$ and produces output patch representations $o_j$. The global model uses a block-causal attention mask, which restricts attention to be up to and including the current patch within the current document. This model consumes the bulk of the flops during both pre-training and inference, which is why the decision of when to invoke it—determined by the patching function—allows BLT to control and vary the amount of compute expended for different portions of the input and output.

The local encoder, denoted $\mathcal{E}$, is a lightweight transformer-based model with $l_{\mathcal{E}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers. Its role is to efficiently map a sequence of input bytes $b_i$ into expressive patch representations $p_j$. The encoder uses a local block-causal attention mask, where each byte attends to a fixed window of $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ preceding bytes. A key architectural innovation in the encoder is the addition of cross-attention layers that pool byte representations into patch representations. The encoder also incorporates hash n-gram embeddings, which augment the byte-level representations with information about preceding byte sequences, capturing local context that would otherwise be lost in the pooling process.

The local decoder, denoted $\mathcal{D}$, is similarly a lightweight transformer-based model with $l_{\mathcal{D}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers. It decodes the output patch representations $o_j$ from the global model back into raw bytes $y_i$. The decoder takes as input both the global patch representations and the hidden representations produced by the local encoder, allowing it to leverage information from the encoding process. Like the encoder, the decoder uses cross-attention layers, but with the roles reversed: byte representations serve as queries, and patch representations serve as keys and values. This allows the decoder to reconstruct the byte-level sequence from the patch-level representations produced by the global model.

The division of labor between the local and global models is central to BLT's efficiency. The local models handle the byte-level details—encoding bytes into patches and decoding patches back into bytes—at a low computational cost because they are small and operate with local attention. The global model handles the high-level reasoning and prediction at the patch level, where the expensive computation is performed but only at a fraction of the positions. This architecture embodies the principle that compute should be allocated dynamically based on data complexity: the small local models process every byte, while the large global model is invoked only at patch boundaries, where the data complexity warrants the additional computation.

# From Bytes to Patches: Patching Methods

## Formal Definition of a Patching Function and Its Relationship to Computational Cost

At the heart of the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) lies a simple but powerful idea: rather than processing every byte individually or relying on a pre-defined tokenizer to group bytes into fixed units, we can dynamically group bytes into computational units called *patches*. Before exploring specific patching strategies, we must establish a formal framework that defines what a patching function does and how it relates to the computational cost of the model.

A patching function $f_p$ takes as input a sequence of bytes $\pmb{x} = \{x_i \mid i = 1, \ldots, n\}$ of length $n$ and produces a sequence of $m < n$ patches $\pmb{p} = \{p_j \mid j = 1, \ldots, m\}$. The function works by mapping each byte position $x_i$ to a binary value in the set $\{0, 1\}$, where a value of $1$ indicates the start of a new patch. In other words, the patching function inserts boundaries between certain bytes, and the groups of bytes between consecutive boundaries constitute the patches. The number of patches $m$ is always less than the number of bytes $n$, because each patch must contain at least one byte.

The reason patches matter so much for computational cost is that in both token-based and patch-based models, the dominant computational expense comes from the number of steps executed by the main Transformer. In a standard token-based LLM, this main Transformer processes every token, and the cost scales with the number of tokens. In BLT, the main Transformer—called the Latent Global Transformer—processes every patch. Therefore, the number of patches needed to encode a given sequence of data is the primary determinant of how much computation the model expends on that data.

The *average patch size*, defined as the ratio of the total number of bytes to the number of patches ($n/m$), is the main factor controlling the cost of processing data during both training and inference. A larger average patch size means fewer patches, which means fewer steps through the expensive global Transformer, and thus lower computational cost. Conversely, a smaller average patch size means more patches and more computation. This relationship between patch size and cost is what makes the choice of patching function so consequential: different patching functions produce different average patch sizes and, just as importantly, distribute computation differently across the input.

What makes patching particularly powerful as a computational mechanism is that it allows the model to *dynamically allocate compute* based on the complexity of the data. In a token-based model, every token receives the same amount of computation regardless of how predictable or unpredictable it is. In contrast, a well-designed patching function can create smaller patches (more computation) in regions of the data that are complex and unpredictable, and larger patches (less computation) in regions that are simple and predictable. This is the key insight that BLT exploits to achieve better efficiency than tokenization-based models. The patching function, therefore, is not just a preprocessing step—it is a fundamental mechanism for controlling where and how much the model thinks.

## Strided Patching Every K Bytes: Simplicity and Its Downsides

The most straightforward approach to grouping bytes into patches is to create patches of a fixed size $k$. This method, known as *strided patching*, simply inserts a patch boundary every $k$ bytes. For example, with $k = 4$, the byte sequence would be divided into patches of exactly four bytes each (except possibly the last patch, which might be shorter). This approach was used in MegaByte, an earlier byte-level model architecture.

Strided patching has several practical advantages. First, it is extremely easy to implement. During both training and inference, one simply counts bytes and inserts a boundary every $k$-th byte. Second, it provides a straightforward mechanism for controlling the average patch size and thus the computational cost: to halve the number of patches, one simply doubles $k$. This makes it trivial to adjust the flop cost of the model by changing a single hyperparameter.

However, strided patching comes with significant downsides that make it suboptimal for large-scale language modeling. The first and most fundamental problem is that compute is not dynamically allocated to where it is needed most. Because the boundaries are placed at fixed intervals regardless of the content of the data, the model may waste a Transformer step on a patch that contains only trivially predictable bytes—for instance, a patch consisting entirely of whitespace characters in a code file. Conversely, a fixed-size patch might not allocate sufficient compute for a region of bytes that is dense with information, such as a complex mathematical expression where every byte carries significant meaning. The rigidity of the stride means that the model treats all regions of the data equally, which is precisely the inefficiency that BLT aims to overcome.

The second problem is that strided patching leads to inconsistent and non-contextual patching of similar byte sequences. Because the patch boundaries are determined solely by position rather than content, the same word appearing at different positions in a sequence may be split in different ways. For example, the word "transformer" might be split as "trans" + "former" in one occurrence and "transf" + "ormer" in another, depending on where it happens to fall relative to the stride. This inconsistency means that the model cannot learn stable representations for recurring byte patterns, since the same pattern may be fragmented differently each time it appears. This lack of consistency hinders the model's ability to generalize and compress the data effectively.

These limitations motivate the search for patching strategies that are sensitive to the content and structure of the data, rather than relying on arbitrary positional boundaries.

## Space Patching: Using Whitespace-Like Bytes as Natural Boundaries

A simple yet effective improvement over strided patching was proposed by SpaceByte, which creates new patches after any space-like bytes. Space-like bytes are defined as any byte that is not a Latin character, digit, or UTF-8 continuation byte. Additionally, each patch must contain at least one non-space-like byte, ensuring that patches are not empty of meaningful content.

The intuition behind space patching is that spaces and similar characters serve as natural boundaries for linguistic units in many languages. In English and many other languages, words are separated by spaces, making whitespace a natural candidate for patch boundaries. By placing patch boundaries at these natural linguistic boundaries, space patching ensures that words are patched consistently across different sequences—the same word will generally be grouped into the same patch regardless of where it appears in the text.

Space patching also has the property that it allocates a latent Transformer step (i.e., more flops) to model every word. This is beneficial because the first byte of a word is typically the hardest to predict, while subsequent bytes are easier. For example, consider the question "Who composed the Magic Flute?" followed by a blank to be filled in. Predicting the first byte of the answer is very difficult because the space of possible answers is enormous—it could be the name of any composer. However, once the first character "M" is predicted, the number of likely choices is dramatically reduced, making the completion "Mozart" comparatively easy to predict. By allocating a Transformer step to each word, space patching ensures that the model has sufficient compute available for the hard prediction of the first byte, while the easier subsequent bytes can be handled by the lighter-weight local decoder model.

Despite these advantages, space patching has notable limitations. First, it cannot gracefully handle all languages and domains. Many languages do not use spaces to separate words—for example, Chinese and Japanese text typically does not contain inter-word spaces—and code or structured data may not follow the same linguistic conventions. In such cases, space patching may produce patches that are too long, too short, or poorly aligned with the meaningful units of the data.

Second, and perhaps most importantly, space patching cannot vary the patch size. Because patches are determined by the locations of spaces in the text, the average patch size is fixed by the linguistic properties of the data and cannot be adjusted by the model designer. This means that there is no mechanism to trade off between patch size and computational cost—unlike strided patching, where one can simply change $k$, or entropy patching, where one can adjust the entropy threshold. The inability to control patch size limits the flexibility of space patching as a mechanism for balancing compute and performance.

## Entropy Patching: Using a Small Byte-Level LM to Identify High-Entropy Byte Positions as Patch Boundaries

Rather than relying on a rule-based heuristic such as whitespace, BLT introduces a data-driven approach called *entropy patching* that uses entropy estimates to derive patch boundaries. The key insight is that the model should allocate more compute (i.e., create smaller patches) to regions of the data where the next byte is difficult to predict, and less compute (i.e., create larger patches) where the next byte is easy to predict. This is precisely the kind of dynamic allocation that the patching mechanism is designed to enable.

To implement entropy patching, BLT trains a small byte-level autoregressive language model on the same training data as the full BLT model. This entropy model is used to compute the entropy of the next-byte prediction at each position in the sequence. Specifically, given the entropy model's distribution $p_e$ over the byte vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$, the entropy at byte position $i$ is computed as:

$$H(x_i) = -\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_e(x_i = v \mid \pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_e(x_i = v \mid \pmb{x}_{<i})$$

This entropy value quantifies how uncertain the next-byte prediction is at position $i$. A high entropy means the model is very uncertain about what byte comes next—this is a point where more compute should be allocated. A low entropy means the model is confident about the next byte—this is a point where less compute is needed.

Patch boundaries are then identified at positions where the entropy exceeds some criterion, indicating that a new patch should begin. The idea is that a new patch boundary should be placed just before a byte that is difficult to predict, so that the expensive global Transformer is invoked to process the patch that begins with this hard prediction. The subsequent, easier bytes within the patch can then be handled by the lighter-weight local decoder.

The entropy model used in BLT's experiments is a Transformer with approximately 100 million parameters, 14 layers, and a hidden dimensionality of 512, using sliding window attention over 512 bytes. The remaining hyperparameters match those of the local and global transformers. The authors also experimented with different model sizes, receptive fields, and architectures. Notably, when the receptive field of the entropy model is small enough, the trained model can be encoded in an efficient lookup table, making the entropy computation extremely fast.

An important practical detail is that patch boundaries are identified during a lightweight preprocessing step executed during dataloading. This means that the entropy model is run on the data before the main BLT model processes it, and the patch boundaries are pre-computed and stored. This is different from some prior approaches where a classifier is trained end-to-end to predict entropy-based patch boundaries.

The choice of the entropy threshold directly controls the average patch size and thus the computational cost. A lower threshold means that more bytes will exceed the threshold, resulting in more patch boundaries, smaller patches, and more computation. A higher threshold means fewer boundaries, larger patches, and less computation. This provides a natural and flexible mechanism for controlling the trade-off between compute and performance, which is a key advantage over space patching. In the BLT experiments, entropy thresholds are estimated to achieve desired average patch sizes on the pretraining data mix.

## Global Entropy Threshold vs. Approximate Monotonicity Constraint for Boundary Identification

Once the entropies $H(x_i)$ have been computed at each byte position, BLT must decide which positions constitute patch boundaries. The paper experiments with two methods for making this determination.

The first method uses a *global entropy threshold*. A patch boundary is placed at position $t$ if the entropy at that position exceeds a global threshold $\theta_g$:

$$H(x_t) > \theta_g$$

This method is intuitive and simple: any byte position where the entropy is high enough—regardless of the surrounding context—triggers a new patch. The threshold $\theta_g$ is a single hyperparameter that can be tuned to achieve a desired average patch size. A lower threshold will result in more boundaries and smaller patches, while a higher threshold will result in fewer boundaries and larger patches.

The second method uses an *approximate monotonicity constraint*. Rather than comparing the entropy to an absolute threshold, this method compares the entropy at the current position to the entropy at the previous position. A patch boundary is placed at position $t$ if the increase in entropy from the previous position exceeds a threshold $\theta_r$:

$$H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r$$

This approach can be interpreted as identifying points that break the approximate monotonically decreasing entropy within a patch. The intuition is that within a patch, the entropy should generally decrease as the model gains information from the preceding bytes—once the first byte of a word is predicted, the remaining bytes become progressively easier. A new patch should begin when this decreasing trend is broken, i.e., when the entropy suddenly increases again, signaling the start of a new, harder prediction.

The approximate monotonicity constraint has a practical advantage over the global threshold: it suffers less from what the authors call "entropy drift" caused by changes in context length. The authors observe that entropy patching yields progressively larger patches in structured content like multiple-choice tasks, which are often very repetitive. These variations are caused by lower entropy on the repeated content found in the entropy model's context. For the large-scale run of BLT-Entropy with patch size 4.5, the authors reset the entropy context with new lines and use the approximate monotonicity constraint, as it is more robust to these context-dependent variations. Importantly, this change only affects how entropies are computed; the procedure for identifying the value of the entropy threshold remains the same.

Both methods are compared in the experiments, and the choice between them involves a trade-off. The global threshold is simpler and more directly interpretable, but may be sensitive to context-dependent shifts in entropy levels. The approximate monotonicity constraint is more robust to such shifts, but is a relative measure that depends on the local entropy profile rather than an absolute level of uncertainty.

## Incremental Patching: The Formal Property Required for Autoregressive Generation

A crucial consideration for any patching scheme is whether it can be applied *incrementally* during autoregressive generation. During generation, BLT must decide whether the current position in the byte sequence is at a patch boundary or not, because this determines whether the expensive Latent Global Transformer is invoked. Critically, this decision must be made *independently* of the rest of the sequence, which has not yet been generated. The model cannot look ahead to future bytes to determine how the current sequence should be segmented.

This requirement is formalized by the property of *incremental patching*. A patching scheme $f_p$ satisfies the incremental patching property if and only if:

$$f_p(\pmb{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\pmb{x})_{<i}$$

This equation states that the patching decisions made on a prefix of the sequence $\pmb{x}_{<i}$ must be identical to the patching decisions made on the full sequence $\pmb{x}$, restricted to that same prefix. In other words, adding more bytes to the end of the sequence must not change any of the patch boundary decisions made on the earlier bytes. If this property is violated, then the model would need to "re-patch" earlier portions of the sequence every time a new byte is generated, which would be computationally infeasible during autoregressive generation.

Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) tokenization does *not* satisfy the incremental patching property. This is because the same prefix can be tokenized differently depending on what bytes follow it. BPE works by greedily merging the most frequent byte pairs in the vocabulary, and the optimal merge decisions at a given position can depend on what bytes are available to merge with in the subsequent context. As a result, BPE cannot be applied incrementally during generation—the tokenizer would need to see the full sequence to determine the tokenization, which is impossible when the sequence is being generated one byte at a time. This is why BPE-based models use tokens as their basic unit of generation, rather than bytes.

The paper notes that using a special delimiter token to indicate patch boundaries can turn BPE into an incremental patching scheme, but this increases the byte-sequence length, negating some of the efficiency benefits. Entropy patching, by contrast, naturally satisfies the incremental patching property: the entropy at position $i$ depends only on the preceding bytes $\pmb{x}_{<i}$, and thus the patch boundary decision at position $i$ is determined entirely by the prefix and is not affected by any future bytes. This makes entropy patching (as well as space patching and strided patching) suitable for autoregressive generation, unlike BPE.

## Comparison of Patches (Dynamic, No Fixed Vocabulary) vs. Tokens (BPE, Fixed Vocabulary)

The distinction between patches and tokens is fundamental to understanding the advantages of BLT over tokenization-based models. The paper uses the term "tokens" to refer to byte-groups drawn from a *finite* vocabulary determined prior to training, as produced by tokenizers such as BPE. In contrast, "patches" refer to dynamically grouped sequences of bytes *without* a fixed vocabulary.

A BPE tokenizer, such as the one used in Llama 3, works by pre-computing a fixed vocabulary of tokens (e.g., 128,000 tokens) before the model is trained. Each token corresponds to a specific sequence of bytes, and any input text is tokenized by greedily matching these pre-defined byte sequences. The vocabulary is static—it does not change during training or inference, and the set of possible tokens is fixed at the time of vocabulary construction.

Patches, on the other hand, are not constrained to any pre-defined vocabulary. A patch can be *any* group of bytes, and the grouping is determined dynamically based on the content and context of the data. The entropy patching method, for example, creates patches based on the entropy of next-byte predictions, which depends on the specific context of each sequence. This means that the same byte sequence may be patched differently in different contexts, and the set of possible patches is not limited to any pre-defined set.

A critical difference between patches and tokens is that with tokens, the model has no direct access to the underlying byte features. Once a BPE tokenizer maps a sequence of bytes to a token, the model only sees the token's embedding—it does not see the individual bytes that make up the token. This means the model cannot, for example, inspect the spelling or character-level structure of a token's constituent bytes. In contrast, BLT's architecture is designed so that the model maintains access to byte-level information through the local encoder and decoder, which operate directly on bytes. The local encoder processes the raw bytes within each patch to create patch representations, and the local decoder converts patch representations back into individual bytes. This means that BLT always has access to the byte-level structure of the data, even as it processes patches at a higher level of abstraction.

This difference has significant implications for robustness and character-level understanding. Because token-based models lose access to byte-level information, they struggle with tasks that require understanding the internal structure of words, such as spelling, phonology, or manipulation of individual characters. BLT, by contrast, maintains this information and demonstrates superior performance on character-level tasks, robustness to input noise, and low-resource machine translation.

## How BLT Redefines the Tradeoff Between Vocabulary Size and Compute

A crucial improvement of BLT over tokenization-based models is that it redefines the trade-off between vocabulary size and compute. In standard LLMs, there is an inherent tension between the size of the vocabulary and the computational efficiency of the model.

Increasing the size of the vocabulary in a token-based model means that tokens are, on average, larger—because the tokenizer has more options for merging byte sequences into longer tokens. Larger tokens mean fewer tokens per sequence, which means fewer steps for the model to process the data and thus lower inference cost. However, increasing the vocabulary size also increases the size of the output projection layer of the model, because the model must project from its hidden dimension to a vector of size equal to the vocabulary in order to produce a probability distribution over the next token. This output projection layer can be enormous—for example, Llama 3's 128,000-token vocabulary requires an embedding table that is four times larger than Llama 2's 32,000-token vocabulary. This increased vocabulary size adds significant parameters and computational cost, offsetting some of the savings from having fewer tokens.

This trade-off leaves tokenization-based approaches with very little room to achieve significant variations in token size and inference cost. The vocabulary cannot be made much larger without the embedding table becoming prohibitively expensive, and it cannot be made much smaller without increasing the sequence length and computational cost. As a result, token-based models are effectively stuck in a narrow range of average token sizes—Llama 2 has an average token size of 3.7 bytes, and Llama 3 has an average token size of 4.4 bytes, a modest increase that required a quadrupling of the vocabulary.

BLT breaks free of this trade-off entirely. Because patches do not use a fixed vocabulary, there is no embedding table whose size is tied to the average patch size. The average patch size can be adjusted freely by changing the entropy threshold, without any impact on the size of the model's output projection. The local decoder, which converts patch representations back into bytes, always projects to the same byte vocabulary of size 256, regardless of the average patch size. This means that BLT can use much larger average patch sizes—6 or even 8 bytes—without any penalty in terms of model parameters.

The computational savings from larger patch sizes are significant. Inference flops are inversely proportional to the average patch size, so using a patch size of 8 bytes instead of the 4.4 bytes of Llama 3 would lead to nearly 50% inference flop savings. Moreover, these savings can be reallocated: because the global latent Transformer is run less often with larger patches, the saved compute can be used to grow the size of the global Transformer, increasing model capacity without increasing inference cost. This is the key insight behind BLT's inference-flop-controlled scaling experiments, which show that BLT models achieve better scaling trends than tokenization-based architectures by simultaneously increasing both patch size and model size within a fixed inference budget.

The paper observes that larger patch sizes appear to perform better as model and data size increase. BLT with a patch size of 8 starts at a significantly worse point compared to BPE Llama 2 at 1B parameters, but ends up better than BPE at 7B scale. This suggests that larger patch sizes might perform even better at larger scales, and that even larger patch sizes could be feasible as model size and training compute grow. This is a scaling dimension that is simply unavailable to tokenization-based models, which are constrained by the vocabulary size trade-off.

# The BLT Architecture

## Architecture Overview: Global Autoregressive Model Plus Local Encoder and Decoder

The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) is built upon a fundamental rethinking of how language models process raw byte sequences. Rather than relying on a fixed-vocabulary tokenizer that groups bytes into predefined tokens before training, BLT dynamically groups bytes into patches and processes these patches through a carefully designed three-component architecture. The key insight is that not all bytes require the same amount of computational effort: some bytes, such as the first character of a new word, involve high-entropy predictions that benefit from deep computation, while others, such as the ending bytes of a predictable word, are low-entropy and can be handled with much lighter computation. The architecture is designed to allocate compute accordingly.

At the highest level, BLT consists of three interconnected transformer-based modules. The first is a large **Latent Global Transformer** ($\mathcal{G}$), which serves as the primary computational engine of the model. This global model operates not on individual bytes, but on **patch representations**—compact, latent vectors that each encode a dynamically sized group of bytes. Because the global transformer processes patches rather than bytes, it runs far fewer computational steps than a byte-level model would require, yet it handles the most cognitively demanding predictions.

Flanking the global transformer are two smaller, lightweight models: the **Local Encoder** ($\mathcal{E}$) and the **Local Decoder** ($\mathcal{D}$). The local encoder's job is to take a sequence of raw input bytes and efficiently compress them into the patch representations that the global transformer consumes. The local decoder performs the inverse operation: it takes the output patch representations produced by the global transformer and expands them back into a sequence of raw bytes. Both local models have far fewer layers than the global transformer ($l_{\mathcal{E}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$ and $l_{\mathcal{D}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$), reflecting their role as efficient auxiliary modules rather than the primary reasoning engine.

This architectural decomposition is what enables BLT to achieve both the flexibility of byte-level modeling and the efficiency of token-level modeling. The global transformer concentrates expensive compute where it matters most—on the high-level, high-entropy decisions that occur at patch boundaries—while the local models handle the more routine, byte-level predictions at a fraction of the cost. The flow of information proceeds as follows: raw bytes enter the local encoder, which pools them into patch representations; the global transformer processes these patch representations autoregressively; and the local decoder expands the resulting output patch representations back into individual bytes. The global transformer consumes the bulk of the floating-point operations (FLOPs) during both pre-training and inference, so the choice of when to invoke it—determined by the patching scheme—directly controls the computational cost of the model.

## The Latent Global Transformer: Block-Causal Attention and the Main Computational Engine

The Latent Global Transformer, denoted $\mathcal{G}$, is the heart of the BLT architecture. It is an autoregressive transformer model with $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers that maps a sequence of latent input patch representations $\{p_j\}$ into a sequence of output patch representations $\{o_j\}$. The subscript $j$ is used throughout to index patches, while $i$ is reserved for indexing individual bytes—a convention that helps clarify which level of granularity the model is operating at. The global transformer's architecture closely follows that of modern transformer-based LLMs, with feed-forward layers and self-attention mechanisms that constitute the bulk of the model's parameters and computational cost.

A critical design choice is the **block-causal attention mask** used by the global transformer. In a standard causal (autoregressive) language model, each position in the sequence can attend to itself and all preceding positions, but not to any future positions. The block-causal mask extends this idea to the patch level: each patch representation can attend to all patches up to and including the current patch within the current document, but cannot attend to future patches or patches that belong to a different document. This ensures that the model remains autoregressive at the patch level—it can only use information from patches that have already been processed—and that document boundaries are respected, preventing information from leaking across separate documents during training.

The global transformer's role as the main computational engine cannot be overstated. Because the feed-forward network layers in a transformer are applied at every step of the sequence, and because these layers typically dominate the parameter count and FLOP count of large models, the number of steps at which the global transformer operates directly determines the model's computational cost. In a tokenization-based LLM, this number of steps is determined by the number of tokens, which is in turn determined by the tokenizer's vocabulary size—a fixed property set before training. In BLT, however, the number of steps is determined by the number of patches, which is controlled by the patching function. By dynamically selecting longer patches when the data is predictable and shorter patches when it is complex, BLT can allocate more compute to the global transformer exactly where it is needed, and less compute where the predictions are easy.

This dynamic allocation is what distinguishes BLT from both tokenization-based models and prior byte-level architectures. In a tokenization-based model, every token receives the same amount of computation regardless of whether it represents a high-entropy prediction (such as the first word of a sentence) or a low-entropy one (such as the suffix of a common word). In a naive byte-level model, every byte receives a full transformer step, which is prohibitively expensive at scale. BLT threads this needle by using the global transformer only at patch boundaries—points of high entropy where the model's predictive power is most needed—and delegating the remaining byte-level predictions to the more efficient local models.

## The Local Encoder: Lightweight Transformer with Cross-Attention for Pooling Bytes into Patch Representations

The Local Encoder, denoted $\mathcal{E}$, is responsible for transforming a sequence of raw input bytes into the patch representations that the global transformer consumes. It is a lightweight transformer-based model with $l_{\mathcal{E}}$ layers, where $l_{\mathcal{E}}$ is much smaller than $l_{\mathcal{G}}$, the number of layers in the global transformer. The encoder's architecture follows the standard transformer design but introduces a critical modification: the addition of a **cross-attention layer** after each transformer layer, whose function is to pool byte-level representations into patch-level representations.

The processing pipeline of the local encoder begins with the input bytes. Each byte $b_i$ is embedded using an embedding matrix of shape $\mathbb{R}^{256 \times h_{\mathcal{E}}}$, where 256 is the size of the byte vocabulary and $h_{\mathcal{E}}$ is the hidden dimensionality of the encoder. These initial embeddings, denoted $x_i$, capture only the identity of the individual byte at position $i$. To enrich these embeddings with contextual information about the surrounding bytes, the encoder augments them with **hash n-gram embeddings** before any transformer layers are applied—a technique discussed in detail in the next section.

After augmentation, the byte representations pass through a series of alternating **transformer layers** and **cross-attention layers**. The transformer layers in the encoder use a **local block-causal attention mask**: each byte attends to a fixed window of $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ preceding bytes. This windowed attention is a departure from the full block-causal attention used in the global transformer, and it reflects the encoder's role as a local, efficient processor. The window can cross dynamic patch boundaries—meaning a byte near the end of one patch can attend to bytes in the preceding patch—but it cannot cross document boundaries, ensuring that information does not leak between separate documents.

The cross-attention layers that follow each transformer layer are where the actual pooling from bytes to patches occurs. Each cross-attention layer takes the byte-level hidden representations produced by the preceding transformer layer and, using a query mechanism based on patch representations, aggregates the byte representations into updated patch representations. This design is inspired by the Perceiver architecture, with the key difference that in BLT, the "latent" representations correspond to variable, dynamically determined patches rather than a fixed set of latent vectors. Each patch query attends only to the bytes that belong to that specific patch, ensuring that the resulting patch representation faithfully encodes the information contained in its constituent bytes.

The output of the encoder is a set of patch representations $P \in \mathbb{R}^{n_p \times h_{\mathcal{G}}}$, where $n_p$ is the number of patches and $h_{\mathcal{G}}$ is the hidden dimensionality of the global transformer. These representations serve as the input to the global transformer. Because the encoder's hidden dimensionality $h_{\mathcal{E}}$ is typically smaller than $h_{\mathcal{G}}$, the cross-attention mechanism maintains the patch representations as multiple heads of dimension $h_{\mathcal{E}}$ during the attention computation, and concatenates them into $h_{\mathcal{G}}$ dimensions afterward. This design allows the encoder to operate efficiently at a smaller dimensionality while still producing representations that are compatible with the larger global transformer.

## Encoder Hash n-gram Embeddings: Incorporating Byte n-gram Context via Hash Functions and Embedding Tables

A key challenge in byte-level modeling is that individual bytes carry very little information on their own. A single byte value, such as the ASCII code for the letter "a," tells the model nothing about the surrounding context—is this "a" the start of a word, the middle of a word, or part of a longer sequence? In tokenization-based models, this problem is sidestepped because tokens already encode multi-byte subword units. In BLT, the local encoder must build up this contextual information from scratch, starting from individual bytes. The **hash n-gram embeddings** are a critical mechanism for doing so efficiently.

The idea is to model each byte $b_i$ not just individually, but also as part of a **byte n-gram**—a sequence of $n$ consecutive bytes ending at position $i$. For each byte position $i$ and each n-gram size $n$ from 3 to 8, the encoder constructs a byte-gram:

$$g_{i,n} = \{b_{i-n+1}, \ldots, b_i\}$$

These n-grams capture local context at multiple scales: a 3-gram might capture a common letter trigram like "ing," while an 8-gram might capture an entire short word or a meaningful subword unit. The n-gram sizes from 3 to 8 are chosen to cover a range of local contexts without being so large that the embedding tables become unwieldy or the n-grams become too sparse. When $i < n$, meaning there are not enough preceding bytes to form an n-gram of size $n$, that n-gram size is simply omitted for that position.

To represent these n-grams, the encoder uses **hash n-gram embeddings**. Each byte n-gram $g_{i,n}$ is mapped through a hash function to an index in a fixed-size embedding table $E_n^{hash}$, with a separate table for each n-gram size $n \in \{3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8\}$. The hash function used is a **RollPolyHash**, and the index is computed as:

$$\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}) = \text{RollPolyHash}(g_{i,n}) \% |E_n^{hash}|$$

The use of hashing is a practical necessity: the number of possible n-grams grows exponentially with $n$, so maintaining a full embedding table for every possible n-gram would be infeasible. Hashing allows the encoder to use fixed-size embedding tables while still assigning distinct (or near-distinct) embeddings to most observed n-grams. The potential for hash collisions—where two different n-grams map to the same embedding—is mitigated by using sufficiently large embedding tables. In the paper's experiments, 500,000 hash entries with a single hash function are used for all BLT models.

The augmented embedding for byte position $i$ is then computed by summing the individual byte embedding with all the n-gram hash embeddings:

$$e_i = x_i + \sum_{n=3,\ldots,8} E_n^{hash}(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}))$$

This sum is then normalized by dividing by the number of n-gram sizes plus one (to account for the individual byte embedding). The normalization ensures that the scale of the augmented embedding remains consistent regardless of how many n-gram sizes are applicable at a given position. The resulting normalized embedding $e_i$ is passed as input to the local encoder model.

The hash n-gram embeddings serve a role analogous to what tokenization provides in token-based models: they give the encoder access to local, multi-byte context from the very first layer. Without them, the encoder's transformer layers would need to build up this context entirely through attention, which would require more layers and more computation. Experiments in the paper show that hash embeddings are vital to bringing BLT's performance to match that of tokenizer-based models, with particularly strong benefits on Wikipedia and GitHub data. After approximately 300,000 hash entries, however, there are diminishing returns, and the gains from hash embeddings appear to be largely complementary with the gains from cross-attention, as they improve performance on different datasets.

## Encoder Multi-Headed Cross-Attention: Perceiver-Style Attention with Patch-Specific Masking and Pooling Initialization

The cross-attention mechanism in the local encoder is one of the most distinctive components of the BLT architecture. It is the mechanism by which byte-level representations are pooled into patch representations, and it is what allows the encoder to bridge the gap between the fine-grained byte level and the coarser patch level. The design closely follows the input cross-attention module of the **Perceiver architecture**, with one major difference: in the Perceiver, the latent representations are a fixed set of learned vectors, while in BLT, they correspond to variable, dynamically determined patch representations.

The cross-attention module operates on two sets of representations: **queries**, which correspond to patch representations, and **keys/values**, which correspond to byte representations from the preceding transformer layer. The query vector for each patch $p_j$ is initialized by **pooling** the byte representations corresponding to that patch—specifically, max-pooling is used in the paper's experiments—and then applying a linear projection $\mathcal{E}_C \in \mathbb{R}^{h_{\mathcal{E}} \times (h_{\mathcal{E}} \times U_{\mathcal{E}})}$, where $U_{\mathcal{E}}$ is the number of encoder cross-attention heads. Formally, if $f_{\text{bytes}}(p_j)$ denotes the sequence of bytes corresponding to patch $p_j$, then:

$$P_{0,j} = \mathcal{E}_C(f_{\text{bytes}}(p_j))$$

where $f$ is the pooling function. This initialization is important because it gives the cross-attention a meaningful starting point: rather than beginning with a random or fixed query, the query for each patch is initialized with information drawn from the very bytes it is meant to represent.

The cross-attention computation itself follows the standard scaled dot-product attention formula. At each cross-attention layer $l$, the patch representations are updated as:

$$P_l = P_{l-1} + W_o\left(\text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V\right)$$

where the queries, keys, and values are computed as:

$$Q_j = W_q(P_{l-1,j}), \quad K_i = W_k(h_{l-1,i}), \quad V_i = W_v(h_{l-1,i})$$

Here, $W_q$, $W_k$, $W_v$, and $W_o$ are the projection matrices for queries, keys, values, and the output, respectively. The keys and values are projections of the byte representations $h_{l-1,i}$ from the previous transformer layer (or the augmented embeddings $e_i$ for the first cross-attention layer). A crucial aspect of this design is the **patch-specific masking strategy**: each query $Q_j$ only attends to the keys and values that correspond to the bytes in patch $j$. This means that the cross-attention is not global—the representation for a given patch is computed solely from the bytes that constitute that patch, not from bytes belonging to other patches. This ensures that each patch representation faithfully encodes only the information from its constituent bytes.

Because the cross-attention uses multiple heads (following the standard multi-headed attention paradigm) and the patch representations have a larger dimensionality ($h_{\mathcal{G}}$) than the byte representations ($h_{\mathcal{E}}$), the design maintains $P_l$ as multiple heads of dimension $h_{\mathcal{E}}$ during the cross-attention computation. These heads are later concatenated into $h_{\mathcal{G}}$ dimensions, producing patch representations that are directly compatible with the global transformer. This dimensional upscaling through concatenation is an elegant way to bridge the smaller encoder and the larger global model without requiring expensive projection matrices.

Additional design details of the encoder cross-attention include the use of **pre-LayerNorm** on the queries, keys, and values before the attention computation, the absence of **positional embeddings** in the cross-attention module (since positional information is already captured by the byte representations from the transformer layers), and a **residual connection** around the cross-attention block, which allows the patch representations to accumulate information across multiple cross-attention layers while preserving the information from earlier layers.

## The Local Decoder: Decoding Global Patch Representations Back into Raw Bytes

The Local Decoder, denoted $\mathcal{D}$, performs the inverse operation of the local encoder: it takes the output patch representations produced by the global transformer and decodes them into a sequence of raw bytes. Like the encoder, the decoder is a lightweight transformer-based model with $l_{\mathcal{D}}$ layers, where $l_{\mathcal{D}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$. Its role is to expand each patch representation back into the individual bytes that it represents, predicting the byte sequence autoregressively as a function of previously decoded bytes.

A key architectural difference between the decoder and the encoder is the order in which cross-attention and transformer layers are applied. In the encoder, the transformer layer is applied first (to process the byte representations), followed by the cross-attention layer (to pool bytes into patches). In the decoder, this order is **reversed**: the cross-attention layer is applied first, to create byte-level representations from the patch representations, and then the transformer layer operates on the resulting byte sequence. This ordering reflects the decoder's function: it must first "unpack" the patch representation into byte-level representations, and then process those byte-level representations to make byte-by-byte predictions.

The decoder takes as input not only the output patch representations $o_j$ from the global transformer, but also the **hidden representations produced by the local encoder** for the byte sequence. This is a crucial design choice: by reusing the encoder's hidden states, the decoder has access to the byte-level features that the encoder already computed, which provides valuable context for predicting the next bytes. Specifically, the initial byte representations for the decoder's cross-attention are initialized as the byte embeddings from the last encoder layer, denoted $h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$. This connection between the encoder and decoder means that information flows not only through the patch representations (via the global transformer) but also directly at the byte level, creating a richer information pathway.

The decoder predicts a sequence of raw bytes $y_i$ as a function of previously decoded bytes. Because it operates at the byte level, the decoder must produce output over the full byte vocabulary of size 256, which requires an output projection layer with a vocabulary dimension of 256. This is one of the few places in the BLT architecture where a vocabulary projection is needed, and it is much smaller than the vocabulary projections used in tokenization-based models (which typically have vocabularies of 32,000 to 128,000 tokens). The decoder's transformer layers use a local attention window of size $w_{\mathcal{D}}$, similar to the encoder's windowed attention, allowing each byte to attend to a fixed window of preceding bytes.

The decoder plays a critical role in BLT's efficiency. Because the global transformer operates at the patch level, it cannot directly produce byte-level predictions. The decoder fills this gap, but at a much lower computational cost than running the global transformer on every byte. The decoder's lightweight design—with few layers and a small hidden dimensionality—means that the cost of byte-level prediction is kept low, while the bulk of the model's reasoning capacity is concentrated in the global transformer's patch-level processing.

## Decoder Multi-Headed Cross-Attention: Reversed Query/Key-Value Roles Compared to the Encoder

The cross-attention mechanism in the local decoder mirrors that of the encoder, but with a critical inversion: the roles of queries and keys/values are **interchanged**. In the encoder cross-attention, the patch representations serve as queries, and the byte representations serve as keys and values—the model is asking, "for each patch, what information should I extract from the constituent bytes?" In the decoder cross-attention, the byte representations serve as queries, and the patch representations serve as keys and values—the model is asking, "for each byte, what information should I extract from the patch representation to help predict this byte?" This reversal reflects the decoder's function: rather than pooling bytes into patches, it must distribute patch-level information back out to the individual bytes.

The initial byte representations for the cross-attention are initialized as the byte embeddings from the last encoder layer, $h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$. This initialization provides the decoder with a starting point that already contains rich byte-level features computed by the encoder. The subsequent byte representations for each decoder layer $l$, denoted $d_{l,i}$, are computed through the following sequence of operations:

$$D_0 = h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$$

$$B_l = D_{l-1} + W_o\left(\text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V\right)$$

where the queries, keys, and values are:

$$Q_i = W_q(d_{l-1,i}), \quad K_j = W_k(\mathcal{D}_C(o_j)), \quad V_j = W_v(\mathcal{D}_C(o_j))$$

Here, $W_q$ is the query projection operating on the byte representations $d_{l-1}$ from the previous decoder transformer layer (or $h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$ for the first layer), while $W_k$ and $W_v$ are the key and value projection matrices operating on the patch representations $o_j$ from the global model, processed through a linear transformation and split operation $\mathcal{D}_C$. The output $B \in \mathbb{R}^{h_{\mathcal{D}} \times n_b}$, where $n_b$ is the number of output bytes, is then passed through a decoder transformer layer to produce the next decoder representations $D_l$.

As in the encoder cross-attention, the decoder cross-attention uses multiple heads, pre-LayerNorm on the queries, keys, and values, no positional embeddings, and a residual connection around the cross-attention module. The residual connection is particularly important here because it allows the decoder to retain the byte-level information from the encoder (initialized in $D_0$) while augmenting it with patch-level information from the global transformer's outputs.

The conceptual elegance of this design is that the decoder cross-attention acts as an information **broadcast** mechanism: the global transformer's patch representation, which encodes high-level information about the entire patch, is distributed to each byte within that patch through the attention mechanism. Each byte query attends to the patch key/value and extracts the information most relevant to predicting that particular byte. This allows the decoder to produce different byte predictions from the same patch representation, depending on the position of the byte within the patch and the context provided by previously decoded bytes.

## Architectural Details: SwiGLU, RoPE, RMSNorm, Flash Attention, and Flex Attention for Dynamic Masks

The transformer blocks in BLT—both the local models and the global transformer—largely follow the architecture of Llama 3, incorporating several modern design choices that have been shown to improve the performance and training stability of large language models.

The **SwiGLU activation function** is used in the feed-forward layers of all transformer blocks. SwiGLU is a gated linear unit that combines element-wise multiplication with a Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU) gating mechanism, providing a smooth, differentiable activation that has been shown to outperform standard ReLU or GELU activations in large-scale transformer models. The use of SwiGLU in the feed-forward layers is consistent across the local encoder, local decoder, and global transformer.

**Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE)** with a base frequency of $\theta = 500000$ are used, but only in the self-attention layers. RoPE encodes positional information by applying a rotation to the query and key vectors based on their position in the sequence, allowing the model to capture relative positional relationships without requiring explicit positional embedding parameters. The choice of $\theta = 500000$ follows the Llama 3 configuration. Importantly, RoPE is not used in the cross-attention layers, as positional information in those layers is already captured by the byte representations from the preceding transformer layers. The distinction between where RoPE is and is not applied reflects a careful design philosophy: positional embeddings are used where they add value (in self-attention, where the model needs to understand the relative positions of elements in the sequence) and omitted where they would be redundant (in cross-attention, where the positional information is already encoded in the input representations).

**RMSNorm** (Root Mean Square Normalization) is used for layer normalization throughout all transformer blocks. RMSNorm is a simplified variant of LayerNorm that normalizes by the root mean square of the activations without subtracting the mean, reducing computational cost while maintaining the benefits of normalization for training stability. The use of pre-normalization (applying normalization before the attention or feed-forward computation, rather than after) is standard in modern transformer architectures and is employed throughout BLT.

For attention implementation, BLT leverages two different technologies depending on the type of attention mask being used. **Flash Attention** is used for all self-attention layers that employ fixed, standard attention masks, such as the block-causal mask in the global transformer or the fixed-window block-causal mask in the local models. Flash Attention is a highly optimized implementation that reduces memory usage and improves speed by avoiding the materialization of the full attention matrix, making it essential for efficient training and inference at scale.

However, the cross-attention layers in the encoder and decoder present a unique challenge: they involve **dynamic, patch-dependent masks** that vary based on the specific patch boundaries in each input sequence. Unlike the fixed block-causal or windowed masks used in self-attention, the patch-specific masking in cross-attention cannot be precomputed or represented as a simple, regular pattern. To handle this efficiently, BLT uses **Flex Attention**, a PyTorch feature that allows for the creation of fused implementations of attention with arbitrary, dynamically determined masks. Flex Attention compiles the attention computation with the specific mask pattern into an efficient kernel, significantly speeding up training compared to a naive implementation that would need to apply the mask at runtime.

The window size for fixed-width attention masks is set to 512 bytes in both the local encoder and local decoder. This means that in the local models, each byte can attend to the 512 preceding bytes, providing a substantial local context window without the computational cost of full sequence-length attention. The global transformer, by contrast, uses full block-causal attention with no window restriction, as its sequence length (in patches) is much shorter and full attention is computationally feasible.

These architectural details, while individually consistent with modern transformer design, collectively contribute to BLT's ability to operate efficiently at the byte level. The combination of SwiGLU for expressive feed-forward computation, RoPE for positional awareness, RMSNorm for training stability, and the dual attention implementation strategy (Flash Attention for fixed masks, Flex Attention for dynamic masks) ensures that the model can be trained and deployed at scale without sacrificing the flexibility that comes from dynamic patching and byte-level modeling. The careful selection of which techniques to apply where—such as using RoPE only in self-attention and using windowed attention only in the local models—reflects a deep understanding of the different computational roles played by each component of the architecture.

# Experimental Setup and Evaluation Metrics

## Pre-training Datasets

The experimental validation of the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) requires carefully curated training data to ensure that comparisons with tokenization-based baselines are both fair and informative. To this end, the authors utilize two distinct pre-training datasets, each serving a specific purpose within the broader experimental framework.

The first dataset is the Llama 2 dataset, which comprises approximately two trillion tokens collected from a variety of publicly available sources. These sources are subsequently cleaned and filtered to improve overall data quality. The Llama 2 dataset serves as the foundation for scaling law experiments, where the goal is to determine the optimal number of training tokens as prescribed by the Llama 3 scaling laws. This dataset is used to identify the best architectural choices for BLT by enabling controlled comparisons across different model sizes and patching strategies. By adhering to the compute-optimal training regime established for Llama 3, the authors ensure that BLT models are evaluated under the same theoretical optimality conditions that govern tokenization-based models, providing a robust and rigorous baseline.

The second dataset, referred to as BLT-1T, is a newly constructed dataset containing one trillion tokens gathered from various public sources, including a subset of the pre-training data released by Datacomp-LM. The BLT-1T dataset is used for a complete pre-training run that enables direct comparison with Llama 3 on downstream benchmark tasks. This dataset is designed to be of higher quality than the Llama 2 dataset and provides a larger and more diverse training corpus for evaluating the full capabilities of BLT models beyond the compute-optimal regime. The authors note that neither dataset includes any data gathered from Meta products or services, ensuring that the experimental results are reproducible and not dependent on proprietary data sources.

For baseline experiments involving tokenizer-based models, the authors use the Llama 3 tokenizer with a vocabulary size of 128,000 tokens. This choice is motivated by the observation that the Llama 3 tokenizer produces stronger baseline performance than the Llama 2 tokenizer, which has a smaller vocabulary of 32,000 tokens. By selecting the stronger tokenizer as the baseline, the authors ensure that any improvements demonstrated by BLT are not artifacts of a weak baseline but represent genuine architectural advantages.

## Entropy Model

A central innovation of BLT is entropy patching, which dynamically segments byte sequences into patches based on the predictability of the next byte. This patching strategy requires a mechanism for estimating the entropy of next-byte predictions, and the authors fulfill this role with a separately trained byte-level language model.

The entropy model used in the experiments is a byte-level autoregressive transformer trained on the same data distribution as the full BLT model. Unless otherwise specified, the entropy model consists of 100 million parameters, 14 transformer layers, and a hidden dimensionality of 512. It employs sliding window attention with a window size of 512 bytes, meaning that the model's predictions at any given byte position are conditioned on the preceding 512 bytes. The remaining hyperparameters of the entropy model, such as the activation function, layer normalization, and positional encoding strategy, are kept consistent with those used in the local and global transformers of the BLT architecture.

The entropy model computes, for each byte position $i$ in a sequence, the entropy of the next-byte prediction under its learned distribution $p_e$ over the byte vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$. Specifically, the entropy at position $i$ is calculated as:

$$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i})$$

This entropy value quantifies the uncertainty of the next-byte prediction: a high entropy indicates that the next byte is difficult to predict given the preceding context, while a low entropy indicates that the next byte is highly predictable. The patching mechanism uses these entropy values to identify natural boundaries where the model should allocate additional computational resources.

The authors explore different configurations of the entropy model to understand how its size and receptive field affect scaling performance. In ablation studies, they train byte-level entropy models with sizes ranging from 1 million to 100 million parameters and context window lengths from 64 to 512 bytes. Their findings indicate that scaling performance is positively correlated with both the size of the entropy model and the length of its context window, though with diminishing returns once the model exceeds approximately 50 million parameters. An important practical observation is that when the receptive field of the entropy model is sufficiently small, the trained model can be encoded as an efficient lookup table, substantially reducing the computational overhead of patching during both training and inference.

## Entropy Threshold Selection and Context Handling for Structured Content

Once the entropy model produces per-byte entropy values, the next step is to convert these values into actual patch boundaries. The authors experiment with two methods for identifying boundaries given the entropy sequence $H(x_i)$. The first method uses a global constraint, marking a patch boundary at any position where the entropy exceeds a fixed global threshold $\theta_g$:

$$H(x_t) > \theta_g$$

The second method uses an approximate monotonicity constraint, identifying boundaries at positions where the entropy increases relative to the previous position by more than a threshold $\theta_r$:

$$H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r$$

This second approach can be interpreted as identifying points that break the approximate monotonically decreasing entropy trend within a patch. The intuition is that within a patch, entropy tends to decrease as the model gains context about the current word or phrase, and a sudden increase signals the beginning of a new, harder prediction.

For models using entropy-based patching, the authors estimate a patching threshold that achieves a desired average patch size on the pre-training data mixture. This threshold is determined during a lightweight preprocessing step that is executed during data loading, making it distinct from prior approaches that train a separate classifier to predict entropy-based patch boundaries.

The authors observe an important empirical phenomenon when applying entropy patching to structured content such as multiple-choice tasks. In such content, which is often highly repetitive, entropy patching yields progressively larger patches. This occurs because the entropy model encounters lower entropy on repeated content within its context window, leading to an effect the authors term "entropy drift." For example, on MMLU benchmarks, the repeated formatting of multiple-choice questions causes the entropy model to assign low entropy to many byte positions, resulting in unusually large patches that may not be appropriate for the task.

To mitigate this issue, for the large-scale BLT-Entropy model with an average patch size of 4.5 bytes, the authors reset the entropy model's context at new lines. This means that the entropy model's context is cleared whenever a new line is encountered, preventing the accumulation of low-entropy context from repetitive structured content. Additionally, they use the approximate monotonicity constraint rather than the global threshold, as the monotonicity approach suffers less from entropy drift caused by changes in context length. Importantly, this adjustment only affects how entropies are computed; the same procedure is followed to identify the value of the entropy threshold from the training data.

## Equalizing Context Length for Fair Comparisons

A critical methodological concern in comparing BLT with tokenization-based models is ensuring that no model receives an unfair advantage from processing longer contexts. Since BLT's patch size can be arbitrarily chosen—unlike the fixed vocabulary of tokenization-based models—larger patch sizes naturally result in fewer patches for the same number of bytes, which could allow the global transformer to attend over a larger effective byte context. To prevent this confounding factor, the authors implement a careful context equalization procedure.

The key principle is that the number of bytes in each batch remains constant in expectation across all models, regardless of their patch size or tokenization scheme. This means that models with larger patch sizes have their sequence lengths proportionally reduced. On the Llama 2 dataset, the authors use an 8,000-byte context, while on the BLT-1T dataset, the context is increased to 16,000 bytes on average. The batch size is maintained at 16 million bytes on average across all experiments.

While the average batch size is held constant, the dynamic nature of patching means that different batches of data yield different ratios of bytes to patches. For implementation efficiency, the authors pack batches of patches to avoid padding steps in the more computationally expensive latent transformer, ensuring that every batch contains the same number of patches. During training, byte sequences are padded and potentially truncated to 12,000 bytes and 24,000 bytes for the Llama 2 and BLT-1T datasets, respectively. This prevents memory spikes that could arise from sequences with unusually large patches.

This equalization procedure is essential for drawing valid conclusions from the scaling experiments. Without it, models with larger patch sizes would effectively see more bytes per training example, confounding the effects of patch size with the effects of context length. By holding the byte-level context constant, the authors isolate the impact of the architectural choices—patching strategy, model size, and compute allocation—from any advantage that might accrue from simply processing longer contexts.

## FLOPs Estimation Methodology

To compare BLT with tokenization-based models in a compute-controlled manner, the authors develop a detailed FLOPs estimation methodology that accounts for the unique architectural components of BLT. The methodology largely follows the equations for transformer FLOPs computation established in the Chinchilla scaling laws, which include FLOPs for feed-forward layers, query-key-value-output (QKVO) projections in self-attention layers, and the computation of attention and output projections.

A notable departure from standard FLOPs accounting is the treatment of the input embedding layer. The authors assume that the input embedding layer is implemented as an efficient lookup operation rather than a dense matrix multiplication, rendering it a zero-FLOP operation. This assumption is reasonable for byte-level models, where the input vocabulary is only 256, making lookup-based embeddings trivially efficient. Following previous work, the backward pass is estimated to have twice the number of FLOPs as the forward pass.

The total FLOPs per byte for BLT models is computed by summing the FLOPs across all architectural components: the local encoder transformer, the global latent transformer, the local decoder transformer, and the cross-attention blocks in both the encoder and decoder. The formula is:

$$\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}} = \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{G}}, l_{\mathcal{G}}, m=n_{ctx}/n_p, V=0) / n_p + \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=w_{\mathcal{E}}, V=0) + \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=w_{\mathcal{D}}, V=256) + \text{Cross Attn. FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=n_p, r=n_p/k) \times k/n_p + \text{Cross Attn. FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=k, r=k/n_p)$$

Here, $n_{ctx}$ is the sequence length in bytes, $n_p$ is the patch size, $r$ is the ratio of queries to key/values in the cross-attention, and $k$ is the ratio of patch-dimension to byte-dimension (i.e., the number of local model splits that concatenate to form a global model representation, with $k=2$ in the experiments). The variable $V$ corresponds to the vocabulary size for the output projection, which is only used in the local decoder (set to 256, the size of the byte vocabulary). The parameter $m$ denotes the context length used for attention, which varies depending on whether a module operates on the byte sequence or the patch sequence.

Several important observations follow from this formulation. First, the global latent transformer's FLOPs are divided by the patch size $n_p$, reflecting the fact that the global transformer processes one representation per patch rather than per byte. This is the primary mechanism by which larger patch sizes reduce inference cost. Second, the local encoder and decoder operate with fixed window attention ($w_{\mathcal{E}}$ and $w_{\mathcal{D}}$), so their FLOPs scale linearly with the number of bytes rather than the number of patches. Third, the cross-attention FLOPs in the encoder are scaled by $k/n_p$, reflecting the ratio of byte-level to patch-level computation. This formulation makes explicit how the total computational budget is distributed across BLT's components and why larger patch sizes preferentially reduce the cost of the global transformer while leaving the byte-level modules largely unaffected.

## Bits-Per-Byte as a Tokenizer-Independent Metric

A fundamental challenge in comparing byte-level models with tokenization-based models is the choice of evaluation metric. Perplexity, the standard metric for language modeling, is inherently tied to the tokenization scheme used by the model. It measures the average uncertainty per token, but since different tokenizers produce different numbers of tokens for the same text, perplexity values are not directly comparable across tokenization schemes. A model with a tokenizer that produces fewer, longer tokens will naturally have lower perplexity, not because it models the data better, but because each prediction is easier when conditioned on more preceding bytes.

To address this incompatibility, the authors adopt Bits-Per-Byte (BPB) as a tokenizer-independent alternative to perplexity. BPB normalizes the cross-entropy loss by the total number of bytes in the data, rather than the number of tokens. Specifically, BPB is defined as:

$$\text{BPB}(x) = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\pmb{x})}{\ln(2) \cdot n_{\text{bytes}}}$$

where $\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\pmb{x})$ is the sum of cross-entropy losses over the data $\pmb{x}$, and $n_{\text{bytes}}$ is the total number of bytes in $\pmb{x}$. The division by $\ln(2)$ converts the natural logarithm-based cross-entropy (measured in nats) into bits, providing a consistent unit of information across all models.

BPB has several desirable properties for comparing byte-level and token-level models. First, it is defined in terms of bytes, which are a universal unit of digital text representation, independent of any particular tokenization scheme. Second, it provides a direct measure of how efficiently a model compresses the data: a lower BPB indicates that the model assigns higher probability to the observed data, corresponding to better compression. Third, BPB enables fair comparison between models with different average token or patch sizes, since the normalization is always with respect to the same byte-level unit.

The authors follow prior work in the byte-level modeling literature, including ByT5, MegaByte, and MambaByte, in adopting BPB as the primary language modeling metric. This choice is essential for the scaling law experiments, where BLT models with different patch sizes must be compared with BPE models with different vocabulary sizes. Without a tokenizer-independent metric, differences in perplexity could be attributed to the tokenization scheme rather than the model's actual predictive capabilities.

## Transformer Architecture Hyperparameters

To ensure that BLT models are directly comparable with state-of-the-art tokenization-based models, the authors design the transformer blocks within BLT to closely follow the architecture of Llama 3. This architectural alignment applies to all transformer blocks in BLT, including both the local models (encoder and decoder) and the global latent transformer.

The feed-forward layers in all transformer blocks use the SwiGLU activation function, which combines the Swish activation with a gating mechanism to improve the expressiveness and efficiency of the feed-forward computation. Rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) with a rotation base $\theta = 500,000$ are applied exclusively in the self-attention layers, providing a relative positional encoding scheme that generalizes better to longer sequences than absolute positional embeddings. Layer normalization is performed using RMSNorm, which is a computationally efficient variant of layer normalization that does not require mean subtraction.

For attention computation, the authors use Flash Attention for all self-attention layers that employ fixed attention masks, such as the block-causal mask used in the global transformer and the fixed-window block-causal mask used in the local models. The window size for fixed-width attention masks is set to 512. However, the cross-attention layers in the encoder and decoder involve dynamic, patch-dependent masks that cannot be handled by standard Flash Attention implementations. To address this, the authors use Flex Attention, a flexible attention implementation that produces fused kernels for arbitrary attention patterns, significantly speeding up training for the cross-attention modules.

This careful alignment with the Llama 3 architecture ensures that any performance differences between BLT and Llama 3 can be attributed to the novel aspects of BLT—namely, the patching mechanism, the byte-level local models, and the cross-attention modules—rather than to differences in the underlying transformer architecture. By holding the transformer design constant, the authors isolate the effects of their architectural innovations.

## BLT-Specific Hyperparameters

The authors conduct experiments across two principal directions: scaling trend analysis and downstream task evaluation. To cover these directions comprehensively, they train models at five different scales: 400 million, 1 billion, 2 billion, 4 billion, and 8 billion parameters. The architecture hyperparameters for each model scale are specified to ensure that the global latent transformer matches the size and architecture of the corresponding BPE transformer used as the baseline.

For the local encoder, the authors use max-pooling to initialize the queries for the first cross-attention layer. This pooling operation aggregates the byte representations within each patch into an initial patch representation, which is then refined through the cross-attention mechanism. The hash n-gram embeddings use 500,000 hash entries with a single hash function, covering n-gram sizes ranging from 3 to 8. These embeddings augment the byte representations with information about preceding byte sequences, compensating for the limited receptive field of the local encoder's fixed-window attention.

The learning rate is set to $4 \times 10^{-4}$ for all models. This choice follows a hyperparameter search conducted at the 400M and 1B model scales, which compared learning rates between $1 \times 10^{-3}$ and $1 \times 10^{-4}$ and found that the same learning rate is optimal for both token-based and BLT models. This finding simplifies the experimental setup, as no separate learning rate tuning is needed for BLT models relative to their BPE counterparts.

For the scaling trend experiments on the Llama 2 dataset, the authors use training batch sizes as recommended by the Llama 3 scaling laws, or their equivalent in bytes. This ensures that both BLT and BPE models are trained under compute-optimal conditions, as determined by the Chinchilla-style scaling analysis. For optimization, the authors use the AdamW optimizer with $\beta_1 = 0.9$ and $\beta_2 = 0.95$, and $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$. The learning rate schedule consists of a linear warm-up of 2,000 steps followed by cosine decay to zero. A weight decay of 0.1 is applied, and global gradient clipping is enforced at a threshold of 1.0 to ensure training stability.

An important BLT-specific consideration is the number of layers in the local models. Ablation studies reveal that when paired with hash n-gram embeddings, BLT performs well with an extremely lightweight encoder consisting of just one layer, while benefiting from a heavier decoder. This asymmetry reflects the different roles of the two local models: the encoder needs only to produce expressive patch representations, a task that is substantially aided by the n-gram embeddings, while the decoder must generate the full byte sequence from patch representations, requiring more computational capacity. For models with larger patch sizes (e.g., patch size 8), the authors use 3 encoder layers instead of 1, reflecting the greater demand on the encoder when patches contain more bytes.

The authors also investigate the effect of n-gram hash embedding vocabulary size, testing settings of 0, 100,000, 200,000, and 400,000 entries. Their findings indicate that hash embeddings improve performance across all domains, with particularly notable improvements on Wikipedia and GitHub data. At the 8B scale, increasing from 300,000 to 500,000 hashes changes performance by approximately 0.001 bits per byte over 15,000 steps, indicating diminishing returns beyond 300,000 hashes. Nevertheless, the hash embeddings are shown to be vital for bringing BLT's performance to match that of tokenizer-based models, and their gains appear largely complementary with the cross-attention improvements, as the two mechanisms provide benefits on different datasets.

# Scaling Trends: Compute-Optimal and Beyond

The question of whether byte-level language models can scale as effectively as tokenization-based models has long been a central challenge in the field. Prior byte-level architectures, while demonstrating promising results at small scales, consistently fell short of matching the performance of tokenizer-based models when scaled up in both parameter count and training data. This chapter presents the core scaling results of the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT), demonstrating that BLT not only matches but in certain regimes exceeds the performance of BPE-based models such as Llama 3 across compute-optimal settings, downstream task evaluations, and fixed-inference-flop scaling regimes. These results represent a landmark achievement: for the first time, a byte-level architecture without fixed-vocabulary tokenization achieves parity with state-of-the-art tokenizer-based models at scales reaching 8B parameters and 4T training bytes.

# Parameter-Matched Compute-Optimal Scaling: BLT vs. BPE Llama 3 on Llama 2 Data

The foundational scaling comparison in this work is conducted under the compute-optimal training regime, which is theoretically designed to achieve the best performance on the training dataset within a given training budget. This regime follows the methodology established by Chinchilla scaling laws, which prescribe an optimal ratio of model parameters to training data for a given computational budget. For the baseline, the authors use BPE models trained with the Llama 3 tokenizer on the Llama 2 dataset, comprising 2 trillion tokens collected from publicly available sources. The compute-optimal ratio of parameters to training data is determined according to the guidelines established by Llama 3, providing a robust and well-calibrated baseline against which BLT can be evaluated.

The experiment proceeds by training compute-optimal BPE and BLT models across four different sizes, ranging from 1B to 8B parameters. For each BPE model, a corresponding BLT model is trained on the same data, using a Latent Global Transformer that matches the size and architecture of the corresponding BPE Transformer. This parameter-matching strategy ensures that the primary comparison isolates the effect of the byte-level patching architecture from confounding differences in model capacity. Training flops are plotted against language modeling performance, measured in Bits-Per-Byte (BPB) on a representative subset of the training data mixture. BPB is used rather than perplexity because perplexity depends on the specific tokenizer used; BPB normalizes the cross-entropy loss by the number of bytes, making it a tokenizer-independent metric that allows fair comparison between byte-level and token-level models.

The results reveal that BLT models either match or outperform their BPE counterparts across all model sizes and flop budgets. This trend holds consistently as model size and training flops are scaled up. To the best of the authors' knowledge, BLT is the first byte-level Transformer architecture to achieve matching scaling trends with BPE-based models in the compute-optimal regime. This finding validates the assumption that the optimal ratio of parameters to training compute, as determined for BPE-based models, also applies to BLT, or at least is not too far off from BLT's own optimum. This is an important practical result, as it means that the extensive scaling law research conducted for tokenizer-based models can be leveraged when designing and training BLT models, without requiring an entirely new scaling law framework from scratch.

# The Role of Architectural Improvements and Dynamic Patching in Matching BPE Scaling

Achieving parity with BPE-based models is not accomplished by any single design choice but rather through the combination of several architectural innovations and the dynamic patching scheme. The paper systematically isolates the contributions of each component to demonstrate that both architectural improvements and dynamic patching are crucial.

To understand the importance of these components, the authors compare several intermediate architectures against Llama 3. The first comparison involves SpaceByte, approximated using BLT with space-patching but without n-gram embeddings and without cross-attention. While SpaceByte improves over MegaByte (which uses static strided patching), it remains far from Llama 3 performance. This demonstrates that simply improving the patching scheme from fixed-size to whitespace-based, while beneficial, is insufficient on its own to close the gap with tokenizer-based models.

The full BLT architecture incorporates two key architectural improvements beyond patching: cross-attention layers in the local encoder and decoder, and hash n-gram embeddings. The cross-attention mechanism in the encoder pools byte representations into patch representations, allowing each patch query to attend only to the bytes within its corresponding patch. This is inspired by the Perceiver architecture but differs in that the latent representations correspond to variable-length patches rather than a fixed set of latent vectors. In the decoder, the roles are reversed: byte representations serve as queries attending to patch representations as keys and values, enabling the model to decode patch-level information back into byte-level predictions. The hash n-gram embeddings augment the byte embeddings with information about preceding byte sequences of lengths 3 through 8, using a rolling polynomial hash function to map n-grams to indices in fixed-size embedding tables. This provides the model with local context beyond what a single byte embedding can capture, analogous to how subword tokens implicitly encode multi-byte patterns.

When both architectural improvements and dynamic entropy-based patching are combined, BLT achieves performance on par with state-of-the-art tokenizer-based models such as Llama 3 at scale. The entropy-based patching scheme, which uses next-byte prediction entropies from a small byte-level language model to identify patch boundaries, ensures that computational resources are allocated where the data is most complex and unpredictable. This is a fundamental departure from tokenization, where the same amount of compute is allocated to every token regardless of prediction difficulty. Together, these innovations allow BLT to match BPE scaling trends, validating the hypothesis that intelligent, data-driven compute allocation combined with expressive byte-to-patch mapping can overcome the historical performance gap between byte-level and token-level models.

# Effect of Patch Size on Scaling: Patch Sizes of 6 and 8 Bytes vs. Llama 2/3 Token Sizes

One of the most striking advantages of BLT over tokenization-based models is the flexibility it provides in controlling the average patch size. In standard tokenization-based LLMs, the average token size is determined by the vocabulary size of the tokenizer, and changing it requires trade-offs that are difficult to navigate. For example, Llama 3 increases the average token size from 3.7 to 4.4 bytes compared to Llama 2, but does so at the cost of increasing the size of its embedding table by 4x. This trade-off between vocabulary size and compute leaves little room for tokenization-based approaches to achieve significant variations in token size and inference cost.

BLT, by contrast, has no fixed vocabulary for patches. The average patch size can be arbitrarily chosen by adjusting the entropy threshold used for patching, with significant implications for computational cost. Since inference flops are inversely proportional to the average patch size, larger patches mean the expensive global Latent Transformer is invoked less frequently, leading to substantial savings. The paper demonstrates that BLT can achieve similar scaling trends with average patch sizes of 6 and even 8 bytes, compared to the BPE tokenizers of Llama 2 and Llama 3, which have average token sizes of 3.7 and 4.4 bytes respectively.

The scaling behavior with different patch sizes reveals an intriguing pattern. At smaller model scales (e.g., 1B parameters), BLT with a patch size of 8 bytes starts at a significantly worse point compared to BPE Llama 2. However, as model size and training compute increase, the larger patch size model catches up and eventually surpasses BPE at around 7B scale. This suggests that larger patch sizes, which may initially seem disadvantageous due to coarser compute allocation, actually perform better at larger scales. The hypothesis is that as models grow, the relative cost of the byte-level local encoder and decoder modules decreases compared to the global Latent Transformer, making the savings from larger patches more impactful. Furthermore, it suggests that even larger patch sizes could be feasible as model size and training compute continue to grow.

A critical methodological detail in these experiments is the equalization of context length. To ensure fair comparison and prevent BLT models with larger patch sizes from gaining an unfair advantage by processing more bytes per context, the authors maintain the same average batch size in bytes across all models. This means that models with larger patch sizes have correspondingly shorter sequence lengths in terms of patches. On the Llama 2 data, an 8k byte context is used, while on the BLT-1T dataset, the context is increased to 16k bytes on average, with a constant batch size of 16M bytes on average.

# Downstream Task Evaluations: BLT-Entropy vs. BLT-Space vs. Llama 3 on Reasoning, Knowledge, and Coding Benchmarks

While language modeling performance measured in Bits-Per-Byte provides a fundamental comparison, the ultimate test of an architecture is its performance on downstream tasks that require reasoning, world knowledge, and code generation. To assess these capabilities, the authors train an 8B BLT model beyond the compute-optimal ratio on the BLT-1T dataset, a larger and higher-quality dataset comprising 1 trillion tokens gathered from various public sources including a subset of the Datacomp-LM pre-training data. Three models are compared on this dataset, all trained with an equivalent flop budget: a BPE Llama 3 tokenizer-based model, a BLT model employing space-patching (BLT-Space), and a BLT model utilizing entropy-based patching (BLT-Entropy).

The evaluation suite encompasses both classification and generation tasks. The classification tasks include ARC-Easy (0-shot), ARC-Challenge (0-shot), HellaSwag (0-shot), PIQA (0-shot), and MMLU (5-shot), covering common sense reasoning and world knowledge. For these tasks, a prompt-scoring method is employed, calculating the likelihood over choice characters and reporting average accuracy. The coding-related generation tasks include MBPP (3-shot) and HumanEval (0-shot), which evaluate the ability to generate Python code, with pass@1 scores reported.

The BLT-Entropy model outperforms the Llama 3 model on 4 out of 7 tasks, a notable achievement for a byte-level model trained on the same number of bytes. The authors attribute this improvement to two factors: better use of training compute via dynamic patching, which allocates more compute to harder predictions, and the direct modeling of byte-level information as opposed to tokens, which provides the model with finer-grained access to the data. On the other hand, BLT-Space underperforms the Llama 3 tokenizer on all but one task. However, it achieves a significant reduction in inference flops due to its larger average patch size of 6 bytes, compared to approximately 4.5 bytes for both the BPE and entropy-patching based models on the training data mix. This larger patch size means that, with the same training budget, the BLT-Space model covers 30% more data than the other two models, which may push it further from the compute-optimal point and partially explain its lower downstream performance. These results highlight the trade-off between inference efficiency and task performance, and suggest that entropy-based patching provides a better balance between the two.

# Inference-Time Entropy Threshold Adjustment for Improved Task Performance

A unique feature of entropy-based patching is that the entropy threshold, which determines when a new patch boundary is created, can be adjusted at inference time without retraining the model. A lower threshold means more patch boundaries, leading to more frequent invocation of the global Latent Transformer and thus more compute per byte, while a higher threshold means fewer boundaries and less compute. This provides a flexible mechanism for trading compute for performance at inference time, which is not available in tokenization-based models where the token size is fixed by the tokenizer.

For the BLT-Entropy model, the entropy threshold during training is set to 0.6 on the training data mix, yielding an average patch size of approximately 4.5 bytes. At inference time, the threshold is adjusted to 0.1, which is lower and thus creates more patch boundaries, effectively increasing the number of global transformer steps. This adjustment is found to improve task performance at the cost of more inference steps. The improvement is intuitive: by lowering the threshold, the model allocates more compute to predictions that are moderately uncertain, which may be particularly beneficial for structured tasks like multiple-choice questions where the model benefits from finer-grained processing.

This inference-time adjustment capability is a significant practical advantage of BLT. It allows practitioners to dynamically tune the compute-performance trade-off based on the specific application or task at hand, without needing to train separate models for different operating points. In contrast, a tokenizer-based model like Llama 3 has a fixed token size determined at training time, and the only way to change it is to retrain the model with a different tokenizer, which is expensive and may not be feasible in practice.

# Patches Scale Better Than Tokens: Simultaneously Increasing Model and Patch Size Under a Fixed Inference Budget

The most novel scaling insight of this work is the demonstration that patches scale better than tokens, in the sense that BLT models can simultaneously increase both model size and patch size while maintaining a fixed inference flop budget. This is a capability that is fundamentally unavailable to tokenization-based models due to the rigid trade-off between vocabulary size and compute that tokenization imposes.

The mechanism works as follows. Increasing the average patch size means that the global Latent Transformer, which consumes the bulk of the flops during both training and inference, is invoked less frequently. The compute saved by this reduction can be reallocated to grow the size of the global Latent Transformer itself, since it processes fewer patches per sequence and therefore can afford to have more parameters per patch. In other words, the model can be made larger (in terms of the global transformer parameters) while keeping the total inference flops constant, because the larger model processes fewer patches. This creates a new dimension for scaling that is orthogonal to the traditional approach of simply increasing model size and training data.

In tokenization-based models, the analogous operation would be to increase the vocabulary size to create larger tokens on average, thereby reducing the number of tokens processed. However, increasing the vocabulary size requires increasing the size of the embedding table and the output projection layer, which adds parameters that do not contribute proportionally to model capacity. The Llama 3 tokenizer, for instance, achieves an average token size of 4.4 bytes by using a vocabulary of 128K tokens, which requires an embedding table 4x larger than Llama 2's 32K vocabulary. This trade-off effectively limits the practical range of token sizes that can be explored. BLT, by contrast, can freely adjust the patch size by changing the entropy threshold, with no increase in embedding table size since the byte vocabulary is fixed at 256.

This new scaling dimension has significant practical implications. In real-world deployments, inference cost is often the binding constraint, and the ability to deploy a larger, more capable model within a fixed inference budget is highly valuable. The traditional approach to scaling models within a fixed inference budget is to use techniques like quantization or distillation, which reduce model size at some cost to performance. BLT offers a complementary approach: by increasing the patch size, the model can be made larger while keeping inference flops constant, potentially achieving better performance than a smaller model with more compute per token.

# Fixed Inference-Flop Scaling Study: Crossover Points Where BLT Surpasses BPE Models

To rigorously test the hypothesis that larger models taking fewer steps on larger patches perform better than smaller models taking more steps, the authors conduct a fixed inference-flop scaling study. This study compares models that are designed to have equivalent inference flop budgets but differ in their architecture and patch size. Starting from model sizes of 400M and 3.6B parameters with the Llama 2 tokenizer, flop-equivalent models are found using the Llama 3 tokenizer and BLT-Entropy models with average patch sizes of 6 and 8 bytes on the training data mix. For the patch size 8 models, 3 encoder layers are used instead of 1 to accommodate the larger patches. Each model is then trained for various training flop budgets.

The results demonstrate that BLT models achieve better scaling trends than tokenization-based architectures for both inference flop classes. A consistent pattern emerges across the experiments: BPE models perform better with small training budgets, but BLT models surpass them not far beyond the compute-optimal regime. The crossover point, defined as the training flop budget at which BLT begins to outperform the corresponding BPE model, is a key metric for understanding when the BLT architecture becomes advantageous.

For the smaller inference flop class (starting from 400M parameters), the crossover point occurs at approximately 3x the compute-optimal budget. For the larger inference flop class (starting from 3.6B parameters), the crossover point shifts closer to the compute-optimal point, at approximately 2.5x the compute-optimal budget. This shift indicates that the advantage of BLT becomes apparent sooner as the model scale increases, which is consistent with the observation that larger patch sizes perform better at larger model scales. In practice, it is common to train models well beyond the compute-optimal point; for example, Llama 3.1 has been trained on two orders of magnitude more data than what is compute-optimal for that model size. This means that the crossover points observed in the study fall well within the training regimes that are commonly used in practice.

The practical implication is that while BLT may not be the optimal choice for models trained with very limited compute (where the model has not yet been trained enough to benefit from the patch-based architecture), it becomes increasingly advantageous as training compute increases. For the large-scale models that are typically deployed in production, where training compute far exceeds the compute-optimal point, BLT offers a compelling alternative to tokenization-based models, providing better performance within a fixed inference budget.

# Observations on How Larger Patch Sizes Benefit from Larger Model Scales

The scaling study reveals a consistent and important pattern: larger patch sizes, which may seem disadvantageous at small model scales, become increasingly competitive and even superior as model scale grows. This observation has both practical and theoretical implications for the future of byte-level language modeling.

At the 1B parameter scale, BLT with a patch size of 8 bytes starts at a significantly worse performance point compared to BPE Llama 2. However, by the 7B scale, this same configuration surpasses BPE performance. This pattern is consistent across the fixed inference-flop scaling study as well, where the patch size 8 model shows a steeper scaling trend in the larger flop class, overtaking other models sooner. The authors attribute this phenomenon, in part, to the decreasing share of total flops used by the byte-level local encoder and decoder modules as the model scales.

When growing total parameters 20x from 400M to 8B, the authors only roughly double BLT's local model parameters. This is because the local models, which operate on individual bytes, do not need to scale as dramatically as the global Latent Transformer to maintain performance. Since larger patch sizes only reduce the flops of the global Latent Transformer and not the byte-level local modules, the relative benefit of larger patches increases as the global transformer's share of total flops grows. Concretely, the BLT-Entropy model with patch size 8 went from 1.6x to 1.7x of the Llama 2 model size when moving to the larger model scale, reflecting the fact that the local model parameters become a smaller fraction of the total as the model grows.

This observation suggests a promising direction for future scaling. As models continue to grow beyond 8B parameters, the local models will constitute an even smaller fraction of total parameters, making larger patch sizes even more beneficial. It is plausible that even larger patch sizes, beyond 8 bytes, could become feasible and advantageous at these scales. Furthermore, this finding implies that the optimal patch size is not a fixed quantity but rather a function of model scale, with larger models favoring larger patches. This is a dimension of scaling that is entirely absent from tokenization-based models, where the token size is fixed by the tokenizer and cannot be adjusted based on model scale without retraining.

In summary, the scaling trends presented in this chapter establish BLT as a viable and increasingly advantageous alternative to tokenization-based models as scale increases. The ability to dynamically allocate compute, to adjust patch size independently of model architecture, and to simultaneously grow model and patch size within a fixed inference budget represents a paradigm shift in how language models can be scaled. The consistent pattern of larger patch sizes becoming more competitive at larger scales points toward a future where byte-level models with large patches may become the dominant approach for training and deploying language models at scale.

# Robustness and Character-Level Understanding

One of the most compelling motivations for working directly with raw bytes rather than pre-defined tokens is the potential for improved robustness and a deeper understanding of the sub-word structure of language. Tokenization-based models, by grouping bytes into fixed tokens before the model ever sees them, introduce a layer of abstraction that can obscure character-level information and make models brittle when faced with noisy or unusual input. In this chapter, we explore how the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) leverages its direct access to byte-level data to achieve significant improvements in robustness to input noise, character-level understanding, phonological awareness, and low-resource machine translation. We also examine a practical method for converting pre-trained token-based models into BLT models, effectively "byte-ifying" them to combine the benefits of large-scale pre-training with the advantages of byte-level modeling.

## Noisy Data Evaluation: Testing Robustness to Input Perturbations

A fundamental limitation of tokenization-based models is their sensitivity to input noise. Because tokens are determined by a fixed vocabulary established prior to training, perturbations at the character level can cause tokens to fragment in unpredictable ways, leading to degraded performance. BLT, by contrast, processes raw bytes directly and can in principle handle character-level variations more gracefully. To measure this advantage empirically, the paper introduces a systematic evaluation framework that applies five distinct character-level noising strategies to standard benchmark tasks, creating noised versions that test the model's resilience.

The five noising strategies are designed to probe different aspects of character-level robustness. The first strategy, called **AntSpeak**, converts the entire text into uppercase characters that are space-separated, producing a highly unusual representation that disrupts the normal tokenization of words. For example, a sentence like "The cat sat" would become "T H E C A T S A T." This strategy is particularly challenging for token-based models because the space-separated characters do not correspond to any tokens in the vocabulary, forcing the tokenizer to fall back on single-character tokens that may not have been common in the training data. The second strategy, **Drop**, randomly removes 10\% of the characters from the text. This simulates typos or transmission errors and can split tokens in ways the model has not seen during training. The third strategy, **RandomCase**, converts 50\% of the characters to uppercase and 50\% to lowercase randomly throughout the text. Since tokenizers are typically case-sensitive, this can dramatically alter the token sequence for a given input. The fourth strategy, **Repeat**, repeats 20\% of the characters up to a maximum of four times, introducing redundant characters that again disrupt normal tokenization. The fifth strategy, **UpperCase**, simply transforms all characters in the text to uppercase, which can merge or split tokens compared to their lowercase counterparts.

During evaluation, each noising strategy is applied to either the prompt, the completion, or both as separate tasks, and the average scores across these conditions are reported. The paper presents results on noised versions of the HellaSwag benchmark, a common-sense reasoning task that requires selecting the most appropriate sentence completion from a set of choices. The findings are striking: BLT outperforms tokenizer-based models across the board in terms of robustness, with an average advantage of 8 points over a model trained on the same data. Even more impressively, BLT improves over the Llama 3.1 model, despite that model having been trained on a much larger dataset. This result demonstrates that the robustness advantage of BLT is not merely a consequence of training data size but stems from the architectural decision to model bytes directly. When a character is dropped, repeated, or case-altered, BLT can still process the surrounding bytes in context, whereas a token-based model may encounter an entirely unfamiliar token sequence.

## Grapheme-to-Phoneme Conversion: Phonological Awareness

Beyond robustness to noise, direct byte-level modeling offers the potential for deeper phonological awareness—the ability to understand the relationship between how words are written (graphemes) and how they are pronounced (phonemes). This capability is important for tasks such as text-to-speech, speech recognition, and language learning applications. Tokenization-based models, which treat tokens as atomic units, may struggle to learn the mapping between spelling and pronunciation because the internal structure of tokens is hidden from the model.

To assess BLT's phonological capabilities, the paper evaluates performance on the Grapheme-to-Phoneme (G2P) task using the Phonology Bench benchmark. In this task, the model is given a sequence of graphemes—characters representing a word—and must produce a transcription of that word's pronunciation as a sequence of phonemes. The evaluation is conducted in a 5-shot setting, meaning the model is provided with five example grapheme-to-phoneme mappings before being asked to perform the task on new words. This few-shot setup tests whether the model has internalized the systematic relationships between spelling and pronunciation, rather than merely memorizing specific word-pronunciation pairs.

The results show that BLT outperforms the baseline Llama 3 1T tokenizer-based model on this task. This advantage arises because BLT processes each character (byte) individually and can therefore learn the regularities and irregularities of grapheme-to-phoneme correspondences directly from the data. For example, the model can learn that the sequence "ough" is pronounced differently in "through," "though," "tough," and "cough" by attending to the surrounding character context, whereas a token-based model may treat "ough" as part of larger tokens that do not share a consistent phonological pattern. This phonological awareness is a natural consequence of working at the byte level, where the model must build up its understanding of words from their constituent characters rather than treating pre-grouped tokens as indivisible units.

## The CUTE Benchmark: Composition, Orthographic Similarity, and Character Manipulation

The CUTE (Character Understanding and Text Editing) benchmark provides a comprehensive evaluation of a model's character-level understanding capabilities. The benchmark comprises several tasks that are broadly classified into three categories: understanding composition, understanding orthographic similarity, and the ability to manipulate sequences. These tasks pose a significant challenge for most tokenizer-based models because they require the model to reason about the internal structure of words—information that tokenization is specifically designed to abstract away.

Understanding composition tasks test whether the model can recognize how words are built from their constituent characters. For example, the model might be asked to identify which characters make up a given word or to determine whether one word is a substring of another. Understanding orthographic similarity tasks test whether the model can recognize words that share character-level patterns, such as rhyming words or words with similar spellings. Character manipulation tasks go further, requiring the model to actively edit text at the character level—deleting, inserting, or rearranging characters to produce a desired output.

The results on CUTE reveal a dramatic advantage for BLT. The BLT-Entropy model outperforms both BPE Llama 3 models by more than 25 points on this benchmark. This is a substantial margin, especially considering that BLT was trained on 16 times less data than Llama 3.1. The paper highlights that token-based models appear to possess some knowledge of their tokens' spellings—they can sometimes correctly identify the characters that make up a token—but they struggle to effectively utilize this information to manipulate text. In other words, the token-based models have latent knowledge of character-level structure but cannot reliably access and apply it, whereas BLT's direct access to bytes makes character manipulation a natural operation.

## Near-Perfect Performance on Spelling Tasks

Within the CUTE benchmark, the character manipulation category includes spelling tasks that require the model to spell out words character by character. The paper reports that BLT achieves 99.9\% accuracy on both spelling tasks in this category. This near-perfect performance stands in stark contrast to the struggles of token-based models, which often cannot reliably decompose their tokens into individual characters.

The reason for this disparity is fundamental to the difference between the two architectures. A token-based model receives tokens as input and processes them as atomic units; the model never directly observes the bytes that compose each token. While the model's embedding layer may incidentally encode some information about the character composition of tokens (since similar tokens may have similar embeddings), this information is not guaranteed to be complete or accessible. In contrast, BLT processes each byte individually and builds up word representations from these bytes through its local encoder and global transformer. The model has explicit, direct access to every character in every word, making spelling a straightforward task—it simply needs to report the bytes it has already processed.

The paper notes that word deletion and insertion are the only two tasks in the CUTE benchmark where the BPE model performs better than BLT. These word manipulation tasks involve operating on whole words as units, which aligns more naturally with the token-based model's representation of words as single tokens. However, the gap is not too wide, and the paper suggests that building from characters to words may be easier than the reverse—deriving character-level understanding from token-level representations. This observation has important implications for model design: starting from bytes and building up to higher-level representations may be a more natural and effective approach than starting with tokens and trying to recover character-level information.

## Low-Resource Machine Translation on FLORES-101

The advantages of byte-level modeling extend beyond English-centric tasks to multilingual settings, where they become particularly pronounced for lower-resource languages. The paper evaluates BLT on machine translation using the FLORES-101 benchmark, which covers translation into and out of six popular language families and twenty-one lower-resource languages with various scripts. Translation quality is measured using SentencePiece BLEU scores.

The results demonstrate that BLT outperforms a model trained with the Llama 3 tokenizer, achieving a 2-point overall advantage in translating into English and a 0.5-point advantage in translating from English. When the results are broken down by language pair, a clear pattern emerges: for popular language pairs (such as Spanish-to-English or French-to-English), BLT performs comparably to or slightly better than Llama 3. However, for numerous language pairs within lower-resource language families, BLT significantly outperforms Llama 3.

This advantage for low-resource languages is a direct consequence of working at the byte level. Tokenizers with fixed vocabularies must allocate vocabulary entries to different languages, and the allocation is typically biased toward high-resource languages that appear more frequently in the training data. Low-resource languages may be represented by longer sequences of sub-word tokens (since fewer whole words are in the vocabulary), or worse, may be decomposed into individual byte tokens that the model treats as meaningless units. BLT, by contrast, processes all languages uniformly as bytes and dynamically groups them into patches based on entropy, regardless of the language. This means that the model can learn the statistical regularities of any language from its byte-level representation, without being handicapped by a vocabulary that was optimized for other languages. The effectiveness of byte modeling for generalizing to long-tail byte sequences—rare languages, unusual scripts, and uncommon character patterns—is a powerful advantage of the BLT architecture.

## Byte-ifying Pretrained Models: Initializing BLT from Llama 3.1

While BLT offers compelling advantages, training a large language model from scratch requires enormous computational resources. Pre-trained token-based models, such as Llama 3.1, represent a massive investment of compute and data. A natural question arises: can we leverage the knowledge encoded in these pre-trained models to accelerate BLT training, rather than starting from random initialization? The paper explores this possibility through a process that can be described as "byte-ifying" a pre-trained token-based model.

The approach is conceptually straightforward: the global transformer parameters of BLT are initialized with the corresponding weights from a pre-trained Llama 3.1 model. Since the global transformer in BLT operates on patch representations that play a role analogous to token representations in a standard transformer, many of the weights can be transferred directly. The local encoder and local decoder, which are unique to BLT and have no counterpart in a token-based model, are initialized from scratch. After initialization, the entire model is fine-tuned, but with an important asymmetry in learning rates: the global transformer weights (inherited from Llama 3.1) are updated using one-tenth the learning rate employed for the local encoder and local decoder. This conservative learning rate for the global transformer prevents catastrophic forgetting of the knowledge encoded in the pre-trained weights, while the higher learning rate for the local models allows them to rapidly learn how to encode bytes into patch representations and decode patch representations back into bytes.

The model is trained for the Llama 3 optimal number of steps, which represents the compute-optimal training duration determined for the Llama 3 architecture. This approach can be viewed as transforming a tokenizer-based model into a tokenizer-free one—effectively converting a pre-trained Llama 3.1 model into a BLT model. The pre-trained model's knowledge of language, reasoning, and world knowledge is preserved in the global transformer, while the local models learn the interface between bytes and patches.

## Distillation Results: Reduced Training FLOPs with Competitive Performance

The results of this initialization strategy are promising. The BLT model initialized from Llama 3.1 significantly outperforms both the Llama 3 baseline and a BLT baseline trained from scratch with the same number of training flops. This demonstrates that the knowledge transferred from the pre-trained model provides a substantial head start that more than compensates for the additional parameters and complexity introduced by the local encoder and decoder.

When compared to the BLT-Entropy model trained on a significantly larger dataset (1T tokens), the BLT model initialized from Llama 3.1 still achieves superior performance on the MMLU benchmark, despite having been trained with far fewer total flops. This suggests that byte-ifying pre-trained models can be an effective approach to significantly reducing training costs while achieving competitive or superior performance on downstream tasks.

To provide a comprehensive comparison, the paper also includes the original Llama 3.1 model trained on 15T tokens—substantially more data than any of the BLT variants. When evaluated against this model, the BLT derived from Llama 3.1 experiences a slight performance decline on MMLU and HumanEval, but a more significant drop on other tasks. This indicates that while the byte-ification approach is effective at preserving much of the pre-trained model's capability, there is still a gap that needs to be closed. The more significant drops on certain tasks suggest that the local encoder and decoder may not have been trained long enough to fully learn the byte-to-patch and patch-to-byte mappings, or that the data mixture and hyperparameters need further optimization for the BLT architecture.

## Analysis of Remaining Gaps and Implications for Future Work

The remaining gaps between byte-ified BLT models and their token-based counterparts highlight several important directions for future work. First, the data mixture used for fine-tuning the byte-ified model may need to be optimized specifically for BLT. The original Llama 3.1 was trained on a carefully curated mixture of 15T tokens, and the same mixture may not be optimal when expressed as bytes with dynamic patching. Different domains may require different patch sizes or entropy thresholds, and the optimal mixture for byte-level training may differ from that for token-level training.

Second, the learning rate schedule for the global transformer versus the local models may need further refinement. The 10:1 ratio used in the experiments is a reasonable heuristic, but the optimal ratio may depend on the model size, the amount of pre-training in the global transformer, and the complexity of the byte-to-patch mapping. A more sophisticated schedule that adapts the learning rates based on the relative convergence of the global and local models could improve results.

Third, the entropy model used for patching in the byte-ified model may benefit from being co-trained or fine-tuned with the main model. In the current approach, the entropy model is trained separately on the same data distribution, but its patching decisions may not perfectly align with the needs of the global transformer that was originally trained on tokens. A joint optimization of the entropy model and the main model could lead to better patch boundaries that are more suited to the inherited global transformer weights.

Finally, the paper notes that further work is needed to not only retain the benefits of byte-ification but to push performance beyond that of the original tokenizer-based models. The vision is that by starting from a strong pre-trained token-based model and converting it to a byte-level architecture, we can achieve the best of both worlds: the knowledge and capabilities learned through large-scale token-based pre-training, combined with the robustness, character-level understanding, and dynamic compute allocation of BLT. The initial results are encouraging and suggest that this approach could significantly reduce the cost of adopting byte-level architectures, making them accessible without requiring a full from-scratch training run.

# Ablations and Architectural Justifications

In this chapter, we systematically ablate the key architectural and design choices that define the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT). Ablation studies are an essential component of architectural research because they isolate the contribution of each design decision, allowing us to understand which elements are necessary, which are redundant, and how different components interact. The BLT architecture introduces several novelties—entropy-based patching, cross-attention modules for byte-to-patch and patch-to-byte conversion, hash $n$-gram embeddings, and an asymmetric distribution of layers between the local encoder and decoder—each of which could plausibly be replaced or removed. By carefully controlling for confounding factors such as context length, training compute, and model size, the ablations presented here provide a rigorous justification for the final BLT configuration used at the 8B parameter scale on the BLT-1T dataset.

Throughout this chapter, we return to a central principle of the ablation methodology: all comparisons are conducted in a flop-controlled manner. This means that when two architectural variants are compared, care is taken to ensure that both expend approximately the same number of floating point operations during training, so that differences in performance can be attributed to the architectural choice rather than to differences in compute budget. Where relevant, the experiments also control for context length, ensuring that models with different patch sizes see the same number of bytes in expectation, preventing any model from gaining an unfair advantage by simply attending over a longer span of text.

#

## Entropy Model Hyperparameters

The entropy model plays a pivotal role in BLT because it determines where patch boundaries are placed. Recall that entropy patching works by computing the entropy of the next-byte prediction distribution under a small byte-level language model, then inserting a patch boundary at any byte position where the entropy exceeds a threshold. The quality of this entropy model—its size, its receptive field, and consequently its ability to estimate meaningful next-byte uncertainties—directly influences the quality of the patching decisions, and thereby the efficiency and performance of the entire BLT pipeline.

To study the effect of entropy model size and context window length on scaling performance, the authors train byte-level entropy transformer models across a range of sizes from 1M to 100M parameters, and with context window lengths varying from 64 to 512 bytes. These entropy models are trained on the same data distribution as the full BLT model. The scaling law curves are then constructed using 400M and 1B BLT models trained on the Llama 2 dataset, and performance is measured in bits-per-byte (BPB) against training flops. This setup allows the researchers to isolate how the quality of the entropy model propagates through to the final language modeling performance.

The findings reveal that scaling performance is positively correlated with both dimensions of the entropy model—larger models and longer context windows both lead to better downstream BLT performance. This result is intuitive: a larger entropy model can better capture the statistical regularities of byte sequences, producing more accurate entropy estimates, while a longer context window allows the model to leverage more distant information when assessing the predictability of the next byte. For instance, a model with a 64-byte context may fail to recognize that a word beginning several bytes ago is nearly complete and thus highly predictable, whereas a model with a 512-byte context can recognize this pattern and correctly assign a low entropy to the corresponding byte positions.

However, the improvements show diminishing returns beyond 50M parameters. This suggests that while a high-quality entropy model is important, it need not be enormous relative to the main BLT model. At the 8B scale, the authors use a 100M-parameter entropy model with 14 layers, a hidden dimensionality of 512, and a sliding window attention of 512 bytes. This represents a reasonable balance: the entropy model is large enough to produce high-quality entropy estimates, but small enough that the computational overhead of running it during data loading remains modest compared to the cost of the global latent transformer.

An additional practical consideration is that when the receptive field of the entropy model is small enough, the trained model can be encoded as an efficient lookup table. This is relevant for deployment scenarios where running a neural network for every byte during inference would be prohibitively slow; a lookup table can provide the same entropy estimates with minimal computational overhead. The trade-off, of course, is that a smaller receptive field yields lower-quality entropy estimates, and the ablation results confirm that this comes at a cost to scaling performance.

#

## Patching Scheme Ablation Under Controlled Context Length

The choice of patching function is perhaps the most consequential architectural decision in BLT, because it determines how raw bytes are grouped into the computational units processed by the global latent transformer. The paper introduces and compares four distinct patching schemes: strided patching with a fixed stride of 4 or 6 bytes, space patching (which creates new patches after space-like bytes), BPE tokenizer-based patching (using the Llama 3 tokenizer), and entropy-based patching using a small byte language model. Each scheme embodies a different philosophy about how to segment byte sequences, and the ablation is designed to determine which philosophy yields the best scaling behavior.

A critical methodological detail in this ablation is the control of context length. Dynamic patching methods, by their nature, reduce the effective sequence length in terms of patches, which could allow a model to attend over a larger span of bytes for the same number of patch steps. To prevent this from confounding the comparison, all models are trained and evaluated with the same expected number of bytes in each sequence. This means that models with larger patch sizes have their sequence lengths proportionally reduced, so that every patching scheme sees the same amount of textual context in bytes. This control ensures that differences in performance are due to the quality of the patching scheme—not to differences in how much text the model can see at once.

The results, presented in the scaling law curves, show that all dynamic patching schemes outperform static (strided) patching. This finding is consistent with the core motivation of BLT: compute should be allocated dynamically based on the complexity of the prediction, not uniformly across all byte positions. Strided patching, by grouping bytes into fixed-size chunks regardless of content, wastes transformer steps on highly predictable regions (such as repeated whitespace in code or common suffixes in English words) while under-allocating compute for informationally dense regions (such as mathematical notation or the first character of a new word).

Among the dynamic schemes, space patching emerges as a surprisingly strong competitor to entropy-based patching. Space patching exploits the natural linguistic boundary provided by whitespace characters, allocating a new transformer step for each word. This ensures that words are patched consistently across different contexts and that transformer steps are reserved for the hard predictions that often follow spaces—such as the first byte of an answer to a question. However, space patching has inherent limitations: it cannot gracefully handle all languages and domains (many languages do not use whitespace as word boundaries), and it cannot vary the patch size, meaning that the compute allocated per word is fixed regardless of whether the word is easy or hard to predict. Entropy-based patching, by contrast, adapts to the actual predictability of the data and can produce patches of varying sizes, making it more flexible and ultimately more effective at scale.

BPE tokenizer-based patching, which uses the Llama 3 tokenizer to define patch boundaries, serves as an interesting bridge between the tokenization and patching worlds. However, as discussed earlier in the paper, BPE is not an incremental patching scheme—the same prefix can be tokenized differently depending on the continuation, which creates complications for autoregressive generation. The ablation results show that entropy-based patching achieves the best scaling trends, validating the data-driven approach to identifying patch boundaries.

#

## Downstream Task Comparison of Patching Schemes

While scaling law curves measured in bits-per-byte provide a controlled metric for comparing patching schemes, the ultimate test of any language model architecture is its performance on downstream tasks that reflect real-world capabilities. To this end, the authors present benchmark evaluations for BLT models comparing tokenizer-based models, space patching, and entropy-based patching, all trained on the Llama 2 dataset for the optimal number of steps as determined by Llama 3 scaling laws.

The downstream task comparison reveals that the advantages of entropy-based patching observed in the scaling trends do indeed carry forward to benchmark evaluations. Although space patching is a simpler strategy that does not require running an entropy model during training, the entropy-based approach consistently yields better performance on standard reasoning, world knowledge, and code generation benchmarks. This confirms that the gains from dynamic, entropy-driven compute allocation are not merely artifacts of the bits-per-byte metric but translate into meaningful improvements on tasks that require reasoning, factual knowledge, and code synthesis.

It is worth noting that the space patching results in the downstream task comparison are from earlier experimental runs that did not include cross-attention modules, but the authors observe that similar trends hold even when cross-attention is added. This suggests that the superiority of entropy-based patching is robust to other architectural choices, further strengthening the case for its adoption as the default patching scheme in BLT.

The comparison also highlights the practical trade-offs between the two dynamic patching schemes. Space patching, with its larger average patch size of approximately 6 bytes, achieves significant reductions in inference flops compared to entropy-based patching, which has an average patch size of approximately 4.5 bytes on the training data mix. However, this efficiency comes at the cost of task performance, particularly on benchmarks that require fine-grained character-level understanding or robustness to input noise. The choice between the two schemes thus depends on the intended use case: applications that prioritize inference efficiency may favor space patching, while those that prioritize task performance should use entropy-based patching.

#

## Cross-Attention Ablation: Placement and Query Initialization

The cross-attention modules in BLT serve as the bridge between the byte-level and patch-level representations. In the local encoder, cross-attention pools byte representations into patch representations; in the local decoder, it does the reverse, expanding patch representations back into byte representations. The design of these modules—including where they are placed, how their queries are initialized, and how many are used—is the subject of a detailed ablation using 1B parameter models.

The ablation explores several configurations. For the encoder cross-attention, three query initialization strategies are tested: (1) using the same learned embedding for every global state, (2) using a hash embedding of the bytes in the patch, and (3) using a pooling of the encoder hidden representation of the patch bytes at the given encoder layer. These strategies differ in how much byte-level information is incorporated into the initial patch query, which affects the quality of the attention computation and the resulting patch representation.

The results reveal a clear and important finding: using cross-attention in the decoder is most effective. The decoder cross-attention, where byte representations serve as queries and patch representations serve as keys and values, is critical for producing accurate byte-level predictions from the global model's patch representations. This makes intuitive sense: the decoder's job is to generate individual bytes, and the cross-attention mechanism provides each byte position with access to the relevant patch-level information, enabling the model to condition its byte-level predictions on the higher-level representations produced by the global transformer.

In the encoder, the picture is more nuanced. The ablation finds only a slight improvement from using cross-attention, and this improvement is contingent on using pooling initialization of queries. The other initialization strategies—the same learned embedding for every global state and hash embeddings of the patch bytes—do not yield significant improvements. This suggests that the encoder cross-attention is most effective when the initial query for each patch is derived from the actual byte representations within that patch, rather than from a generic or hash-based initialization. The pooling approach ensures that the cross-attention starts from a meaningful summary of the patch's content, allowing the attention mechanism to refine this summary rather than learning it from scratch.

The asymmetry between the encoder and decoder results—that cross-attention is more important in the decoder than in the encoder—can be understood by considering the information flow in the architecture. The encoder must compress a variable number of byte representations into a single patch representation, a task that can be reasonably accomplished with pooling alone, especially when augmented with hash $n$-gram embeddings that capture local byte patterns. The decoder, however, must expand a single patch representation into multiple byte predictions, a task that requires each byte position to selectively access different aspects of the patch representation. Cross-attention is naturally suited for this selective retrieval, making it indispensable in the decoder.

#

## Cross-Attention Benefits on Common-Crawl and with Larger Patch Sizes

Beyond the general question of whether cross-attention helps, the ablation also examines where and under what conditions it provides the greatest benefit. Two specific findings emerge: cross-attention is particularly helpful on Common-Crawl data, and it becomes more important when larger patch sizes are used.

The benefit on Common-Crawl is likely due to the nature of web data, which is heterogeneous, noisy, and contains a wide variety of text formats, languages, and topics. In such diverse data, the mapping from bytes to meaningful patch representations is more complex than in, say, well-structured code or Wikipedia articles. Cross-attention, by allowing each patch representation to be computed through a learned attention mechanism over its constituent bytes, provides the flexibility needed to handle this diversity. A simple pooling operation, by contrast, applies the same aggregation strategy regardless of content, which may be insufficient for the varied patterns found in web crawl data.

The interaction with patch size is equally significant. When patch sizes are larger, each patch encompasses more bytes, and the task of compressing those bytes into a single representation becomes harder. With a patch size of 4, pooling a handful of byte embeddings may suffice; with a patch size of 8, the pooling must summarize twice as many bytes, increasing the risk of information loss. Cross-attention mitigates this by allowing the model to learn which bytes are most relevant for each aspect of the patch representation, rather than uniformly averaging all bytes together. This finding has direct implications for scaling: as BLT models are scaled up with larger patch sizes to improve inference efficiency, cross-attention becomes increasingly important for maintaining performance.

This result also helps explain the architectural choices made for the larger BLT models. For the patch size 8 models in the fixed inference scaling study, the authors use 3 encoder layers instead of 1, reflecting the need for more processing capacity in the encoder when patches are larger. The combination of cross-attention and additional encoder layers ensures that the byte-to-patch compression remains effective even as the patch size grows, allowing BLT to reap the computational savings of larger patches without sacrificing the quality of the patch representations.

#

## n-Gram Hash Embedding Vocabulary Size Ablation

Hash $n$-gram embeddings are a key component of the BLT encoder, providing each byte position with information about the preceding bytes through byte $n$-grams of sizes 3 through 8. These embeddings are stored in hash tables of fixed size, and the choice of table size involves a trade-off: larger tables can store more distinct $n$-gram representations, reducing hash collisions and improving the quality of the embeddings, but they also increase the parameter count and memory footprint of the model.

The ablation examines hash table sizes of 0 (no hash embeddings), 100K, 200K, and 400K, and at the 8B scale, the authors also report results for 500K and 300K hashes. The results, measured in bits-per-byte after 15K training steps at the 1B scale, reveal several important patterns.

First, hash embeddings help across all data domains, confirming that incorporating local byte context through $n$-grams is broadly beneficial. The magnitude of the benefit, however, varies by domain. The largest improvements are observed on Wikipedia and GitHub, where hash embeddings yield a 0.04 bits-per-byte difference compared to no hash embeddings. On other domains, the improvement is smaller, around 0.01 bits-per-byte. This domain variation likely reflects the fact that Wikipedia articles and code repositories contain many repeated $n$-gram patterns—such as common English word fragments, programming keywords, and syntactic markers—that are well captured by hash embeddings. In domains where the text is more variable or where longer-range dependencies dominate, the local $n$-gram information is less predictive, and the benefit of hash embeddings is correspondingly smaller.

Second, the returns from increasing the hash table size diminish rapidly. At the 8B scale, going from 500K to 300K hashes changes performance by only approximately 0.001 bits-per-byte after 15K steps, a negligible difference. This indicates that the majority of the benefit from hash embeddings is captured with a relatively modest table size, and that the primary value of the embeddings comes from their existence rather than from having an extremely large vocabulary. The diminishing returns can be understood through the lens of hash collisions: with 300K hash entries and $n$-grams of sizes 3 through 8, the collision rate is already low enough that most distinct $n$-grams map to distinct entries, so further increasing the table size yields little additional benefit.

Based on these findings, the authors use 500K hashes with a single hash function and $n$-gram sizes ranging from 3 to 8 for all BLT models. This choice represents a point well into the diminishing returns regime, ensuring that the hash embeddings are not a bottleneck on performance, while keeping the parameter overhead of the embedding tables manageable relative to the total model size.

#

## Complementarity of Hash Embeddings and Cross-Attention

A natural question that arises when multiple architectural components are introduced is whether they provide redundant or complementary benefits. If hash $n$-gram embeddings and cross-attention both serve to incorporate byte-level information into patch representations, perhaps they are solving the same problem and only one is needed. The ablation results address this question directly and reveal that the two components are largely complementary, providing improvements on different datasets.

The complementarity can be understood by considering the different types of information each component captures. Hash $n$-gram embeddings provide a fixed, content-based representation of local byte patterns—the embedding for a given $n$-gram is the same regardless of context, determined entirely by the hash function and the embedding table. This makes them well suited for capturing recurring local patterns, such as common word fragments or syntactic markers, that appear frequently in the training data. Cross-attention, by contrast, produces a context-dependent representation—the attention weights and thus the contribution of each byte to the patch representation vary depending on the surrounding bytes and the evolving query vector. This makes cross-attention better suited for handling cases where the relevance of different bytes within a patch depends on the broader context.

The empirical results bear out this analysis: hash embeddings provide the greatest benefit on Wikipedia and GitHub, where local patterns are highly repetitive and predictable, while cross-attention provides the greatest benefit on Common-Crawl, where the diversity of content requires context-dependent aggregation. The fact that these two components excel on different data domains means that their benefits are additive rather than redundant, and both are needed to bring BLT's performance to parity with tokenizer-based models across the full range of training data.

This finding has an important implication for architectural design: when multiple mechanisms are introduced to address the same challenge, it is not sufficient to evaluate them in isolation. The interactions between components can be complex, and only a systematic ablation that tests each component both individually and in combination can reveal whether they are redundant or complementary. The BLT ablations demonstrate that the architecture's components are carefully chosen to address different aspects of the byte-to-patch mapping problem, with minimal redundancy.

#

## Local Model Layer Allocation: Lightweight Encoder with Heavier Decoder

The final ablation concerns the distribution of layers between the local encoder and local decoder. Both of these modules are lightweight relative to the global latent transformer—that is, $l_{\mathcal{E}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$ and $l_{\mathcal{D}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$—but the question of how to allocate layers between them remains. The ablation, presented in a table of various settings for the number of encoder and decoder layers, reveals a clear and somewhat surprising finding: when paired with hash $n$-gram embeddings, BLT works well with an encoder that is extremely lightweight—just one layer—while the decoder benefits from being heavier.

This asymmetry is consistent with the cross-attention findings discussed earlier. The encoder's primary task is to compress bytes into patch representations, and with hash $n$-gram embeddings providing rich local byte context, much of the information needed for this compression is already available in the input embeddings. A single encoder layer, combined with cross-attention that pools byte representations into patch representations, suffices to produce expressive patch representations. Adding more encoder layers yields diminishing returns because the essential information—the byte identities and their local $n$-gram context—is already captured in the input.

The decoder, however, faces a more demanding task: it must generate individual bytes from patch representations, which requires not only expanding the patch information but also modeling the sequential dependencies among bytes within a patch. The decoder must predict each byte conditional on the previously decoded bytes within the same patch, a task that benefits from more layers of processing. A heavier decoder can better model the complex distributions over byte sequences within patches, leading to more accurate byte-level predictions.

This finding has practical implications for model design. When scaling BLT to larger sizes, the local models do not need to grow proportionally to the global transformer. The authors note that when growing total parameters 20-fold from 400M to 8B, they only roughly double the local model parameters. This is because the local models operate on bytes, which remain a fixed 256-size vocabulary regardless of the global model's size, and the essential information they need to capture—local byte patterns and byte-to-patch mappings—does not become more complex as the global model grows. The global latent transformer, which operates on patch representations and must model long-range dependencies, is where most of the additional parameters should be allocated as the model scales.

The combination of a lightweight encoder and a heavier decoder also has implications for the computational cost of BLT. Because the encoder is run on every byte during both training and inference, keeping it small directly reduces the per-byte computational overhead. The decoder, while heavier, is also run on every byte, but its additional layers enable more accurate byte generation, which is critical for the model's overall language modeling performance. The asymmetric allocation thus strikes a balance between efficiency and quality, minimizing cost where the task is simpler (encoding) and investing compute where the task is harder (decoding).

In summary, the ablations presented in this chapter provide a comprehensive justification for the BLT architecture. Each component—entropy-based patching, cross-attention in the decoder, hash $n$-gram embeddings, and the asymmetric encoder-decoder design—contributes meaningfully to the model's performance, and the components are largely complementary rather than redundant. The findings also reveal important scaling considerations: cross-attention becomes more important with larger patch sizes, hash embeddings show diminishing returns beyond a modest table size, and the local models need not grow proportionally with the global transformer. These insights collectively inform the architectural choices that enable BLT to match the performance of tokenizer-based models like Llama 3 at scale, while offering significant advantages in inference efficiency and robustness.

# Related Work and Historical Context

The development of the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) did not emerge in a vacuum. Rather, it represents the culmination of years of research aimed at building language models that operate directly on raw bytes or characters, thereby sidestepping the limitations of fixed-vocabulary tokenization. To understand the significance of BLT's contributions, it is essential to trace the historical trajectory of byte- and character-level language modeling, from the earliest recurrent neural network approaches to the modern patching-based transformer architectures that paved the way for BLT. This chapter surveys that landscape, highlighting the motivations, successes, and limitations of each generation of approaches, and ultimately situating BLT as a synthesis and extension of these prior efforts.

#

## Character-Level RNNs: Early Motivations and Advantages on Morphologically Rich Languages

Character-level language modeling has been a vibrant area of research since the early days of neural language models. The fundamental motivation was elegantly simple: by modeling text at the character (or byte) level, a model could organically handle out-of-vocabulary words without resorting to back-off methods or special tokens. This was particularly valuable for languages with rich morphology, where the number of distinct word forms can be extremely large due to productive affixation, compounding, and other morphological processes. In such languages, a word-level model would necessarily encounter many rare or unseen word forms, whereas a character-level model could decompose any word into its constituent characters and leverage shared sub-word patterns.

Early work by Sutskever, Mikolov, and others demonstrated the viability of character-level recurrent neural networks (RNNs) for language modeling. These models processed text one character at a time, maintaining a hidden state that accumulated information across the sequence. Graves extended this line of work with generative models that could produce coherent text character by character, showcasing the creative potential of character-level modeling. A significant advance came from Kim et al., who trained a model that processed characters on the input side using convolutional and highway networks, which then fed into LSTM-based RNNs. This architecture was able to match the performance of the then-state-of-the-art RNN-based language models on English and, crucially, outperform them on morphologically rich languages, validating the long-held hypothesis that character-level representations provide a natural advantage when word forms are highly variable.

The advantages of character-level modeling extended beyond language modeling proper. Kenter and Zweig applied byte-level LSTM models to machine comprehension tasks and found that they outperformed word-level models on morphologically rich Turkish and Russian, further underscoring the utility of fine-grained input representations for understanding languages with complex morphology. Along similar lines, Zhang et al. used character-based convolutional models for classification tasks and found that they outperformed word-level models on certain tasks, suggesting that the benefits of character-level modeling were not confined to language modeling alone. Chung et al. further advanced the field with hierarchical LSTM models that used boundary detectors at each level to discover latent hierarchical structure in text, improving performance on character-level language modeling by explicitly modeling the multi-level nature of language. ByteNet, developed by Kalchbrenner et al., took a different architectural approach, using CNN-based layers on characters rather than attention for machine translation, demonstrating that convolutional architectures could also be effectively applied at the character level.

Despite these successes, character-level RNNs and convolutional models faced fundamental limitations in scalability. The sequential nature of RNNs made parallelization difficult, and the sheer length of character sequences meant that training on large datasets was slow. These limitations would only be partially addressed by the advent of the transformer architecture, which, as we shall see, brought its own set of trade-offs to the character-level setting.

#

## Character-Level Transformers: Challenges of Scaling and the Gap from Word-Level Models

The introduction of the transformer architecture by Vaswani et al., with its self-attention mechanism enabling parallel computation over sequences, marked a paradigm shift in natural language processing. When combined with subword tokenization techniques such as Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE), as introduced by Sennrich et al., transformers achieved dramatic improvements on language modeling and benchmark tasks. However, the success of word- and subword-level transformers also implicitly defined an inductive bias about the level of abstraction at which language models should operate, raising the question of whether character-level transformers could match the performance of their subword-level counterparts.

Al-Rfou et al. took up this challenge by training very deep transformers directly on character sequences. To facilitate training of these deep models, they employed auxiliary losses, and their models were able to outperform previous LSTM-based character language models. However, a significant gap remained between character-level and word-level language models in terms of performance, indicating that the transformer architecture alone was not sufficient to close the gap at the character level. This gap was further confirmed by GPT-2, which observed that on large-scale datasets such as the one-billion-word benchmark, byte-level language models were not competitive with word-level models, suggesting that the challenge of scaling character-level models was not merely a matter of architectural capacity but also of computational efficiency.

Choe and Choe demonstrated an important proof of concept: byte-level language models based on transformers could in fact outperform subword-level language models with comparable parameters. However, this came at a significant cost—the models required much more compute and took much longer to train. This tension between performance and efficiency would become a central theme in the development of byte-level models. El-Bayeh et al. trained a BERT model called CharFormer that built word representations by applying convolutions on character embeddings, demonstrating improvements in the medical domain, but again at the cost of substantially more compute. Clark et al. developed CANINE, a 150-million-parameter encoder-only model that operated directly on character sequences, using a deep transformer stack at its core (similar in spirit to BLT's global model) combined with a local transformer and strided convolutions to downsample input characters. CANINE outperformed the equivalent token-level encoder-only model (mBERT) on downstream multilingual tasks, providing further evidence that character-level models could achieve strong performance when equipped with appropriate architectural mechanisms for managing sequence length.

A key insight that emerged from this body of work was that directly modeling bytes or characters, rather than subword units, inevitably increases the sequence length of the input. For a transformer, longer sequences mean more expensive attention computations, scaling quadratically with sequence length. This made it challenging to efficiently scale byte-level transformer models to the same data and parameter regimes as token-based models. As BLT's authors would later observe, prior works attempted to mitigate this cost through more efficient self-attention mechanisms or attention-free architectures, but these approaches primarily helped train small models. At scale, the computational cost of a transformer is dominated by the large feed-forward network layers that run on every byte, not the cost of the attention mechanism itself—a crucial observation that would motivate the patching-based approaches discussed in subsequent sections.

#

## Byte-Level Models Without Patching: ByT5, CANINE, CharFormer, and MambaByte

Several notable approaches attempted to build byte-level models without any form of patching, instead processing every byte directly through the full model. While these models demonstrated impressive capabilities in terms of robustness and character-level understanding, they all suffered from the fundamental inefficiency of processing long byte sequences, which made scaling to large model and data sizes prohibitively expensive.

CANINE, developed by Clark et al., was a 150-million-parameter encoder-only model that operated directly on character sequences. Its architecture featured a deep transformer stack at its core, conceptually similar to BLT's global model, combined with a local transformer and strided convolutions to downsample the input characters. By downsampling characters before applying the full transformer, CANINE partially addressed the sequence length problem, though it did not employ dynamic patching and remained limited to encoder-only tasks. CANINE's success in outperforming mBERT on downstream multilingual tasks demonstrated the potential of character-level processing, but its relatively small scale and encoder-only design left open the question of whether such approaches could scale to decoder-based generative models at the scale of modern large language models.

CharFormer, introduced by El-Bayeh et al., took a different approach to building word representations from characters. Rather than using attention to pool character representations, CharFormer applied convolutions on character embeddings to construct higher-level representations. While this approach demonstrated improvements in the medical domain—where domain-specific terminology and out-of-vocabulary words are common—it also expended substantially more compute than token-based models, limiting its scalability.

ByT5, developed by Xue et al., was perhaps the most comprehensive exploration of byte-level encoder-decoder models without patching. ByT5 operated directly on byte sequences without any form of grouping or downsampling, applying full transformer attention over every byte. The model exhibited improved robustness to noise and was competitive with tokenizer-based models when trained on four times less data. However, the lack of patching meant that the model needed to compute expensive attention operations over every byte, making it extremely compute-heavy. ByT5's findings were important in demonstrating the robustness advantages of byte-level modeling, but its computational inefficiency precluded scaling to the levels of modern large language models. The authors of BLT would later cite the prohibitively costly nature of directly training on bytes at scale as a key motivation for their patching-based approach.

More recently, Wang et al. developed MambaByte, which applied the Mamba architecture—a state-space model capable of maintaining a fixed-size memory state over very large context lengths—to byte-level language modeling. Unlike transformer-based approaches, MambaByte did not use patching and instead processed every byte through the Mamba architecture. At the 350-million-parameter scale, MambaByte was able to outperform byte-level transformer models in a flop-controlled setting in terms of bits-per-byte on several datasets. This result was significant because it demonstrated that attention-free architectures could offer a path to efficient byte-level modeling. However, as BLT's authors noted, the computational savings from attention-free architectures primarily benefit small models. At scale, the dominant cost is the feed-forward network layers applied at every byte, which patching-based approaches address by reducing the number of times these expensive layers are invoked.

Together, these approaches demonstrated clear advantages of byte-level modeling—robustness to noise, character-level understanding, and improved handling of morphologically rich and low-resource languages—but they also highlighted the fundamental scalability challenge that patching-based approaches would seek to address.

#

## Patching-Based Approaches: Hourglass Transformer, Dynamic Patching, and Arithmetic Coding

The core idea behind patching is to group sequences of bytes into larger units called patches, which then serve as the primary units of computation for the main transformer model. By reducing the number of steps at which the expensive global transformer operates, patching can dramatically reduce computational cost while potentially retaining the benefits of byte-level modeling. Several approaches explored this idea with varying degrees of sophistication.

The Hourglass Transformer, developed by Nawrot et al., experimented with static patching-based downsampling and upsampling. The architecture featured a transformer that first processed individual bytes, then downsampled them into patches by grouping fixed numbers of adjacent bytes, applied a deeper transformer stack on the patch representations, and finally upsampled back to the byte level. This hierarchical structure, resembling the shape of an hourglass, allowed the model to capture both fine-grained byte-level information and higher-level patterns at the patch level. The Hourglass Transformer outperformed other byte-level baselines at the 150-million-parameter scale, demonstrating the viability of patching as a mechanism for efficient byte-level modeling.

Nawrot et al. subsequently extended this work with dynamic patching schemes, moving beyond fixed-size patches to context-dependent groupings. They explored several approaches to determining patch boundaries, including a boundary predictor that was learned in an end-to-end fashion, a boundary predictor supervised using certain tokenizers, and an entropy-based patching model similar to the approach BLT would later adopt. The entropy-based approach used the entropy of next-byte predictions from a small language model to identify points of high uncertainty, placing patch boundaries at these points. This approach was shown to outperform vanilla transformers on language modeling tasks at the 40-million-parameter scale on approximately 400 million tokens. This work was particularly significant for BLT, as it introduced the concept of entropy-based patching that BLT would adopt and scale to models of up to 8 billion parameters and 4 trillion training bytes.

Lester et al. took a different approach, investigating training on sequences compressed using arithmetic coding. Arithmetic coding is a form of entropy coding that can achieve compression rates beyond what BPE can offer, as it is not constrained by a fixed vocabulary. They used an equal-info windows technique to segment the compressed stream into windows of approximately equal information content, and were able to outperform byte-level baselines on language modeling tasks. However, their approach underperformed subword baselines, suggesting that compression-based approaches, while innovative, had not yet reached the level of performance needed to compete with established tokenization methods.

A common theme across these patching-based approaches was the recognition that not all bytes require the same amount of computational effort. Some bytes, such as the continuation of a common word, are highly predictable and require little computation, while others, such as the first byte of a new word or a character in a complex mathematical expression, are much less predictable and benefit from additional computational resources. The patching approaches differed in how they identified these boundaries—static methods used fixed positions, dynamic methods used learned predictors, and entropy-based methods used information-theoretic measures—but all shared the goal of reducing the number of expensive global transformer steps while maintaining model quality.

#

## MegaByte: Fixed Static Patching and Concatenation, and Its Limitations at Scale

MegaByte, introduced by Yu et al., was a decoder-only causal language model that represented a significant step forward in patching-based byte-level modeling. The architecture used fixed static patching, grouping bytes into patches of a predetermined size $k$, and employed concatenation of byte representations to convert them into patch representations. A local model on the decoder side was then used to convert patch representations back into individual bytes. The authors demonstrated that MegaByte could match tokenizer-based models at the 1-billion-parameter scale on a dataset of approximately 400 billion bytes, a notable achievement that suggested patching-based approaches could be competitive with tokenization-based methods.

The MegaByte architecture was conceptually similar to BLT in its use of a global model operating on patches and a local model for byte-level processing. However, it differed in several crucial respects. First, the patching was fixed and static, with bytes grouped into patches of a constant size $k$. As BLT's authors would later observe, this strided patching approach comes with significant downsides: compute is not dynamically allocated to where it is needed most, and it leads to inconsistent and non-contextual patching of similar byte sequences, such as the same word being split differently depending on its position. Second, MegaByte did not include a local encoder model; the patch representations were formed by simple concatenation of byte embeddings, without the benefit of learned encoding or cross-attention mechanisms. Third, MegaByte was evaluated at a scale and in a compute regime that did not fully reflect the compute-optimal training setups used by state-of-the-art token-based models such as Llama 3.

When BLT's authors ablated MegaByte in their experiments, they found that static patching lagged behind current state-of-the-art compute-optimally trained tokenizer-based models in a flop-controlled setting. Specifically, in scaling law experiments, they approximated SpaceByte using BLT's architecture with space patching but without n-gram embeddings and cross-attention, and found that while SpaceByte improved over MegaByte, it remained far from Llama 3 performance. This finding highlighted that simply adopting a patching-based approach was not sufficient to match the performance of modern token-based models—architectural innovations and dynamic patching were needed to bridge the gap.

The limitations of MegaByte at scale can be attributed to several factors. The fixed patching scheme fails to account for the varying information density of different parts of the input, wasting computation on predictable bytes while potentially under-allocating compute for information-dense regions. The lack of a learned encoder means that the patch representations are less expressive than they could be, as they do not benefit from the kind of cross-attention-based pooling that BLT employs. Furthermore, the concatenation approach to forming patch representations does not provide a mechanism for the model to learn how to best aggregate byte-level information into patch-level representations, a capability that BLT's cross-attention modules are specifically designed to enable.

#

## SpaceByte: Whitespace-Based Patching with a Local Encoder Model

SpaceByte, developed by Slagle and Listerman, addressed some of the limitations of MegaByte by introducing a simple yet effective improvement: rather than using fixed-size patches, SpaceByte created new patches after any space-like bytes, which serve as natural boundaries for linguistic units in many languages. Space-like bytes were defined as any byte that is not a Latin character, digit, or UTF-8 continuation byte, with the additional constraint that each patch must contain at least one non-space-like byte. This ensured that words were patched in the same way across sequences and that computational effort was allocated for hard predictions, which often follow spaces.

The key insight behind SpaceByte was that predicting the first byte of a word is typically much harder than predicting the remaining bytes. For example, predicting the first byte of the answer to the question "Who composed the Magic Flute?" is much harder than predicting the remaining bytes once the first character "M" is known, since the first character significantly reduces the number of likely choices, making the completion "Mozart" comparatively easy. By placing patch boundaries at word boundaries, SpaceByte ensured that the global transformer was invoked precisely at the points where its computational power was most needed.

SpaceByte also introduced a local encoder model, a lightweight transformer that processed byte-level information before it was fed to the global model. This was a significant architectural improvement over MegaByte, which relied on simple concatenation. The local encoder allowed the model to build more expressive byte representations before they were pooled into patch representations, though it did not employ the cross-attention mechanism that BLT would later introduce.

Slagle and Listerman found that SpaceByte improved over MegaByte and, in some domains such as GitHub and arXiv, even outperformed tokenized-based transformer models in a compute-controlled setting at the 1-billion-parameter scale. This was an encouraging result, suggesting that dynamic patching based on linguistic boundaries could be a viable path to competitive byte-level modeling. However, SpaceByte had its own limitations. Most fundamentally, whitespace-based patching cannot gracefully handle all languages and domains. Many languages do not use spaces between words, and in domains such as code or mathematics, whitespace does not consistently align with boundaries between linguistic or semantic units. Moreover, space patching cannot vary the patch size—the patch size is determined by the length of words in the input, with no mechanism for controlling the average patch size or adapting it to different computational budgets.

When BLT's authors evaluated SpaceByte in their experiments, they found that while it improved over MegaByte, it remained far from the performance of Llama 3. In their downstream task evaluations, the BLT-Space variant underperformed the Llama 3 tokenizer-based model on all but one task. However, it did achieve a significant reduction in inference flops due to its larger average patch size of 6 bytes, compared to approximately 4.5 bytes for BPE and entropy-patching-based models. This highlighted a key trade-off: space patching could achieve computational efficiency but at the cost of task performance, suggesting that more sophisticated patching methods were needed to achieve both efficiency and quality simultaneously.

#

## How BLT Advances Beyond Prior Patching-Based and Byte-Level Work

The Byte Latent Transformer represents a convergence of the insights and innovations from prior byte-level and patching-based approaches, combined with several novel architectural and methodological advances that enable it to match the performance of state-of-the-art tokenization-based models at scale for the first time. BLT's contributions can be understood as addressing the specific limitations of each prior approach while introducing new capabilities that were not previously available.

The most fundamental advance of BLT over prior patching-based approaches is its use of entropy-based dynamic patching. Unlike MegaByte's fixed static patching, which allocates the same amount of compute to every patch regardless of its complexity, and unlike SpaceByte's whitespace-based patching, which is limited to languages and domains where spaces serve as natural boundaries, BLT's entropy patching uses a data-driven approach to identify points of high uncertainty in the next-byte prediction. A small byte-level auto-regressive language model is trained on the training data, and next-byte entropies are computed under this model's distribution. Patch boundaries are then placed at points where the entropy exceeds a threshold, ensuring that the global transformer is invoked precisely where the data complexity demands it. This approach generalizes across all languages and domains, as it does not rely on any language-specific heuristics, and it provides a natural mechanism for controlling the average patch size by adjusting the entropy threshold.

BLT also introduces significant architectural innovations over prior approaches. Unlike MegaByte, which lacks a learned encoder and relies on concatenation, BLT employs a lightweight local encoder model with cross-attention layers that efficiently map sequences of bytes into expressive patch representations. The encoder uses hash n-gram embeddings to incorporate information about preceding bytes, allowing the model to capture local context that would be lost in a simple pooling operation. The cross-attention module, inspired by the Perceiver architecture but adapted for variable-length patch representations, allows each patch representation to attend specifically to the bytes that constitute it, ensuring that the patch representation captures the relevant byte-level information. Similarly, the local decoder uses cross-attention in the reverse direction, with byte representations as queries attending to patch representations as keys and values, allowing the model to effectively decode patch-level information back into individual bytes.

A crucial architectural insight in BLT is the separation of the model into three components: a large global latent transformer that operates on patch representations and consumes the bulk of the computational budget, and two small local models (encoder and decoder) that handle byte-level processing. This separation allows the model to dynamically allocate compute by choosing when to invoke the expensive global transformer. The local models, being much smaller, can afford to process every byte without incurring prohibitive computational cost. This design directly addresses the observation that at scale, the cost of a transformer is dominated by feed-forward network layers, not attention, and that prior approaches to efficient byte-level modeling focused primarily on reducing attention costs rather than reducing the number of times the expensive feed-forward layers are invoked.

BLT also breaks free of the efficiency trade-offs that constrain tokenization-based models. In standard LLMs, increasing the vocabulary size means larger tokens on average (and therefore fewer model steps) but also a larger output dimension for the final projection layer and embedding table. For example, Llama 3 increases the average token size from 3.7 to 4.4 bytes at the cost of increasing the size of its embedding table fourfold compared to Llama 2. This trade-off leaves little room for tokenization-based approaches to achieve significant variations in token size and inference cost. BLT, by contrast, can arbitrarily increase the patch size without increasing the vocabulary, because patches do not correspond to a fixed vocabulary. This unlocks a new dimension for scaling: model size and patch size can be increased simultaneously while maintaining the same inference flop budget. Longer patch sizes save compute, which can be reallocated to grow the global latent transformer, as it is run less often. BLT's fixed inference scaling experiments demonstrated that this approach yields significantly better scaling trends than tokenization-based architectures.

The empirical results of BLT at scale represent a milestone for byte-level modeling. Prior byte-level models either achieved competitive performance only at small scales or were prohibitively expensive to scale up. BLT, for the first time, matched the training flop-controlled performance of Llama 3 at scales up to 8 billion parameters and 4 trillion training bytes, while using up to 50% fewer flops at inference. In downstream task evaluations, BLT-Entropy outperformed the Llama 3 model on 4 out of 7 tasks while being trained on the same number of bytes. Beyond performance, BLT demonstrated significant qualitative improvements over tokenization-based models, including robustness to input noise (outperforming Llama 3 by an average of 8 points on noised HellaSwag), character-level understanding (outperforming BPE Llama 3 models by more than 25 points on the CUTE benchmark), phonology (outperforming Llama 3 on grapheme-to-phoneme conversion), and low-resource machine translation (achieving a 2-point overall advantage in translating into English on the FLORES-101 benchmark).

BLT also demonstrated the ability to leverage existing pre-trained tokenizer-based models, opening a path for "byte-ifying" models like Llama 3.1 that have been trained on more than 15 trillion tokens. By initializing the global transformer parameters of BLT with those of a pre-trained Llama 3.1 model and updating them at a reduced learning rate while training the local encoder and decoder from scratch, BLT from Llama 3.1 significantly outperformed both Llama 3 and BLT baselines trained with the same number of flops. This approach suggests a practical path to combining the benefits of byte-level modeling with the vast investments that have been made in training token-based models.

In summary, BLT advances beyond prior work by combining entropy-based dynamic patching for compute allocation, cross-attention-based encoder and decoder architectures for expressive byte-to-patch and patch-to-byte mappings, hash n-gram embeddings for local context modeling, and the ability to scale model and patch size simultaneously within a fixed inference budget. These innovations, grounded in the insights and lessons of a decade of research on character- and byte-level language modeling, enable BLT to achieve what no prior byte-level model has achieved: matching the performance of state-of-the-art tokenization-based models at scale, with significant improvements in efficiency, robustness, and character-level understanding.

# Limitations, Future Directions, and Conclusion

## Limitations: Reliance on BPE-Derived Scaling Laws

A central limitation of the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) as presented in this work concerns the manner in which the training budget—specifically, the ratio of model parameters to training data—was determined. Throughout the experiments, the authors adopt the scaling laws derived for Llama 3, which were calculated for Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE)-based transformer models. These scaling laws, originally formulated by \citet{hoffmann2022training} and refined by \citet{dubey2024llama}, prescribe the compute-optimal number of training tokens (or, in BLT's case, training bytes) for a given model parameter count. The authors use these laws to determine how many steps to train each BLT model variant, ranging from 400M to 8B parameters.

The concern here is subtle but important. Scaling laws are architecture-dependent: they describe the relationship between model size, data size, and performance for a particular class of models. BPE-based transformers and BLT differ in fundamental ways—BLT operates on dynamically sized patches rather than fixed tokens, employs a hybrid architecture with local encoder/decoder models and a global latent transformer, and allocates compute non-uniformly across the input sequence based on entropy. These architectural differences mean that the compute-optimal ratio of parameters to data for BPE models may not directly transfer to BLT. It is possible, for instance, that BLT models benefit from training on more data relative to their parameter count than BPE models do, given that dynamic patching allows BLT to process more bytes per unit of compute in the global transformer. Alternatively, the optimal ratio could shift in the other direction if the local encoder and decoder modules introduce different scaling dynamics.

The authors acknowledge this limitation explicitly, noting that the scaling laws were "calculated for BPE-level transformers and may lead to suboptimal (data, parameter sizes) ratios in the case of BLT." They observe that their results validate the assumption that the optimal ratio is "at least not too far off," since BLT models trained under these assumptions match or outperform their BPE counterparts. However, the absence of dedicated scaling laws for BLT means that the experiments may not represent the true compute-optimal frontier for this architecture. The implications are twofold: first, some experiments may be undertrained relative to what is optimal for BLT, and second, the crossover points where BLT surpasses BPE in fixed-inference-flop comparisons could shift if BLT were trained according to its own optimal scaling laws. This limitation is particularly relevant for the fixed-inference scaling experiments, where the relative positions of different architectures on the scaling curves depend sensitively on the training budget allocation.

## Limitations: Wall-Clock Efficiency Gaps

A second acknowledged limitation pertains to the gap between theoretical FLOP comparisons and actual wall-clock training and inference times. The paper's central efficiency claims are grounded in FLOP counts—the authors compute the number of floating-point operations required for each model variant and use this as the primary metric for comparing BLT against BPE-based models. This is a principled choice: FLOPs are hardware-agnostic and provide a clean theoretical basis for comparing architectural efficiency. However, the paper acknowledges that theoretical FLOP counts do not directly translate to wall-clock performance in practice.

The root of this discrepancy lies in the software ecosystem. Existing transformer libraries and codebases—including optimized kernels for attention, feed-forward layers, and embedding lookups—have been developed and refined over years specifically for tokenizer-based transformer architectures. Frameworks such as FlashAttention \citep{dao2022flashattention} are highly optimized for the standard attention patterns used in BPE models, such as block-causal masks over uniform-length token sequences. BLT, by contrast, introduces several architectural elements that deviate from this standard template. The cross-attention layers in the encoder and decoder involve dynamic, patch-dependent attention masks that change based on the patching boundaries, which vary from sequence to sequence. The local encoder and decoder operate on byte sequences with local windowed attention, and the global transformer operates on variable-length patch sequences. These non-standard attention patterns require specialized implementations.

The authors address this by using FlexAttention, a PyTorch library that produces fused implementations for custom attention masks, to handle the dynamic patch-dependent masks in cross-attention layers. This represents a significant engineering effort to bring BLT's implementation closer to the efficiency of standard transformer code. Nevertheless, the authors note that "our implementations may yet not be at parity with tokenizer-based models in terms of wall-clock time and may benefit from further optimizations." This means that while BLT may require fewer FLOPs than a BPE model of comparable quality, the actual time to train or run inference with BLT could be longer if the implementation is not as heavily optimized. This gap is a practical limitation that affects the real-world deployment of BLT, even if the theoretical efficiency advantages are clear. Closing this gap would require investment in developing optimized kernels specifically designed for BLT's architectural patterns, analogous to the years of engineering that have been devoted to optimizing BPE-based transformers.

## Future Work: End-to-End Learned Patching and Dedicated BLT Scaling Laws

The paper identifies two closely related directions for future research that address the limitations discussed above. The first is the development of end-to-end learned patching. In the current BLT architecture, patching is determined by a separately trained entropy model—a small byte-level language model (approximately 100M parameters) that computes next-byte entropies, which are then thresholded to identify patch boundaries. This entropy model is trained in advance on the same data distribution as the main BLT model, and its patching decisions are fixed during BLT training. This introduces a degree of indirection: the patching policy is not directly optimized for the downstream objective of the global transformer. The entropy model is a proxy for the true objective, which is to allocate compute in a way that maximizes the global model's language modeling performance.

An end-to-end approach to learned patching would integrate the boundary-decision mechanism directly into the BLT training objective, allowing the model to jointly learn where to place patch boundaries and how to predict within patches. This is conceptually appealing because it would allow the model to discover patch boundaries that are optimal for the specific architecture and task, rather than relying on the heuristic that high-entropy bytes should serve as boundary points. However, end-to-end learned patching introduces significant technical challenges. The patching decision is discrete—each byte is either a boundary or not—which makes it non-differentiable. Approaches such as reinforcement learning, differentiable approximations, or the straight-through estimator could be explored. Additionally, the authors note that their current approach, where a lightweight classifier predicts entropy-based boundaries, is similar in spirit to the work of \citet{nawrot-etal-2023-efficient}, suggesting that learned boundary prediction is a promising but non-trivial direction.

The second direction is the calculation of dedicated scaling laws for BLT. As discussed above, the current experiments rely on BPE-derived scaling laws to determine the compute-optimal training budget. Developing scaling laws specific to BLT would involve systematically varying model size, data size, and patch size to fit iso-loss curves, analogous to the approach taken by \citet{hoffmann2022training} for standard transformers. This is a substantial experimental undertaking but could yield significant benefits. If BLT's optimal parameter-to-data ratio differs from BPE's, then the scaling experiments presented in the paper may underestimate BLT's true potential. For example, if BLT benefits from training on relatively more data than BPE models of the same size, then the crossover points where BLT surpasses BPE in the fixed-inference scaling experiments could occur earlier—i.e., at lower training budgets—than what is reported. Dedicated scaling laws would also provide guidance for how to jointly scale the three components of BLT (encoder, global transformer, and decoder) as total model size increases, which is a question not fully resolved by the current experiments. The authors observe that when growing total parameters from 400M to 8B, they only roughly double BLT's local model parameters, suggesting that the local models become a smaller fraction of total compute at larger scales. Dedicated scaling laws would provide a principled basis for these architectural decisions.

## Future Work: Fully Leveraging Byte-ified Pre-trained Token Models

A particularly intriguing direction for future work emerges from the experiments on initializing BLT from a pre-trained Llama 3.1 model, as described in Section 5 of the paper. In these experiments, the global transformer parameters of BLT are initialized with the weights of a pre-trained Llama 3.1 model, and the local encoder and decoder are trained from scratch with a higher learning rate while the global transformer is fine-tuned at one-tenth the learning rate. The results are promising: the resulting model, referred to as "BLT from Llama 3.1," outperforms both the Llama 3 baseline and a BLT baseline trained from scratch with the same number of FLOPs. On the MMLU benchmark, it even outperforms the BLT-Entropy model trained on a significantly larger dataset of 1T tokens, suggesting that leveraging pre-trained weights can substantially reduce the training cost of BLT.

This setup can be interpreted as a method for "byte-ifying" a tokenizer-based model—transforming a pre-trained Llama 3.1 model into a tokenizer-free BLT model. The appeal of this approach is clear: it allows BLT to benefit from the vast computational investment that has already been made in training large tokenizer-based models, rather than requiring BLT to be trained from scratch on equivalent data. However, the current results also reveal limitations. When compared to the original Llama 3.1 model trained on 15T tokens, the byte-ified BLT model experiences a slight performance decline on MMLU and HumanEval, and a more significant drop on other tasks. This suggests that the transfer of knowledge from the pre-trained global transformer to the BLT architecture is not yet fully effective.

The authors identify several potential reasons for this performance gap and corresponding avenues for improvement. First, the data mixtures and hyperparameters used for fine-tuning the byte-ified model may not be optimally configured for the BLT architecture. The local encoder and decoder, which are trained from scratch, need to learn how to effectively interface with the pre-trained global transformer, and the learning rate schedule, data composition, and training duration may all need to be adjusted. Second, the patching scheme used during byte-ification may need to be adapted to the pre-trained model's representations. The global transformer was trained on BPE tokens, and its internal representations are structured around token-level abstractions. The BLT encoder must learn to produce patch representations that are compatible with these pre-existing token-level representations, which may require careful alignment of patch boundaries with token boundaries during the initial phase of training. Future work in this direction could explore curriculum-based training strategies, where the encoder and decoder are first trained to approximate the token-level representations before being fine-tuned jointly with the global transformer. The authors suggest that further work "may uncover methods that not only retain the benefits of bytefying, but also push performance beyond that of these tokenizer-based models without training them from scratch," making this a high-value direction for practical deployment.

## Summary of Contributions

The Byte Latent Transformer introduces several interconnected contributions that collectively advance the state of byte-level language modeling. The first and most fundamental contribution is the concept of dynamic compute allocation through entropy-based patching. Unlike tokenization-based models, which allocate the same amount of compute to every token regardless of its predictability, BLT segments byte sequences into patches at points of high next-byte entropy. This means that more compute—specifically, more steps of the large global transformer—is allocated to regions of the input where predictions are difficult and uncertain, while predictable regions are grouped into larger patches and processed with fewer transformer steps. The entropy model that determines these boundaries is a lightweight byte-level language model that estimates the information content of each byte given its context. This data-driven approach to compute allocation is a principled alternative to the heuristic compression employed by BPE tokenizers, which group bytes based on frequency rather than predictability.

The second contribution is the demonstration that BLT matches the training FLOP-controlled performance of Llama 3 at scales up to 8B parameters and 4T training bytes. This is the first time a byte-level transformer architecture has achieved parity with state-of-the-art tokenization-based models at this scale. The authors present a comprehensive flop-controlled scaling study, training BLT models at 400M, 1B, 2B, 4B, and 8B parameter scales, and show that BLT either matches or outperforms BPE-based Llama 3 models when both are trained with the same FLOP budget. This result validates the core architectural choices of BLT, including the combination of entropy-based patching, hash n-gram embeddings, and cross-attention modules in the encoder and decoder. On downstream task evaluations, the BLT-Entropy model trained on the BLT-1T dataset outperforms Llama 3 on 4 out of 7 benchmarks, including common sense reasoning, world knowledge, and code generation tasks.

The third contribution concerns inference efficiency. Because BLT's patch size can be arbitrarily controlled by adjusting the entropy threshold, the model can trade a small amount of evaluation performance for substantial inference FLOP savings. The paper reports that BLT achieves up to 50\% fewer inference FLOPs than Llama 3 while maintaining comparable performance. This is possible because larger average patch sizes mean the global transformer processes fewer steps per unit of input data. In contrast, BPE-based models are constrained by their fixed vocabulary size: increasing the average token size requires increasing the vocabulary, which in turn increases the size of the embedding and output projection layers. This tradeoff leaves little room for BPE models to achieve significant variations in token size, as illustrated by the fact that Llama 3 increased its average token size from 3.7 to 4.4 bytes only at the cost of a 4x increase in embedding table size compared to Llama 2.

The fourth contribution is improved robustness and sub-word understanding. Because BLT operates directly on raw bytes, it has access to character-level information that tokenizer-based models lack. The paper demonstrates this through several evaluations: BLT outperforms BPE models on noised versions of benchmark tasks (with an average advantage of 8 points on noised HellaSwag), on the CUTE benchmark for character-level understanding (with improvements of more than 25 points, including 99.9\% accuracy on spelling tasks), on the Grapheme-to-Phoneme task, and on low-resource machine translation from the FLORES-101 benchmark. These results indicate that byte-level modeling provides a genuine advantage in handling the long tail of data—sequences, languages, and patterns that are underrepresented in the training data and poorly served by fixed-vocabulary tokenizers.

## The New Scaling Dimension: Simultaneously Growing Model and Patch Size

One of the most significant conceptual contributions of the BLT architecture is the introduction of a new dimension for scaling large language models. In traditional tokenization-based architectures, the primary lever for scaling is model size—increasing the number of parameters, layers, and hidden dimensions of the transformer. The vocabulary size and average token size are essentially fixed design choices that cannot be easily varied after training, and the tradeoff between vocabulary size and compute (larger vocabularies yield larger average token sizes but also larger embedding tables) severely limits the practical range of token sizes. This means that for a fixed inference budget, the only way to improve a BPE model is to train it on more data or find more efficient training methods; the inference cost per token is determined by the architecture and cannot be reduced without reducing model size.

BLT breaks free of this constraint by decoupling the patch size from any fixed vocabulary. The patch size is controlled by the entropy threshold, which can be adjusted independently of the model architecture. This creates a new scaling dimension: for a fixed inference FLOP budget, one can simultaneously increase the model size and the patch size. Larger patch sizes reduce the number of global transformer steps per byte of input, which saves compute. This saved compute can be reinvested in a larger global transformer, because the larger transformer is invoked less frequently. The key insight is that the global transformer's FLOPs scale with the number of patches, not the number of bytes, so increasing the patch size directly reduces the per-byte cost of the global transformer while the local encoder and decoder (which operate on bytes) are relatively inexpensive.

The paper provides empirical evidence for this scaling strategy through fixed-inference-FLOP scaling experiments. Starting from model sizes of 400M and 3.6B parameters, the authors identify FLOP-equivalent BLT models with larger patch sizes (6 and 8 bytes on average) and correspondingly larger global transformers. The results show that BLT models achieve better scaling trends than BPE-based architectures within both inference FLOP classes tested. In both cases, BPE models perform better with small training budgets but are surpassed by BLT beyond the compute-optimal regime. Importantly, the crossover point where BLT surpasses BPE shifts closer to the compute-optimal point as the model scale increases, suggesting that the advantage of the patch-based scaling dimension grows with model size. The authors observe that the larger patch size 8 model has a steeper scaling trend in the larger FLOP class, overtaking other models sooner. This is attributed, in part, to the decreasing share of total FLOPs consumed by the byte-level encoder and decoder modules at larger scales. When growing total parameters 20x from 400M to 8B, the local model parameters only roughly double, meaning that the global transformer—which is the component affected by patch size—accounts for an increasing fraction of total compute. This makes larger patch sizes more effective at larger scales, as the savings from longer patches are applied to a larger share of the total computation.

This new scaling dimension has practical implications for how language models are deployed. In practice, many state-of-the-art models are trained well beyond the compute-optimal point—Llama 3.1, for example, was trained on two orders of magnitude more data than what is compute-optimal for its model size. This overtraining regime is motivated by the desire to achieve better performance at a fixed inference cost, since inference is performed many more times than training. BLT's patch-size scaling dimension provides an additional and complementary lever for improving the inference-cost-to-performance ratio, one that is unavailable to tokenizer-based architectures. The authors demonstrate that, in this overtraining regime, BLT's advantage over BPE becomes more pronounced, making it a particularly attractive architecture for deployment scenarios where inference cost is the primary concern.

## Broader Implications for Tokenizer-Free LLM Architectures

The results presented in the BLT paper carry implications that extend beyond the specific architecture and experimental settings described. Perhaps most fundamentally, the paper challenges the prevailing assumption in the field that tokenization is a necessary and unavoidable component of large language model training. Since the widespread adoption of subword tokenization methods such as BPE \citep{sennrich-etal-2016-neural}, the standard pipeline for training LLMs has included a tokenization step that maps raw text to a sequence of tokens drawn from a fixed vocabulary. This step has been taken for granted even as other components of the pipeline—model architecture, training data, optimization methods—have undergone rapid innovation. The BLT paper demonstrates that this step is not necessary: a byte-level model can match the performance of a tokenization-based model at scale, while simultaneously offering advantages in efficiency, robustness, and flexibility.

The implications of removing tokenization are multifaceted. Tokenization introduces several well-documented shortcomings: sensitivity to domain and modality shifts (a tokenizer trained on web text may perform poorly on code or mathematical notation), sensitivity to input noise (a single character change can alter the tokenization of an entire word), lack of orthographic knowledge (tokens do not expose their constituent characters to the model), and multilingual inequity (languages with different scripts or morphological structures are tokenized at different granularities, leading to unfair allocation of compute across languages). BLT addresses all of these issues by operating directly on bytes. The paper's robustness experiments provide concrete evidence: BLT outperforms BPE models on noised inputs, on character-level manipulation tasks, on phonological transcription, and on low-resource machine translation. These improvements are not marginal; the 25-point improvement on the CUTE benchmark and the 8-point improvement on noised HellaSwag suggest that the byte-level representation provides the model with capabilities that are fundamentally difficult to recover when the model's input is mediated by a tokenizer.

The architecture of BLT also suggests a general principle for future model design: compute should be allocated dynamically based on the information content of the input, not uniformly across all input units. This principle is applicable beyond byte-level modeling. Any model that processes sequences—whether of characters, audio samples, image patches, or other units—could potentially benefit from a patching mechanism that allocates more compute to high-entropy regions and less to low-entropy regions. The entropy-based patching approach used in BLT provides one instantiation of this principle, but other methods for estimating information density could also be explored. The cross-attention mechanism that BLT uses to interface between byte-level and patch-level representations is also a general architectural pattern that could be adapted to other hierarchical modeling scenarios.

Looking forward, the paper's results suggest several broader trends. First, as the software ecosystem matures and optimized implementations for byte-level architectures become available, the wall-clock efficiency gap between BLT and tokenization-based models is likely to narrow, making BLT's theoretical FLOP advantages more directly realized in practice. Second, the development of dedicated scaling laws for BLT could reveal even more favorable scaling trends than those reported in the paper, potentially shifting the crossover points with BPE models to lower training budgets. Third, the byte-ification approach—converting pre-trained tokenizer-based models into byte-level models—offers a practical pathway for the field to transition toward tokenizer-free architectures without discarding the enormous computational investment that has already been made in training existing models. If this approach can be refined to fully preserve and extend the performance of the pre-trained model, it could accelerate the adoption of byte-level architectures across the field. Taken together, these directions suggest that the BLT architecture represents not just an alternative to tokenization-based models, but a foundation for a new paradigm in which language models are trained and deployed without the constraints imposed by fixed-vocabulary tokenization.
