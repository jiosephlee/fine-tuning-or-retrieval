\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

### Why do larger patch sizes perform better at larger model scales?
Question:
In Section 5.1, the paper observes that 'BLT with patch size of 8 starts at a significantly worse point compared to BPE Llama 2 at 1B but ends up better than BPE at 7B scale,' and suggests larger patch sizes perform better at larger scales. The paper attributes this 'in part, to the decreasing share of total FLOPs used by the byte-level Encoder and Decoder modules.' But I don't follow this logic. If the local models become a smaller fraction of total compute, how does that explain why larger patches (which reduce global transformer FLOPs) become more favorable? Is the argument that at larger scales, the global transformer is so dominant that you can afford to reduce its frequency without much quality loss?
Answer:
You're on the right track with your intuition, but the argument is more specifically about how FLOP savings from larger patches translate into model capacity growth, and why this matters more at larger scales.

The key insight is that in a FLOP-controlled comparison, larger patches reduce the number of global transformer steps (since fewer patches means the latent transformer runs less often), but they do **not** reduce the FLOPs of the local encoder and decoder, which still process every byte. So the FLOP savings from increasing patch size only apply to the global transformer portion of the total compute budget.

At small scales (e.g., $1\text{B}$ parameters), the local models constitute a substantial fraction of total FLOPs, because the paper notes that when scaling total parameters $20\times$ from $400\text{M}$ to $8\text{B}$, local model parameters only roughly double. This means the local models are relatively large compared to the global transformer at small scales. When you increase the patch size, the FLOP savings from fewer global transformer steps are partially "diluted" by the unchanged local model overhead, so the net savings available to reallocate to a larger global transformer are modest. The benefit of larger patches is therefore limited.

At larger scales (e.g., $8\text{B}$ parameters), the global latent transformer has grown dramatically while the local models have barely grown, so the global transformer dominates total compute. Now when you increase the patch size, the FLOP savings are a much larger fraction of total compute, because you're reducing the cost of the component that constitutes most of the budget. These freed-up FLOPs can be reallocated to make the global transformer even larger. The paper illustrates this concretely: with patch size 8, the BLT model grew from $1.6\times$ to $1.7\times$ the size of the Llama 2 model when moving to the larger FLOP class, meaning more capacity became available at the same inference cost.

So your interpretation is essentially correct but with an important refinement: it's not just that you can "afford" to reduce the global transformer's frequency at larger scales. Rather, in a fixed-inference-FLOP setting, the savings from reducing that frequency become more substantial as a fraction of total compute, allowing you to reallocate more compute into a larger global transformer. The paper's core claim is that this reallocation becomes increasingly favorable as models scale up, which is why larger patch sizes start at a disadvantage at $1\text{B}$ (where local model overhead eats into the savings) but overtake BPE at $7\text{B}$ (where the global transformer dominates and the savings translate into meaningful capacity gains).
