\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

### Why does the local decoder need encoder hidden states as input?
Question:
In Section 3.2, the local decoder takes as input 'the hidden representations produced by the local encoder for the byte-sequence' (specifically $h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$, the output of the last encoder layer). This seems odd — why would the decoder need information from the encoder? The decoder's job is to take global patch representations $o_j$ and produce bytes $y_i$. If the decoder already has the patch representations from the global transformer, what additional information does it get from the encoder's hidden states? Is this related to autoregressive decoding where the decoder needs byte-level context from previously generated bytes?
Answer:
The local decoder needs the encoder's hidden states because BLT's decoder is **autoregressive at the byte level** — it predicts each byte as a function of previously decoded bytes within a patch. The encoder's hidden states $h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$ provide the initial byte-level representations that make this autoregressive decoding possible, rather than forcing the decoder to build byte-level context from scratch.

To see why this matters, consider the decoder's computation as described in Section 3.3.1. The decoder cross-attention uses byte representations as **queries** and the global patch representations $o_j$ as **keys and values**. These byte representations must be initialized to *something* before the first cross-attention layer, and the paper initializes them as $D_0 = h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$. The encoder has already processed the byte sequence through its transformer layers with local block-causal attention and hash n-gram embeddings, producing rich contextualized representations at each byte position. By passing these to the decoder, the decoder starts with byte representations that already encode local byte-level context — information about preceding bytes, n-gram statistics, and local patterns — rather than starting from raw byte embeddings alone.

The global patch representations $o_j$ from the latent transformer contain high-level semantic information computed over the entire patch sequence, but they operate at the **patch** granularity, not the **byte** granularity. A single patch representation $o_j$ must somehow guide the prediction of multiple individual bytes $y_i$ within that patch. The cross-attention mechanism in the decoder achieves this by letting each byte-level query attend to the relevant patch representation (the key/value), effectively "broadcasting" patch-level information down to the byte level. But the queries themselves need to carry byte-level identity and positional context — which is exactly what $h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$ provides.

This design also avoids redundant computation. The encoder has already invested computational effort in producing contextualized byte representations with n-gram hash embeddings and local attention. Reconstructing equivalent byte-level context inside the decoder would waste parameters and flops. Since the local encoder and decoder are both lightweight ($l_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{D}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$), sharing the encoder's representations with the decoder is an efficient way to give the decoder a strong byte-level starting point, which it then refines through its own cross-attention and transformer layers while incorporating the global information from $o_j$.

In essence, the global patch representations tell the decoder *what* to generate at a high level, while the encoder hidden states give the decoder the *byte-level scaffolding* it needs to generate individual bytes autoregressively, conditioning on previously decoded bytes within the same patch.
