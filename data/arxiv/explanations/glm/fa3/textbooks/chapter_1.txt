Chapter 1: Introduction to Attention and the Need for Speed

## The Transformer Architecture and the Role of Attention

The Transformer architecture, introduced by Vaswani et al. in 2017, has become the dominant neural network design for a vast range of tasks in natural language processing, computer vision, audio processing, and beyond. At the heart of the Transformer is the attention mechanism, which allows the model to selectively focus on different parts of the input sequence when producing each element of the output. Unlike earlier recurrent architectures that process tokens one at a time in sequence, the attention mechanism enables every position in the input to directly attend to every other position, capturing long-range dependencies in a single computational step. This architectural innovation is what gives Transformers their remarkable flexibility and power, but it also introduces a significant computational cost that scales with the size of the input.

In a standard Transformer, attention is instantiated as multi-head attention (MHA). Each attention head operates on its own set of query, key, and value projections derived from the input. For a single head, let $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ be the query, key, and value matrices, where $N$ is the sequence length and $d$ is the head dimension. The attention output $\mathbf{O}$ is computed as $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$, followed by $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$, and finally $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$, where $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{d}$ is a scaling factor applied to prevent the dot-product scores from growing too large in magnitude. The softmax operation is applied row-wise, and in practice one subtracts the row maximum of $\mathbf{S}$ before exponentiating to maintain numerical stability. In multi-head attention, this entire computation is replicated across multiple heads and across the batch dimension, and the results are concatenated and projected to produce the final output. Because each head independently performs this query-key-value computation, the attention mechanism is embarrassingly parallel across heads and batches, a property that proves critical for efficient GPU implementation.

The attention layer is not merely one component among many in the Transformer; it constitutes the primary computational bottleneck. The reason is that the two matrix multiplications involving $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ and $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$ together dominate the floating-point operation count of the forward pass, and the backward pass requires even more matrix multiplications to compute gradients. Given the current trend toward ever-larger models and ever-longer input sequences, attention has become the single most expensive operation in the forward and backward passes of Transformer-based large language models (LLMs). As a result, optimizing attention computation is one of the most impactful ways to improve the efficiency of the entire model.

## Quadratic Scaling of Self-Attention with Sequence Length

The fundamental computational challenge of attention stems from the matrix $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$, which has shape $N \times N$. Because every query must be compared against every key, the number of entries in the attention score matrix grows quadratically with the sequence length $N$. Computing $\mathbf{S}$ requires $O(N^2 d)$ floating-point operations, computing the softmax over $\mathbf{S}$ requires $O(N^2)$ operations, and computing $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$ requires another $O(N^2 d)$ operations. The total operation count for the forward pass is therefore $O(N^2 d)$, and the backward pass, which involves additional matrix multiplications to compute gradients with respect to $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$, roughly multiplies this by a factor of 2.5. In terms of FLOPs, the forward pass requires $4 \cdot N^2 \cdot d \cdot H$ operations, where $H$ is the number of heads, and the backward pass requires approximately 2.5 times that amount due to the need for five matrix multiplications (compared to two in the forward pass) when using recomputation to avoid storing intermediate results.

This quadratic scaling means that doubling the sequence length quadruples the computation and memory required for attention. For a sequence of length 1,024, the attention matrix $\mathbf{S}$ has about one million entries; for a sequence of length 16,384, it has over 268 million entries. In a naive (or "standard") implementation of attention, these intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ are materialized to the GPU's high-bandwidth memory (HBM), meaning they are written to and read from global memory during the computation. This creates enormous memory traffic: the $N \times N$ matrices must be written once and read at least once, resulting in $O(N^2)$ memory accesses that can easily dominate the overall runtime, especially since HBM bandwidth, while large, is finite and becomes a bottleneck relative to the raw compute capacity of modern GPUs. The quadratic scaling in both computation and memory thus poses a fundamental barrier to extending Transformers to long sequences.

## Applications Driving the Need for Long-Context Attention

The pressure to scale attention to longer sequences comes from a broad range of emerging applications and capabilities. In natural language processing, there is growing interest in models that can reason over multiple long documents simultaneously, such as in the LongT5 and SCROLLS benchmarks, which require the model to process tens of thousands of tokens at once. Similarly, techniques like YaRN extend the effective context window of pretrained models, enabling them to handle inputs far longer than those seen during training. These long-context capabilities are essential for tasks such as document summarization, multi-document question answering, and retrieval-augmented generation, where the model must synthesize information spread across a large corpus.

In the domain of code generation and software engineering, models are increasingly expected to understand and reason over entire codebases. Systems such as Code Llama and StarCoder process large code files and, in some cases, multiple files together to capture cross-file dependencies and project-level structure. As codebases can easily span hundreds of thousands of lines, the ability to attend over long contexts directly translates into more capable code assistants. Without efficient long-context attention, these models would be limited to processing individual functions or small snippets, severely constraining their usefulness.

Beyond text and code, long-context attention is critical for processing high-resolution images, where an image may be decomposed into thousands of patch tokens; audio, where long waveforms or spectrogram sequences must be modeled; and video, where temporal sequences of frames can produce extremely long token sequences. The Conformer architecture, for example, applies attention to audio sequences, and video diffusion models require attention over extended temporal spans. Each of these modalities benefits from the ability to attend over long sequences, and each is bottlenecked by the same quadratic scaling problem.

Finally, in interactive applications such as recommendation systems with long user histories and agent workflows that operate over long planning horizons, the ability to maintain and attend over extended context is essential. A recommendation model that can attend over a user's entire interaction history can capture richer preferences, and an agent that can reason over a long sequence of prior actions and observations can make more informed decisions. In all of these cases, the practical utility of the model is directly tied to how efficiently attention can be computed over long sequences, making attention optimization a first-order concern for advancing the capabilities of Transformer-based systems.

## Approaches to Efficient Attention: Approximation, Software Optimization, and Alternative Architectures

The research community has pursued several complementary directions to address the quadratic scaling of attention. One major line of work seeks to approximate the attention computation, reducing its complexity from $O(N^2)$ to something sub-quadratic or even linear. Linear attention, for example, replaces the softmax kernel with a feature map that allows the key-value product to be computed first, avoiding the explicit construction of the $N \times N$ attention matrix. Other approximation methods, such as Performer (which uses random feature approximations of the softmax kernel), similarly aim to reduce the computational cost while accepting some loss in accuracy. These approaches can offer significant speedups for very long sequences, but they typically introduce trade-offs in terms of expressiveness and may not match the quality of exact attention, particularly for tasks that rely on precise attention patterns.

A second direction focuses on software optimization of exact attention. Rather than changing the mathematical computation, these approaches restructure how attention is implemented on the GPU to minimize wasted work and memory traffic. The work of Rabe and Staats on self-attention chunking and, most notably, the FlashAttention lineage introduced by Dao et al. fall into this category. The key insight of FlashAttention is that attention can be computed exactly—producing the same result as standard attention—while avoiding the materialization of the intermediate $N \times N$ matrices to global memory. This is achieved through a tiling strategy that keeps intermediate results in faster on-chip memory (shared memory and registers) and fuses all attention operations into a single GPU kernel. By doing so, FlashAttention dramatically reduces memory reads and writes, yielding substantial speedups without any approximation. This line of work is the focus of the present chapter and the broader paper on which it is based.

A third direction explores alternative architectures that replace attention altogether. Models such as RWKV, RetNet, and Mamba propose recurrent or state-space formulations that achieve linear scaling in sequence length while retaining some of the benefits of attention, such as the ability to capture long-range dependencies. These architectures are particularly attractive for inference, where they can process tokens in constant time per step. However, they represent a more radical departure from the Transformer paradigm and face open questions about whether they can match the performance of attention-based models across the full range of tasks. In practice, attention remains the dominant mechanism in state-of-the-art models, and optimizing its implementation continues to be the most immediately impactful approach for improving efficiency.

## The FlashAttention Lineage: From FlashAttention-1 to FlashAttention-3

The FlashAttention lineage represents the most successful line of software optimization for exact attention, and understanding its evolution is essential for appreciating the contributions of FlashAttention-3. The original FlashAttention, introduced by Dao et al., was motivated by the observation that the bottleneck in standard attention is not the floating-point computation itself but rather the memory traffic incurred by writing and reading the large intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ to and from HBM. FlashAttention proposed a novel tiling strategy that partitions the query, key, and value matrices into blocks and processes them in a tiled fashion, keeping intermediate results in on-chip shared memory and registers. Crucially, it leveraged a local version of the softmax computation—maintaining running statistics (the row maximum and row sum) across blocks—that allows the correct softmax result to be computed without ever materializing the full $N \times N$ matrix. By fusing all attention operations (the two matrix multiplications and the softmax) into a single GPU kernel, FlashAttention eliminated the expensive intermediate reads and writes to global memory, achieving a significant speedup over standard attention while producing mathematically identical results.

FlashAttention-2, also introduced by Dao, restructured the original algorithm to further improve GPU utilization. The key changes included parallelizing the computation over the sequence length dimension (in addition to the batch and head dimensions) and restructuring the inner loop of the forward pass to iterate over blocks of the key and value matrices. This improved the occupancy and distribution of work across the GPU's streaming multiprocessors (SMs), leading to better hardware utilization. FlashAttention-2 became the de facto standard for efficient attention and was widely adopted in practice. However, as GPU architectures evolved, particularly with the introduction of NVIDIA's Hopper (H100) architecture, it became clear that FlashAttention-2 was not fully exploiting the new hardware's capabilities.

FlashAttention-3, the subject of the paper underlying this chapter, builds on FlashAttention-2 by explicitly incorporating three new hardware-aware techniques: producer-consumer asynchrony through warp-specialization, overlapping of softmax with asynchronous block-wise matrix multiplications (GEMMs), and hardware-accelerated low-precision computation using FP8. These techniques are designed specifically to take advantage of features present in the Hopper architecture, such as the asynchronous Tensor Cores (exposed via the WGMMA instruction), the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) for asynchronous memory copies, and support for FP8 low-precision arithmetic. The result is a dramatic improvement in both throughput and accuracy: FlashAttention-3 achieves 1.5--2.0$\times$ speedup over FlashAttention-2 in the FP16 forward pass, reaching up to 740 TFLOPs/s (75\% of the H100's theoretical peak), and close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s in FP8, while also reducing numerical error in FP8 by 2.6$\times$ compared to standard per-tensor quantization.

## Limitations of FlashAttention-2 on Modern GPUs: The 35% Utilization Problem

Despite its success, FlashAttention-2 achieves only about 35\% utilization on the NVIDIA H100 GPU, compared to the 80--90\% utilization achieved by optimized matrix multiplication (GEMM) kernels on the same hardware. This gap is striking and motivates the development of FlashAttention-3. Understanding why FlashAttention-2 underutilizes the H100 requires examining both implementation-level and algorithmic-level factors.

At the implementation level, FlashAttention-2 was originally designed for the Ampere architecture and does not make use of Hopper-specific instructions. For example, it does not use the WGMMA (warpgroup matrix multiply-accumulate) instruction, which is Hopper's asynchronous interface to the Tensor Cores, nor does it use the TMA (Tensor Memory Accelerator) for asynchronous memory copies. Instead, it relies on Ampere-era synchronous instructions, which do not allow the same degree of overlap between computation and data movement. Other works, such as ThunderKitten and cuDNN 9, have demonstrated that using Hopper-specific instructions and tile-based abstractions can improve attention performance, suggesting that implementation-level changes alone can yield meaningful gains.

However, the more fundamental limitation of FlashAttention-2 is algorithmic. Its algorithm adheres to a simplified synchronous execution model and makes no explicit use of two critical hardware trends: asynchrony and low precision. Asynchrony arises from hardware specialization: modern GPUs include dedicated hardware units for specific operations, such as Tensor Cores for matrix multiplication and TMA for memory loading, which operate independently of the general-purpose CUDA cores that handle logic, integer, and floating-point computation. This separation creates the potential for overlapping different types of work—for example, performing a matrix multiplication on the Tensor Cores while simultaneously loading data from global memory or executing non-matmul operations like the exponential function on the CUDA cores. FlashAttention-2's synchronous design does not exploit this potential.

The performance gap is particularly severe for non-matmul operations. On the H100, FP16 matrix multiplication achieves 989 TFLOPs/s, but special functions such as the exponential (needed for softmax) achieve only about 3.9 TFLOPs/s—a disparity of over 250$\times$. For a typical attention forward pass with head dimension 128 in FP16, there are 512 times more matmul FLOPs than exponential operations, but since the exponential throughput is 256 times lower, the exponential can consume up to 50\% of the total cycles relative to the matmul. With FP8, the matmul throughput doubles while the exponential throughput remains unchanged, making the imbalance even worse. Because FlashAttention-2 executes matmul and softmax sequentially within each iteration of its inner loop, the non-matmul operations create a serial bottleneck that limits overall throughput.

Low precision is the second missed opportunity. The H100 supports FP8 computation on its Tensor Cores, which can deliver twice the throughput of FP16 or BF16 for the same power and chip area. However, FlashAttention-2 does not target FP8, leaving a significant performance improvement unrealized. Adopting FP8 is nontrivial: it requires addressing layout conformance constraints imposed by the FP8 WGMMA instruction (which mandates that operands be contiguous in the inner, or "k-major," dimension) and managing quantization error, especially in the presence of outlier features common in large language models.

These limitations—implementation-level mismatch with Hopper instructions, algorithmic inability to overlap matmul with non-matmul operations, and lack of FP8 support—collectively explain the 35\% utilization of FlashAttention-2 on the H100. FlashAttention-3 addresses each of these through its three core techniques: warp-specialization with pingpong scheduling to overlap data movement and computation, two-stage pipelining to overlap softmax with asynchronous GEMMs, and block quantization with incoherent processing to enable accurate FP8 computation. The remainder of this text elaborates on each of these techniques in detail.
