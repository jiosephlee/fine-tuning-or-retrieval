Chapter 6: Pingpong Scheduling: Overlapping GEMMs Across Warpgroups

# Throughput Asymmetry: 989 TFLOPS FP16 Matmul vs. 3.9 TFLOPS Special Functions on H100

Modern GPUs are throughput processors that achieve high performance through concurrency and asynchrony, but not all computational operations enjoy the same level of hardware acceleration. On the NVIDIA H100 SXM5 GPU, there is a dramatic asymmetry between the throughput available for matrix multiplication and the throughput available for special functions such as the exponential. Specifically, the H100 delivers 989 TFLOPS of FP16 matrix multiplication, powered by dedicated hardware units called Tensor Cores. In stark contrast, the same GPU delivers only 3.9 TFLOPS for special functions like the exponential operation, which is critical for computing softmax in attention. This asymmetry is not incidental; it is a direct consequence of hardware specialization, in which the GPU dedicates specialized silicon to the most common and computationally expensive operations in machine learning workloads. Tensor Cores are purpose-built accelerators for matrix multiplication, while special functions are handled by a separate, less parallelized hardware unit known as the multi-function unit.

The 3.9 TFLOPS figure for special functions can be derived from the CUDA programming guide, which specifies that 16 operations of special functions can be performed per streaming multiprocessor (SM) per clock cycle. The H100 SXM5 has 132 SMs and a boost clock speed of 1830 MHz, so multiplying these together yields $16 \times 132 \times 1830 \text{ MHz} \approx 3.9$ TFLOPS. This is approximately 256 times lower than the FP16 matmul throughput of 989 TFLOPS. Understanding this asymmetry is essential for designing efficient attention algorithms, because the attention computation involves both GEMMs (handled by Tensor Cores) and softmax (which requires exponentials handled by the multi-function unit). If these operations are executed sequentially without overlap, the lower-throughput special functions become a significant bottleneck, leaving the high-throughput Tensor Cores idle for a substantial fraction of the total execution time.

# The Exponential Bottleneck in Softmax: Why It Dominates Non-GEMM Time

The softmax operation is a core component of attention, applied row-wise to the score matrix $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$. To compute softmax, one must evaluate the exponential function $\exp(\cdot)$ for each element of the (locally adjusted) score matrix. This is in addition to other non-matmul operations such as row-wise maximum, subtraction, row-wise summation, and division. Among these, the exponential is the most computationally expensive because it is a transcendental function that must be evaluated by the multi-function unit, which has dramatically lower throughput than the Tensor Cores.

To quantify the impact, consider the attention forward pass in FP16 with head dimension 128. In this setting, there are 512 times more matmul FLOPs compared to exponential operations. However, since the exponential has 256 times lower throughput than matmul, the exponential operations can consume up to 50% of the total cycle time compared to matmul. This means that even though the exponential represents a tiny fraction of the total FLOPs, its low throughput causes it to dominate the non-GEMM portion of the execution time. The remaining non-GEMM operations, such as floating point multiply-add for rescaling and row reductions, also run on the CUDA cores at lower throughput than Tensor Cores, but the exponential is particularly severe because it requires the multi-function unit.

The key insight is that the exponential is performed by a separate hardware unit from the Tensor Cores. This means that, in principle, the two operations can be executed concurrently: the Tensor Cores can be performing matrix multiplication while the multi-function unit is evaluating exponentials for the softmax. Without explicit overlap, however, the standard execution model would serialize these operations, leaving one hardware unit idle while the other is active. The FlashAttention-3 algorithm is designed precisely to exploit this concurrency opportunity.

# The Case for FP8: Doubling Matmul Throughput While Exponential Stays Constant

The throughput disparity between matmul and special functions becomes even more severe when moving to lower-precision number formats. On Hopper GPUs, the FP8 Tensor Cores deliver twice the throughput of FP16 or BF16 Tensor Cores for matrix multiplication. The WGMMA instruction can target FP8 Tensor Cores to achieve this 2x throughput improvement per SM. However, the throughput of special functions such as the exponential does not change with the precision of the GEMM operands, because these operations are performed on the softmax intermediate results, which remain in FP32 regardless of the input precision.

In the FP16 setting with head dimension 128, the exponential could take up to 50% of the cycle time compared to matmul. With FP8, the matmul throughput doubles to approximately 1978 TFLOPS, but the exponential throughput remains at 3.9 TFLOPS. The ratio of matmul FLOPs to exponential FLOPs stays the same (since it depends on the algorithm, not the precision), but now the throughput gap widens from 256x to 512x. As a result, the exponential would take an even larger fraction of the total time if operations are not overlapped. Specifically, without overlap, the exponential could take up to 100% of the matmul time in the FP8 setting, effectively halving the achievable utilization.

This observation makes a compelling case for techniques that overlap softmax (and its exponential operations) with GEMM execution. If the exponential can be scheduled to run concurrently with GEMMs on the Tensor Cores, then the effective throughput of the non-GEMM operations becomes less of a bottleneck, because they are hidden behind the GEMM latency. This is the fundamental motivation behind the pingpong scheduling technique described in the following sections.

# Pingpong Scheduling: Using bar.sync to Alternate GEMM and Softmax Between Two Consumer Warpgroups

The pingpong scheduling technique builds on the warp-specialization scheme of FlashAttention-3. In the warp-specialized design, the warps within a CTA (cooperative thread array) are divided into producer and consumer roles. Producer warps are responsible for data movement, issuing TMA (Tensor Memory Accelerator) loads to bring tiles of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ from HBM to shared memory. Consumer warps are responsible for computation, issuing WGMMA instructions to perform the GEMMs and CUDA core instructions to perform the softmax. On Hopper, there are typically two consumer warpgroups (each consisting of 4 contiguous warps, i.e., 128 threads), and the pingpong scheduling exploits the presence of these two consumer warpgroups.

The core idea of pingpong scheduling is to stagger the execution phases of the two consumer warpgroups such that when one warpgroup is performing softmax (which uses the multi-function unit and CUDA cores), the other warpgroup is performing GEMMs (which uses the Tensor Cores). Since these two hardware units are independent, both can operate simultaneously, effectively hiding the low-throughput softmax behind the high-throughput matmul. To achieve this, the algorithm uses synchronization barriers (\texttt{bar.sync} instructions) to enforce a specific ordering of GEMM operations across the two warpgroups.

Specifically, the barriers are used to force the GEMMs of warpgroup 1 to be scheduled before the GEMMs of warpgroup 2. The GEMMs in the attention forward pass consist of two matmuls per inner loop iteration: GEMM0, which computes $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, and GEMM1, which computes $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{V}$. The barriers ensure that GEMM1 of one iteration from warpgroup 1 and GEMM0 of the next iteration from warpgroup 1 are both scheduled before the corresponding GEMMs of warpgroup 2. As a result, while warpgroup 1's GEMMs are executing on the Tensor Cores, warpgroup 2's softmax is executing on the multi-function unit and CUDA cores. Then the roles swap: warpgroup 2 performs its GEMMs while warpgroup 1 performs its softmax. This alternating pattern is what gives the technique its name, "pingpong" scheduling, as the two warpgroups alternate between GEMM and softmax phases like players in a table tennis match.

The technical mechanism relies on the asynchronous nature of WGMMA on Hopper. Unlike prior architectures where Tensor Core operations were synchronous, Hopper's WGMMA instruction is asynchronous: it is issued and then the issuing thread can continue executing other instructions while the Tensor Cores process the matmul. A subsequent wait or commit operation is used to synchronize with the completion of the WGMMA. This asynchrony is what allows the barriers to enforce ordering without stalling the entire CTA, because a warpgroup can issue its WGMMA and then proceed to other work (such as softmax) while the Tensor Cores are still computing.

# Illustration of the Pingpong Execution Timeline

The pingpong execution timeline can be understood by tracing the operations of the two consumer warpgroups over successive iterations of the main loop. Consider two consumer warpgroups, $W_1$ and $W_2$. In the idealized timeline, the execution proceeds as follows.

In the first phase, $W_1$ issues its GEMM0 (computing $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ for the current iteration) and GEMM1 (computing $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{V}$ for the previous iteration) on the Tensor Cores. The \texttt{bar.sync} instruction ensures that $W_2$ does not issue its GEMMs until $W_1$ has issued its GEMMs. While $W_1$'s GEMMs are executing on the Tensor Cores, $W_2$ performs its softmax operations, which include computing the row-wise maximum, evaluating the exponential, computing the row-wise sum, and rescaling the output accumulator $\mathbf{O}$. These softmax operations use the multi-function unit and CUDA cores, which are independent of the Tensor Cores.

In the second phase, the roles reverse. $W_2$ issues its GEMM0 and GEMM1 on the Tensor Cores (after $W_1$ has issued its GEMMs, as enforced by the barrier). While $W_2$'s GEMMs are executing, $W_1$ performs its softmax operations. This alternation continues for each iteration of the main loop, creating a steady-state pipeline where the Tensor Cores and the multi-function unit are both utilized concurrently.

It is important to note that the actual execution in practice is not as clean as the idealized description. The compiler (NVCC) may rearrange instructions, and the actual overlap depends on the relative latencies of the GEMM and softmax operations. Nevertheless, the general pattern of alternation between the two warpgroups holds, and the concurrent use of Tensor Cores and multi-function units provides a meaningful performance improvement. # Performance Impact: Moving from 570 to 620-640 TFLOPS for FP16 Forward Pass

The empirical impact of pingpong scheduling is significant. Without the pingpong overlap, the baseline warp-specialized implementation of FlashAttention-3 (using only producer-consumer asynchrony without overlapping GEMM and softmax across consumer warpgroups) achieves approximately 570 TFLOPS for the FP16 forward pass with head dimension 128 and sequence length 8192. With the addition of pingpong scheduling, this performance improves to 620-640 TFLOPS, representing a 9-12% improvement in throughput.

The ablation study in the paper further confirms the contribution of these algorithmic improvements. The combination of warp-specialization and GEMM-softmax pipelining (including both the intra-warpgroup 2-stage pipelining and the inter-warpgroup pingpong scheduling) leads to a speedup from 570 to 661 TFLOPS for the specific configuration of batch size 4, sequence length 8448, 16 heads, and head dimension 128. This confirms that the asynchronous execution techniques, of which pingpong scheduling is a key component, provide measurable and significant performance gains.

The fact that the improvement is not larger can be attributed to several factors. First, the pingpong scheduling is not as clean in practice as in the idealized model, because the compiler may reorder instructions and the actual latencies may not perfectly align. Second, there are other overheads in the algorithm, such as register pressure and barrier synchronization costs, that partially offset the gains from overlapping. Third, the softmax operations include not only the exponential but also other non-GEMM operations (such as row reductions and rescaling) that may not all be perfectly hidden behind the GEMMs.

Despite these practical limitations, the pingpong scheduling technique demonstrates that careful exploitation of hardware asynchrony, specifically the independence of Tensor Cores and the multi-function unit, can yield meaningful performance improvements for attention computation. This technique, combined with the intra-warpgroup 2-stage pipelining that overlaps WGMMA with softmax within a single warpgroup, enables FlashAttention-3 to achieve up to 740 TFLOPS (75% utilization) on the H100 GPU for FP16, and close to 1.2 PFLOPS for FP8, representing a 1.5-2.0x speedup over FlashAttention-2.
