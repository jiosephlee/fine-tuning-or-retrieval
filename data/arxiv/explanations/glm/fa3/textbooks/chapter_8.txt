\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

Chapter 8: FP8 FlashAttention-3: Layout Conformance and In-Kernel Transpose

The transition from FP16 to FP8 precision in FlashAttention-3 introduces a host of challenges that go beyond simply changing the data type of the operands. While FP8 Tensor Cores on the Hopper architecture can deliver twice the throughput of their FP16 counterparts, exploiting this capability within the fused attention kernel requires navigating a labyrinth of layout constraints imposed by the WGMMA (warpgroup matrix multiply-accumulate) instruction. Unlike FP16 WGMMA, which is relatively permissive about the memory layout of its input operands, FP8 WGMMA enforces strict conformance rules that conflict with the natural data arrangements found in attention computation. This chapter dissects these constraints and the techniques developed in FlashAttention-3 to overcome them, focusing on the two central problems: the need to transpose the $\mathbf{V}$ matrix tiles and the need to reconcile the mismatched register layouts of FP32 accumulators and FP8 operands when chaining two back-to-back GEMMs in a single kernel.

## FP8 WGMMA k-Major Constraint: Why V Needs to Be Contiguous in Sequence Length

To understand the layout challenge, we must first recall the structure of attention computation. The forward pass of attention consists of two matrix multiplications performed in sequence: the first computes the score matrix $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$, and the second computes the output matrix $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P} \mathbf{V}$, where $\mathbf{P}$ is the softmax of $\mathbf{S}$. In the tiled algorithm of FlashAttention-3, these are performed block by block: for each block $\mathbf{Q}_i$, the kernel iterates over blocks $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$, computing $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$ and then accumulating $\mathbf{O}_i += \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$.

The WGMMA instruction on Hopper, which performs these GEMM operations asynchronously on the Tensor Cores, imposes specific layout requirements on its input operands depending on their precision. The paper defines the concept of "mn-major" and "k-major" layouts: given a GEMM call to multiply $A \times B^\top$ for an $M \times K$-matrix $A$ and an $N \times K$-matrix $B$, the $A$ or $B$ operand is said to be mn-major if it is contiguous in the outer $M$ or $N$ dimension, and k-major if it is contiguous in the inner $K$-dimension. For FP16 WGMMA, both mn-major and k-major input operands are accepted when the operands reside in shared memory (SMEM). This flexibility means that FP16 FlashAttention-3 does not need to worry about the contiguous dimension of its input tiles.

For FP8 WGMMA, however, only the k-major format is supported. This is a critical restriction. Let us examine its implications for each GEMM in attention. In the first GEMM, $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$, the inner reduction dimension is the head dimension $d$. The input tensors $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ are typically stored with the head dimension $d$ as the contiguous (innermost) dimension, which naturally satisfies the k-major requirement. The second GEMM, $\mathbf{O}_i += \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$, has $\mathbf{V}_j$ as the second operand. Here, the inner reduction dimension for $\mathbf{V}_j$ (playing the role of $B$ in the GEMM) is also the head dimension $d$. But $\mathbf{V}$, like $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$, is typically stored with the head dimension $d$ as the contiguous dimension. This means $\mathbf{V}$ is contiguous in the $d$ dimension, which corresponds to the $K$-dimension of the GEMM — but wait, we need to be more careful about the layout convention.

Let us think about this more precisely. In the second GEMM, $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$, the matrix $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$ has shape $B_r \times B_c$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$ has shape $B_c \times d$. The inner dimension is $B_c$ (the sequence length block size). For the WGMMA instruction, the $A$ operand is $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$ and the $B$ operand is $\mathbf{V}$. The k-major constraint requires that the operands be contiguous in the inner $K$-dimension. For the $B$ operand ($\mathbf{V}$), the inner dimension is $d$ (the head dimension), but the data is stored contiguous in $d$. However, the paper states that for the second GEMM, $\mathbf{V}$ (or rather the tiles of $\mathbf{V}$ loaded into SMEM) needs to be contiguous in the sequence length dimension. This is because in the GEMM $A \times B^\top$, $\mathbf{V}$ plays the role of $B$, and $B$ has shape $N \times K$, so the $K$-dimension for $\mathbf{V}$ is the sequence length dimension $N$ (or $B_c$ at the block level). But the natural storage of $\mathbf{V}$ has the head dimension $d$ as the contiguous dimension, not the sequence length. Therefore, to satisfy the k-major constraint for FP8 WGMMA in the second GEMM, $\mathbf{V}$ tiles must be transposed so that they become contiguous in the sequence length dimension rather than the head dimension.

This is the fundamental layout problem: the natural storage format of $\mathbf{V}$ does not conform to the k-major requirement of FP8 WGMMA for the second GEMM in attention. Unlike FP16, where the WGMMA instruction accepts both mn-major and k-major layouts, FP8 leaves no room for flexibility. The algorithm must somehow produce $\mathbf{V}$ tiles in the correct layout before they can be fed into the second FP8 WGMMA.

## Option 1: Pre-Processing Transpose in GMEM and Its Limitations

One natural approach to solving the layout conformance problem is to transpose the entire $\mathbf{V}$ matrix in global memory (GMEM/HBM) before the attention kernel executes. If $\mathbf{V}$ is stored such that the sequence length dimension $N$ is contiguous and the head dimension $d$ is the stride dimension, then tiles of $\mathbf{V}$ loaded by TMA into SMEM would automatically be contiguous in the sequence length dimension, satisfying the k-major constraint without any in-kernel manipulation. The paper describes two sub-options for implementing this pre-processing transpose.

The first sub-option, denoted (1a), is to fuse the transpose operation into the epilogue of a preceding step in the model, such as the rotary embedding computation. Rotary embeddings are applied to $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ before attention, and their output writes $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ to GMEM. In principle, one could also transpose $\mathbf{V}$ during this write-back, producing a transposed $\mathbf{V}$ tensor in GMEM as a side effect of the rotary embedding kernel. This would incur essentially no additional cost since the rotary embedding is memory-bandwidth bound — the GPU spends most of its time moving data between GMEM and SMEM, not computing — and adding a transpose to the store operation would not increase the number of memory transactions. However, the paper notes that this approach is difficult to integrate into a standard library. The rotary embedding kernel and the attention kernel are typically separate operations in a deep learning framework, and fusing a transpose into the epilogue of rotary embedding creates a tight coupling between two otherwise independent operations. This coupling complicates the software stack, as the rotary embedding kernel would need to know that its output will be consumed by an FP8 attention kernel and would need to produce $\mathbf{V}$ in a transposed layout. Such cross-kernel dependencies are fragile and hard to maintain in a general-purpose library like PyTorch or Hugging Face.

The second sub-option, denoted (1b), is to call a standalone pre-processing transpose kernel that reads $\mathbf{V}$ from GMEM, transposes it, and writes it back to GMEM in the transposed layout. The paper references the fact that an optimized transpose kernel can achieve speeds near the bandwidth of the device, meaning the kernel itself is efficient. However, the problem with this approach is that it introduces an additional pass over the $\mathbf{V}$ data. In certain situations, such as LLM inference, the computation is already memory-bandwidth bound: the GPU spends most of its time reading data from GMEM, and the attention computation itself is relatively light compared to the memory traffic. Adding a separate transpose kernel means reading $\mathbf{V}$ from GMEM and writing it back to GMEM once more, effectively doubling the memory traffic for $\mathbf{V}$. In a memory-bound regime, this additional memory traffic translates directly into wasted time, making the standalone transpose approach too costly. The paper explicitly states that option (1b) is "too wasteful in a memory-bound situation such as inference."

Both sub-options of the pre-processing transpose approach thus suffer from practical limitations: (1a) is hard to integrate into standard libraries due to cross-kernel coupling, and (1b) is too wasteful in memory-bound scenarios. These limitations motivate the alternative approach of performing the transpose inside the attention kernel itself.

## Option 2: In-Kernel Transpose Using LDSM/STSM Instructions

Given the limitations of pre-processing the transpose in GMEM, FlashAttention-3 opts for an in-kernel transpose: the $\mathbf{V}$ tiles are loaded into SMEM in their natural (head-dimension-contiguous) layout and then transposed within the kernel before being used in the second FP8 WGMMA. The key enabler for this in-kernel transpose is a pair of specialized hardware instructions: LDSM (\texttt{ldmatrix}) and STSM (\texttt{stmatrix}).

The LDSM (load matrix) and STSM (store matrix) instructions are designed for efficiently moving data between SMEM and the register file (RMEM) at a fine granularity. Specifically, these instructions involve a warp of threads collectively loading data from SMEM to registers (LDSM) or storing data from registers to SMEM (STSM) at a granularity of 128 bytes. What makes these instructions particularly valuable for the transpose problem is that they are capable of performing layout transformations — including transposes — as part of the memory copy operation. That is, when data is loaded from SMEM to RMEM via LDSM and then stored back to SMEM via STSM with a transpose modifier, the tiles can be effectively transposed without any explicit arithmetic operations. This is a form of data manipulation that is performed by the memory access hardware itself, leveraging the collective action of all 32 threads in a warp.

Moreover, LDSM and STSM are register-efficient instructions. They use a small number of registers per thread to accomplish the collective data movement, which is important because the attention kernel already has high register pressure due to the need to store tiles of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{S}$, $\mathbf{P}$, $\mathbf{O}$, and other intermediate quantities. Because of this register efficiency, the in-kernel transpose can be executed in the producer warpgroup — the set of warps responsible for issuing TMA loads from GMEM to SMEM — rather than in the consumer warpgroup that performs the GEMM and softmax computations. This is a significant advantage, as it allows the transpose to be overlapped with the computation in the consumer warpgroup, effectively hiding the cost of the transpose.

## LDSM/STSM for FP8: Packing 8-Bit Entries and Handling Transpose Limitations

The LDSM and STSM instructions were originally designed for 16-bit data types. In the PTX (parallel thread execution) documentation, these instructions are described as copying $8 \times 8$ matrices with 16-bit entries. To use them for FP8 data, which has 8-bit entries, the paper describes a packing strategy: two 8-bit FP8 values are packed into a single 16-bit slot, allowing the existing LDSM/STSM hardware to handle FP8 data at the same throughput as it handles 16-bit data. This effectively doubles the amount of FP8 data that can be moved per instruction compared to FP16, which is consistent with the goal of FP8 computation.

However, this packing strategy introduces a complication when it comes to transposition. The transpose versions of LDSM and STSM operate on 16-bit entries as atomic units: they can rearrange the positions of 16-bit values but cannot split apart the two 8-bit values that have been packed into a single 16-bit slot. In other words, if two FP8 values $a$ and $b$ are packed together as a single 16-bit unit $\{a, b\}$, the transpose version of LDSM/STSM can move this packed unit to a different position in the matrix, but it cannot separate $a$ from $b$ and place them in different rows or columns of the transposed output. This means that a simple LDSM-then-STSM-with-transpose sequence is insufficient to perform a proper tile-wise transpose of FP8 data.

To work around this limitation, the paper describes the need for additional register-level data manipulations between the LDSM and STSM operations. After loading the packed FP8 data from SMEM into registers via LDSM, the kernel must perform certain register movements — shuffling or permuting the 8-bit values within the packed 16-bit units — before storing them back to SMEM via STSM with the transpose modifier. The paper does not provide the full details of these register movements, but the key insight is that the in-kernel transpose of FP8 tiles requires a combination of LDSM (to load data from SMEM to registers), intermediate register manipulations (to unpack and repack the 8-bit values in the correct order for transposition), and STSM (to store the transposed data back to SMEM). Despite this added complexity, the overall approach remains more efficient than the alternatives because it avoids extra GMEM traffic and leverages the high-throughput, register-efficient LDSM/STSM instructions.

## Executing In-Kernel Transpose in the Producer Warpgroup Under the Shadow of WGMMAs

A critical optimization in the FP8 FlashAttention-3 algorithm is the scheduling of the in-kernel transpose relative to the GEMM computations. The warp-specialized design of FlashAttention-3 divides the warps in a CTA (cooperative thread array) into producer warpgroups and consumer warpgroups. The producer warpgroup is responsible for issuing TMA loads to bring tiles of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ from GMEM to SMEM, while the consumer warpgroup is responsible for issuing WGMMA instructions to perform the GEMM computations and executing the softmax operations. Since the LDSM/STSM instructions are register-efficient and do not require the large register allocations needed for WGMMA accumulators, the in-kernel transpose of $\mathbf{V}$ tiles can be assigned to the producer warpgroup.

The key insight is that the transpose of the next $\mathbf{V}$ tile can be overlapped with — or "hidden in the shadow of" — the two WGMMA operations that the consumer warpgroup is performing with the preceding $\mathbf{V}$ tile and the current $\mathbf{K}$ tile. Specifically, in the main loop of the algorithm, the consumer warpgroup performs two GEMMs per iteration: the first GEMM computes $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$, and the second GEMM computes $\mathbf{O}_i += \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$. These WGMMAs are asynchronous: once issued, the Tensor Cores work on them while the CUDA cores are free to perform other work. The producer warpgroup, having loaded $\mathbf{V}_{j+1}$ into SMEM via TMA, can use the LDSM/STSM sequence to transpose $\mathbf{V}_{j+1}$ while the consumer warpgroup's WGMMAs for iteration $j$ are still in flight. After the first iteration of the loop, the transpose of $\mathbf{V}_{j+1}$ is arranged to execute in the "shadow" of the two WGMMAs that involve $\mathbf{V}_j$ and $\mathbf{K}_j$.

This scheduling is made possible by the asynchronous nature of both TMA and WGMMA. The TMA loads and the LDSM/STSM transpose in the producer warpgroup do not block the WGMMA instructions in the consumer warpgroup, and vice versa. The only synchronization points are the barriers that ensure a tile has been loaded (and, for $\mathbf{V}$, transposed) before the consumer warpgroup issues a WGMMA that reads it, and the barriers that ensure the consumer warpgroup has finished reading a tile from SMEM before the producer warpgroup overwrites that buffer stage with new data. By carefully managing these synchronization points and using a multi-stage circular SMEM buffer, the algorithm ensures that the transpose work is effectively hidden behind the GEMM computation, contributing little to no additional latency.

## FP32 Accumulator vs. FP8 Operand Layout Mismatch in Registers

The layout conformance challenges of FP8 WGMMA extend beyond the SMEM-level transpose of $\mathbf{V}$. A second, more subtle problem arises when one attempts to chain two FP8 WGMMAs back-to-back within a single kernel, as is required in attention where the output of the first GEMM ($\mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$) serves as the input to the second GEMM ($\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$).

In the first GEMM, the FP8 WGMMA computes $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$ using FP8 operands and accumulates the result in FP32. The FP32 accumulator is held in the register file (RMEM) across the threads of the warpgroup. After the softmax operation (which is performed in FP32 or FP16 on the CUDA cores), the result $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$ must serve as the $A$ operand of the second FP8 WGMMA, $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$. However, the second FP8 WGMMA expects its $A$ operand to be in FP8 format and laid out in registers according to a specific FP8 operand layout.

The paper reveals a critical mismatch: the memory layout of the FP32 accumulator from the first WGMMA is different from the layout assumed for the FP8 operand $A$ of the second WGMMA. That is, the entries of the result matrix $\mathbf{S}$ (and hence $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$) are distributed across the threads of the warpgroup in a certain pattern determined by the FP32 accumulator layout, but the second FP8 WGMMA expects its $A$ operand entries to be distributed across threads according to a different pattern. If one were to simply take the FP32 accumulator, apply the softmax, quantize to FP8, and feed it directly into the second WGMMA, the data would be in the wrong register layout, and the WGMMA would compute an incorrect result.

The paper illustrates these two layouts by depicting fragments of the FP32 accumulator layout and the FP8 operand layout. In the FP32 accumulator layout, the entries are held in registers per thread in a certain order — let us denote them as $\{d_0, d_1, d_2, d_3, d_4, d_5, d_6, d_7, \ldots\}$. In the FP8 operand layout, the entries are arranged in a different order across the registers of each thread. This difference means that a transformation step is needed between the two WGMMAs to rearrange the data from the accumulator layout to the operand layout.

## Byte Permute Instructions for Transforming Accumulator Layout for Dependent GEMMs

To bridge the layout gap between the FP32 accumulator of the first WGMMA and the FP8 operand of the second WGMMA, FlashAttention-3 uses byte permute instructions. Byte permute instructions are low-level hardware instructions that can rearrange individual bytes within registers, allowing the kernel to reorganize the data layout at a very fine granularity without needing to go through SMEM or perform expensive cross-thread communication.

The paper describes a specific permutation that transforms the accumulator layout into the operand layout. Referring to the FP32 accumulator fragment with entries ordered as $\{d_0, d_1, d_2, d_3, d_4, d_5, d_6, d_7\}$ per thread, the byte permute instructions rearrange this into the order $\{d_0, d_1, d_4, d_5, d_2, d_3, d_6, d_7\}$. That is, the third and fourth pairs of entries are swapped: positions 2 and 3 (originally $d_2$ and $d_3$) are exchanged with positions 4 and 5 (originally $d_4$ and $d_5$), yielding the new order. This register permutation is then replicated over every 8 bytes of the register data, ensuring that the entire tile is transformed consistently.

In terms of the logical shape of the $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$ tile (which is the quantized version of the softmax output that serves as the $A$ operand of the second GEMM), this byte permutation effectively permutes the columns of the tile. For example, the paper notes that columns $\{0, 1, 8, 9\}$ (in some indexing scheme) now become the first four columns of the rearranged tile. This is a purely layout-level transformation: the mathematical content of the matrix is unchanged, but the way its entries are distributed across the registers of the threads in the warpgroup is reorganized to match what the second FP8 WGMMA expects.

The use of byte permute instructions is significant because it provides a way to perform this layout transformation entirely within the register file, without needing to write data to SMEM and read it back in a different layout. This keeps the transformation fast and avoids consuming SMEM bandwidth, which is a shared resource that is already under pressure from the TMA loads and the pingpong scheduling of the algorithm.

## Column/Row Permutation Coordination Between P Tile and V Tile

The byte permutation of the $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$ tile introduces a complication: since the columns of $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$ are permuted, the rows of $\mathbf{V}$ (which is the other operand in the second GEMM) must be correspondingly permuted for the matrix multiplication to produce the correct result. In a standard GEMM $A \times B$, if the columns of $A$ are permuted, the rows of $B$ must be permuted in the same way so that the inner products are computed between the correct pairs of elements. Specifically, if column $j$ of $A$ is moved to position $\pi(j)$, then row $j$ of $B$ must also be moved to position $\pi(j)$, so that the product $A \times B$ remains unchanged.

In the context of FlashAttention-3, the $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$ tile's columns are permuted by the byte permute instructions, and the $\mathbf{V}$ tile's rows must undergo a matching permutation. The paper describes how this coordination is achieved through the in-kernel transpose. Recall that the $\mathbf{V}$ tile is transposed in the producer warpgroup using LDSM and STSM instructions before being stored in SMEM for consumption by the second WGMMA. The key insight is that the in-kernel transpose can be arranged to write out a matching row permutation of the $\mathbf{V}$ tile. That is, during the process of loading $\mathbf{V}$ from SMEM to registers (LDSM), manipulating the packed FP8 entries, and storing the transposed result back to SMEM (STSM), the kernel can simultaneously perform a row permutation that mirrors the column permutation applied to $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$ by the byte permute instructions.

This additional degree of freedom — the ability to control the row permutation of $\mathbf{V}$ during the in-kernel transpose — is what makes the layout transformation approach viable. The paper notes that this freedom afforded by doing the in-kernel transpose eliminates the need for shuffle instructions to change register ownership across threads. In a previous approach described by the authors in other work, they had considered using shuffle instructions (which move data between registers of different threads in a warp) to reconcile the accumulator and operand layouts. However, shuffle instructions are more expensive and complex than byte permute instructions, and they require coordination across threads that can disrupt the instruction schedule. By instead performing the row permutation of $\mathbf{V}$ during the in-kernel transpose, FlashAttention-3 avoids the need for cross-thread shuffles entirely, keeping the layout transformation local to each thread's register file (via byte permutes for $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$) and to the SMEM-level transpose (via LDSM/STSM for $\mathbf{V}$).

The coordination between the two permutations — the column permutation of $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$ via byte permutes and the row permutation of $\mathbf{V}$ via the in-kernel transpose — is a elegant example of how the FlashAttention-3 algorithm exploits the flexibility of multiple hardware primitives to satisfy the strict layout requirements of FP8 WGMMA. The result is a system where the layout transformations needed for dependent FP8 GEMMs are performed efficiently and in parallel with the main computation, without introducing additional memory traffic or expensive cross-thread communication. This is what enables FP8 FlashAttention-3 to achieve close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s on the H100 GPU, nearly doubling the throughput of the FP16 version while maintaining acceptable numerical accuracy through block quantization and incoherent processing.
