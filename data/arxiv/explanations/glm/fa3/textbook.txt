# Introduction to Attention and the Need for Speed

## The Transformer Architecture and the Role of Attention

The Transformer architecture, introduced by Vaswani et al. in 2017, has become the dominant neural network design for a vast range of tasks in natural language processing, computer vision, audio processing, and beyond. At the heart of the Transformer is the attention mechanism, which allows the model to selectively focus on different parts of the input sequence when producing each element of the output. Unlike earlier recurrent architectures that process tokens one at a time in sequence, the attention mechanism enables every position in the input to directly attend to every other position, capturing long-range dependencies in a single computational step. This architectural innovation is what gives Transformers their remarkable flexibility and power, but it also introduces a significant computational cost that scales with the size of the input.

In a standard Transformer, attention is instantiated as multi-head attention (MHA). Each attention head operates on its own set of query, key, and value projections derived from the input. For a single head, let $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ be the query, key, and value matrices, where $N$ is the sequence length and $d$ is the head dimension. The attention output $\mathbf{O}$ is computed as $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$, followed by $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$, and finally $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$, where $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{d}$ is a scaling factor applied to prevent the dot-product scores from growing too large in magnitude. The softmax operation is applied row-wise, and in practice one subtracts the row maximum of $\mathbf{S}$ before exponentiating to maintain numerical stability. In multi-head attention, this entire computation is replicated across multiple heads and across the batch dimension, and the results are concatenated and projected to produce the final output. Because each head independently performs this query-key-value computation, the attention mechanism is embarrassingly parallel across heads and batches, a property that proves critical for efficient GPU implementation.

The attention layer is not merely one component among many in the Transformer; it constitutes the primary computational bottleneck. The reason is that the two matrix multiplications involving $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ and $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$ together dominate the floating-point operation count of the forward pass, and the backward pass requires even more matrix multiplications to compute gradients. Given the current trend toward ever-larger models and ever-longer input sequences, attention has become the single most expensive operation in the forward and backward passes of Transformer-based large language models (LLMs). As a result, optimizing attention computation is one of the most impactful ways to improve the efficiency of the entire model.

## Quadratic Scaling of Self-Attention with Sequence Length

The fundamental computational challenge of attention stems from the matrix $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$, which has shape $N \times N$. Because every query must be compared against every key, the number of entries in the attention score matrix grows quadratically with the sequence length $N$. Computing $\mathbf{S}$ requires $O(N^2 d)$ floating-point operations, computing the softmax over $\mathbf{S}$ requires $O(N^2)$ operations, and computing $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$ requires another $O(N^2 d)$ operations. The total operation count for the forward pass is therefore $O(N^2 d)$, and the backward pass, which involves additional matrix multiplications to compute gradients with respect to $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$, roughly multiplies this by a factor of 2.5. In terms of FLOPs, the forward pass requires $4 \cdot N^2 \cdot d \cdot H$ operations, where $H$ is the number of heads, and the backward pass requires approximately 2.5 times that amount due to the need for five matrix multiplications (compared to two in the forward pass) when using recomputation to avoid storing intermediate results.

This quadratic scaling means that doubling the sequence length quadruples the computation and memory required for attention. For a sequence of length 1,024, the attention matrix $\mathbf{S}$ has about one million entries; for a sequence of length 16,384, it has over 268 million entries. In a naive (or "standard") implementation of attention, these intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ are materialized to the GPU's high-bandwidth memory (HBM), meaning they are written to and read from global memory during the computation. This creates enormous memory traffic: the $N \times N$ matrices must be written once and read at least once, resulting in $O(N^2)$ memory accesses that can easily dominate the overall runtime, especially since HBM bandwidth, while large, is finite and becomes a bottleneck relative to the raw compute capacity of modern GPUs. The quadratic scaling in both computation and memory thus poses a fundamental barrier to extending Transformers to long sequences.

## Applications Driving the Need for Long-Context Attention

The pressure to scale attention to longer sequences comes from a broad range of emerging applications and capabilities. In natural language processing, there is growing interest in models that can reason over multiple long documents simultaneously, such as in the LongT5 and SCROLLS benchmarks, which require the model to process tens of thousands of tokens at once. Similarly, techniques like YaRN extend the effective context window of pretrained models, enabling them to handle inputs far longer than those seen during training. These long-context capabilities are essential for tasks such as document summarization, multi-document question answering, and retrieval-augmented generation, where the model must synthesize information spread across a large corpus.

In the domain of code generation and software engineering, models are increasingly expected to understand and reason over entire codebases. Systems such as Code Llama and StarCoder process large code files and, in some cases, multiple files together to capture cross-file dependencies and project-level structure. As codebases can easily span hundreds of thousands of lines, the ability to attend over long contexts directly translates into more capable code assistants. Without efficient long-context attention, these models would be limited to processing individual functions or small snippets, severely constraining their usefulness.

Beyond text and code, long-context attention is critical for processing high-resolution images, where an image may be decomposed into thousands of patch tokens; audio, where long waveforms or spectrogram sequences must be modeled; and video, where temporal sequences of frames can produce extremely long token sequences. The Conformer architecture, for example, applies attention to audio sequences, and video diffusion models require attention over extended temporal spans. Each of these modalities benefits from the ability to attend over long sequences, and each is bottlenecked by the same quadratic scaling problem.

Finally, in interactive applications such as recommendation systems with long user histories and agent workflows that operate over long planning horizons, the ability to maintain and attend over extended context is essential. A recommendation model that can attend over a user's entire interaction history can capture richer preferences, and an agent that can reason over a long sequence of prior actions and observations can make more informed decisions. In all of these cases, the practical utility of the model is directly tied to how efficiently attention can be computed over long sequences, making attention optimization a first-order concern for advancing the capabilities of Transformer-based systems.

## Approaches to Efficient Attention: Approximation, Software Optimization, and Alternative Architectures

The research community has pursued several complementary directions to address the quadratic scaling of attention. One major line of work seeks to approximate the attention computation, reducing its complexity from $O(N^2)$ to something sub-quadratic or even linear. Linear attention, for example, replaces the softmax kernel with a feature map that allows the key-value product to be computed first, avoiding the explicit construction of the $N \times N$ attention matrix. Other approximation methods, such as Performer (which uses random feature approximations of the softmax kernel), similarly aim to reduce the computational cost while accepting some loss in accuracy. These approaches can offer significant speedups for very long sequences, but they typically introduce trade-offs in terms of expressiveness and may not match the quality of exact attention, particularly for tasks that rely on precise attention patterns.

A second direction focuses on software optimization of exact attention. Rather than changing the mathematical computation, these approaches restructure how attention is implemented on the GPU to minimize wasted work and memory traffic. The work of Rabe and Staats on self-attention chunking and, most notably, the FlashAttention lineage introduced by Dao et al. fall into this category. The key insight of FlashAttention is that attention can be computed exactly—producing the same result as standard attention—while avoiding the materialization of the intermediate $N \times N$ matrices to global memory. This is achieved through a tiling strategy that keeps intermediate results in faster on-chip memory (shared memory and registers) and fuses all attention operations into a single GPU kernel. By doing so, FlashAttention dramatically reduces memory reads and writes, yielding substantial speedups without any approximation. This line of work is the focus of the present chapter and the broader paper on which it is based.

A third direction explores alternative architectures that replace attention altogether. Models such as RWKV, RetNet, and Mamba propose recurrent or state-space formulations that achieve linear scaling in sequence length while retaining some of the benefits of attention, such as the ability to capture long-range dependencies. These architectures are particularly attractive for inference, where they can process tokens in constant time per step. However, they represent a more radical departure from the Transformer paradigm and face open questions about whether they can match the performance of attention-based models across the full range of tasks. In practice, attention remains the dominant mechanism in state-of-the-art models, and optimizing its implementation continues to be the most immediately impactful approach for improving efficiency.

## The FlashAttention Lineage: From FlashAttention-1 to FlashAttention-3

The FlashAttention lineage represents the most successful line of software optimization for exact attention, and understanding its evolution is essential for appreciating the contributions of FlashAttention-3. The original FlashAttention, introduced by Dao et al., was motivated by the observation that the bottleneck in standard attention is not the floating-point computation itself but rather the memory traffic incurred by writing and reading the large intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ to and from HBM. FlashAttention proposed a novel tiling strategy that partitions the query, key, and value matrices into blocks and processes them in a tiled fashion, keeping intermediate results in on-chip shared memory and registers. Crucially, it leveraged a local version of the softmax computation—maintaining running statistics (the row maximum and row sum) across blocks—that allows the correct softmax result to be computed without ever materializing the full $N \times N$ matrix. By fusing all attention operations (the two matrix multiplications and the softmax) into a single GPU kernel, FlashAttention eliminated the expensive intermediate reads and writes to global memory, achieving a significant speedup over standard attention while producing mathematically identical results.

FlashAttention-2, also introduced by Dao, restructured the original algorithm to further improve GPU utilization. The key changes included parallelizing the computation over the sequence length dimension (in addition to the batch and head dimensions) and restructuring the inner loop of the forward pass to iterate over blocks of the key and value matrices. This improved the occupancy and distribution of work across the GPU's streaming multiprocessors (SMs), leading to better hardware utilization. FlashAttention-2 became the de facto standard for efficient attention and was widely adopted in practice. However, as GPU architectures evolved, particularly with the introduction of NVIDIA's Hopper (H100) architecture, it became clear that FlashAttention-2 was not fully exploiting the new hardware's capabilities.

FlashAttention-3, the subject of the paper underlying this chapter, builds on FlashAttention-2 by explicitly incorporating three new hardware-aware techniques: producer-consumer asynchrony through warp-specialization, overlapping of softmax with asynchronous block-wise matrix multiplications (GEMMs), and hardware-accelerated low-precision computation using FP8. These techniques are designed specifically to take advantage of features present in the Hopper architecture, such as the asynchronous Tensor Cores (exposed via the WGMMA instruction), the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) for asynchronous memory copies, and support for FP8 low-precision arithmetic. The result is a dramatic improvement in both throughput and accuracy: FlashAttention-3 achieves 1.5--2.0$\times$ speedup over FlashAttention-2 in the FP16 forward pass, reaching up to 740 TFLOPs/s (75\% of the H100's theoretical peak), and close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s in FP8, while also reducing numerical error in FP8 by 2.6$\times$ compared to standard per-tensor quantization.

## Limitations of FlashAttention-2 on Modern GPUs: The 35% Utilization Problem

Despite its success, FlashAttention-2 achieves only about 35\% utilization on the NVIDIA H100 GPU, compared to the 80--90\% utilization achieved by optimized matrix multiplication (GEMM) kernels on the same hardware. This gap is striking and motivates the development of FlashAttention-3. Understanding why FlashAttention-2 underutilizes the H100 requires examining both implementation-level and algorithmic-level factors.

At the implementation level, FlashAttention-2 was originally designed for the Ampere architecture and does not make use of Hopper-specific instructions. For example, it does not use the WGMMA (warpgroup matrix multiply-accumulate) instruction, which is Hopper's asynchronous interface to the Tensor Cores, nor does it use the TMA (Tensor Memory Accelerator) for asynchronous memory copies. Instead, it relies on Ampere-era synchronous instructions, which do not allow the same degree of overlap between computation and data movement. Other works, such as ThunderKitten and cuDNN 9, have demonstrated that using Hopper-specific instructions and tile-based abstractions can improve attention performance, suggesting that implementation-level changes alone can yield meaningful gains.

However, the more fundamental limitation of FlashAttention-2 is algorithmic. Its algorithm adheres to a simplified synchronous execution model and makes no explicit use of two critical hardware trends: asynchrony and low precision. Asynchrony arises from hardware specialization: modern GPUs include dedicated hardware units for specific operations, such as Tensor Cores for matrix multiplication and TMA for memory loading, which operate independently of the general-purpose CUDA cores that handle logic, integer, and floating-point computation. This separation creates the potential for overlapping different types of work—for example, performing a matrix multiplication on the Tensor Cores while simultaneously loading data from global memory or executing non-matmul operations like the exponential function on the CUDA cores. FlashAttention-2's synchronous design does not exploit this potential.

The performance gap is particularly severe for non-matmul operations. On the H100, FP16 matrix multiplication achieves 989 TFLOPs/s, but special functions such as the exponential (needed for softmax) achieve only about 3.9 TFLOPs/s—a disparity of over 250$\times$. For a typical attention forward pass with head dimension 128 in FP16, there are 512 times more matmul FLOPs than exponential operations, but since the exponential throughput is 256 times lower, the exponential can consume up to 50\% of the total cycles relative to the matmul. With FP8, the matmul throughput doubles while the exponential throughput remains unchanged, making the imbalance even worse. Because FlashAttention-2 executes matmul and softmax sequentially within each iteration of its inner loop, the non-matmul operations create a serial bottleneck that limits overall throughput.

Low precision is the second missed opportunity. The H100 supports FP8 computation on its Tensor Cores, which can deliver twice the throughput of FP16 or BF16 for the same power and chip area. However, FlashAttention-2 does not target FP8, leaving a significant performance improvement unrealized. Adopting FP8 is nontrivial: it requires addressing layout conformance constraints imposed by the FP8 WGMMA instruction (which mandates that operands be contiguous in the inner, or "k-major," dimension) and managing quantization error, especially in the presence of outlier features common in large language models.

These limitations—implementation-level mismatch with Hopper instructions, algorithmic inability to overlap matmul with non-matmul operations, and lack of FP8 support—collectively explain the 35\% utilization of FlashAttention-2 on the H100. FlashAttention-3 addresses each of these through its three core techniques: warp-specialization with pingpong scheduling to overlap data movement and computation, two-stage pipelining to overlap softmax with asynchronous GEMMs, and block quantization with incoherent processing to enable accurate FP8 computation. The remainder of this text elaborates on each of these techniques in detail.

# Background: Multi-Head Attention Mathematics

The attention mechanism is the computational backbone of the Transformer architecture, and understanding its mathematical structure is essential before one can appreciate the algorithmic and hardware-level optimizations that follow. In this chapter, we lay out the precise mathematical formulation of multi-head attention, covering both the forward pass and the backward pass. We establish the notation, derive the key expressions, and discuss how the computation is parallelized across heads and batches. We also address the critical issue of numerical stability in the softmax computation and explain how floating-point operation counts are derived for both the forward and backward passes. All of this material serves as the foundation for the optimized algorithms discussed in subsequent chapters.

# Forward Pass: Computing $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$, and $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$

Let $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ be the query, key, and value input sequences associated with a single attention head. These matrices each live in $\mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$, where $N$ is the sequence length and $d$ is the head dimension. The forward pass of attention consists of three steps that transform these inputs into the output matrix $\mathbf{O} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$.

The first step computes the pre-attention scores $\mathbf{S}$ by taking the inner product between every query vector and every key vector. Formally, we compute:

$$\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$$

where $\alpha$ is a scaling factor. Each entry $\mathbf{S}_{ij}$ measures how much the $i$-th query attends to the $j$-th key, scaled by $\alpha$. The result is an $N \times N$ matrix, which is quadratic in the sequence length—this is the source of the computational bottleneck that motivates the FlashAttention line of work.

The second step applies the softmax function row-wise to $\mathbf{S}$, producing the attention weight matrix $\mathbf{P}$:

$$\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S}) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$$

The softmax function, applied to each row independently, normalizes the scores so that each row of $\mathbf{P}$ sums to one. This converts the raw scores into a probability distribution over the sequence positions for each query. Each entry $\mathbf{P}_{ij}$ then represents the proportion of attention that query $i$ places on value $j$.

The third and final step computes a weighted combination of the value vectors using the attention weights:

$$\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P} \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$$

Each row of the output $\mathbf{O}_i$ is thus a convex combination of the rows of $\mathbf{V}$, where the mixing coefficients come from the corresponding row of $\mathbf{P}$. The output $\mathbf{O}$ has the same shape as the inputs, $\mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$, and represents the attention result for this head.

The entire forward pass therefore involves two matrix multiplications (the $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ product and the $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$ product) sandwiching a row-wise softmax. Understanding the data dependencies between these three steps is crucial for the optimized algorithms that follow, as the sequential relationship between the GEMMs and the softmax is the primary obstacle to full hardware utilization.

# Scaling Factor and Numerical Stability via Rowmax Subtraction

The scaling factor $\alpha$ is typically set to $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{d}$, where $d$ is the head dimension. This choice is motivated by the observation that the dot products $\mathbf{Q}_i \cdot \mathbf{K}_j$ grow in magnitude with the dimension $d$, since each dot product is a sum of $d$ terms. Without scaling, for large $d$, the entries of $\mathbf{S}$ would grow large in magnitude, pushing the softmax into regions where its gradients become vanishingly small. Dividing by $\sqrt{d}$ keeps the variance of the scores approximately constant regardless of head dimension, promoting stable training.

Beyond the scaling factor, there is a further numerical subtlety in the softmax computation. The softmax function involves computing exponentials of the entries of $\mathbf{S}$. When the entries of $\mathbf{S}$ are large, the exponential function $\exp(\mathbf{S}_{ij})$ can overflow, producing infinities and destroying the computation. To prevent this, in practice one subtracts $\mathrm{rowmax}(\mathbf{S})$ from $\mathbf{S}$ before applying the exponential. That is, for each row $i$, one computes:

$$\mathbf{P}_{ij} = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{S}_{ij} - \max_j \mathbf{S}_{ij})}{\sum_{j'} \exp(\mathbf{S}_{ij'} - \max_j \mathbf{S}_{ij})}$$

This shift does not change the result of the softmax, since the maximum is subtracted from both the numerator and denominator and cancels out. However, it ensures that the largest argument to the exponential in each row is zero, so the largest exponential value is exactly one, eliminating any risk of overflow. This rowmax subtraction is not merely a practical detail—it is fundamental to the algorithmic structure of FlashAttention, because it is what enables the local (block-wise) computation of softmax without needing to materialize the entire $N \times N$ score matrix. By tracking a running maximum across blocks of the key sequence and rescaling partial sums accordingly, one can compute the exact softmax output incrementally, which is the core insight of the FlashAttention tiling strategy.

# Multi-Head Attention: Parallelism Across Heads and Batches

In the full Transformer model, the attention mechanism is not applied once but multiple times in parallel across different "heads." For multi-head attention (MHA), each head has its own set of query, key, and value projections. That is, the model learns separate projection matrices for each head, producing distinct $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ matrices per head. Each head can thus attend to different aspects of the input and capture different patterns of relationships between sequence positions.

Crucially, the attention computation for each head is independent of every other head. This means the forward pass parallelizes across the number of heads: all heads can be computed simultaneously, either on different hardware units or interleaved on the same GPU through parallel threadblocks. In addition to parallelism across heads, the computation also parallelizes across the batch dimension. When processing a batch of sequences, each sequence in the batch has its own set of queries, keys, and values, and the attention computation for each batch element is independent of the others.

This two-level parallelism—across heads and across batches—is exploited heavily in GPU implementations. The total work is distributed across the GPU's streaming multiprocessors by assigning different (batch, head) pairs to different threadblocks. This is why the FlashAttention algorithms are described from the perspective of a single CTA operating on a single tile of the query matrix: the embarrassingly parallel nature of the computation across the batch size, number of heads, and query sequence length means that one only needs to specify the per-CTA algorithm, and the GPU scheduler handles the rest. The same parallelism applies to grouped query attention (GQA) and multi-query attention (MQA) variants, where the tensor indexing is adjusted to avoid duplicating $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ in high-bandwidth memory.

# Backward Pass: Chain Rule Gradients $\mathbf{dV}$, $\mathbf{dP}$, $\mathbf{dS}$, $\mathbf{dQ}$, and $\mathbf{dK}$

During training, one must compute the gradients of a scalar loss $\phi$ with respect to the inputs $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ in order to update the model parameters. Let $\mathbf{d}(-) = \partial \phi / \partial (-)$ denote the gradient of $\phi$ with respect to a given tensor. Given the output gradient $\mathbf{dO} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$, the backward pass computes $\mathbf{dQ}$, $\mathbf{dK}$, and $\mathbf{dV}$ by applying the chain rule through the three steps of the forward pass in reverse order.

Starting from the output $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$, the gradient with respect to $\mathbf{V}$ is obtained by differentiating through the matrix multiplication $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$. Since $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$, the gradient flows through $\mathbf{P}$ (which is held fixed for this particular gradient) to $\mathbf{V}$:

$$\mathbf{dV} = \mathbf{P}^\top \mathbf{dO} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$$

Simultaneously, the gradient with respect to $\mathbf{P}$ is:

$$\mathbf{dP} = \mathbf{dO} \mathbf{V}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$$

Next, one must propagate the gradient through the softmax operation to obtain the gradient with respect to the pre-attention scores $\mathbf{S}$. This step, denoted as $\mathbf{dS} = \mathrm{dsoftmax}(\mathbf{dP})$, is the most subtle part of the backward pass and is described in detail in the next section.

Once $\mathbf{dS}$ is obtained, the gradients with respect to $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ follow from differentiating the score computation $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$. The gradient with respect to $\mathbf{Q}$ is:

$$\mathbf{dQ} = \alpha \mathbf{dS} \mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$$

and the gradient with respect to $\mathbf{K}$ is:

$$\mathbf{dK} = \alpha \mathbf{dS}^\top \mathbf{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$$

Note that the scaling factor $\alpha$ appears in both gradients because $\mathbf{S}$ depends linearly on $\alpha$. Also observe the symmetry: $\mathbf{dQ}$ involves $\mathbf{dS}$ multiplied by $\mathbf{K}$, while $\mathbf{dK}$ involves $\mathbf{dS}^\top$ multiplied by $\mathbf{Q}$, reflecting the transpose relationship in the original $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ product.

The backward pass thus requires five matrix multiplications in total: $\mathbf{P}^\top \mathbf{dO}$ for $\mathbf{dV}$, $\mathbf{dO} \mathbf{V}^\top$ for $\mathbf{dP}$, $\alpha \mathbf{dS} \mathbf{K}$ for $\mathbf{dQ}$, $\alpha \mathbf{dS}^\top \mathbf{Q}$ for $\mathbf{dK}$, and a fifth matrix multiplication that arises in the recomputation strategy used to avoid storing the large $N \times N$ intermediate matrices. As with the forward pass, the backward pass computation parallelizes across the number of heads and batches for multi-head attention.

# The $\mathrm{dsoftmax}$ Operation and Its Role in Backpropagation

The softmax function is a nonlinear operation that maps a vector $\mathbf{s}$ to a probability distribution $\mathbf{p} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{s})$, where each component is $\mathbf{p}_i = \exp(\mathbf{s}_i) / \sum_j \exp(\mathbf{s}_j)$. When we need to propagate gradients through softmax during backpropagation, we must compute the Jacobian of the softmax function. For a vector $\mathbf{s}$, the derivative of $\mathrm{softmax}$ at $\mathbf{s}$ is the matrix:

$$\frac{\partial \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{s})}{\partial \mathbf{s}} = \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{p}) - \mathbf{p} \mathbf{p}^\top$$

where $\mathbf{p} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{s})$ and $\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{p})$ is the diagonal matrix with the entries of $\mathbf{p}$ on the diagonal. Given an upstream gradient $\mathbf{dp}$, the gradient with respect to $\mathbf{s}$ is then:

$$\mathbf{ds} = (\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{p}) - \mathbf{p} \mathbf{p}^\top) \mathbf{dp}$$

This formula is applied row-wise to the matrix $\mathbf{dP}$ to produce $\mathbf{dS}$, and we write $\mathbf{dS} = \mathrm{dsoftmax}(\mathbf{dP})$ for this row-wise application. The operation is written as $\mathrm{dsoftmax}$ to emphasize that it is the backward pass (the "d" or derivative) of the softmax function, not the softmax function itself.

The structure of this Jacobian reveals something important: each output gradient $\mathbf{ds}_i$ depends on all the upstream gradients $\mathbf{dp}_j$ within the same row, weighted by the attention probabilities $\mathbf{p}$. The diagonal term $\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{p})$ represents the direct contribution of each probability to its own gradient, while the outer product term $\mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^\top$ represents the coupling between different positions through the normalization constant. This coupling is what makes softmax more expensive to differentiate than a simple elementwise operation, and it is a key consideration in the design of efficient backward pass kernels.

In the context of FlashAttention, the $\mathrm{dsoftmax}$ operation must be computed in a block-wise fashion, consistent with the tiled algorithm that avoids materializing the full $N \times N$ matrices. The attention probabilities $\mathbf{P}$ and the normalization statistics (the rowwise maximum $m_i$ and the rowwise sum $\ell_i$) are recomputed or stored as needed during the backward pass, enabling the exact computation of $\mathrm{dsoftmax}$ without the memory overhead of the standard attention implementation.

# FLOP Counting for Forward and Backward Passes

Understanding the computational cost of attention is essential for evaluating the efficiency of different implementations. The dominant operations in attention are the matrix multiplications, and the total floating-point operations (FLOPs) can be counted precisely.

For the forward pass, there are two matrix multiplications. The first, $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, multiplies an $N \times d$ matrix by a $d \times N$ matrix, which requires $2NdN = 2N^2d$ FLOPs (each of the $N^2$ output entries requires $d$ multiply-accumulate operations, and each multiply-accumulate counts as two FLOPs). The second, $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$, multiplies an $N \times N$ matrix by an $N \times d$ matrix, which also requires $2N^2d$ FLOPs. The softmax operation itself involves $O(N^2)$ operations for the exponentials and normalizations, but these are negligible compared to the $O(N^2 d)$ matmul FLOPs when $d$ is reasonably large. Thus, the total forward pass FLOPs per head are $2N^2d + 2N^2d = 4N^2d$.

Accounting for all heads, the forward pass FLOP count is:

$$4 \cdot \text{seqlen}^2 \cdot \text{head dimension} \cdot \text{number of heads}$$

where seqlen $= N$. When causal masking is applied—meaning the attention is restricted so that position $i$ can only attend to positions $j \leq i$—approximately half of the entries in $\mathbf{S}$ are zeroed out, so the FLOP count is divided by 2.

For the backward pass, the chain rule requires five matrix multiplications (as opposed to two in the forward pass). The paper notes that there are 2 matmuls in the forward pass and 5 matmuls in the backward pass, the latter being due to recomputation strategies that avoid storing the large intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$. The backward pass FLOPs are thus 2.5 times the forward pass FLOPs:

$$\text{backward FLOPs} = 2.5 \times \text{forward FLOPs}$$

This ratio of 2.5 is important for benchmarking: when one measures the achieved TFLOPs/s of an attention kernel, one must correctly account for whether the measurement is for the forward pass, the backward pass, or both, and apply the appropriate FLOP count to convert from wall-clock time to throughput. For example, the paper reports that FlashAttention-3 achieves up to 740 TFLOPs/s in FP16 on the H100 GPU for the forward pass, which corresponds to 75% of the theoretical peak of approximately 989 TFLOPs/s for FP16 matrix multiplication on that device.

The FLOP count also helps explain why the softmax, despite being computationally cheap in terms of FLOPs, can become a significant bottleneck. On the H100 GPU, the throughput of special functions such as exponential is only about 3.9 TFLOPs/s, compared to 989 TFLOPs/s for FP16 matrix multiplication—a ratio of over 250$\times$. Even though there are 512$\times$ more matmul FLOPs than exponential FLOPs for head dimension 128 in FP16, the dramatically lower throughput of the exponential means that it can consume up to 50% of the total cycles. This observation is a primary motivation for the overlapping techniques in FlashAttention-3, which schedule the softmax computation concurrently with the matrix multiplications to hide its latency.

# GPU Hardware Architecture and Execution Model

Modern deep learning workloads place extraordinary demands on the hardware that executes them. The attention mechanism at the heart of the Transformer architecture is perhaps the most striking example: computing self-attention scores requires matrix multiplications whose cost scales quadratically with the sequence length, making attention the primary computational bottleneck in large language models and long-context applications. To accelerate such workloads, GPU designers have increasingly relied on hardware specialization—dedicated silicon for specific operations like matrix multiplication and memory movement—and on lower-precision arithmetic formats that deliver higher throughput for the same power and chip area. Understanding these hardware features is essential for designing algorithms like FlashAttention-3 that extract maximum performance from modern GPUs.

This chapter explains the hardware characteristics of the NVIDIA Hopper architecture (exemplified by the H100 GPU) that FlashAttention-3 exploits. We focus on four broad themes: the organization of GPU memory into a hierarchy of data locales, the organization of GPU execution into a hierarchy of thread groups, the asynchronous execution capabilities that allow computation and data movement to overlap, and the low-precision number formats that dramatically increase throughput. Throughout, we connect these hardware features to the algorithmic decisions made in FlashAttention-3.

# Memory Hierarchy: Global Memory, L2 Cache, Shared Memory, and Register File

A GPU's memory system is organized as a hierarchy of storage locations, or "locales," with a fundamental trade-off between capacity and bandwidth: the larger the storage, the lower its bandwidth. At the top of this hierarchy sits global memory (GMEM), also known as HBM (High Bandwidth Memory). This is the off-chip dynamic random-access memory (DRAM) that is accessible to all streaming multiprocessors (SMs) on the GPU. On the H100, HBM provides the largest storage capacity—80 gigabytes on the SXM5 variant—but has the lowest bandwidth relative to the on-chip memories. All input tensors, such as the query matrix $\mathbf{Q}$, key matrix $\mathbf{K}$, and value matrix $\mathbf{V}$, initially reside in HBM. When a standard attention implementation materializes the intermediate score matrix $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$ and the probability matrix $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$, these large $N \times N$ matrices are written to and read from HBM, which is precisely the bottleneck that FlashAttention was designed to eliminate.

Data loaded from HBM passes transparently through an on-chip L2 cache, which sits between HBM and the SMs. The L2 cache is a hardware-managed cache, meaning the programmer does not have explicit control over what resides there, but it helps reduce the effective latency and bandwidth cost of repeated accesses to the same data in HBM.

Within each SM, there is a small, on-chip, programmer-managed, highly banked cache called shared memory (SMEM). Shared memory is much faster than HBM but far smaller in capacity. The programmer has fine-grained control over what data resides in SMEM and when it is loaded or evicted, which makes it a critical resource for performance optimization. In FlashAttention-3, tiles of the query, key, and value matrices are loaded from HBM into shared memory, where they serve as operands for matrix multiply-accumulate operations. The circular shared memory buffer with multiple stages, used in the warp-specialized pipelining scheme, is a prime example of how careful management of SMEM can hide memory latency.

Finally, at the finest level of the memory hierarchy, each SM contains a register file (RMEM). Each thread has access to at most 256 registers that are private to itself. Registers are the fastest storage on the GPU but also the most limited in capacity. Matrix multiply-accumulate instructions on Hopper accumulate their results in registers, and intermediate values such as the running maximum $m_i$ and running sum $\ell_i$ in the FlashAttention-3 softmax computation are also held in registers. The tension between wanting to hold more data in registers for pipelining and the limited register capacity per thread is a recurring theme in the algorithm's design. For instance, the two-stage GEMM-softmax pipelining in FlashAttention-3 requires an additional $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ matrix to be kept in registers, consuming $B_r \times B_c \times \text{sizeof}(\text{float})$ bytes per threadblock, which can conflict with the desire to use larger tile sizes.

# Thread Hierarchy: Threads, Warps, Warpgroups, CTAs, Clusters, and Grids

The GPU's programming model is organized around a hierarchy of logical execution units. From the finest to the coarsest level, this hierarchy consists of threads, warps, warpgroups, threadblocks (also called cooperative thread arrays or CTAs), threadblock clusters, and grids.

A thread is the smallest unit of execution on a GPU. Individual threads execute the same program (a "kernel") but can take different paths through the code based on their thread index and other data. Threads are grouped into warps, each consisting of 32 threads that execute instructions in lockstep on a single SM. The warp is the fundamental scheduling unit on an NVIDIA GPU: when an SM selects work to execute, it schedules entire warps, and all threads in a warp execute the same instruction at the same time (though some threads may be inactive if their branch diverges).

On the Hopper architecture, warps are further grouped into warpgroups, which consist of 4 contiguous warps (128 threads). The warpgroup is an important organizational unit for FlashAttention-3 because the key computational instruction—the warpgroup-wide matrix multiply-accumulate (WGMMA) instruction—operates at the warpgroup level. When FlashAttention-3 divides the warps of a CTA into producer and consumer roles, it assigns these roles at the warpgroup granularity.

A threadblock, or cooperative thread array (CTA), is a group of threads that are co-scheduled on the same SM. All threads within a CTA can communicate through shared memory and synchronize their execution through barrier instructions. The CTA is the unit by which work is distributed across SMs: when a kernel is launched, the GPU scheduler assigns CTAs to SMs, and each CTA executes independently (or semi-independently, in the case of clusters) on its assigned SM. In FlashAttention-3, each CTA is responsible for computing a tile $\mathbf{O}_i$ of the output matrix from a tile $\mathbf{Q}_i$ of the query matrix, iterating over all tiles of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$.

Hopper introduces threadblock clusters, which are groups of CTAs co-scheduled on the same graphics processing cluster (GPC—a group of SMs). CTAs within the same cluster can communicate through distributed shared memory, enabling collective operations across CTAs. At the coarsest level, a grid encompasses all CTAs launched for a given kernel. The grid is the complete set of work to be done and is mapped onto the GPU's SMs by the hardware scheduler.

# Relationship Between Memory and Thread Hierarchies

The memory and thread hierarchies are closely interlinked, and understanding their relationship is essential for grasping how FlashAttention-3 achieves its performance. The key principle is that access to each level of the memory hierarchy is governed by the thread's position in the thread hierarchy.

Threads within the same CTA are co-scheduled on the same SM and therefore share access to that SM's shared memory. This means that all warps and warpgroups within a CTA can read from and write to the same SMEM, enabling them to collaborate on shared data. In FlashAttention-3, the producer warpgroup loads tiles of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ from HBM into shared memory using TMA instructions, and the consumer warpgroups then read these tiles from shared memory to use as operands for WGMMA matrix multiplications. The circular SMEM buffer with multiple stages allows the producer to load data for the next iteration while the consumer processes data from the current iteration, creating a pipeline that hides memory latency.

In contrast to shared memory, each thread's registers are private to that thread. No other thread can directly access a thread's register file. This means that when a warp or warpgroup needs to share data held in registers, it must use explicit shuffle or permute instructions to move values between threads. FlashAttention-3 encounters this challenge when dealing with FP8 WGMMA: the layout of the FP32 accumulator in registers differs from the layout expected for the operand of the next FP8 WGMMA, requiring byte permute instructions to rearrange the register contents.

CTAs within the same cluster, being co-scheduled on the same GPC, can access each other's shared memory through the distributed shared memory mechanism. This allows for collaboration at a finer granularity than would otherwise be possible across SM boundaries. At the broadest level, all CTAs in a grid share access to global memory (HBM), but with the highest latency and lowest bandwidth in the hierarchy.

# Asynchrony on Hopper: Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA)

GPUs are throughput processors: rather than trying to minimize the latency of any single operation, they achieve high performance by keeping many operations in flight simultaneously. This is accomplished through concurrency and asynchrony, which allow the GPU to hide memory access latencies and execution latencies by overlapping different types of work.

A critical enabler of asynchrony on the Hopper architecture is the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA), a dedicated hardware unit for asynchronous memory copies between global memory (HBM) and shared memory (SMEM). On prior architectures like Ampere, memory copies between HBM and SMEM were issued by individual threads, which tied up valuable computational resources and made it difficult to fully overlap data movement with computation. The TMA, by contrast, is a specialized unit that can issue bulk memory transfers without occupying the CUDA cores.

The key advantage of TMA is that issuing a TMA load does not stall the issuing thread or warp on the completion of other loads or computations. The TMA operates asynchronously: once a load is issued, the requesting warp can continue executing other instructions while the hardware handles the data transfer in the background. When the data has arrived in shared memory, a synchronization mechanism (such as a barrier with the \texttt{commit} and \texttt{wait} operations shown in the FlashAttention-3 pseudocode) notifies the consumer that the data is ready.

In FlashAttention-3, the producer warpgroup uses TMA to load tiles of $\mathbf{Q}_i$, $\mathbf{K}_j$, and $\mathbf{V}_j$ from HBM into the circular SMEM buffer. Because TMA loads are asynchronous, the producer can issue multiple loads without waiting for earlier ones to complete, which is essential for building up a pipeline of data in the multi-stage circular buffer. For example, in the first $s$ iterations of the producer mainloop (where $s$ is the number of stages in the buffer), no waits are issued at all because the buffer is still being filled. This ability to overlap data movement with computation is one of the three main techniques that FlashAttention-3 uses to achieve its dramatic speedup.

# Asynchronous Tensor Cores: WGMMA Instruction and Its Execution Model

The second major source of asynchrony on Hopper is the asynchronous execution of matrix multiply-accumulate operations on the Tensor Cores. On prior architectures, the instruction for executing matrix multiplications on Tensor Cores (known as MMA) was synchronous from the perspective of the warp that issued it: the warp would wait for the MMA to complete before proceeding to the next instruction. On Hopper, the warpgroup-wide matrix multiply-accumulate instruction (WGMMA) is asynchronous.

WGMMA is issued at the warpgroup level (4 warps, 128 threads) and can source its input operands directly from shared memory, unlike the Ampere MMA instruction which required operands to be loaded into registers first. Once a WGMMA is issued, the warpgroup can continue executing other instructions—particularly non-matmul operations such as the floating-point multiply-add and exponential functions needed for softmax—while the Tensor Cores perform the matrix multiplication in the background. The warpgroup later issues a \texttt{wait} instruction to ensure that the WGMMA result is available in the accumulator registers before using it.

This asynchrony is the key enabler of the intra-warpgroup GEMM-softmax overlapping technique in FlashAttention-3. In the two-stage pipelining algorithm, the consumer warpgroup issues a WGMMA to compute $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$ but does not immediately wait for it to complete. Instead, it proceeds to issue the second WGMMA for $\mathbf{O}_i = \mathbf{O}_i + \tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}} \mathbf{V}_{j-1}$ and then performs the softmax computation on $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ from the previous iteration. Only after the softmax is complete does it wait for the first WGMMA to finish. This overlaps the low-throughput non-GEMM operations (softmax) with the high-throughput GEMM operations, significantly improving Tensor Core utilization.

The performance motivation for this overlap is stark: on the H100 SXM5 GPU, FP16 matmul achieves 989 TFLOPs/s, while special functions such as the exponential (necessary for softmax) achieve only 3.9 TFLOPs/s. For attention with head dimension 128, there are 512x more matmul FLOPs than exponential operations, but the exponential has 256x lower throughput, meaning that exponential can take up to 50% of the cycles compared to matmul. Without overlapping these operations, the Tensor Cores would sit idle while the multi-function units compute the exponential, wasting a significant fraction of the GPU's computational capacity.

# Warp-Specialization: Producer-Consumer Model and Register Reallocation

Hopper's support for asynchronous TMA and WGMMA enables a programming model called warp-specialization, in which the warps within a CTA are divided into producer and consumer roles. Producer warps are responsible for data movement—they issue TMA loads to bring data from HBM into shared memory. Consumer warps are responsible for computation—they issue WGMMA instructions and perform the softmax and other arithmetic. By separating these roles, the compiler can more easily generate optimal instruction schedules, because each warp's instruction stream is homogeneous (either all data movement or all computation).

In FlashAttention-3, a CTA typically contains two warpgroups: one producer warpgroup and one consumer warpgroup (or two consumer warpgroups, as in the pingpong scheduling scheme). The producer warpgroup issues TMA loads for $\mathbf{Q}_i$, $\mathbf{K}_j$, and $\mathbf{V}_j$, commits to notify the consumer that the data is ready, and waits for the consumer to release a stage of the circular buffer before overwriting it. The consumer warpgroup waits for data to arrive in shared memory, performs the GEMMs and softmax, and releases the buffer stage when it is done with the data.

A complementary feature of Hopper that supports warp-specialization is the ability to dynamically reallocate registers between warpgroups using the \texttt{setmaxnreg} instruction. Since the producer warpgroup only needs to issue TMA loads (which requires only a single thread), it does not need many registers. The consumer warpgroup, on the other hand, needs registers to hold the WGMMA accumulators, intermediate softmax values, and pipeline buffers. By deallocating registers from the producer and reallocating them to the consumer, the algorithm ensures that the consumer has enough register capacity to maintain the multi-stage pipeline without spilling to shared memory, which would degrade performance.

In the FlashAttention-3 pseudocode, the producer warpgroup deallocates a predetermined number of registers at the start, while the consumer warpgroup reallocates registers as a function of the number of consumer warps. This dynamic allocation is particularly important for the two-stage and three-stage pipelining schemes, which require additional registers to hold extra blocks of the score matrix $\mathbf{S}$.

Warp-specialization also enables the pingpong scheduling scheme, in which two consumer warpgroups alternate between doing GEMMs and doing softmax. Synchronization barriers (\texttt{bar.sync} instructions) are used to force the GEMMs of warpgroup 1 to be scheduled before the GEMMs of warpgroup 2, so that the softmax of warpgroup 1 is scheduled while warpgroup 2 is performing its GEMMs, and vice versa. This creates a "pingpong" pattern that keeps both the Tensor Cores and the multi-function units busy, improving performance from 570 TFLOPs/s to 620-640 TFLOPs/s in one benchmark configuration.

# Low-Precision Number Formats: FP16, BF16, FP8, and Throughput Benefits

A major trend in GPU hardware design is the introduction of progressively lower-precision number formats, each offering higher throughput for the same power and chip area. This trend began with FP16 (half-precision floating point) on the Pascal architecture in 2017, continued with BF16 (bfloat16) on Ampere in 2020, and reached FP8 on Hopper and FP4 on Blackwell.

On the H100 GPU, the WGMMA instruction can target FP8 Tensor Cores to deliver 2x the throughput per SM compared to FP16 or BF16. This means that for the same hardware resources, the GPU can perform twice as many floating-point operations per second when using FP8 instead of FP16. For attention, which is dominated by matrix multiplications, this translates directly to a potential doubling of throughput: FlashAttention-3 in FP8 reaches close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s, compared to 740 TFLOPs/s for FP16.

The FP8 format used on Hopper is e4m3, which allocates 4 bits for the exponent and 3 bits for the mantissa (plus a sign bit). This gives a total of 8 bits per value, compared to 16 bits for FP16 or BF16. The reduced mantissa precision means that FP8 can represent fewer significant digits, leading to higher numerical error. Moreover, large language models typically have outlier values—features or activations that are much larger in magnitude than most other values—which make quantization to FP8 particularly challenging, as a single scaling factor may not adequately represent the full dynamic range of the data.

Despite these accuracy challenges, the throughput benefits of FP8 are so compelling that FlashAttention-3 invests significant effort in adapting the algorithm to use FP8 Tensor Cores. The key insight is that the accuracy loss can be mitigated through algorithmic techniques—block quantization and incoherent processing—while the throughput benefits are a pure hardware advantage. The result is that FP8 FlashAttention-3 achieves 2.6x lower numerical error than a baseline FP8 attention implementation using per-tensor quantization, while approaching the theoretical peak throughput of the FP8 Tensor Cores.

# FP8 WGMMA Layout Constraints: K-Major Requirement and Accumulator Layout Differences

While FP8 offers compelling throughput benefits, using it effectively requires navigating a set of layout constraints imposed by the FP8 WGMMA instruction. These constraints are more restrictive than those for FP16 WGMMA and have significant implications for the design of the attention algorithm.

To understand these constraints, we first need to define the concept of matrix layout in the context of WGMMA. Given a GEMM call to multiply $A \times B^\top$ for an $M \times K$-matrix $A$ and an $N \times K$-matrix $B$, we say that the $A$ or $B$ operand is "mn-major" if it is contiguous in the outer $M$ or $N$ dimension, and "k-major" if it is contiguous in the inner $K$-dimension. Contiguity here refers to the memory layout: in a k-major matrix, elements that differ only in the $K$-dimension are adjacent in memory, while in an mn-major matrix, elements that differ only in the $M$ or $N$-dimension are adjacent.

For FP16 WGMMA, both mn-major and k-major input operands are accepted for operands in shared memory. This flexibility means that the input tensors $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$, which are typically given as contiguous in the head dimension $d$ (which corresponds to the $K$-dimension for the first GEMM $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$), can be used directly without any layout transformation. However, for FP8 WGMMA, only the k-major format is supported. This means that for the second GEMM $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$, where $\mathbf{V}$ serves as the $B$ operand with $K = d$, the tiles of $\mathbf{V}$ loaded into shared memory must be contiguous in the sequence length dimension $N$ (the $M$-dimension of $\mathbf{V}$ in the GEMM). Since the input $\mathbf{V}$ is typically stored as contiguous in the head dimension, and TMA loads cannot change the contiguous dimension, a layout transformation is needed.

FlashAttention-3 addresses this by performing an in-kernel transpose of $\mathbf{V}$ tiles after loading them into shared memory. This transpose is implemented using the LDSM (\texttt{ldmatrix}) and STSM (\texttt{stmatrix}) instructions, which allow a warp of threads to collectively load data from SMEM to RMEM and store it back to SMEM at a granularity of 128 bytes. These instructions are register-efficient and can be executed in the producer warpgroup, and they are capable of transposing layouts during the memory copy. By arranging for the transpose of the next $\mathbf{V}$ tile to execute in the "shadow" of the WGMMAs that process the preceding $\mathbf{V}$ and current $\mathbf{K}$ tiles, the transpose overhead is effectively hidden.

A second layout challenge arises from the difference between the memory layout of the FP32 accumulator of an FP8 WGMMA and the layout assumed for its operand $A$ when held in registers. When two GEMMs are fused back-to-back in a single kernel—as in attention, where $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ is computed first and then $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$ uses $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$ as its $A$ operand—the output of the first WGMMA (the accumulator) must be transformed into a format suitable as input to the second WGMMA. FlashAttention-3 accomplishes this using byte permute instructions that rearrange the entries in the register file, changing the order of the accumulator fragments to match the expected operand layout. Specifically, the entries are reordered in a pattern like $\{d0\ d1\ d4\ d5\ d2\ d3\ d6\ d7\}$, and this permutation is replicated over every 8 bytes. This register permutation corresponds to a column permutation of the $\mathbf{P}$ tile, which is compensated by arranging the in-kernel transpose to write out a matching row permutation of the $\mathbf{V}$ tile. This clever coordination between the transpose and the register permutation eliminates the need for expensive cross-thread shuffle instructions.

These layout constraints illustrate a broader principle: exploiting low-precision hardware often requires bridging the gap between the formats and layouts that the hardware expects and those that naturally arise from the algorithm. The success of FP8 FlashAttention-3 depends as much on these layout transformations as on the raw throughput of the FP8 Tensor Cores.

# From Standard Attention to FlashAttention

## Standard Attention: Materializing S and P to HBM and the Memory Bandwidth Problem

The attention mechanism is the central computational bottleneck in the Transformer architecture. Given query, key, and value matrices $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$, where $N$ is the sequence length and $d$ is the head dimension, the attention output $\mathbf{O}$ is computed through three stages. First, the attention scores are computed as $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$, where $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{d}$ is a scaling factor. Second, the softmax is applied row-wise to obtain $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S}) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$. Third, the output is computed as $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$.

The standard implementation of attention, as used in early Transformer implementations, materializes the intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ to HBM (high bandwidth memory), also known as global memory (GMEM). This means that after computing $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$, the entire $N \times N$ matrix of scores is written out to HBM. Then, the softmax operation reads $\mathbf{S}$ back from HBM, computes $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$, and writes $\mathbf{P}$ back to HBM. Finally, the output computation reads $\mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ from HBM to produce $\mathbf{O}$. The problem here is one of memory bandwidth: the GPU's memory hierarchy is structured such that the off-chip DRAM (HBM/GMEM) has high capacity but relatively low bandwidth compared to the on-chip memories. Each of these intermediate reads and writes to HBM is expensive because HBM bandwidth is a scarce resource, and the $O(N^2)$ size of $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ means a large volume of data must traverse the memory bus. As sequence length grows, the cost of these intermediate materializations dominates the computation, making attention the primary bottleneck for large language models and long-context applications.

The key insight is that the matrix multiplications themselves (computing $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{O}$) are compute-bound operations that can efficiently utilize the GPU's Tensor Cores, but the intermediate reads and writes of $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ to HBM introduce unnecessary memory traffic that throttles overall throughput. In a standard attention implementation, the actual floating-point computation is dwarfed by the time spent moving data between HBM and the compute units. This memory bandwidth bottleneck is the fundamental problem that FlashAttention was designed to solve.

## FlashAttention-1: Tiling Strategy and Fusing Attention into a Single Kernel

FlashAttention, introduced by Dao et al., addresses the memory bandwidth problem by fusing all attention operations into a single GPU kernel. The core idea is to eliminate the intermediate reads and writes of $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ to HBM entirely. Instead of materializing the full $N \times N$ score matrix, FlashAttention uses a tiling strategy that processes the attention computation in blocks, keeping intermediate results in the much faster on-chip shared memory (SMEM) and registers (RMEM).

The tiling strategy works as follows. The query matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ is divided into blocks of size $B_r \times d$, and the key and value matrices $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ are divided into blocks of size $B_c \times d$. The algorithm operates on one tile $\mathbf{Q}_i$ at a time, iterating over all tiles $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$ of the key and value matrices. For each pair of tiles, it computes a block of the score matrix $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$ directly in registers or shared memory, applies the softmax locally, and immediately uses the result to update the output tile $\mathbf{O}_i$ by computing $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$. Crucially, the blocks of $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ are never written to HBM. They exist only transiently in on-chip memory during the computation of each tile pair.

By fusing all operations into a single kernel, FlashAttention ensures that HBM is accessed only to read the input matrices $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, $\mathbf{V}$ and to write the final output $\mathbf{O}$. The intermediate results live entirely in the GPU's fast on-chip memory hierarchy. This dramatically reduces the memory traffic from $O(N^2)$ reads and writes of $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ to just $O(Nd)$ reads of the inputs and writes of the output. The tiling approach is exact -- it produces the same result as standard attention -- but avoids the memory bandwidth bottleneck that plagues the standard implementation.

## Local Softmax: Block-wise Computation with Rescaling

The key algorithmic challenge in FlashAttention is computing the softmax in a block-wise manner. The standard softmax for a row $\mathbf{s}$ requires knowing the maximum value of the entire row for numerical stability (to prevent overflow in the exponential function), and the sum of exponentials for normalization. When processing blocks of $\mathbf{K}$ sequentially, we only have access to a block of scores $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}$ at a time, not the full row of $\mathbf{S}$. FlashAttention solves this by maintaining running statistics -- the running maximum $m_i$ and the running sum $\ell_i$ -- and rescaling the output accordingly as each new block is processed.

Specifically, for each new block $j$, the algorithm computes the block of scores $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$. It then updates the running maximum: $m_i^{\mathrm{old}} = m_i$ and $m_i = \mathrm{max}(m_i^{\mathrm{old}}, \mathrm{rowmax}(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}))$. The exponentials are computed relative to the new maximum: $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} = \mathrm{exp}(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} - m_i)$. The running normalization factor is updated as $\ell_i = \mathrm{exp}(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i) \ell_i + \mathrm{rowsum}(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)})$. The crucial step is that the output accumulator $\mathbf{O}_i$ must also be rescaled to account for the change in the running maximum: $\mathbf{O}_i = \mathrm{diag}(\mathrm{exp}(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i))^{-1} \mathbf{O}_i + \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$. After all blocks have been processed, the final output is normalized by the total running sum: $\mathbf{O}_i = \mathrm{diag}(\ell_i)^{-1} \mathbf{O}_i$.

The rescaling factor $\mathrm{exp}(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i)$ accounts for the fact that the previous output was computed relative to the old maximum, and now must be adjusted to be relative to the new (larger) maximum. This local softmax computation is what makes the tiling strategy possible: it allows the algorithm to process the key and value matrices in blocks while maintaining numerical stability and exact correctness. The intermediate results -- the scores, the exponentials, and the softmax outputs -- are all kept in FP32 for numerical stability, which means FlashAttention actually achieves lower numerical error than the standard implementation where intermediate results may be stored in lower precision in HBM.

## FlashAttention-2: Parallelizing Over Sequence Length and Restructuring the Inner Loop

FlashAttention-2, introduced by Dao, restructured the original FlashAttention algorithm to improve GPU occupancy and work distribution. The key change was to parallelize over the sequence length dimension of the query matrix, in addition to the batch size and number of heads that were already parallelized in the original FlashAttention. In the first version, the parallelization was primarily over the batch size and number of heads, with the query sequence length dimension handled sequentially within each threadblock. FlashAttention-2 restructured this so that the forward pass parallelizes across the batch size, number of heads, and query sequence length, with the inner loop iterating over blocks of the key and value matrices.

The inner loop was also restructured to improve efficiency. In the original FlashAttention, the inner loop performed more work per iteration than necessary, leading to lower occupancy. FlashAttention-2 streamlined this by reducing the amount of non-matmul computation in the inner loop and better balancing the work between the two matrix multiplications (computing $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ and $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$). The algorithm processes one query tile $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and iterates over key/value tiles $\mathbf{K}_j$, $\mathbf{V}_j$, computing the local softmax and accumulating the output, as described above. By parallelizing over the query sequence length, FlashAttention-2 ensures that for long sequences, the GPU has enough threadblocks to keep all streaming multiprocessors (SMs) occupied, improving hardware utilization.

The result was a significant improvement in performance, particularly for longer sequences where the original FlashAttention's limited parallelism left GPU resources idle. FlashAttention-2 became the standard for fast, exact attention computation and was widely adopted in practice.

## Why FlashAttention-2 Underutilizes Hopper GPUs: Synchronous Model and Lack of Asynchrony/Low-Precision Exploitation

Despite its improvements, FlashAttention-2 achieves only about 35% utilization on NVIDIA's Hopper H100 GPU, compared to the 80-90% utilization achieved by optimized matrix multiplication (GEMM) kernels on the same hardware. This gap points to fundamental limitations in FlashAttention-2's algorithmic design relative to the capabilities of modern GPU architectures.

The first issue is that FlashAttention-2 adheres to a simplified synchronous execution model. The algorithm assumes that operations happen in sequence: the first GEMM completes, then the softmax runs, then the second GEMM runs. This is wasteful on Hopper GPUs, which have dedicated hardware units for different types of operations that can execute concurrently. Specifically, Hopper features the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) for asynchronous memory copies between HBM and shared memory, and the Tensor Cores (exposed via the warpgroup-wide WGMMA instruction) perform matrix multiplication asynchronously. The softmax operations -- which involve floating-point multiply-add and exponential computations -- run on the separate CUDA cores and multi-function units. In FlashAttention-2's synchronous model, these units sit idle while waiting for each other: the Tensor Cores are idle during softmax, and the CUDA cores are idle during GEMM.

The second issue is that FlashAttention-2 makes no use of low-precision computation. Modern GPUs provide specialized hardware for FP8 (and even FP4 on Blackwell) matrix multiplication, which can deliver double or quadruple the throughput of FP16/BF16 for the same power and chip area. FlashAttention-2 operates entirely in FP16/BF16, leaving this performance on the table. The technical challenge is that FP8 introduces higher numerical error, and large language models exhibit outlier features that are difficult to quantize accurately without special techniques like block quantization and incoherent processing.

These two gaps -- the failure to exploit asynchrony to overlap computation with data movement and non-GEMM with GEMM operations, and the failure to leverage low-precision hardware -- are what motivate FlashAttention-3. By redesigning the algorithm around warp-specialized producer-consumer patterns, overlapping softmax with GEMMs through pipelined execution, and adapting the algorithm for FP8 with careful attention to layout constraints and quantization accuracy, FlashAttention-3 is able to achieve up to 75% utilization on the H100, reaching 740 TFLOPs/s in FP16 and close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s in FP8. The evolution from standard attention through FlashAttention and FlashAttention-2 thus sets the stage for understanding how hardware-aware algorithm design can close the gap between attention and optimized GEMM kernels.

# FlashAttention-3: Producer-Consumer Asynchrony via Warp-Specialization

## Warp-Specialization: Dividing Warpgroups into Producers and Consumers

Modern GPU architectures such as NVIDIA's Hopper introduce dedicated hardware units that perform specialized tasks asynchronously from the general-purpose CUDA cores. Two such units are central to the design of FlashAttention-3: the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA), which handles asynchronous memory copies between global memory (GMEM, also called HBM) and shared memory (SMEM), and the Tensor Cores, exposed through the warpgroup-wide WGMMA instruction, which perform high-throughput matrix multiplication (GEMM) operations and can source their operands directly from shared memory. Because these units operate asynchronously, the threads that issue their instructions do not need to stall while the corresponding data movement or computation completes. This asynchrony creates an opportunity: if different groups of threads are assigned distinct roles, one group can issue data movement instructions while another group performs computation on data that has already arrived, and the two activities can proceed concurrently.

FlashAttention-3 exploits this opportunity through a technique called warp-specialization. In a standard GPU kernel, all warps (groups of 32 threads) within a cooperative thread array (CTA, also called a threadblock) perform the same sequence of operations. In contrast, a warp-specialized kernel divides the warps of a CTA into distinct roles: some warps act as producers that only issue data movement instructions, and others act as consumers that only perform computation. In the context of FlashAttention-3, the producer warpgroups (groups of 4 contiguous warps, totaling 128 threads) are responsible for issuing TMA loads to bring tiles of the query matrix $\mathbf{Q}$, key matrix $\mathbf{K}$, and value matrix $\mathbf{V}$ from HBM into shared memory. The consumer warpgroups, on the other hand, wait for the data to arrive in shared memory and then issue WGMMA instructions to perform the matrix multiplications that constitute the attention computation, along with the non-matmul operations such as softmax.

The key insight is that TMA loads are issued by a single thread and do not require the involvement of the entire warpgroup, whereas WGMMA computations benefit from having as many registers as possible available to hold intermediate results. By separating producers and consumers into different warpgroups, the compiler is better able to generate optimal instruction schedules, since each warpgroup has a simpler, more focused set of instructions to execute. The producer warpgroups issue asynchronous loads and then commit their completion, while the consumer warpgroups wait on those commits before processing the data. This division of labor is the foundation upon which the rest of the FlashAttention-3 performance improvements are built.

## Circular SMEM Buffer with s-Stage Pipeline for Overlapping Loads with Computation

To fully exploit the asynchrony of TMA and WGMMA, FlashAttention-3 employs a circular (ring) buffer in shared memory with $s$ stages. The idea of a multi-stage buffer is to allow the producer to load data for future iterations ahead of time, while the consumer is still processing data from the current iteration. This creates a pipeline where data movement and computation overlap across iterations rather than being strictly serialized.

In a single-stage scheme, the producer would load a tile of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$, the consumer would process it, and only then would the producer load the next tile. This would leave the TMA unit idle while the consumer computes, and leave the Tensor Cores idle while the producer loads the next tile. With an $s$-stage circular buffer, the shared memory is divided into $s$ slots. The producer fills these slots in round-robin order: on iteration $j$, it loads $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$ into slot $j \mod s$ of the buffer. The consumer, meanwhile, reads from the same slots in the same round-robin order, processing the data that the producer loaded earlier. As long as the consumer is still processing data from slot $j \mod s$, the producer must wait before overwriting that slot; this is enforced by barrier synchronization, discussed in the next section. But if the consumer has finished with that slot, the producer can immediately write new data into it, even if the consumer is still processing data from other slots.

The number of stages $s$ controls how far ahead the producer can get relative to the consumer. A larger $s$ allows more overlap and better hiding of memory latency, but it also requires more shared memory since all $s$ slots must coexist in SMEM simultaneously. In practice, $s$ is chosen to balance this trade-off based on the available shared memory budget and the tile sizes. The circular buffer structure ensures that the same physical shared memory is reused across iterations, avoiding the need to allocate separate buffers for every tile of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$.

This pipelining scheme is a form of software pipelining, where the loop iterations are overlapped by explicitly managing the flow of data through a multi-stage buffer. The producer is always working ahead of the consumer by up to $s$ iterations, filling the buffer, while the consumer drains the buffer at its own pace. When the pipeline is full, both the TMA and the Tensor Cores can be kept busy simultaneously, dramatically improving hardware utilization.

## Barrier Synchronization Between Producers and Consumers

The producer and consumer warpgroups must coordinate their access to the circular SMEM buffer to ensure that the consumer never reads data that the producer has not yet finished loading, and that the producer never overwrites data that the consumer is still using. FlashAttention-3 achieves this coordination through barrier synchronization, managed by a pipeline object that uses \texttt{bar.sync} instructions.

The synchronization protocol works as follows. When the producer finishes loading a tile into a particular stage of the buffer, it commits to the pipeline, signaling that the data in that stage is ready for consumption. The consumer, before reading from a stage, waits on the corresponding commit to ensure the data has arrived. Conversely, before the producer writes to a stage, it waits to confirm that the consumer has released that stage, indicating that the consumer has finished reading the previous contents and the slot is safe to overwrite. The consumer releases a stage after it has completed all computation that depends on the data in that stage.

This protocol creates a set of dependencies that enforce correctness while still allowing maximal overlap. On the very first $s$ iterations of the producer's main loop, no waits are needed for buffer stages to be consumed, because the buffer is initially empty and all $s$ stages are available for writing. This is the "filling" phase of the pipeline. Once the buffer is full, the producer enters steady state where it must wait for the consumer to release a stage before reusing it. Similarly, the consumer's first wait is for the initial data to be loaded, after which it enters its own steady-state loop. In steady state, the producer and consumer operate concurrently: the producer is loading tiles for upcoming iterations while the consumer processes tiles from earlier iterations, with the barrier synchronization ensuring that they never conflict on the same buffer stage.

The pipeline object that manages these barriers abstracts away the low-level details of \texttt{bar.sync} instructions, making the algorithm description cleaner. However, the underlying mechanism relies on hardware-supported barrier instructions available on the Hopper architecture, which provide efficient inter-warpgroup synchronization without requiring global memory round-trips.

## Register Allocation and Deallocation with setmaxnreg for Warpgroup Role Optimization

A critical resource management challenge in warp-specialized kernels is the distribution of registers among warpgroups. The GPU's register file (RMEM) is a finite per-SM resource, with each thread having at most 256 registers available. In a warp-specialized kernel, producer and consumer warpgroups have very different register needs. The consumer warpgroups, which perform WGMMA matrix multiplications and maintain intermediate results such as accumulators for the output matrix $\mathbf{O}_i$, the running maximum $m_i$, and the running sum $\ell_i$, require a large number of registers to avoid spilling to shared memory or global memory. The producer warpgroups, on the other hand, primarily issue TMA loads, which require only minimal register usage since a single thread can issue a TMA instruction.

Hopper provides the \verb|setmaxnreg| instruction, which allows dynamic reallocation of registers between warpgroups at runtime. FlashAttention-3 uses this instruction to give consumer warpgroups a larger share of the register file and producer warpgroups a smaller share. Specifically, at the beginning of the kernel, the producer warpgroup deallocates a predetermined number of registers, effectively surrendering them. The consumer warpgroup then reallocates those registers, increasing its own register budget. This rebalancing is performed once at kernel launch, before the main loops begin, and the allocation remains fixed for the duration of the kernel.

The benefit of this approach is that consumer warpgroups can use larger tile sizes and maintain more intermediate values in registers without spilling, which is critical for achieving high Tensor Core utilization. Register spilling, which occurs when the register file is too small to hold all needed values and some must be stored to slower memory, can significantly degrade performance. By shifting registers from the producer (which does not need them) to the consumer (which does), \verb|setmaxnreg| helps minimize spilling and keeps the most performance-critical data in the fastest tier of the memory hierarchy.

The amount of reallocation is determined as a function of the number of consumer warps, the tile sizes, and the specific computations the consumer must perform. In practice, this is tuned through profiling to find the sweet spot where register pressure is minimized without unnecessarily constraining the producer warpgroup's ability to issue loads.

## Algorithm Pseudocode: CTA-Level View of Warp-Specialized FlashAttention-3 Forward Pass

The warp-specialized forward pass of FlashAttention-3, without the additional intra-consumer GEMM-softmax overlapping that will be described in later sections, is presented as a CTA-level algorithm. The algorithm operates on a tile $\mathbf{Q}_i$ of the query matrix of size $B_r \times d$ to compute the corresponding output tile $\mathbf{O}_i$. The key and value matrices are divided into $T_c = \lceil N / B_c \rceil$ blocks of size $B_c \times d$, where $B_c$ is the key block size.

The algorithm begins with a conditional branch. If the executing warpgroup is a producer, it first deallocates registers via \verb|setmaxnreg|, reducing its register footprint. It then issues a TMA load for $\mathbf{Q}_i$ from HBM to shared memory and commits upon completion to notify the consumer. It then enters its main loop over $j$ from $0$ to $T_c - 1$. In each iteration, it waits for the $(j \mod s)$th stage of the circular buffer to be consumed (i.e., released by the consumer), issues TMA loads for $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$ into that stage, and commits upon completion to notify the consumer.

If the executing warpgroup is a consumer, it first reallocates registers via \verb|setmaxnreg| as a function of the number of consumer warps. It then initializes the on-chip (register-resident) accumulator $\mathbf{O}_i = 0 \in \mathbb{R}^{B_r \times d}$, the running maximum $m_i = -\infty \in \mathbb{R}^{B_r}$, and the running sum $\ell_i = 0 \in \mathbb{R}^{B_r}$. It waits for $\mathbf{Q}_i$ to be loaded into shared memory. It then enters its main loop over $j$ from $0$ to $T_c - 1$. In each iteration, it waits for $\mathbf{K}_j$ to be available in shared memory, computes $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^T$ via WGMMA (an SS-GEMM, since both operands are in shared memory), commits and waits for completion. It then performs the local softmax computation: it stores the old running maximum $m_i^{\mathrm{old}} = m_i$, updates the running maximum to $m_i = \max(m_i^{\mathrm{old}}, \mathrm{rowmax}(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}))$, computes $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} = \exp(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} - m_i)$, and updates $\ell_i = \exp(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i) \ell_i + \mathrm{rowsum}(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)})$. After the softmax, it waits for $\mathbf{V}_j$ to be available in shared memory, computes $\mathbf{O}_i = \mathrm{diag}(\exp(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i))^{-1} \mathbf{O}_i + \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$ via WGMMA (an RS-GEMM, since the first operand $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$ is in registers and the second operand $\mathbf{V}_j$ is in shared memory), commits and waits, and finally releases the $(j \mod s)$th stage of the buffer for the producer. After the loop, the consumer normalizes $\mathbf{O}_i = \mathrm{diag}(\ell_i)^{-1} \mathbf{O}_i$ and computes the log-sum-exp $L_i = m_i + \log(\ell_i)$, then writes $\mathbf{O}_i$ and $L_i$ to HBM.

The SS and RS prefixes on the GEMM labels indicate whether the first operand is sourced from shared memory (SS) or from the register file (RS). This distinction matters because WGMMA instructions can accept operands from either memory location, and the choice affects how the data must be laid out and addressed. The local softmax computation, which computes a running maximum and running sum to avoid materializing the full $N \times N$ attention matrix, is the same algorithmic innovation from the original FlashAttention, but here it is integrated into the warp-specialized, asynchronously pipelined execution model.

## Execution Flow: How TMA Asynchrony Avoids Stalling and Fills the Pipeline Buffer

To understand the performance benefit of the warp-specialized design, it is instructive to trace the execution flow of the algorithm and observe how TMA asynchrony prevents stalling. When the kernel launches, both producer and consumer warpgroups begin executing concurrently. The producer immediately issues a TMA load for $\mathbf{Q}_i$. Crucially, issuing a TMA instruction does not stall the issuing thread; the TMA hardware unit begins the data transfer in the background, and the thread continues to the next instruction. The producer then enters its main loop and begins issuing TMA loads for $\mathbf{K}_0, \mathbf{V}_0, \mathbf{K}_1, \mathbf{V}_1, \ldots$ in sequence, each time committing to the pipeline upon completion.

During the first $s$ iterations of the producer's loop, no wait statements will actually block, because all $s$ stages of the circular buffer are initially free. This is the pipeline filling phase: the producer races ahead, loading up to $s$ tiles of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ into the buffer without waiting for the consumer to catch up. Meanwhile, the consumer is waiting for $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and then $\mathbf{K}_0$ to appear in shared memory. Once the first tiles arrive, the consumer begins its computation. From this point onward, the pipeline enters steady state: the producer continues to load future tiles while the consumer processes current ones. The TMA asynchrony ensures that the producer is never blocked waiting for a single load to complete before issuing the next one; it simply issues loads back-to-back and lets the TMA hardware manage the actual data transfers.

The consumer's execution is also designed to avoid unnecessary stalling. Within each iteration, the consumer waits for $\mathbf{K}_j$ in shared memory (which the producer has already loaded ahead of time if the pipeline is full), performs the first GEMM, does the softmax, waits for $\mathbf{V}_j$ (which was loaded in parallel with the first GEMM and softmax by the producer), and performs the second GEMM. The overlap between the producer's loading of $\mathbf{V}_j$ and the consumer's computation of the first GEMM and softmax is a direct consequence of the multi-stage buffer and the asynchronous TMA: the producer can load $\mathbf{V}_j$ into one stage of the buffer while the consumer reads $\mathbf{K}_j$ from another stage.

If the consumer is faster than the producer (computation finishes before the next data is ready), the consumer will stall waiting for the next buffer stage to be filled, and the bottleneck is memory bandwidth. If the producer is faster than the consumer (data arrives before the consumer is ready), the producer will stall waiting for a buffer stage to be released, and the bottleneck is compute throughput. In either case, the overlap between data movement and computation ensures that both the TMA and the Tensor Cores are utilized as much as possible, limited only by the slower of the two. This is in contrast to a non-warp-specialized design, where the same warps must alternate between issuing loads and performing computation, leading to serial execution where the TMA sits idle during computation and the Tensor Cores sit idle during loading.

The warp-specialization scheme with the circular SMEM buffer thus serves as the foundation for FlashAttention-3's performance improvements. By decoupling data movement from computation at the warp level and using barrier synchronization to coordinate access to a multi-stage buffer, the algorithm achieves significant overlap between memory accesses and compute, bringing the GPU's utilization from FlashAttention-2's 35% on the H100 closer to the 75% achieved by the full FlashAttention-3 system. This warp-specialization foundation also enables the subsequent optimization of overlapping softmax with GEMMs within the consumer warpgroups, which further pushes utilization even higher.

# Pingpong Scheduling: Overlapping GEMMs Across Warpgroups

# Throughput Asymmetry: 989 TFLOPS FP16 Matmul vs. 3.9 TFLOPS Special Functions on H100

Modern GPUs are throughput processors that achieve high performance through concurrency and asynchrony, but not all computational operations enjoy the same level of hardware acceleration. On the NVIDIA H100 SXM5 GPU, there is a dramatic asymmetry between the throughput available for matrix multiplication and the throughput available for special functions such as the exponential. Specifically, the H100 delivers 989 TFLOPS of FP16 matrix multiplication, powered by dedicated hardware units called Tensor Cores. In stark contrast, the same GPU delivers only 3.9 TFLOPS for special functions like the exponential operation, which is critical for computing softmax in attention. This asymmetry is not incidental; it is a direct consequence of hardware specialization, in which the GPU dedicates specialized silicon to the most common and computationally expensive operations in machine learning workloads. Tensor Cores are purpose-built accelerators for matrix multiplication, while special functions are handled by a separate, less parallelized hardware unit known as the multi-function unit.

The 3.9 TFLOPS figure for special functions can be derived from the CUDA programming guide, which specifies that 16 operations of special functions can be performed per streaming multiprocessor (SM) per clock cycle. The H100 SXM5 has 132 SMs and a boost clock speed of 1830 MHz, so multiplying these together yields $16 \times 132 \times 1830 \text{ MHz} \approx 3.9$ TFLOPS. This is approximately 256 times lower than the FP16 matmul throughput of 989 TFLOPS. Understanding this asymmetry is essential for designing efficient attention algorithms, because the attention computation involves both GEMMs (handled by Tensor Cores) and softmax (which requires exponentials handled by the multi-function unit). If these operations are executed sequentially without overlap, the lower-throughput special functions become a significant bottleneck, leaving the high-throughput Tensor Cores idle for a substantial fraction of the total execution time.

# The Exponential Bottleneck in Softmax: Why It Dominates Non-GEMM Time

The softmax operation is a core component of attention, applied row-wise to the score matrix $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$. To compute softmax, one must evaluate the exponential function $\exp(\cdot)$ for each element of the (locally adjusted) score matrix. This is in addition to other non-matmul operations such as row-wise maximum, subtraction, row-wise summation, and division. Among these, the exponential is the most computationally expensive because it is a transcendental function that must be evaluated by the multi-function unit, which has dramatically lower throughput than the Tensor Cores.

To quantify the impact, consider the attention forward pass in FP16 with head dimension 128. In this setting, there are 512 times more matmul FLOPs compared to exponential operations. However, since the exponential has 256 times lower throughput than matmul, the exponential operations can consume up to 50% of the total cycle time compared to matmul. This means that even though the exponential represents a tiny fraction of the total FLOPs, its low throughput causes it to dominate the non-GEMM portion of the execution time. The remaining non-GEMM operations, such as floating point multiply-add for rescaling and row reductions, also run on the CUDA cores at lower throughput than Tensor Cores, but the exponential is particularly severe because it requires the multi-function unit.

The key insight is that the exponential is performed by a separate hardware unit from the Tensor Cores. This means that, in principle, the two operations can be executed concurrently: the Tensor Cores can be performing matrix multiplication while the multi-function unit is evaluating exponentials for the softmax. Without explicit overlap, however, the standard execution model would serialize these operations, leaving one hardware unit idle while the other is active. The FlashAttention-3 algorithm is designed precisely to exploit this concurrency opportunity.

# The Case for FP8: Doubling Matmul Throughput While Exponential Stays Constant

The throughput disparity between matmul and special functions becomes even more severe when moving to lower-precision number formats. On Hopper GPUs, the FP8 Tensor Cores deliver twice the throughput of FP16 or BF16 Tensor Cores for matrix multiplication. The WGMMA instruction can target FP8 Tensor Cores to achieve this 2x throughput improvement per SM. However, the throughput of special functions such as the exponential does not change with the precision of the GEMM operands, because these operations are performed on the softmax intermediate results, which remain in FP32 regardless of the input precision.

In the FP16 setting with head dimension 128, the exponential could take up to 50% of the cycle time compared to matmul. With FP8, the matmul throughput doubles to approximately 1978 TFLOPS, but the exponential throughput remains at 3.9 TFLOPS. The ratio of matmul FLOPs to exponential FLOPs stays the same (since it depends on the algorithm, not the precision), but now the throughput gap widens from 256x to 512x. As a result, the exponential would take an even larger fraction of the total time if operations are not overlapped. Specifically, without overlap, the exponential could take up to 100% of the matmul time in the FP8 setting, effectively halving the achievable utilization.

This observation makes a compelling case for techniques that overlap softmax (and its exponential operations) with GEMM execution. If the exponential can be scheduled to run concurrently with GEMMs on the Tensor Cores, then the effective throughput of the non-GEMM operations becomes less of a bottleneck, because they are hidden behind the GEMM latency. This is the fundamental motivation behind the pingpong scheduling technique described in the following sections.

# Pingpong Scheduling: Using bar.sync to Alternate GEMM and Softmax Between Two Consumer Warpgroups

The pingpong scheduling technique builds on the warp-specialization scheme of FlashAttention-3. In the warp-specialized design, the warps within a CTA (cooperative thread array) are divided into producer and consumer roles. Producer warps are responsible for data movement, issuing TMA (Tensor Memory Accelerator) loads to bring tiles of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ from HBM to shared memory. Consumer warps are responsible for computation, issuing WGMMA instructions to perform the GEMMs and CUDA core instructions to perform the softmax. On Hopper, there are typically two consumer warpgroups (each consisting of 4 contiguous warps, i.e., 128 threads), and the pingpong scheduling exploits the presence of these two consumer warpgroups.

The core idea of pingpong scheduling is to stagger the execution phases of the two consumer warpgroups such that when one warpgroup is performing softmax (which uses the multi-function unit and CUDA cores), the other warpgroup is performing GEMMs (which uses the Tensor Cores). Since these two hardware units are independent, both can operate simultaneously, effectively hiding the low-throughput softmax behind the high-throughput matmul. To achieve this, the algorithm uses synchronization barriers (\texttt{bar.sync} instructions) to enforce a specific ordering of GEMM operations across the two warpgroups.

Specifically, the barriers are used to force the GEMMs of warpgroup 1 to be scheduled before the GEMMs of warpgroup 2. The GEMMs in the attention forward pass consist of two matmuls per inner loop iteration: GEMM0, which computes $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, and GEMM1, which computes $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{V}$. The barriers ensure that GEMM1 of one iteration from warpgroup 1 and GEMM0 of the next iteration from warpgroup 1 are both scheduled before the corresponding GEMMs of warpgroup 2. As a result, while warpgroup 1's GEMMs are executing on the Tensor Cores, warpgroup 2's softmax is executing on the multi-function unit and CUDA cores. Then the roles swap: warpgroup 2 performs its GEMMs while warpgroup 1 performs its softmax. This alternating pattern is what gives the technique its name, "pingpong" scheduling, as the two warpgroups alternate between GEMM and softmax phases like players in a table tennis match.

The technical mechanism relies on the asynchronous nature of WGMMA on Hopper. Unlike prior architectures where Tensor Core operations were synchronous, Hopper's WGMMA instruction is asynchronous: it is issued and then the issuing thread can continue executing other instructions while the Tensor Cores process the matmul. A subsequent wait or commit operation is used to synchronize with the completion of the WGMMA. This asynchrony is what allows the barriers to enforce ordering without stalling the entire CTA, because a warpgroup can issue its WGMMA and then proceed to other work (such as softmax) while the Tensor Cores are still computing.

# Illustration of the Pingpong Execution Timeline

The pingpong execution timeline can be understood by tracing the operations of the two consumer warpgroups over successive iterations of the main loop. Consider two consumer warpgroups, $W_1$ and $W_2$. In the idealized timeline, the execution proceeds as follows.

In the first phase, $W_1$ issues its GEMM0 (computing $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ for the current iteration) and GEMM1 (computing $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{V}$ for the previous iteration) on the Tensor Cores. The \texttt{bar.sync} instruction ensures that $W_2$ does not issue its GEMMs until $W_1$ has issued its GEMMs. While $W_1$'s GEMMs are executing on the Tensor Cores, $W_2$ performs its softmax operations, which include computing the row-wise maximum, evaluating the exponential, computing the row-wise sum, and rescaling the output accumulator $\mathbf{O}$. These softmax operations use the multi-function unit and CUDA cores, which are independent of the Tensor Cores.

In the second phase, the roles reverse. $W_2$ issues its GEMM0 and GEMM1 on the Tensor Cores (after $W_1$ has issued its GEMMs, as enforced by the barrier). While $W_2$'s GEMMs are executing, $W_1$ performs its softmax operations. This alternation continues for each iteration of the main loop, creating a steady-state pipeline where the Tensor Cores and the multi-function unit are both utilized concurrently.

It is important to note that the actual execution in practice is not as clean as the idealized description. The compiler (NVCC) may rearrange instructions, and the actual overlap depends on the relative latencies of the GEMM and softmax operations. Nevertheless, the general pattern of alternation between the two warpgroups holds, and the concurrent use of Tensor Cores and multi-function units provides a meaningful performance improvement. The figure referenced in the paper (Figure illustrating pingpong scheduling) depicts this idealized timeline, showing the staggered GEMM and softmax phases of the two warpgroups.

# Performance Impact: Moving from 570 to 620-640 TFLOPS for FP16 Forward Pass

The empirical impact of pingpong scheduling is significant. Without the pingpong overlap, the baseline warp-specialized implementation of FlashAttention-3 (using only producer-consumer asynchrony without overlapping GEMM and softmax across consumer warpgroups) achieves approximately 570 TFLOPS for the FP16 forward pass with head dimension 128 and sequence length 8192. With the addition of pingpong scheduling, this performance improves to 620-640 TFLOPS, representing a 9-12% improvement in throughput.

The ablation study in the paper further confirms the contribution of these algorithmic improvements. The combination of warp-specialization and GEMM-softmax pipelining (including both the intra-warpgroup 2-stage pipelining and the inter-warpgroup pingpong scheduling) leads to a speedup from 570 to 661 TFLOPS for the specific configuration of batch size 4, sequence length 8448, 16 heads, and head dimension 128. This confirms that the asynchronous execution techniques, of which pingpong scheduling is a key component, provide measurable and significant performance gains.

The fact that the improvement is not larger can be attributed to several factors. First, the pingpong scheduling is not as clean in practice as in the idealized model, because the compiler may reorder instructions and the actual latencies may not perfectly align. Second, there are other overheads in the algorithm, such as register pressure and barrier synchronization costs, that partially offset the gains from overlapping. Third, the softmax operations include not only the exponential but also other non-GEMM operations (such as row reductions and rescaling) that may not all be perfectly hidden behind the GEMMs.

Despite these practical limitations, the pingpong scheduling technique demonstrates that careful exploitation of hardware asynchrony, specifically the independence of Tensor Cores and the multi-function unit, can yield meaningful performance improvements for attention computation. This technique, combined with the intra-warpgroup 2-stage pipelining that overlaps WGMMA with softmax within a single warpgroup, enables FlashAttention-3 to achieve up to 740 TFLOPS (75% utilization) on the H100 GPU for FP16, and close to 1.2 PFLOPS for FP8, representing a 1.5-2.0x speedup over FlashAttention-2.

# Intra-Warpgroup Overlapping: 2-Stage GEMM-Softmax Pipelining

## Sequential Dependencies in the Attention Inner Loop

To understand why overlapping GEMM and softmax operations within a single warpgroup is challenging, we must first examine the inherent sequential dependencies that exist in the attention computation's inner loop. Recall from the FlashAttention algorithm that for each query tile $\mathbf{Q}_i$, the algorithm iterates over blocks of the key and value matrices, $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$, performing two matrix multiplications (GEMMs) interleaved with a local softmax computation in each iteration $j$.

The first GEMM, which we call GEMM0, computes the attention scores $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^T$. This matrix product captures the similarity between the query tile and the current key tile. The output $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}$ is immediately consumed by the softmax operation, which must compute the row-wise maximum $m_i$, the exponentiated scores $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} = \exp(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} - m_i)$, and the row sums $\ell_i$ needed for normalization. The softmax operation cannot begin until GEMM0 completes, because it needs the full matrix of scores to compute the row-wise statistics.

The second GEMM, which we call GEMM1, then computes the partial output update $\mathbf{O}_i = \mathrm{diag}(\exp(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i))^{-1} \mathbf{O}_i + \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$. This operation takes the exponentiated and rescaled scores $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$ as its left operand and the value tile $\mathbf{V}_j$ as its right operand. Therefore, GEMM1 depends on the softmax computation, which in turn depends on GEMM0. This creates a chain of dependencies: GEMM0 $\rightarrow$ softmax $\rightarrow$ GEMM1, all within a single iteration of the inner loop.

In the baseline warp-specialized algorithm presented in Algorithm 1 of the paper, these dependencies are enforced by explicit "commit and wait" statements after each GEMM. Specifically, after computing $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^T$ on line 17, the algorithm commits and waits, meaning it blocks until the WGMMA instruction completes and the result is available in registers. Only then does the softmax computation proceed on lines 18 and 19. Similarly, after computing the output update on line 22, the algorithm again commits and waits, serializing the execution before the next iteration begins. These wait statements are the synchronization barriers that prevent any overlap between the GEMMs and the softmax within a single iteration, effectively forcing the three stages to execute one after another in lockstep.

The fundamental problem is that while the Tensor Cores are performing the matrix multiplication for GEMM0, the CUDA cores and multi-function units that handle the softmax computation sit idle. Conversely, while the softmax is being computed, the Tensor Cores sit idle. This is particularly wasteful given the stark throughput asymmetry between these hardware units: on the H100 SXM5 GPU, FP16 matrix multiply achieves 989 TFLOPS, while special function operations such as the exponential (needed for softmax) achieve only 3.9 TFLOPS. For attention with head dimension 128, the exponential operations have 256 times lower throughput than matmul, meaning they can consume up to 50% of the total cycle time despite representing far fewer floating-point operations. With FP8, the situation worsens because matmul throughput doubles while the exponential throughput remains unchanged.

## Breaking Dependencies Through Cross-Iteration Pipelining with Register Buffers

The key insight that enables intra-warpgroup overlapping is that while dependencies within a single iteration are unavoidable, one can break the serialization by pipelining across iterations. The idea is analogous to a factory assembly line: rather than waiting for a complete product to pass through all stations before starting the next one, multiple products are in various stages of completion simultaneously. In the context of the attention algorithm, this means that while iteration $j$ is performing its softmax computation, the GEMMs of iteration $j+1$ can be initiated concurrently, provided we maintain separate buffers to hold the intermediate results of each stage.

To implement this cross-iteration pipelining, the algorithm introduces additional register buffers to hold the intermediate scores matrix for the "next" iteration while the "current" iteration's softmax and GEMM1 are still in progress. Specifically, instead of maintaining a single buffer $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ for the attention scores, we introduce a second buffer $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$. When the first GEMM of iteration $j+1$ computes $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_{j}^T$, it writes its output to this separate buffer, which does not conflict with the softmax computation that is operating on $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ from iteration $j$. Similarly, the exponentiated scores $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ and $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{next}}$ are kept in separate register buffers.

The critical enabler for this scheme is the asynchronous nature of the WGMMA instruction on Hopper GPUs. Unlike earlier architectures where matrix multiply instructions were synchronous, the WGMMA instruction on Hopper can be issued and then left to complete in the background. The algorithm can issue a WGMMA, commit it (signaling that the instruction has been launched), but deliberately choose not to wait for its completion. This allows the warpgroup to proceed with other work — specifically, the softmax computation — while the Tensor Cores are still performing the matrix multiplication. Only later, when the result is actually needed, does the algorithm issue a wait to ensure the WGMMA has completed.

By maintaining two sets of buffers — one for the "current" iteration and one for the "next" iteration — the algorithm creates a two-stage pipeline where the GEMM1 of iteration $j$ overlaps with the softmax of iteration $j+1$, and the GEMM0 of iteration $j+1$ overlaps with the GEMM1 and softmax of iteration $j$. This pipelining across iterations is what breaks the sequential dependency chain that would otherwise force each iteration to complete fully before the next one begins.

## The 2-Stage Algorithm: Overlapping GEMM1 of Iteration $j$ with Softmax of Iteration $j+1$

The two-stage GEMM-softmax pipelining algorithm replaces the consumer mainloop of the warp-specialized algorithm with an overlapped version. To understand its structure, let us trace through the execution flow of Algorithm 2 from the paper.

The algorithm begins with a prologue that handles the zeroth iteration without overlap, establishing the initial state. It first waits for $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and $\mathbf{K}_0$ to be loaded into shared memory, then computes $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_0^T$ using WGMMA. After committing and waiting for this first GEMM to complete, it releases the shared memory buffer stage used for $\mathbf{K}_0$ and proceeds to compute the initial softmax statistics: the row-wise maximum $m_i$, the exponentiated scores $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}}$, and the normalization accumulator $\ell_i$, based on $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$. It also rescales the output accumulator $\mathbf{O}_i$ accordingly.

The main loop then runs from $j = 1$ to $j = T_c - 2$, where $T_c$ is the number of key/value blocks. In each iteration $j$, the algorithm performs the following steps in a carefully orchestrated sequence. First, it waits for $\mathbf{K}_j$ to be loaded into shared memory. Then it issues the first WGMMA to compute $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^T$ and commits it but does not wait for completion. This is the crucial "fire and forget" step that allows the Tensor Cores to begin working on the next iteration's GEMM0 while the warpgroup moves on to other work.

Next, the algorithm waits for $\mathbf{V}_{j-1}$ to be loaded into shared memory and issues the second WGMMA to compute $\mathbf{O}_i = \mathbf{O}_i + \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}} \mathbf{V}_{j-1}$. Again, it commits but does not wait. At this point, both the GEMM0 of iteration $j$ and the GEMM1 of iteration $j-1$ are executing asynchronously on the Tensor Cores. The algorithm then waits specifically for the GEMM0 of iteration $j$ (the $\mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^T$ computation) to complete, so that $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ is available in registers. It then performs the softmax computation on $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$, computing the updated $m_i$, $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{next}}$, and $\ell_i$. During this softmax computation, the GEMM1 of iteration $j-1$ (the $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}} \mathbf{V}_{j-1}$ computation) is still running on the Tensor Cores, thus achieving the desired overlap.

After the softmax completes, the algorithm waits for the GEMM1 of iteration $j-1$ to finish, rescales $\mathbf{O}_i$ to account for the updated running maximum $m_i$, and releases the shared memory buffer stages for both $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_{j-1}$. Finally, it copies $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ to $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ and $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{next}}$ to $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}}$, effectively advancing the pipeline by one iteration. The loop then repeats with the roles of "current" and "next" swapped.

After the main loop completes, the algorithm handles the final iteration as an epilogue. It waits for the last value tile $\mathbf{V}_{T_c - 1}$ to be loaded, computes the final output update $\mathbf{O}_i = \mathbf{O}_i + \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{last}} \mathbf{V}_{T_c - 1}$ using WGMMA, and commits and waits for completion. It then performs the final rescaling of $\mathbf{O}_i$ based on the accumulated maximum $m_i$, computes the log-sum-exp $L_i = m_i + \log(\ell_i)$, and writes both $\mathbf{O}_i$ and $L_i$ to HBM.

## Detailed Pseudocode for the Consumer Warpgroup Forward Pass

The full pseudocode for the two-stage pipelined consumer warpgroup forward pass is presented as Algorithm 2 in the paper. It serves as a drop-in replacement for the consumer path of the warp-specialized algorithm (Algorithm 1), with the producer warpgroup handling TMA loads remaining unchanged. Together, the producer-consumer warp-specialization and the two-stage intra-warpgroup pipelining comprise the complete FlashAttention-3 algorithm for FP16 precision.

The algorithm assumes that the matrices $\mathbf{Q}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{B_r \times d}$ and $\mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ are in HBM, with key block size $B_c$ yielding $T_c = \lceil \frac{N}{B_c} \rceil$ blocks. The consumer warpgroup begins by reallocating a predetermined number of registers based on the number of consumer warps, using the \texttt{setmaxnreg} instruction on Hopper. This register reallocation is important because the consumer warpgroup, which performs the WGMMA instructions, benefits from having a larger share of the register file than the producer warpgroup, which only needs enough registers to issue TMA loads.

The on-chip state is initialized as $\mathbf{O}_i = (0) \in \mathbb{R}^{B_r \times d}$, $\ell_i = (0) \in \mathbb{R}^{B_r}$, and $m_i = (-\infty) \in \mathbb{R}^{B_r}$. The zeroth iteration is then handled as a prologue: waiting for $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and $\mathbf{K}_0$ in shared memory, computing $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_0^T$ via WGMMA (commit and wait), releasing the buffer stage for $\mathbf{K}$, and computing the initial softmax statistics from $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ while rescaling $\mathbf{O}_i$.

The main loop (lines 8 through 20 in the algorithm) executes the overlapped pipeline as described in the previous section. The key lines to understand are lines 9 and 11, where the WGMMA instructions are issued with "commit but do not wait" semantics. Line 9 computes $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^T$ and commits without waiting, launching the GEMM0 for iteration $j$ asynchronously. Line 11 computes $\mathbf{O}_i = \mathbf{O}_i + \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}} \mathbf{V}_{j-1}$ and also commits without waiting, launching the GEMM1 for iteration $j-1$ asynchronously. Between these two commits and their corresponding waits, the softmax computation on line 13 executes, achieving the overlap between non-GEMM and GEMM operations.

## Role of Asynchronous WGMMA: Commit Without Wait to Enable Overlap

The asynchronous nature of the WGMMA instruction on Hopper GPUs is the technological foundation that makes the two-stage pipeline possible. To understand why, consider what would happen if WGMMA were synchronous, as the matrix multiply instructions were on earlier architectures such as Ampere. With a synchronous instruction, each GEMM would block the issuing warp until the result is ready in registers. The algorithm would have no choice but to execute GEMM0, then softmax, then GEMM1 in strict sequence, exactly as in the baseline warp-specialized algorithm. There would be no opportunity to overlap the Tensor Core computation with the CUDA core computation, because the warp issuing the GEMM would be stalled until the GEMM completes.

The WGMMA instruction on Hopper, however, is designed for asynchronous execution. When a warpgroup issues a WGMMA instruction, the instruction is dispatched to the Tensor Cores, and control returns to the warpgroup immediately. The Tensor Cores then execute the matrix multiply in the background while the warpgroup is free to perform other work. The "commit" operation signals that the WGMMA has been properly launched and its input operands are committed, but it does not block. The "wait" operation, issued later, is what blocks the warpgroup until the Tensor Cores have finished the computation and the result is available in the register file.

This asynchronous dispatch model is what allows the two-stage algorithm to issue two WGMMA instructions back-to-back without waiting for either to complete, and then proceed to perform the softmax computation on the CUDA cores while both GEMMs are executing on the Tensor Cores. The algorithm only waits when the result of a particular GEMM is actually needed — for example, when the softmax needs the scores matrix $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$, it waits for the GEMM0 that produces it. By carefully ordering the commits, waits, and non-GEMM operations, the algorithm ensures that the Tensor Cores are kept busy with GEMMs while the CUDA cores simultaneously handle the softmax computation, maximizing the utilization of both hardware units.

It is worth noting that the term "WGMMA" in the algorithm pseudocode serves as a metonym for asynchronous GEMM more generally. While the specific implementation targets the Hopper architecture's WGMMA instruction, the algorithmic principle of using asynchronous matrix multiply to overlap with non-matmul computation is operative for any GPU architecture with sufficiently robust asynchronous execution capabilities.

## Practical Consideration 1: Compiler Reordering and SASS Verification

The pseudocode in Algorithm 2 represents an idealized execution order that achieves the maximum possible overlap between GEMM and softmax operations. However, in practice, the compiler (NVCC, NVIDIA's CUDA compiler) may rearrange instructions for optimization purposes, potentially disrupting the carefully crafted pipeline sequence. The compiler's instruction scheduling passes analyze dependencies between instructions and may reorder them to improve register usage, reduce stalls, or take advantage of instruction-level parallelism. While this is generally beneficial for ordinary code, it can be problematic for algorithms like the two-stage pipeline where the ordering of WGMMA commits, waits, and non-GEMM operations is precisely tuned for a specific overlap pattern.

For example, the compiler might decide to move a wait instruction earlier, collapsing the window during which the softmax can overlap with the GEMM, or it might reorder register operations in a way that inadvertently introduces a dependency that serializes the pipeline. Such reordering could lead to unexpected behavior or diminished performance gains, as the carefully constructed overlap between Tensor Core and CUDA core computation is broken.

To verify that the compiler actually generates the expected overlapped code, the authors analyze the SASS (Source Assembly) code — the low-level assembly that NVCC produces after all optimization passes. The SASS analysis, presented in Section 3.2 of the paper, confirms that the compiler generates overlapped code as intended, with WGMMA and non-WGMMA instructions interleaved in the pattern prescribed by the algorithm. This verification is important because it demonstrates that the theoretical pipeline structure is preserved through the compilation process and that the expected performance gains are realized in practice.

## Practical Consideration 2: Register Pressure from Extra $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ Buffer

The two-stage pipeline requires additional register storage to maintain the intermediate results needed for cross-iteration overlap. Specifically, the algorithm must keep an extra copy of the scores matrix, $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$, in registers while the current iteration's softmax and GEMM1 are still in progress. This extra buffer occupies $B_r \times B_c \times \text{sizeof}(\text{float})$ bytes per threadblock, where $B_r$ is the query block size (number of rows in the query tile) and $B_c$ is the key block size (number of rows in the key tile). Since each score entry is stored as a 32-bit float (FP32), the additional register demand can be substantial, especially for larger block sizes.

Register pressure is a critical concern in GPU kernel design because each streaming multiprocessor has a limited register file. On Hopper, each thread can access at most 256 registers, and the total register file is shared among all threads running on the SM. When a kernel uses more registers per thread, fewer threads can be co-scheduled on the same SM, reducing the occupancy and potentially hurting the kernel's ability to hide memory and instruction latencies. If the register demand exceeds the available capacity, the compiler will spill registers to local memory (which resides in slow global memory), severely degrading performance.

The additional $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ buffer creates a tension between the pipeline depth and the block size. Larger block sizes for $B_r$ and $B_c$ are generally desirable because they increase the ratio of useful computation to overhead (such as softmax statistics and boundary handling) and improve the efficiency of the Tensor Cores. However, larger blocks also require more registers for both the GEMM operands and the pipeline buffers. The two-stage pipeline's extra $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ buffer exacerbates this tension, as it directly increases register usage in proportion to the block sizes.

In practice, the authors note that trade-offs must be made based on profiling results. The optimal block size and pipeline depth depend on the specific GPU architecture, the head dimension, and the sequence length. The \texttt{setmaxnreg} instruction on Hopper, which dynamically reallocates registers between warpgroups, provides some flexibility in managing this tension, as the consumer warpgroup can be given a larger share of the register file at the expense of the producer warpgroup. Nonetheless, careful tuning is required to ensure that register spilling is minimized while maintaining sufficient block sizes for Tensor Core efficiency.

## Practical Consideration 3: 3-Stage Pipelining Extension and Its Trade-offs

The two-stage pipeline described above overlaps the GEMM1 of one iteration with the softmax of the next. A natural question is whether the overlap can be extended further to hide even more of the softmax computation behind GEMMs. The authors propose a three-stage variant that would additionally overlap the second GEMM with the softmax from a subsequent iteration, creating a deeper pipeline with more concurrent operations.

In the three-stage pipeline, additional register buffers are needed to hold intermediate results for yet another iteration ahead. While the two-stage pipeline maintains buffers for $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ and $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$, the three-stage pipeline would require a third buffer, say $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next2}}$, to hold the scores for the iteration after next. This further increases the register pressure discussed in the previous section, making the trade-off between tile size and pipeline depth even more difficult to balance.

The potential benefit of the three-stage pipeline is higher Tensor Core utilization, as more of the softmax computation can be hidden behind the GEMMs. However, the authors note that this comes at the cost of significantly more register usage, which may force smaller block sizes and thus reduce the efficiency of each individual GEMM. The net performance effect depends on whether the increased overlap outweighs the reduced GEMM efficiency from smaller blocks. The authors provide a detailed description of the three-stage algorithm and its evaluation results in Section B.2 of the appendix, indicating that the three-stage approach is feasible but requires careful tuning to achieve net gains.

The authors also note that the number of stages in the overlapping scheme is bounded by, but need not equal, the number $s$ of stages in the circular shared memory buffer. The circular buffer is used by the producer warpgroup to pipeline TMA loads of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ blocks, and its depth $s$ determines how many loads can be in flight simultaneously. The GEMM-softmax pipeline stages operate on data that has already been loaded into shared memory, so the pipeline depth is constrained by the availability of loaded data but is otherwise independent of $s$. This separation of concerns — the shared memory buffer managed by the producer and the register pipeline managed by the consumer — is a key design principle that allows each aspect of the algorithm to be tuned independently.

In summary, the two-stage GEMM-softmax pipelining represents a careful balance between the performance gains from overlapping computation and the resource costs of maintaining additional pipeline stages. The three-stage extension pushes this balance further, offering the potential for higher Tensor Core utilization at the expense of increased register pressure, and its viability depends on the specific hardware configuration and workload characteristics.

# FP8 FlashAttention-3: Layout Conformance and In-Kernel Transpose

The transition from FP16 to FP8 precision in FlashAttention-3 introduces a host of challenges that go beyond simply changing the data type of the operands. While FP8 Tensor Cores on the Hopper architecture can deliver twice the throughput of their FP16 counterparts, exploiting this capability within the fused attention kernel requires navigating a labyrinth of layout constraints imposed by the WGMMA (warpgroup matrix multiply-accumulate) instruction. Unlike FP16 WGMMA, which is relatively permissive about the memory layout of its input operands, FP8 WGMMA enforces strict conformance rules that conflict with the natural data arrangements found in attention computation. This chapter dissects these constraints and the techniques developed in FlashAttention-3 to overcome them, focusing on the two central problems: the need to transpose the $\mathbf{V}$ matrix tiles and the need to reconcile the mismatched register layouts of FP32 accumulators and FP8 operands when chaining two back-to-back GEMMs in a single kernel.

## FP8 WGMMA k-Major Constraint: Why V Needs to Be Contiguous in Sequence Length

To understand the layout challenge, we must first recall the structure of attention computation. The forward pass of attention consists of two matrix multiplications performed in sequence: the first computes the score matrix $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$, and the second computes the output matrix $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P} \mathbf{V}$, where $\mathbf{P}$ is the softmax of $\mathbf{S}$. In the tiled algorithm of FlashAttention-3, these are performed block by block: for each block $\mathbf{Q}_i$, the kernel iterates over blocks $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$, computing $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$ and then accumulating $\mathbf{O}_i += \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$.

The WGMMA instruction on Hopper, which performs these GEMM operations asynchronously on the Tensor Cores, imposes specific layout requirements on its input operands depending on their precision. The paper defines the concept of "mn-major" and "k-major" layouts: given a GEMM call to multiply $A \times B^\top$ for an $M \times K$-matrix $A$ and an $N \times K$-matrix $B$, the $A$ or $B$ operand is said to be mn-major if it is contiguous in the outer $M$ or $N$ dimension, and k-major if it is contiguous in the inner $K$-dimension. For FP16 WGMMA, both mn-major and k-major input operands are accepted when the operands reside in shared memory (SMEM). This flexibility means that FP16 FlashAttention-3 does not need to worry about the contiguous dimension of its input tiles.

For FP8 WGMMA, however, only the k-major format is supported. This is a critical restriction. Let us examine its implications for each GEMM in attention. In the first GEMM, $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$, the inner reduction dimension is the head dimension $d$. The input tensors $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ are typically stored with the head dimension $d$ as the contiguous (innermost) dimension, which naturally satisfies the k-major requirement. The second GEMM, $\mathbf{O}_i += \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$, has $\mathbf{V}_j$ as the second operand. Here, the inner reduction dimension for $\mathbf{V}_j$ (playing the role of $B$ in the GEMM) is also the head dimension $d$. But $\mathbf{V}$, like $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$, is typically stored with the head dimension $d$ as the contiguous dimension. This means $\mathbf{V}$ is contiguous in the $d$ dimension, which corresponds to the $K$-dimension of the GEMM — but wait, we need to be more careful about the layout convention.

Let us think about this more precisely. In the second GEMM, $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$, the matrix $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$ has shape $B_r \times B_c$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$ has shape $B_c \times d$. The inner dimension is $B_c$ (the sequence length block size). For the WGMMA instruction, the $A$ operand is $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$ and the $B$ operand is $\mathbf{V}$. The k-major constraint requires that the operands be contiguous in the inner $K$-dimension. For the $B$ operand ($\mathbf{V}$), the inner dimension is $d$ (the head dimension), but the data is stored contiguous in $d$. However, the paper states that for the second GEMM, $\mathbf{V}$ (or rather the tiles of $\mathbf{V}$ loaded into SMEM) needs to be contiguous in the sequence length dimension. This is because in the GEMM $A \times B^\top$, $\mathbf{V}$ plays the role of $B$, and $B$ has shape $N \times K$, so the $K$-dimension for $\mathbf{V}$ is the sequence length dimension $N$ (or $B_c$ at the block level). But the natural storage of $\mathbf{V}$ has the head dimension $d$ as the contiguous dimension, not the sequence length. Therefore, to satisfy the k-major constraint for FP8 WGMMA in the second GEMM, $\mathbf{V}$ tiles must be transposed so that they become contiguous in the sequence length dimension rather than the head dimension.

This is the fundamental layout problem: the natural storage format of $\mathbf{V}$ does not conform to the k-major requirement of FP8 WGMMA for the second GEMM in attention. Unlike FP16, where the WGMMA instruction accepts both mn-major and k-major layouts, FP8 leaves no room for flexibility. The algorithm must somehow produce $\mathbf{V}$ tiles in the correct layout before they can be fed into the second FP8 WGMMA.

## Option 1: Pre-Processing Transpose in GMEM and Its Limitations

One natural approach to solving the layout conformance problem is to transpose the entire $\mathbf{V}$ matrix in global memory (GMEM/HBM) before the attention kernel executes. If $\mathbf{V}$ is stored such that the sequence length dimension $N$ is contiguous and the head dimension $d$ is the stride dimension, then tiles of $\mathbf{V}$ loaded by TMA into SMEM would automatically be contiguous in the sequence length dimension, satisfying the k-major constraint without any in-kernel manipulation. The paper describes two sub-options for implementing this pre-processing transpose.

The first sub-option, denoted (1a), is to fuse the transpose operation into the epilogue of a preceding step in the model, such as the rotary embedding computation. Rotary embeddings are applied to $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ before attention, and their output writes $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ to GMEM. In principle, one could also transpose $\mathbf{V}$ during this write-back, producing a transposed $\mathbf{V}$ tensor in GMEM as a side effect of the rotary embedding kernel. This would incur essentially no additional cost since the rotary embedding is memory-bandwidth bound — the GPU spends most of its time moving data between GMEM and SMEM, not computing — and adding a transpose to the store operation would not increase the number of memory transactions. However, the paper notes that this approach is difficult to integrate into a standard library. The rotary embedding kernel and the attention kernel are typically separate operations in a deep learning framework, and fusing a transpose into the epilogue of rotary embedding creates a tight coupling between two otherwise independent operations. This coupling complicates the software stack, as the rotary embedding kernel would need to know that its output will be consumed by an FP8 attention kernel and would need to produce $\mathbf{V}$ in a transposed layout. Such cross-kernel dependencies are fragile and hard to maintain in a general-purpose library like PyTorch or Hugging Face.

The second sub-option, denoted (1b), is to call a standalone pre-processing transpose kernel that reads $\mathbf{V}$ from GMEM, transposes it, and writes it back to GMEM in the transposed layout. The paper references the fact that an optimized transpose kernel can achieve speeds near the bandwidth of the device, meaning the kernel itself is efficient. However, the problem with this approach is that it introduces an additional pass over the $\mathbf{V}$ data. In certain situations, such as LLM inference, the computation is already memory-bandwidth bound: the GPU spends most of its time reading data from GMEM, and the attention computation itself is relatively light compared to the memory traffic. Adding a separate transpose kernel means reading $\mathbf{V}$ from GMEM and writing it back to GMEM once more, effectively doubling the memory traffic for $\mathbf{V}$. In a memory-bound regime, this additional memory traffic translates directly into wasted time, making the standalone transpose approach too costly. The paper explicitly states that option (1b) is "too wasteful in a memory-bound situation such as inference."

Both sub-options of the pre-processing transpose approach thus suffer from practical limitations: (1a) is hard to integrate into standard libraries due to cross-kernel coupling, and (1b) is too wasteful in memory-bound scenarios. These limitations motivate the alternative approach of performing the transpose inside the attention kernel itself.

## Option 2: In-Kernel Transpose Using LDSM/STSM Instructions

Given the limitations of pre-processing the transpose in GMEM, FlashAttention-3 opts for an in-kernel transpose: the $\mathbf{V}$ tiles are loaded into SMEM in their natural (head-dimension-contiguous) layout and then transposed within the kernel before being used in the second FP8 WGMMA. The key enabler for this in-kernel transpose is a pair of specialized hardware instructions: LDSM (\texttt{ldmatrix}) and STSM (\texttt{stmatrix}).

The LDSM (load matrix) and STSM (store matrix) instructions are designed for efficiently moving data between SMEM and the register file (RMEM) at a fine granularity. Specifically, these instructions involve a warp of threads collectively loading data from SMEM to registers (LDSM) or storing data from registers to SMEM (STSM) at a granularity of 128 bytes. What makes these instructions particularly valuable for the transpose problem is that they are capable of performing layout transformations — including transposes — as part of the memory copy operation. That is, when data is loaded from SMEM to RMEM via LDSM and then stored back to SMEM via STSM with a transpose modifier, the tiles can be effectively transposed without any explicit arithmetic operations. This is a form of data manipulation that is performed by the memory access hardware itself, leveraging the collective action of all 32 threads in a warp.

Moreover, LDSM and STSM are register-efficient instructions. They use a small number of registers per thread to accomplish the collective data movement, which is important because the attention kernel already has high register pressure due to the need to store tiles of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{S}$, $\mathbf{P}$, $\mathbf{O}$, and other intermediate quantities. Because of this register efficiency, the in-kernel transpose can be executed in the producer warpgroup — the set of warps responsible for issuing TMA loads from GMEM to SMEM — rather than in the consumer warpgroup that performs the GEMM and softmax computations. This is a significant advantage, as it allows the transpose to be overlapped with the computation in the consumer warpgroup, effectively hiding the cost of the transpose.

## LDSM/STSM for FP8: Packing 8-Bit Entries and Handling Transpose Limitations

The LDSM and STSM instructions were originally designed for 16-bit data types. In the PTX (parallel thread execution) documentation, these instructions are described as copying $8 \times 8$ matrices with 16-bit entries. To use them for FP8 data, which has 8-bit entries, the paper describes a packing strategy: two 8-bit FP8 values are packed into a single 16-bit slot, allowing the existing LDSM/STSM hardware to handle FP8 data at the same throughput as it handles 16-bit data. This effectively doubles the amount of FP8 data that can be moved per instruction compared to FP16, which is consistent with the goal of FP8 computation.

However, this packing strategy introduces a complication when it comes to transposition. The transpose versions of LDSM and STSM operate on 16-bit entries as atomic units: they can rearrange the positions of 16-bit values but cannot split apart the two 8-bit values that have been packed into a single 16-bit slot. In other words, if two FP8 values $a$ and $b$ are packed together as a single 16-bit unit $\{a, b\}$, the transpose version of LDSM/STSM can move this packed unit to a different position in the matrix, but it cannot separate $a$ from $b$ and place them in different rows or columns of the transposed output. This means that a simple LDSM-then-STSM-with-transpose sequence is insufficient to perform a proper tile-wise transpose of FP8 data.

To work around this limitation, the paper describes the need for additional register-level data manipulations between the LDSM and STSM operations. After loading the packed FP8 data from SMEM into registers via LDSM, the kernel must perform certain register movements — shuffling or permuting the 8-bit values within the packed 16-bit units — before storing them back to SMEM via STSM with the transpose modifier. The paper does not provide the full details of these register movements, but the key insight is that the in-kernel transpose of FP8 tiles requires a combination of LDSM (to load data from SMEM to registers), intermediate register manipulations (to unpack and repack the 8-bit values in the correct order for transposition), and STSM (to store the transposed data back to SMEM). Despite this added complexity, the overall approach remains more efficient than the alternatives because it avoids extra GMEM traffic and leverages the high-throughput, register-efficient LDSM/STSM instructions.

## Executing In-Kernel Transpose in the Producer Warpgroup Under the Shadow of WGMMAs

A critical optimization in the FP8 FlashAttention-3 algorithm is the scheduling of the in-kernel transpose relative to the GEMM computations. The warp-specialized design of FlashAttention-3 divides the warps in a CTA (cooperative thread array) into producer warpgroups and consumer warpgroups. The producer warpgroup is responsible for issuing TMA loads to bring tiles of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ from GMEM to SMEM, while the consumer warpgroup is responsible for issuing WGMMA instructions to perform the GEMM computations and executing the softmax operations. Since the LDSM/STSM instructions are register-efficient and do not require the large register allocations needed for WGMMA accumulators, the in-kernel transpose of $\mathbf{V}$ tiles can be assigned to the producer warpgroup.

The key insight is that the transpose of the next $\mathbf{V}$ tile can be overlapped with — or "hidden in the shadow of" — the two WGMMA operations that the consumer warpgroup is performing with the preceding $\mathbf{V}$ tile and the current $\mathbf{K}$ tile. Specifically, in the main loop of the algorithm, the consumer warpgroup performs two GEMMs per iteration: the first GEMM computes $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$, and the second GEMM computes $\mathbf{O}_i += \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$. These WGMMAs are asynchronous: once issued, the Tensor Cores work on them while the CUDA cores are free to perform other work. The producer warpgroup, having loaded $\mathbf{V}_{j+1}$ into SMEM via TMA, can use the LDSM/STSM sequence to transpose $\mathbf{V}_{j+1}$ while the consumer warpgroup's WGMMAs for iteration $j$ are still in flight. After the first iteration of the loop, the transpose of $\mathbf{V}_{j+1}$ is arranged to execute in the "shadow" of the two WGMMAs that involve $\mathbf{V}_j$ and $\mathbf{K}_j$.

This scheduling is made possible by the asynchronous nature of both TMA and WGMMA. The TMA loads and the LDSM/STSM transpose in the producer warpgroup do not block the WGMMA instructions in the consumer warpgroup, and vice versa. The only synchronization points are the barriers that ensure a tile has been loaded (and, for $\mathbf{V}$, transposed) before the consumer warpgroup issues a WGMMA that reads it, and the barriers that ensure the consumer warpgroup has finished reading a tile from SMEM before the producer warpgroup overwrites that buffer stage with new data. By carefully managing these synchronization points and using a multi-stage circular SMEM buffer, the algorithm ensures that the transpose work is effectively hidden behind the GEMM computation, contributing little to no additional latency.

## FP32 Accumulator vs. FP8 Operand Layout Mismatch in Registers

The layout conformance challenges of FP8 WGMMA extend beyond the SMEM-level transpose of $\mathbf{V}$. A second, more subtle problem arises when one attempts to chain two FP8 WGMMAs back-to-back within a single kernel, as is required in attention where the output of the first GEMM ($\mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$) serves as the input to the second GEMM ($\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$).

In the first GEMM, the FP8 WGMMA computes $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$ using FP8 operands and accumulates the result in FP32. The FP32 accumulator is held in the register file (RMEM) across the threads of the warpgroup. After the softmax operation (which is performed in FP32 or FP16 on the CUDA cores), the result $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$ must serve as the $A$ operand of the second FP8 WGMMA, $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$. However, the second FP8 WGMMA expects its $A$ operand to be in FP8 format and laid out in registers according to a specific FP8 operand layout.

The paper reveals a critical mismatch: the memory layout of the FP32 accumulator from the first WGMMA is different from the layout assumed for the FP8 operand $A$ of the second WGMMA. That is, the entries of the result matrix $\mathbf{S}$ (and hence $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$) are distributed across the threads of the warpgroup in a certain pattern determined by the FP32 accumulator layout, but the second FP8 WGMMA expects its $A$ operand entries to be distributed across threads according to a different pattern. If one were to simply take the FP32 accumulator, apply the softmax, quantize to FP8, and feed it directly into the second WGMMA, the data would be in the wrong register layout, and the WGMMA would compute an incorrect result.

The paper illustrates these two layouts by depicting fragments of the FP32 accumulator layout and the FP8 operand layout. In the FP32 accumulator layout, the entries are held in registers per thread in a certain order — let us denote them as $\{d_0, d_1, d_2, d_3, d_4, d_5, d_6, d_7, \ldots\}$. In the FP8 operand layout, the entries are arranged in a different order across the registers of each thread. This difference means that a transformation step is needed between the two WGMMAs to rearrange the data from the accumulator layout to the operand layout.

## Byte Permute Instructions for Transforming Accumulator Layout for Dependent GEMMs

To bridge the layout gap between the FP32 accumulator of the first WGMMA and the FP8 operand of the second WGMMA, FlashAttention-3 uses byte permute instructions. Byte permute instructions are low-level hardware instructions that can rearrange individual bytes within registers, allowing the kernel to reorganize the data layout at a very fine granularity without needing to go through SMEM or perform expensive cross-thread communication.

The paper describes a specific permutation that transforms the accumulator layout into the operand layout. Referring to the FP32 accumulator fragment with entries ordered as $\{d_0, d_1, d_2, d_3, d_4, d_5, d_6, d_7\}$ per thread, the byte permute instructions rearrange this into the order $\{d_0, d_1, d_4, d_5, d_2, d_3, d_6, d_7\}$. That is, the third and fourth pairs of entries are swapped: positions 2 and 3 (originally $d_2$ and $d_3$) are exchanged with positions 4 and 5 (originally $d_4$ and $d_5$), yielding the new order. This register permutation is then replicated over every 8 bytes of the register data, ensuring that the entire tile is transformed consistently.

In terms of the logical shape of the $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$ tile (which is the quantized version of the softmax output that serves as the $A$ operand of the second GEMM), this byte permutation effectively permutes the columns of the tile. For example, the paper notes that columns $\{0, 1, 8, 9\}$ (in some indexing scheme) now become the first four columns of the rearranged tile. This is a purely layout-level transformation: the mathematical content of the matrix is unchanged, but the way its entries are distributed across the registers of the threads in the warpgroup is reorganized to match what the second FP8 WGMMA expects.

The use of byte permute instructions is significant because it provides a way to perform this layout transformation entirely within the register file, without needing to write data to SMEM and read it back in a different layout. This keeps the transformation fast and avoids consuming SMEM bandwidth, which is a shared resource that is already under pressure from the TMA loads and the pingpong scheduling of the algorithm.

## Column/Row Permutation Coordination Between P Tile and V Tile

The byte permutation of the $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$ tile introduces a complication: since the columns of $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$ are permuted, the rows of $\mathbf{V}$ (which is the other operand in the second GEMM) must be correspondingly permuted for the matrix multiplication to produce the correct result. In a standard GEMM $A \times B$, if the columns of $A$ are permuted, the rows of $B$ must be permuted in the same way so that the inner products are computed between the correct pairs of elements. Specifically, if column $j$ of $A$ is moved to position $\pi(j)$, then row $j$ of $B$ must also be moved to position $\pi(j)$, so that the product $A \times B$ remains unchanged.

In the context of FlashAttention-3, the $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$ tile's columns are permuted by the byte permute instructions, and the $\mathbf{V}$ tile's rows must undergo a matching permutation. The paper describes how this coordination is achieved through the in-kernel transpose. Recall that the $\mathbf{V}$ tile is transposed in the producer warpgroup using LDSM and STSM instructions before being stored in SMEM for consumption by the second WGMMA. The key insight is that the in-kernel transpose can be arranged to write out a matching row permutation of the $\mathbf{V}$ tile. That is, during the process of loading $\mathbf{V}$ from SMEM to registers (LDSM), manipulating the packed FP8 entries, and storing the transposed result back to SMEM (STSM), the kernel can simultaneously perform a row permutation that mirrors the column permutation applied to $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$ by the byte permute instructions.

This additional degree of freedom — the ability to control the row permutation of $\mathbf{V}$ during the in-kernel transpose — is what makes the layout transformation approach viable. The paper notes that this freedom afforded by doing the in-kernel transpose eliminates the need for shuffle instructions to change register ownership across threads. In a previous approach described by the authors in other work, they had considered using shuffle instructions (which move data between registers of different threads in a warp) to reconcile the accumulator and operand layouts. However, shuffle instructions are more expensive and complex than byte permute instructions, and they require coordination across threads that can disrupt the instruction schedule. By instead performing the row permutation of $\mathbf{V}$ during the in-kernel transpose, FlashAttention-3 avoids the need for cross-thread shuffles entirely, keeping the layout transformation local to each thread's register file (via byte permutes for $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$) and to the SMEM-level transpose (via LDSM/STSM for $\mathbf{V}$).

The coordination between the two permutations — the column permutation of $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$ via byte permutes and the row permutation of $\mathbf{V}$ via the in-kernel transpose — is a elegant example of how the FlashAttention-3 algorithm exploits the flexibility of multiple hardware primitives to satisfy the strict layout requirements of FP8 WGMMA. The result is a system where the layout transformations needed for dependent FP8 GEMMs are performed efficiently and in parallel with the main computation, without introducing additional memory traffic or expensive cross-thread communication. This is what enables FP8 FlashAttention-3 to achieve close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s on the H100 GPU, nearly doubling the throughput of the FP16 version while maintaining acceptable numerical accuracy through block quantization and incoherent processing.

# FP8 Accuracy: Block Quantization and Incoherent Processing

## FP8 (e4m3) Format: Mantissa Limitations and Quantization Error

The FP8 format used in FlashAttention-3 is the e4m3 variant, which allocates 4 bits to the exponent and 3 bits to the mantissa. This allocation of bits reflects a design choice that prioritizes dynamic range over precision, since the exponent determines the range of representable values while the mantissa controls the precision of each individual value. With only 3 bits dedicated to the mantissa, the FP8 format can represent a value using just eight distinct mantissa patterns (including the implicit leading one), which yields significantly less numerical precision than the FP16 or BF16 formats commonly used in deep learning. The FP16 format, by comparison, uses 10 bits for the mantissa, and BF16 uses 7 bits, both of which provide far finer granularity in representing fractional values. The reduction to 3 mantissa bits in FP8 means that the relative error introduced by representing any given number can be substantially larger, particularly when the values being represented span a wide range of magnitudes.

This limited precision has direct consequences for attention computation. In the attention mechanism, the intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ and the softmax-normalized $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$ require careful numerical handling. When the inputs $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ are quantized to FP8, the quantization error propagates through the matrix multiplications and the softmax operation, potentially accumulating in ways that degrade the quality of the final output $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$. The throughput advantage of FP8—doubling the matrix multiply (GEMM) throughput compared to FP16 on Hopper GPUs—comes at the cost of this increased numerical error, creating a fundamental tension between speed and accuracy that must be addressed through algorithmic techniques.

## Outlier Features in LLMs: Why They Make Quantization Difficult

A well-documented phenomenon in large language models is the presence of outlier features—values that are much larger in magnitude than the vast majority of other activations. These outliers arise naturally during training and inference and have been observed to concentrate in specific channels or dimensions of the hidden representations. When quantizing a tensor that contains such outliers, the quantization scale must be set to accommodate the largest values, which means that the scale is driven by a tiny fraction of extreme values. Since the FP8 format has limited resolution (due to the 3-bit mantissa), using a single scale factor calibrated to the outliers means that the majority of values—which are much smaller—are represented with very few effective bits of precision. In effect, the outliers "hog" the dynamic range, leaving insufficient precision for the bulk of the data.

This problem is especially acute in attention because the matrices $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ are derived from the hidden states of the model, which contain these outlier features. When the quantization is performed with a single scale per tensor (per-tensor scaling), a single outlier in any element of the tensor can force the scale to be large enough that most other elements lose almost all meaningful precision when represented in FP8. The paper highlights this by noting that large models "typically have outlier values that are much larger in magnitude than most other values, making quantization difficult." To validate their accuracy improvements, the authors simulate this phenomenon by generating entries of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ from the distribution $\mathcal{N}(0, 1) + \mathcal{N}(0, 100) \cdot \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.001)$, meaning that each entry is normally distributed with zero mean and standard deviation 1, but for approximately 0.1% of entries, an additional independent term with standard deviation 10 is added. This construction directly models the outlier regime: most values are moderate, but a small fraction are dramatically larger.

## Per-Tensor Scaling vs. Block Quantization: Finer Granularity for Better Accuracy

The standard approach to FP8 quantization, as referenced in the paper, is per-tensor scaling, in which a single scalar scale factor is maintained per tensor—one for $\mathbf{Q}$, one for $\mathbf{K}$, and one for $\mathbf{V}$. This approach is simple and has minimal overhead, since only three additional scalar values need to be stored and managed. However, per-tensor scaling is particularly vulnerable to the outlier problem described above: a single extreme value anywhere in the tensor forces the scale to accommodate it, degrading the representation of all other values.

Block quantization addresses this limitation by using a finer granularity. Instead of a single scale for the entire tensor, one scale factor is maintained per block. In the context of FlashAttention-3, the tensor is split into blocks of size $B_r \times d$ for $\mathbf{Q}$ and $B_c \times d$ for $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$, where $B_r$ and $B_c$ are the query and key block sizes used in the tiled attention algorithm, and $d$ is the head dimension. By quantizing each block separately, outliers are confined to affecting only the scale of their own block, leaving other blocks free to use a smaller, more appropriate scale that preserves precision for their values. This localizes the damage from outliers and dramatically reduces the overall quantization error.

## Block Quantization Implementation: One Scalar Per Block

The implementation of block quantization in FlashAttention-3 is naturally aligned with the tiled structure of the algorithm. The FlashAttention algorithm already operates on blocks of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$: the query matrix is divided into $T_r = \lceil \frac{N}{B_r} \rceil$ blocks of size $B_r \times d$, and the key and value matrices are divided into $T_c = \lceil \frac{N}{B_c} \rceil$ blocks of size $B_c \times d$. Block quantization assigns one scalar scale factor per such block. When the attention computation processes a block $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$, the per-block scales for $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and $\mathbf{K}_j$ can be incorporated into the computation at no additional cost, since the rescaling simply involves multiplying by the appropriate scale factors. The paper notes that "as the FlashAttention-3 algorithm naturally operates on blocks, we can scale each block of $\mathbf{S}$ to account for this block quantization at no computation cost." This is a key advantage: the block structure that is already present in the algorithm for performance reasons is exactly the structure needed to make block quantization efficient.

## Fusing Block Quantization with Preceding Operations at No Cost

A critical practical consideration is how and when the quantization to FP8 is performed. In a typical transformer model, the operations preceding attention include projections and the rotary embedding (RoPE) step. The paper observes that the rotary embedding operation is memory-bandwidth bound, meaning that its performance is limited by the rate at which data can be read from and written to memory, rather than by the rate of computation. In such memory-bound scenarios, adding additional computation that operates on the same data—such as computing per-block scale factors and applying them to produce FP8 quantized outputs—does not increase the runtime, because the bottleneck is data movement, not arithmetic. The paper explicitly states that block quantization "can be fused with an operation right before attention (e.g., rotary embedding) with no additional slow down (since rotary embedding is memory-bandwidth bound)." This fusion is essential for making block quantization practical: rather than introducing a separate quantization pass that reads and writes the entire tensor, the quantization is performed in-place as part of an operation that would have touched the same data anyway, effectively making the quantization free in terms of wall-clock time.

## Incoherent Processing: Multiplying Q and K with a Random Orthogonal Matrix

Even with block quantization, outliers within a single block can still degrade accuracy. Incoherent processing is a complementary technique that addresses outliers at a more fundamental level by transforming the data before quantization. The key idea is to multiply both $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ before quantizing to FP8. An orthogonal matrix satisfies $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top = \mathbf{I}$, and this property is what makes the transformation safe for attention: the product $(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top\mathbf{K}^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, which is exactly the pre-softmax score matrix $\mathbf{S}$ up to the scaling factor $\alpha$. In other words, the attention output is unchanged by this transformation.

The benefit of this transformation comes from the way it affects the distribution of values. When $\mathbf{Q}$ is multiplied by $\mathbf{M}$, each entry of the resulting matrix $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}$ is a weighted sum of entries from a row of $\mathbf{Q}$, with weights given by a column of $\mathbf{M}$. If $\mathbf{M}$ is random and orthogonal, these weights are effectively random, and by the properties of random sums, each entry of $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}$ tends to be a "spread out" version of the original entries. Outlier values, which were extreme in specific positions, get distributed across many positions in the transformed matrix, reducing the maximum magnitude of any single entry. The paper describes this as serving to "spread out" the outliers, since each entry of $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}$ or $\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M}$ is a random sum of entries of $\mathbf{Q}$ or $\mathbf{K}$, thus reducing quantization error. After this spreading, the quantization scale needed for each block is smaller and more uniform, leading to better overall precision in the FP8 representation.

## Why Orthogonal Multiplication Preserves Attention Output

The mathematical guarantee that incoherent processing does not change the attention output rests on the orthogonality of $\mathbf{M}$. The attention score matrix is computed as $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$. If we replace $\mathbf{Q}$ with $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ with $\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M}$, the new score matrix becomes $\alpha (\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top\mathbf{K}^\top$. Since $\mathbf{M}$ is orthogonal, $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top = \mathbf{I}$, so this simplifies to $\alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top = \mathbf{S}$. The softmax of $\mathbf{S}$ and the subsequent multiplication by $\mathbf{V}$ are therefore identical with or without the transformation. The transformation only affects the intermediate FP8 representation of $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$, not the mathematical result of the attention computation. This is a powerful property: it allows the technique to improve numerical accuracy without introducing any bias or approximation into the computation, as long as the matrix $\mathbf{M}$ is exactly orthogonal.

## Practical Implementation: Product of Random Diagonal ±1 Matrices and Hadamard Matrix

While the concept of multiplying by a random orthogonal matrix is mathematically clean, the practical challenge is the computational cost of the matrix multiplication. A naive implementation would require $O(d^2)$ operations to multiply a $d$-dimensional vector by a $d \times d$ matrix, which could be prohibitively expensive for typical head dimensions (e.g., $d = 128$ or $d = 256$). To make incoherent processing practical, the paper follows the approach of Chee et al. and Tseng et al. and chooses $\mathbf{M}$ to be a structured random orthogonal matrix: specifically, the product of random diagonal matrices with entries $\pm 1$ and a Hadamard matrix. A Hadamard matrix is a square matrix whose entries are all $\pm 1$ (up to a normalization factor) and whose rows are mutually orthogonal. The key advantage of the Hadamard matrix is that multiplying a vector by it can be performed in $O(d \log d)$ time using the fast Walsh-Hadamard transform, rather than $O(d^2)$ for a general dense matrix. The random diagonal $\pm 1$ matrices, when multiplied with the Hadamard matrix, produce a random orthogonal matrix that retains the computational efficiency of the Hadamard structure while providing the randomization needed for the incoherent processing effect.

The combination of the diagonal matrices and the Hadamard matrix ensures that the resulting $\mathbf{M}$ is orthogonal (since both components are orthogonal, and the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal), while the fast Hadamard transform makes the multiplication efficient. The paper notes that this multiplication "can be fused with the rotary embedding at no extra computation cost," leveraging the same memory-bandwidth-bound argument as for block quantization. Since the rotary embedding already reads and writes the query and key tensors, the additional Hadamard transform and diagonal sign flips can be applied to the same data in the same kernel pass, making the entire incoherent processing step effectively free in terms of runtime.

## Combined Effect and Validation

When block quantization and incoherent processing are used together, they address the FP8 accuracy problem at two complementary levels. Block quantization ensures that the scale factor is locally appropriate, preventing a single outlier from degrading the precision of the entire tensor. Incoherent processing, applied before quantization, spreads out the outliers so that they are less extreme relative to the other values in each block, making the per-block quantization even more effective. The paper validates this combined approach by measuring the root mean squared error (RMSE) against a reference implementation in FP64, using the outlier-simulating distribution $\mathcal{N}(0, 1) + \mathcal{N}(0, 100) \cdot \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.001)$. The results show that FP8 FlashAttention-3 with block quantization and incoherent processing achieves 2.6$\times$ lower numerical error than a baseline FP8 attention implementation that uses standard per-tensor quantization. This confirms that the combination of finer-grained scaling and outlier spreading can recover much of the accuracy lost by moving to the lower-precision FP8 format, making high-throughput FP8 attention a viable option for large language model training and inference.

# Empirical Validation: Benchmarking FlashAttention-3

In this chapter, we turn from the algorithmic innovations of FlashAttention-3 to the empirical evidence that validates them. The developers of FlashAttention-3 conducted extensive benchmarks to demonstrate that their algorithmic improvements—warp-specialization, GEMM-softmax pipelining, and FP8 support with block quantization and incoherent processing—translate into measurable speedups on modern GPU hardware. Understanding these empirical results is essential not only for appreciating the practical impact of the work, but also for developing intuition about how attention kernels are evaluated and compared in practice. We will walk through the experimental setup, the results for both FP16 and FP8 precision across forward and backward passes, comparisons with vendor-optimized libraries, and the methodology used to compute throughput in terms of FLOPs.

# Experimental Setup: H100 SXM5 GPU, CUTLASS Primitives, Benchmark Configurations

The empirical evaluation of FlashAttention-3 was conducted on an NVIDIA H100 80GB SXM5 GPU, which is based on the Hopper architecture discussed earlier in this text. This is a significant detail because the entire design of FlashAttention-3 is tailored to the specific hardware features of Hopper, including the asynchronous Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) for memory loads, the warpgroup-wide WGMMA instructions for asynchronous matrix multiplication, and the FP8 Tensor Cores. By using the H100 as the benchmark platform, the authors ensure that they are measuring the performance gains that arise from actually exploiting these hardware capabilities, rather than gains that would be achievable on any GPU regardless of architecture.

The implementation of FlashAttention-3 relies on primitives from CUTLASS (CUDA Templates for Linear Algebra Subroutines), specifically the WGMMA and TMA abstractions. CUTLASS provides a layer of abstraction over the low-level PTX (Parallel Thread Execution) instructions that directly program the GPU's hardware units. By using these primitives, the authors are able to express the warp-specialized producer-consumer scheme and the overlapped GEMM-softmax pipeline at a manageable level of abstraction, while still generating highly optimized code that targets the specific instruction sets available on Hopper. This choice of implementation framework is important for reproducibility and for understanding how research-grade GPU kernels are typically built in practice.

The benchmark configurations span a range of settings designed to stress the attention kernel under conditions representative of real workloads. The sequence length was varied across the values $512$, $1\text{k}$, $2\text{k}$, $4\text{k}$, $8\text{k}$, and $16\text{k}$, covering both relatively short and long-context regimes. The batch size was adjusted so that the total number of tokens across all sequences in the batch was held constant at $16\text{k}$, ensuring that the total computational workload remained comparable across different sequence lengths and allowing for a fair comparison of throughput. The hidden dimension was set to $2048$, which is a common choice in large language models. The head dimension was varied among $64$, $128$, and $256$, corresponding to $32$, $16$, or $8$ heads, respectively. This variation in head dimension is important because it affects the balance between matmul and non-matmul operations: larger head dimensions increase the fraction of total FLOPs that come from matrix multiplications, which are handled by the high-throughput Tensor Cores, while smaller head dimensions increase the relative cost of softmax and other non-matmul operations.

FlashAttention-3 was compared against several baselines: a standard attention implementation in PyTorch (which materializes the intermediate attention matrices to HBM), FlashAttention-2 (the predecessor that does not exploit Hopper-specific asynchrony or low-precision features), FlashAttention-2 implemented in Triton (which does use H100-specific instructions but does not incorporate the new algorithmic ideas of FlashAttention-3), and NVIDIA's cuDNN library implementation of FlashAttention-2 optimized for H100 GPUs. The inclusion of the cuDNN baseline is particularly noteworthy, as cuDNN is a closed-source, vendor-optimized library that represents the state of the art for attention on Hopper GPUs. Outperforming cuDNN would demonstrate that the algorithmic innovations of FlashAttention-3 go beyond what can be achieved through implementation-level optimization alone.

# FP16 Forward Pass Results: 1.5-2.0x Speedup over FlashAttention-2, Up to 740 TFLOPs/s

The FP16 forward pass results represent the primary benchmark for FlashAttention-3, as the forward pass is the most critical computation during inference and a significant portion of training. The results demonstrate that FlashAttention-3 achieves a $1.5$–$2.0\times$ speedup over FlashAttention-2 in the forward pass across the tested configurations. This is a substantial improvement, especially considering that FlashAttention-2 itself was already a highly optimized kernel. The speedup arises from the three main algorithmic innovations: warp-specialization that hides memory latency by separating data movement into producer warps and computation into consumer warps, the pingpong scheduling that overlaps softmax computation of one warpgroup with GEMM execution of another, and the intra-warpgroup two-stage pipelining that overlaps the second WGMMA of one iteration with the softmax of the next.

In terms of absolute throughput, FlashAttention-3 reaches up to $740$ TFLOPs/s in FP16. To put this number in context, the H100 SXM5 GPU has a theoretical maximum FP16 throughput of approximately $989$ TFLOPs/s. Thus, FlashAttention-3 achieves about $75\%$ utilization of the theoretical peak, which is a remarkably high figure for an attention kernel. By contrast, FlashAttention-2 achieved only about $35\%$ utilization on the H100. The gap between these two utilization figures illustrates the extent to which FlashAttention-2 was leaving hardware resources idle—resources that FlashAttention-3 successfully activates through its use of asynchrony and pipelining.

Compared to the standard PyTorch attention implementation, FlashAttention-3 can be up to $3$–$16\times$ faster, with the largest speedups occurring at longer sequence lengths. This is expected, as the standard implementation materializes the full $N \times N$ attention matrix $\mathbf{S}$ and softmax matrix $\mathbf{P}$ to HBM, incurring quadratic memory traffic that becomes increasingly expensive as the sequence length grows. FlashAttention-3, inheriting the tiling strategy from its predecessors, avoids these intermediate writes entirely.

FlashAttention-3 also outperforms FlashAttention-2 in Triton by approximately $1.5\times$. The Triton implementation of FlashAttention-2 does use H100-specific instructions, demonstrating that simply using Hopper instructions without the algorithmic redesign of FlashAttention-3 is not sufficient to achieve the same level of performance. The gap highlights the importance of the warp-specialization and pipelining techniques, which go beyond instruction selection to fundamentally restructure how work is scheduled across the GPU's execution units.

# FP16 Backward Pass Results: 1.5-1.75x Speedup over FlashAttention-2

The backward pass of attention, which computes the gradients $\mathbf{dQ}$, $\mathbf{dK}$, and $\mathbf{dV}$ given the output gradient $\mathbf{dO}$, is more computationally intensive than the forward pass. It involves five matrix multiplications (compared to two in the forward pass), due to the need to recompute the softmax probabilities and propagate gradients through the attention mechanism. Despite this additional complexity, FlashAttention-3 achieves a $1.5$–$1.75\times$ speedup over FlashAttention-2 in the backward pass.

The speedup in the backward pass is somewhat smaller than in the forward pass (where it was $1.5$–$2.0\times$). This is a common pattern in kernel optimization: the backward pass has a different structure of dependencies and a higher ratio of matmul to non-matmul operations, which can limit the opportunities for overlapping computation. In the backward pass, there are more GEMM operations that must be executed sequentially due to data dependencies, leaving less room for the softmax and other non-matmul operations to be hidden behind matmuls. Nevertheless, the warp-specialization scheme still provides benefits by allowing data movement for subsequent blocks to proceed in parallel with computation on current blocks.

The backward pass results are significant for training workloads, where the backward pass typically dominates the total compute time. A $1.5$–$1.75\times$ speedup in the backward pass translates directly into faster training iterations, which can reduce the wall-clock time and cost of training large language models. Since attention is often the computational bottleneck for long-context training, this improvement can have a meaningful impact on the overall training throughput.

# Comparison with cuDNN: FlashAttention-3 Surpasses Vendor-Optimized Library for Medium and Long Sequences

One of the most striking results in the empirical validation is the comparison between FlashAttention-3 and NVIDIA's cuDNN library. cuDNN is a closed-source, vendor-optimized deep learning library that is specifically tuned for NVIDIA GPUs. For the H100, cuDNN includes an implementation of FlashAttention-2 that has been optimized by NVIDIA's engineers with full knowledge of the hardware. This makes cuDNN a formidable baseline—it represents the best that can be achieved through implementation-level optimization of the FlashAttention-2 algorithm on Hopper.

The results show that for medium and long sequences (sequence lengths of $1\text{k}$ and above), FlashAttention-3 outperforms cuDNN. This is a remarkable achievement because it demonstrates that the algorithmic innovations of FlashAttention-3—the warp-specialized producer-consumer scheme, the pingpong scheduling, and the intra-warpgroup GEMM-softmax pipelining—provide benefits that go beyond what the vendor was able to extract from the FlashAttention-2 algorithm through low-level optimization. In other words, the gains come from redesigning the algorithm to match the hardware's asynchronous execution model, not merely from writing more efficient code for the same algorithm.

For shorter sequences, the performance gap between FlashAttention-3 and cuDNN narrows, and in some cases cuDNN may be competitive or ahead. This can be attributed to several factors. At shorter sequence lengths, there is less work to amortize the overhead of setting up the warp-specialized pipeline, and the kernel launch and synchronization costs become a larger fraction of the total runtime. Additionally, the FP16 implementation of FlashAttention-3 uses a persistent kernel design and load balancing strategy, which helps maintain high occupancy across a range of sequence lengths, but the benefits of persistence are most pronounced when there is sufficient work to keep the GPU busy.

The fact that FlashAttention-3, an open-source project, surpasses the closed-source vendor library for medium and long sequences is particularly significant for the research community. It means that researchers and developers who rely on open-source software can achieve state-of-the-art attention performance without being dependent on proprietary libraries. This aligns with the authors' goal of open-sourcing FlashAttention-3 and integrating it with PyTorch and Hugging Face libraries.

# FP8 Forward Pass Results: Close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s

The FP8 results represent the frontier of attention kernel performance. By targeting the FP8 Tensor Cores on Hopper, FlashAttention-3 nearly doubles the throughput compared to FP16, reaching close to $1.2$ PFLOPs/s (petraflops per second). This is an extraordinary figure: it means that a single H100 GPU can execute over one quadrillion floating-point operations per second on attention computation when using FP8 precision. The FP8 Tensor Cores deliver twice the throughput per SM compared to FP16 or BF16 Tensor Cores, and FlashAttention-3 is able to harness this throughput effectively.

Achieving close to $1.2$ PFLOPs/s requires overcoming several challenges specific to FP8. As described in the algorithm section, FP8 WGMMA instructions impose stricter layout constraints than FP16: both operands must be in k-major format (contiguous in the inner reduction dimension), whereas FP16 WGMMA accepts both mn-major and k-major operands. This necessitates the in-kernel transpose of $\mathbf{V}$ tiles using LDSM and STSM instructions, and the byte permute instructions to reconcile the FP32 accumulator layout with the FP8 operand layout. The fact that these transformations can be performed without significant overhead—often hidden in the shadow of WGMMA computations—is a testament to the effectiveness of the warp-specialized design, where the producer warpgroup handles the transpose while the consumer warpgroup performs GEMMs.

The FP8 results are reported for head dimension $256$ in the main text, with full results across other head dimensions provided in an appendix. The choice to highlight headdim $256$ in the main text likely reflects the fact that larger head dimensions benefit more from FP8, as the matmul operations dominate the total compute, making the doubling of Tensor Core throughput more impactful.

# FP8 vs. FP16 Performance Comparison Across Head Dimensions and Causal Masking

The relationship between FP8 and FP16 performance in FlashAttention-3 varies depending on the head dimension and whether causal masking is applied. These variations arise from the interplay between several factors: the ratio of matmul to non-matmul operations, the overhead of the in-kernel transpose required for FP8, the presence or absence of a persistent kernel design, and the effect of causal masking on the amount of computation.

For head dimension $64$, FP8 FlashAttention-3 is ahead of its FP16 counterpart. This may seem surprising, as one might expect the overhead of the in-kernel transpose and layout transformations to be more significant relative to the computation at smaller head dimensions. However, at head dimension $64$, the total FLOPs per block are smaller, meaning that the kernel is more likely to be memory-bound or at least less compute-bound. In this regime, the doubled Tensor Core throughput of FP8 provides a clear advantage, as the kernel can process more data per unit of memory bandwidth.

For head dimensions $128$ and $256$, FP8 is competitive with FP16 in the absence of causal masking—specifically, the paper notes that FP8 is "at par" with FP16 for these settings. This suggests that the overhead of the FP8-specific transformations (transpose, byte permutes, layout conformance) approximately offsets the throughput advantage of FP8 Tensor Cores at these head dimensions. The larger GEMMs at headdim $128$ and $256$ mean that the kernel is more compute-bound, so the doubled FP8 throughput should in principle provide a larger benefit; however, the additional non-matmul work required for FP8 layout management eats into this advantage.

With causal masking, FP8 falls behind FP16 for head dimensions $128$ and $256$. Causal masking reduces the amount of computation by approximately half (since only the lower triangular portion of the attention matrix is computed), which changes the balance between matmul and non-matmul operations. With less matmul work to amortize the FP8 overhead, the layout transformations become a proportionally larger cost. Additionally, causal masking can reduce the effective tile sizes and make it harder to keep the Tensor Cores fully utilized.

An important factor noted by the authors is that the FP8 implementation of FlashAttention-3 does not include a persistent kernel design, whereas the FP16 implementation does. A persistent kernel keeps the kernel running on the GPU across multiple work items, reducing launch overhead and improving load balancing. The absence of persistence in FP8 partly explains why FP8 does not perform as well for small sequence lengths and causal masking compared to FP8 cuDNN kernels. The authors identify integrating a persistent kernel design into the FP8 kernel as a future improvement.

# FLOP Calculation Methodology for Forward and Backward Passes

To convert raw runtime measurements into throughput figures (TFLOPs/s), one must know the total number of floating-point operations performed by the attention computation. The authors use a standard formula for the forward pass FLOPs:

$$\text{FLOPs}_{\text{forward}} = 4 \cdot \text{seqlen}^2 \cdot \text{head dimension} \cdot \text{number of heads}.$$

The factor of $4$ arises from the two matrix multiplications in the forward pass. The first matmul computes $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$, which requires $2 \cdot N \cdot N \cdot d$ FLOPs (one multiply and one add per element of the $N \times N \times d$ tensor contraction). The second matmul computes $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P} \mathbf{V}$, which similarly requires $2 \cdot N \cdot N \cdot d$ FLOPs. Together, these two matmuls contribute $4 \cdot N^2 \cdot d$ FLOPs per head, and multiplying by the number of heads gives the total. The softmax operations (exponential, subtraction, division) are not counted in this formula, as they are non-matmul operations whose FLOP count is lower-order compared to the matmuls.

When causal masking is applied, approximately half of the entries in the attention matrix $\mathbf{S}$ are masked out (set to $-\infty$ before softmax), so only the lower triangular portion is computed. The FLOP count is therefore divided by $2$:

$$\text{FLOPs}_{\text{forward, causal}} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 4 \cdot \text{seqlen}^2 \cdot \text{head dimension} \cdot \text{number of heads}.$$

For the backward pass, the FLOP count is multiplied by $2.5$ relative to the forward pass. This factor reflects the fact that the backward pass involves $5$ matrix multiplications (compared to $2$ in the forward pass), due to the chain rule computation of gradients. Specifically, the backward pass computes $\mathbf{dV} = \mathbf{P}^\top \mathbf{dO}$, $\mathbf{dP} = \mathbf{dO} \mathbf{V}^\top$, $\mathbf{dQ} = \alpha \mathbf{dS} \mathbf{K}$, $\mathbf{dK} = \alpha \mathbf{dS}^\top \mathbf{Q}$, plus the recomputation of $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$ to obtain $\mathbf{P}$ (which is needed for the $\mathrm{dsoftmax}$ operation). This gives $5$ matmuls in the backward pass versus $2$ in the forward, hence the $2.5\times$ multiplier:

$$\text{FLOPs}_{\text{backward}} = 2.5 \cdot \text{FLOPs}_{\text{forward}}.$$

The "recomputation" strategy used in FlashAttention (both version 2 and 3) is worth noting here. Rather than storing the intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ from the forward pass to be reused in the backward pass (which would require quadratic memory), the backward pass recomputes $\mathbf{S}$ from $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$. This trades additional computation for reduced memory traffic, which is a favorable trade-off on modern GPUs where compute is abundant but memory bandwidth is the bottleneck. The $2.5\times$ FLOP multiplier for the backward pass already accounts for this recomputation.

Once the FLOP count is established, the throughput in TFLOPs/s is simply:

$$\text{Throughput} = \frac{\text{FLOPs}}{\text{Runtime (seconds)}} \cdot 10^{-12}.$$

This throughput figure is then compared against the theoretical peak throughput of the GPU to compute the utilization percentage. For FP16 on the H100, the theoretical peak is approximately $989$ TFLOPs/s, so FlashAttention-3's $740$ TFLOPs/s corresponds to $740/989 \approx 75\%$ utilization. For FP8, the theoretical peak is approximately twice the FP16 peak, and FlashAttention-3's close to $1.2$ PFLOPs/s represents a similarly high fraction of the FP8 peak.

In summary, the empirical validation demonstrates that the algorithmic innovations of FlashAttention-3 translate into substantial, measurable speedups on the H100 GPU. The FP16 forward pass achieves $75\%$ utilization of theoretical peak throughput, a dramatic improvement over the $35\%$ utilization of FlashAttention-2. The backward pass and FP8 results further confirm that the benefits of warp-specialization, pipelining, and low-precision support extend across different precision settings and computation modes. The fact that FlashAttention-3 surpasses the vendor-optimized cuDNN library for medium and long sequences validates the approach of redesigning the attention algorithm to match the asynchronous execution model of modern GPUs, rather than merely optimizing the implementation of an existing algorithm.

# Ablation Studies and Numerical Error Analysis

# Ablation Setup: Fixed Parameters and Experimental Configuration

To rigorously assess the individual and combined contributions of the algorithmic innovations introduced in FlashAttention-3, the authors conduct an ablation study that systematically isolates each technique. The experimental configuration is carefully chosen to reflect a realistic workload while holding all parameters fixed, so that any observed performance differences can be attributed solely to the algorithmic improvements rather than variations in problem size or shape.

The ablation is performed on non-causal FP16 FlashAttention-3, meaning that no causal mask is applied and the computation is carried out in FP16 precision. The fixed parameters are set as follows: a batch size of 4, a sequence length of 8448, 16 attention heads, and a head dimension of 128. These values are representative of a medium-to-long-sequence training scenario, where attention is a significant computational bottleneck and the benefits of overlapping computation should manifest most clearly. With a hidden dimension of 2048 (implied by 16 heads of dimension 128), the total number of tokens processed is $4 \times 8448 = 33792$, which is large enough to saturate the GPU and produce stable throughput measurements.

The choice of non-causal attention for the ablation is deliberate. Causal masking introduces additional control flow and conditional logic into the kernel, which can obscure the clean measurement of how well the warp-specialization and pipelining schemes hide latency. By using the non-causal variant, the authors ensure that every block of the key and value matrices is fully processed for every query block, making the computation pattern uniform and the performance measurements more directly attributable to the overlapping techniques under investigation.

All experiments are conducted on an H100 80GB SXM5 GPU, the same hardware targeted throughout the FlashAttention-3 paper. This GPU features asynchronous Tensor Cores (exposed via WGMMA instructions), the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) for asynchronous memory copies, and support for dynamic register reallocation via `setmaxnreg`, all of which are prerequisites for the warp-specialization and pipelining schemes being ablated. The fixed parameter setting allows for a controlled comparison where the only variables are whether warp-specialization is enabled and whether the 2-stage GEMM-softmax pipelining is active.

# Ablation Results: Contributions of Warp-Specialization and 2-Stage Pipelining

The ablation study quantifies the performance impact of two key innovations: warp-specialization (with its associated pingpong scheduling) and the 2-stage overlapping of GEMM and softmax operations within the consumer warpgroup. The results confirm that these algorithmic improvements, which exploit the asynchronous execution model of the Hopper GPU, lead to a significant and measurable speedup.

The baseline for the ablation is a version of FlashAttention-3 that does not use warp-specialization or intra-consumer overlapping—essentially corresponding to the algorithm described in Algorithm 1 of the paper, where the producer warpgroup loads data into shared memory via TMA and the consumer warpgroup executes the GEMMs and softmax in a strictly sequential fashion, with explicit wait statements serializing the execution of softmax and GEMMs. This baseline achieves approximately 570 TFLOPs/s for the given parameter configuration. While this already represents a substantial improvement over FlashAttention-2 (which achieves only about 35% utilization on the H100), it leaves significant performance on the table by not exploiting the asynchronous capabilities of the hardware.

When warp-specialization is added, the producer and consumer warpgroups operate concurrently: the producer issues TMA loads for the next blocks of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ while the consumer is still processing the current blocks. This hides the memory latency of global memory accesses behind computation, a classic technique for throughput-oriented processors. However, within the consumer warpgroup, the GEMMs and softmax still execute sequentially—the consumer must wait for each WGMMA to complete before beginning the softmax operations that depend on its output, and must wait for the softmax to complete before issuing the next dependent WGMMA.

The 2-stage pipelining technique breaks this sequential dependency by introducing additional register buffers and reorganizing the inner loop so that the softmax for iteration $j$ is overlapped with the second WGMMA ($\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$) of the previous iteration and the first WGMMA ($\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$) of the current iteration. Specifically, as described in Algorithm 2 of the paper, within the mainloop the WGMMA computing $\mathbf{O}_i = \mathbf{O}_i + \tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}} \mathbf{V}_{j-1}$ (line 12) is issued without an immediate wait, and the softmax operations based on $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ (line 14) proceed concurrently while the WGMMA executes asynchronously on the Tensor Cores. The key insight is that the softmax operations—rowmax, exponential, rowsum, and rescaling—are performed by the multi-function units and CUDA cores, which are distinct hardware units from the Tensor Cores that execute WGMMA. Therefore, these operations can genuinely run in parallel.

The combined effect of warp-specialization and 2-stage pipelining is an increase from 570 TFLOPs/s to 661 TFLOPs/s, representing a roughly 16% improvement attributable solely to better exploitation of asynchrony within the kernel. This improvement is on top of the gains already achieved by FlashAttention-3 over FlashAttention-2 through the use of Hopper-specific instructions and the basic warp-specialization scheme. The authors also note that the pingpong scheduling between two consumer warpgroups (where the softmax of one warpgroup is overlapped with the GEMMs of the other) contributes an additional improvement, for example from 570 to 620–640 TFLOPs/s for a similar configuration with sequence length 8192, and the 2-stage pipelining within each warpgroup pushes performance even further.

The ablation also highlights the trade-offs involved in deeper pipelining. The authors propose a 3-stage variant that would further overlap the second WGMMA with softmax, but note that this requires even more registers to maintain additional stages of intermediate results. Each additional stage in the pipeline necessitates keeping another copy of $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ in registers, costing $B_r \times B_c \times \text{sizeof}(\text{float})$ bytes per threadblock. This increased register pressure can conflict with the desire to use larger tile sizes (which are themselves register-hungry), and the optimal balance between pipeline depth and tile size must be determined empirically through profiling.

# Numerical Error Methodology: FP64 Reference and Outlier Simulation

A critical aspect of validating FlashAttention-3 is ensuring that the speedups achieved through asynchronous execution and low-precision computation do not come at an unacceptable cost in numerical accuracy. To this end, the authors design a rigorous numerical error evaluation methodology that compares the output of FlashAttention-3 against a high-precision reference implementation and simulates the challenging distributional properties of real-world LLM activations.

The reference implementation is computed in FP64 (double precision floating point), which provides 53 bits of mantissa precision and serves as a near-exact ground truth. By computing attention in FP64, the authors obtain an output tensor that is effectively free from the quantization and rounding errors introduced by lower-precision formats. The root mean squared error (RMSE) between the output of each tested implementation and the FP64 reference is then used as the primary metric for numerical accuracy. RMSE is a natural choice here because it penalizes large deviations more heavily than small ones, capturing the impact of outlier-induced errors that can be particularly damaging in downstream tasks.

The design of the input data is equally important. The authors recognize that randomly generated inputs from a standard normal distribution would not adequately stress the numerical precision of the attention computation, as such inputs lack the extreme outlier values that are known to arise in large language models. Prior work has established that LLM activations and weights frequently contain outlier features—specific dimensions or channels whose magnitudes are significantly larger than the typical values. These outliers pose a fundamental challenge for low-precision quantization, as a single large value can force the quantization scale to be large, thereby reducing the precision available for the vast majority of smaller values.

To simulate this realistic and challenging scenario, the authors generate the entries of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ according to the following mixture distribution:

$$\mathcal{N}(0, 1) + \mathcal{N}(0, 100) \cdot \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.001).$$

This distribution means that each entry is drawn from a standard normal distribution with zero mean and unit variance, but for approximately 0.1% of entries, an additional independent term is added that is normally distributed with zero mean and a standard deviation of 10 (since the variance is 100, the standard deviation is $\sqrt{100} = 10$). The Bernoulli indicator variable activates this outlier term with probability 0.001, so roughly one in a thousand entries receives a large perturbation. This construction faithfully reproduces the essential statistical property of LLM activations: a heavy-tailed distribution where most values are well-behaved but a small fraction are extreme outliers. The presence of these outliers makes quantization to FP8 particularly challenging, as the quantization scale must accommodate the full dynamic range of the data, including the outliers, at the cost of precision for the bulk of the values.

The RMSE is computed by taking the element-wise difference between the output of each implementation and the FP64 reference, squaring these differences, averaging over all elements, and taking the square root. This provides a single scalar summary of the numerical error that can be compared across implementations and precision levels.

# FP16 Numerical Error: FlashAttention-2 and FlashAttention-3 Both 1.7x Better Than Standard

In FP16 precision, the numerical error results reveal that both FlashAttention-2 and FlashAttention-3 achieve 1.7$\times$ lower RMSE compared to a standard implementation of attention, when measured against the FP64 reference. This finding is significant because it demonstrates that the algorithmic innovations introduced in FlashAttention-3—warp-specialization, pingpong scheduling, and 2-stage GEMM-softmax pipelining—do not degrade numerical accuracy relative to its predecessor.

The reason for this improved accuracy over the standard implementation is rooted in how the FlashAttention family of algorithms manages intermediate computations. In a standard attention implementation, the intermediate score matrix $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ and the probability matrix $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$ are materialized to HBM (global memory). When these matrices are written to and read from HBM, they are typically stored in FP16 precision to conserve memory bandwidth. This means that the softmax computation, which involves the exponential function and row-wise normalization, is effectively carried out in FP16 precision after the initial FP16 matrix multiplication, introducing rounding errors at each step.

In contrast, both FlashAttention-2 and FlashAttention-3 keep the intermediate results—specifically, the softmax rescaling factors, the running maximum $m_i$, the running sum $\ell_i$, and the blocks of the score matrix $\mathbf{S}$—in FP32 (single precision) registers throughout the computation. The matrix multiplications themselves are accumulated in FP32, as this is the native accumulator precision for both FP16 and FP8 WGMMA instructions on the Hopper GPU. By maintaining these intermediate results in FP32 rather than truncating them to FP16, the FlashAttention algorithms avoid the compounding rounding errors that plague the standard implementation. The rescaling of the output $\mathbf{O}_i$ by the factor $\mathrm{exp}(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i)$, which is a critical part of the online softmax algorithm, is also performed in FP32, further preserving numerical fidelity.

The fact that FlashAttention-3 matches the FP16 accuracy of FlashAttention-2 is particularly noteworthy given the more aggressive overlapping of operations in FlashAttention-3. The 2-stage pipelining scheme interleaves WGMMA and softmax operations across iterations, which could in principle introduce additional sources of numerical error if, for example, the rescaling of $\mathbf{O}_i$ from a previous iteration is not properly synchronized with the accumulation of new results. However, the algorithm is carefully designed so that each block of $\mathbf{O}_i$ is rescaled by the correct factor before being accumulated with the new partial result, and all rescaling is performed in FP32. The numerical validation confirms that this careful design succeeds in maintaining the same level of accuracy as the simpler, sequential FlashAttention-2 algorithm.

# FP8 Numerical Error: FlashAttention-3 with Block Quantization and Incoherent Processing 2.6x Better Than Baseline

The FP8 numerical error results represent one of the most significant contributions of FlashAttention-3, demonstrating that the combination of block quantization and incoherent processing can substantially mitigate the accuracy challenges inherent to low-precision attention computation. The results show that FlashAttention-3 in FP8 achieves 2.6$\times$ lower RMSE than a baseline FP8 attention implementation that uses standard per-tensor quantization.

The FP8 format used in FlashAttention-3 is the e4m3 format, which allocates 4 bits for the exponent and 3 bits for the mantissa (plus a sign bit). This provides a dynamic range similar to FP16 but with dramatically reduced precision in the mantissa—only 3 bits means that only 8 distinct mantissa values are representable within each exponent binade. When outliers are present in the input tensors, per-tensor quantization must choose a single scale factor that accommodates the largest magnitude values in the entire tensor. This large scale factor means that the majority of values, which are much smaller, are quantized to only a few distinct FP8 representable values, leading to severe information loss.

FlashAttention-3 addresses this problem through two complementary techniques. The first is block quantization: instead of using a single scale factor per tensor, the algorithm maintains one scale factor per block. Specifically, for each of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$, the tensor is split into blocks of size $B_r \times d$ or $B_c \times d$ (matching the block structure already used by the FlashAttention algorithm), and each block is quantized independently. This means that an outlier confined to one block only affects the scale factor for that block, leaving the quantization of other blocks unaffected. Since the FlashAttention-3 algorithm naturally operates on blocks, the rescaling of each block of $\mathbf{S}$ to account for the per-block quantization scales can be performed at essentially no computational cost—it is simply a multiplication by a known scalar that can be folded into the existing rescaling logic. Moreover, the block quantization itself can be fused with a preceding operation such as rotary embedding, which is memory-bandwidth bound and thus has spare computational capacity to absorb the quantization overhead without slowing down.

The second technique is incoherent processing, which directly targets the outlier problem at a more fundamental level. The key idea is to multiply both $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ before quantizing to FP8. Since $\mathbf{M}$ is orthogonal, $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top = \mathbf{I}$, and therefore:

$$(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top\mathbf{K}^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top,$$

meaning that the attention output is unchanged by this transformation. The effect of multiplying by $\mathbf{M}$ is to spread out the outliers: each entry of $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}$ or $\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M}$ is a weighted sum of multiple entries of $\mathbf{Q}$ or $\mathbf{K}$, which by the central limit theorem tends to produce values with more uniform magnitudes. Extreme outliers are diluted because they are combined with many non-outlier values, resulting in a distribution that is more amenable to uniform quantization. In practice, rather than using a fully random orthogonal matrix (which would require $O(d^2)$ computation to apply), the authors follow prior work and choose $\mathbf{M}$ to be the product of random diagonal matrices of $\pm 1$ and a Hadamard matrix. This structured orthogonal matrix can be applied in $O(d \log d)$ time and, like block quantization, can be fused with the rotary embedding operation at no additional computational cost.

The combined effect of these two techniques is a 2.6$\times$ reduction in RMSE compared to the baseline FP8 implementation. This is a substantial improvement that makes FP8 attention a viable option for practical use, where the 2$\times$ throughput gain of FP8 over FP16 (reaching close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s on the H100) would otherwise be undermined by unacceptable accuracy degradation, particularly in models with prominent outlier features.

# Baseline FP8 Attention Configuration: Per-Tensor Scaling, FP32 Accumulator, FP16 Softmax

To provide a fair and meaningful comparison for the FP8 numerical error evaluation, the authors define a baseline FP8 attention implementation that represents a straightforward application of FP8 precision without the advanced techniques employed by FlashAttention-3. Understanding this baseline configuration is essential for appreciating the magnitude of the improvement achieved by block quantization and incoherent processing.

The baseline FP8 attention uses per-tensor scaling for quantization, meaning that a single scale factor is computed for each of the entire $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ tensors. This is the standard approach advocated in early FP8 training frameworks, where the scale factor is typically chosen as the maximum absolute value of the tensor (or a percentile thereof) to ensure that all values fall within the representable range of the FP8 format. While simple and efficient, per-tensor scaling is particularly vulnerable to outliers: a single large value in the tensor forces the scale factor to be large, which in turn means that all other values are quantized to a coarse grid of FP8 representable numbers, losing significant precision.

In terms of the computation itself, the baseline performs the matrix multiplications in FP8 using the FP8 Tensor Cores, with the accumulators in FP32. This is a natural and necessary choice, as accumulating FP8 products directly in FP8 would lead to catastrophic precision loss due to the limited dynamic range of the format. The FP32 accumulator provides 23 bits of mantissa precision, which is sufficient to faithfully accumulate the partial sums of the matrix product. This aspect of the baseline is actually the same as in FlashAttention-3, since both use WGMMA instructions that natively accumulate in FP32.

Where the baseline differs more substantially is in the handling of the softmax intermediate results. In the baseline FP8 attention, the intermediate softmax computation—the exponential, row-wise maximum, and normalization—is performed in FP16 precision. This is a reasonable middle ground: FP16 provides more precision than FP8 for these sensitive non-linear operations, while being more memory-efficient than FP32. However, it is less precise than the approach used in FlashAttention-3, where (as in the FP16 case) the softmax intermediates are maintained in FP32 registers throughout the computation.

The comparison between this baseline and FlashAttention-3 in FP8 is thus designed to isolate the impact of the quantization strategy (per-tensor vs. block quantization with incoherent processing) while keeping other aspects of the computation as similar as possible. The 2.6$\times$ improvement in RMSE achieved by FlashAttention-3 can therefore be attributed primarily to the more sophisticated quantization approach, which adaptively handles the non-uniform distribution of values in real-world LLM activations. This result has important practical implications: it suggests that the throughput benefits of FP8 attention (nearly doubling the TFLOPs/s compared to FP16) can be realized without the severe accuracy penalties that would otherwise make FP8 attention unsuitable for high-quality model training and inference.

# Discussion, Limitations, and Future Directions

## Summary of Contributions: Asynchrony and Low-Precision for Attention Efficiency

FlashAttention-3 represents a significant advance in the efficient computation of attention on modern GPU hardware, demonstrating that explicit awareness of hardware asynchrony and low-precision capabilities can yield dramatic improvements in both speed and accuracy. The paper introduces three principal techniques that, together, propel attention utilization on the NVIDIA H100 (Hopper) GPU from approximately 35% under FlashAttention-2 to as high as 75% under FlashAttention-3 with FP16 precision, reaching up to 740 TFLOPs/s. With FP8 precision, the method approaches 1.2 PFLOPs/s, nearly doubling the FP16 throughput.

The first technique, producer-consumer asynchrony through warp-specialization, exploits the fact that Hopper GPUs possess dedicated hardware units for memory movement (the Tensor Memory Accelerator, or TMA) and matrix multiplication (Tensor Cores exposed via the warpgroup-wide WGMMA instruction), both of which operate asynchronously relative to the general-purpose CUDA cores. By assigning different warps within a cooperative thread array (CTA) to producer roles (issuing TMA loads from global memory to shared memory) and consumer roles (issuing WGMMA instructions and performing softmax computations), FlashAttention-3 creates a software pipeline that can hide memory and instruction issue latencies far more effectively than the synchronous model of FlashAttention-2. This warp-specialized design is further enhanced by the dynamic reallocation of registers between warpgroups via the `setmaxnreg` instruction, so that consumer warpgroups performing matrix multiplications obtain a larger share of the register file than producer warpgroups, which need only a single thread to issue TMA commands.

The second technique, overlapping softmax with asynchronous block-wise GEMMs, addresses a critical bottleneck: non-matmul operations such as the exponential function used in softmax have dramatically lower throughput than matmul operations. On the H100, FP16 matmul achieves 989 TFLOPs/s while special functions like `exp` achieve only 3.9 TFLOPs/s. Although the matmul operations dominate the total FLOP count by a factor of 512× for head dimension 128, the exponential's 256× lower throughput means it can consume up to 50% of the cycle time relative to matmul. FlashAttention-3 addresses this imbalance through two complementary pipelining strategies. First, a pingpong scheduling scheme uses synchronization barriers to interleave the GEMMs and softmax computations of two consumer warpgroups, so that the softmax of one warpgroup executes while the other performs its GEMMs, and vice versa. Second, an intra-warpgroup two-stage pipeline breaks sequential dependencies between the softmax of one iteration and the GEMMs of adjacent iterations by maintaining additional buffers for intermediate score matrices in registers, allowing the second WGMMA of iteration $j$ to overlap with the softmax of iteration $j+1$.

The third technique, hardware-accelerated low-precision GEMM with FP8, nearly doubles the measured throughput by targeting the FP8 Tensor Cores. This required overcoming significant engineering challenges, including bridging the layout conformance requirements between FP32 accumulators and FP8 operands through in-kernel transposition using LDSM/STSM instructions and register permutation via byte permute instructions. To mitigate the accuracy loss inherent to FP8's limited 3-bit mantissa, the paper introduces block quantization (maintaining one scaling scalar per block rather than per tensor) and incoherent processing (multiplying $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix before quantization to spread out outlier values). Together, these techniques reduce numerical error by 2.6× compared to standard FP8 attention with per-tensor quantization.

The empirical validation confirms that FlashAttention-3 achieves 1.5–2.0× speedup over FlashAttention-2 in the forward pass and 1.5–1.75× in the backward pass, with FP16 precision. Compared to a standard PyTorch attention implementation, FlashAttention-3 can be 3–16× faster. For medium and long sequences (1k tokens and above), it even surpasses the speed of NVIDIA's closed-source cuDNN library optimized for H100 GPUs. These results collectively demonstrate that algorithmic redesign informed by hardware characteristics—in particular, asynchrony and low-precision support—can have a transformative impact on the efficiency and accuracy of attention computation.

## Limitation: Optimization for LLM Inference (vs. Training Focus)

The paper acknowledges that FlashAttention-3 has been primarily evaluated and optimized in the context of training workloads, where the forward and backward passes are both exercised over a range of sequence lengths and head dimensions. While this focus is well-motivated—training large language models constitutes the dominant computational burden for the Transformer architecture—the authors note that optimizing for LLM inference remains an open challenge that has not been fully addressed in this work.

Inference workloads differ from training in several important respects that are relevant to attention optimization. During inference, particularly in the autoregressive decoding phase, the computation is often memory-bandwidth bound rather than compute bound, since each forward pass processes a small number of new query tokens against a potentially large cache of keys and values from prior context. This contrasts with the training regime, where large batches of queries are processed against the full key-value sequences, making the computation predominantly compute bound and thus well suited to the GEMM-heavy optimizations of FlashAttention-3. The paper notes that the pre-processing transpose of $\mathbf{V}$ in global memory—a requirement of the FP8 algorithm—is "too wasteful in a memory-bound situation such as inference," which is why the in-kernel transpose approach was adopted instead. This observation underscores the tension between the FP8 design choices optimized for training and the distinct demands of inference.

Furthermore, the paper does not evaluate FlashAttention-3 in scenarios involving key-value caching, which is a standard optimization in production inference systems. The interaction between FlashAttention-3's tiling strategy, its warp-specialized pipeline, and the memory access patterns of cached key-value tensors during inference remains unexplored. The authors list "optimizing for LLM inference" as the first of their stated limitations, suggesting that future work is needed to adapt the algorithm to the inference setting, where considerations such as prefill-decode phases, variable batch sizes, and the overhead of kernel launches for short sequences may require different algorithmic trade-offs than those optimized for training.

## Limitation: Persistent Kernel Design Not Yet Integrated into FP8 Kernel

A second limitation explicitly identified by the authors concerns the persistent kernel design and load balancing strategy. The paper reveals that the FP16 implementation of FlashAttention-3 incorporates a persistent kernel design, while the FP8 implementation does not. A persistent kernel is one that launches a fixed number of threadblocks that remain resident on the streaming multiprocessors (SMs) for the duration of the computation, processing multiple tiles of work in sequence, rather than launching a new threadblock for each tile. This design can reduce the overhead associated with kernel launches and improve SM occupancy, particularly for workloads with many small tiles.

The absence of a persistent kernel in the FP8 implementation has measurable performance consequences. The paper notes that this difference "partly explains why FP8 FlashAttention-3 does not perform as well for small sequence length and causal masking compared to the FP8 cuDNN kernels." When the sequence length is small, there are fewer tiles to process, and the overhead of kernel launch and scheduling becomes a larger fraction of the total runtime. A persistent kernel design would amortize this overhead by keeping threadblocks active across multiple tiles. Similarly, with causal masking, approximately half of the entries in the attention score matrix are skipped, which reduces the computational load per tile and exacerbates the relative impact of launch overhead.

The load balancing strategy, which presumably redistributes work across SMs to ensure uniform utilization, is also present only in the FP16 variant. Without it, the FP8 kernel may suffer from uneven work distribution, particularly when causal masking creates tiles of varying computational cost. The authors indicate that integrating a persistent kernel design into the FP8 kernel is a goal for future work, which would be expected to close the performance gap for small sequence lengths and causal masking scenarios.

This limitation is notable because it suggests that the full potential of FP8 FlashAttention-3 has not yet been realized. The FP8 results reported in the paper—approaching 1.2 PFLOPs/s—are achieved despite the absence of the persistent kernel optimization, implying that further improvements are possible. The gap between the FP8 and FP16 implementations in terms of software engineering completeness also highlights the practical challenges of adapting the sophisticated kernel infrastructure developed for one precision level to another, particularly when additional layout transformations and register permutations are required.

## Limitation: Understanding Low-Precision Attention Effects in Large-Scale Training

The third limitation identified by the authors concerns the understanding of how low-precision attention affects model training at scale. While the paper demonstrates that FP8 FlashAttention-3 achieves 2.6× lower numerical error than a baseline FP8 attention implementation with per-tensor quantization, and validates this on synthetic data designed to simulate outlier features in large language models, the question of whether this level of accuracy is sufficient for stable large-scale training remains open.

The synthetic distribution used for numerical error validation generates entries of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ according to $\mathcal{N}(0, 1) + \mathcal{N}(0, 100) \cdot \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.001)$, which models outlier features by adding, with probability 0.1%, an independent Gaussian term with standard deviation 10 to each entry. While this distribution captures the essential phenomenon of outliers that are much larger in magnitude than typical values—a known characteristic of activations in large language models—it does not capture the full complexity of real training dynamics. During large-scale training, the distribution of activations evolves over time, the correlation structure between queries, keys, and values changes across layers and training steps, and the interaction between attention quantization error and other sources of numerical noise (such as gradient noise, weight quantization, and stochastic regularization) is not well understood.

The paper notes that FP8 with the e4m3 format uses only 3 bits for the mantissa and 4 bits for the exponent, which inherently limits the representable precision. The techniques of block quantization and incoherent processing mitigate but do not eliminate quantization error. Block quantization reduces the dynamic range within each block by maintaining per-block scaling factors, while incoherent processing spreads outlier values across multiple entries through multiplication with a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ (implemented as the product of random diagonal matrices of $\pm 1$ and a Hadamard matrix for computational efficiency). These techniques reduce the root mean squared error (RMSE) by 2.6× relative to per-tensor quantization, but the paper does not evaluate whether this error level is acceptable for training convergence, model quality, or downstream task performance.

The authors list "understanding the effects of low-precision attention in large-scale training" as a key direction for future investigation. This includes questions such as whether the accumulated attention error over millions of training steps leads to degraded model quality, whether certain attention patterns (e.g., long-range dependencies, sparse attention) are more sensitive to quantization, and whether the block quantization granularity and incoherent processing parameters need to be adapted dynamically during training. These questions are critical for the practical adoption of FP8 attention in production training pipelines, where even small degradations in model quality can have significant consequences at scale.

## Generalizability: Applicability to Non-Hopper GPU Architectures

The paper repeatedly emphasizes that while the techniques of FlashAttention-3 are described and evaluated in the context of NVIDIA's Hopper architecture, the algorithmic principles are not specific to this platform. In the introduction, the authors include a footnote stating that "our algorithm is operative for any GPU architecture with sufficiently robust asynchronous execution and low-precision capabilities." In the discussion, they reiterate that "though we have focused on Hopper GPUs in this work, we expect that the techniques developed here will apply to other hardware accelerators."

This generalizability claim rests on the observation that the three core techniques—warp-specialized producer-consumer asynchrony, GEMM-softmax overlapping, and low-precision GEMM with accuracy mitigation—are motivated by architectural trends that extend beyond any single GPU vendor or generation. The separation of specialized hardware units for matrix multiplication, memory movement, and scalar computation is a broader industry trend, as is the progression toward lower numerical precisions. The paper traces this progression explicitly: FP16 was introduced on Pascal GPUs in 2017, BF16 on Ampere in 2020, FP8 on Hopper, and FP4 on Blackwell. Each step in this progression doubles or quadruples the available throughput for the same power and chip area, making low-precision computation an increasingly attractive target for optimization.

The warp-specialization technique requires that the GPU support asynchronous execution of data movement and computation by separate hardware units, so that producer and consumer warps can operate concurrently without stalling each other. The specific mechanisms on Hopper—TMA for asynchronous memory copies and asynchronous WGMMA for Tensor Core operations—are concrete instantiations of this requirement, but analogous capabilities exist or are emerging on other platforms. Similarly, the GEMM-softmax overlapping technique requires that matrix multiplication instructions be asynchronous (so that non-matmul operations can be interleaved with them), and the low-precision technique requires hardware support for reduced-precision Tensor Cores along with the ability to manage layout conformance between accumulators and operands.

The accuracy mitigation techniques of block quantization and incoherent processing are algorithmic in nature and do not depend on specific hardware features. Block quantization can be applied wherever per-tensor quantization is used, simply by maintaining finer-grained scaling factors. Incoherent processing, which multiplies $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ before quantization, can be fused with preceding operations (such as rotary embeddings) at no additional computation cost regardless of the target hardware. These techniques are therefore broadly portable.

However, the paper does not provide empirical validation on non-Hopper architectures, and the specific performance characteristics—such as the degree of overlap achievable, the register pressure trade-offs, and the effectiveness of the pingpong scheduling—may vary across platforms. The implementation details, including the use of `setmaxnreg` for register reallocation, `bar.sync` instructions for synchronization, and byte permute instructions for layout transformation, are Hopper-specific and would need to be adapted to the instruction sets and register file organizations of other architectures. The paper's claim of generalizability is therefore principled but unvalidated beyond Hopper, and the authors acknowledge that extending to other hardware is a direction for future work.

## Future Hardware: Blackwell GPUs and FP4 Precision Implications

The paper situates FlashAttention-3 within a broader trend of decreasing numerical precision in GPU architectures, noting that "low precision such as FP8 in Hopper and FP4 in Blackwell, continuing the trend of FP16 (Pascal in 2017) and BF16 (Ampere in 2020), is a proven technique to get double or quadruple throughput for the same power and chip area." This observation points toward the next generation of hardware—NVIDIA's Blackwell architecture—and the implications of FP4 precision for attention computation.

The progression from FP8 to FP4 would continue the pattern of doubling throughput for matrix multiplication, potentially enabling even faster attention computation. However, FP4 precision, with its extremely limited representational capacity (likely only 1 or 2 bits of mantissa depending on the specific format), would exacerbate the accuracy challenges already addressed for FP8. The techniques of block quantization and incoherent processing, which reduce FP8 numerical error by 2.6×, would become even more critical at FP4 precision, where the quantization error per element is substantially larger. It is reasonable to expect that finer-grained quantization (potentially per-row or per-column scaling rather than per-block) and more aggressive incoherent processing (possibly with multiple rounds of random orthogonal multiplication or alternative outlier suppression techniques) would be needed to maintain acceptable accuracy.

The layout conformance challenges that the paper describes for FP8—where the FP32 accumulator layout differs from the FP8 operand layout, requiring byte permute instructions to bridge the gap—may become even more stringent at FP4, potentially requiring additional transformation steps or different register manipulation strategies. The in-kernel transpose approach, which uses LDSM/STSM instructions to convert between mn-major and k-major layouts, may need to be adapted if FP4 Tensor Cores impose different layout requirements.

The paper does not provide specific details about Blackwell's architecture beyond the mention of FP4 support, and the techniques for FP4 attention remain speculative. However, the paper's framework—combining asynchrony for latency hiding, pipelining for GEMM-softmax overlap, and accuracy-aware low-precision quantization—provides a principled foundation for extending FlashAttention to future hardware. The authors' expectation that "the techniques developed here will apply to other hardware accelerators" implicitly encompasses future architectures like Blackwell, where the trend toward greater hardware specialization and lower precision continues.

## Open-Source Availability and Integration with PyTorch and Hugging Face

The authors commit to open-sourcing FlashAttention-3 under a permissive license, making the implementation available at `https://github.com/Dao-AILab/flash-attention`. This continues the tradition of the FlashAttention series, which has been widely adopted in the research and open-source communities. The decision to release the code with a permissive license is significant because it lowers the barrier to adoption, allowing researchers and practitioners to integrate the optimized attention kernel into their own projects without licensing restrictions.

Beyond simply releasing the source code, the authors announce their intention to integrate FlashAttention-3 with PyTorch and Hugging Face libraries. This integration strategy is designed to maximize the reach and impact of the work, as these libraries are among the most widely used frameworks for deep learning research and deployment. By integrating directly into PyTorch, FlashAttention-3 would become accessible as a drop-in replacement for standard attention in any model that uses PyTorch's attention APIs, automatically accelerating training and inference without requiring users to modify their model code. Similarly, integration with Hugging Face's transformer library would bring the performance benefits to the vast ecosystem of pre-trained models and fine-tuning pipelines built on top of it.

The open-source release is particularly valuable because FlashAttention-3's implementation involves intricate low-level GPU programming, including the use of CUTLASS primitives for WGMMA and TMA abstractions, manual register management via `setmaxnreg`, and careful instruction scheduling to achieve the desired overlap between GEMM and softmax operations. Reproducing these optimizations from scratch would be a substantial engineering effort, and the open-source release makes these advances immediately accessible to the community. The code also serves as a reference implementation for the algorithmic techniques described in the paper, enabling other researchers to build upon, validate, and extend the work.

The integration with popular libraries also addresses a practical concern: the gap between algorithmic research and production adoption. By providing integration paths into the standard deep learning software stack, the authors ensure that FlashAttention-3's performance improvements can be realized by practitioners who may not have the expertise or resources to implement the low-level GPU optimizations themselves. This approach has been instrumental in the adoption of prior versions of FlashAttention, which are now widely used in both research and production settings.

## Broader Impact: Enabling New Long-Context Applications

The paper opens by emphasizing that attention is the primary computational bottleneck of the Transformer architecture and that scaling to longer contexts will "unlock new capabilities." The authors enumerate several categories of applications that benefit from longer context windows, including modeling and reasoning over multiple long documents, processing files in large codebases, handling high-resolution images, audio, and video, supporting user interactions with long conversation histories, and enabling agent workflows with long planning horizons. Each of these applications requires processing sequences that may extend to tens or hundreds of thousands of tokens, making the quadratic cost of standard attention a fundamental barrier.

FlashAttention-3 directly addresses this barrier by reducing the constant factor in the quadratic cost. While the algorithm remains $O(N^2)$ in sequence length (since it computes exact attention without approximation), the 1.5–2.0× speedup over FlashAttention-2 and the up to 75% hardware utilization mean that the practical limit on context length is pushed further for a given computational budget. This has immediate implications for the long-context applications described in the paper. For document understanding, faster attention enables processing more documents simultaneously or processing longer individual documents within a reasonable time frame. For code understanding, it allows models to attend over larger portions of a codebase, potentially improving the ability to track dependencies and understand project-level structure. For multi-modal applications, it facilitates the processing of high-resolution visual or audio inputs that, when tokenized, produce very long sequences.

The FP8 results have particularly significant implications for long-context applications. By approaching 1.2 PFLOPs/s, FP8 FlashAttention-3 nearly doubles the throughput available for attention computation, which could be redirected toward extending context lengths, increasing batch sizes, or training larger models. The 2.6× accuracy improvement over standard FP8 attention, achieved through block quantization and incoherent processing, is critical because it means that the speedup of FP8 does not come at an unacceptable cost in model quality—a trade-off that would otherwise limit the practical utility of low-precision attention in applications where accuracy is paramount.

The paper also situates FlashAttention-3 within the broader landscape of approaches to scaling attention, noting that alternatives include approximate attention methods, other software optimizations, and alternative architectures such as RWKV, Retentive Networks, and Mamba. By improving exact attention to achieve 75% utilization on Hopper GPUs, FlashAttention-3 narrows the gap between exact and approximate methods, potentially reducing the incentive to accept the accuracy trade-offs inherent in approximation. The authors express the hope that "a faster and more accurate primitive such as attention will unlock new applications in long-context tasks," suggesting that the primary impact of this work may be not in the raw speedup numbers but in the new capabilities that become feasible when attention is no longer the binding bottleneck.

The combination of algorithmic innovation, hardware-aware optimization, open-source release, and library integration positions FlashAttention-3 as a foundational primitive that could accelerate progress across the entire ecosystem of Transformer-based models. As context lengths continue to grow in response to the demands of increasingly complex applications, the efficiency and accuracy of attention computation will remain a critical factor, and the techniques introduced in this work—warp-specialized asynchrony, GEMM-softmax pipelining, and accuracy-aware low-precision quantization—provide a framework for continued improvement on future hardware architectures.
