\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 10: Experimental Setup: Datasets, Baselines, and Protocols

# Datasets: Adult, Bank, Blood, Car, Communities, Credit-g, Diabetes, Heart, Myocardial, Cultivars, NHANES, Sequence-type, Solution-mix

This work evaluates FeatLLM on a deliberately diverse collection of 13 tabular datasets drawn from common benchmarks, recent UCI additions, and synthetic tasks. Each dataset is treated as a supervised classification task (binary or multi-class) in order to measure how well the framework extracts and uses rule-based features under few-shot conditions. Below are the datasets and the prediction targets used in the experiments (phrasing follows the experimental paper):

- Adult: predict whether an individual earns over \$50,000 annually.  
- Bank: predict whether a customer will subscribe to a term deposit.  
- Blood: predict whether donors will return for subsequent donations.  
- Car: predict the quality of a car.  
- Communities: predict the crime rate in a specified region.  
- Credit-g: predict whether an individual poses a good or bad credit risk.  
- Diabetes: predict the presence of diabetes in an individual.  
- Heart: predict the occurrence of coronary artery disease in an individual.  
- Myocardial: predict whether an individual suffers from chronic heart failure.  
- Cultivars: predict how high the grain yield of a soybean cultivar will be.  
- NHANES: predict the person's age group from their record.  
- Sequence-type: classify a given sequence as one of four types: arithmetic, geometric, Fibonacci, or Collatz.  
- Solution-mix: predict whether the percentage concentration of a mixture from four solutions exceeds 0.5.

These datasets vary in difficulty, number of attributes, and domain (healthcare, finance, synthetic math sequences, agriculture). Each dataset comes with natural-language feature names and definitions; FeatLLM uses those descriptions as part of its prompt design. The paper notes that some datasets such as Communities and Myocardial contain over 100 attributes, a detail that directly influences prompt construction and motivates the bagging/ensemble strategy described later.

When building the LLM prompts, the few-shot examples are serialized into textual records. The serialization used in the experiments is the same as in prior LLM-for-tabular work and is shown compactly as:

\[
\text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i, F) = ``\ f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ \ldots\ f_d\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \ \text{Answer:}\ \mathbf{y}^i\ ".
\]

In the above, $F=\{f_j\}_{j=1}^d$ are the human-readable feature names and $\mathbf{x}^i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is an example’s feature vector. For the k-shot regime studied in the paper, only a small number $k$ of labeled samples ($k < N$) are randomly sampled from the full dataset $\mathcal{D}$ to build prompts and to train the downstream lightweight models.

# High-dimensional cases (e.g., Communities, Myocardial) and prompt-length implications

A practical challenge when interfacing tabular data with LLMs is prompt length. Serializing every feature for every example grows the textual prompt quickly; datasets with many attributes (for example, Communities and Myocardial, which have on the order of 100+ attributes) can produce input longer than the model’s comfortable context window or degrade LLM performance when prompts become unwieldy. The paper emphasizes that previous LLM-based tabular methods suffer or become infeasible as the number of serialized features increases.

FeatLLM addresses this limitation with two design choices that are important for reproducing the experiments and for reasoning about performance:

1. Fixed, compact rule extraction: during each LLM query the model is instructed to output a fixed small set of rules per class (the experiments fix this to $R=10$ rules per class). Limiting the number of rules controls the downstream feature dimensionality and prevents runaway prompt or feature growth.

2. Bagging + ensemble: to cope with many original features without requiring extremely long prompts, FeatLLM applies a bagging-style subsampling of features and/or instances in each trial. In each ensemble trial the LLM sees only a subset of columns or examples, produces rules based on that smaller view, and the system turns those rules into binary features. Repeating this process across trials and averaging predictions yields coverage across the full feature space without ever requiring a single oversized prompt. This strategy reduces per-query prompt length and trades a modest number of LLM queries for robustness and scalability.

Beyond prompt length, high-dimensional inputs increase the risk that a naive, per-example LLM classifier learns spurious correlations from very few examples. FeatLLM’s prompt structure (see later sections) also mitigates this by encouraging the LLM to reason about which features are plausibly related to the task (a two-step reasoning instruction), which empirically reduces extraction of rules based on irrelevant columns.

# Newly added UCI datasets (post-2023) and synthetic sets to avoid LLM memorization

When evaluating systems that leverage large pretrained LLMs it is important to guard against testing on tasks or data that the LLM may have seen during its pretraining. The paper explicitly includes two relatively recent UCI datasets (Cultivars and NHANES) that were donated to the UCI Machine Learning Repository after September 2023, which helps ensure they were not part of the LLM API’s pre-training data (the experiments used the gpt-3.5-turbo-0613 API). In addition, two synthetic datasets (Sequence-type and Solution-mix) were included precisely because synthetic generation prevents memorization by pretraining: by construction they could not have been present in the LLM’s training corpora. These choices strengthen claims about the LLM’s ability to use general prior knowledge and the few-shot examples, rather than merely recalling memorized dataset-specific artifacts.

# Baselines: LogReg, XGBoost, RandomForest, SCARF, STUNT, TabPFN, In-context, TABLET, TabLLM

The paper compares FeatLLM against a broad set of baselines spanning traditional machine learning, semi-/self-supervised approaches for tabular data, and prior LLM-based methods. Knowing the role and assumptions of each baseline helps interpret experimental results.

- Logistic regression (LogReg), XGBoost, RandomForest: standard supervised tabular learners. These serve as strong and widely-used classical baselines.

- SCARF and STUNT: methods that exploit large amounts of unlabeled data to improve few-shot performance. The paper points out that access to unlabeled datasets in sufficient quantity may be infeasible in many real-world settings, but these baselines represent the class of semi-supervised or unsupervised-augmentation approaches.

- TabPFN: a method that mixes many synthetic distribution families to pretrain a model (a Bayesian neural network) which can then produce predictions for new tabular tasks with no additional training. TabPFN’s design is appealing for few-shot regimes thanks to the pre-learned prior it encodes.

- In-context learning: a direct LLM baseline where few-shot examples are embedded into a single prompt and the LLM is used end-to-end for predictions at inference time (one LLM call per test sample).

- TABLET: enhances in-context prompts with external hints generated from an auxiliary classifier in the form of rule-sets and prototypes to improve inference quality.

- TabLLM: an LLM-based tabular learner trained with parameter-efficient tuning techniques (for example IA3 or LoRA in other contexts). The TabLLM setup in the paper follows its original experimental choices and—importantly—restricts itself to LLMs with public checkpoints (for example the T0 family is used as in the original TabLLM paper). That restriction means the experimental replication used T0 for TabLLM rather than GPT-3.5, because GPT-3.5 is accessible only via an API and does not have a released trainable checkpoint.

When reproducing results, the paper used GPT-3.5 for in-context learning baselines and T0 for TabLLM per the original TabLLM setup. For the classical machine learning baselines (LogReg, XGBoost, RandomForest) the paper used grid-search combined with $k$-fold cross validation to select hyperparameters.

# FeatLLM settings: GPT-3.5, temperature ~0.5, $R=10$ rules/class, $T=20$ ensembles, Adam $\text{lr}=0.01$, 200 epochs

Reproducing FeatLLM requires a few concrete implementation choices and hyperparameters that were fixed in the experiments:

- LLM backbone and sampling settings. The experiments use GPT-3.5 (the gpt-3.5-turbo-0613 API) as the LLM backbone for feature (rule) generation. The sampling temperature is set to $0.5$ and top-$p$ is kept at the API default of $1$. The paper also notes that the framework is LLM-agnostic and reports results with other backbones in the Appendix.

- Rules and ensembles. Each LLM query is instructed to produce a fixed number of rules per class; in the experiments $R=10$ rules per class. To build a robust predictor the entire rule-generation and feature-parsing process is repeated $T=20$ times (ensemble trials), introducing randomness between trials through sampling temperature and by randomizing the order of few-shot demonstrations. Bagging is used to subsample features or instances per trial to control prompt length and to increase diversity across trials.

- Linear downstream model. After parsing rules into binary features, FeatLLM trains a simple linear model without bias for each class. Let $\mathbf{z}_k^i\in\{0,1\}^R$ denote the binary feature vector for sample $i$ and class $k$ (the indicators of whether each of the $R$ rules for class $k$ fired on that sample). The paper computes logits and probabilities as

\[
\text{logit}_k^i \;=\; \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i, \qquad
\mathbf{p}^i \;=\; \text{Softmax}([\text{logit}_1^i,\ldots,\text{logit}_c^i]),
\]

where $\mathbf{w}_k$ are trainable weights for class $k$, $c$ is the number of classes, and the $\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)$ projection enforces non-negativity so that generated features cannot directly reduce a class’s score. The linear model is trained using cross-entropy loss.

- Optimization and epochs. The linear model is optimized with the Adam optimizer at a learning rate of $0.01$ for up to $200$ epochs. To select the appropriate stopping epoch the experiments use $k$-fold cross validation (choice of $k$ is described below).

- Parsing LLM output into executable feature code. The LLM’s natural-language rules are parsed into executable code to transform the original tabular rows into binary rule features. Because LLM outputs can vary syntactically, FeatLLM uses the LLM itself to produce a short parsing function: the prompt includes the function signature, input/output descriptions, and the inferred rules, and the LLM returns a concise program snippet which is then executed via Python’s \texttt{exec()} to convert data into binary features.

- Ensembling prediction. The final prediction for sample $i$ is the average across the $T$ trials of the class probability vectors produced by each trial’s trained linear model. Averaging over trials increases robustness to incorrect rules produced in any single trial.

# Model selection with $k$-fold CV; grid search for classical baselines; TabLLM with T0 per original setup

To make fair comparisons, the experiments follow standard model-selection protocols:

- Classical baselines (LogReg, XGBoost, RandomForest) are tuned via grid-search combined with $k$-fold cross-validation. The paper sets the value of $k$ to either $2$ or $4$ depending on the shot regime, chosen so that every fold’s training set contains at least one example of each class (this is important in extreme low-shot settings).

- For FeatLLM’s lightweight linear model, $k$-fold cross validation is used to choose the optimal training epoch (early stopping) among the 200 epochs.

- LLM-based baselines: in-context learning baselines used the same GPT-3.5 API as FeatLLM for fair comparison of end-to-end LLM inference. TabLLM is reproduced using the publicly-available T0 model in accordance with the original TabLLM setup because TabLLM’s protocol requires a trainable checkpoint (GPT-3.5 is available only as an API, not as a trainable checkpoint).

These choices are intended to avoid giving undue advantage to any single method due to hyperparameter or model-selection differences.

# Compute environment and API usage notes

A few practical implementation and compute details are relevant when reproducing or extending the experiments:

- Hardware used for timing experiments. The runtime comparison reported in the paper (on the Adult dataset with a 16-shot training set) uses one A100 GPU as the default compute device for methods that have local training or inference; TabLLM required model parallelism and was evaluated using four A100 GPUs. These hardware choices affect wall-clock training and inference time but do not alter algorithmic behavior.

- API usage and cost considerations. A key design point of FeatLLM is that it requires only API-level access to the LLM (no LLM fine-tuning). The system uses a modest number of LLM queries during feature engineering: the paper reports that FeatLLM requires only 30 API queries in the reported experimental setup, and that these queries can be parallelized. After feature generation and lightweight linear-model training, inference is performed without any per-sample LLM queries; this makes FeatLLM’s inference cost comparable to classical tabular models and markedly lower than end-to-end LLM baselines that require an LLM call per test sample.

- Prompt sampling choices to encourage diversity. To generate diverse rule sets across ensemble trials, the system uses stochastic sampling from the LLM (temperature $=0.5$ in the experiments) and randomizes the order of few-shot demonstrations in the prompt; both mechanisms introduce trial-to-trial variability that the ensemble can exploit.

# Protocols, metrics, and practical recommendations

The empirical protocol in the paper is configured to give statistically meaningful comparisons under low-shot conditions and to make results reproducible:

- Repetitions and metrics. Experiments are repeated three times with different random choices of training/test splits (and other stochastic elements), and results are reported as the mean and standard deviation of the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC). Reporting both mean and standard deviation helps assess average performance and variability in low-data regimes.

- Hyperparameter choices and sensitivity. The paper studies two central hyperparameters: the number of ensemble trials $T$ and the number of rules per class $R$. They find that increasing $T$ improves performance up to a point (performance gains flatten around $T\approx 20$ in their experiments), and that $R=10$ is a reasonable trade-off between richer feature descriptions and prompt/feature-space blowup in the few-shot setting. These empirical observations informed the fixed choices used across the reported results.

- Practical trade-offs. When reproducing experiments, keep in mind the following practical points emphasized by the authors: (1) bagging subsamples of features and examples help keep prompt sizes manageable and also increase ensemble diversity; (2) forcing a fixed, modest number of rules per class controls the downstream feature dimensionality; (3) parsing LLM outputs via small code snippets produced by the LLM itself is robust to surface-level syntactic variations; and (4) because FeatLLM performs no per-sample LLM calls at test time, it is substantially more efficient at inference than end-to-end LLM baselines.

Taken together, these dataset choices, baseline replications, hyperparameter settings, and compute considerations form a consistent experimental setup. They enable a fair comparison between FeatLLM and both classical and LLM-augmented baselines in low-shot tabular learning, while addressing important practical constraints like prompt length, LLM access model (API vs. trainable checkpoint), and the risk of dataset memorization by pretrained LLMs.
