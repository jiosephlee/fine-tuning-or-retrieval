\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 1: Motivation and Overview of FeatLLM

# Importance of tabular prediction in real-world applications (finance, healthcare)
Tabular data—rows of records and columns of attributes—is the dominant data format in many high-stakes domains. In finance, transactional and customer records determine credit risk, fraud detection, and investment decisions; in healthcare, structured patient records and lab values inform diagnoses, risk stratification, and treatment planning. In both domains, the cost of labels is often high: medical labels require expert review, and financial outcomes may be rare or delayed. Because of this, practitioners routinely face settings with only a few labeled examples per task, yet need reliable, explainable predictions. These constraints make it essential to design learning systems that do well with small labeled datasets, can leverage prior knowledge, and produce outputs that domain experts can inspect and trust.

The FeatLLM paper motivates its approach from this practical reality: powerful prior knowledge encoded in large language models (LLMs) can be valuable for tabular tasks, but the way we apply that knowledge must respect data-efficiency, interpretability, and operational constraints (such as limited access to model internals). FeatLLM aims to bridge domain prior knowledge and lightweight predictors in a way that is suitable for real-world tabular learning problems—especially those in finance and healthcare where both data scarcity and interpretability matter.

# Few-shot tabular challenges: scarce labels, overfitting, spurious correlations
Few-shot tabular learning is hard for several interlocking reasons. First, with only a handful of labeled examples ($k$ shots), a flexible model can easily overfit the idiosyncrasies of those samples rather than learn generalizable relationships. This leads to brittle models that perform poorly on new data. Second, tabular datasets are heterogeneous: columns can be numeric, categorical, or special structured values, and there is no single set of augmentations or invariances that apply across datasets the way image flips or crops do for vision. The paper emphasizes that common few-shot strategies—data augmentation, contrastive objectives, or naive transfer—are often ineffective for tabular data because of this heterogeneity. Third, spurious correlations are a persistent risk: when only a few labels are available, coincidental associations between irrelevant features and the label can dominate training, leading to wrong conclusions and poor generalization.

Because of these challenges, successful few-shot solutions tend to rely on additional sources of inductive bias—either from other datasets, handcrafted rules, or strong priors. The FeatLLM approach leverages the rich, general prior knowledge contained in LLMs as a source of bias, but it does so in a way intended to minimize overfitting and avoid amplifying spurious correlations from the few available examples.

# Limitations of current LLM-for-tables: per-sample LLM inference, fine-tuning access barriers, prompt-length limits
Recent work has tried to use LLMs directly for tabular prediction by serializing table rows into text and asking the model to predict labels. While this can work in some few-shot settings, the paper points out three practical limitations that hamper real-world deployment.

First, most end-to-end LLM approaches require at least one LLM call per test sample at inference time. That makes inference computationally expensive and latency-sensitive systems impractical because each prediction must pass through the large model. Second, achieving high accuracy often requires fine-tuning or parameter-efficient tuning of the LLM (e.g., LoRA/IA3). For many state-of-the-art LLMs, however, only black-box API access is available; there is no way to fine-tune the model or obtain its checkpoints. That blocks practical application of fine-tuning–dependent methods on the very models that have the strongest priors. Third, the standard serialization approach (writing every feature value into the prompt) runs into context-window limits when tables have many columns. Long prompts either become infeasible or degrade performance, preventing straightforward scaling to datasets with hundreds of attributes.

Together, these limitations motivate a different use of LLMs: instead of placing the LLM in the prediction loop for every sample, can we harness LLMs once to produce informative artifacts (features, rules) and then use cheap downstream models for fast inference?

# Core idea: use LLMs to extract human-like rules as features instead of making end-to-end predictions
FeatLLM reframes the role of an LLM from a per-sample predictor to an automated feature engineer. The central idea is simple and powerful: ask the LLM to read the task description, feature names and definitions, and a small set of examples, and then to produce human-readable decision rules that characterize each class. Each rule is a condition on original features (for example, "age > 65 and ejection_fraction < 35") that can be evaluated deterministically on every row. Those rule evaluations become binary features: for a given sample, a rule feature is 1 if the sample satisfies the condition and 0 otherwise.

This approach has several intuitions behind it. Rules are compact, interpretable encodings of domain knowledge; they can capture prior knowledge that the LLM learned during pretraining (for example, risk factors that are known clinically). By transforming rules into binary features, the inference-time model becomes a simple, cheap classifier (in the paper, a linear model with non-negative weights) that is trained only on the few labeled points to learn which rules matter for the target task. Because the LLM is only used to generate rule templates once (or a bounded number of times for ensembling), there is no need to query the LLM at inference time, eliminating per-sample inference cost. Because the LLM is prompted to reason explicitly about relationships before deriving rules, FeatLLM reduces the chance that the model latches onto spurious correlations in the few-shot examples.

A critical design element is structured prompting: the LLM is guided through a two-step reasoning instruction. In Step 1 the model is asked to use prior knowledge (without being shown examples) to infer which features are likely to relate to the task; in Step 2 it combines that prior knowledge with the few-shot examples to produce concrete, testable rules for each class. This two-stage approach encourages the LLM to focus on semantically relevant features and to avoid overfitting purely to the few examples.

# Pipeline at a glance: rule extraction, code parsing, binary feature generation, simple downstream model, ensembling
The FeatLLM pipeline consists of a small number of clearly separated stages.

Prompting and rule extraction. Each query to the LLM contains three prompt components designed to make rule extraction reliable. The basic information description includes the task phrased as a question, natural-language feature names with types/definitions, and a small set of serialized examples. The paper uses the following serialization for an example $(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i)$ with feature names $F=\{f_j\}_{j=1}^d$:
\[
\text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i, F) = \text{`` } f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ \ldots\ f_d\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \text{Answer: }\mathbf{y}^i\text{''}.
\]
The reasoning instruction then guides the LLM through the two-step thought process described above (general feature-task relationships, then example-conditioned rule derivation). Finally, the response instruction constrains the output format so that the resulting rules are structured in a consistent way for downstream parsing (for example, a fixed number of rules per class).

Parsing rules into executable code. Because LLM rule outputs are natural language and can vary syntactically, FeatLLM uses the LLM again as a parser: the prompt asks the LLM to translate the rules into simple program code (Python). That generated code is executed (the paper runs it via Python's exec()) to compute binary rule-features for every sample. This leverages the LLM's strength at mapping flexible natural language into programmatic logic and avoids brittle handcrafted parsing.

Binary feature generation and simple training. The output of parsing is a set of rule-based binary features. For class $k$, each sample $i$ has a binary vector $\mathbf{z}_k^i\in\{0,1\}^R$ indicating which of the $R$ rules for that class are satisfied. FeatLLM then fits a simple linear model (one per class, combined through a softmax) to learn the importance of each rule. The paper uses a non-negativity projection on weights so that features cannot actively reduce the score for a class; the logit and probability computation is:
\begin{align}
\text{logit}_k^i &= \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i, \\
\mathbf{p}^i &= \text{Softmax}\big([\text{logit}_1^i,\ldots,\text{logit}_c^i]\big).
\end{align}
The model is trained by minimizing cross-entropy on the few labeled examples to learn the weights $\mathbf{w}_k$, which effectively selects and weights the LLM-generated features.

Bagging and ensembling for robustness and prompt-length control. To increase robustness and to avoid exceeding prompt-length limits, FeatLLM repeats the rule-generation process $T$ times with randomization (e.g., randomized example order and a nonzero sampling temperature to encourage diverse rules) and with bagging over features or instances in each trial. Each trial produces a small rule set and a trained weight vector $\mathbf{w}[t]$; final predictions average the per-trial probabilities. This ensemble reduces the impact of any single bad rule set and lets each trial keep its prompt short by focusing on a subset of features or examples.

Implementation choices used in experiments include extracting $R=10$ rules per class and using $T=20$ ensembles; the backbone LLM in the reported experiments is GPT-3.5. The linear weights are trained with Adam (learning rate 0.01) for up to 200 epochs with cross-validation for early stopping. Because the LLM is only queried a limited number of times (the paper reports only a few dozen API queries for training), the approach is practical under API-only access budgets.

# Key advantages: API-only access, no LLM at inference, prompt-size mitigation via bagging, interpretability benefits
FeatLLM combines several practical advantages that stem directly from its design.

API-only access and no fine-tuning need. FeatLLM requires only LLM inferences to generate rule sets; it does not need to fine-tune the LLM. That means it works with large closed-box models that only expose an inference API, addressing a major deployment constraint for state-of-the-art LLMs. Because the learned downstream predictor is a small model trained on rule features, all inference for new samples is fast and cheap; there is no per-sample API call.

Lower inference cost and operational feasibility. By moving the expensive LLM reasoning out of the per-sample loop, FeatLLM achieves inference-time costs comparable to conventional tabular learners (e.g., logistic regression), while still leveraging LLM priors. The paper's runtime comparisons show that FeatLLM's inference time is similar to conventional methods and far lower than methods that query the LLM for each test point.

Prompt-size mitigation and coverage via bagging. Because each ensemble trial only needs to consider a subset of columns and examples, the prompts remain short enough even for datasets with many attributes (the paper evaluates datasets with over 100 attributes). Bagging over features also increases coverage: different trials can focus on different parts of the feature space, and the ensemble aggregates those complementary perspectives.

Interpretability and human-readability. The generated features are explicit, human-readable rules that domain experts can inspect and reason about. This aids debugging and trust: a clinician or analyst can review the top-weighted rules and judge whether they make sense. Additionally, the downstream training step assigns weights to rules and can filter out rules that receive negligible weight, producing a compact, interpretable model.

Robustness to spurious features. Because the prompt explicitly asks the LLM to first reason about feature–label relationships using prior knowledge before producing rules, and because an ensemble averages across multiple rule sets, FeatLLM tends to generate fewer rules based on irrelevant or noisy columns. In the paper's controlled experiment where many spurious columns are appended, FeatLLM showed smaller performance degradation than alternative methods, indicating its rules-focused strategy reduces the impact of spurious correlations.

# Empirical summary: robust gains across 13 datasets; average ~10% AUC improvement vs TabLLM and STUNT in few-shot
The paper evaluates FeatLLM on a diverse collection of 13 datasets, including standard benchmarks (Adult, Bank, Credit-g, Heart, Diabetes, Myocardial, Communities), more recently added datasets (Cultivars, NHANES), and synthetic tasks (Sequence-type, Solution-mix) used to guard against memorization effects. These tasks cover binary and multiclass targets and include some datasets with over 100 features—challenging cases for prompt-based LLM methods.

Across these datasets in low-shot settings, FeatLLM consistently ranks first or second among the baselines considered. Baselines include conventional tabular learners (logistic regression, XGBoost, RandomForest), few-shot and pretraining-based methods (STUNT, SCARF, TabPFN), and LLM-based approaches (in-context prompting, TABLET, TabLLM). Notably, FeatLLM achieves substantial improvements in the few-shot regime: the paper reports an average improvement of roughly 10% in AUC when compared to strong LLM-based baselines such as TabLLM and STUNT. In some cases, FeatLLM with only 4 labeled examples is comparable to conventional tabular learners that were trained with 32 labeled examples, illustrating effective use of prior knowledge.

Additional analyses in the paper support why FeatLLM works in practice. An ablation study shows that the two-step reasoning instruction, inclusion of feature descriptions, weight tuning of the linear model, and ensembling each contribute positively to performance. Quality checks indicate that a majority of LLM-generated rules have positive signal (many have per-rule AUROC > 0.5 before tuning), and the downstream tuning step further de-prioritizes irrelevant rules. In runtime comparisons, FeatLLM requires only a modest number of API queries (the paper notes around thirty in its setup) and achieves inference-time efficiency comparable to standard models.

Taken together, the motivation, algorithmic design, and empirical results in the paper illustrate a practical way to leverage LLMs’ general knowledge for tabular few-shot learning: use the LLM as a feature engineer to produce compact, interpretable rules that a cheap downstream model can exploit. This reframing addresses key deployment constraints (API-only models, latency, context-window limits) while improving few-shot accuracy and maintaining interpretability—a blend that is highly attractive for real-world tabular applications such as finance and healthcare.
