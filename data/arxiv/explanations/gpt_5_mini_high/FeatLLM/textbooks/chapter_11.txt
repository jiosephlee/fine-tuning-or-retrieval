\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 11: Results: Accuracy, Robustness, and Efficiency

# AUC comparisons across datasets: FeatLLM as top or second performer
The primary quantitative claim in the paper is that FeatLLM attains consistently strong predictive performance across a diverse set of tabular tasks. The evaluation used 13 datasets spanning domains (finance, healthcare, synthetic tasks, etc.), and performance was reported using the area under the ROC curve (AUC), averaged over three random train/test splits (with mean and standard deviation). Across these datasets and few-shot settings, FeatLLM either ranked first or second among the set of baselines. Concretely, the paper reports that FeatLLM yields large improvements over other LLM-based approaches and traditional baselines, with an average advantage on the order of 10% versus alternatives such as TabLLM and STUNT.

Why AUC matters here: AUC measures the tradeoff between true positive rate and false positive rate across thresholds, and is widely used for tabular binary and multi-class problems where class imbalance can matter. The paper uses AUC as a consistent scalar to compare few-shot behavior across heterogeneous tasks; the consistent ranking of FeatLLM implies that the rule-based features it generates produce discriminative signals that generalize across domains even when only a few labeled examples are available.

Intuition for the superior accuracy. FeatLLM leverages two complementary mechanisms to achieve this performance. First, the LLM injects domain priors and commonsense reasoning into compact, interpretable rules that are converted into binary features; these features often capture informative decision boundaries that a simple linear combination can turn into accurate class likelihoods. Second, ensembling (multiple LLM rule-generation trials with bagging) reduces the chance that any single noisy rule-set dominates predictions. Finally, a small downstream training stage (learning non-negative weights over the rule features) adapts the LLM-derived signals to the observed few-shot data, boosting accuracy further.

# Data efficiency: 4-shot FeatLLM vs 32-shot classical baselines
One of the standout empirical claims is that FeatLLM attains the same level of performance with far fewer labeled examples than many classical tabular learners. The paper states that, in their experiments, FeatLLM with only 4 labeled examples (4-shot) achieves comparable performance to traditional methods trained with 32 labeled examples on the same tasks. This is a concrete expression of data efficiency: FeatLLM relies on the LLM’s prior knowledge to produce features that substitute for much of the label information that would otherwise be needed to learn useful representations from scratch.

Why this happens, intuitively. In few-shot regimes the dominant challenge is insufficient signal to estimate parameters or discover meaningful feature transformations. FeatLLM sidesteps that by having the LLM propose domain-relevant conditions (rules) that partition the input space in ways aligned with the task. Those rules act like distilled expert features: each rule can be thought of as a high-signal engineered feature. With even a handful of labels, the downstream linear model can learn which of those rules are predictive (by learning non-negative weights) rather than having to discover structure only from the few labels. The paper’s ablation further corroborates this intuition: the benefit of “reasoning” and supplying feature descriptions is especially large in the lowest-shot regimes, while the benefit of weight tuning grows as more labeled data become available.

# Handling many-feature datasets where direct LLM baselines struggle or fail
A common and practical bottleneck for LLM-based tabular methods that serialize whole rows into text is the context-window (prompt length) limitation. When datasets have many columns (the paper cites examples like Communities and Myocardial with over $100$ attributes), serializing full rows plus demonstrations can exceed feasible prompt sizes or degrade performance.

FeatLLM approaches this problem structurally. Rather than forcing all features into a single large prompt or invoking the LLM per test sample, FeatLLM uses feature bagging and ensembling: each LLM query focuses on a small, random subset of columns and a small set of demonstrations, generating a compact set of rules for that subset. Repeating this process across many trials (the paper typically used $T=20$ ensembles and $R=10$ rules per class per trial) collects diverse rule-based features that, when parsed and combined, provide wide coverage of the original feature space without ever requiring a single enormous prompt. Because each LLM interaction is limited to a manageable serialization, this design overcomes prompt length constraints while still recovering predictive structure across many attributes.

By contrast, the paper notes that other LLM-based baselines (in-context learning, TABLET, TabLLM) were unable to infer on datasets with a large number of features: either the prompt became infeasible or performance declined sharply. FeatLLM’s bagging + ensemble design therefore provides a practical, scalable path for applying LLM priors to wide tables.

# Training and inference time: low inference latency comparable to classical ML
A key practical advantage of FeatLLM is that the costly LLM interactions are confined to an up-front feature-engineering phase; at inference time the system runs a compact learned model over binary rule features, avoiding per-sample LLM calls. This design leads to low inference latency comparable to classical ML methods (e.g., logistic regression, RandomForest, XGBoost), as reported in the runtime comparisons on the Adult dataset (with a 16-shot training set). In the paper’s experimental setup, FeatLLM’s inference time was similar to conventional approaches, while other LLM-based methods (which route each test sample through an LLM or require heavy fine-tuning) had substantially higher inference times.

On the training side, FeatLLM is also practical: the downstream training is simply fitting a linear model (non-negative weights) to the binary features. The LLM interactions required to generate rule-sets are modest in number and can be parallelized. The paper reports only a small fixed number of API queries for rule generation (see the next section), and uses a standard optimizer (Adam, learning rate $0.01$, up to $200$ epochs) with $k$-fold cross-validation to select epochs. In summary, FeatLLM trades a small, one-time LLM cost for fast, classical inference thereafter.

# Cost profile: limited API calls vs per-sample LLM inference in other methods
From an operational and monetary perspective, the difference between a one-time or limited number of LLM queries and per-sample LLM inference is large. FeatLLM is explicitly designed to minimize API usage: it confines LLM calls to the rule-generation and rule-parsing phases. The paper emphasizes that FeatLLM required only about $30$ API queries in their setup — a small and parallelizable budget — and after that the system performs all predictions without further LLM access. This contrasts with many in-context approaches that must send every test sample to the LLM, incurring per-sample latency and cost that scale linearly with the number of predictions.

There is also a practical accessibility advantage: FeatLLM operates with only API-level access to an LLM (the paper used GPT-3.5 in experiments), avoiding the need for training or fine-tuning capabilities that are not available for many leading LLMs. Methods that require parameter-efficient fine-tuning or access to model checkpoints (e.g., TabLLM in its original design) are more difficult to apply in settings where only an inference API is available.

# Situations where STUNT/TabPFN shine and their overall trade-offs
The paper evaluates other modern few-shot or semi-supervised tabular approaches and notes where they excel and where they fall short. Two such methods are STUNT and TabPFN:

- TabPFN pre-trains a model on a large mixture of synthetic distributions so that at test time it can perform Bayesian-like inference on small datasets without further training. This synthetic pretraining can produce excellent few-shot behavior on some tasks because it provides a very broad prior over plausible data-generating processes.

- STUNT leverages large quantities of unlabeled data (and augmentation strategies) to improve few-shot performance via semi-supervised learning.

Where these methods shine. The paper observes that STUNT and TabPFN produced significant improvements on certain individual datasets — precisely those where their respective priors (synthetic distributions for TabPFN, or available unlabeled data for STUNT) match the target task characteristics. When those favorable conditions hold, these methods can outperform conventional learners and even some LLM-based approaches.

The trade-offs. However, the paper emphasizes that these gains are not uniformly realized across the diverse set of tasks considered. TabPFN’s synthetic pretraining is a fixed prior that may not match all real-world distributions; STUNT’s reliance on plentiful unlabeled data is often impractical in applied scenarios. Empirically, their overall enhancement over standard classical models was not consistently large across the 13 datasets. In contrast, FeatLLM’s use of LLM priors plus an ensemble bagging strategy is more flexible: it requires only a modest number of labeled examples and API access to an LLM, and it scales to many-feature tables without needing extra unlabeled datasets or heavy pretraining.

Concluding remarks
The experimental results in the paper together make a consistent case: by using LLMs as feature engineers (producing interpretable rules that are parsed into binary features), and then fitting a lightweight downstream model, FeatLLM achieves a favorable combination of accuracy, robustness, and efficiency in low-shot tabular learning. Its ensemble and bagging design handles many-feature tables and limits prompt sizes, its one-time LLM usage keeps inference fast and cheap, and its feature-tuning step adapts generated rules to observed labels. Ablations reported in the paper (removing tuning, ensemble, feature descriptions, or the reasoning step) all reduce performance, underlining that the accuracy, robustness, and efficiency are consequences of the full design working together.
