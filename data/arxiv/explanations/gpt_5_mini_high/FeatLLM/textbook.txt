\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Motivation and Overview of FeatLLM

# Importance of tabular prediction in real-world applications (finance, healthcare)
Tabular data—rows of records and columns of attributes—is the dominant data format in many high-stakes domains. In finance, transactional and customer records determine credit risk, fraud detection, and investment decisions; in healthcare, structured patient records and lab values inform diagnoses, risk stratification, and treatment planning. In both domains, the cost of labels is often high: medical labels require expert review, and financial outcomes may be rare or delayed. Because of this, practitioners routinely face settings with only a few labeled examples per task, yet need reliable, explainable predictions. These constraints make it essential to design learning systems that do well with small labeled datasets, can leverage prior knowledge, and produce outputs that domain experts can inspect and trust.

The FeatLLM paper motivates its approach from this practical reality: powerful prior knowledge encoded in large language models (LLMs) can be valuable for tabular tasks, but the way we apply that knowledge must respect data-efficiency, interpretability, and operational constraints (such as limited access to model internals). FeatLLM aims to bridge domain prior knowledge and lightweight predictors in a way that is suitable for real-world tabular learning problems—especially those in finance and healthcare where both data scarcity and interpretability matter.

# Few-shot tabular challenges: scarce labels, overfitting, spurious correlations
Few-shot tabular learning is hard for several interlocking reasons. First, with only a handful of labeled examples ($k$ shots), a flexible model can easily overfit the idiosyncrasies of those samples rather than learn generalizable relationships. This leads to brittle models that perform poorly on new data. Second, tabular datasets are heterogeneous: columns can be numeric, categorical, or special structured values, and there is no single set of augmentations or invariances that apply across datasets the way image flips or crops do for vision. The paper emphasizes that common few-shot strategies—data augmentation, contrastive objectives, or naive transfer—are often ineffective for tabular data because of this heterogeneity. Third, spurious correlations are a persistent risk: when only a few labels are available, coincidental associations between irrelevant features and the label can dominate training, leading to wrong conclusions and poor generalization.

Because of these challenges, successful few-shot solutions tend to rely on additional sources of inductive bias—either from other datasets, handcrafted rules, or strong priors. The FeatLLM approach leverages the rich, general prior knowledge contained in LLMs as a source of bias, but it does so in a way intended to minimize overfitting and avoid amplifying spurious correlations from the few available examples.

# Limitations of current LLM-for-tables: per-sample LLM inference, fine-tuning access barriers, prompt-length limits
Recent work has tried to use LLMs directly for tabular prediction by serializing table rows into text and asking the model to predict labels. While this can work in some few-shot settings, the paper points out three practical limitations that hamper real-world deployment.

First, most end-to-end LLM approaches require at least one LLM call per test sample at inference time. That makes inference computationally expensive and latency-sensitive systems impractical because each prediction must pass through the large model. Second, achieving high accuracy often requires fine-tuning or parameter-efficient tuning of the LLM (e.g., LoRA/IA3). For many state-of-the-art LLMs, however, only black-box API access is available; there is no way to fine-tune the model or obtain its checkpoints. That blocks practical application of fine-tuning–dependent methods on the very models that have the strongest priors. Third, the standard serialization approach (writing every feature value into the prompt) runs into context-window limits when tables have many columns. Long prompts either become infeasible or degrade performance, preventing straightforward scaling to datasets with hundreds of attributes.

Together, these limitations motivate a different use of LLMs: instead of placing the LLM in the prediction loop for every sample, can we harness LLMs once to produce informative artifacts (features, rules) and then use cheap downstream models for fast inference?

# Core idea: use LLMs to extract human-like rules as features instead of making end-to-end predictions
FeatLLM reframes the role of an LLM from a per-sample predictor to an automated feature engineer. The central idea is simple and powerful: ask the LLM to read the task description, feature names and definitions, and a small set of examples, and then to produce human-readable decision rules that characterize each class. Each rule is a condition on original features (for example, "age > 65 and ejection_fraction < 35") that can be evaluated deterministically on every row. Those rule evaluations become binary features: for a given sample, a rule feature is 1 if the sample satisfies the condition and 0 otherwise.

This approach has several intuitions behind it. Rules are compact, interpretable encodings of domain knowledge; they can capture prior knowledge that the LLM learned during pretraining (for example, risk factors that are known clinically). By transforming rules into binary features, the inference-time model becomes a simple, cheap classifier (in the paper, a linear model with non-negative weights) that is trained only on the few labeled points to learn which rules matter for the target task. Because the LLM is only used to generate rule templates once (or a bounded number of times for ensembling), there is no need to query the LLM at inference time, eliminating per-sample inference cost. Because the LLM is prompted to reason explicitly about relationships before deriving rules, FeatLLM reduces the chance that the model latches onto spurious correlations in the few-shot examples.

A critical design element is structured prompting: the LLM is guided through a two-step reasoning instruction. In Step 1 the model is asked to use prior knowledge (without being shown examples) to infer which features are likely to relate to the task; in Step 2 it combines that prior knowledge with the few-shot examples to produce concrete, testable rules for each class. This two-stage approach encourages the LLM to focus on semantically relevant features and to avoid overfitting purely to the few examples.

# Pipeline at a glance: rule extraction, code parsing, binary feature generation, simple downstream model, ensembling
The FeatLLM pipeline consists of a small number of clearly separated stages.

Prompting and rule extraction. Each query to the LLM contains three prompt components designed to make rule extraction reliable. The basic information description includes the task phrased as a question, natural-language feature names with types/definitions, and a small set of serialized examples. The paper uses the following serialization for an example $(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i)$ with feature names $F=\{f_j\}_{j=1}^d$:
\[
\text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i, F) = \text{`` } f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ \ldots\ f_d\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \text{Answer: }\mathbf{y}^i\text{''}.
\]
The reasoning instruction then guides the LLM through the two-step thought process described above (general feature-task relationships, then example-conditioned rule derivation). Finally, the response instruction constrains the output format so that the resulting rules are structured in a consistent way for downstream parsing (for example, a fixed number of rules per class).

Parsing rules into executable code. Because LLM rule outputs are natural language and can vary syntactically, FeatLLM uses the LLM again as a parser: the prompt asks the LLM to translate the rules into simple program code (Python). That generated code is executed (the paper runs it via Python's exec()) to compute binary rule-features for every sample. This leverages the LLM's strength at mapping flexible natural language into programmatic logic and avoids brittle handcrafted parsing.

Binary feature generation and simple training. The output of parsing is a set of rule-based binary features. For class $k$, each sample $i$ has a binary vector $\mathbf{z}_k^i\in\{0,1\}^R$ indicating which of the $R$ rules for that class are satisfied. FeatLLM then fits a simple linear model (one per class, combined through a softmax) to learn the importance of each rule. The paper uses a non-negativity projection on weights so that features cannot actively reduce the score for a class; the logit and probability computation is:
\begin{align}
\text{logit}_k^i &= \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i, \\
\mathbf{p}^i &= \text{Softmax}\big([\text{logit}_1^i,\ldots,\text{logit}_c^i]\big).
\end{align}
The model is trained by minimizing cross-entropy on the few labeled examples to learn the weights $\mathbf{w}_k$, which effectively selects and weights the LLM-generated features.

Bagging and ensembling for robustness and prompt-length control. To increase robustness and to avoid exceeding prompt-length limits, FeatLLM repeats the rule-generation process $T$ times with randomization (e.g., randomized example order and a nonzero sampling temperature to encourage diverse rules) and with bagging over features or instances in each trial. Each trial produces a small rule set and a trained weight vector $\mathbf{w}[t]$; final predictions average the per-trial probabilities. This ensemble reduces the impact of any single bad rule set and lets each trial keep its prompt short by focusing on a subset of features or examples.

Implementation choices used in experiments include extracting $R=10$ rules per class and using $T=20$ ensembles; the backbone LLM in the reported experiments is GPT-3.5. The linear weights are trained with Adam (learning rate 0.01) for up to 200 epochs with cross-validation for early stopping. Because the LLM is only queried a limited number of times (the paper reports only a few dozen API queries for training), the approach is practical under API-only access budgets.

# Key advantages: API-only access, no LLM at inference, prompt-size mitigation via bagging, interpretability benefits
FeatLLM combines several practical advantages that stem directly from its design.

API-only access and no fine-tuning need. FeatLLM requires only LLM inferences to generate rule sets; it does not need to fine-tune the LLM. That means it works with large closed-box models that only expose an inference API, addressing a major deployment constraint for state-of-the-art LLMs. Because the learned downstream predictor is a small model trained on rule features, all inference for new samples is fast and cheap; there is no per-sample API call.

Lower inference cost and operational feasibility. By moving the expensive LLM reasoning out of the per-sample loop, FeatLLM achieves inference-time costs comparable to conventional tabular learners (e.g., logistic regression), while still leveraging LLM priors. The paper's runtime comparisons show that FeatLLM's inference time is similar to conventional methods and far lower than methods that query the LLM for each test point.

Prompt-size mitigation and coverage via bagging. Because each ensemble trial only needs to consider a subset of columns and examples, the prompts remain short enough even for datasets with many attributes (the paper evaluates datasets with over 100 attributes). Bagging over features also increases coverage: different trials can focus on different parts of the feature space, and the ensemble aggregates those complementary perspectives.

Interpretability and human-readability. The generated features are explicit, human-readable rules that domain experts can inspect and reason about. This aids debugging and trust: a clinician or analyst can review the top-weighted rules and judge whether they make sense. Additionally, the downstream training step assigns weights to rules and can filter out rules that receive negligible weight, producing a compact, interpretable model.

Robustness to spurious features. Because the prompt explicitly asks the LLM to first reason about feature–label relationships using prior knowledge before producing rules, and because an ensemble averages across multiple rule sets, FeatLLM tends to generate fewer rules based on irrelevant or noisy columns. In the paper's controlled experiment where many spurious columns are appended, FeatLLM showed smaller performance degradation than alternative methods, indicating its rules-focused strategy reduces the impact of spurious correlations.

# Empirical summary: robust gains across 13 datasets; average ~10% AUC improvement vs TabLLM and STUNT in few-shot
The paper evaluates FeatLLM on a diverse collection of 13 datasets, including standard benchmarks (Adult, Bank, Credit-g, Heart, Diabetes, Myocardial, Communities), more recently added datasets (Cultivars, NHANES), and synthetic tasks (Sequence-type, Solution-mix) used to guard against memorization effects. These tasks cover binary and multiclass targets and include some datasets with over 100 features—challenging cases for prompt-based LLM methods.

Across these datasets in low-shot settings, FeatLLM consistently ranks first or second among the baselines considered. Baselines include conventional tabular learners (logistic regression, XGBoost, RandomForest), few-shot and pretraining-based methods (STUNT, SCARF, TabPFN), and LLM-based approaches (in-context prompting, TABLET, TabLLM). Notably, FeatLLM achieves substantial improvements in the few-shot regime: the paper reports an average improvement of roughly 10% in AUC when compared to strong LLM-based baselines such as TabLLM and STUNT. In some cases, FeatLLM with only 4 labeled examples is comparable to conventional tabular learners that were trained with 32 labeled examples, illustrating effective use of prior knowledge.

Additional analyses in the paper support why FeatLLM works in practice. An ablation study shows that the two-step reasoning instruction, inclusion of feature descriptions, weight tuning of the linear model, and ensembling each contribute positively to performance. Quality checks indicate that a majority of LLM-generated rules have positive signal (many have per-rule AUROC > 0.5 before tuning), and the downstream tuning step further de-prioritizes irrelevant rules. In runtime comparisons, FeatLLM requires only a modest number of API queries (the paper notes around thirty in its setup) and achieves inference-time efficiency comparable to standard models.

Taken together, the motivation, algorithmic design, and empirical results in the paper illustrate a practical way to leverage LLMs’ general knowledge for tabular few-shot learning: use the LLM as a feature engineer to produce compact, interpretable rules that a cheap downstream model can exploit. This reframing addresses key deployment constraints (API-only models, latency, context-window limits) while improving few-shot accuracy and maintaining interpretability—a blend that is highly attractive for real-world tabular applications such as finance and healthcare.

Background: Few-Shot Learning on Tabular Data

# Definition of $k$-shot learning and evaluation with minimal labeled data
Few-shot learning refers to setting up a supervised learning problem where only a very small number of labeled examples are available to train a model. In the notation used throughout the source paper, we consider a tabular dataset
\[
\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}^i, \mathbf{y}^i)\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]
where each $\mathbf{x}^i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ (or a mixed-type vector over $d$ attributes) and $\mathbf{y}^i$ is the corresponding label. In a $k$-shot experiment one randomly samples only $k$ labeled examples (with $k < N$) from $\mathcal{D}$ to form the training set used to learn the model. The goal is to obtain models that generalize from these very small labeled sets.

Because the training sets are so small, evaluation practices must reflect high variance due to sampling. The paper evaluates few-shot methods by repeating experiments with different random choices of the $k$ labeled samples and reports averaged performance (and standard deviation) across runs. It also uses cross-validation procedures to choose training hyperparameters and to reduce variance in model selection (see the section on cross-validation below). Across the paper’s experiments, comparisons are made at multiple shot levels (for example $k=4,8,16,32$), and a striking empirical finding is that a well-designed few-shot method can sometimes achieve performance with $4$ shots comparable to baselines that required $32$ shots.

# Heterogeneity in tabular features: numerical, categorical, mixed types
Tabular data commonly contains a heterogeneous mix of feature types: continuous numeric measurements, categorical attributes with a finite set of labels, ordinal fields, and other structured columns. The source paper emphasizes that feature descriptions that include value type and (optionally) a short definition are important when a model — particularly a language model used to reason about features — must interpret columns correctly. For categorical variables the paper gives the example of including explicit lists of possible categories in the feature description so downstream processing (such as rule extraction) can form well-typed conditions.

This heterogeneity matters in practical model design. The paper points out two concrete consequences. First, when using natural-language interfaces (for example, serializing rows for an LLM), the representation must preserve type information so the reasoning engine does not produce type-mismatched rules (e.g., comparing a textual category with a numerical threshold). Second, some datasets used in the experiments (for example, “Communities” and “Myocardial”) have over one hundred attributes; this large attribute count amplifies the challenges of working with heterogeneous columns because long serialized representations push against prompt/context-size limitations of language models. In short, mixed feature types and large numbers of columns create both semantic and practical constraints that few-shot learners must handle.

# Risks in low-data settings: overfitting and spurious correlations
With only a handful of labeled examples, two central risks dominate: overfitting and learning spurious correlations. The paper explicitly notes that learning from small numbers of labels increases the chance that a model will latch onto coincidental associations present in the small training set rather than real predictive structure. This is a primary motivation for bringing in additional sources of inductive bias (for example, prior knowledge encoded in pretrained models or synthetic priors) or additional unlabeled data.

The authors illustrate these risks experimentally. They run an experiment on the Heart dataset where they attach randomly selected columns from an unrelated dataset (Adult) to introduce noisy, irrelevant features. As the number of unrelated columns increases, many baseline methods show substantial performance degradation. The proposed FeatLLM approach degrades far less; the paper attributes this to the system’s explicit stage of inferring relationships between features and targets (leveraging prior knowledge) and extracting rule-based features only from columns judged relevant. Empirically, rules based on noisy columns accounted for only about 1–2% of the total rules generated. The paper also cautions that FeatLLM is not immune: if truly related but misaligned columns are attached, the system can still adopt spurious causal beliefs (an observation discussed in the appendix).

# Semi/unsupervised tabular methods: VIME, SubTab, TabNet; contrastive approaches like SCARF
A common strategy to alleviate label scarcity is to exploit unlabeled data through semi-supervised or self-supervised objectives. The paper groups several prior directions under this umbrella. One approach is to use large amounts of unlabeled data with carefully designed augmentations to enable semi-supervised learning; examples cited include methods such as SubTab and VIME. Another family uses self-supervised reconstruction objectives: the method listed as TabNet is given as an example of approaches that mask or corrupt parts of the data and train the model to reconstruct the masked portions. A different self-supervised strategy is to design augmentations and then apply contrastive learning objectives; SCARF is cited as representative of contrastive-style approaches for tabular data.

Two practical caveats are raised in the paper. First, leveraging unlabeled data assumes that such data is available and appropriate for the target task, which may not hold in many real-world settings — the paper explicitly notes that obtaining large quantities of unlabeled data may be infeasible. Second, despite the promise of these techniques, the authors remark that these unsupervised and semi-supervised strategies have not been especially effective in the strict few-shot regimes considered in the paper.

# Transfer and synthetic priors: TransTab for column semantics; TabPFN for learned Bayesian priors
Another way to inject prior knowledge into few-shot tabular learning is to learn transferable structure across many different tables or to pretrain on synthetic data whose distributional variety encodes useful inductive biases. The paper discusses two approaches in this direction.

TransTab learns models on a broad range of tabular datasets with the explicit goal of capturing semantic relationships between columns by using column names (not just raw values). This cross-table transfer of column semantics enables zero-shot or few-shot generalization to new tables by leveraging the learned mapping from column metadata to predictive structure.

TabPFN follows a different idea: instead of collecting many real tables, it mixes a variety of synthetic distributions to construct diverse pretraining tasks and uses these to train a Bayesian neural network. After pretraining, TabPFN performs inference on a target task by providing both the training and test samples to the network without further training; the synthetic priors encoded during pretraining help the model generalize in few-shot settings. The paper reports that such synthetic-prior approaches can surpass traditional tree-based methods and are beneficial in few-shot regimes.

The paper also mentions LLM-based transfer and tuning approaches (for example TabLLM), which apply parameter-efficient tuning schemes (like IA3) to adapt language models to tabular prediction tasks when possible. Practical constraints arise here as well: some high-performing LLMs do not provide training APIs or public checkpoints and therefore cannot be fine-tuned in this way.

# Why standard augmentations are hard for tabular data
A recurring observation in the paper is that standard augmentation strategies that work well in other modalities (images, audio, text) are difficult to apply to tabular data. The central reason given is heterogeneity: tabular datasets contain a mixture of feature types and semantics, and there is no single, universally appropriate transformation that preserves label semantics across all columns and datasets. Because augmentations must respect the semantics of each column (for example, categorical vs. continuous, lawful ranges, domain constraints), it is challenging to design augmentations that are broadly applicable. The paper therefore argues that augmentation-based semi-supervised methods have limited success in strict few-shot settings and that alternative ways of importing inductive bias (transfer learning, synthetic priors, or leveraging pretrained language models’ prior knowledge) are worth pursuing.

# Common metrics (AUC/AUROC) and cross-validation strategies in low-shot
In the experimental methodology of the paper, the main evaluation metric reported is the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC / AUROC). For each dataset and shot level the authors report averaged AUC values (and standard deviations) across multiple random repetitions to capture sampling variability inherent in few-shot settings.

Model selection and hyperparameter tuning in these low-data regimes are performed using cross-validation. The paper uses $k$-fold cross-validation both to select the number of training epochs (for the simple downstream models) and to perform grid search for conventional baselines. Practical choices for $k$ in these experiments are conservative (for example $k=2$ or $k=4$) to guarantee that every fold’s training set contains at least one example of each class when only a handful of labeled samples are available. These cross-validation practices help stabilize hyperparameter choices and reduce the risk of overfitting the small labeled training sets.

Closing remark
The paper situates few-shot tabular learning as a setting where model inductive bias and external priors matter as much as the supervised signal. Heterogeneous columns, scarcity of labeled examples, and the difficulty of designing universal augmentations make conventional few-shot recipes less effective for tabular data, motivating approaches that bring additional structure — whether via unlabeled data, transfer across tables, synthetic priors, or leveraging pretrained language models’ knowledge — while carefully validating using AUC and cross-validation procedures appropriate for low-shot regimes.

LLMs for Tabular Data: Methods and Limitations

# LLM capabilities and in-context learning for unseen tasks
Large Language Models (LLMs) possess a remarkable ability to generalize to new tasks without task-specific parameter updates. Trained on massive text corpora, these models encode a broad set of prior knowledge and reasoning patterns that can be leveraged in new settings. In the tabular learning context this prior knowledge can substitute for or augment domain expertise: an LLM, given a description of a medical prediction task, can draw on its background knowledge about clinical patterns and risk factors to help interpret sparse training examples. The paper presents this capability as the foundation for applying LLMs as a source of inductive bias in low-shot regimes.

A concrete way to exploit this behavior is in-context learning: the LLM receives a task description and a small collection of example input–label pairs inside the same prompt used for inference. With these few-shot demonstrations the model can “attend” to which features and example patterns appear salient and produce task-relevant outputs without any parameter tuning. In the tabular setting this means converting rows of a table into textual examples and adding them to the prompt; the LLM then reasons about the relationship between the serialized features and the target. The core insight is that this reasoning can extract structure and rules from very few labeled examples by leveraging the LLM’s pretrained world knowledge together with the in-prompt examples.

# Serializing tables and tasks into natural language prompts
Because LLMs are fundamentally text models, a necessary step for any language-interfaced tabular approach is serialization: converting rows and columns of a table into a natural-language representation that can be placed into a prompt. The standard serialization used in the paper takes an example $(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i)$ together with the feature name set $F=\{f_j\}_{j=1}^d$ and renders it as a short textual description of each feature followed by the label. Formally the paper writes this as
$\text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i, F) = \text{`` } f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ \dots\ f_d\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \text{Answer:}\ \mathbf{y}^i\text{''}$,
so a single row becomes a compact, human-readable sentence sequence.

A practical prompt for tabular tasks commonly includes three parts. First, a basic information block states the task as a question (for example, ``Does this patient's data indicate chronic heart failure? Yes or no?''), enumerates feature names and types (and category values for categorical features), and contains a few serialized demonstration examples with their labels. Second, reasoning instructions can be added: the paper finds it helpful to guide the LLM’s internal problem solving with a two-step instruction that first asks the model to infer general tendencies or causal relationships between features and the label (without looking at the demonstrations), and then to combine that prior knowledge with the few-shot examples to generate concrete decision criteria. Third, response instructions constrain output format so the LLM returns machine-parsable rule-like statements.

This serialization approach is widely used in recent work: data and feature descriptions are expressed in natural language, then fed into the LLM either for in-context prediction or for parameter-efficient tuning. The benefit is straightforward—LLMs can reason over textual feature descriptions and examples—but serialization also creates a practical challenge: as the number of features $d$ or the number of examples grows, the serialized prompt length increases, potentially exceeding the model’s context window or degrading performance.

# Parameter-efficient tuning (LoRA, IA3) vs prompt-only inference
When adapting LLMs to tabular tasks researchers have taken two broad approaches. One is prompt-only or in-context inference: the model is left unchanged and all task conditioning is done via the prompt (task description and few-shot examples). The other is to adapt the model’s parameters, typically with parameter-efficient tuning methods; the paper cites techniques such as LoRA and IA3 as representative examples of that strategy. Both strategies have been explored in recent tabular-learning research.

These two choices trade off accessibility and accuracy. Prompt-only in-context methods are attractive because they require only API-level inference access: you can form a prompt containing the serialized examples and ask the model to output predictions. However, for end-to-end prediction this naive usage requires an LLM call per test sample, which is computationally expensive at inference time and often impractical in production. In contrast, parameter-efficient tuning adapts a (small subset of) model parameters to a specific task and can yield higher accuracy, but it requires the ability to perform training on the model—that means access to training code, model checkpoints, or a tuning API. As the paper emphasizes, many state-of-the-art LLMs (the examples referenced include models available only through inference APIs) do not expose such tuning capabilities, rendering fine-tuning–based approaches infeasible in many real-world deployments.

The experiments in the paper illustrate this trade-off concretely: TabLLM, a baseline that uses parameter-efficient tuning (IA3 in the prior work), requires LLM checkpoints and therefore used a different publicly available backbone (T0) in the comparison, whereas the in-context baselines used the GPT-3.5 API. Thus, the choice between tuning and prompt-only inference is not just about learning dynamics but about what level of access is available to the practitioner.

# Representative baselines: In-context prompting, TABLET, TabLLM, LIFT/AnyPredict
The literature contains several representative language-interfaced approaches for tabular learning; the paper evaluates or discusses a subset that captures the main design patterns.

In-context prompting (termed simply “In-context” in the evaluations) places the few-shot serialized examples and the task description directly into the prompt and asks the LLM to produce labels at inference time without any parameter updates. This is the simplest, prompt-only paradigm.

TABLET augments in-context prompting with auxiliary hints derived from an external classifier: the prompt includes rule-sets and prototypes produced by that classifier to improve the LLM’s inference. In other words, TABLET uses a hybrid of external symbolic guidance and LLM reasoning inside the prompt to try to improve few-shot predictions.

TabLLM follows the parameter-adaptation route. It applies parameter-efficient tuning (the paper cites IA3) to adapt the LLM to tabular tasks and then uses the adapted model for inference. Because parameter-adaptive approaches require training access, TabLLM is constrained to LLMs for which checkpoints and training tools are available.

Earlier works such as LIFT and AnyPredict (cited in the paper) exemplify the overall pattern of serializing features and tasks into natural language then employing the LLM via in-context learning or tuning. Each of these baselines explores a point in the design space formed by (a) how tabular inputs are serialized, (b) whether and how auxiliary information or structured hints are provided in the prompt, and (c) whether the LLM is tuned or only queried.

# Observed challenges: inference cost per sample, restricted fine-tuning access for API-only models, long-context fragility
Applying LLMs directly for end-to-end tabular prediction uncovers several consistent, practical limitations.

First, inference cost per sample. Prompt-only approaches that ask the LLM to produce a label for each test row require one model query per test sample. This leads to high computational and latency costs at inference time. The paper contrasts this with the FeatLLM idea of using the LLM only as a feature engineer: in FeatLLM the LLM is queried a modest number of times to generate a set of rules (feature transforms), and a lightweight downstream model (e.g., a linear classifier) is used for all test-time predictions, avoiding per-sample LLM calls and substantially reducing inference latency.

Second, restricted fine-tuning access. High-performing parameter-adaptation methods need the ability to train or fine-tune the model. The paper highlights that many top-performing LLMs are available only through inference APIs and do not expose training interfaces or checkpoints. Thus, parameter-efficient tuning approaches (LoRA, IA3 and similar) may be inapplicable in practice for models that are API-only, limiting the set of LLMs that can be adapted with these techniques.

Third, long-context fragility. Serializing many features into a prompt increases its length; models have finite context windows and their performance can drop as prompt length grows. The paper observes datasets with large numbers of attributes (e.g., datasets with over 100 columns) pose concrete challenges for prompt-based methods. In experiments the authors report that some LLM-based methods (In-context, TABLET, TabLLM) failed to produce usable inferences on high-dimensional tables, whereas an approach that controls prompt size is needed for robust performance on many-feature datasets.

# When direct LLM prediction underperforms or becomes infeasible (many-feature tables)
There are clear, empirical conditions under which direct LLM prediction becomes suboptimal or infeasible. The paper identifies several of them and demonstrates both the failure modes and remedies.

Large numbers of features are a primary issue. When $d$ is large the serialized prompt either exceeds the model’s context window or becomes so long that the LLM can no longer effectively reason across all columns; in the authors’ experiments, several LLM-based baselines could not infer on datasets with many attributes. Closely related is the risk of spurious correlations in low-shot settings: with few labeled examples an unconstrained LLM can latch on to incidental patterns in irrelevant columns and produce rules that do not generalize. The paper’s spurious-correlation experiment—attaching unrelated columns from a different dataset to a target table—shows that many models degrade as noise features accumulate, whereas a method that explicitly reasons about task–feature relevance and filters rules can be more robust.

FeatLLM exemplifies one design that mitigates these problems. Instead of issuing per-sample LLM queries, it asks the LLM (via a structured prompt) to produce class-specific decision rules and then parses those rules into binary features. Concretely, for a dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i)\}_{i=1}^N$ with feature names $F$, the LLM produces rule sets which are converted to per-class binary vectors $\mathbf{z}_k^i\in\{0,1\}^R$ indicating, for class $k$, which of the $R$ extracted rules each sample satisfies. A simple learned scoring model maps those binary features to class logits via
$\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot\mathbf{z}_k^i$, and probabilities are obtained with a softmax,
$\mathbf{p}^i=\text{Softmax}([\text{logit}_1^i,\ldots,\text{logit}_c^i])$.
This design has three practical advantages emphasized in the paper: (1) inference for many test rows does not require any LLM calls (only evaluation of the learned linear-like model), (2) by constraining rule generation with task and feature descriptions plus a two-step reasoning instruction the LLM is encouraged to rely on prior knowledge and avoid spurious correlations, and (3) prompt-length issues are alleviated by applying bagging and ensembling: each ensemble trial asks the LLM to produce rules over a subset of features or examples, so the individual prompts stay short while the ensemble covers many features overall.

The experiments corroborate these claims: FeatLLM achieves strong few-shot performance across a range of datasets, is robust to added noisy columns (rules derived from noisy columns constituted only about 1–2% of the total), and has much lower test-time latency than methods that require per-sample LLM inference. The paper also cautions that no method is immune to all failure modes: FeatLLM can still pick up spurious or incorrect rules in some settings (for example, when closely related but misleading columns are present), which motivates the ensemble and tuning steps that filter out weak or irrelevant rules.

In summary, direct LLM prediction (prompt-only per-sample inference) can be simple to set up but becomes costly and fragile as the number of features grows or when only API-level access is available. Parameter-efficient tuning can improve accuracy but requires training access that many production LLMs do not expose. Hybrid strategies that use LLMs as structured feature engineers—extracting rules or transformations in a small number of queries and then training a lightweight model for test-time prediction—offer a pragmatic middle path that addresses the main limitations observed in practice.

Reframing LLMs as Feature Engineers

# What is feature engineering and why it matters in tabular ML
Feature engineering is the process of transforming raw data into new variables (features) that make the predictive relationships in a dataset easier for a model to exploit. In tabular machine learning, where columns can be heterogeneous (continuous, categorical, ordinal) and each column often has a domain-specific meaning (a feature name and definition), carefully designed features can dramatically improve predictive accuracy, simplify downstream models, and increase interpretability. The paper frames feature engineering as especially important in few-shot regimes: when only a handful of labeled examples are available, purely data-driven learners are prone to overfitting and to learning spurious correlations. In such settings, domain knowledge—about which attributes matter and how they interact—becomes disproportionately valuable.

Large Language Models (LLMs) carry a broad spectrum of prior knowledge learned from vast text corpora. That prior knowledge can act as a substitute for human expertise when crafting features. Instead of manually hand-engineering rules or transformations, the idea introduced in the paper is to prompt an LLM to act as an automated feature engineer: to read a task description and a few labeled examples, infer what conditions tend to indicate each class, and output interpretable criteria (rules). Those criteria are then converted into concrete, machine-usable features. This reframing recognizes feature engineering itself as the main role for the LLM, leaving prediction to a compact, conventional model learned from the (few) training examples.

# Rules as class-conditional criteria: translating domain priors into features
At the core of the approach is the observation that many human-understandable predictive signals are naturally expressed as rules or criteria: "If age is greater than 60 and smoking history is present, then risk is high." FeatLLM prompts an LLM to produce exactly these kinds of class-conditional criteria. The process is structured to encourage robust, domain-informed rules rather than naively memorized associations.

Formally, the setting begins with a labeled tabular dataset
$\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}^i, \mathbf{y}^i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$,
where each sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ has $d$ input attributes and the dataset comes with natural-language feature names $F=\{f_j\}_{j=1}^d$ and their definitions. For few-shot experiments only $k$ labeled examples (with $k \ll N$) are used to prompt the LLM and to train the downstream model.

Prompt design is split into three complementary components. First, the basic information section supplies the task as a short, question-like description, the feature definitions (including types and possible categories for categorical attributes), and a set of few-shot example demonstrations. Each example is serialized into text so the LLM can read it; the paper uses the following template for serialization:
$\text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i, F) =$  
\quad\ \ $\text{`` }f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ \ldots\ f_d\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \ \text{Answer:}\ \mathbf{y}^i\text{''}$.
This places concrete examples alongside human-readable feature descriptions so the LLM can ground its prior knowledge in observed data.

Second, the reasoning instruction steers the LLM to follow a two-step inference process. Step 1 asks the model to use general knowledge (independently of the sample examples) to identify which features are likely relevant to the task and how they tend to relate to outcomes. Step 2 then asks the LLM to synthesize class-conditional rules by combining that prior knowledge with the few-shot demonstrations. Splitting reasoning this way prevents the LLM from latching onto spurious correlations that appear only in the tiny example set and encourages reliance on plausible causal or semantic associations. To control complexity, the system fixes the number of rules per class (the paper uses 10) so the prompt neither under-represents nor overwhelms the downstream pipeline.

Third, the response instruction constrains output format. The LLM is told exactly how each rule should be written and how rules should be grouped by class. Constraining format reduces syntactic variability and makes downstream parsing more reliable.

Once the LLM returns natural-language rules, they are converted into binary features. For class $k$ the LLM produces $R$ rules; each sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ is evaluated against those rules to produce a binary vector $\mathbf{z}_k^i\in\{0,1\}^R$ indicating which class-$k$ rules are satisfied. Parsing natural-language rules reliably is nontrivial: LLM output can vary syntactically (e.g., "less than" vs. "$<$"). Rather than hand-engineering a complex parser, FeatLLM uses the LLM again—prompting it to emit a small executable function (Python code) that evaluates the rules—and executes that returned code (via Python's \texttt{exec()}) to create the binary features. This leverages the LLM's strengths in both semantic understanding and code generation to turn textual rules into deterministic, reproducible feature computations.

# Advantages of LLM-generated rules: interpretability, portability, and reduced inference cost
LLM-generated rules offer a trio of practical advantages emphasized in the paper.

Interpretability: The features are explicit, human-readable conditions. A practitioner can inspect each rule and understand which combinations of input values drive predictions; they are not opaque latent representations. The paper empirically shows that a large fraction of generated rules have meaningful predictive signal (many rules produce AUROC > 0.5 with respect to the target), and that the downstream training step naturally down-weights or discards unhelpful rules. In practice the model learns per-rule weights and a simple threshold (the paper uses a threshold of 0.1 on learned weights) can identify rules that should be ignored, providing a principled way to prune noisy or spurious criteria.

Portability and ease of deployment: FeatLLM only requires API-level access to the LLM. It does not require model weights, fine-tuning APIs, or training the LLM itself. This is important because many high-performing LLMs are available only through inference APIs (the paper mentions the relevance of this limitation). By confining LLM use to a one-time or small number of rule-generation queries, the method avoids the practical burdens of parameter-efficient tuning or model-parallel training. Additionally, because the final inference phase uses only conventional code (binary feature checks) and a small learned model, the deployment stack is lightweight and platform-agnostic.

Reduced inference cost and latency: A common drawback of end-to-end LLM-based tabular methods is that they require an LLM invocation per test sample, which is expensive in both latency and monetary cost. FeatLLM instead uses the LLM up-front to produce a transformed dataset (the rule-based binary features). At inference time, the system evaluates the binary conditions and runs a simple predictive model. As reported, inference latency for FeatLLM is comparable to conventional tabular methods (logistic regression, XGBoost, RandomForest), whereas per-sample LLM approaches incur substantially higher inference time. Moreover, FeatLLM typically requires only a modest number of API queries (the paper reports on the order of thirty queries in their experiments), which can be parallelized and is far less than querying an LLM per test instance.

Robustness via ensemble and bagging: The paper further improves reliability by repeating rule generation multiple times and combining models. Randomness is injected via higher sampling temperature in the LLM and by randomizing the order of few-shot demonstrations (order matters in in-context learning). Each trial creates a different rule set; bagging is used to select subsets of features or instances per trial, which both diversifies the rule ensembles and mitigates prompt-length limits (because each prompt can be kept short by selecting subsets). Averaging predictions across trials increases resistance to noisy rules: if some trials generate incorrect criteria, their effect is drowned out by more accurate rule sets from other trials.

# Binary features from rules vs end-to-end LLM predictions
End-to-end LLM tabular approaches typically serialize a sample (or a set of training examples plus a test row) into text and ask the LLM to produce a label or probability for that specific sample. These approaches either (a) require an LLM inference for every test example (in-context prediction), or (b) fine-tune the LLM so it can be used as a predictor without per-sample few-shot context. Both routes have practical disadvantages: per-sample inference is slow and costly, while fine-tuning demands access and compute that may not be available for many LLM services.

FeatLLM takes a different path: it treats the LLM as a generator of features instead of as the ultimate predictor. Converting rules into binary features produces a conventional tabular dataset on which a small predictive model (in the paper, a linear model with non-negative weights) is trained. Concretely, for class $k$ the model learns weight vector $\mathbf{w}_k$ and computes logits and probabilities as
$\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i$
and
$\mathbf{p}^i = \text{Softmax}(\,[\text{logit}_1^i,\ldots,\text{logit}_c^i]\,)$.
Learning uses cross-entropy loss on the few-shot labeled examples. The non-negativity constraint (implemented by the $\max(\cdot,0)$ projection) ensures that satisfying a rule cannot decrease the predicted likelihood of its associated class during training, aligning the learned weights with the intuitive semantics of rule-based evidence.

This separation—LLM for feature discovery, simple model for inference—brings several concrete benefits shown in the experiments. FeatLLM achieves strong few-shot performance, often exceeding end-to-end LLM baselines and classical models when labeled data are scarce. For example, the paper reports that with only 4 labeled examples FeatLLM reaches performance comparable to conventional models trained with substantially more data (e.g., 32 shots). Moreover, because prediction no longer depends on the LLM, inference cost is low, and the approach scales to datasets with many columns by using bagging to keep prompts compact during rule generation. The paper also reports that attempting to adapt the ensemble/bagging idea to end-to-end LLM prediction would be cost-prohibitive, since it would multiply the already-large number of per-sample LLM inferences.

# Comparison to decision trees and association rule mining
Rules are a familiar output in machine learning: decision trees produce decision-path rules, and association rule mining extracts frequently co-occurring itemsets and their conditional relationships. FeatLLM produces rule-like artifacts as well, but the origin, role, and downstream use of these rules differ in important ways highlighted by the paper.

First, origin and inductive bias. Decision trees and association-rule algorithms typically derive rules purely from the available data. In low-shot regimes this can cause them to pick up spurious patterns that happen to appear in a tiny sample. FeatLLM, by contrast, leverages LLM prior knowledge as a source of inductive bias: its two-step reasoning prompt first asks the model to apply general knowledge about which features matter before grounding that knowledge in the few examples. This encourages rules that reflect plausible domain relationships rather than accidental associations in the labeled mini-sample. The paper's spurious-correlation experiments support this: when many irrelevant columns are appended, FeatLLM degrades less than baselines, and only a small percentage (about 1–2%) of generated rules came from noisy columns.

Second, structure and downstream usage. Decision trees encode hierarchical splits where the path conditions combine via AND; association rules report patterns and measures like support and confidence. FeatLLM produces a fixed number of per-class rules that are treated as independent binary features. Rather than making a single tree-based decision, the downstream model learns non-negative weights over those binary indicators and combines them (via a dot product and softmax) into class probabilities. This decouples rule discovery from rule weighting: rules are interpretable units that can be flexibly combined, weighted, or pruned after observing the labeled examples.

Third, practicality in few-shot settings and deployment constraints. Association-rule mining and tree learning can require enough data to estimate reliable frequencies or splits; FeatLLM injects LLM priors to compensate for sparse labels. Moreover, FeatLLM is explicitly designed to work with LLMs available only via inference APIs: it does not require the LLM to be fine-tuned or to provide gradient-level access. This portability makes it a practical alternative in real-world scenarios where access to LLM internals is restricted.

Finally, FeatLLM borrows an ensemble philosophy reminiscent of random forests: multiple independently generated rule sets are combined (bagging over features or instances and averaging predictions) to increase robustness. Thus, FeatLLM situates itself conceptually between purely data-driven rule learners and human-crafted rule systems: it produces interpretable rules like those methods, but creates them by blending broad prior knowledge and small-sample evidence and then uses a statistical learning step to weight and refine them.

# Takeaways
Reframing an LLM as a feature engineer changes the role of large language models in tabular prediction: instead of being the black-box predictor queried for each sample, an LLM becomes a tool for extracting interpretable, class-conditional criteria that convert semantic prior knowledge into explicit binary features. FeatLLM operationalizes this idea with careful prompt design (basic information, two-step reasoning, and response format), a pragmatic parsing strategy that uses the LLM to emit executable code, a constrained downstream learner that enforces non-negative rule weights, and an ensemble/bagging strategy to improve robustness and manage prompt size. Empirically, this pipeline delivers strong few-shot performance, produces features that are often informative on their own, and reduces inference cost compared to per-sample LLM prediction—making LLM-driven, human-interpretable feature generation a practical approach for tabular problems with scarce labeled data.

FeatLLM Pipeline: End-to-End Overview

# Problem formulation
We consider a supervised tabular classification problem. Let the labeled dataset be
\[
\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}^i, \mathbf{y}^i)\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]
where each input $\mathbf{x}^i$ is a $d$-dimensional vector of table attributes and $\mathbf{y}^i$ is the corresponding class label drawn from a finite set of classes (say $c$ classes). The dataset is accompanied by natural-language feature names and, optionally, short definitions; we denote the feature name set by $F = \{f_j\}_{j=1}^d$. Feature types can be heterogeneous (numerical, categorical, etc.), and the feature descriptions in $F$ typically mention these types and possible categories when appropriate.

In the few-shot setting studied by FeatLLM, only a small number $k$ of labeled examples (with $k \ll N$) are available to build the model. Concretely, for $k$-shot experiments we randomly select $k$ labeled samples from $\mathcal{D}$ as the training set and use the rest for evaluation. The goal is to leverage the LLM’s prior knowledge and the small set of labeled examples to produce features and a lightweight downstream learner that together deliver accurate predictions in this low-data regime.

# Overall stages: prompting for rules, parsing rules, new feature construction, simple modeling, ensembling
FeatLLM decomposes the end-to-end workflow into a small number of conceptually distinct stages. The pipeline is designed so that the LLM is used to discover human-interpretable decision criteria (rules) only during feature-discovery time; a lightweight model then uses these discovered features at inference without further LLM calls. The stages are:

1. Prompting the LLM to produce rules. The LLM is given a structured prompt that contains: (a) basic information about the task and the features, (b) a reasoning instruction that guides how the LLM should combine its prior knowledge with the few-shot examples, and (c) response-format instructions that constrain the syntactic form of the rules the LLM should output. The output of this stage is a set of natural-language rules for each class (e.g., conditions such as “age > 60 and blood_pressure > 140 implies class = X”).

2. Parsing rules into executable checks. The LLM’s rule text may vary in wording and syntax. FeatLLM uses the LLM again as a code writer: it prompts the LLM to translate the natural-language rules into a small program (Python functions) that, given a sample $\mathbf{x}$, computes whether each rule is satisfied. The generated code is executed (e.g., via Python’s \texttt{exec()}) to obtain deterministic rule-evaluation routines.

3. New feature construction. Each rule becomes a binary feature. If there are $R$ rules per class and $c$ classes, then for a given sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ the pipeline produces binary vectors $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$ for $k=1,\dots,c$, where the $r$-th entry of $\mathbf{z}_k^i$ is 1 if rule $r$ for class $k$ is satisfied on $\mathbf{x}^i$, and 0 otherwise. These binary features summarize the LLM’s class-wise criteria for membership.

4. Simple modeling (learning importance of rules). A lightweight, conventional ML model (the FeatLLM paper uses a linear model without bias as an example) is trained on the new binary features to estimate class likelihoods. Each class $k$ has an associated weight vector $\mathbf{w}_k \in \mathbb{R}^R$ indicating how important each rule is. The weights are learned using the available $k$-shot labeled examples by optimizing cross-entropy loss.

5. Bagging and ensembling. The entire process (steps 1–4) is repeated $T$ times with randomized prompts and subsampling (bagging) to obtain an ensemble of models. The predicted class probabilities from the $T$ models are averaged to form the final prediction. Bagging reduces prompt length per trial (by selecting subsets of features or instances) and increases robustness by combining diverse rule sets.

These stages create an efficient separation between rule discovery (LLM-assisted, training-time) and run-time prediction (purely programmatic feature evaluation plus a lightweight statistical model).

# Where LLMs are used (training-time feature discovery) vs not used (inference)
FeatLLM deliberately confines the use of LLMs to the feature-discovery portion of the pipeline. Specifically, LLMs are invoked to (1) reason about the task and propose human-readable rules for each class, and (2) translate the natural-language rules into executable parsing code. Both uses are performed during the training or preparation stage and typically involve only a modest number of LLM API calls (the paper reports that the overall training process can be carried out with a small number of queries that can be parallelized).

At inference time, for each new sample FeatLLM does not call the LLM. Instead, the sample is fed into the locally-executable rule functions (the code generated in parsing) to compute the binary rule features, and the learned lightweight model (the non-negative linear weights and softmax) is used to obtain class probabilities. This design eliminates the need for one LLM call per test sample, dramatically reducing inference latency and API cost compared to end-to-end LLM approaches that must serialize each test row into the prompt and query the LLM for every prediction. It also removes the requirement to fine-tune the LLM — FeatLLM operates with API-only access and relies on in-context prompting for discovery rather than parameter tuning.

# Few-shot serialization format: "f_j is x_j. ... Answer: y"
FeatLLM uses a simple, consistent serialization format for example demonstrations included in the prompt. Each example $(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i)$ is converted into a compact textual description of the form used in previous LLM-based tabular studies. In the paper this is written as:
\[
\text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i, F) =
\quad ``\ f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ \ldots\ f_d\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \ \text{Answer:}\ \mathbf{y}^i\ ".
\]
In words, this means that each feature name $f_j$ is written followed by the observed value $\mathbf{x}^i_j$, separated by periods, and the example ends with a labeled answer. The prompt’s “basic information” portion includes these serialized examples (the few-shot demonstrations), the task description phrased as a question, and the feature descriptions (including type information and category examples for categorical variables). This consistent format helps the LLM interpret the example rows and relate feature names to values when composing rules.

# Non-negative linear scoring per class and softmax probabilities
After rule-derived binary features are produced for a sample, FeatLLM scores class likelihoods with a simple, interpretable linear scoring function per class. Denote by $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$ the binary vector of rule-satisfaction indicators for class $k$ on sample $i$, and by $\mathbf{w}_k \in \mathbb{R}^R$ the corresponding trainable weight vector. The model computes a class logit per class $k$ as
\[
\text{logit}_k^i \;=\; \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,
\]
where $\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)$ denotes the element-wise ReLU projection that enforces non-negativity of effective weights. The vector of logits across classes is converted to class probabilities via the softmax:
\[
\mathbf{p}^i \;=\; \text{Softmax}\big([\text{logit}_1^i, \ldots, \text{logit}_c^i]\big).
\]

Two design choices are important to understand. First, each class has its own rule set and weight vector, so a rule that is targeted at recognizing class $k$ contributes only to $\text{logit}_k$. Second, the weights are projected to the non-negative orthant before dotting with binary features. This projection ensures that satisfying a rule cannot reduce the logit for its associated class, which matches the interpretation of rules as affirmative evidence for class membership. The weights are learned from the few-shot labeled examples by minimizing the cross-entropy loss between $\mathbf{p}^i$ and the ground-truth labels. In practice the training is done with a small optimizer such as Adam (the paper uses learning rate $0.01$ and trains for a fixed number of epochs with cross-validation for early-selection), which is inexpensive because the model dimensionality is modest ($R$ rules per class).

Training the simple linear model performs two crucial functions: it calibrates the relative importance of rules, and it filters out unhelpful or spurious rules by assigning them small weights. In experiments this tuning step improves performance compared to naive counting of satisfied rules.

# Bagging and ensembling to mitigate prompt length and improve robustness
FeatLLM uses repetition with randomization and subsampling to both reduce per-prompt length (thus handling datasets that would otherwise exceed the LLM context window) and to improve robustness to imperfect rule generation.

The method runs $T$ independent trials. In each trial the prompt to the LLM is varied by (a) randomizing the order of the few-shot demonstrations (the order can affect LLM in-context behavior), (b) sampling a subset of features or instances (bagging) to keep prompt size small when the original table is wide, and (c) using stochastic decoding (higher temperature) so that the LLM produces diverse rule sets across trials. Each trial yields a set of rules, parsing code, the binary-feature construction, and a trained weight vector $\{\mathbf{w}_k^{[t]}\}_{k=1}^c$ for that trial $t$.

At inference the $T$ models produce $T$ probability vectors $\{\mathbf{p}^i_{[t]}\}_{t=1}^T$ for a sample $\mathbf{x}^i$, and FeatLLM aggregates them by averaging:
\[
\bar{\mathbf{p}}^i \;=\; \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \mathbf{p}^i_{[t]}.
\]
Averaging probabilities combines diverse but complementary rule-sets, yielding two main benefits emphasized in the paper. First, if some trials produce incorrect or noisy rules, correct rules from other trials can compensate, producing a more reliable ensemble prediction (this leverages LLM self-consistency). Second, bagging keeps each single prompt within context-length constraints even for very wide tables: rather than trying to ask the LLM to summarize all $d$ columns at once, FeatLLM asks it to reason about a subset in each trial and then aggregates.

In the empirical setup of the paper, commonly used hyperparameters are $T=20$ trials and $R=10$ rules per class, and the LLM decoding temperature and prompt construction are tuned to encourage useful diversity without excessive noise. The authors report that as $T$ grows performance improves but plateaus (so moderate ensemble sizes suffice in practice), and that $R=10$ achieves a good balance between expressivity and overfitting in low-shot regimes.

--- 

This end-to-end description shows how FeatLLM converts a few labeled rows and feature descriptions into programmatically-evaluable rule features, learns a small non-negative linear scoring model on top, and combines multiple such learners via bagging and ensembling. The result is a system that leverages LLM prior knowledge for feature engineering while keeping inference cheap and deterministic (no per-sample LLM calls), and that uses standard supervised learning to calibrate and filter the automatically-generated features.

Designing Prompts to Extract High-Quality Rules

# Basic information block: task question, label schema, feature descriptions with data types and category enumerations
The basic information block is the foundation of any prompt that asks an LLM to produce task-specific rules. Its role is to supply the LLM with the minimal but essential context it needs to interpret the table, to map column names into semantics the model can reason about, and to ground the prediction objective. In FeatLLM this block contains (1) a short task description formulated as a question (for example, in a medical setting: “Does this patient's myocardial infarction complications data indicate chronic heart failure? Yes or no?”), (2) a description of the label schema so the LLM knows what answers it should produce, and (3) a description of the dataset columns $F=\{f_j\}_{j=1}^d$ including their value types and, when applicable, enumerations of possible categories. Supplying the type and categorical options explicitly signals which operations make sense on a feature (e.g., comparison for numeric features, membership checks for categorical features) and therefore reduces meaningless or type-mismatched rules.

When a small set of labeled examples is included as demonstrations (see the next section), each demonstration is serialized into text using a fixed, human-readable format. FeatLLM uses a serialization function that converts a sample $(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i)$ into a compact sentence-per-feature description with the answer appended. In the paper this is written as:
\begin{align}
&\text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i, F) = \nonumber \\ 
&\quad ``\ f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ ... \ f_d\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \ \text{Answer:}\ \mathbf{y}^i\ "
\end{align}
This explicit, regularized presentation of features and labels makes the demonstrations consistent and easier for the LLM to consume. The basic information block therefore acts both as a semantic map (names → meanings and types) and as the environment definition in which the LLM should reason.

# In-context demonstrations: few-shot samples serialized into text
In-context learning depends on presenting a few labeled examples that illustrate the mapping from inputs to labels. In FeatLLM those demonstrations are the serialized samples described above. Because FeatLLM targets few-shot settings, only a small number $k$ (for example $k=4$ in many experiments) of labeled samples are included in the prompt. The serialized demonstrations show the model concrete instances of the task and how labels correspond to feature configurations; Step 2 of the reasoning instruction (below) explicitly asks the LLM to use those examples when deriving rules.

Two practical implications follow. First, demonstrations must be compact and consistent, since the prompt has limited size and each extra example reduces space available for the other components (feature descriptions and reasoning instructions). Second, the order of demonstrations can affect the LLM’s output, so FeatLLM later exploits this sensitivity by randomizing demonstration order across ensemble trials to increase diversity of rule sets. In short, serialized in-context examples show the LLM “what success looks like” within the context established by the basic information block, and they are the data anchor for deriving concrete rules tied to the observed labels.

# Two-step reasoning instruction: Step 1 (general prior knowledge) then Step 2 (rule deduction using examples)
A core engineering decision in FeatLLM is to guide the LLM’s inference with a two-step reasoning instruction. The prompt opens with a short chain-of-thought–style preamble and then separates the task into (1) a prior-knowledge step and (2) an example-driven rule extraction step. In Step 1 the LLM is explicitly asked to infer general tendencies or causal relationships between features and the task using its world knowledge and common sense — crucially, this step is performed without referencing the few-shot demonstrations. The goal is to let the model surface domain priors (for example, “higher age tends to increase risk for some diseases”) so that subsequent rule construction is biased toward plausible, semantically meaningful patterns.

Step 2 instructs the LLM to use the example demonstrations together with the insights generated in Step 1 to deduce concrete, class-specific rules. By separating these two phases, the prompt encourages the model to combine its global priors with local evidence from the few examples rather than to rely solely on spurious correlations that might appear in a tiny sample. Practically, this reduces the chance that the LLM will produce rules built from features that are irrelevant in expectation and helps it focus on semantically supported columns. The paper reports that this structured reasoning leads to better rule quality, especially in very low-shot regimes where the few examples alone are not sufficient to reveal reliable tendencies.

# Fixing the number of rules per class (e.g., $R=10$) to balance coverage and prompt size
When asking an LLM to enumerate rules, it is important to control how many rules are produced. FeatLLM adopts a fixed number of rules per class to limit both the dimensionality of the generated feature space and the length of the downstream processing pipeline. Concretely, the implementation used in the paper sets the number of rules per class to $R=10$ (this choice is discussed and validated in the analysis section). Requesting a fixed count achieves two practical effects: it prevents the LLM from producing too few rules (which might under-cover the decision boundary) and it prevents it from producing an unwieldy number of rules that would increase feature dimensionality and inflate prompt/parse costs.

Fixing $R$ also simplifies downstream training because every class contributes the same number of candidate binary features (rules), yielding a regular, predictable input for the simple linear model that FeatLLM fits to learn rule weights. The paper’s experiments show that $R=10$ is a reasonable trade-off in low-shot settings: it provides adequate coverage of plausible conditions without overly increasing the risk of overfitting in the small-sample regime or overflowing prompt capacity.

# Type-aware constraints and avoidance of mismatched comparisons
An LLM without explicit type cues can produce syntactically valid but semantically invalid rules (for example, applying “greater than” to a categorical field). To prevent such mismatches FeatLLM explicitly includes type information for every feature in the basic information block and instructs the LLM to consult these types when forming rules. For categorical features the prompt can list the allowed categories; for numeric features the prompt indicates they are continuous or integer-valued. This type-aware presentation nudges the model to pick operators that make sense: comparisons and ranges for numeric features, equality or membership checks for categorical features, and presence/absence tests for binary flags.

Enforcing type-awareness in the prompt reduces downstream parsing complexity and improves the semantic validity of the rules the LLM produces, which in turn yields binary features that actually reflect meaningful conditions on the original data. The paper highlights that this constraint is important for avoiding nonsensical conditions (e.g., “gender > 0.5”) and for increasing the chance that automatically-generated code can be used to convert the natural-language rule into a deterministic Boolean test against table rows.

# Response-format instructions: rule structure, logical operators (AND/OR), numeric/comparative expressions
To make the LLM’s output reliably machine-parsable and to constrain the space of syntactic variations the model might use, FeatLLM includes explicit response-format instructions in the prompt. These instructions define the required format for the response for each answer class and the grammatical structure each rule should follow. Typical guidance asks the LLM to produce a numbered list of rules for each class, where each rule is a simple conditional composed of elementary predicates (for example, “feature > threshold”, “feature in {A, B}”, or “feature == value”) possibly combined by the logical operators AND/OR. By prescribing this surface structure the prompt reduces ambiguity and makes it easier to translate the rules into code that computes the corresponding binary feature.

Despite these constraints, the paper notes that LLM outputs can still vary in surface realization (for instance using “greater than” instead of the symbol “>”), so FeatLLM does not rely solely on rigid string parsing. Instead it sometimes uses an additional LLM-assisted parsing step: the inferred rules and a brief specification (function name, inputs and outputs) are sent back to the LLM and it is asked to return simple executable code that implements each rule. This leverages the LLM’s robustness to linguistic variability while still producing deterministic rule-checks: the generated code is then executed (for example via Python’s exec()) to produce the binary features. The combination of prescribed response format and a fallback LLM-to-code parsing step provides both structure and resilience to syntactic noise.

# Managing prompt length via sampling/bagging of features and instances
A practical limitation in any LLM-centered pipeline is prompt size: many tabular datasets have dozens or hundreds of attributes and a long list of serialized examples can exhaust the model’s context window. FeatLLM addresses this by combining rule-generation with a bagging-style sampling strategy and ensembling. Each trial samples a subset of features and/or a subset of training instances to include in the prompt; the LLM then produces rules conditioned on that smaller view. By repeating this process $T$ times and aggregating the resulting rule-based features (the paper uses $T=20$ ensembles in experiments), FeatLLM both reduces per-prompt length and creates a diverse pool of candidate rules that together cover more of the original feature space.

This bagging approach provides two benefits discussed in the paper. First, sampling features keeps each prompt short enough to fit the LLM’s context window, enabling FeatLLM to operate on wide tables without serializing all columns at once. Second, the ensemble of rule sets increases robustness: incorrect or spurious rules from some trials can be outweighed by correct rules from other trials. The paper also uses a higher sampling temperature and randomizes the order of the demonstrations across trials to encourage diversity in the generated rules. Ultimately, the sampled prompts plus ensembling trade off per-trial completeness for scalable, robust coverage across many features and instances.

# Concluding guidance and recommended settings
Putting these elements together yields a practical recipe for extracting high-quality, machine-actionable rules with an LLM in few-shot tabular settings. The prompt should start with a compact basic information block that frames the task as a clear question and enumerates feature names, types and possible categories. A small number of serialized in-context demonstrations should be included to show examples of inputs and labels. The reasoning instruction should be explicitly divided into Step 1 (eliciting general prior knowledge without examples) and Step 2 (using the demonstrations to derive per-class rules), and the prompt should require a fixed number of rules per class (the paper uses $R=10$). Response-format constraints should prescribe simple conditional templates and allow AND/OR combinations; because surface forms vary, include a fall-back LLM-based parsing-to-code stage to obtain executable tests. Finally, if the table is wide or the prompt risks becoming too long, apply sampling/bagging of features and instances and repeat the process across $T$ trials to build an ensemble of rules. These practices, as validated in the paper, balance semantic correctness, parseability, and scalability when using LLMs as automatic feature engineers for few-shot tabular learning.

From Natural-Language Rules to Executable Features

# Parsing challenges: noisy LLM outputs and syntactic variability (e.g., ">" vs "greater than")
One of the central difficulties when converting natural-language rules into executable features is that the rules produced by a Large Language Model (LLM) are not guaranteed to follow a single rigid grammar. Even when prompts constrain format, LLM outputs exhibit syntactic variability: logical connectives may be written in different ways, numeric comparisons can be expressed either with symbols (e.g., ">") or in words (e.g., "greater than"), and categorical labels may be paraphrased or capitalized differently. The FeatLLM paper explicitly calls out this variety, noting that an LLM "might alter expressions, like substituting '>' or '<' with phrases like 'greater than' or 'less than'." This variability makes naive, hand-written parsers brittle: a rule parser that expects the exact token ">" will fail on the semantically identical phrase "greater than 10".

Heterogeneous tabular feature types amplify the parsing problem. The same natural-language phrase can refer to a numeric threshold, a categorical membership test, or a textual pattern depending on the feature's declared type. FeatLLM guards against some type mismatches by supplying feature type information (and categorical examples) to the LLM during rule generation, but even then the generated rule text can deviate from the intended syntactic pattern. In short, the pipeline must tolerate syntactic noise while preserving semantic correctness: the downstream component that produces binary "rule satisfied" indicators must understand the intended meaning of varied textual expressions.

Because of this fragility, FeatLLM avoids attempting to enumerate or hard-code every possible syntactic variant. Instead, it treats the LLM not only as the generator of rules but also as a robust translator from noisy natural-language rules into unambiguous, executable code that evaluates those rules on data. This design choice reduces the need for brittle pattern-matching parsers and leverages the LLM's semantic understanding to handle synonymy and rephrasing.

# Using an LLM to synthesize parser code from rules with a clear function signature and I/O spec
FeatLLM's parsing strategy is to let the LLM itself produce a small piece of code that implements the semantics of the inferred rules. The key prompt engineering element is to give the LLM a clear function signature and input/output specification, together with the natural-language rules to be parsed. Concretely, the pipeline places the function name, an explanation of the function inputs and outputs, and the list of inferred rules into a prompt, and then asks the LLM to return code implementing that function. The paper explains this succinctly: rather than building complex program code by hand, they "leverage the LLM itself" because it "is good at understanding the semantic meaning despite syntactic changes in text, and it has the ability to generate a simple code with instructions."

The I/O spec in the prompt serves two purposes. First, it constrains the LLM's output format, making parsing of the generated code straightforward: the returned artifact is a callable function that deterministically maps a row of the tabular input into binary checks. Second, providing type and category information in the prompt lets the LLM choose appropriate code idioms (e.g., membership checks for categorical features, numeric comparisons for continuous features). In FeatLLM this was part of the broader "response instruction" and "basic information description" framing used when eliciting rules: feature types and example categories are explicitly provided, and the LLM is instructed on the required format for rules. Appendix examples in the paper illustrate that supplying a function signature and I/O description yields compact, executable translations of each natural-language rule.

Because the LLM has been trained on code corpora, it is comfortable producing short, idiomatic code that normalizes synonyms (e.g., turning "greater than 10" into a numeric comparison) and implements logical combinations (AND/OR) as boolean expressions. In effect, the LLM converts noisy, free-form rule text into a normalized, machine-executable representation that removes ambiguity about operators, value formats, and membership tests.

# Executing generated code (e.g., Python exec) to compute rule satisfaction at scale
Once the LLM returns code that implements the rules' semantics, FeatLLM executes that code to transform raw tabular rows into binary rule-features. The paper states that "the generated code is executed using Python’s exec() function to perform data conversion." The execution produces, for each sample and for each generated rule, a binary indicator that records whether the sample satisfies the rule.

These generated binary features are organized per class. If there are $R$ rules per class and $c$ classes, then for sample $i$ FeatLLM denotes the rule-feature vector for class $k$ as $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$. The downstream predictor is deliberately simple: a linear model without bias maps these binary features into per-class logits and probabilities. The paper uses the following formulation:

\[
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,
\qquad
\mathbf{p}^i = \text{Softmax}\bigl([\text{logit}_1^i, \ldots, \text{logit}_c^i]\bigr),
\]

where $\mathbf{w}_k$ are trainable weights that capture the importance of each rule for class $k$, and the $\max(\cdot,0)$ projection enforces non-negativity so that generated features cannot actively reduce a class's likelihood during learning. The model is trained by optimizing cross-entropy on the few-shot labeled examples to learn $\mathbf{w}_k$.

To increase robustness and to cope with prompt-size limits, FeatLLM repeats the entire rule-generation + parsing + model-training process multiple times and ensembles the resulting predictors. The paper reports using ensembles to average predicted probabilities across $T$ trials (they use $T=20$ in experiments), and they randomize prompt elements (e.g., demonstration order) and set a non-zero sampling temperature during rule generation to encourage diversity. Bagging — selecting random subsets of features or instances for each trial — both increases diversity and reduces prompt length for datasets with many columns. Executing the generated code for each trial produces a collection of binary-feature matrices that are then scored, weighted, and averaged to produce final predictions at inference.

# Handling data types, categorical mappings, thresholds, and missing values
FeatLLM tackles type-related parsing errors primarily by supplying explicit feature type information in the prompt used to generate rules. When the prompt includes whether a column is numeric or categorical and optionally enumerates category values for categorical variables, the LLM is guided to produce rules consistent with feature type. The paper emphasizes that "when formulating rules, we let the LLM refer to the type of each feature in the basic information description part to avoid type mismatches in rules." This makes it less likely that the LLM will generate, for example, a numeric comparison for a categorical column or propose an impossible category value.

For categorical mappings, the prompt can list valid categories; the LLM then tends to generate membership-style rules (e.g., "feature X is in {A, B}"), and the synthesized parser code implements those as boolean membership checks. For numeric thresholds, the LLM often uses natural-language comparators ("greater than 5"), and the generated code normalizes these to explicit numeric comparisons. Because the LLM is responsible for producing code, it resolves the syntactic variability in comparators and threshold expressions: textual synonyms become consistent operators inside the synthesized function.

The paper does not provide a detailed treatment of missing values; it focuses on parsing rules and converting them into binary checks. As such, the handling of NaNs or missing entries is not explicitly described in the main method. In practice, the synthesized parser code (produced by the LLM) carries the responsibility for any conversion logic it includes; because FeatLLM relies on the LLM to produce correct conversion and checking behavior, any explicit missing-value policy would have to be encoded in the prompt or result from the LLM's default handling. The paper does not prescribe a canonical missing-value strategy.

# Validation strategies: unit tests, sanity checks, and fallback behaviors
FeatLLM uses several empirical and model-level mechanisms to validate and prune rules, rather than relying on handcrafted parser unit tests described in the paper. The validation workflow described in the paper includes measurement of feature informativeness, weight-based filtering after tuning, and ensembling-based consistency:

- Feature quality measurement. The paper measures the informativeness of generated binary features by computing the AUROC between each binary rule-feature and the target labels. They report the fraction of generated rules with AUROC $> 0.5$, confirming that the majority of rules are informative "before tuning." This per-feature AUROC is a straightforward sanity check that quantifies whether a rule correlates with the target.

- Weight tuning and thresholding. After generating binary features, FeatLLM fits the linear model and learns non-negative weights $\mathbf{w}_k$. The fitted weights provide an immediate, data-driven signal of a rule's utility. The paper filters out low-importance rules by thresholding learned weights: rules with weight below a chosen cutoff (they used $0.1$ in analysis) are treated as effectively irrelevant. This filtering is an automated fallback that leaves only rules supported by the few-shot data.

- Ensemble self-consistency. FeatLLM generates multiple independent rule-sets across trials (varying prompt order, sampling temperature, and via bagging). The observation in the paper is that "rules commonly inferred across multiple trials are more likely to be accurate." Thus, rules that appear repeatedly in different trials gain credence; ensemble averaging also mitigates the effect of occasional incorrect rules. Bagging further acts as a robustness check by forcing the system to work with different feature subsets or instance subsets.

- Cross-validation for training choices. For the small linear model trained on the binary features, the paper uses $k$-fold cross-validation to select the optimal number of epochs, which is a standard sanity check to avoid overfitting in the few-shot setting.

The paper does not describe a formal suite of unit tests or program-level static checks for the LLM-produced code. Instead, these empirical and model-driven diagnostics (AUROC, learned-weight pruning, repeated-trial consistency, and cross-validation) function as the practical validation and fallback mechanisms within FeatLLM.

# Security and safety considerations for code execution (sandboxing, auditing)
In FeatLLM the bridge from natural-language rules to executable features is concretely implemented by having the LLM return code and then executing that code via Python's exec(). The paper explicitly states that "the generated code is executed using Python’s exec() function to perform data conversion." Beyond this implementation detail, the main manuscript does not elaborate on runtime security, sandboxing, or code-auditing procedures for the generated code. The paper's broader discussion of impact focuses on how LLM priors can introduce societal biases into predictions and emphasizes the importance of analyzing those biases; it does not provide a treatment of code-execution safety or sandbox architectures in the experiments.

Therefore, within the scope of FeatLLM as described in the paper, the semantics are: (1) elicit code from an LLM with a clear I/O spec; (2) execute that code to translate rows into binary rule-features with Python's exec(); and (3) rely on empirical model-level checks (feature AUROC, weight-filtering, ensembling) to detect and mitigate bad or irrelevant rules. If a deployment requires explicit operational safety policies (sandboxing, static analysis, or execution auditing), those measures are outside the methods and analyses provided by the FeatLLM paper. The paper documents the executable-code approach and its downstream validation by model performance, but it does not prescribe a specific security framework for running LLM-generated code.

Modeling Class Likelihood with Simple, Constrained Models

# Per-class binary vectors $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$ from rule satisfaction
FeatLLM converts the LLM-generated rules into a compact, uniform representation that a light-weight classifier can use. For each target class $k$ the LLM produces $R$ human-readable rules; after parsing and executing those rules on an input sample $\mathbf{x}^i$, each rule yields a Boolean indicator: the rule is either satisfied (1) or not (0). Collecting these indicators for class $k$ gives the per-sample, per-class binary vector
\[
\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R,
\]
whose $r$th entry is 1 exactly when rule $r$ for class $k$ holds on $\mathbf{x}^i$.

This representation has several practical consequences. First, it homogenizes heterogeneous raw columns (numeric, categorical, text) into a single binary feature type, which simplifies downstream modeling and helps avoid type mismatch issues. Second, by building rule sets per class, the representation focuses predictive capacity on features that are directly interpretable as criteria for particular labels; a single sample therefore comes to be represented by up to $c$ such binary vectors (one per class), or equivalently by the concatenation of all class-rule indicators (total dimension $cR$). Because the rules are generated once and parsed into executable code, these binary features are deterministic at inference time and free of per-sample LLM queries.

# Non-negative weight vectors and dot-product scoring per class
Once rules are converted into binary indicators, FeatLLM assigns a trainable non-negative weight vector to each class, denoted $\mathbf{w}_k$. The per-class score (logit) for sample $i$ is computed by a dot product between the weight vector and the binary rule indicators for that class, with an elementwise projection that enforces non-negativity:
\[
\text{logit}_k^i \;=\; \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i .
\]
This is a linear scoring rule without an additive bias term; each satisfied rule contributes additively to the class score and its contribution is scaled by the corresponding learned weight. The max operator is applied elementwise, i.e., $\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)$ clamps any negative parameter values to zero before the dot product.

Interpreting the parameters, each coordinate of $\mathbf{w}_k$ represents the strength or importance of the corresponding rule for predicting class $k$. The dot product sums those strengths for all rules that the sample satisfies, producing a single scalar score per class. Separate weight vectors for each class allow the same rule text (or analogous rule forms) to play different roles for different classes if the system happens to produce rules that overlap between classes.

# Softmax over class scores to obtain probabilities
The model converts the collection of per-class logits into a probability distribution over classes via the softmax function. If there are $c$ classes, and for sample $i$ we have logits $\{\text{logit}_1^i,\dots,\text{logit}_c^i\}$, then the class probability vector is
\[
\mathbf{p}^i \;=\; \text{Softmax}\bigl([\text{logit}_1^i,\dots,\text{logit}_c^i]\bigr),
\]
so that $p_k^i = \exp(\text{logit}_k^i)\big/\sum_{j=1}^c \exp(\text{logit}_j^i)$.

In FeatLLM the final prediction can also incorporate an ensemble across multiple independent trials (different LLM rule generations and bagging choices). If trial $t$ produces a probability vector $\mathbf{p}^i[t]$, the ensemble average used for final decision-making is
\[
\overline{\mathbf{p}}^i \;=\; \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \mathbf{p}^i[t],
\]
i.e., FeatLLM averages the per-trial class probability vectors rather than averaging raw logits. Averaging probabilities is the approach described in the paper for combining the outputs of multiple independently trained linear scorers built from distinct rule-sets.

# Training with cross-entropy, Adam optimizer, and k-fold early stopping
FeatLLM learns the weight parameters $\{\mathbf{w}_k\}$ by fitting the simple constrained linear model to the few labeled examples available. The learning objective is the standard multi-class cross-entropy loss computed from the softmax probabilities:
\[
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W}) \;=\; -\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{train}}}\sum_{k=1}^c y_k^i \log p_k^i,
\]
where $y_k^i$ is the one-hot indicator for the true class of sample $i$ and $\mathbf{W}$ denotes the collection of all class weight vectors. Optimization is performed with the Adam optimizer; in the experiments reported in the paper the learning rate was set to $0.01$ and training was run for up to $200$ epochs. To avoid overfitting in the few-shot regime, k-fold cross-validation is used to select the optimal number of epochs (i.e., to perform early stopping): the training process monitors validation performance across folds and selects the epoch giving best generalization.

These choices keep the downstream fitting procedure extremely cheap compared to tuning or querying a large language model per example. Because the forward computation is a small dot product per class and the number of parameters is $cR$, training is fast even when repeated across trials for ensemble construction. The paper reports that this light-weight tuning step meaningfully improves performance versus leaving all rule-weights equal (see the ablation discussion below).

# Why non-negativity aligns with rule semantics and aids interpretability
A central design decision in FeatLLM is to enforce non-negativity of the rule weights. This constraint has a direct semantic rationale: the rules are generated by the LLM as "criteria" or conditions associated with an answer class. By construction, a rule intended to support class $k$ should, when satisfied, increase evidence for that class rather than decrease it. Clamping weights to be non-negative enforces this intuition: every rule can only add non-negative evidence to its associated class's score.

Beyond semantic alignment, the non-negativity constraint aids interpretability and downstream filtering. The magnitude of each learned non-negative weight directly orders rules by importance: large weights point to highly predictive rules, small or (clamped) zero weights indicate rules that are uninformative or possibly spurious. In the paper's analyses the authors use this property to quantify the quality of generated features and to filter out weak rules (they reported using a threshold of around $0.1$ on learned weights in one diagnostic experiment to mark and remove low-importance rules). Because the contributions are additive and non-negative, inspecting the weight vector is a straightforward way to explain which rules mattered for a particular class.

Finally, in the low-shot setting the non-negativity constraint limits the hypothesis space in a way that matches the intended semantics of the engineered features. That structural regularization—choosing a model family that encodes prior knowledge about what rules should mean—helps the simple linear classifier generalize from very few labeled examples.

# Alternatives and ablations: simple rule counts vs learned weights; logistic regression variants; regularization
The paper evaluates several ablations that illuminate the role of learned non-negative weights and other components. The primary ablation that isolates the effect of weight learning is called "-Tuning": instead of learning per-rule weights, the model simply sums the binary indicators for each class and uses that sum as the class logit. Concretely, this corresponds to setting every weight equal to 1 and computing $\text{logit}_k^i = \mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{z}_k^i$. The reported results show that removing the tuning step degrades performance across datasets; importantly, the benefit of learning weights grows as the number of labeled shots increases, because with more labels one can estimate which rules are truly more important and assign them larger weights.

The model used in FeatLLM is, mathematically, a constrained linear classifier followed by softmax. In an unconstrained formulation this is equivalent to multinomial logistic regression (a linear map to logits plus softmax). FeatLLM’s choices differ in two ways: it omits an additive bias term, and it enforces non-negativity on rule weights. The paper does not report experiments using other variants such as unconstrained logistic regression on the rule features or explicit $L_2$ regularization of the rule weights; instead it relies on k-fold cross-validation for epoch selection (early stopping), the positivity constraint, and ensembling with bagging to control variance. Removing the ensemble ("-Ensemble") is another ablation reported in the paper and it consistently lowers performance, showing that repeated, diverse rule generation plus averaging yields more robust predictors.

Finally, the paper examines ablations that affect rule generation (not the downstream linear model): omitting the feature descriptions or the first reasoning step in the prompt both reduce the predictive quality of generated rules and thus downstream performance. These results emphasize that model choices (counting vs learned weights) and prompt/design choices (how rules are generated) both contribute to the final behavior. In practice, FeatLLM’s combination—LLM-generated, parsed binary rules together with a small non-negative linear model trained by cross-entropy—strikes a balance between interpretability, semantic alignment, and empirical accuracy in the few-shot tabular learning problems evaluated by the authors.

Ensembling and Bagging for Robustness and Scalability

# Motivation: compensate for occasional bad rules and reduce prompt-size pressure

LLMs are powerful rule generators, but they are not perfect: a single prompt can produce incorrect, incomplete, or noisy rules. In FeatLLM this is important because the entire downstream classifier is built on the binary features derived from those rules. A few poor rules can meaningfully degrade a model that relies on a small number of such features (especially in few-shot regimes). At the same time, many real tabular datasets have large numbers of columns; serializing all of them into a single prompt can exceed context limits or produce extremely long prompts that harm LLM utility. These two limitations — occasional bad rule outputs and prompt-size pressure — motivate an ensemble approach that (a) dilutes the effect of bad rules by averaging over many independently generated rule-sets, and (b) reduces per-prompt length by letting each prompt only cover a subset of the full feature set.

FeatLLM addresses both problems by repeating the entire rule-generation → parsing → feature-creation → lightweight-model training pipeline multiple times, each time with different randomized inputs. Instead of relying on a single set of rules to cover every feature and every nuance of the data, FeatLLM produces many complementary rule-sets. If some trials produce incorrect or irrelevant rules, their influence is reduced in the final aggregate prediction because correct rules from other trials can compensate. Similarly, by partitioning features (or examples) across trials, each prompt can be kept short enough to be handled reliably by the LLM, while the union of trials still explores relationships across the whole dataset.

# Diversity via LLM temperature and randomized demo ordering

An ensemble is effective only if its individual members are reasonably diverse. FeatLLM deliberately injects diversity into the LLM-driven rule generation. Two practical levers are used: the LLM sampling temperature and the order of few-shot demonstrations in the prompt.

Temperature controls the stochasticity of the LLM's outputs. FeatLLM sets a nonzero temperature during rule generation so that repeated calls do not deterministically reproduce the same text; the experiments reported used temperature $0.5$ (with top-p set to $1$). This stochasticity makes the LLM produce different but plausible rule-sets across trials, increasing the variety of candidate features the overall system can consider.

The second lever is the ordering of few-shot demonstrations. The LLM’s in-context behavior depends on the examples provided and their arrangement; FeatLLM randomizes the order of the demonstration examples across trials so that the LLM will “attend” to different aspects of the same small training set on different trials. The paper observed that changing the order of demonstrations changes LLM outputs, and exploiting this produces a meaningful source of diversity in the generated rules.

Together, these two mechanisms convert a single deterministic feature-engineering pass into an ensemble of diverse, complementary passes. This diversity is crucial: repeated independent rule-sets are more likely to capture different valid cues from the few-shot data and the LLM’s prior knowledge, and averaging their downstream predictions produces a more robust final decision.

# Feature and instance bagging per trial to fit within context limits

When datasets have many columns or when including many example demonstrations would make the prompt too long, FeatLLM uses bagging to reduce per-trial prompt length. For each trial the system randomly selects a subset of features and/or a subset of training examples to include in that trial’s prompt. The LLM is then asked to generate rules only using that restricted view of the data. This per-trial selection keeps the serialized prompt short enough to fit the LLM’s context window and reduces the cognitive load on the LLM in each query.

Bagging serves two roles simultaneously. Practically, it reduces prompt size so the LLM can be used on wide tables (datasets such as Communities or Myocardial with dozens or hundreds of columns). Statistically, bagging increases ensemble diversity because different trials see different slices of the data and therefore tend to produce different rule-sets. Over many trials the ensemble collects many complementary binary features: while a single trial’s rules might capture only a small subset of informative columns, the union across trials covers a broader set of potentially useful signals. The paper emphasizes that this is particularly useful in the low-shot setting: when a single prompt cannot cover all potentially informative columns, an ensemble of bagged prompts can collectively explore them without ever requiring an excessively long prompt.

# Number of trials T and probability averaging as the final predictor

Each trial in FeatLLM produces a set of binary features (one per extracted rule per class), a trained set of non-negative linear weights for those features, and consequently a per-trial probabilistic predictor. Let the trained weights from trial $t$ be $\mathbf{w}[t]$. For a given sample $i$, trial $t$ computes per-class logits and probabilities using the rule-derived binary features $\mathbf{z}_k^i[t]$ for class $k$ exactly as in the core inference step:

\[
\text{logit}_k^{i}[t] \;=\; \max(\mathbf{w}_k[t], 0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i[t], \qquad
\mathbf{p}^{i}[t] \;=\; \text{Softmax}\big(\,[\text{logit}_{1}^{i}[t],\dots,\text{logit}_{c}^{i}[t]]\big).
\]

The final FeatLLM prediction for sample $i$ is obtained by averaging the per-trial probability vectors:

\[
\mathbf{p}_{\text{final}}^{i} \;=\; \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbf{p}^{i}[t].
\]

Averaging probabilities (rather than, say, averaging rules or raw logits) produces a stable, calibrated aggregate that benefits from the diversity among trials while respecting the per-trial learned weights. In practice, the paper used $T=20$ ensembles and $R=10$ rules per class in experiments. Hyper-parameter analysis showed that performance generally improves as $T$ increases, but gains saturate around $T\approx 20$, so that value provides a good trade-off between computation and performance in the reported experiments. Similarly, choosing $R=10$ per class balances information content against prompt length and the risk of overfitting in a low-shot regime.

# Self-consistency: consensus across trials as a reliability signal

A useful by-product of repeating rule generation is that frequently reproduced rules across trials act as a natural reliability signal. The authors connect this to the idea of LLM self-consistency: rules that the LLM returns repeatedly under different randomized prompts and demonstrations are more likely to capture genuine, useful relationships (either from prior knowledge or from the few training examples) than rules that appear only once or twice. In FeatLLM this manifests in two ways.

First, rules that recur across trials produce binary features that are repeatedly supported by multiple independently trained linear models, increasing their overall contribution to the averaged probability. Second, the ensemble design naturally downweights idiosyncratic or spurious rules because their influence is diluted in the mean over $T$ trials. The paper reports empirical evidence consistent with this intuition: the majority of generated rules are informative (many yield AUROC $>0.5$ against the target before tuning), and after tuning spurious rules are further filtered out by learned weights. In the spurious-feature experiment the framework also showed smaller performance degradation than baselines, and the authors note that rules based on noisy, irrelevant columns accounted for only about $1\!-\!2\%$ of the rules generated, illustrating how consensus and tuning together improve reliability.

# Compute and budget considerations; parallelizing API calls

Ensembling increases the number of LLM calls at generation time, so computational cost and API budget are important considerations. FeatLLM reduces the practical burden in several ways that the paper highlights.

First, feature-generation and rule-parsing occur only during training/time-of-feature-construction; once the binary features and the simple downstream model (a linear model with non-negative projected weights) are in place, inference on new samples requires no LLM queries. This design choice yields very low inference latency comparable to conventional tabular models. In contrast, many other LLM-based tabular methods need an LLM call per test sample, driving up inference costs dramatically.

Second, in the experiments the authors report that FeatLLM required only 30 API queries in the training/building phase (a modest cost that can be parallelized). The ensemble trials are independent by design, so their LLM queries can be executed in parallel to reduce wall-clock time. This parallelizability, together with the relatively small number of required queries in reported experiments, makes ensemble generation practical in real-world settings.

Finally, the paper cautions that applying the same bagging-and-ensemble idea to LLM-only end-to-end approaches would be far less practical: those methods would double or more the number of per-sample LLM inferences, making computation and latency prohibitive. FeatLLM’s separation — LLMs for offline feature engineering and a cheap model for online inference — is the key architectural decision that makes ensembling both effective and computationally feasible.

# Practical guidance and trade-offs

The experiments and analyses in the paper suggest straightforward practical recommendations. Use ensembling to stabilize rule-derived features and to cover wide tables without exceeding prompt limits; introduce diversity via nonzero temperature and randomized demonstration ordering; use feature/instance bagging per trial so that each prompt fits comfortably in the LLM’s context window; and aggregate by averaging per-trial class probability vectors as the final prediction. In the reported settings, $T\!=\!20$ ensemble trials and $R\!=\!10$ rules per class struck a good balance between performance and prompt complexity; temperature $0.5$ and top-p $=1$ were used during generation. Because trials are independent, parallelizing the LLM calls is an effective way to keep wall-clock time manageable, and the end-to-end inference remains cheap because it does not require LLM access.

Taken together, these design choices make FeatLLM robust to occasional LLM mistakes, scalable to wide tabular datasets, and practical from a computational and budget perspective, while preserving the interpretability advantage of rule-based features.

Experimental Setup: Datasets, Baselines, and Protocols

# Datasets: Adult, Bank, Blood, Car, Communities, Credit-g, Diabetes, Heart, Myocardial, Cultivars, NHANES, Sequence-type, Solution-mix

This work evaluates FeatLLM on a deliberately diverse collection of 13 tabular datasets drawn from common benchmarks, recent UCI additions, and synthetic tasks. Each dataset is treated as a supervised classification task (binary or multi-class) in order to measure how well the framework extracts and uses rule-based features under few-shot conditions. Below are the datasets and the prediction targets used in the experiments (phrasing follows the experimental paper):

- Adult: predict whether an individual earns over \$50,000 annually.  
- Bank: predict whether a customer will subscribe to a term deposit.  
- Blood: predict whether donors will return for subsequent donations.  
- Car: predict the quality of a car.  
- Communities: predict the crime rate in a specified region.  
- Credit-g: predict whether an individual poses a good or bad credit risk.  
- Diabetes: predict the presence of diabetes in an individual.  
- Heart: predict the occurrence of coronary artery disease in an individual.  
- Myocardial: predict whether an individual suffers from chronic heart failure.  
- Cultivars: predict how high the grain yield of a soybean cultivar will be.  
- NHANES: predict the person's age group from their record.  
- Sequence-type: classify a given sequence as one of four types: arithmetic, geometric, Fibonacci, or Collatz.  
- Solution-mix: predict whether the percentage concentration of a mixture from four solutions exceeds 0.5.

These datasets vary in difficulty, number of attributes, and domain (healthcare, finance, synthetic math sequences, agriculture). Each dataset comes with natural-language feature names and definitions; FeatLLM uses those descriptions as part of its prompt design. The paper notes that some datasets such as Communities and Myocardial contain over 100 attributes, a detail that directly influences prompt construction and motivates the bagging/ensemble strategy described later.

When building the LLM prompts, the few-shot examples are serialized into textual records. The serialization used in the experiments is the same as in prior LLM-for-tabular work and is shown compactly as:

\[
\text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i, F) = ``\ f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ \ldots\ f_d\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \ \text{Answer:}\ \mathbf{y}^i\ ".
\]

In the above, $F=\{f_j\}_{j=1}^d$ are the human-readable feature names and $\mathbf{x}^i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is an example’s feature vector. For the k-shot regime studied in the paper, only a small number $k$ of labeled samples ($k < N$) are randomly sampled from the full dataset $\mathcal{D}$ to build prompts and to train the downstream lightweight models.

# High-dimensional cases (e.g., Communities, Myocardial) and prompt-length implications

A practical challenge when interfacing tabular data with LLMs is prompt length. Serializing every feature for every example grows the textual prompt quickly; datasets with many attributes (for example, Communities and Myocardial, which have on the order of 100+ attributes) can produce input longer than the model’s comfortable context window or degrade LLM performance when prompts become unwieldy. The paper emphasizes that previous LLM-based tabular methods suffer or become infeasible as the number of serialized features increases.

FeatLLM addresses this limitation with two design choices that are important for reproducing the experiments and for reasoning about performance:

1. Fixed, compact rule extraction: during each LLM query the model is instructed to output a fixed small set of rules per class (the experiments fix this to $R=10$ rules per class). Limiting the number of rules controls the downstream feature dimensionality and prevents runaway prompt or feature growth.

2. Bagging + ensemble: to cope with many original features without requiring extremely long prompts, FeatLLM applies a bagging-style subsampling of features and/or instances in each trial. In each ensemble trial the LLM sees only a subset of columns or examples, produces rules based on that smaller view, and the system turns those rules into binary features. Repeating this process across trials and averaging predictions yields coverage across the full feature space without ever requiring a single oversized prompt. This strategy reduces per-query prompt length and trades a modest number of LLM queries for robustness and scalability.

Beyond prompt length, high-dimensional inputs increase the risk that a naive, per-example LLM classifier learns spurious correlations from very few examples. FeatLLM’s prompt structure (see later sections) also mitigates this by encouraging the LLM to reason about which features are plausibly related to the task (a two-step reasoning instruction), which empirically reduces extraction of rules based on irrelevant columns.

# Newly added UCI datasets (post-2023) and synthetic sets to avoid LLM memorization

When evaluating systems that leverage large pretrained LLMs it is important to guard against testing on tasks or data that the LLM may have seen during its pretraining. The paper explicitly includes two relatively recent UCI datasets (Cultivars and NHANES) that were donated to the UCI Machine Learning Repository after September 2023, which helps ensure they were not part of the LLM API’s pre-training data (the experiments used the gpt-3.5-turbo-0613 API). In addition, two synthetic datasets (Sequence-type and Solution-mix) were included precisely because synthetic generation prevents memorization by pretraining: by construction they could not have been present in the LLM’s training corpora. These choices strengthen claims about the LLM’s ability to use general prior knowledge and the few-shot examples, rather than merely recalling memorized dataset-specific artifacts.

# Baselines: LogReg, XGBoost, RandomForest, SCARF, STUNT, TabPFN, In-context, TABLET, TabLLM

The paper compares FeatLLM against a broad set of baselines spanning traditional machine learning, semi-/self-supervised approaches for tabular data, and prior LLM-based methods. Knowing the role and assumptions of each baseline helps interpret experimental results.

- Logistic regression (LogReg), XGBoost, RandomForest: standard supervised tabular learners. These serve as strong and widely-used classical baselines.

- SCARF and STUNT: methods that exploit large amounts of unlabeled data to improve few-shot performance. The paper points out that access to unlabeled datasets in sufficient quantity may be infeasible in many real-world settings, but these baselines represent the class of semi-supervised or unsupervised-augmentation approaches.

- TabPFN: a method that mixes many synthetic distribution families to pretrain a model (a Bayesian neural network) which can then produce predictions for new tabular tasks with no additional training. TabPFN’s design is appealing for few-shot regimes thanks to the pre-learned prior it encodes.

- In-context learning: a direct LLM baseline where few-shot examples are embedded into a single prompt and the LLM is used end-to-end for predictions at inference time (one LLM call per test sample).

- TABLET: enhances in-context prompts with external hints generated from an auxiliary classifier in the form of rule-sets and prototypes to improve inference quality.

- TabLLM: an LLM-based tabular learner trained with parameter-efficient tuning techniques (for example IA3 or LoRA in other contexts). The TabLLM setup in the paper follows its original experimental choices and—importantly—restricts itself to LLMs with public checkpoints (for example the T0 family is used as in the original TabLLM paper). That restriction means the experimental replication used T0 for TabLLM rather than GPT-3.5, because GPT-3.5 is accessible only via an API and does not have a released trainable checkpoint.

When reproducing results, the paper used GPT-3.5 for in-context learning baselines and T0 for TabLLM per the original TabLLM setup. For the classical machine learning baselines (LogReg, XGBoost, RandomForest) the paper used grid-search combined with $k$-fold cross validation to select hyperparameters.

# FeatLLM settings: GPT-3.5, temperature ~0.5, $R=10$ rules/class, $T=20$ ensembles, Adam $\text{lr}=0.01$, 200 epochs

Reproducing FeatLLM requires a few concrete implementation choices and hyperparameters that were fixed in the experiments:

- LLM backbone and sampling settings. The experiments use GPT-3.5 (the gpt-3.5-turbo-0613 API) as the LLM backbone for feature (rule) generation. The sampling temperature is set to $0.5$ and top-$p$ is kept at the API default of $1$. The paper also notes that the framework is LLM-agnostic and reports results with other backbones in the Appendix.

- Rules and ensembles. Each LLM query is instructed to produce a fixed number of rules per class; in the experiments $R=10$ rules per class. To build a robust predictor the entire rule-generation and feature-parsing process is repeated $T=20$ times (ensemble trials), introducing randomness between trials through sampling temperature and by randomizing the order of few-shot demonstrations. Bagging is used to subsample features or instances per trial to control prompt length and to increase diversity across trials.

- Linear downstream model. After parsing rules into binary features, FeatLLM trains a simple linear model without bias for each class. Let $\mathbf{z}_k^i\in\{0,1\}^R$ denote the binary feature vector for sample $i$ and class $k$ (the indicators of whether each of the $R$ rules for class $k$ fired on that sample). The paper computes logits and probabilities as

\[
\text{logit}_k^i \;=\; \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i, \qquad
\mathbf{p}^i \;=\; \text{Softmax}([\text{logit}_1^i,\ldots,\text{logit}_c^i]),
\]

where $\mathbf{w}_k$ are trainable weights for class $k$, $c$ is the number of classes, and the $\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)$ projection enforces non-negativity so that generated features cannot directly reduce a class’s score. The linear model is trained using cross-entropy loss.

- Optimization and epochs. The linear model is optimized with the Adam optimizer at a learning rate of $0.01$ for up to $200$ epochs. To select the appropriate stopping epoch the experiments use $k$-fold cross validation (choice of $k$ is described below).

- Parsing LLM output into executable feature code. The LLM’s natural-language rules are parsed into executable code to transform the original tabular rows into binary rule features. Because LLM outputs can vary syntactically, FeatLLM uses the LLM itself to produce a short parsing function: the prompt includes the function signature, input/output descriptions, and the inferred rules, and the LLM returns a concise program snippet which is then executed via Python’s \texttt{exec()} to convert data into binary features.

- Ensembling prediction. The final prediction for sample $i$ is the average across the $T$ trials of the class probability vectors produced by each trial’s trained linear model. Averaging over trials increases robustness to incorrect rules produced in any single trial.

# Model selection with $k$-fold CV; grid search for classical baselines; TabLLM with T0 per original setup

To make fair comparisons, the experiments follow standard model-selection protocols:

- Classical baselines (LogReg, XGBoost, RandomForest) are tuned via grid-search combined with $k$-fold cross-validation. The paper sets the value of $k$ to either $2$ or $4$ depending on the shot regime, chosen so that every fold’s training set contains at least one example of each class (this is important in extreme low-shot settings).

- For FeatLLM’s lightweight linear model, $k$-fold cross validation is used to choose the optimal training epoch (early stopping) among the 200 epochs.

- LLM-based baselines: in-context learning baselines used the same GPT-3.5 API as FeatLLM for fair comparison of end-to-end LLM inference. TabLLM is reproduced using the publicly-available T0 model in accordance with the original TabLLM setup because TabLLM’s protocol requires a trainable checkpoint (GPT-3.5 is available only as an API, not as a trainable checkpoint).

These choices are intended to avoid giving undue advantage to any single method due to hyperparameter or model-selection differences.

# Compute environment and API usage notes

A few practical implementation and compute details are relevant when reproducing or extending the experiments:

- Hardware used for timing experiments. The runtime comparison reported in the paper (on the Adult dataset with a 16-shot training set) uses one A100 GPU as the default compute device for methods that have local training or inference; TabLLM required model parallelism and was evaluated using four A100 GPUs. These hardware choices affect wall-clock training and inference time but do not alter algorithmic behavior.

- API usage and cost considerations. A key design point of FeatLLM is that it requires only API-level access to the LLM (no LLM fine-tuning). The system uses a modest number of LLM queries during feature engineering: the paper reports that FeatLLM requires only 30 API queries in the reported experimental setup, and that these queries can be parallelized. After feature generation and lightweight linear-model training, inference is performed without any per-sample LLM queries; this makes FeatLLM’s inference cost comparable to classical tabular models and markedly lower than end-to-end LLM baselines that require an LLM call per test sample.

- Prompt sampling choices to encourage diversity. To generate diverse rule sets across ensemble trials, the system uses stochastic sampling from the LLM (temperature $=0.5$ in the experiments) and randomizes the order of few-shot demonstrations in the prompt; both mechanisms introduce trial-to-trial variability that the ensemble can exploit.

# Protocols, metrics, and practical recommendations

The empirical protocol in the paper is configured to give statistically meaningful comparisons under low-shot conditions and to make results reproducible:

- Repetitions and metrics. Experiments are repeated three times with different random choices of training/test splits (and other stochastic elements), and results are reported as the mean and standard deviation of the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC). Reporting both mean and standard deviation helps assess average performance and variability in low-data regimes.

- Hyperparameter choices and sensitivity. The paper studies two central hyperparameters: the number of ensemble trials $T$ and the number of rules per class $R$. They find that increasing $T$ improves performance up to a point (performance gains flatten around $T\approx 20$ in their experiments), and that $R=10$ is a reasonable trade-off between richer feature descriptions and prompt/feature-space blowup in the few-shot setting. These empirical observations informed the fixed choices used across the reported results.

- Practical trade-offs. When reproducing experiments, keep in mind the following practical points emphasized by the authors: (1) bagging subsamples of features and examples help keep prompt sizes manageable and also increase ensemble diversity; (2) forcing a fixed, modest number of rules per class controls the downstream feature dimensionality; (3) parsing LLM outputs via small code snippets produced by the LLM itself is robust to surface-level syntactic variations; and (4) because FeatLLM performs no per-sample LLM calls at test time, it is substantially more efficient at inference than end-to-end LLM baselines.

Taken together, these dataset choices, baseline replications, hyperparameter settings, and compute considerations form a consistent experimental setup. They enable a fair comparison between FeatLLM and both classical and LLM-augmented baselines in low-shot tabular learning, while addressing important practical constraints like prompt length, LLM access model (API vs. trainable checkpoint), and the risk of dataset memorization by pretrained LLMs.

Results: Accuracy, Robustness, and Efficiency

# AUC comparisons across datasets: FeatLLM as top or second performer
The primary quantitative claim in the paper is that FeatLLM attains consistently strong predictive performance across a diverse set of tabular tasks. The evaluation used 13 datasets spanning domains (finance, healthcare, synthetic tasks, etc.), and performance was reported using the area under the ROC curve (AUC), averaged over three random train/test splits (with mean and standard deviation). Across these datasets and few-shot settings, FeatLLM either ranked first or second among the set of baselines. Concretely, the paper reports that FeatLLM yields large improvements over other LLM-based approaches and traditional baselines, with an average advantage on the order of 10% versus alternatives such as TabLLM and STUNT.

Why AUC matters here: AUC measures the tradeoff between true positive rate and false positive rate across thresholds, and is widely used for tabular binary and multi-class problems where class imbalance can matter. The paper uses AUC as a consistent scalar to compare few-shot behavior across heterogeneous tasks; the consistent ranking of FeatLLM implies that the rule-based features it generates produce discriminative signals that generalize across domains even when only a few labeled examples are available.

Intuition for the superior accuracy. FeatLLM leverages two complementary mechanisms to achieve this performance. First, the LLM injects domain priors and commonsense reasoning into compact, interpretable rules that are converted into binary features; these features often capture informative decision boundaries that a simple linear combination can turn into accurate class likelihoods. Second, ensembling (multiple LLM rule-generation trials with bagging) reduces the chance that any single noisy rule-set dominates predictions. Finally, a small downstream training stage (learning non-negative weights over the rule features) adapts the LLM-derived signals to the observed few-shot data, boosting accuracy further.

# Data efficiency: 4-shot FeatLLM vs 32-shot classical baselines
One of the standout empirical claims is that FeatLLM attains the same level of performance with far fewer labeled examples than many classical tabular learners. The paper states that, in their experiments, FeatLLM with only 4 labeled examples (4-shot) achieves comparable performance to traditional methods trained with 32 labeled examples on the same tasks. This is a concrete expression of data efficiency: FeatLLM relies on the LLM’s prior knowledge to produce features that substitute for much of the label information that would otherwise be needed to learn useful representations from scratch.

Why this happens, intuitively. In few-shot regimes the dominant challenge is insufficient signal to estimate parameters or discover meaningful feature transformations. FeatLLM sidesteps that by having the LLM propose domain-relevant conditions (rules) that partition the input space in ways aligned with the task. Those rules act like distilled expert features: each rule can be thought of as a high-signal engineered feature. With even a handful of labels, the downstream linear model can learn which of those rules are predictive (by learning non-negative weights) rather than having to discover structure only from the few labels. The paper’s ablation further corroborates this intuition: the benefit of “reasoning” and supplying feature descriptions is especially large in the lowest-shot regimes, while the benefit of weight tuning grows as more labeled data become available.

# Handling many-feature datasets where direct LLM baselines struggle or fail
A common and practical bottleneck for LLM-based tabular methods that serialize whole rows into text is the context-window (prompt length) limitation. When datasets have many columns (the paper cites examples like Communities and Myocardial with over $100$ attributes), serializing full rows plus demonstrations can exceed feasible prompt sizes or degrade performance.

FeatLLM approaches this problem structurally. Rather than forcing all features into a single large prompt or invoking the LLM per test sample, FeatLLM uses feature bagging and ensembling: each LLM query focuses on a small, random subset of columns and a small set of demonstrations, generating a compact set of rules for that subset. Repeating this process across many trials (the paper typically used $T=20$ ensembles and $R=10$ rules per class per trial) collects diverse rule-based features that, when parsed and combined, provide wide coverage of the original feature space without ever requiring a single enormous prompt. Because each LLM interaction is limited to a manageable serialization, this design overcomes prompt length constraints while still recovering predictive structure across many attributes.

By contrast, the paper notes that other LLM-based baselines (in-context learning, TABLET, TabLLM) were unable to infer on datasets with a large number of features: either the prompt became infeasible or performance declined sharply. FeatLLM’s bagging + ensemble design therefore provides a practical, scalable path for applying LLM priors to wide tables.

# Training and inference time: low inference latency comparable to classical ML
A key practical advantage of FeatLLM is that the costly LLM interactions are confined to an up-front feature-engineering phase; at inference time the system runs a compact learned model over binary rule features, avoiding per-sample LLM calls. This design leads to low inference latency comparable to classical ML methods (e.g., logistic regression, RandomForest, XGBoost), as reported in the runtime comparisons on the Adult dataset (with a 16-shot training set). In the paper’s experimental setup, FeatLLM’s inference time was similar to conventional approaches, while other LLM-based methods (which route each test sample through an LLM or require heavy fine-tuning) had substantially higher inference times.

On the training side, FeatLLM is also practical: the downstream training is simply fitting a linear model (non-negative weights) to the binary features. The LLM interactions required to generate rule-sets are modest in number and can be parallelized. The paper reports only a small fixed number of API queries for rule generation (see the next section), and uses a standard optimizer (Adam, learning rate $0.01$, up to $200$ epochs) with $k$-fold cross-validation to select epochs. In summary, FeatLLM trades a small, one-time LLM cost for fast, classical inference thereafter.

# Cost profile: limited API calls vs per-sample LLM inference in other methods
From an operational and monetary perspective, the difference between a one-time or limited number of LLM queries and per-sample LLM inference is large. FeatLLM is explicitly designed to minimize API usage: it confines LLM calls to the rule-generation and rule-parsing phases. The paper emphasizes that FeatLLM required only about $30$ API queries in their setup — a small and parallelizable budget — and after that the system performs all predictions without further LLM access. This contrasts with many in-context approaches that must send every test sample to the LLM, incurring per-sample latency and cost that scale linearly with the number of predictions.

There is also a practical accessibility advantage: FeatLLM operates with only API-level access to an LLM (the paper used GPT-3.5 in experiments), avoiding the need for training or fine-tuning capabilities that are not available for many leading LLMs. Methods that require parameter-efficient fine-tuning or access to model checkpoints (e.g., TabLLM in its original design) are more difficult to apply in settings where only an inference API is available.

# Situations where STUNT/TabPFN shine and their overall trade-offs
The paper evaluates other modern few-shot or semi-supervised tabular approaches and notes where they excel and where they fall short. Two such methods are STUNT and TabPFN:

- TabPFN pre-trains a model on a large mixture of synthetic distributions so that at test time it can perform Bayesian-like inference on small datasets without further training. This synthetic pretraining can produce excellent few-shot behavior on some tasks because it provides a very broad prior over plausible data-generating processes.

- STUNT leverages large quantities of unlabeled data (and augmentation strategies) to improve few-shot performance via semi-supervised learning.

Where these methods shine. The paper observes that STUNT and TabPFN produced significant improvements on certain individual datasets — precisely those where their respective priors (synthetic distributions for TabPFN, or available unlabeled data for STUNT) match the target task characteristics. When those favorable conditions hold, these methods can outperform conventional learners and even some LLM-based approaches.

The trade-offs. However, the paper emphasizes that these gains are not uniformly realized across the diverse set of tasks considered. TabPFN’s synthetic pretraining is a fixed prior that may not match all real-world distributions; STUNT’s reliance on plentiful unlabeled data is often impractical in applied scenarios. Empirically, their overall enhancement over standard classical models was not consistently large across the 13 datasets. In contrast, FeatLLM’s use of LLM priors plus an ensemble bagging strategy is more flexible: it requires only a modest number of labeled examples and API access to an LLM, and it scales to many-feature tables without needing extra unlabeled datasets or heavy pretraining.

Concluding remarks
The experimental results in the paper together make a consistent case: by using LLMs as feature engineers (producing interpretable rules that are parsed into binary features), and then fitting a lightweight downstream model, FeatLLM achieves a favorable combination of accuracy, robustness, and efficiency in low-shot tabular learning. Its ensemble and bagging design handles many-feature tables and limits prompt sizes, its one-time LLM usage keeps inference fast and cheap, and its feature-tuning step adapts generated rules to observed labels. Ablations reported in the paper (removing tuning, ensemble, feature descriptions, or the reasoning step) all reduce performance, underlining that the accuracy, robustness, and efficiency are consequences of the full design working together.

Ablations and Diagnostic Analyses

# Ablations
An ablation study probes which components of FeatLLM are responsible for its empirical gains. In the paper, four controlled ablations were evaluated: (1) -Tuning, which removes the learned weighting of rule-features and instead aggregates them by simple counting; (2) -Ensemble, which omits the repeated rule-generation + bagging ensemble; (3) -Description, which removes the explicit feature descriptions from the prompt; and (4) -Reasoning, which drops the first reasoning step where the LLM is asked to infer general tendencies between features and the task (the two-step decomposition is reduced to the single step that uses examples directly).

Each ablation targets an identifiable mechanism in FeatLLM and reveals its role. -Tuning replaces the trained linear combination of rule-features by a uniform count. Concretely, with tuning the model computes logits per class using a non-negative weight vector learned from data:
$\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,$
where $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$ is the binary vector of $R$ rule-features for class $k$. The -Tuning variant instead uses the unweighted count,
$\text{logit}_k^i = \sum_{r=1}^{R} z_{k,r}^i,$
which corresponds to fixing all weights to 1. The empirical effect is a measurable drop in AUC: learned weights allow the model to down-weight noisy or weak rules and to up-weight strongly predictive ones, so removing tuning hurts, particularly when enough labeled data exist to estimate weights reliably.

-Ensemble evaluates the contribution of repeated rule generation with bagging and averaging. The full method produces $T$ independent rule-sets and trained weight vectors $\{\mathbf{w}[t]\}_{t=1}^T$, computes per-trial class probabilities $\mathbf{p}^i[t]$ (via the logit and softmax), and aggregates by averaging:
$\mathbf{p}^i = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbf{p}^i[t].$
Dropping this ensemble reduces robustness: single rule-sets are more vulnerable to occasional poor rule generations from the LLM, especially under the stochasticity of in-context queries, and bagging is also the mechanism that keeps individual prompt sizes manageable when many features exist.

-Description tests whether giving the LLM explicit feature type/name/definition matters. Omitting descriptions forces the LLM to attempt rule generation from examples alone, increasing the chance of type mismatches (e.g., comparing a categorical field as if it were numeric) or of paying attention to misleading columns. The ablation shows that including feature descriptions improves the quality of rules, especially when labeled examples are few.

-Reasoning removes the Step-1 instruction that asks the LLM to first infer general, prior tendencies between features and the task before composing concrete rules from examples. Empirically, removing this step reduces performance: the two-step decomposition helps the model rely on domain priors (from the LLM’s pretraining) instead of overfitting to a tiny set of examples, so the generated rules are more semantically grounded and less likely to reflect spurious correlations.

Across the datasets and shot regimes reported in the paper, every ablation led to degraded AUC relative to the full FeatLLM pipeline, showing that these components each contribute materially to the final performance.

# Shot-dependent effects
The importance of individual components changes with the amount of labeled data (the number of shots). Two patterns emerged from the analyses in the paper.

First, as the number of labeled examples grows, the benefit of weight tuning becomes larger. With more examples it becomes statistically feasible to estimate per-rule importances accurately. In the extreme low-shot setting there simply is not enough signal to learn reliable weights, so the difference between learned weighting and uniform counting shrinks. But once a reasonable number of shots is available, the learned $\mathbf{w}_k$ allow the model to suppress noisy rules and magnify informative ones, producing a larger improvement in AUC.

Second, when shots are extremely scarce, components that feed LLM prior knowledge become more valuable. The feature descriptions and the two-step reasoning instruction matter most in the ultra-low-shot regime because they inject extra structure and domain priors into rule generation. With only a couple of labeled examples, an LLM that has been guided about feature semantics and asked to articulate general tendencies is far less likely to latch onto spurious idiosyncrasies of the provided examples. Thus, descriptions and the explicit Step-1 reasoning instruction produce proportionally larger gains when data are minimal.

These shot-dependent behaviors underline a practical recommendation: rely more on careful prompt engineering (feature descriptions, structured reasoning) when labels are very few; when you can obtain more labeled data, allocate effort to learning weights for the discovered features so the downstream learner can calibrate feature importances.

# Feature quality
Two linked diagnostic measurements were used to quantify the informativeness of LLM-generated rules. For each generated rule (which becomes a binary feature across samples), the authors computed the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) between that binary feature and the target label. From these per-rule AUROCs they reported two summary statistics: the average AUROC across rules and the fraction of rules whose AUROC exceeded $0.5$ (i.e., rule is better than random).

Before any tuning, a majority of the created rules were already informative: many rule-features had AUROC $>0.5$, demonstrating that the LLM can leverage prior knowledge plus the few examples to produce meaningful criteria. This validates the central premise that LLMs can act as effective feature engineers in low-shot tabular problems.

Tuning the linear model further improves the effective feature set by assigning small weights to weak or misleading rules and larger weights to informative ones. Empirically the authors applied a simple pruning step after learning: any rule whose learned weight fell below a threshold (they used $0.1$, chosen by inspecting the overall weight histogram) was considered effectively irrelevant. After pruning by this threshold, the fraction of remaining useful rules (AUROC $>0.5$) increased—weak rules were filtered out automatically by the tuning process.

The mathematical view ties these diagnostics together. For class $k$, the feature vector for sample $i$ is $\mathbf{z}_k^i\in\{0,1\}^R$ and the class logit is
$\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot\mathbf{z}_k^i.$
Learning $\mathbf{w}_k$ via cross-entropy on the few labeled samples both discovers which rules correlate with the target in the observed data and provides an operational mechanism to discard low-utility rules without separate heuristics.

# Spurious correlation study
To evaluate robustness to irrelevant features, the paper ran an experiment that simulates spurious correlations by augmenting the Heart dataset with columns drawn randomly from the Adult dataset—columns that are unrelated to the Heart task. The setup fixed an 8-shot labeled training regime and progressively increased the number of unrelated columns appended to the Heart features. Methods that require large unlabeled corpora (e.g., SCARF and STUNT) were excluded from this comparison because the experiment assumed no extra unlabeled data.

The observed pattern was that FeatLLM exhibited the least performance degradation as more unrelated columns were appended, compared to the conventional baselines tested. Two mechanisms explain this robustness. First, the two-step reasoning prompt biases the LLM to consider which features make sense given the task before forming concrete rules, so it is less prone to picking idiosyncratic patterns from irrelevant columns. Second, ensemble + bagging distributes rule generation across randomized subsets of features and few-shot orders; incorrect rules that happen to exploit noise in some trials are diluted when probabilities are averaged across trials.

Quantitatively, the paper reports that rules based on the noisy (appended) columns accounted for only about 1–2% of the total generated rules, a small fraction. This indicates that the LLM’s prior-guided rule generation and the overall FeatLLM pipeline together are effective at filtering out irrelevant columns in low-shot scenarios.

# Caveat: vulnerability when appended features are closely related
The favorable spurious-robustness result comes with an important caveat documented in the paper. If the appended columns are not arbitrary noise but are closely related to the target task (for example, if they are drawn from a dataset that shares causal structure or strong statistical associations with the Heart labels), FeatLLM can still learn spurious correlations and misunderstand causal relationships. In such cases the LLM’s prior knowledge and the few examples may legitimately suggest rules that correlate with the target in the observed data but are not causally valid for out-of-sample or shifted contexts.

This vulnerability is not unique to FeatLLM — it reflects the fundamental difficulty of distinguishing causation from correlation with limited labeled data. The paper explicitly observes this failure mode (reported in the Appendix) and highlights it as a practical limitation: when additional columns are semantically or statistically close to the target, rule generation may produce plausible but spurious rules. Users should therefore treat rules generated from weakly supervised settings with appropriate skepticism and, where possible, validate them on held-out or domain-perturbed data.

# Hyperparameters: ensembles and rules-per-class
Two hyperparameters control the balance between prompt size, diversity, and downstream capacity: the number of ensembles $T$ (the number of independent rule-generation trials) and the number of rules per class $R$ produced in each trial.

Increasing $T$ generally improves performance because it increases the chance of generating high-quality rules and allows averaging to cancel idiosyncratic mistakes. The empirical curves reported in the paper show diminishing returns: performance improves as $T$ rises but begins to plateau around $T\approx 20$. Beyond that point additional trials give only marginal benefits while increasing API cost and total computation.

The choice of $R$ trades feature expressivity against prompt length and overfitting risk. Producing more rules per class raises the dimensionality of the downstream model, which can increase information but also elevates the danger of overfitting when labeled data are scarce. In the experiments there was no statistically large difference across a range of $R$, and $R=10$ per class was chosen as a practical sweet spot considering prompt length constraints and empirical behavior. The paper suggests that a larger $R$ can be tolerated if more shots are available, but in ultra-low-shot settings smaller $R$ helps avoid over-parameterization.

Two operational knobs further contribute to hyperparameter behavior. First, bagging (selecting random subsets of features or instances per trial) reduces the prompt length for any single LLM query and increases diversity across trials. Second, stochasticity in the LLM queries (higher temperature and randomized ordering of few-shot examples) is deliberately used to increase rule diversity across the ensemble. Together these choices let FeatLLM manage large feature sets and obtain robust predictions without issuing an LLM query per test sample.

Practical Guidance, Implementation, and Reproducibility

# End-to-end checklist: data curation, feature names/definitions, task phrasing
FeatLLM assumes a standard tabular input: a labeled dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i)\}_{i=1}^N$ with $d$ input columns and natural-language feature names $F=\{f_j\}_{j=1}^d$ plus (optionally) separate human-readable definitions for those feature names. Before you start prompting an LLM, collect and prepare those elements: (1) the small set of labeled examples you will use for few-shot demonstrations (the paper reports experiments with $k$ from very small values up to 32); (2) human-readable feature names and, when available, short definitions or descriptions of what each column means; and (3) the task description / label information written as a concise question (the paper uses question-style prompts such as “Does this patient’s myocardial infarction complications data indicate chronic heart failure? Yes or no?”).

When preparing demonstrations, serialize each example into text using the format used in the paper. In LaTeX notation the paper writes the serializer as
$\text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i, F) = \text{" }f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ ...\ f_d\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \ \text{Answer:}\ \mathbf{y}^i\text{"}$.
Concretely, include feature-value pairs in natural language followed by the ground-truth label. For categorical features include the typical categories in the basic information part of the prompt so the LLM can reason about them correctly.

Finally, decide and record the FeatLLM hyperparameters you will use. The experiments in the paper set the number of rules per class to $R=10$ and the number of ensemble trials to $T=20$, and used GPT-3.5 with temperature $0.5$ and top-p $=1$. These values are a practical starting point because they control prompt length (rules per class) and the degree of ensemble averaging (trials). Keep these choices in your experiment configuration so results can be reproduced.

# Crafting clear demonstrations and response templates
FeatLLM relies on in-context learning, so the way you present demonstrations and the response format matters. The paper uses a three-part prompt structure: (1) a basic information description (task phrasing, feature descriptions, categorical value examples, and serialized few-shot demonstrations); (2) a reasoning instruction that guides the model’s internal chain-of-thought; and (3) a response instruction that constrains the output format to make parsing tractable.

For demonstrations, follow the serializer above and choose representative few-shot examples that exhibit variations relevant to the task. The reasoning instruction in FeatLLM is explicit: it splits the model’s process into two steps. Step 1 asks the LLM to state general tendencies or causal relationships between features and the target using prior knowledge (without looking at the demonstrations). Step 2 then asks the LLM to use the few-shot demonstrations plus the Step 1 summary to propose decisive rules for each class. This two-step structure is intended to reduce spurious correlations (because Step 1 grounds the model in general background knowledge) and to make the subsequent rule extraction more robust.

For response templates, instruct the LLM to produce a fixed-format list of rules per class and to follow a specific syntactic template for each rule (the paper enforces such formatting to make parsing easier). The prompt should tell the model how many rules to supply per class (the paper fixes this to 10) and remind it of the feature types so that, for example, it does not produce a numeric comparison on a categorical feature. The response instruction may also allow logical operators (AND / OR) where appropriate; the parser stage converts these structured rule strings into executable checks.

# Choosing bag sizes for features/instances to fit context windows
FeatLLM uses bagging (feature / instance sub-sampling) in each ensemble trial to keep each prompt within an LLM’s context window and to increase diversity across trials. The core idea in the paper is simple: when your table has many columns or you must include many demonstration lines, select only a subset of features and/or training instances for a given trial’s prompt. Repeat the whole rule-generation+parsing+training pipeline multiple times with different bags and average the resulting probability outputs across trials.

The experiments provide practical hyperparameter guidance: the authors used $T=20$ ensemble trials and $R=10$ rules per class. Their hyperparameter sweep showed that average performance improves as $T$ increases but plateaus near $T\approx 20$, and that $R=10$ was a useful trade-off between expressivity and prompt length. Choose bag sizes (how many features and examples per prompt) so that the serialized text remains well within your LLM’s context limit; smaller bags reduce per-prompt length but typically require more ensemble trials to cover all columns. In datasets with many columns (the paper cites Communities and Myocardial as examples), bagging is the mechanism that makes FeatLLM feasible.

# Designing parsing prompts and validating generated code
The rules that FeatLLM asks the LLM to produce are written in natural language; converting those textual rules into binary features (one per rule per class) is a required parsing step. The paper’s pragmatic solution is to use the LLM again to produce a small piece of code that implements the rules. Concretely, form a parsing prompt that includes a function name, a clear description of the inputs and outputs, and the inferred natural-language rules; ask the LLM to output simple executable code (for example, a Python function) that, given a tabular row, returns the rule-satisfaction booleans.

This approach leverages the LLM’s ability to understand semantic variants (“greater than” vs. “>”, wordy expressions, synonyms) so you do not have to write a brittle hand-coded parser that tries to cover all textual patterns. The paper then executes the generated code using Python’s exec() to perform the conversion from rows to binary rule-features. In the experiments, the LLM-generated code is the exact mechanism used to build the vectors $\mathbf{z}_k^i\in\{0,1\}^{R}$ for each class $k$ and sample $i$.

# Debugging misparsed rules and adding guardrails for types/units
The authors anticipate syntactic and stylistic variation in LLM outputs (for example, replacing “>” with “greater than”), and they deploy several guardrails in the prompt design to reduce parsing failures. First, the basic information section of the prompt includes the type of each feature (numeric, categorical, etc.) and, for categorical features, the allowed categories; this reduces type-mismatch rules. Second, the response instruction forces a fixed template for each rule and a fixed number of rules per class (the paper uses 10), which keeps the parsing problem bounded.

Even with these protections, some rules may be noisy or irrelevant. FeatLLM addresses this by learning per-rule importance from data: after converting rules to binary features, the pipeline fits a simple linear model with non-negative projected weights and cross-entropy loss (see the next section). The tuning step down-weights or effectively ignores rules that are not supported by the labeled data. In the paper’s analysis the authors computed AUROC between each generated rule-feature and the target and showed that many rules with low relevance are filtered out by tuning; they used a practical threshold (0.1) on learned weights to mark rules as effectively unused in a post-hoc analysis. The positivity projection $\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)$ in the model (see the next section) is itself a guardrail: it enforces that rules only contribute non-negatively to the class logits.

# Managing cost/latency; batching and parallel API queries
One of FeatLLM’s key operational advantages reported in the paper is that LLM calls are required only during rule generation and parsing, not at per-sample inference time. The experiments report that the training-stage LLM usage is modest: the paper states the method “requires only 30 API queries” in their timing comparison and that these queries are trivially parallelizable across ensemble trials. In contrast, approaches that ask an LLM to predict each test sample incur a large per-sample inference cost.

Because FeatLLM’s inference schema runs a simple learned model (a linear combination of binary rule-features) without calling the LLM, inference latency and cost are comparable to conventional tabular models. In practice, the paper used temperature $=0.5$ for the LLM when sampling multiple trials and a top-p of $1$. The paper also notes that a higher sampling temperature is used to encourage diversity among trials for the ensemble. These concrete numbers are the operational knobs reported in the experiments and are suitable defaults when reproducing the work.

# Handling missing data, class imbalance, and multiclass setups
Multiclass tasks are explicitly supported by FeatLLM. The method generates $R$ rules per class and builds binary vectors $\mathbf{z}_k^i\in\{0,1\}^R$ for each class $k$. The paper uses a per-class linear score and a softmax to obtain probabilities. In the paper this is written as
\[
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,\qquad
\mathbf{p}^i = \text{Softmax}\big([\text{logit}_1^i,\dots,\text{logit}_c^i]\big),
\]
and the linear-model weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ are trained with cross-entropy loss. This design makes the method applicable to both binary and multi-class problems.

The paper does not develop a dedicated missing-data imputation strategy; FeatLLM’s prompt design asks that feature types and typical categories be provided in the basic information block. If your dataset contains missing entries, FeatLLM as described in the paper expects the table and the serialized demonstrations to reflect the same conventions the parser code will handle; the paper does not prescribe additional imputation or special handling. For class imbalance, the paper reports that, when performing $k$-fold cross-validation for epoch selection, they set $k$ to values (2 or 4) that guarantee each fold’s training set contains at least one example of every class; ensure folds and shot selection follow the same constraint to avoid degenerate training splits.

# Worked example outline (e.g., Heart/Myocardial): prompt, rules, parsing, training, interpretation
Below is a concise, paper-grounded sequence of steps as used for datasets such as Heart and Myocardial.

1. Prepare the dataset and meta-data. Collect the $k$ labeled examples you will show in-context, and assemble human-readable feature names and optional short definitions. For categorical columns, list the categories.

2. Build the prompt. Include (a) a short task description phrased as a question with label semantics; (b) feature descriptions including type information; (c) serialized $k$-shot demonstrations using the serializer $\text{Serialize}(\cdot)$ shown earlier; (d) a reasoning instruction that asks the LLM first to state general relationships (Step 1) and then to derive rules using the demonstrations (Step 2); and (e) a response instruction that fixes the rule template and asks for exactly $R=10$ rules per class.

3. Generate rules with the LLM. Run $T$ trials (the paper uses $T=20$), randomizing the order of the few-shot demonstrations and setting temperature (the paper used $0.5$, raising it as needed for ensemble diversity). Optionally bag features/instances so each prompt stays within the context window.

4. Parse rules to code. For each trial submit a parsing prompt that contains a function name, input/output description, and the textual rules; ask the LLM to emit simple Python code that evaluates the rules for a row and returns binary indicators. Execute that generated code (the paper used Python’s exec()) to produce $\mathbf{z}_k^i$ for all samples.

5. Train the lightweight model. For each trial fit non-negative-projected linear weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ per class to the rule-feature vectors by minimizing cross-entropy. The paper used the Adam optimizer with learning rate $0.01$ and trained for $200$ epochs, selecting the optimal epoch with $k$-fold cross-validation. The logits and probabilities follow the equation above.

6. Ensemble predictions. Average the per-trial softmax probabilities $\mathbf{p}^i$ across the $T$ trials to obtain final predictions.

7. Interpretation. The learned weight $\mathbf{w}_k$ indicates the importance of each rule for class $k$. In the paper’s analyses rules with very small learned weights are interpreted as uninformative; the authors used a threshold of $0.1$ in post-hoc analyses to quantify how many rules the model effectively ignored.

This sequence is exactly the pipeline used in the experiments that showed FeatLLM often outperforms alternative few-shot tabular baselines and is robust to extraneous columns when the reasoning instruction guides rule extraction.

# Reproducibility practices: seeding, logging, and configuration management
The paper reports several reproducibility choices you should mirror when implementing FeatLLM. First, each experimental setting was repeated multiple times with different randomness in train/test splits, and results are reported as averages with standard deviations (the paper repeats experiments three times). Second, hyperparameters used in the experiments are explicitly stated: GPT-3.5 with temperature $0.5$ and top-p $=1$, $T=20$ ensembles, $R=10$ rules per class, Adam optimizer with learning rate $0.01$, and $200$ training epochs with epoch selection by $k$-fold cross-validation (with $k$ chosen to ensure each training fold contains every class). The authors also release code (an anonymized GitHub link is provided in the paper), which you should use to confirm exact prompt templates and runtime configurations.

When you reproduce or extend the method, record the same set of metadata the paper reports: the LLM model and API parameters (model name, temperature, top-p), number of ensemble trials $T$, number of rules $R$, optimizer and learning rate, number of epochs, how $k$-shot samples were selected, how bagging was done (feature/instance counts per bag), and the number of LLM API queries incurred. The paper emphasizes that the total number of API calls is modest (their reported runs needed only about 30 API queries) and that the calls can be parallelized; logging them is part of an auditable experimental record. Finally, report averaged metrics and standard deviations over multiple random splits as the paper does, and make your prompt text and parsing prompts available alongside code so exact runs can be regenerated.

Concluding remark
This chapter distilled the practical, paper-grounded recipe for building, validating, and reproducing FeatLLM experiments: ensure clear feature metadata and question-style task phrasing, use the two-step reasoning prompt and fixed-format rule outputs, parse rules via an LLM-to-code pass and exec() the result to generate binary features, train a simple non-negative linear aggregator using cross-entropy, and use bagging plus an ensemble of $T$ trials to scale to many features and improve robustness. The paper’s experiments and ablations provide concrete defaults ($R=10$, $T=20$, temperature $0.5$, Adam $lr=0.01$, $200$ epochs) that are good starting points for implementation and reproducibility.

Limitations, Broader Impacts, and Future Directions

# Dependence on LLM prior knowledge: biases, misinformation, and domain shift
FeatLLM's core idea is to harness the "rich prior knowledge" encoded in large language models (LLMs) as a substitute for scarce labeled data. The framework deliberately leverages that prior knowledge in two places: (1) a Step‑1 reasoning prompt that asks the LLM to state general tendencies or causal relationships between features and the target (so the model can surface commonsense or domain priors), and (2) a Step‑2 prompt that combines those priors with a few serialized examples to derive concrete rule-based features. The empirical results in the paper show this strategy is effective in few‑shot settings — FeatLLM often outperforms other few‑shot baselines and generates many features that have non‑trivial AUROC with respect to the target (the paper reports that most generated rule features are informative before tuning, and that tuning further filters irrelevant rules).

This reliance on LLM priors is a double‑edged sword. When the LLM's prior knowledge is accurate and appropriately aligned with the target domain, it provides the missing inductive bias that few labels cannot supply. The paper demonstrates this advantage by applying FeatLLM even to datasets that were unlikely to be memorized by the LLM (newly added UCI datasets and synthetic datasets), where the LLM still used commonsense or domain reasoning to produce useful rules. At the same time, the authors explicitly acknowledge that priors can dominate and that analyses of societal biases and misinformation in LLMs are an important research direction (see the paper's Broader Impact statement).

Two concrete failure modes are visible or discussed in the paper. First, the LLM can articulate incorrect causal relationships or overgeneralize priors, producing rules that reflect bias or misinformation rather than the true data-generating process. Second, under domain shift — when the target population differs substantially from the population reflected in the LLM's training data — the LLM's priors may be misleading. The authors empirically illustrate related fragility: while FeatLLM is relatively robust to randomly added irrelevant columns (it degrades less than many baselines in the spurious‑correlation experiment), they also report cases (in the Appendix) where FeatLLM can learn spurious correlations or misunderstand causal relationships if the appended columns are closely related in misleading ways.

Practical implication: any deployment of FeatLLM must treat the LLM's priors as a strong, but fallible, source of knowledge. The framework's advantage in low‑shot settings arises precisely because the LLM injects knowledge beyond the few labeled points; when that knowledge is incorrect, the model's outputs will reflect those errors. The paper frames investigation of these biases and their societal impacts as important future work and motivates human oversight in high‑stakes settings (discussed further below).

# Safety and security risks in code generation/execution; sandboxing and audits
A distinctive implementation detail of FeatLLM is that rule text produced by the LLM is converted into executable parsing code by the LLM itself, and that generated code is run to transform raw tabular inputs into the binary rule features. Concretely, the paper states that the generated parsing code is executed using Python's exec() function to perform data conversion. The authors also document that the LLM's textual outputs can be syntactically noisy (for example, using "greater than" instead of the symbol ">"), which motivates automated parsing.

Because FeatLLM executes code that is generated (and therefore indirectly specified) by an LLM, this step introduces important safety and security considerations. The paper does not attempt a systematic mitigation or evaluation of runtime safety in the code-execution step; it focuses on the proof of concept and empirical performance gains. As a result, code generation and execution constitute a limitation in the current work: malformed or maliciously formed code can cause runtime failures, silent data corruption, or other undesirable behaviors in a production system if not handled carefully.

For practitioners and researchers building on FeatLLM, the paper's pipeline makes clear that code-execution risk is an area that needs attention. The authors do not provide production‑grade countermeasures in this work, so adopting safeguards (for example, executing generated parsing code only after validation, limiting the set of permissible operations, or running the code in a restricted environment) is a necessary practical step before deployment. The paper flags the parsing difficulty and uses the LLM to normalize rules into code as a pragmatic choice, but it leaves systematic safety engineering — such as sandboxing, audits, or formal parsing approaches — as an open operational requirement to make the method production‑ready.

# Scaling to larger labeled datasets and richer feature types beyond Boolean rules
FeatLLM is explicitly designed and evaluated for few‑shot/tabular settings. The authors state in the conclusion that the framework "is designed to focus on low‑shot learning regime only" and identify as future work the challenge of expanding to larger numbers of labeled samples and "exploring various feature types beyond rules." Several aspects of the paper illuminate why scaling is non‑trivial.

First, the learning dynamics change as sample size grows. In the few‑shot regime, the LLM's priors and the hand‑designed rule primitives provide most of the inductive bias; the downstream linear model only needs to learn the relative importance of rule features. The paper's ablation study shows that as the number of shots increases, weight tuning gains importance: when more data are available, estimating and fine‑tuning the weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ that combine rule features becomes both feasible and valuable. Thus, simply increasing data without rethinking representation capacity, regularization, or model class may not be the most effective path.

Second, FeatLLM's current feature representation is rule‑based and binary: each generated rule yields a 0/1 feature indicating whether a sample satisfies a rule for a class. Binary rule features are highly interpretable and effective in low‑shot settings, but with abundant labels more expressive or higher‑capacity features (continuous transforms, interaction terms, parametrized feature templates, or learned embeddings) may be necessary to capture subtler patterns. The authors list "various feature types beyond rules" as explicit future work; designing these richer primitives will require (a) redefining how an LLM should be prompted to produce structured numeric transformations rather than Boolean tests, and (b) changing the downstream model and learning objectives to make proper use of those richer features.

Third, the engineering tradeoffs around prompt size and computational cost matter as the problem scales. The paper addresses the token‑count limitation by combining feature bagging with ensembling: a single LLM prompt extracts only a subset of features, and repeated trials with different bags produce an ensemble of rule sets. This strategy mitigates context‑window limits and produces robustness, but it also multiplies the number of LLM queries. In the experiments the authors set the ensemble size and rules per class to concrete values (20 ensembles, 10 rules per class) and report that, in their setup, the training-stage LLM usage was modest (for example, they note the method required only a limited number of API queries in their timing comparison). Moving to larger datasets may require re‑engineering the bagging/ensemble schedule, optimizing the number of prompts, or introducing hierarchical generation schemes so that the method remains computationally practical.

# Extending to regression/ranking and continuous outputs
The FeatLLM paper frames and evaluates the method in a classification context: prompts ask yes/no or categorical questions, rules are produced for each class, and the downstream logit-based combination produces class probabilities (the paper formulates logits as $\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i$ followed by a softmax over classes). The methodology and experiments therefore target discrete outputs.

Extending the same idea to regression, ranking, or other continuous outputs is not covered in the paper and is an open direction. Conceptually, the shift from discrete to continuous targets raises two main issues. First, per‑class rule generation is no longer a natural decomposition: classification lends itself to "rules that indicate class membership," whereas regression targets require features that correlate with varying magnitudes of the target. Second, the loss function and downstream model would need to be changed (for example, replacing cross‑entropy with mean squared error or a ranking loss), and the representation of generated features may need to move beyond binary indicators to continuous or parametric transformations that better express dose–response relationships.

The authors do not implement such extensions here, but they flag the broader ambition of richer feature types and larger‑sample regimes. These are the natural stepping stones toward adapting the FeatLLM idea to regression and ranking: (a) ask the LLM to propose numeric transformations or parametric rule‑scores rather than purely Boolean checks, (b) allow generated features to be real‑valued, and (c) fit appropriate loss functions and models. The paper leaves the technical realization of these ideas to future work.

# Integration with AutoML, TabPFN-like priors, and interpretability frameworks
FeatLLM sits in a landscape of tabular learning approaches that combine priors, synthetic data pretraining, and automated model selection. The paper compares FeatLLM against several such baselines (including TabPFN and other conventional or LLM‑based methods) and documents that FeatLLM often achieves superior few‑shot performance while keeping inference cheap (because inference does not require calling the LLM per sample). The authors also note that ideas like bagging and ensembling are conceptually adaptable to other LLM‑based approaches, but doing so would often increase per‑sample inference cost and thus may be impractical.

There are several promising integration points implied by the paper. One is to treat FeatLLM as a feature‑generation module within a larger AutoML or model‑selection pipeline: once the LLM produces a bank of candidate features (rules or richer transforms), an AutoML system could search over model classes and regularization strategies that best exploit those features. Another is to combine FeatLLM's rule features with learned priors from approaches like TabPFN: for example, a synthetic‑data pretrainer could provide a Bayesian prior over how combinations of features map to labels, while FeatLLM supplies human‑readable and data‑driven candidate features; integrating both signals could improve robustness across regimes. Finally, FeatLLM's rule outputs are inherently amenable to interpretability frameworks: the rules themselves are interpretable, and the learned non‑negative weights on rule features provide a simple importance ordering. The authors explicitly point to interpretability as a target for future work.

The paper does not attempt these integrations, but its experimental findings — that rule features are both informative and filterable by tuning, and that ensemble bagging reduces prompt‑size issues — suggest FeatLLM could be combined with AutoML, prior‑based pretraining, or interpretability toolsets to build more automated, reliable workflows.

# Human-in-the-loop validation for high-stakes domains (healthcare/finance)
The authors highlight that FeatLLM is particularly relevant in domains where labeled data is costly to obtain — notably healthcare and finance. They also emphasize the need for further analysis of "societal biases and misinformation embedded in the prior knowledge of LLMs" since those priors can directly shape predictions and potentially dominate limited labeled data.

Because LLM‑derived rules can be interpretable (they are textual conditions) and the downstream model is simple (a non‑negative linear combination of rule indicators), FeatLLM is well suited to human‑in‑the‑loop workflows: domain experts can inspect, validate, and edit candidate rules before they are used for downstream training or inference. The paper does not experimentally study human vetting, but it frames such oversight as a natural and important safeguard, especially in high‑stakes applications. For deployment in regulated or safety‑critical contexts, the authors' Broader Impact discussion implies that a human reviewer should evaluate generated rules for correctness, fairness, and plausibility; additional checks might include testing rule behavior on held‑out or synthetic edge cases and monitoring feature importance learned by $\mathbf{w}_k$ during tuning.

In short, FeatLLM's human‑readable artifacts (rules and learned weights) make it practical to add expert review steps; the paper positions this as a prudent strategy rather than reporting it as an implemented part of the experiments.

# Future work highlighted by authors: more feature types, large-sample regimes, deeper interpretability
The paper's conclusion summarizes the principal directions the authors consider important next steps: expanding FeatLLM to (1) craft new features for datasets with larger numbers of samples, (2) explore various feature types beyond Boolean rules, and (3) bring deeper interpretability to the framework.

Expanding to larger sample sizes will change the balance between LLM priors and empirical evidence: weight estimation and regularization strategies will need to be rethought so the downstream model properly integrates many labeled examples while retaining useful prior structure. Exploring richer feature types means moving beyond yes/no checks to incorporate numeric transforms, parametric rule‑scores, interaction functions, or other structured features that capture continuous and higher‑order patterns. Deepening interpretability involves both algorithmic and empirical work: algorithmically, by designing generation prompts and downstream architectures that produce stable, auditable features; empirically, by measuring rule stability across ensembles, analyzing how learned weights reflect rule quality, and auditing generated rules for biases.

The paper provides empirical motivations and preliminary technical scaffolding for each of these directions (for example, ablation studies that show the importance of reasoning prompts and feature descriptions in few‑shot settings, and experiments demonstrating that ensemble/bagging mitigates prompt length limitations). Turning these directions into concrete, production‑ready methods remains an open research program that the authors explicitly invite.

# Concluding perspective
FeatLLM demonstrates that LLMs can act as powerful feature engineers in low‑shot tabular learning: by extracting textual rules, parsing them into features, and training a lightweight downstream model, the framework attains strong few‑shot performance while keeping inference cheap and interpretable. At the same time, the paper is candid about limitations and open questions. The approach depends critically on the correctness and appropriateness of LLM priors, it introduces a code‑generation and execution step whose safety implications are not resolved in this work, and it is intentionally scoped to classification in the few‑shot regime with Boolean rules as the primary feature type. The authors identify extending to richer feature types, larger sample regimes, and deeper interpretability — as well as more systematic analysis of bias and societal impact — as the next frontiers. For students and researchers, FeatLLM offers a compelling proof of concept and a clear set of technical and operational challenges to tackle before similar systems can be relied on in safety‑critical or large‑scale applications.
