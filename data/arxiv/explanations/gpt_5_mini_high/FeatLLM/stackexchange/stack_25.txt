\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### How did you tune ensemble size and rules-per-class without inducing selection bias?
Question:
You suggest that performance saturates around $T=20$ ensembles and that $R=10$ rules per class is a reasonable trade-off. Were these settings fixed a priori for all datasets, or tuned per dataset or per shot count? If tuned, how did you avoid overfitting hyperparameters to test performance in the few-shot setting?
Answer:
Short answer: we did not tune $T$ (ensemble size) or $R$ (rules per class) on each test set or per-shot; both were chosen as global, fixed settings ($T=20$, $R=10$) based on a prior sensitivity analysis reported in the paper (Implementation details, Sec. “Hyper-parameter analysis”, and the Appendix). Those global choices were used for all datasets and shot counts in the main experiments, so no test-set hyperparameter fitting was performed.

Why that choice is defensible: the paper reports a hyper-parameter sweep showing the marginal benefit of more ensembles quickly dimines and “begin[s] to converge (i.e., around $20$)”, while varying the number of rules produced no statistically significant improvements; $R=10$ was picked as a practical trade-off between representational power, prompt length, and overfitting risk in the few-shot regime. In other words, the authors used an aggregated sensitivity study (across settings) to find a conservative operating point rather than per-dataset tuning.

How this avoids selection bias in practice: (1) the same fixed $T$ and $R$ were applied to every dataset and shot level in the reported experiments (so no per-dataset “cherry‑picking”); (2) for any training-time choices that depend on data (e.g., number of epochs for fitting the linear weights) the paper uses $k$-fold cross‑validation, not test labels; and (3) results are averaged over multiple random train/test splits and standard deviations are reported, which reduces the chance that the reported numbers reflect an accident of a particular split or hyperparameter setting. The Appendix also contains the sensitivity/ablation plots that show performance is fairly stable around the chosen values, supporting that the global choice did not overfit the reported test sets.

Intuition behind the numbers: ensembles reduce variance but give diminishing returns when individual LLM-generated rule-sets are correlated, so $T\approx 20$ is enough to average out noise in practice; increasing $R$ raises the feature dimensionality (more capacity) which helps until you hit a few-shot overfitting regime and prompt-length constraints, so $R=10$ is a conservative balance.

If you want to be even more careful in future work, the standard safeguards are to select hyperparameters on a held-out validation set or via nested cross‑validation (never on test labels) or to report results for multiple fixed choices; the paper’s approach—global, pre-specified values validated by sensitivity plots—follows that conservative principle.
