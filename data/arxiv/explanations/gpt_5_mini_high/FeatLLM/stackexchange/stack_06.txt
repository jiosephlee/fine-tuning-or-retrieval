\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### What are the exact bagging settings for instances and features, and how were they chosen?
Question:
You mention using bagging to reduce prompt size and improve robustness by sampling subsets of features and instances per trial. What specific sampling rates did you use (e.g., fraction of features, fraction of training examples) and are these fixed across datasets? How sensitive is performance to these bagging hyperparameters, especially on wide tables with hundreds of columns?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not give a fixed “fraction of features” or “fraction of instances” number in the main text. What it does report and fix across the reported experiments are the ensemble size and the number of rules per class: the authors used $T=20$ ensemble trials and $R=10$ rules per class for all datasets. The paper describes that, within each trial, they use bagging to pick a subset of features and/or a subset of examples to (a) keep the prompt short enough for the LLM and (b) create diverse, weak rule-generators, but the exact sampling fraction (e.g. “pick $30\%$ of columns”) is not explicitly reported in the body — that detail appears to be managed per dataset in the code/appendix.

Why that matters and how to think about it. Bagging here is used for two purposes that pull in opposite directions: reducing prompt length (so you must sample fewer features per trial) and covering predictive features across the ensemble (so you want enough features sampled across trials). The paper’s reported hyperparameter analysis focuses on two knobs they fixed and studied explicitly: the number of ensembles $T$ (performance improves and saturates around $T\!\approx\!20$) and the number of rules per class $R$ (they picked $R=10$ as a good tradeoff between information and overfitting in low-shot). They show empirically that increasing $T$ helps until convergence, and that too many rules inflates feature dimensionality and can hurt few-shot learning.

A simple, useful quantitative view (not new to the paper) is to treat each trial as sampling $s$ features out of $d$ total. The probability that a particular feature is seen at least once across $T$ independent trials is
$$p_{\text{covered}} = 1 - \bigl(1 - \tfrac{s}{d}\bigr)^T.$$
This formula immediately gives design intuition and concrete trade-offs. For example, if $d=200$ and you sample $s=20$ features per trial ($s/d=0.1$) with $T=20$ trials then
$$p_{\text{covered}} = 1 - 0.9^{20} \approx 0.88,$$
so each column has about an $88\%$ chance to appear in at least one trial. If you want $95\%$ coverage with $s/d=0.1$ you would need
$$T \ge \frac{\ln(1-0.95)}{\ln(1-0.1)} \approx 29$$
trials. Equivalently, increasing $s$ (more features per trial) reduces the number of trials needed, but increases prompt length.

Practical consequences / sensitivity (what the paper implies and what to do):
- The paper demonstrates robustness to many noisy columns (their spurious-column experiment), which indicates that FeatLLM tolerates noisy feature sampling so long as the ensemble and rule-filtering (weight tuning) are used. In that experiment rules derived from noisy columns were rare ($1\text{--}2\%$).
- The most sensitive hyperparameters in their experiments were $T$ (ensembles) and $R$ (rules per class). They fixed these across datasets and found $T\!=\!20$, $R\!=\!10$ to work well. The paper does not present a direct ablation over the per-trial feature/instance sampling fraction; instead the guidance is pragmatic: limit what's put into each prompt so the prompt fits the LLM and rely on the ensemble to cover the rest.
- For very wide tables (hundreds of columns) the natural strategy is to cap the absolute number of columns per trial (e.g., tens of columns) rather than use a fixed fraction. Then use the coverage formula above to choose $T$ that gives acceptable chance of seeing any given column. If predictive features are sparse, you must either increase $s$ or increase $T$ to avoid missing them.

Practical recipe (aligned with the paper’s design principles):
1. Hold $R=10$ and start with $T\approx 20$ (these are the paper’s defaults).
2. Choose $s$ so the serialized prompt (feature descriptions + $k$-shot examples + reasoning instructions) fits comfortably in the LLM input window; a practical cap used in similar workflows is on the order of $20\text{--}50$ columns per trial for GPT-3.5-sized prompts, but the exact cap depends on how verbose your feature descriptions and few-shot examples are.
3. If $d$ is large, use the coverage formula to check whether $T$ with your chosen $s$ gives acceptable coverage; increase $T$ if coverage is too low or increase $s$ if you can afford longer prompts.
4. If you have many labeled examples (beyond the $k$ few-shot regime), you can also bag instances per trial to keep each prompt short—again the paper treats instance bagging as an optional lever for larger training sets, while their few-shot experiments often rely on the $k$-shot subsample itself.

If you need the exact sampling code or the precise fractions they used per dataset, the paper points to the released code (the GitHub link in the paper) and to Appendix/hyperparameter figures for more implementation-level details; checking that repository/appendix is the fastest way to see the authors’ concrete per-dataset choices.
