\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### How are overlapping or conflicting rules across classes handled at inference?
Question:
Because rules are class-specific, a sample may satisfy rules for multiple classes simultaneously. The current scheme scores each class independently and relies on softmax normalization. Did you experiment with shared or global rules, mutual-exclusion constraints, or penalty terms to resolve conflicts? How common are conflicts in your datasets, and do they degrade accuracy?
Answer:
Short answer: we did not add explicit shared/global rules or mutual‑exclusion constraints. Conflicts are handled implicitly by the per‑class scoring, the non‑negative weight projection, and the softmax normalization, and in practice tuning plus ensembling/bagging keeps conflicting or spurious rules from hurting performance in the datasets we tried.

More detail and intuition: FeatLLM generates a separate binary feature vector for each class (the paper denotes the binary feature for sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ and class $k$ as $\mathbf{z}_k^i\in\{0,1\}^R$) and then scores each class independently with a simple linear model. Concretely we compute
$$
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,\qquad
\mathbf{p}^i=\text{Softmax}\big([\text{logit}_1^i,\dots,\text{logit}_c^i]\big),
$$
so each rule acts like a positive “vote” for its class and the softmax makes the classes compete at the end. The projection $\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)$ forces weights into a non‑negative subspace so generated rules cannot directly suppress a class’s score — they only provide positive evidence — and the downstream training learns which rules are important (large $\mathbf{w}_k$) versus which are noise (small $\mathbf{w}_k$).

What we tried instead of explicit constraints: we rely on weight tuning and on ensemble/bagging. Tuning learns to downweight irrelevant or misleading rules (the paper shows that after tuning many low‑utility rules end up with very small weights and are effectively filtered out), and the ensemble of multiple LLM trials (with randomized prompts/bagging) makes the system robust: occasional wrong rules from one trial are outweighed by correct rules from others. Ablations in the paper show removing tuning (\textsf{-Tuning}) or removing the ensemble (\textsf{-Ensemble}) degrades performance, which supports this implicit conflict‑resolution strategy.

How common are conflicts and do they hurt accuracy? Empirically conflicts/spurious rules were rare in our experiments: the paper reports that the “majority” of generated rules are informative (measured by AUROC vs. the target) and, in the spurious‑column test, rules derived from injected noisy columns amounted to only about 1–2% of the total. Overall FeatLLM showed small degradation under added noisy features compared with baselines. That said, the paper also notes failure modes: if closely related but misleading columns are attached, the LLM can generate spurious but plausible rules and those can harm performance (see the Appendix), so conflicts can matter in adversarially correlated settings.

Avenues if you want stricter conflict handling: the paper did not experiment with global/shared rules, explicit mutual‑exclusion constraints, or penalty terms, but these are natural extensions. Options include learning shared rule representations across classes, allowing negative weights (to model inhibitory rules), adding pairwise penalties for jointly active rules across classes, or moving to structured inference (e.g., constrained optimization or CRF‑style global normalization). These would likely require more labeled data or more sophisticated optimization, which is why the current paper prioritizes a simple, data‑efficient approach.

In short: FeatLLM resolves overlaps mainly by learning per‑rule positive weights and using softmax competition plus ensembling; explicit mutual‑exclusion mechanisms were not used in the paper, conflicts were uncommon in our benchmarks, and when they appeared the tuning/ensemble pipeline usually contained their impact.
