\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### How is $k$-fold cross-validation defined with very small $k$-shot training sets?
Question:
The paper uses $k$-fold cross-validation to select training epochs of the linear model, ensuring each fold has at least one example per class. When total labeled shots are as small as 4 or 8, how do you split folds while maintaining class coverage? What exact $k$ values were used per dataset and shot level, and how do you avoid high variance or leakage in such tiny splits?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper uses very small values of $k$ (either $k=2$ or $k=4$) for the $k$-fold cross-validation used to select the number of training epochs, and it enforces per-class coverage when forming the folds. The authors do not publish a per-dataset table of which exact $k$ was used for every dataset/shot combination in the main text; instead they state the rule that $k$ is chosen as $2$ or $4$ so that every training split contains at least one example of each class. They also mitigate variance by repeating experiments and by the ensemble/bagging procedure.

Explanation and practical details grounded in the paper
The paper explicitly says: “We employ $k$-fold cross-validation for optimal epoch selection… The value of $k$ is set either as $2$ or $4$, guaranteeing that the training set includes at least one example of each class.” In practice that means the authors use very small $k$ so the per-fold training set is not excessively tiny, and they enforce a class-coverage constraint when they build the folds (this is most naturally implemented via stratified or constrainted splitting / resampling until the condition is satisfied).

A few concrete points to make the mechanics clear. If you have $s$ labeled shots and choose $k$ folds then each fold has about $s/k$ examples and the training portion in each fold has about $s\cdot (k-1)/k$ examples. For small $s$ you therefore must choose $k$ small enough that the training portion can plausibly contain at least one instance of every class. For example, for binary problems:
- with $s=4$ and $k=2$ each fold is $\approx 2$ samples and the training portion is $2$ samples; you must ensure the two training samples together contain at least one positive and one negative example (the authors enforce this).
- with $s=8$ and $k=4$ each fold is $\approx 2$ samples and the training portion is $\approx 6$ samples, which is usually sufficient to include both classes if the original $s$ had both classes represented.

How leakage and high variance are handled (as described by the paper)
The paper keeps the test set strictly held-out: the $k$-shot examples used to generate features and to train the linear model come from the training side only. Cross-validation is used only inside that small training set to select the number of epochs for the linear model (not to peek at the test set), so there is no leakage to the evaluation set.

To cope with the high variance inherent to such tiny training sets the authors combine several practical measures: they repeat experiments with different random draws of the $k$-shot training sets (they report mean and standard deviation across three random seeds), they build an ensemble of rule-sets (they run the full LLM rule generation + linear fitting pipeline $T=20$ times with bagging and temperature randomness), and they keep the downstream model simple (a constrained linear model with non-negative weights and only epoch selection via CV). Those steps reduce sensitivity to any single random tiny split and stabilize reported metrics.

What the paper does and does not give you
The paper gives the policy (“use $k=2$ or $k=4$, enforce at least one example of each class in the training fold”) and reports the repeated/ensembled evaluation protocol, but it does not publish a per-dataset table mapping every shot level to the exact $k$ used in each case in the main text. If you need the exact split choices for replication, the code repository (anonymized link in the paper) or the appendix (if included) is the place to check; otherwise a practical and faithful reproduction is to follow their rule: choose $k\in\{2,4\}$ so that each fold’s training portion contains at least one example of every class, resample splits until that condition holds, and average results across multiple random draws and the ensemble.

If you want a reproducible recipe: sample your $s$ shots from the training pool, form stratified/constrainted $k$-folds with $k=2$ (for the smallest $s$ such as $4$) or $k=4$ (for larger small $s$ such as $8$), run CV inside that training set only to pick epochs, repeat the whole pipeline several times and use the ensemble/bagging described in the paper to reduce variance. This matches the authors’ stated approach and avoids test leakage while preserving per-class coverage.
