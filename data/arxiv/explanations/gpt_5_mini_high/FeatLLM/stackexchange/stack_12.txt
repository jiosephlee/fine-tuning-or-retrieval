\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### Do you filter out impossible thresholds or unit-mismatch errors suggested by the LLM?
Question:
The LLM may propose thresholds that do not match data scales or units (e.g., 'age > 200' or 'cholesterol < 1'). Do you provide summary statistics of each feature to the LLM to ground thresholds, or post-filter rules that are almost always false or true on the training data? If a rule fires for 0% of training examples, is it pruned before training the linear model?
Answer:
Short answer: The paper does not do an explicit pre-filter step that removes threshold rules based on numeric summary statistics or on $0\%$ coverage before training. Instead, FeatLLM tries to prevent type/scale mistakes up front by giving the LLM feature types (and optionally definitions) plus a few serialized example values in the prompt so the LLM can “see” the scale, and then relies on downstream tuning + ensembling to suppress or remove bad rules after they are generated.

Concretely, the Prompt Design phase supplies each feature’s type and (optionally) its definition and few-shot example values in the “basic information” block, and the reasoning instruction explicitly tells the LLM to refer to feature types when writing rules. That is the paper’s primary mechanism for reducing unit/type mismatches and wildly implausible thresholds (e.g., telling the LLM “age is an integer in years” and showing example ages helps the LLM propose thresholds in the right ballpark).

After the LLM outputs rules they are parsed into binary features and used by a very simple downstream model. The paper uses a non-negative linear scoring per class
$$
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,\qquad
\mathbf{p}^i=\text{Softmax}([\text{logit}_1^i,\dots,\text{logit}_c^i]),
$$
which is trained (cross-entropy) on the few-shot examples. The authors then explicitly filter features post-hoc by learned weight: empirically they inspected the weight histogram and used a threshold (they report using $0.1$) to mark rules as unimportant and prune them (“After tuning” in their feature-quality table). They also use bagging/ensemble across multiple rule-generation trials so that occasional bad rules from single trials are down-weighted in the ensemble.

Two practical implications relevant to your examples: a rule that never fires on the training set produces an all-zero binary column; such a column does not affect the training loss (its gradient is zero) and so its weight is essentially unconstrained by data and it’s inert for that training run. It will either remain near its initialization or be removed later by the post-training weight thresholding; the paper does not remove these zero-coverage rules before fitting. Conversely, rules that fire almost always (constant columns) carry little discriminative signal and will typically get small weights during tuning and then be pruned by the same post-filter.

If you need stricter guarantees against impossible thresholds (e.g., to absolutely prevent thresholds outside observed ranges), the paper does not implement that, but the natural extensions are straightforward: include numeric summary statistics (min/max/quantiles) in the prompt so the LLM is explicitly anchored, or post-validate parsed numeric thresholds against dataset min/max and automatically clamp or discard rules whose thresholds are impossible on the training set. Those ideas are suggested improvements rather than parts of FeatLLM as described in the paper.
