\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### Is the projection $\max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)$ implemented per-step and how are gradients handled at 0?
Question:
For the linear model, the paper defines $\text{logit}_k = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k$ to enforce non-negativity. Is this realized by a ReLU on the weights each forward pass, a parametrization like $\mathbf{w}_k = \exp(\theta_k)$, or a constraint in the optimizer? If you use a pointwise $\max(\cdot,0)$, how do you handle the non-differentiability at 0 and do you observe optimization getting stuck with many weights at exactly 0?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper writes the model as
$$\text{logit}_k = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k$$
and says it “projects these weights into a positive subspace,” but it does not state the precise implementation. In practice there are three common ways to realize that equation, each with different gradient behavior at $0$ — the paper reports using Adam to train the linear model but does not specify which of these they used. Below I explain the options, their gradient behaviour at $0$, and practical recommendations.

If you literally implement the paper’s formula with a per‑forward ReLU/clamp on the weights (keep a free parameter vector $\mathbf{w}$ and in forward do $\mathbf{w}_{+}=\max(\mathbf{w},0)$), automatic differentiation uses the ReLU subgradient: for a single weight $w_j$
$$\frac{\partial \text{logit}}{\partial w_j} = \mathbf{1}[w_j>0]\; z_j,$$
(and the value at exactly $0$ is a subgradient chosen by the framework, typically $0$). Intuition: only weights that are strictly positive and only rule‑features that are active ($z_j=1$) receive nonzero gradient. Consequence: if a weight is negative (or ends up exactly zero with zero subgradient) it receives no gradient signal and, absent other terms (e.g. weight decay or explicit projection), it will typically stay nonpositive — so you can get “dead” parameters that never recover unless they were initialized positive or nudged by another mechanism.

An alternative is a smooth parametrization that guarantees positivity, for example
$$\mathbf{w}=\text{softplus}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\quad\text{or}\quad \mathbf{w}=\exp(\boldsymbol{\theta}).$$
With softplus ($\text{softplus}(x)=\log(1+e^x)$) the mapping is smooth and $\partial \mathbf{w}/\partial\theta$ is the sigmoid, never zero. That means even when $\mathbf{w}$ is very small the underlying parameter $\theta$ still receives a gradient and can move across the “zero” boundary — you avoid the dead‑weight problem. Exponential also enforces strict positivity and is differentiable, but gradients scale with $\mathbf{w}$ and can be numerically large/small; softplus is usually the gentlest, most stable choice.

A third option is projected optimization: after each unconstrained optimizer step you clip the parameters to nonnegative (set negative entries to $0$). This enforces strict nonnegativity, and the objective you optimize is effectively with a constraint. However, the gradient you computed used the clamped forward pass (so negative parameters often produced zero gradient), and after projection those entries sit at $0$ with a zero subgradient — so they can also remain stuck unless you add other forces (random initialization on the positive side, explicit exploration, proximal terms, or occasional small additive noise).

Which approach is advisable? If you want a faithful implementation of the paper’s equation and you care about stable training and avoiding dead weights, use a smooth positive parametrization (softplus is a common default). If you want sparse/sparse‑interpretable weights with many exact zeros, clamp/ReLU on weights gives that behaviour but risks parameters getting stuck at zero; projected updates can also produce strict zeros but have the same dead‑zone issue. The non‑differentiability at $0$ itself is not a showstopper — frameworks use subgradients — but it is the zero subgradient that causes the “stuck” phenomenon, not the mathematical nondifferentiability per se.

Finally, note what the paper reports: they define the positive projection in the equations and say they “project these weights into a positive subspace,” and in Implementation they only state using Adam with lr $0.01$ for $200$ epochs (no further detail). They do not report training failures due to many weights being stuck exactly at zero; they also filter out rules with learned weights below a threshold ($0.1$) when assessing feature quality, which suggests in their runs many useful weights learned nonnegligible positive values. If you reproduce this you can start with the simple clamp-on-forward implementation (it matches the paper text) but if you observe dead weights in your few‑shot regime, switch to $\text{softplus}(\theta)$ or initialize more weights positive to avoid that pathology.
