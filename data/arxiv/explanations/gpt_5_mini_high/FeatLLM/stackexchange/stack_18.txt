\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### Why restrict to non-negative weights instead of allowing negative evidence via learned negatives?
Question:
Restricting $\mathbf{w}_k \ge 0$ means that satisfying a rule can only increase the logit for class $k$. Did you consider letting the linear model learn negative contributions for some rules (e.g., 'absence of symptom X reduces probability of disease Y')? Would that harm interpretability, or did you empirically find it reduces robustness in few-shot settings?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper enforces non‑negative weights (they use the projection/logit formula
$\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i$) to keep each LLM‑generated rule a simple, positive piece of evidence for its class. That choice improves interpretability and acts as a conservative prior that helps robustness in very low‑shot regimes. The authors do not report a direct comparison to unconstrained/negative weights, so the decision is motivated by semantics and inductive‑bias considerations rather than an explicit ablation in the text.

Why non‑negativity helps (intuitively and technically). The LLM in FeatLLM is asked to produce class‑specific rules — each rule is phrased as a condition that indicates class $k$. If the linear model is constrained to non‑negative weights then each learned weight is directly interpretable as “how much satisfying this rule increases evidence for class $k$.” Allowing negative weights breaks that simple mapping: a rule that was authored to be positive evidence for class $k$ could end up being learned as negative evidence (i.e., when it fires it lowers the class logit). That creates a semantic mismatch between generated rule text and the learned model, which reduces interpretability for users who read rules and expect them to mean what they say.

From a robustness / few‑shot perspective, non‑negativity is also a useful regularizer. In few‑shot settings the learned weights are noisy; if you allow unconstrained negative weights then a spurious co‑occurrence in the tiny training set can produce a large negative weight and severely hurt predictions (a single spurious rule firing could penalize a correct class). Constraining weights to be non‑negative imposes a one‑sided monotonic prior — rules can add evidence but they can’t actively “punish” a class — which reduces variance and the chance that an overfitted negative coefficient flips predictions on rare patterns. This design aligns with what the paper emphasizes: the framework relies heavily on LLM prior knowledge (rules) in low‑shot regimes, and it is sensible to keep the downstream model conservative.

What you lose and alternatives. Allowing negative weights makes the model more flexible and could capture genuine inhibitory signals (e.g., “absence of symptom X strongly reduces disease Y probability”), but that flexibility costs interpretability and increases overfitting risk when data are scarce. If you really expect negative evidence to be important, a cleaner approach consistent with FeatLLM’s pipeline is to have the LLM generate explicit negative/evidence‑against rules (or to generate rules for the other classes). That keeps the semantics of each rule consistent while still representing inhibitory information. Other options are to train with unconstrained weights but add strong regularization (L1/L2, early stopping) or to let the LLM label each rule’s polarity and use that as a signed feature — but those are not evaluated in the paper.

Empirical note and suggested experiments. The paper does not include a direct experiment comparing the current non‑negative formulation to an unconstrained (signed) linear model, so we can’t point to its measured effect there. If you want to test this empirically, run three controlled variants on the same few‑shot splits: (A) the paper’s version $\text{logit}_k=\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot\mathbf{z}_k$, (B) unconstrained linear weights $\text{logit}_k=\mathbf{w}_k\cdot\mathbf{z}_k$, and (C) unconstrained but with strong regularization. Track AUC, the distribution and stability of learned weight signs across seeds, and inspect whether negative weights mostly correspond to rules that are semantically “anti‑class” (maybe they should have been generated for another class). That will reveal whether negative weights actually capture meaningful inhibitory effects or just amplify noise in the low‑shot regime.

Takeaway. Non‑negativity is primarily a design/interpretability and low‑shot robustness prior: it keeps rules semantically aligned with their textual meaning and prevents fragile negative signals from dominating decisions when labeled data are scarce. If you have more labeled data or explicit negative evidence from the LLM, relaxing the constraint and/or making rule polarity explicit are reasonable next steps to explore.
