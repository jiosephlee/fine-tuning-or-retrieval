\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### How is per-rule AUROC computed in the 'Quality of features' analysis for multiclass tasks?
Question:
The paper reports the ratio of 'useful features' with AUROC greater than 0.5, before and after tuning. For multiclass datasets, how is per-rule AUROC defined? Is it one-vs-rest per class, macro-averaged across classes, or another scheme? Also, why is a learned-weight threshold of 0.1 used for pruning, and how sensitive are conclusions to that choice?
Answer:
Short answer: for multiclass tasks each generated rule is treated as a binary predictor for the class the rule was written for (one‑vs‑rest), so the per‑rule AUROC is computed between the binary rule activation and the indicator $1(y = k)$ for that rule’s target class. The paper then reports the fraction of rules whose AUROC exceeds $0.5$ (before tuning). After tuning, they prune rules whose learned non‑negative weight is below $0.1$ (the $0.1$ cutoff was chosen by inspecting the overall histogram of learned weights), and report the retained/“useful” fraction after that pruning.

Why this is the natural choice (technical detail + intuition). In the method each rule set is generated for a specific class $k$ and produces binary features $z_k^i \in \{0,1\}$ (Section “Inferring class likelihood via rules”). So for a particular rule $r$ for class $k$ you compute
$AUROC_{k,r} = AUROC\left(\{z_{k,r}^i\}_{i=1}^N, \{1[y^i=k]\}_{i=1}^N\right)$,
i.e. the area under the ROC curve for predicting whether a sample belongs to class $k$ using that single binary feature. $AUROC > 0.5$ means the rule selects positives more often than negatives (better than random), which is why the paper uses $0.5$ as the usefulness threshold. The paper then aggregates across all rules (and across classes) to get the dataset‑level ratio of useful features.

What the pruning threshold $0.1$ means and why it was used. The downstream linear model learns class‑specific weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ and uses the positive part in logits (Eq. (1)): $\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot\mathbf{z}_k^i$. A small learned weight for a rule means that the rule contributes almost nothing to the class logit on the labeled data, so it is effectively uninformative. The authors say they set the pruning threshold to $0.1$ “taking into consideration the overall histogram of weights” — i.e., they inspected the distribution of learned (nonnegative) weights and picked $0.1$ as a simple cut separating near‑zero/noise weights from clearly informative weights. This is a heuristic, not a statistically derived constant.

How sensitive are the conclusions to that choice? The paper does not publish a formal sensitivity sweep of the $0.1$ cutoff. In general you should expect three regimes: if the weight distribution shows a clear gap between a mass near zero and a separate mass of larger weights, any cutoff inside that gap (e.g. $0.05$–$0.2$) will produce similar retained fractions and the conclusions will be robust; if the weight distribution is continuous with no gap, the retained fraction (and hence the reported “after tuning” number) will vary smoothly with the threshold and conclusions may depend on the exact cutoff. A practical way to check stability is to (a) plot the histogram of learned weights, (b) sweep the threshold (e.g. $0.01$, $0.05$, $0.1$, $0.2$) and report retained‑feature ratio and downstream accuracy as functions of the threshold. If you want a less arbitrary rule you can also (i) use cross‑validated thresholding, (ii) keep the top-$k$ weights per class, or (iii) encourage sparsity via $L_1$ regularization so the model itself pushes many weights to (near) $0$ and reduces dependence on a hand‑picked cutoff.

If you want to reproduce exactly what the paper most likely did: for every generated rule $r$ associated with class $k$ compute $AUROC_{k,r}$ as above, get the “Before tuning” fraction = $\dfrac{\#\ \text{rules with } AUROC_{k,r} > 0.5}{\text{total }\#\ \text{rules}}$. Then train the linear model as in Eq. (1), inspect the learned $\mathbf{w}_k$ (apply the positive clamp), and count as kept those rules with weight $\geq 0.1$; the paper’s “After tuning” numbers reflect that pruning (they chose $0.1$ after looking at the overall weight histogram). For complete certainty you can confirm the exact aggregation used by checking the authors’ released code.
