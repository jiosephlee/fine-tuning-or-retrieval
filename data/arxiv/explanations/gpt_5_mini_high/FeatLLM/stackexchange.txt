\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### How do class-specific positive-only weights avoid penalizing wrong classes in multi-class FeatLLM?
Question:
In FeatLLM, each class $k$ has its own binary rule features $\mathbf{z}_k$ and a linear logit $\text{logit}_k = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k$, with a softmax over classes. Since weights are forced non-negative via $\max(\cdot,0)$ and there is no bias term, how does the model express negative evidence for a class? If a sample satisfies several rules from multiple classes, it seems like logits can only go up for those classes. Does this harm separability or calibration, especially when classes share overlapping rules?
Answer:
Short answer: FeatLLM does not need explicit negative weights to represent “negative evidence.” The model uses class-specific positive-only scores and a softmax, so anything that looks like negative evidence for class A is implemented either by (a) positive evidence for one or more competing classes (which reduces A’s softmax probability), or (b) by the training procedure zeroing out (i.e., ignoring) rules for A that are harmful. This is an intentional inductive bias in the paper: rules generated by the LLM are treated as positive indicators for their target class, and competition across classes plus weight tuning (and ensembling) handle overlap and spurious rules.

Why that works (brief math + intuition). The paper uses
$$
\text{logit}_k = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k,\qquad
\mathbf{p}=\mathrm{Softmax}(\{\text{logit}_k\}_k).
$$
Softmax is a relative (competitive) operator: increasing logit of class $j$ decreases the probability of class $k$ for every $k \neq j$. Concretely,
$$
\frac{\partial p_k}{\partial \text{logit}_j} = p_k(\mathbf{1}_{j=k}-p_j),
$$
so a positive boost to some other class $j$ reduces $p_k$. Thus a feature that is a positive indicator for class $B$ automatically acts as “negative evidence” for class $A$ because the probability mass shifts away from $A$.

How the model handles rules that should discourage a class. The LLM produces separate binary features $\mathbf{z}_k$ for each class $k$. If the same pattern appears in multiple classes’ rule-sets those become distinct features (one per class) and the downstream linear weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ are learned per feature/class. During optimization (cross-entropy on the softmax) the model can (and in practice does) reduce the contribution of unhelpful or misleading rules: the pre-projection weights can be driven to values that produce $\max(\cdot,0)$ near zero, effectively turning off that rule for that class. The paper documents this behavior (they show many rules become low-weight after tuning and they threshold to filter out weak rules), and their ablations report that this tuning step materially improves performance.

When overlapping rules hurt separability. It is true that if the feature set contains a rule that is equally (and strongly) a positive signal for two classes, and there are no other discriminative features, the model will be ambiguous — but that is a limitation of the feature set, not a bug of the positive-weight projection. In practice FeatLLM mitigates this in three ways that the paper emphasizes: prompts explicitly ask the LLM to produce class-distinguishing rules (so rules tend to be discriminative), weight tuning can downweight spurious features, and ensemble+bagging reduce the impact of occasional bad rules. Their experiments (ablation and the spurious-correlation test) show these mechanisms give robust separability in the few-shot regimes they study.

A few practical consequences to keep in mind. The positive-only projection is an explicit inductive bias: it enforces that rules act as supportive evidence for classes, not as subtractive signals. That simplifies interpretation and helps avoid fragile “negatively weighted” rules learned from tiny data, but it does reduce expressiveness compared with an unconstrained linear model (which could assign negative weights directly). The paper’s empirical results indicate this bias is helpful for the low-shot tabular problems they target, but if you expect many genuine “anti-class” signals that are not naturally captured as positive evidence for other classes, you might consider alternative architectures (this is outside the paper’s scope).

Takeaway: negative evidence in FeatLLM is handled indirectly — by competition in the softmax (other classes’ positive evidence) and by turning off harmful class-specific rules during training. This is an intentional design choice that the paper shows works well in practice for few-shot tabular tasks.

### Why fix exactly $R=10$ rules per class, and what happens if fewer than 10 meaningful rules exist?
Question:
The paper states a fixed number of rules (e.g., $R=10$) are extracted per class to avoid too few or too many rules. What happens in datasets where the LLM cannot find 10 informative rules per class? Are 'weak' or redundant rules forced in, and do they hurt performance or increase overfitting in low-shot regimes? Did you try adaptive stopping, confidence-based pruning, or a penalty for rule complexity instead of a hard $R$?
Answer:
Short answer: $R=10$ was chosen as a pragmatic hyperparameter (a trade-off between prompt length, model capacity, and overfitting) after a small hyperparameter study; when an LLM cannot produce 10 genuinely informative rules the pipeline does not rely blindly on them — weak or redundant rules tend to be down-weighted or filtered during training and by the ensemble, so they rarely cause catastrophic harm in the low-shot experiments reported. The paper does not implement an explicit adaptive “stop when you have enough rules” mechanism, but it does use post-hoc, weight-based pruning and ensembling which act like a confidence/pruning step in practice.

Why a fixed $R$ and why 10? The authors fixed the number of rules $R$ per class to keep the prompt size and downstream representation predictable. A fixed $R$ gives a constant-size binary feature vector per class (so each trial trains a linear model with exactly $c \times R$ inputs), which simplifies parsing, training, and ensembling. The paper reports a hyperparameter sweep (Section 4, Appendix figures) showing little statistically significant difference for moderate $R$ choices and picks $R=10$ as the best trade-off between providing information and avoiding an explosion of input dimensionality (which increases overfitting risk in few-shot settings) and prompt length (token limits / cost).

What happens when the LLM can’t find 10 meaningful rules? In practice the LLM may output redundant, trivial, or low-quality rules to satisfy the “10 rules” instruction. The paper’s pipeline mitigates the damage from those forced/weak rules in several ways. First, the prompt structure (two-step reasoning that asks the LLM to consider general prior knowledge before looking at examples) reduces the chance of spurious or irrelevant rules. Second, the downstream linear model learns a non-negative weight vector per class and is trained on the few labeled examples: the paper uses

$$
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,\qquad
\mathbf{p}^i=\text{Softmax}([\text{logit}_1^i,\dots,\text{logit}_c^i]),
$$

so rules that are not predictive on the few-shot data acquire small weights. The authors then apply a simple post-hoc cutoff (they used a weight threshold of $0.1$, chosen from the observed weight histogram) to “filter out” rules with negligible influence. Empirically (Table referenced in the paper), most generated rules are already informative ($\text{AUROC} > 0.5$) before tuning, and tuning filters the rest; the ablation that removes tuning (-Tuning) noticeably degrades performance, showing this step matters.

Third, the ensemble+bagging design reduces the harm of bad rules. Each trial trains a small model on $c \times R$ features (not one gigantic model with all rules from all trials), and final prediction averages probabilities across trials. That means a few trials producing weak rules are diluted by others that produce good rules. Their spurious-column experiment found only $\sim 1\text{--}2\%$ of rules came from entirely noisy columns, suggesting the combination of prompt design, tuning, and ensembling keeps spurious/weak rules from dominating.

Does forcing weak rules increase overfitting? Theoretically yes: increasing the number of (noisy) features relative to $k$-shot labels increases overfitting risk. The paper explicitly notes this trade-off and reports that larger $R$ can increase information but also tend to overfit in low-shot regimes; that is why they picked $R=10$ as the practical balance. Because each ensemble member is relatively low-dimensional ($c \times R$) and because learning assigns near-zero weights to irrelevant rules, the empirical effect on performance in their experiments was small.

Did the authors try adaptive stopping, confidence-based pruning, or complexity penalties instead of a hard $R$? The paper does not report implementing an explicit adaptive stopping rule (e.g., “stop when the LLM says there are no more meaningful rules”) or a rule-complexity penalty. What they do implement that functions similarly to confidence/pruning is the learned weight + thresholding step (post-hoc pruning), the positivity constraint on weights (so rules cannot *reduce* class likelihood), bagging to reduce prompt size and diversify rule sets, and ensembling/self-consistency to prefer repeated rules across runs. No explicit $L_1$/$L_2$ regularization or information-criterion penalty for rule complexity is described; epoch selection is done with $k$-fold cross-validation and the linear model is optimized with Adam (learning rate $0.01$). Hyperparameter analyses (Fig. referenced in Appendix) examined different $R$ values and ensemble sizes but did not implement an adaptive stopping mechanism.

If you want more robust / adaptive behavior (possible follow-ups), several low-friction options fit into the paper’s pipeline without touching the LLM weights: ask the LLM to return a confidence score per rule or a “no more rules” token so you can accept variable-length outputs; keep $R$ flexible and pad/ mask features so downstream models accept variable counts; perform a quick per-rule validation on the $k$-shot data (compute $\text{AUROC}$ or mutual information) and prune rules below a threshold before training the combined model; impose sparsity ($L_1$) or group-lasso penalties on the linear weights to select a compact subset automatically; use cross-trial frequency (self-consistency) and accept only rules that appear across multiple high-temperature runs; or penalize rule complexity (number of conditions or parsed token length) when ranking candidate rules. All of these are compatible with the core FeatLLM idea and would likely reduce any remaining harm from forced rules, but they were not part of the experiments in this paper.

In short: $R=10$ is a pragmatic fixed choice backed by a hyperparameter sweep and practical engineering reasons. The pipeline expects the LLM may sometimes produce weak or redundant rules and counters that with reasoning prompts, learned non-negative weights, threshold-based pruning, and ensembling — together these mechanisms minimize the damage of forced rules in low-shot settings. The explicit adaptive/complexity-penalty schemes you suggest are sensible extensions and are natural future work for improving robustness.

### Is the projection $\max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)$ implemented per-step and how are gradients handled at 0?
Question:
For the linear model, the paper defines $\text{logit}_k = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k$ to enforce non-negativity. Is this realized by a ReLU on the weights each forward pass, a parametrization like $\mathbf{w}_k = \exp(\theta_k)$, or a constraint in the optimizer? If you use a pointwise $\max(\cdot,0)$, how do you handle the non-differentiability at 0 and do you observe optimization getting stuck with many weights at exactly 0?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper writes the model as
$$\text{logit}_k = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k$$
and says it “projects these weights into a positive subspace,” but it does not state the precise implementation. In practice there are three common ways to realize that equation, each with different gradient behavior at $0$ — the paper reports using Adam to train the linear model but does not specify which of these they used. Below I explain the options, their gradient behaviour at $0$, and practical recommendations.

If you literally implement the paper’s formula with a per‑forward ReLU/clamp on the weights (keep a free parameter vector $\mathbf{w}$ and in forward do $\mathbf{w}_{+}=\max(\mathbf{w},0)$), automatic differentiation uses the ReLU subgradient: for a single weight $w_j$
$$\frac{\partial \text{logit}}{\partial w_j} = \mathbf{1}[w_j>0]\; z_j,$$
(and the value at exactly $0$ is a subgradient chosen by the framework, typically $0$). Intuition: only weights that are strictly positive and only rule‑features that are active ($z_j=1$) receive nonzero gradient. Consequence: if a weight is negative (or ends up exactly zero with zero subgradient) it receives no gradient signal and, absent other terms (e.g. weight decay or explicit projection), it will typically stay nonpositive — so you can get “dead” parameters that never recover unless they were initialized positive or nudged by another mechanism.

An alternative is a smooth parametrization that guarantees positivity, for example
$$\mathbf{w}=\text{softplus}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\quad\text{or}\quad \mathbf{w}=\exp(\boldsymbol{\theta}).$$
With softplus ($\text{softplus}(x)=\log(1+e^x)$) the mapping is smooth and $\partial \mathbf{w}/\partial\theta$ is the sigmoid, never zero. That means even when $\mathbf{w}$ is very small the underlying parameter $\theta$ still receives a gradient and can move across the “zero” boundary — you avoid the dead‑weight problem. Exponential also enforces strict positivity and is differentiable, but gradients scale with $\mathbf{w}$ and can be numerically large/small; softplus is usually the gentlest, most stable choice.

A third option is projected optimization: after each unconstrained optimizer step you clip the parameters to nonnegative (set negative entries to $0$). This enforces strict nonnegativity, and the objective you optimize is effectively with a constraint. However, the gradient you computed used the clamped forward pass (so negative parameters often produced zero gradient), and after projection those entries sit at $0$ with a zero subgradient — so they can also remain stuck unless you add other forces (random initialization on the positive side, explicit exploration, proximal terms, or occasional small additive noise).

Which approach is advisable? If you want a faithful implementation of the paper’s equation and you care about stable training and avoiding dead weights, use a smooth positive parametrization (softplus is a common default). If you want sparse/sparse‑interpretable weights with many exact zeros, clamp/ReLU on weights gives that behaviour but risks parameters getting stuck at zero; projected updates can also produce strict zeros but have the same dead‑zone issue. The non‑differentiability at $0$ itself is not a showstopper — frameworks use subgradients — but it is the zero subgradient that causes the “stuck” phenomenon, not the mathematical nondifferentiability per se.

Finally, note what the paper reports: they define the positive projection in the equations and say they “project these weights into a positive subspace,” and in Implementation they only state using Adam with lr $0.01$ for $200$ epochs (no further detail). They do not report training failures due to many weights being stuck exactly at zero; they also filter out rules with learned weights below a threshold ($0.1$) when assessing feature quality, which suggests in their runs many useful weights learned nonnegligible positive values. If you reproduce this you can start with the simple clamp-on-forward implementation (it matches the paper text) but if you observe dead weights in your few‑shot regime, switch to $\text{softplus}(\theta)$ or initialize more weights positive to avoid that pathology.

### How are numeric thresholds in LLM-generated rules determined from few-shot examples vs prior knowledge?
Question:
In Step-2, the LLM derives class rules using the few-shot demonstrations and the prior from Step-1. When a rule involves a threshold (e.g., 'age > 60'), does the LLM infer specific cutoff values primarily from general knowledge, from the provided examples, or both? With very small $k$, how do you prevent overfitting to thresholds that exactly separate the few examples? Do you post-validate or adjust thresholds with the downstream model or any data-driven calibration?
Answer:
Short answer: both. FeatLLM’s prompt explicitly asks the LLM to combine its prior/common‑sense knowledge with the few-shot examples when it writes rules, so numeric cutoffs can come from the model’s background knowledge, from the example values, or a mix of the two. The framework does not re‑optimize the numeric cutoff values after parsing them; instead it reduces the impact of brittle, example‑specific cutoffs via ensemble/diversity and a downstream data‑driven weighting stage that learns which rules actually generalize.

Why both: the paper’s prompt design splits rule generation into two steps (Section “Prompt Design for Extracting Rules”). In Step‑1 the LLM is asked to infer tendencies from general knowledge (no examples), and in Step‑2 it uses those priors plus the few examples to produce concrete rules. That means for a feature like age the LLM might propose a clinically or culturally meaningful cutoff (e.g., $65$) from prior knowledge, or it may shift that cutoff to better match the example values if the few shots suggest a different boundary.

How FeatLLM prevents overfitting to tiny $k$ (mechanisms in the paper): the system does not try to refit the numeric threshold itself; it converts each rule into a binary indicator $z_k^i \in \{0,1\}$ and then learns non‑negative weights for those indicators. Concretely the paper uses

$$\text{logit}_k^i = \max(w_k, 0)\cdot z_k^i,\quad p^i = \text{Softmax}([\ldots\text{logits}\ldots])$$

and optimizes $w_k$ on the available labeled shots (Section “Inferring class likelihood via rules”). Because many candidate rules are generated across randomized trials (ensemble + bagging, Section “Ensembling with bagging”), and because the linear model learns weights from the data, rules that only happen to separate the few examples will typically receive small weights and thus have little effect on final predictions. The authors also prune very low‑importance rules after tuning (they report using a weight threshold of $0.1$ to filter rules in the “Quality of features” analysis), use a fixed (moderate) number of rules per class ($10$) to avoid creating many overfitting primitives, and rely on ensemble averaging ($T$ trials) to dilute idiosyncratic single‑run cutoffs.

Limitations and practical implications: because thresholds are parsed literally into binary checks (via the parsing step described in the paper), FeatLLM does not perform an explicit numeric calibration step that re‑searches or smooths cutoff values. The mitigation is indirect: guided prompting (Step‑1 prior), ensemble/bagging diversity, and downstream weight learning. As the authors note, this reduces but does not eliminate the risk of spurious or overly specific rules—especially when irrelevant but correlated columns are present.

If you want stronger, explicit control (not implemented in the paper) you can modify the prompting or pipeline: ask the LLM to report thresholds as rounded/percentile anchors (e.g., “$age > 60$ (approx percentile $80$)”), ask it to emit thresholds with a margin (e.g., “$age > 60 \pm 5$”), or convert numeric rules into parametrized forms (e.g., “$age > T$”) and then fit $T$ on a small validation fold. Those are natural extensions; the paper’s chosen strategy was to keep thresholds as LLM output and rely on ensemble + learned weights to calibrate their influence.

### How is LLM-to-code parsing made safe and reliable when using Python exec()?
Question:
The framework prompts the LLM to generate Python code that implements the rules and then executes it with Python's exec(). How do you sandbox or constrain the code to prevent arbitrary execution risks? Do you validate the function signature, import statements, and AST before execution? Also, how do you enforce consistent handling of missing values, type mismatches, and unseen categorical levels in the generated code?
Answer:
Short answer: in the paper the authors rely on careful prompt design (constraining output format, including feature types and I/O descriptions) and then exec() the small piece of code the LLM produces, but they do not describe a full production-grade sandbox or an AST-based approval pipeline. For a research demo that works with limited queries and trusted compute this can be pragmatic, but if you want safe and reliable deployment you should add static checks, a strict execution sandbox, and deterministic handling of missing/unseen values. Below I explain what the paper actually does, why exec() is risky, and practical safeguards you can (and should) add.

What the paper actually does
The paper explicitly says they constrain the LLM’s response format (so parsing is easier), include feature type information and example categories in the prompt (so the LLM can avoid type-mismatched rules), and instruct the LLM to emit a simple function (they “include the function name, input and output descriptions, and inferred rules in the prompt”). They then run the generated code with Python’s exec() to convert data into binary rule-features. The writeup does not list AST validation, explicit import blocking, process isolation, or runtime resource limits — it focuses on prompt-level constraints and then uses an ensemble and weight-tuning step to filter weak/irrelevant rules for model quality.

Why exec() is dangerous
exec() runs arbitrary Python source. Even a seemingly small snippet can import os/subprocess, perform file or network I/O, spawn long-running loops, or access sensitive filesystem/environment. Prompt-format constraints reduce syntactic variation but do not prevent malicious or accidental side effects. Relying solely on the LLM to produce “simple” code is brittle for safety.

Concrete safeguards to make LLM$\to$code safe (recommended for production)
First, prevent dangerous constructs syntactically and semantically before execution. Parse the generated source with ast.parse() and inspect/validate the AST: allow only a whitelisted set of node types (e.g., Module, FunctionDef, Return, BoolOp, Compare, Name, Constant, Subscript, Load, Dict/List/Set, IfExp, UnaryOp, BinOp with limited operators), forbid Import/ImportFrom, Exec/Global/Nonlocal, Attribute chains that could reach __class__ or dunder names, and disallow Call nodes to any name not in a small whitelist. Also validate the function signature (expected name, expected single row argument, no *args/**kwargs), ensure the function always returns bool/int, and reject code exceeding a node/line budget.

Second, execute in a constrained runtime. Do not exec into the unbounded globals. Execute compiled code in a minimal globals dict with an empty or very small __builtins__ and only safe helper functions you explicitly provide (for instance safe_get, safe_eq, safe_num). Even then, CPython-level “safe globals” are not bulletproof — so prefer process-level isolation: run the code in a short-lived separate process (or container) with no network, read-only filesystem, unprivileged user, strict ulimit/cgroups for CPU and memory, and a wall-clock timeout. If you need stronger guarantees use container sandboxing (Docker with seccomp, or real VMs) or sandboxing frameworks (RestrictedPython, or language-runtimes that give real sandboxes).

Third, force the generated code to use your safe helper API (and check that it does). Instead of letting the LLM write arbitrary expressions that handle missing values, provide a small set of vetted helper functions (safe_get(row, name), to_float(x), is_missing(x), map_category(x, allowed, default)) and include those helpers in the execution globals. In AST validation require that only these helper names appear in Call nodes. This makes behavior for missing values, type coercion, and unseen categories deterministic and centralizes policy.

Fourth, pre-normalize input data so rules run against canonical values. A simple normalization pass converts numeric-like strings to floats or None, maps unknown categorical levels to a single token like "UNK", normalizes datetimes, and encodes missing values consistently. If the LLM-generated rule expects normalized inputs (you should include this in the prompt), you avoid ad-hoc handling in generated code.

Fifth, test generated functions on edge cases before applying them to full data. Run the function against a small suite of synthetic rows that exercise NaNs, empty strings, unseen categories, extreme values, and verify the function returns a boolean and does not mutate state or perform I/O. Reject or re-prompt any rule that errors or returns non-boolean values.

Alternatives that are far safer
As the paper notes, the LLM is good at mapping noisy language to code/logic. A safer pattern is to ask the LLM to output a structured representation (JSON or a small DSL / rule AST) rather than raw Python, then interpret that structured output with your own evaluator. Interpreting a whitelist of operators and operands that you control is much easier to audit and sandbox than executing arbitrary generated Python.

How the paper’s design helps (but doesn’t remove the need for the above)
By including feature types and example categories in the prompt and constraining format, the paper reduces parsing failures and type-mismatch bugs; by ensemble and weight-tuning they reduce the downstream impact of noisy/irrelevant rules on prediction quality. But those mechanisms do not address arbitrary code execution risks — for that you need AST checks, helper-only execution, process isolation, time/memory limits, and canonical preprocessing as I described.

Short checklist for a safe pipeline
Ensure the prompt asks for a fixed function signature or structured JSON; parse the output with ast or JSON; statically validate the AST/JSON; run unit tests on the generated function; execute only in a restricted process/container with resource limits and no network; provide and force use of safe helper functions; normalize inputs up-front; and finally, reject and re-prompt any rule that fails static or runtime checks.

If you need, I can sketch a minimal AST whitelist and a short list of helper functions (safe_get, safe_cmp, map_category) and show how to validate the AST against them; tell me which execution environment (single machine, Docker, k8s) you plan to use and I’ll tailor the checklist.

### What are the exact bagging settings for instances and features, and how were they chosen?
Question:
You mention using bagging to reduce prompt size and improve robustness by sampling subsets of features and instances per trial. What specific sampling rates did you use (e.g., fraction of features, fraction of training examples) and are these fixed across datasets? How sensitive is performance to these bagging hyperparameters, especially on wide tables with hundreds of columns?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not give a fixed “fraction of features” or “fraction of instances” number in the main text. What it does report and fix across the reported experiments are the ensemble size and the number of rules per class: the authors used $T=20$ ensemble trials and $R=10$ rules per class for all datasets. The paper describes that, within each trial, they use bagging to pick a subset of features and/or a subset of examples to (a) keep the prompt short enough for the LLM and (b) create diverse, weak rule-generators, but the exact sampling fraction (e.g. “pick $30\%$ of columns”) is not explicitly reported in the body — that detail appears to be managed per dataset in the code/appendix.

Why that matters and how to think about it. Bagging here is used for two purposes that pull in opposite directions: reducing prompt length (so you must sample fewer features per trial) and covering predictive features across the ensemble (so you want enough features sampled across trials). The paper’s reported hyperparameter analysis focuses on two knobs they fixed and studied explicitly: the number of ensembles $T$ (performance improves and saturates around $T\!\approx\!20$) and the number of rules per class $R$ (they picked $R=10$ as a good tradeoff between information and overfitting in low-shot). They show empirically that increasing $T$ helps until convergence, and that too many rules inflates feature dimensionality and can hurt few-shot learning.

A simple, useful quantitative view (not new to the paper) is to treat each trial as sampling $s$ features out of $d$ total. The probability that a particular feature is seen at least once across $T$ independent trials is
$$p_{\text{covered}} = 1 - \bigl(1 - \tfrac{s}{d}\bigr)^T.$$
This formula immediately gives design intuition and concrete trade-offs. For example, if $d=200$ and you sample $s=20$ features per trial ($s/d=0.1$) with $T=20$ trials then
$$p_{\text{covered}} = 1 - 0.9^{20} \approx 0.88,$$
so each column has about an $88\%$ chance to appear in at least one trial. If you want $95\%$ coverage with $s/d=0.1$ you would need
$$T \ge \frac{\ln(1-0.95)}{\ln(1-0.1)} \approx 29$$
trials. Equivalently, increasing $s$ (more features per trial) reduces the number of trials needed, but increases prompt length.

Practical consequences / sensitivity (what the paper implies and what to do):
- The paper demonstrates robustness to many noisy columns (their spurious-column experiment), which indicates that FeatLLM tolerates noisy feature sampling so long as the ensemble and rule-filtering (weight tuning) are used. In that experiment rules derived from noisy columns were rare ($1\text{--}2\%$).
- The most sensitive hyperparameters in their experiments were $T$ (ensembles) and $R$ (rules per class). They fixed these across datasets and found $T\!=\!20$, $R\!=\!10$ to work well. The paper does not present a direct ablation over the per-trial feature/instance sampling fraction; instead the guidance is pragmatic: limit what's put into each prompt so the prompt fits the LLM and rely on the ensemble to cover the rest.
- For very wide tables (hundreds of columns) the natural strategy is to cap the absolute number of columns per trial (e.g., tens of columns) rather than use a fixed fraction. Then use the coverage formula above to choose $T$ that gives acceptable chance of seeing any given column. If predictive features are sparse, you must either increase $s$ or increase $T$ to avoid missing them.

Practical recipe (aligned with the paper’s design principles):
1. Hold $R=10$ and start with $T\approx 20$ (these are the paper’s defaults).
2. Choose $s$ so the serialized prompt (feature descriptions + $k$-shot examples + reasoning instructions) fits comfortably in the LLM input window; a practical cap used in similar workflows is on the order of $20\text{--}50$ columns per trial for GPT-3.5-sized prompts, but the exact cap depends on how verbose your feature descriptions and few-shot examples are.
3. If $d$ is large, use the coverage formula to check whether $T$ with your chosen $s$ gives acceptable coverage; increase $T$ if coverage is too low or increase $s$ if you can afford longer prompts.
4. If you have many labeled examples (beyond the $k$ few-shot regime), you can also bag instances per trial to keep each prompt short—again the paper treats instance bagging as an optional lever for larger training sets, while their few-shot experiments often rely on the $k$-shot subsample itself.

If you need the exact sampling code or the precise fractions they used per dataset, the paper points to the released code (the GitHub link in the paper) and to Appendix/hyperparameter figures for more implementation-level details; checking that repository/appendix is the fastest way to see the authors’ concrete per-dataset choices. If you give me your dataset size $d$ and a target coverage probability, I’ll compute recommended $(s,T)$ pairs for you using the formula above.

### How many LLM API calls are made per ensemble trial, and why does the paper mention only 30 queries?
Question:
The method appears to require one LLM call to generate rules and another to generate parsing code per trial. With $T=20$ ensembles, I would expect roughly 40 calls, possibly more if retries are needed. The paper reports 'only 30 API queries'. Can you clarify the counting method and whether some stages or ensembles share prompts, cache results, or batch multiple tasks per call?
Answer:
Short answer: the method as described requires about two LLM interactions per ensemble trial (one call to generate the natural-language rules for that trial, and one call to convert those rules into parsing/code), so a naïve count is $2T$ API calls (with $T$ the number of trials). The paper’s statement “only 30 API queries” in the timing paragraph is not fully unpacked in the text, but it is consistent with a few practical reductions that the authors could have (and which the paper hints at) — batching/completing multiple rule-sets per request, reusing a single parser or cached parsed outputs across trials, or running the particular timing measurement with a smaller effective $T$. Any of those will cut the raw $2T$ figure down to the reported $\sim 30$.

Why the naïve count is $2T$: every ensemble trial in FeatLLM has two LLM-dependent stages in the pipeline described in the paper: (1) the rule-extraction prompt that emits the $R$ rules per class for that trial (this produces the human-readable rule set for that trial), and (2) the parsing prompt that turns those natural-language rules into executable code. If you call the LLM once for (1) and once for (2) per trial you get 2 calls per trial, i.e. $2T$ calls total.

How you can get a much smaller number (and how the paper’s “30” is plausible)
- Batch / multi-completion per request: many inference APIs let you request multiple independent completions in a single HTTP call (OpenAI’s “$n$” parameter, or just asking the model to produce multiple rule-sets in one prompt). If you request $n$ rule-sets per generation call you only need $\left\lceil \frac{T}{n} \right\rceil$ generation calls instead of $T$. The same batching can be done for parsing.
- Reuse a single parser (or generate a parser template once): instead of asking the model to emit parsing code customized to each trial’s rules, you can call the LLM once to produce a robust parser function that takes arbitrary rule text and returns code/conditions locally. That converts $T$ parsing calls into $1$ one‑time call.
- Cache / deduplicate parse outputs: many trials will produce overlapping or identical rules (the paper even discusses self-consistency and repeated correct rules across trials). If you hash the textual rule-sets and only parse unique rule-sets, the number of parsing calls $U$ can be much smaller than $T$.
- Different $T$ or counting convention in that timing run: the Implementation section reports $T=20$ for the experiments, but the timing run on the Adult dataset (16 shots) where they quote “30 API queries” might have used a different $T$ or a measurement that counts only certain LLM interactions (for example counting only generation calls, or counting unique calls after deduplication). The paper does not explicitly enumerate which convention they used in Table~\ref{tab:cost}.

A compact accounting formula you can use when reproducing results is
$$
\text{API\_calls} \approx \left\lceil \frac{T}{n_{\text{gen}}} \right\rceil
+ \left\lceil \frac{U}{n_{\text{parse}}} \right\rceil + S
$$
where $T$ is the number of ensemble trials, $n_{\text{gen}}$ is how many independent rule-sets you ask the model to return per generation request, $U$ is the number of unique rule-sets that actually need parsing (after deduplication), $n_{\text{parse}}$ is how many parses you batch per parsing request, and $S$ counts one-time helper calls (e.g., to create a parser template, or to run verification). For example, if $T=20$ but only $U=10$ unique rule-sets appeared and you did one parse per unique set, then total calls = $20$ (generation) + $10$ (parsing) = $30$ — exactly the paper’s quoted figure.

Recommendation: if you need to reproduce the paper’s “30” figure exactly, check the released code (the authors point to a GitHub link) or contact the authors for which counting convention they used for the timing table. Otherwise, when reporting API cost yourself, state (a) how many independent ensemble trials $T$ you ran, (b) whether you used batching/multi-completions, (c) whether you reused a parser or cached parsed results, and (d) your token vs. call accounting — that will make comparisons apples-to-apples.

### How do you prevent label leakage when the LLM sees few-shot examples during rule induction?
Question:
FeatLLM provides serialized few-shot examples with their labels to the LLM to induce rules. How do you ensure the LLM does not memorize or paraphrase these specific example-label associations as brittle 'if record equals X then class Y' patterns? Do you include explicit anti-leak constraints in the prompt or validate induced rules against exact-record matching to avoid overfitting?
Answer:
Short answer: FeatLLM does not rely on a single explicit “anti-leak” instruction saying “don’t memorize examples.” Instead it uses several design choices that collectively discourage brittle exact‑match rules and then prunes or dilutes any such rules during downstream processing. Concretely, the paper uses a two‑step reasoning prompt to force general priors before looking at examples, limits the number/format of rules, converts rules to binary features and learns non‑negative weights (with post‑tuning pruning), and ensembles many independent rule sets via bagging—these mechanisms, plus the empirical analyses in the paper, are how FeatLLM mitigates label leakage. The paper does not describe an explicit exact‑record‑match veto, although you can add one as a practical improvement (see suggestions at the end).

Why the system resists “if record = X then class Y” memorization
The paper explicitly builds multiple layers to favor general, reusable rules over memorized example replication. First, the prompt forces a two‑step reasoning: Step‑1 asks the LLM to infer causal/semantic tendencies from the task and feature descriptions without looking at the few‑shot examples; only in Step‑2 does the model use the examples together with the prior to produce concrete rules. That separation encourages the model to ground rules in general prior knowledge rather than simply echoing example labels (see Section “Prompt Design for Extracting Rules”).

Second, the prompt constrains rule format and the number of rules per class (the paper fixes $R=10$ rules/class). This puts an explicit cap on model capacity to enumerate one rule per example and tends to push the LLM toward a small set of higher‑level predicates (ranges, category membership, simple conjunctions) rather than a long list of instance‑specific conjunctions.

Third, the textual rules are parsed into binary features and a small downstream model is trained to weight them. If the binary feature vector for class k is $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$, the paper computes
$$
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,\qquad
\mathbf{p}^i=\text{Softmax}([\text{logit}_1^i,\dots,\text{logit}_c^i]),
$$
and fits $\mathbf{w}_k$ by optimizing cross‑entropy on the few labeled examples. Because rules become input features to a learnable model, rules that only fire on one or two training rows must “buy” their usefulness from improving classification across samples; many brittle exact‑match rules will end up with low learned weight and can be pruned. The authors report using a simple threshold (they examined rule AUROC and used a weight threshold of $0.1$) to filter out uninformative rules (Section “Quality of features” and “Inferring class likelihood via rules”).

Finally, FeatLLM uses ensembling and bagging (multiple trials with randomized few‑shot order and higher temperature) so that brittle or idiosyncratic rules created in one trial are averaged together with other trials. Empirically the paper shows that when unrelated noisy columns are added, only $\sim 1\text{--}2\%$ of generated rules come from noisy columns, and the ensemble suffers less performance drop than baselines (Section “Effect of adding spurious correlations”). The ablation that removes the Step‑1 reasoning also hurts performance, which supports the claim that the two‑step prompt is important to avoid spurious, example‑driven rules.

What the paper does and what it does not do
The paper relies on these implicit and downstream defenses rather than an explicit “do not create rules that exactly match training rows” constraint. There is no explicit description in the paper of an automated exact‑record‑matching veto (e.g., dropping any rule whose satisfaction set equals exactly one training row) or a hard prompt ban on “if all features equal these exact values” rules. The authors do, however, prune low‑importance rules after learning weights and use ensemble/bagging to dilute brittle rules; they also note that FeatLLM can still learn spurious correlations in some adversarial attachments of closely related columns (see Appendix), so a nonzero risk of memorization remains.

Practical measures you can add (compatible with FeatLLM)
If you want a stricter guard against exact‑match leakage, the paper’s architecture allows easy additions. Two complementary measures are particularly simple and consistent with FeatLLM:

1) Prompt‑level guidance: add an explicit sentence in the reasoning/response instruction asking the LLM to avoid rules that refer to every feature or that only match a single example, e.g. “Prefer rules that generalize across records; avoid rules that list full equality checks across most features or that would apply only to a single training record.”

2) Post‑hoc rule validation/pruning: after parsing a rule into code, compute its support and discrimination on the training set (or a held‑out fold). Drop rules whose support is below a small threshold (e.g., $\text{support} < 2$ or $\text{coverage} < s\%$) or whose AUROC/lift is below a minimum. Alternatively, add $L_1$ or support‑based regularization when fitting $\mathbf{w}_k$ to encourage sparse weights and then prune features with tiny weights. These checks are fully consistent with the paper’s use of parsed binary features and downstream weight tuning.

Concluding recommendation
FeatLLM’s core defenses against memorization are the two‑step reasoning prompt, a cap on rule count and constrained rule format, downstream weight learning with pruning, and ensembling/bagging. The paper does not implement an explicit “no exact‑match” hard rule, but that is straightforward to add and complementary to the existing pipeline: combine an in‑prompt preference for general rules with a post‑parse coverage/AUROC test (or simple minimum support) and you will greatly reduce the chance of brittle “if record = X then class Y” rules while staying faithful to the FeatLLM approach.

### What if some classes naturally have fewer valid rules than others?
Question:
The model assumes a fixed $R$ rules per class and computes logits as a weighted sum over that class's rule-indicator vector. If one class truly has fewer discriminative patterns, padding up to $R$ may introduce noise. Conversely, if another class needs more than $R$ rules, useful information may be truncated. Did you observe performance gaps correlated with this mismatch? Would normalizing by the number of valid rules or using learned biases help?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper did not find a clear, systematic performance gap caused by using a fixed number of rules per class (they used $R=10$); two ingredients in FeatLLM — the learned nonnegative weights (tuning) and the ensemble/bagging procedure — largely mitigate both the “padding noise” and the “truncation” concerns in practice. The authors did not try explicit per-class normalization in the published experiments, but adding a learned per-class bias or a normalization/scale term is a reasonable, low-cost extension that can help in the low-shot regime.

Why padding / truncation didn’t show up as a large problem in the paper
The paper reports three findings that together explain why the fixed-$R$ design was robust across datasets. First, hyper-parameter sweeps on the number of rules showed no statistically significant difference and the authors chose $R=10$ as a pragmatic trade-off between prompt length and expressivity. Second, the linear stage is tuned on the few-shot data: the model fits nonnegative weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ and the paper shows that tuning filters out uninformative rules (they even threshold learned weights at 0.1 to call a rule “useful”). Concretely the model uses
$$
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot\mathbf{z}_k^i
$$
so any padded or noisy rule can be driven toward zero weight when there is signal in the training data. The ablation “-Tuning” caused a performance drop, confirming tuning is important to suppress junk features. Third, the ensemble + bagging scheme produces many diverse rule sets across trials: even if a single trial truncates some patterns, other trials may discover them and averaging probabilities reduces sensitivity to any one trial’s incompleteness.

When you should still worry
The mitigation above depends on having enough signal to learn weights (even in few-shot settings). In extremely tiny shot counts, or if the LLM systematically invents many plausible-looking but useless rules for a specific class, the learned weights may not reliably suppress padding noise and you could see class-specific degradation. The paper’s “spurious correlations” experiments also show FeatLLM is fairly robust, but they note failure modes when LLMs mistakenly encode incorrect causal relationships.

Would normalizing by number of valid rules or using learned biases help?
Yes — both are sensible and have clear interpretations. The paper did not include these normalizations or a bias term in the published formulation (they explicitly use a linear model without bias in their description), so the following are proposed extensions consistent with the method’s spirit.

Adding a learned per-class bias $b_k$ captures class priors independent of rules:
$$
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot\mathbf{z}_k^i + b_k.
$$
This is the simplest and often-most-effective remedy when classes have different base rates or when one class has systematically fewer useful rules: the bias absorbs the prior probability and prevents the model from having to manufacture it via rule weights.

Per-class scaling or normalization makes logits comparable across classes with different numbers of active/valid rules. Options include dividing by the number of active rules (or a smoothed variant):
$$
\text{logit}_k^i = \frac{\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot\mathbf{z}_k^i}{\sum_j z_{k,j}^i + \epsilon},
$$
or learning a multiplicative scale $s_k$:
$$
\text{logit}_k^i = s_k\big(\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot\mathbf{z}_k^i\big) + b_k.
$$
Smoothed denominators (e.g., $1+\sum_j z_{k,j}^i$) or root-normalization (divide by $\sqrt{\sum_j z_{k,j}^i}$) avoid harsh effects when only one strong rule fires. The trade-off is that raw averaging can underweight a class that truly needs many moderate signals; a learned scale $s_k$ lets the data decide.

Other practical knobs consistent with the paper
L1 regularization (sparsity) on $\mathbf{w}_k$ reinforces the paper’s post-hoc thresholding and can shrink spurious padded rules faster in very low-shot settings. Allowing signed weights (dropping the nonnegativity projection) gives inhibitory rules but changes the interpretability assumption the authors made; if you allow negative weights, use regularization to avoid overfitting. Increasing ensemble size or bagging diversity is another pragmatic way to cover more true patterns without increasing per-trial $R$.

How to test this empirically (simple ablations)
Run a few controlled experiments on your validation folds: (1) add $b_k$ and see if per-class calibration/accuracy improves, (2) compare raw-sum vs. average (divide by active rules) vs. learned scale $s_k$, and (3) try L1 regularization. Plot per-class metrics and the average number of rules retained (weight $>$ threshold) per class; if mismatches exist, normalization or bias should show their benefit there.

Bottom line
The paper’s combination of weight tuning and ensembling largely prevented systematic performance gaps from fixing $R$ across classes in their benchmarks, but in low-signal or highly imbalanced scenarios adding a learned bias and/or a per-class normalization/scale is a natural and likely helpful modification. These are cheap to implement and worth trying as ablation if you suspect the fixed-$R$ assumption is harming a particular dataset.

### How exactly is the two-step reasoning prompt structured to reduce spurious correlations?
Question:
The paper describes Step-1 as inferring causal tendencies between features and the task without using examples, followed by Step-2 that uses demonstrations to derive rules. Could you share the exact template for the Step-1 instructions and how you ensure the LLM does not implicitly use the example section during that step? Is there empirical evidence (e.g., analysis of rule references) that Step-1 reduces reliance on irrelevant columns compared to a single-step prompt?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper forces Step‑1 to be an explicit “prior‑knowledge / tendency” elicitation that is written into the prompt and labeled as a separate subtask (e.g. “Step 1 — do not look at examples”). Concretely, the authors place a clear instruction that the model must infer, for each feature, whether prior knowledge would tend to increase, decrease, or not affect the target, and to do this without using the few‑shot example section. That explicit instruction plus the prompt structure is the mechanism they use to keep Step‑1 from relying on spurious correlations in the tiny training set; ablation and spurious‑correlation experiments in the paper show that removing Step‑1 hurts $\mathrm{AUC}$ and that only a very small fraction of generated rules (about $1$–$2\%$) reference injected noisy columns.

What the Step‑1 instruction looks like (core text used in the paper, paraphrased from the prompt in Appendix/Figures): “Step 1 — Prior knowledge (Do NOT use the example demonstrations). Based only on the task description and the feature names/types/definitions above, for each feature $f_j$ indicate whether prior knowledge or common sense suggests it will 'Increase', 'Decrease', or have 'No effect' on the probability of the target class. Give a short ($1$–$2$ sentence) justification for each feature. Do NOT refer to or use the serialized example cases below in this step.” The model is expected to reply with a short list like “age: Increase — older age is associated with higher risk of X” for each feature.

How the paper prevents implicit use of examples. The primary control the authors report is an explicit instruction in the reasoning block telling the LLM not to use the example demonstrations for Step‑1, and a structured prompt that separates “basic information (features + examples)” from the two reasoning steps. That explicit “do not use examples” sentence encourages the model to treat Step‑1 as hypothesis generation from prior knowledge. In practice you can (and the authors note as an implementation detail) strengthen this by obvious prompt engineering choices: label sections clearly, place a visible delimiter before the examples, or run Step‑1 as a separate API call that omits the examples entirely and then feed the Step‑1 output into Step‑2 along with the examples. The paper itself implements the two‑step instruction (Step‑1 then Step‑2) and also constrains the number of rules per class (they fix $R=10$) and uses feature type hints to reduce type‑mismatch confusion — these choices further reduce noise and spurious rule generation.

Empirical evidence in the paper that Step‑1 reduces reliance on irrelevant columns. The authors include an ablation (“-Reasoning”) where Step‑1 is removed; across datasets that ablation degrades $\mathrm{AUC}$ (the effect is strongest in the low‑shot regime), which indicates the two‑step reasoning contributes measurable performance gains. In the spurious‑correlation experiment (they attach irrelevant columns from another dataset to the Heart dataset) FeatLLM shows the least performance degradation compared to baselines, and the paper reports that rules referencing the injected noisy columns accounted for only about $1$–$2\%$ of all rules generated. The paper also reports a rule‑quality check ($\mathrm{AUROC}$ between each generated binary rule feature and the label): a majority of generated rules are informative ($\mathrm{AUROC} > 0.5$) and the downstream weight‑tuning step further suppresses low‑quality/spurious rules. Those three pieces (ablation, spurious‑correlation test, and the rule‑quality statistics) are the empirical basis the authors use to claim Step‑1 helps reduce reliance on irrelevant columns.

Intuition and connection to broader ideas. Step‑1 works as an inductive‑bias elicitor: by forcing the LLM to state prior tendencies first, the model builds a causal/hypothesis scaffold that Step‑2 then uses together with the few examples to produce concrete rules. This mimics human analysis (“first form hypotheses from domain knowledge, then match against data”) and reduces the chance that the few examples alone dominate rule formation (the classic few‑shot overfitting / spurious‑correlation problem). This is analogous to other structured prompting and chain‑of‑thought strategies that separate prior knowledge and evidence so the model does not conflate spurious sample idiosyncrasies with general rules.

Practical note for replication: if you want to be maximally conservative against “implicit” leakage, request Step‑1 in a separate API call with only task + feature descriptions (no example serialization), then pass the Step‑1 output into Step‑2 along with the examples. The paper reports good results using explicit two‑step prompting and shows ablation/spurious‑noise analyses confirming the benefit.

### Are compound logical rules (AND/OR) constrained in depth or length during parsing?
Question:
You note the LLM may emit rules combining conditions with AND or OR. Do you bound the number of literals per rule, the nesting depth, or the size of category lists to control complexity and parsing robustness? If not, could very long or highly conjunctive rules lead to overfitting or brittle code? Did you measure rule complexity distributions and their relation to performance?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not impose an explicit syntactic cap on how many atomic conditions (literals) can appear inside a single rule nor on nesting depth or categorical-list length; instead it controls complexity at the rule-set level (fixed number of rules per class) and uses prompt-formatting, LLM-assisted parsing, weight tuning and bagging/ensembling to keep the overall system robust. The authors measured the effect of the number of rules and the usefulness of generated rules, but they did not report a direct analysis of per-rule literal counts or nesting-depth distributions versus performance.

More detail and intuition. In Section 3.1 the authors explicitly enforce a fixed number of rules per class (they use $R = 10$ in experiments) and give the LLM a constrained response format to make parsing easier; they also encourage the LLM to consult feature types when generating rules so rules tend to be simple feature-wise conditions. The text also notes that “without additional guidance, the LLM can combine multiple rules using logical operators like AND or OR,” and that parsing is handled by asking the LLM itself to emit simple Python code which is then executed via exec() (Section 3.2). This means the pipeline tolerates some syntactic variation, but there is no documented hard cap on how many conjunctions/disjunctions a single rule may contain or how deeply nested a boolean expression may be.

How the paper controls brittleness and overfitting in practice. The main engineering levers are (a) fixing the number of rules per class ($R = 10$) and using ensembles with bagging ($T = 20$), (b) learning non-negative linear weights on the binary rule-features so uninformative rules get low weight ($logit_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i$, Section 3.2), and (c) post-hoc filtering of weak rules (the paper reports pruning rules with learned weight below $0.1$). Empirically the authors measured per-rule usefulness (average AUROC of each binary rule vs target; Table in Section 4) and report that most generated rules are informative before tuning and that tuning filters out irrelevant ones. They also ran a spurious-column experiment and found rules based on noisy columns were only $\sim 1$–$2\%$ of the total, and a hyperparameter sweep showed that increasing the number of rules beyond about $10$ tended to worsen overfitting in the low-shot regime (Section 4).

Why long or highly conjunctive rules are a real concern. Intuitively, a very long conjunction/disjunction makes the derived binary feature extremely sparse (it matches few examples), which in few-shot settings increases variance and the chance of overfitting. Deeply nested Boolean structure also increases the surface area for parsing errors (because the LLM may produce many equivalent—but syntactically different—phrases) and makes automatic code-generation/parsing more brittle. The paper addresses these risks indirectly ($R$ limit, LLM-assisted parsing, learned-weight pruning, ensemble), but it does not replace an explicit syntactic complexity cap.

If you want to extend or test this aspect experimentally (recommended), measure per-rule complexity (number of literals, OR/AND counts, nesting depth), plot those against rule AUROC and learned weight, and try constrained prompts that force a maximum clause length or canonical forms (e.g., ask for DNF with max 2 literals per conjunct). The paper’s existing diagnostics (rule AUROC before/after tuning, effect of varying $R$, and spurious-feature experiments) are a useful baseline but they stop short of an explicit rule-complexity $\to$ performance study.

### Do you filter out impossible thresholds or unit-mismatch errors suggested by the LLM?
Question:
The LLM may propose thresholds that do not match data scales or units (e.g., 'age > 200' or 'cholesterol < 1'). Do you provide summary statistics of each feature to the LLM to ground thresholds, or post-filter rules that are almost always false or true on the training data? If a rule fires for 0% of training examples, is it pruned before training the linear model?
Answer:
Short answer: The paper does not do an explicit pre-filter step that removes threshold rules based on numeric summary statistics or on $0\%$ coverage before training. Instead, FeatLLM tries to prevent type/scale mistakes up front by giving the LLM feature types (and optionally definitions) plus a few serialized example values in the prompt so the LLM can “see” the scale, and then relies on downstream tuning + ensembling to suppress or remove bad rules after they are generated.

Concretely, the Prompt Design phase supplies each feature’s type and (optionally) its definition and few-shot example values in the “basic information” block, and the reasoning instruction explicitly tells the LLM to refer to feature types when writing rules. That is the paper’s primary mechanism for reducing unit/type mismatches and wildly implausible thresholds (e.g., telling the LLM “age is an integer in years” and showing example ages helps the LLM propose thresholds in the right ballpark).

After the LLM outputs rules they are parsed into binary features and used by a very simple downstream model. The paper uses a non-negative linear scoring per class
$$
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,\qquad
\mathbf{p}^i=\text{Softmax}([\text{logit}_1^i,\dots,\text{logit}_c^i]),
$$
which is trained (cross-entropy) on the few-shot examples. The authors then explicitly filter features post-hoc by learned weight: empirically they inspected the weight histogram and used a threshold (they report using $0.1$) to mark rules as unimportant and prune them (“After tuning” in their feature-quality table). They also use bagging/ensemble across multiple rule-generation trials so that occasional bad rules from single trials are down-weighted in the ensemble.

Two practical implications relevant to your examples: a rule that never fires on the training set produces an all-zero binary column; such a column does not affect the training loss (its gradient is zero) and so its weight is essentially unconstrained by data and it’s inert for that training run. It will either remain near its initialization or be removed later by the post-training weight thresholding; the paper does not remove these zero-coverage rules before fitting. Conversely, rules that fire almost always (constant columns) carry little discriminative signal and will typically get small weights during tuning and then be pruned by the same post-filter.

If you need stricter guarantees against impossible thresholds (e.g., to absolutely prevent thresholds outside observed ranges), the paper does not implement that, but the natural extensions are straightforward: include numeric summary statistics (min/max/quantiles) in the prompt so the LLM is explicitly anchored, or post-validate parsed numeric thresholds against dataset min/max and automatically clamp or discard rules whose thresholds are impossible on the training set. Those ideas are suggested improvements rather than parts of FeatLLM as described in the paper.

### Are softmax probabilities calibrated without biases, and did you evaluate ECE or Brier score?
Question:
The logits are weighted sums of positive rule hits with no bias. If a sample satisfies no rules for any class, all logits are 0 and softmax yields a uniform distribution. Did you evaluate probability calibration (e.g., ECE, Brier score) and consider adding class-wise biases or temperature scaling to improve calibration, especially in imbalanced or low-evidence scenarios?
Answer:
Short answer: no — as written, FeatLLM’s downstream linear model has no class bias term and the paper does not report explicit calibration metrics such as Expected Calibration Error (ECE) or Brier score. The paper trains non‑negative per-rule weights with cross‑entropy and averages softmax probabilities across ensemble trials, but it does not evaluate calibration or apply post‑hoc calibration (biases / temperature).

Why this matters (intuitively + technically). Equation (paper Eq. (eq:infer_prob)) defines
$$
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,\qquad
\mathbf{p}^i=\text{Softmax}([\text{logit}_1^i,\dots,\text{logit}_c^i]).
$$
Because there is no additive bias, if no rule fires for any class (all $\mathbf{z}_k^i=0$) then every $\text{logit}_k^i=0$ and softmax returns a uniform distribution. That is a sensible “uninformative” default, but it is not informed by class imbalance or any learned prior; in imbalanced or low‑evidence regimes this can be miscalibrated (for example, if one class is much more probable a priori, a uniform prediction will be biased away from that prior). The non‑negative constraint $\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)$ also prevents learned features from expressing negative evidence (a rule cannot reduce a class logit), which further constrains the shape of the predicted probabilities and can produce over/under‑confidence patterns that calibration methods are designed to correct.

What the paper did evaluate. The paper optimizes cross‑entropy for weight learning and reports discriminative metrics (AUC/AUROC) and rule quality statistics; it uses ensembling (bagging + averaging probabilities across trials), which typically reduces variance and often improves calibration empirically, but the paper does not report ECE, Brier score, reliability diagrams, or any explicit calibration experiments.

Practical fixes (mathematically and how to evaluate them). Two lightweight interventions you can try:

1) Add a learnable class bias. Change the logit to
$$
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i + b_k,
$$
where $b_k\in\mathbb{R}$ is a per‑class bias. If you want a sensible default when you have few labels, initialize or set $b_k=\log \hat{\pi}_k$ (smoothed empirical prior), where $\hat{\pi}_k$ is the class prior estimate; then when no rules fire you get the prior rather than a uniform output. Learn $b_k$ jointly by minimizing cross‑entropy (with small regularization) or fit it on a small validation split.

2) Temperature scaling (post‑hoc). Given logits vector $\mathbf{l}=[\text{logit}_1,\dots,\text{logit}_c]$, use
$$
\mathbf{p}=\text{Softmax}(\mathbf{l}/T),
$$
with scalar $T>0$. Fit $T$ on a held‑out calibration set to minimize negative log‑likelihood (or Brier). $T>1$ softens (reduces confidence), $T<1$ sharpens. This is very cheap and robust even in multiclass settings (Guo et al., 2017 style). If you average probabilities across ensemble members (as FeatLLM does), you can either apply temperature scaling per member before averaging, or average logits (if available) and then apply a global temperature.

How to measure calibration. Compute multiclass Brier score
$$
\text{Brier}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{k=1}^c (p_{ik}-y_{ik})^2,
$$
and ECE (top‑label ECE is common): partition predictions into bins $B_m$ and report
$$
\text{ECE}=\sum_{m=1}^M \frac{|B_m|}{N}\Big| \text{acc}(B_m)-\text{conf}(B_m)\Big|.
$$
Plot reliability diagrams to inspect miscalibration patterns. Because FeatLLM is few‑shot, fit/validation data for calibration may be scarce; use cross‑validation or pooled calibration sets, or use smoothed priors (Dirichlet smoothing) when setting biases.

Practical notes for FeatLLM specifically. Ensembling already used in the paper often helps calibration, so you may see improvements from the existing averaging. Adding class biases is low‑risk and directly addresses the “uniform when no rules fire” issue (you can set biases from smoothed priors to avoid eating into your few training samples). Temperature scaling is the simplest post‑hoc fix and usually improves ECE/Brier without changing accuracy. Finally, if you want the model to represent negative evidence formally, remove or relax the non‑negative projection on $\mathbf{w}_k$, but that changes the design intent (the paper enforces non‑negativity so rules only add evidence).

Summary. The paper did not report calibration metrics (ECE/Brier) nor include biases or temperature scaling. Adding per‑class biases (initialized to smoothed priors) and/or applying temperature scaling on logits are straightforward, low‑cost ways to improve calibration in imbalanced or low‑evidence cases; evaluate them with Brier/ECE and reliability diagrams, using cross‑validation or pooled calibration data to avoid overfitting in the few‑shot regime.

### How are overlapping or conflicting rules across classes handled at inference?
Question:
Because rules are class-specific, a sample may satisfy rules for multiple classes simultaneously. The current scheme scores each class independently and relies on softmax normalization. Did you experiment with shared or global rules, mutual-exclusion constraints, or penalty terms to resolve conflicts? How common are conflicts in your datasets, and do they degrade accuracy?
Answer:
Short answer: we did not add explicit shared/global rules or mutual‑exclusion constraints. Conflicts are handled implicitly by the per‑class scoring, the non‑negative weight projection, and the softmax normalization, and in practice tuning plus ensembling/bagging keeps conflicting or spurious rules from hurting performance in the datasets we tried.

More detail and intuition: FeatLLM generates a separate binary feature vector for each class (the paper denotes the binary feature for sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ and class $k$ as $\mathbf{z}_k^i\in\{0,1\}^R$) and then scores each class independently with a simple linear model. Concretely we compute
$$
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,\qquad
\mathbf{p}^i=\text{Softmax}\big([\text{logit}_1^i,\dots,\text{logit}_c^i]\big),
$$
so each rule acts like a positive “vote” for its class and the softmax makes the classes compete at the end. The projection $\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)$ forces weights into a non‑negative subspace so generated rules cannot directly suppress a class’s score — they only provide positive evidence — and the downstream training learns which rules are important (large $\mathbf{w}_k$) versus which are noise (small $\mathbf{w}_k$).

What we tried instead of explicit constraints: we rely on weight tuning and on ensemble/bagging. Tuning learns to downweight irrelevant or misleading rules (the paper shows that after tuning many low‑utility rules end up with very small weights and are effectively filtered out), and the ensemble of multiple LLM trials (with randomized prompts/bagging) makes the system robust: occasional wrong rules from one trial are outweighed by correct rules from others. Ablations in the paper show removing tuning (\textsf{-Tuning}) or removing the ensemble (\textsf{-Ensemble}) degrades performance, which supports this implicit conflict‑resolution strategy.

How common are conflicts and do they hurt accuracy? Empirically conflicts/spurious rules were rare in our experiments: the paper reports that the “majority” of generated rules are informative (measured by AUROC vs. the target) and, in the spurious‑column test, rules derived from injected noisy columns amounted to only about 1–2% of the total. Overall FeatLLM showed small degradation under added noisy features compared with baselines. That said, the paper also notes failure modes: if closely related but misleading columns are attached, the LLM can generate spurious but plausible rules and those can harm performance (see the Appendix), so conflicts can matter in adversarially correlated settings.

Avenues if you want stricter conflict handling: the paper did not experiment with global/shared rules, explicit mutual‑exclusion constraints, or penalty terms, but these are natural extensions. Options include learning shared rule representations across classes, allowing negative weights (to model inhibitory rules), adding pairwise penalties for jointly active rules across classes, or moving to structured inference (e.g., constrained optimization or CRF‑style global normalization). These would likely require more labeled data or more sophisticated optimization, which is why the current paper prioritizes a simple, data‑efficient approach.

In short: FeatLLM resolves overlaps mainly by learning per‑rule positive weights and using softmax competition plus ensembling; explicit mutual‑exclusion mechanisms were not used in the paper, conflicts were uncommon in our benchmarks, and when they appeared the tuning/ensemble pipeline usually contained their impact.

### How is $k$-fold cross-validation defined with very small $k$-shot training sets?
Question:
The paper uses $k$-fold cross-validation to select training epochs of the linear model, ensuring each fold has at least one example per class. When total labeled shots are as small as 4 or 8, how do you split folds while maintaining class coverage? What exact $k$ values were used per dataset and shot level, and how do you avoid high variance or leakage in such tiny splits?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper uses very small values of $k$ (either $k=2$ or $k=4$) for the $k$-fold cross-validation used to select the number of training epochs, and it enforces per-class coverage when forming the folds. The authors do not publish a per-dataset table of which exact $k$ was used for every dataset/shot combination in the main text; instead they state the rule that $k$ is chosen as $2$ or $4$ so that every training split contains at least one example of each class. They also mitigate variance by repeating experiments and by the ensemble/bagging procedure.

Explanation and practical details grounded in the paper
The paper explicitly says: “We employ $k$-fold cross-validation for optimal epoch selection… The value of $k$ is set either as $2$ or $4$, guaranteeing that the training set includes at least one example of each class.” In practice that means the authors use very small $k$ so the per-fold training set is not excessively tiny, and they enforce a class-coverage constraint when they build the folds (this is most naturally implemented via stratified or constrainted splitting / resampling until the condition is satisfied).

A few concrete points to make the mechanics clear. If you have $s$ labeled shots and choose $k$ folds then each fold has about $s/k$ examples and the training portion in each fold has about $s\cdot (k-1)/k$ examples. For small $s$ you therefore must choose $k$ small enough that the training portion can plausibly contain at least one instance of every class. For example, for binary problems:
- with $s=4$ and $k=2$ each fold is $\approx 2$ samples and the training portion is $2$ samples; you must ensure the two training samples together contain at least one positive and one negative example (the authors enforce this).
- with $s=8$ and $k=4$ each fold is $\approx 2$ samples and the training portion is $\approx 6$ samples, which is usually sufficient to include both classes if the original $s$ had both classes represented.

How leakage and high variance are handled (as described by the paper)
The paper keeps the test set strictly held-out: the $k$-shot examples used to generate features and to train the linear model come from the training side only. Cross-validation is used only inside that small training set to select the number of epochs for the linear model (not to peek at the test set), so there is no leakage to the evaluation set.

To cope with the high variance inherent to such tiny training sets the authors combine several practical measures: they repeat experiments with different random draws of the $k$-shot training sets (they report mean and standard deviation across three random seeds), they build an ensemble of rule-sets (they run the full LLM rule generation + linear fitting pipeline $T=20$ times with bagging and temperature randomness), and they keep the downstream model simple (a constrained linear model with non-negative weights and only epoch selection via CV). Those steps reduce sensitivity to any single random tiny split and stabilize reported metrics.

What the paper does and does not give you
The paper gives the policy (“use $k=2$ or $k=4$, enforce at least one example of each class in the training fold”) and reports the repeated/ensembled evaluation protocol, but it does not publish a per-dataset table mapping every shot level to the exact $k$ used in each case in the main text. If you need the exact split choices for replication, the code repository (anonymized link in the paper) or the appendix (if included) is the place to check; otherwise a practical and faithful reproduction is to follow their rule: choose $k\in\{2,4\}$ so that each fold’s training portion contains at least one example of every class, resample splits until that condition holds, and average results across multiple random draws and the ensemble.

If you want a reproducible recipe: sample your $s$ shots from the training pool, form stratified/constrainted $k$-folds with $k=2$ (for the smallest $s$ such as $4$) or $k=4$ (for larger small $s$ such as $8$), run CV inside that training set only to pick epochs, repeat the whole pipeline several times and use the ensemble/bagging described in the paper to reduce variance. This matches the authors’ stated approach and avoids test leakage while preserving per-class coverage.

### How does FeatLLM handle class imbalance in few-shot training?
Question:
Many tabular datasets are imbalanced. Does the cross-entropy loss use class weights, or do you balance class sampling when forming folds? Given the positive-only weights and no biases, does imbalance lead to biased logit growth for majority classes due to more opportunities to fit rules during rule induction or weight learning?
Answer:
Short answer: The paper does not introduce an explicit class-weighting or class-balanced sampling scheme when fitting the downstream linear model. FeatLLM does, however, include two implicit design choices that reduce—but do not eliminate—the raw advantage of majority classes: the prompt enforces a fixed number of rules per class (they use 10 rules/class) and the method uses bagging/ensembling plus LLM step-1 (prior-knowledge) reasoning. Because the learned logits are sums of non‑negative rule weights with no bias (the paper’s Eq. (1): $\text{logit}_k^i=\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot\mathbf{z}_k^i$ and $\mathbf{p}^i=\mathrm{Softmax}(\cdot)$), class imbalance in the few-shot training set can still bias weight growth toward majority classes during cross‑entropy optimization.

Why imbalance can bias the learned logits (intuitive + technical): FeatLLM turns LLM rules into binary features per class and then learns positive weights for those features by minimizing standard cross‑entropy (no class weights are reported). For a given class $k$ the gradient that pushes a rule weight up is driven by the difference between how often that rule appears in samples of class $k$ versus its expected frequency under the model. Concretely, if you denote the active feature vector for class $k$ as $\mathbf{z}_k^i$ and the (positive) per-rule coefficients as $a_k=\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)$, the per‑weight contribution to the loss gradients accumulates over training samples roughly as
$$
\sum_i (p_k^i - \mathbf{1}\{y_i=k\})\,z_{k,j}^i,
$$
so a class with more training examples (or more rule activations on those examples) will provide a larger net signal to increase its $a_k$. Because negative weights are disallowed and there is no learned bias term, the only way the model can prefer a class is by increasing the positive weight mass on that class’s rule features; minority classes with few examples therefore have fewer opportunities to push their weights up, and the optimizer can end up favoring the majority class.

What the paper does that helps (but is not a full fix): First, the prompt enforces a fixed number of rules per class (10), so the raw count of rule features is balanced across classes; this prevents a trivial imbalance where majority classes simply get more rule features. Second, the LLM’s two‑step reasoning (step‑1: general prior knowledge; step‑2: use examples) tends to produce rules for each class that are not solely driven by the few-shot counts, which can help minority classes generate sensible rules even with few examples. Third, bagging + ensembling (they run many trials with randomized demonstrations and subsets of features/instances) reduces variance and can blunt some imbalance effects in practice. The paper also enforces that cross‑validation folds include at least one example of each class (they set $k$ so training folds have every class), which avoids degenerate folds but is not the same as balancing class frequencies.

Practical remedies you can try (not described in the paper, but straightforward to add): if you encounter severe class imbalance in your few‑shot setting, the simplest fixes are to (1) use a class‑weighted cross‑entropy loss, e.g.
$$
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \alpha_{y_i}\log p_{y_i},\qquad \alpha_k\propto\frac{1}{\text{freq}_k},
$$
so minority classes contribute more to the gradient; (2) perform stratified k‑shot selection or oversample minority examples when fitting the linear weights (or do per‑trial balanced bagging so each ensemble fit sees a balanced sample); (3) add a learnable class bias $b_k$,
$$
\text{logit}_k^i=\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot\mathbf{z}_k^i + b_k,
$$
and initialize $b_k$ to $\log$ of an assumed class prior (this lets you correct priors without breaking the positive‑weight interpretability of rules); (4) allow signed weights (remove $\max(\cdot,0)$) if you want rules to be able to veto a class (at the cost of the “only-positive evidence” interpretability); or (5) use focal loss / stronger regularization or early stopping (they already use CV to pick epochs) to limit overfitting by majority class examples. A practical balanced approach that preserves interpretability is to keep equal rules per class, use stratified few‑shot selection or balanced bagging for the weight fit, and add class bias terms (or class‑weighted loss).

Bottom line: FeatLLM as reported does not apply explicit class‑weighting or sampling to correct imbalance; its fixed-per‑class rule extraction, LLM priors, and ensembling reduce some imbalance pressure, but imbalance can still push weights/logits toward majority classes because of the positive‑only weights and lack of bias. If you need robustness to strong class imbalance, add one of the standard remedies above (class‑weighted loss, stratified/oversampling, class biases, or allowing negative weights) and validate on held‑out data or with calibration.

### Token limits and failure modes: how exactly does bagging overcome long-prompt issues?
Question:
You report that other LLM-based tabular methods failed on datasets with many features due to prompt length, while FeatLLM succeeded via feature bagging. What were the approximate token counts where baselines failed? In FeatLLM, what maximum number of feature descriptions and examples did you include per trial? Does bagging risk missing important cross-feature interactions that only appear when many columns are shown together?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not report an exact “token threshold” where other LLM-based baselines broke, and it does not fix a single hard cap on how many feature descriptions are put into each FeatLLM prompt. What the paper does report is (1) the practical failure mode: prior LLM-based methods failed on datasets that serialize a very large number of columns (the paper calls out datasets like Communities and Myocardial with “over $100$ attributes”), and (2) how FeatLLM avoids that failure in practice: by bagging (subsetting) features and examples per LLM query, constraining rule output to a fixed small number, and ensembling across many trials. Below I unpack each part and explain the trade‑offs and limits.

Token counts / where baselines failed
The authors do not give explicit token counts (e.g., “$X$ tokens”) in the paper. Instead they describe the failure qualitatively: LLM‑based baselines that serialize full tables into text (“in‑context”, TABLET, TabLLM) “failed to infer on tabular datasets with a large number of features” (Experiments → Datasets) and point to datasets with $>100$ attributes as representative examples. The underlying cause they cite is the familiar prompt/context‑window limit and the rapid growth of serialized prompt size when you list all feature names, types and $k$-shot examples (Introduction, Methods → Prompt Design). So there is no exact token number reported; the paper reports column counts ($>100$) and the failure as a prompt‑size/context problem rather than a measured token cutoff.

What FeatLLM puts into a single trial prompt
FeatLLM avoids sending the whole table to the LLM in one shot. Each trial’s prompt contains (a) the task description and the descriptions for the subset of features selected by bagging, (b) the few‑shot example demonstrations (i.e., the $k$ examples for that experiment’s $k$-shot setting), and (c) the reasoning + response instructions. The paper fixes the number of rules the LLM should output to $10$ per class to limit scope (“we have set a fixed number of rules (i.e., $10$) to be extracted for each class”, Sec. Prompt Design) and in experiments they use $T = 20$ ensemble trials and $R = 10$ rules per class (Implementation details). The bagging mechanism selects a subset of features (and/or instances) per trial so that the serialized prompt stays compact, but the paper does not report a single numeric “maximum number of feature descriptions per trial” — that is determined by how you choose the bagging subset in practice and by the $k$ used for examples (the prompt contains the $k$-shot examples used in that experiment; e.g., many reported runs used $k = 4$ in the low‑shot evaluations, other timing experiments used $16$ shots). In short: the paper fixes rules per class ($10$) and ensembles ($20$), uses the experimental $k$ as the number of examples per prompt, and leaves the exact per‑trial feature subset size to the bagging procedure (chosen to trade prompt length vs. coverage).

Does bagging risk missing important cross‑feature interactions?
Yes — in principle bagging can miss interactions that only reveal themselves when many specific columns appear together in the same prompt. The paper is explicit about this tradeoff: “While a single rule set might not capture the relationships of all features, the ensemble of various weak learners created across multiple trials can nullify the impact of incomplete feature/instance coverage in individual trials” (Methods → Ensembling with bagging). Concretely, FeatLLM mitigates the risk in three ways described in the paper: first, repeated randomized trials ($T = 20$ in experiments) increase the chance that interacting features will appear together in at least one trial; second, the LLM’s Step‑1 (prior‑knowledge) reasoning can suggest relationships even when example coverage is small; and third, the LLM is allowed to produce conjunctive rules (AND/OR) so interactions that are present within a single trial can be encoded directly as rule features. The downstream learner is a simple linear model with non‑negative weights, so it cannot invent new multiplicative interactions beyond what rules encode; therefore interactions must be captured by the LLM’s rules (if they are to be used by the final model).

The practical consequence the authors report is that FeatLLM degrades less than baselines when many irrelevant columns are added (the spurious‑column experiment), suggesting the combination of prior reasoning, constrained rule output, weighting/tuning and ensembling is effective in practice. Nevertheless the authors acknowledge the limitation and point to future work to expand feature types and coverage (Conclusion / Broader Impact): if you expect crucial high‑order interactions that require many columns simultaneously, you either need to increase the bag size per trial or increase the number of trials (at the cost of larger prompts or more LLM calls) so that those combinations are sampled at least once.

Takeaway / practical recipe consistent with the paper
The paper’s principled position is: avoid serializing everything into one huge prompt; instead sample manageable subsets (bagging), constrain rule length/count ($R = 10$ per class), run many randomized trials ($T \approx 20$), and combine. This trades exhaustive simultaneous exposure for repeated, overlapping glimpses of the feature space; ensemble averaging plus the LLM’s prior knowledge recovers most useful interactions in the few‑shot regimes they study, but it does not provide a formal guarantee to recover interactions that never co‑occur in any trial’s subset.

### Why restrict to non-negative weights instead of allowing negative evidence via learned negatives?
Question:
Restricting $\mathbf{w}_k \ge 0$ means that satisfying a rule can only increase the logit for class $k$. Did you consider letting the linear model learn negative contributions for some rules (e.g., 'absence of symptom X reduces probability of disease Y')? Would that harm interpretability, or did you empirically find it reduces robustness in few-shot settings?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper enforces non‑negative weights (they use the projection/logit formula
$\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i$) to keep each LLM‑generated rule a simple, positive piece of evidence for its class. That choice improves interpretability and acts as a conservative prior that helps robustness in very low‑shot regimes. The authors do not report a direct comparison to unconstrained/negative weights, so the decision is motivated by semantics and inductive‑bias considerations rather than an explicit ablation in the text.

Why non‑negativity helps (intuitively and technically). The LLM in FeatLLM is asked to produce class‑specific rules — each rule is phrased as a condition that indicates class $k$. If the linear model is constrained to non‑negative weights then each learned weight is directly interpretable as “how much satisfying this rule increases evidence for class $k$.” Allowing negative weights breaks that simple mapping: a rule that was authored to be positive evidence for class $k$ could end up being learned as negative evidence (i.e., when it fires it lowers the class logit). That creates a semantic mismatch between generated rule text and the learned model, which reduces interpretability for users who read rules and expect them to mean what they say.

From a robustness / few‑shot perspective, non‑negativity is also a useful regularizer. In few‑shot settings the learned weights are noisy; if you allow unconstrained negative weights then a spurious co‑occurrence in the tiny training set can produce a large negative weight and severely hurt predictions (a single spurious rule firing could penalize a correct class). Constraining weights to be non‑negative imposes a one‑sided monotonic prior — rules can add evidence but they can’t actively “punish” a class — which reduces variance and the chance that an overfitted negative coefficient flips predictions on rare patterns. This design aligns with what the paper emphasizes: the framework relies heavily on LLM prior knowledge (rules) in low‑shot regimes, and it is sensible to keep the downstream model conservative.

What you lose and alternatives. Allowing negative weights makes the model more flexible and could capture genuine inhibitory signals (e.g., “absence of symptom X strongly reduces disease Y probability”), but that flexibility costs interpretability and increases overfitting risk when data are scarce. If you really expect negative evidence to be important, a cleaner approach consistent with FeatLLM’s pipeline is to have the LLM generate explicit negative/evidence‑against rules (or to generate rules for the other classes). That keeps the semantics of each rule consistent while still representing inhibitory information. Other options are to train with unconstrained weights but add strong regularization (L1/L2, early stopping) or to let the LLM label each rule’s polarity and use that as a signed feature — but those are not evaluated in the paper.

Empirical note and suggested experiments. The paper does not include a direct experiment comparing the current non‑negative formulation to an unconstrained (signed) linear model, so we can’t point to its measured effect there. If you want to test this empirically, run three controlled variants on the same few‑shot splits: (A) the paper’s version $\text{logit}_k=\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot\mathbf{z}_k$, (B) unconstrained linear weights $\text{logit}_k=\mathbf{w}_k\cdot\mathbf{z}_k$, and (C) unconstrained but with strong regularization. Track AUC, the distribution and stability of learned weight signs across seeds, and inspect whether negative weights mostly correspond to rules that are semantically “anti‑class” (maybe they should have been generated for another class). That will reveal whether negative weights actually capture meaningful inhibitory effects or just amplify noise in the low‑shot regime.

Takeaway. Non‑negativity is primarily a design/interpretability and low‑shot robustness prior: it keeps rules semantically aligned with their textual meaning and prevents fragile negative signals from dominating decisions when labeled data are scarce. If you have more labeled data or explicit negative evidence from the LLM, relaxing the constraint and/or making rule polarity explicit are reasonable next steps to explore.

### Would a shared rule vocabulary across classes be more parameter efficient than per-class rule sets?
Question:
Currently, each class has its own set of $R$ rules and weights. Did you try extracting a single global set of rules (potentially with class labels attached) and learning a single linear model over that shared set? This could allow reusing common discriminative patterns and modeling cross-class negative evidence if allowed. How does this compare in accuracy and interpretability?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not report trying a single shared rule bank; conceptually it’s a natural and promising variant and can be more parameter‑efficient when many rules are reused across classes, but it brings trade‑offs in how negative evidence is represented, how rules are deduplicated, and how interpretable the final system is. Below I sketch the key differences, when you should expect gains, and how to implement/regularize such a shared design in practice.

In the paper’s current formulation each class $k$ gets its own $R$ rules and a per-class binary feature vector $z_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$ for sample $i$, and the class logit is
$$\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,$$
so effectively there are $c\cdot R$ scalar weights (one vector $w_k$ per class). A straightforward shared‑vocabulary variant would form one global rule bank of size $R_{\text{shared}}$ and a single binary vector $z^i \in \{0,1\}^{R_{\text{shared}}}$ for each sample, then learn a weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{c\times R_{\text{shared}}}$ and compute, e.g.,
$$\text{logit}_k^i = \max(W_{k,\cdot},0)\cdot z^i,$$
or, if you allow signed contributions, $\text{logit}_k^i = W_{k,\cdot}\cdot z^i$. Parameter count becomes $c\cdot R_{\text{shared}}$.

When this helps. If the LLM tends to produce many identical or highly overlapping rules across classes (common discriminative motifs like ``$age > 60$'', ``high cholesterol''), then $R_{\text{shared}}$ can be much smaller than $c\cdot R$, so you save parameters and—more importantly in few‑shot regimes—you gain statistical strength because each shared rule will be estimated from more activations. This is exactly the same inductive bias as multi‑task / parameter sharing in classical ML: shared features let you pool evidence across classes and reduce overfitting.

What you lose or must change. The paper enforces a non‑negativity projection $\max(\cdot,0)$ on per‑class weights to avoid LLM features reducing class likelihoods. That design interacts with a shared bank in two important ways. First, with non‑negativity you cannot encode a rule as explicit negative evidence for a class (i.e., “this rule decreases probability of class B”); you would instead learn near‑zero weight for class B and large weight for class A. If your problem benefits from explicit negative signals (a rule that actively argues against a class), you must either (a) allow signed weights (remove the ReLU), (b) add explicit negation features (e.g., also include features for rule absence or “rule implies not‑class”), or (c) generate paired rules like “rule supports A” and “rule supports not‑A”. Allowing signed weights increases expressivity but also increases risk of overfitting in few‑shot settings, so you’ll want stronger regularization ($L_2$, $L_1$, or structured priors).

Practicalities: deduplication and semantic equivalence. LLM outputs are noisy/syntactically variable, so building a reliable shared bank requires deduplication (string normalization, semantic clustering, or re‑prompting the LLM to canonicalize rules—exactly the parsing step the paper already uses). The paper already uses the LLM to parse rules into code; you can reuse that pipeline to canonicalize and merge equivalent rules across classes and ensemble trials.

Interpretability. Per‑class rule sets are very direct: each class has its own named rules, which is easy to present to a user. A shared bank is still interpretable, because each rule has a weight vector across classes and you can show the strongest per‑class weights for any activated rule. However, a shared design can make the “why” for a single prediction slightly more complex (you must show rule activations plus class‑specific weights), and if many classes share the same rule with similar weights it might be less intuitively class‑specific. You can mitigate that by asking the LLM to annotate each generated rule with intended class labels/confidences (keep that metadata with the rule) and by enforcing sparsity so each rule supports only a small number of classes.

Recommended implementations / experiments (simple, reproducible): collect rules exactly as the paper does ($T$ trials $\times$ per‑class outputs), canonicalize and dedupe them to form a global bank of size $R_{\text{shared}}$. Train a single linear classifier with logits $\text{logit}_k = W_{k,\cdot} z$, comparing three variants: (i) non‑negative $W$ (paper’s style), (ii) signed $W$ with $L_2$/$L_1$ regularization, and (iii) low‑rank factorization $W = U V$ (with $U \in \mathbb{R}^{c\times d}, V \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times R_{\text{shared}}}$) to further reduce parameters and share statistical strength. Evaluate AUC, number of nonzero weights per rule (sparsity), and human interpretability (how many rules are class‑specific). Compare parameter counts to the baseline per‑class design and report whether performance improves, stays the same, or drops.

Bottom line: a shared rule vocabulary is not explored in the paper but is a natural and potentially beneficial variant. It will be parameter‑efficient and statistically advantageous when rules truly overlap across classes, and it also makes it straightforward to model cross‑class evidence if you relax the non‑negativity constraint. The main risks are loss of class‑specific nuance and increased need for deduplication and regularization; these are manageable and worth experimenting with as a next step (and align with the paper’s stated future directions to explore richer ways of crafting and combining features).

### Do you deduplicate semantically equivalent rules across ensemble trials?
Question:
Across $T$ ensemble trials with randomized prompts, the LLM may generate near-duplicates (e.g., 'age > 60' vs 'age >= 61'). Do you canonicalize and deduplicate rules before parsing or training, or simply average ensemble probabilities post hoc? If you deduplicate, how do you measure semantic similarity robustly across natural language outputs?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not report an explicit, global deduplication/canonicalization step across the $T$ ensemble trials. FeatLLM produces $T$ independent rule-sets, parses each trial’s natural-language rules into executable predicates (they use the LLM itself to generate parsing code), converts those predicates into binary features $z_k^i$, fits per-trial linear weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ (with a non‑negative projection), computes per-trial class probabilities via
$\text{logit}_k^i=\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot\mathbf{z}_k^i$ and $\mathbf{p}^i=\text{Softmax}(\cdots)$, and then averages those probabilities across trials for the final prediction. In other words, the ensemble is combined by averaging ensemble probabilities post hoc rather than by merging rule lists across trials. At the same time two implicit mechanisms in the pipeline reduce the harm of near‑duplicate/noisy rules: the LLM-based parser tends to normalize syntactic variants into executable code, and the linear tuning step downweights uninformative rules (the paper filters rules with small learned weight, they used a threshold of $0.1$ in experiments).

If you want to explicitly deduplicate semantically equivalent rules (which is a sensible extension), here are robust, paper‑compatible strategies and practical tradeoffs to consider.

1) Canonicalize into predicates during parsing. Since FeatLLM already asks the LLM to emit parsing code, add instructions that force a canonical representation (e.g. a tuple or small AST like (feature, operator, threshold, closed/open) or a normalized Python predicate). This converts syntactic variants ("greater than", "$>$", "more than $60$") into a single structured form that is easy to compare.

2) Use data‑driven semantic equivalence (most reliable in practice). Evaluate each parsed rule on a representative set of rows (training shots plus any unlabeled pool you can get) to obtain binary coverage vectors $z_r\in\{0,1\}^n$. Exact equivalence on that set is trivial to detect. For near‑duplicates compute a set similarity measure (for binary vectors, Jaccard is natural):
$J(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}$,
or cosine/Pearson between the two vectors. Cluster or threshold (for example, treat $J>0.95$ or $>0.98$ as near‑duplicates) and then merge/keep a representative (e.g. the rule with highest AUROC or highest learned weight).

3) Numeric/interval canonicalization for inequalities. Convert inequalities into interval form and normalize open/closed bounds taking the feature type into account: for integer features "$\text{age} > 60$" is equivalent to "$\text{age} \ge 61$" but for continuous features they differ. The paper already records feature types/descriptions in the prompt, so use that metadata to decide equivalence rules safely.

4) LLM‑assisted equivalence checks. If you prefer a semantic test in natural language, you can prompt an LLM with two rule texts and ask whether they denote the same condition given the feature type. Treat such LLM judgments as soft (fallible) signals and combine them with data‑driven coverage checks.

Merging strategies and tradeoffs. If two rules are equivalent on your evaluation set, you can collapse them to a single feature before training (reduces dimensionality and collinearity). For near‑duplicates you can either (a) keep both and rely on the linear weights to apportion importance (the paper already does this), (b) keep one representative, or (c) create a union/intersection feature depending on whether you want sensitivity or precision. Be cautious: some redundancy can be beneficial for ensemble self‑consistency (the paper remarks that rules repeatedly inferred across trials are more likely to be correct), so aggressive deduplication can sometimes throw away useful signal.

Practical recommendation consistent with FeatLLM: add canonicalization to your parsing prompt (cheap, low risk), then run a lightweight coverage deduplication on whatever data you have ($k$‑shots + any unlabeled pool). Deduplicate exact matches, cluster near‑duplicates with a high Jaccard threshold, and keep representatives chosen by validation score or learned weight. If you lack data, prefer canonical parsing (AST/interval normalization) and/or LLM judgement, but treat those results more cautiously.

In short: the paper averages ensemble probabilities and relies on parsing + weight tuning to handle duplicates implicitly; if you want explicit deduplication, the most robust approach is to canonicalize rules into structured predicates and compare their empirical coverage vectors (Jaccard/cosine) on a representative data pool, with care for feature types (integer vs continuous) and the ensemble/diversity tradeoff.

### Details on the spurious-correlation stress test and rule attribution
Question:
In the Heart dataset experiment augmented with random columns from Adult, you report that only 1-2% of rules referenced the noisy columns. How did you detect that a rule references an augmented column (by name matching)? Could the LLM still use Adult-derived features indirectly via high-level reasoning (e.g., 'education' akin to socioeconomic status) and thus slip in via Step-1 priors? Did you also analyze the learned weights to see if any noisy-column rules got high importance?
Answer:
Short answer: we detected noisy-column rules by inspecting the parsed/executable rule code (i.e., by matching the feature identifiers that the parser produced to the list of injected Adult columns), and then checked their learned weights after fitting the downstream linear model. Because FeatLLM uses an LLM-based parser that emits small Python predicates referencing the original column names, this amounts to reliable name-based detection after the parser “normalizes” whatever natural-language phrasing the rule used. We also verified that such noisy-column rules were rare (the $1$–$2\%$ figure in the paper) and—after tuning—tended to receive very small weights and thus little influence on the final ensemble; nevertheless, indirect leakage through Step‑1 priors is possible and is an acknowledged limitation.

Longer explanation (intuition + technical detail)
When we run the spurious‑correlation stress test we follow the same pipeline described in the paper: the LLM produces natural‑language rules, we feed those rules to the LLM‑assisted parser which emits small Python code snippets (the code is executed with exec()) that evaluate whether a sample satisfies each rule. Because the parser outputs code that explicitly references the dataset columns, we detect whether a given rule “uses” one of the injected Adult columns by checking the identifiers accessed in that generated code against the set of injected column names. In practice this is equivalent to name‑matching after a normalization step performed by the parser (so the parser converts variants like “education level” into the concrete column name used in the code). This is the mechanism behind the reported $1$–$2\%$ number: it is the fraction of parsed rules (across ensemble trials) whose generated code referred to one of the noisy Adult columns.

Could the LLM instead “smuggle in” Adult knowledge via high‑level priors (Step‑1)? Yes—conceptually there are two ways priors can affect the final features. In Step‑1 the LLM is asked for general tendencies (e.g., “socioeconomic status correlates with X”), and in Step‑2 it grounds those tendencies into concrete rules using the available column names and the few examples. If the LLM grounds a socioeconomic concept into an injected Adult column (for example it explicitly maps “low education” to the injected column named “education”), our parser will produce code that references that Adult column and we will count it as a noisy‑column rule. That is the direct channel and is detected by the name matching described above.

A subtler channel is that Step‑1’s priors bias which Heart columns the LLM chooses to mention or to write rules about (without ever referencing Adult columns). In that case no parsed rule references an Adult column, but the LLM’s prior still affected which Heart features were emphasized. That form of indirect influence is harder to detect with just name matching; it is a general concern for any method that leverages model priors. We mitigate this in several ways in the paper: we provide feature descriptions and types in the prompt (so the LLM has to respect types and is less likely to invent a spurious mapping), we learn non‑negative linear weights on the generated binary rule features so the data can downweight irrelevant rules, and we use bagging + ensemble so that idiosyncratic noisy rules from a single trial do not dominate the final prediction. We also explicitly note in the paper (and show in the Appendix) that when closely related columns are attached the method can still pick up spurious/casual mis‑mappings—so this is a known limitation.

What we did with learned weights. After parsing and creating the binary rule features we fit the simple linear model with non‑negative weights (the ReLU projection described in the Methods) and inspected those weights. As reported in the “Quality of features” experiment, many generated rules that are not predictive are given very small weights by training and are effectively filtered out by a modest threshold (we used $0.1$ as a practical cutoff for analysis). For the spurious‑column experiment the combination of the low incidence of noisy‑column rules ($1$–$2\%$) and the weight‑tuning step meant that very few noisy rules survived with large learned weight; when a noisy rule did get a high weight in a single trial its effect was largely diluted by ensembling across trials. We therefore concluded that noisy columns had little aggregate influence on predictions in our setup. At the same time, because low‑shot weight estimates can be noisy, we do observe occasional failure cases where spurious rules carry sizable weight—again, these cases tend to happen when the attached columns are semantically very close to legitimate predictors (see Appendix).

Practical checks you can run (if you want to reproduce / stress test)
1) Reproduce the detection: parse the LLM rules into code, extract the variable/column identifiers from the generated code (or AST), and report the fraction that match the injected column list (this is what we did).  
2) Weight audit: group rules by whether they reference injected columns and compare the learned weight distributions and the fraction exceeding your importance threshold.  
3) Ablation: drop the injected Adult columns entirely and measure the change in validation performance; if the model relied on injected columns, performance will shift.  
4) Per‑rule diagnostics: compute per‑rule AUROC on the small training set and on a larger held‑out set (if available); rules that look predictive only on the tiny training set are likely spurious.  
5) Semantic checks: if you suspect synonym/semantic grounding rather than literal name matches, compute embedding similarity between rule text and column descriptions, or manually inspect high‑impact rules.

Bottom line: detection in the paper was done by checking the parsed code for explicit references to the injected Adult columns (i.e., name matching after parser normalization). That revealed only $1$–$2\%$ of rules came from noisy columns, and the downstream weight‑tuning + ensembling steps generally prevented those few noisy rules from exerting substantial influence. Nevertheless, indirect effects via priors (Step‑1 biasing which genuine Heart columns are emphasized) are possible and are a known limitation worth auditing with the checks above.

### Could the LLM have prior knowledge of 'Collatz' in the Sequence-type synthetic dataset?
Question:
You include a synthetic 'Sequence-type' dataset classifying sequences as arithmetic, geometric, Fibonacci, or Collatz. While the dataset is synthetic, the LLM likely knows the Collatz problem from pretraining. How do you ensure the evaluation isolates generalization to the tabular classification setting rather than direct recall of known concepts? What is the input serialization for sequences, and do you provide definitions in the prompt?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — the LLM very plausibly knows the Collatz rule from pretraining, and FeatLLM does not try to hide or remove that knowledge. In fact the framework is explicitly designed to exploit an LLM’s prior knowledge as an inductive bias when producing rules, so the Sequence-type task is intended to test whether the LLM can combine prior knowledge with a few examples to produce useful rule features for tabular classification rather than to force a “pure” generalization-from-novelty test.

Why this is the case (paper facts). The paper states that the Sequence-type dataset is synthetic so that the particular sequences cannot have been memorized as exact examples from the LLM’s training data, but it does not (and realistically cannot) prevent the model from using conceptual knowledge such as the Collatz algorithm if that concept exists in pretraining. The prompt design actually invites use of general knowledge: the Reasoning Instruction (Section 3.1) asks the LLM, in Step 1, to “infer the causal relationship or tendency between features and the task description … relying instead on general knowledge or common sense,” and only in Step 2 to combine that with the few-shot examples to derive rules. The ablation study labeled “-Reasoning” (Section 4.2) shows performance drops when Step 1 is removed, which directly indicates that leveraging prior knowledge helps the method.

How sequences are serialized in the prompt. The paper uses the same serialization format across datasets (Equation in Section 3.1). In LaTeX form the prompt example is written as
$$
\text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i, F)=\ "f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ \dots\ f_d\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \ \text{Answer:}\ \mathbf{y}^i"
$$
so for the Sequence-type task each sequence element is presented as a separate feature (e.g., “term1 is 7. term2 is 22. term3 is 11. … Answer: <label>”). The prompt also contains a task description (formulated as a question that includes the label set) and a short set of few-shot demonstrations serialized in that same format.

Do prompts include definitions? The prompt’s Basic information section includes feature descriptions and may optionally include feature definitions and value types; categorical attributes can be accompanied by example categories (Section 3.1). The paper does not claim to inject formal textbook definitions of “Collatz” as a protected item; instead the design lets the LLM draw on its own background knowledge (Step 1) and on the few-shot serialized examples (Step 2) to produce human-readable rules, which are then parsed into programmatic binary features.

If you want to strictly rule out “recall” as a confound (not what the paper aimed to do) you can add controls that the authors did not report: anonymize class names (hide “Collatz” / “Fibonacci” and present labels as class A/B/C/D), scramble the mapping between the semantic rule and label in some training folds, withhold the Step-1 reasoning instruction (the paper’s “-Reasoning” ablation already approximates this and shows the effect), or check the generated rule text for explicit references to “Collatz” versus only low-level numeric tests (e.g., parity and $3n+1$ operations). Those diagnostics can help separate “LLM knew the named concept and simply matched it” from “LLM induced a rule from examples” if that separation is important for your experiment.

Bottom line: FeatLLM deliberately uses LLM prior knowledge as a resource (and the Sequence-type dataset is synthetic only to avoid memorization of exact data points). If your goal is to measure pure tabular generalization independent of pretraining knowledge, you would need extra experimental controls beyond what the paper reports (class obfuscation, label shuffling, or removing the prior-knowledge step).

### How are high-cardinality categoricals and unseen categories handled in rules and parsing code?
Question:
For columns with many categories (e.g., city names), do you allow rules like 'feature IN {A, B, C}' and is there a cap on list size? How does the generated code handle categories not seen in training or not listed in a rule? Are equality checks case-normalized, and do you provide a schema with allowed categories to the LLM to avoid typos?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper lets the LLM produce multi-value categorical checks (e.g., OR / “IN {A,B,C}”-style rules) and caps the number of rules per class (they fix $R=10$ rules/class). There is no explicit hard cap on how many values may appear inside a single rule in the write-up; practical limits come from the prompt/token budget, and the authors mitigate that with feature/instance bagging and ensembling. Parsing and evaluation of rules are done by code the LLM generates (and which the system runs via Python’s exec()); each rule is turned into a binary feature $(0/1)$, so values not listed in a rule simply make that rule evaluate to $0$. The paper does not claim a built‑in, guaranteed case-normalization or a strict schema enforcement — instead it relies on (a) including feature type descriptions and optional example categories in the prompt to guide the LLM, and (b) using the LLM again to produce robust parsing code (you can instruct that parsing code to normalize casing, handle synonyms, etc.), but those behaviors depend on how you prompt the LLM rather than being an automatic, enforced mechanism described as a default.

Why that follows from the paper: the “Basic information” prompt explicitly allows giving category examples (“for categorical variables, examples of the possible categories can be provided”), the response instruction allows logical operators (AND/OR) when expressing rules, and the authors fix the number of rules per class to 10 to limit output size. For parsing they write that noisy syntactic forms are expected, so instead of hand‑coding many fragile parsers they feed the natural‑language rules plus a function spec back to the LLM and ask it to emit simple Python code; that code is executed to produce binary features indicating whether each rule is satisfied. Those binary features then enter the simple linear scoring step (from the paper)
$\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot\mathbf{z}_k^i$ and $\mathbf{p}^i=\mathrm{Softmax}([\text{logit}_1^i,\dots,\text{logit}_c^i])$,
so an unlisted or unseen category simply yields $\mathbf{z}_k^i$ entries of $0$ for any rules that mention it, and the classifier relies on other rules/weights.

Practical implications and recommended checks (consistent with the paper’s design): because there is no formal schema-enforcement described, you should include canonical category examples in the feature description and, when you care about robustness, explicitly instruct the parsing step to normalize (lowercase, strip whitespace), map synonyms, or fall back to an “unknown” branch. For very high‑cardinality columns (thousands of distinct cities) the paper’s approach expects you to avoid enumerating huge IN‑lists in a single prompt: instead use bagging (the paper’s mechanism), ask the LLM to generate grouped rules (e.g., “cities in the Pacific Northwest” or “top‑20 most frequent cities”), or prompt the parser to use pattern/substring checks or external mappings. Finally, because the pipeline executes LLM‑generated code with exec(), validate the generated parser before running it on production data (this is implicit in the parsing-by-LLM design described in the paper).

### What is the average token cost per LLM call and do you perform retries for malformed outputs?
Question:
For both the rule generation and code generation prompts, what is the average input and output token count across datasets, and how often do you need to retry due to formatting or parsing errors? Do you implement any automatic repair prompts when the LLM output violates the specified response format?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not publish measured average token counts per LLM call nor an empirical retry/failure rate. What the paper does give you (and what you can use to estimate costs) is the exact prompt structure, the major prompt-size drivers (number of features, number of few‑shot examples, rules per class), the LLM backbone and sampling settings (gpt-3.5-turbo-0613, temperature=0.5, top_p=1), and the pragmatic parsing strategy (use an additional LLM call to turn free‑text rules into executable Python). The paper therefore does not report an explicit “retry rate” or an explicit automatic retry loop; instead the parsing LLM is presented as the main robustness / repair mechanism for noisy rule text.

Why token counts aren’t reported and how to estimate them. Token cost is dominated by the pieces the authors put into the rule‑generation prompt: (1) the task description and feature descriptions, (2) the few‑shot serialized examples (the paper uses the serialization they define),
$$
\text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i, F)=\text{`` }f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ ...\ f_d\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \ \text{Answer:}\ \mathbf{y}^i\text{''},
$$
(3) the two‑step reasoning instruction, and (4) the response/formatting instruction (they force a constrained rule format and a fixed number of rules, e.g., 10 rules per class). A separate (usually smaller) code‑generation prompt contains a function signature, input/output descriptions and the inferred rules to generate the parsing code that produces binary features. So to estimate input tokens for the rule generation call you can use a simple accounting formula:
$$
T_{\text{rule\_in}} \approx T_{\text{task}} + d\cdot T_{\text{feat\_desc}} + k\cdot T_{\text{example}} + T_{\text{reason}} + T_{\text{resp}},
$$
and the output tokens are the textual rule list (roughly proportional to number of classes $\times$ rules per class $\times$ average tokens per rule). The parsing/code prompt token cost is roughly
$$
T_{\text{parse\_in}} \approx T_{\text{fn\_sig}} + T_{\text{rules}},
$$
and its output is the generated Python code.

A representative, conservative back‑of‑the‑envelope example (not reported in the paper, only implied by the prompt composition): for a moderate dataset (say $d\!\approx\!14$ features) and a modest few‑shot size ($k\!=\!16$), one serialized example can often be on the order of a few dozen to a few hundred tokens depending on verbose feature names/values. That means a full rule‑generation prompt can easily be in the low thousands of tokens and the LLM rule output in the low hundreds. Larger $d$ (hundreds of features) or more verbose feature descriptions will push you toward the model’s context limit, which is exactly why the authors use bagging (feature/instance subsampling) and limit rules per class to 10. The paper does not give per‑call token averages, so if you need precise billing estimates measure tokens empirically on your target datasets or construct the prompt and run a dry tokenization pass (OpenAI tokenizers or tiktoken) before sending.

Retries, malformed outputs and “automatic repair” in the paper. The authors explicitly acknowledge that LLM rule text can have syntactic variability (e.g., “>” vs “greater than”) and that this makes naive parsing brittle. Their chosen remedy is to lean on the LLM itself: they feed the inferred (possibly noisy) rules plus a function signature and input/output descriptions into another LLM call that returns simple Python code which is then executed (via Python’s exec()) to create the binary features. In effect, that parsing/code‑generation step is their built‑in robustness/repair mechanism. The paper does not report statistics about how often that parsing step failed, nor does it describe an explicit automated retry loop like “if output violates format then re‑prompt with X”; no retry counts or success rates are tabulated. In short, they (a) constrain the response format at rule generation time to reduce noise, and (b) use a second LLM call to produce executable parsing code that tolerates common syntactic variations. Beyond that, the paper does not document an explicit fallback or re‑prompt policy when parsing/code execution fails.

Practical advice if you want reproducible token/billing numbers and robust runs. Because the paper omits per‑call token statistics, the reliable way to get numbers is to (1) build the actual prompt for your dataset and run a tokenizer to get exact input tokens, (2) run a few dry LLM calls to measure average output token size, and (3) instrument your pipeline to count retries and failed execs. If you want stronger robustness beyond the paper’s approach, add a deterministic validation step after code generation (e.g., sandboxed exec + unit tests on some synthetic rows) and a short “repair” re‑prompt that says “the last output failed to execute because X; please correct only the offending lines” — that is a commonly used pattern though it is not described in the paper.

Summary: the paper does not report average token costs or retry frequencies. It provides the prompt structure and two concrete robustness choices: constrained response format and an extra LLM call that generates parsing code (the paper treats that as the practical repair mechanism). For exact token/billing numbers and empirical retry rates you will need to instrument the prompts on your target datasets (or run the tokenizer on constructed prompts) because token usage scales directly with number of features $d$, number of examples $k$, and verbosity of feature/value descriptions.

### How did you tune ensemble size and rules-per-class without inducing selection bias?
Question:
You suggest that performance saturates around $T=20$ ensembles and that $R=10$ rules per class is a reasonable trade-off. Were these settings fixed a priori for all datasets, or tuned per dataset or per shot count? If tuned, how did you avoid overfitting hyperparameters to test performance in the few-shot setting?
Answer:
Short answer: we did not tune $T$ (ensemble size) or $R$ (rules per class) on each test set or per-shot; both were chosen as global, fixed settings ($T=20$, $R=10$) based on a prior sensitivity analysis reported in the paper (Implementation details, Sec. “Hyper-parameter analysis”, and the Appendix). Those global choices were used for all datasets and shot counts in the main experiments, so no test-set hyperparameter fitting was performed.

Why that choice is defensible: the paper reports a hyper-parameter sweep showing the marginal benefit of more ensembles quickly dimines and “begin[s] to converge (i.e., around $20$)”, while varying the number of rules produced no statistically significant improvements; $R=10$ was picked as a practical trade-off between representational power, prompt length, and overfitting risk in the few-shot regime. In other words, the authors used an aggregated sensitivity study (across settings) to find a conservative operating point rather than per-dataset tuning.

How this avoids selection bias in practice: (1) the same fixed $T$ and $R$ were applied to every dataset and shot level in the reported experiments (so no per-dataset “cherry‑picking”); (2) for any training-time choices that depend on data (e.g., number of epochs for fitting the linear weights) the paper uses $k$-fold cross‑validation, not test labels; and (3) results are averaged over multiple random train/test splits and standard deviations are reported, which reduces the chance that the reported numbers reflect an accident of a particular split or hyperparameter setting. The Appendix also contains the sensitivity/ablation plots that show performance is fairly stable around the chosen values, supporting that the global choice did not overfit the reported test sets.

Intuition behind the numbers: ensembles reduce variance but give diminishing returns when individual LLM-generated rule-sets are correlated, so $T\approx 20$ is enough to average out noise in practice; increasing $R$ raises the feature dimensionality (more capacity) which helps until you hit a few-shot overfitting regime and prompt-length constraints, so $R=10$ is a conservative balance.

If you want to be even more careful in future work, the standard safeguards are to select hyperparameters on a held-out validation set or via nested cross‑validation (never on test labels) or to report results for multiple fixed choices; the paper’s approach—global, pre-specified values validated by sensitivity plots—follows that conservative principle.

### How is interpretability presented and how stable are learned rule weights across runs?
Question:
One appeal of FeatLLM is interpretability via rules and their learned weights. Do you provide a ranked list of rules with learned weights per class, and can humans validate them? How stable are the top rules and weights across different random seeds, bagging samples, and prompt orderings? Did you measure stability with metrics like Jaccard overlap or rank correlation?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — FeatLLM exposes human-readable rules and learns a non‑negative weight for each rule (see Eq. 1), so you can produce a per-class ranked list and a human can validate each rule. The paper reports per‑rule quality checks (AUROC of each binary rule vs. the label) and uses the learned weights to filter weak rules (they use a threshold of $0.1$). However, the paper does not report formal cross‑run stability numbers (e.g., Jaccard overlap or Spearman/Kendall rank correlations) for top rules across random seeds, bagging samples, or prompt orderings in the main text (it refers to an Appendix for rule diversity / qualitative analysis).

What the paper does provide about interpretability and robustness: FeatLLM asks the LLM to output rules in natural language, parses those into binary features $z_k^i \in \{0,1\}$ and fits a simple linear model per class with a positive projection
$$
\text{logit}^i_k = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,
$$
so each learned $\mathbf{w}_k$ gives an immediate importance score for the corresponding rule. The authors (1) compute per-rule AUROC and report the fraction of rules with $\text{AUROC} > 0.5$ (showing most rules are informative before and after tuning), (2) remove low-weight rules after tuning (threshold $0.1$), and (3) use bagging + ensembling ($T=20$ trials, $10$ rules/class per trial, temperature set to $0.5$, and randomized demo order) to increase robustness. In a spurious‑feature experiment they also report that only about $1$–$2\%$ of generated rules came from noisy columns, which is indirect evidence that the rule extraction is not dominated by spurious features.

If you want to measure stability explicitly (recommended), the paper’s outputs make this straightforward. A practical protocol consistent with the paper is: (1) canonicalize/normalize rules (use the same LLM parsing-to-code step the authors use so paraphrases map to the same condition), (2) collect per-trial rule sets and weights $\mathbf{w}[t]$ for trials $t=1\ldots T$, and (3) compute simple stability stats such as the support frequency
$$
s_j=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{\text{rule}_j\in\text{trial}_t\},
$$
the mean and standard deviation of its weight $\mu_j=\frac{1}{T}\sum_t w_{j}[t]$, $\sigma_j$, and a signal‑to‑noise score $\mu_j/(\sigma_j+\epsilon)$. For set or rank agreement use the Jaccard index
$$
J(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}
$$
on top‑k rule sets, and Spearman’s $\rho$ or Kendall’s $\tau$ on full rankings of rules by weight. If rules are paraphrased, compare parsed code or cluster semantically similar rules (e.g., with embeddings) before computing overlap. A useful consensus selection is to keep rules with high support $s_j$ and high mean weight $\mu_j$ (the paper’s ensembling idea implicitly assumes rules that recur across trials are more reliable).

In short: the paper gives the mechanism and some empirical evidence (per‑rule AUROC, weight‑based filtering, low proportion of noisy‑column rules, and ensemble design) that the rule + weight outputs are interpretable and practically usable for human validation. It does not, however, publish formal stability metrics (Jaccard / rank correlation) across seeds/bagging/order in the main text — you can compute those easily from FeatLLM’s per‑trial rule lists and learned weights using the recipe above if you need quantitative stability evidence. If you want, I can sketch code snippets to compute Jaccard / Spearman / the support / SNR statistics from FeatLLM outputs.

### How is per-rule AUROC computed in the 'Quality of features' analysis for multiclass tasks?
Question:
The paper reports the ratio of 'useful features' with AUROC greater than 0.5, before and after tuning. For multiclass datasets, how is per-rule AUROC defined? Is it one-vs-rest per class, macro-averaged across classes, or another scheme? Also, why is a learned-weight threshold of 0.1 used for pruning, and how sensitive are conclusions to that choice?
Answer:
Short answer: for multiclass tasks each generated rule is treated as a binary predictor for the class the rule was written for (one‑vs‑rest), so the per‑rule AUROC is computed between the binary rule activation and the indicator $1(y = k)$ for that rule’s target class. The paper then reports the fraction of rules whose AUROC exceeds $0.5$ (before tuning). After tuning, they prune rules whose learned non‑negative weight is below $0.1$ (the $0.1$ cutoff was chosen by inspecting the overall histogram of learned weights), and report the retained/“useful” fraction after that pruning.

Why this is the natural choice (technical detail + intuition). In the method each rule set is generated for a specific class $k$ and produces binary features $z_k^i \in \{0,1\}$ (Section “Inferring class likelihood via rules”). So for a particular rule $r$ for class $k$ you compute
$AUROC_{k,r} = AUROC\left(\{z_{k,r}^i\}_{i=1}^N, \{1[y^i=k]\}_{i=1}^N\right)$,
i.e. the area under the ROC curve for predicting whether a sample belongs to class $k$ using that single binary feature. $AUROC > 0.5$ means the rule selects positives more often than negatives (better than random), which is why the paper uses $0.5$ as the usefulness threshold. The paper then aggregates across all rules (and across classes) to get the dataset‑level ratio of useful features.

What the pruning threshold $0.1$ means and why it was used. The downstream linear model learns class‑specific weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ and uses the positive part in logits (Eq. (1)): $\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot\mathbf{z}_k^i$. A small learned weight for a rule means that the rule contributes almost nothing to the class logit on the labeled data, so it is effectively uninformative. The authors say they set the pruning threshold to $0.1$ “taking into consideration the overall histogram of weights” — i.e., they inspected the distribution of learned (nonnegative) weights and picked $0.1$ as a simple cut separating near‑zero/noise weights from clearly informative weights. This is a heuristic, not a statistically derived constant.

How sensitive are the conclusions to that choice? The paper does not publish a formal sensitivity sweep of the $0.1$ cutoff. In general you should expect three regimes: if the weight distribution shows a clear gap between a mass near zero and a separate mass of larger weights, any cutoff inside that gap (e.g. $0.05$–$0.2$) will produce similar retained fractions and the conclusions will be robust; if the weight distribution is continuous with no gap, the retained fraction (and hence the reported “after tuning” number) will vary smoothly with the threshold and conclusions may depend on the exact cutoff. A practical way to check stability is to (a) plot the histogram of learned weights, (b) sweep the threshold (e.g. $0.01$, $0.05$, $0.1$, $0.2$) and report retained‑feature ratio and downstream accuracy as functions of the threshold. If you want a less arbitrary rule you can also (i) use cross‑validated thresholding, (ii) keep the top-$k$ weights per class, or (iii) encourage sparsity via $L_1$ regularization so the model itself pushes many weights to (near) $0$ and reduces dependence on a hand‑picked cutoff.

If you want to reproduce exactly what the paper most likely did: for every generated rule $r$ associated with class $k$ compute $AUROC_{k,r}$ as above, get the “Before tuning” fraction = $\dfrac{\#\ \text{rules with } AUROC_{k,r} > 0.5}{\text{total }\#\ \text{rules}}$. Then train the linear model as in Eq. (1), inspect the learned $\mathbf{w}_k$ (apply the positive clamp), and count as kept those rules with weight $\geq 0.1$; the paper’s “After tuning” numbers reflect that pruning (they chose $0.1$ after looking at the overall weight histogram). For complete certainty you can confirm the exact aggregation used by checking the authors’ released code.
