\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### What does "information-theoretically optimal" mean for NF4 quantization of normally distributed weights?
Question:
In the QLoRA paper, NF4 is described as an "information theoretically optimal" data type for zero-mean normal distributions, achieved by ensuring each quantization bin has an equal expected number of values. What exactly is the optimality criterion here? For example, is it (a) maximizing entropy of the quantized codes, (b) minimizing expected distortion under a fixed-rate or variable-rate code (rate–distortion optimality), or (c) maximizing mutual information between original and quantized values? Also, how does this claim relate to the classic Lloyd–Max MSE-optimal scalar quantizer for a Gaussian, which does not in general yield equal-probability bins?
Answer:
Short answer: in QLoRA/NF4 the phrase “information‑theoretically optimal” refers to choosing quantizer bins so that, under the assumed source distribution (zero‑mean Gaussian), each of the $2^k$ output symbols has equal probability. For a deterministic $k$-bit quantizer this is equivalent to maximizing the discrete entropy of the quantized symbol and therefore to maximizing the mutual information between the continuous weight and its $k$-bit code. It is not the same as Lloyd–Max (MSE) optimality: NF4 optimizes an information (entropy / mutual information) criterion, not a mean‑squared‑error rate–distortion criterion.

Why that statement is true (math + intuition). Let $X$ be a continuous weight variable and let $Q(X)$ take $L = 2^k$ discrete levels. Put $p_i = \Pr(Q(X)=i) = \int_{\mathrm{bin}_i} f_X(x)\,dx$. The entropy of the quantized symbol is
$$
H(Q) = -\sum_{i=1}^{L} p_i \log p_i \le \log L,
$$
with equality iff $p_i = 1/L$ for all $i$. Because $Q$ is a deterministic function of $X$, mutual information satisfies
$$
I(X;Q)=H(Q)-H(Q\mid X)=H(Q),
$$
so maximizing $H(Q)$ is the same as maximizing $I(X;Q)$. Thus an equiprobable (quantile) partition attains the maximum possible mutual information for any deterministic $L$-level scalar quantizer. That is exactly the construction described in the paper: NFk uses the quantiles of the standard normal to form equal‑probability bins and then normalizes into the storage range. The paper writes the representative levels as
$$
q_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\Big( Q_X\!\big(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1}\big) + Q_X\!\big(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1}\big)\Big),
$$
where $Q_X$ is the $N(0,1)$ quantile function; this yields bins with equal expected occupancy under the Gaussian model.

How this differs from Lloyd–Max / rate–distortion optimality. The Lloyd–Max quantizer (or the more general rate–distortion solution for a given distortion measure) minimizes expected distortion, e.g. for MSE
$$
D = \mathbb{E}[(X-\widehat{X})^2]
$$
over partitions and reconstruction points. The necessary conditions are centroid and boundary conditions (reconstruction points are conditional means inside each cell, boundaries at the points where assignment to adjacent centroids is equally costly). For a Gaussian source the MSE‑optimal bins generally do not have equal probability; they are chosen to reduce squared error, which concentrates more fine resolution where the density and squared‑error sensitivity demand it. So NF4 and Lloyd–Max solve different optimization problems and will in general produce different partitions and reconstruction points.

Why QLoRA authors prefer the quantile / “information‑theoretic” choice in practice. The paper’s motivation is practical: pretrained network weights are close to zero‑mean normal, so precomputing normal quantiles removes the expensive per‑block quantile estimation and gives guaranteed equal expected occupancy of the 4‑bit codebook. Equal occupancy avoids “wasting” code levels on empty bins (common when outliers skew an absmax rescale) and preserves tail/outlier values by giving them dedicated bins. Empirically this choice (NF4) outperforms simple 4‑bit floats/ints for LLM weights in QLoRA, even though it is not MSE‑optimal. Note also that the NF4 construction uses fixed‑length 4‑bit storage; if the goal were compression with variable‑length codes one would typically want non‑uniform $p_i$ so that frequent symbols get shorter codes (a different criterion).

Summary recommendation. Interpret “information‑theoretically optimal” in the paper as (a) maximizing entropy of the $k$-bit quantized symbols (and because the mapping is deterministic this is equivalent to (c) maximizing mutual information). It is not claiming rate–distortion / MSE optimality (choice (b) would point to Lloyd–Max), and the two objectives trade off differently in practice. The QLoRA paper chooses the information‑maximizing equipprobable quantiles because of the Gaussian weight model, computational convenience, and the empirical benefit for LLM weights.

### Does NF4 use absmax scaling or standard-deviation scaling? Reconciling two statements in the paper
Question:
The NF4 section says to normalize weights into the range $[-1,1]$ with absolute max rescaling, but it also says "Step (3) is equivalent to rescaling the standard deviation of the weight tensor to match the standard deviation of the $k$-bit data type." Absmax scaling is not the same as scaling by standard deviation. Which rescaling does NF4 actually employ in practice for the first-level quantization of $\mathbf{W}$: absmax to $[-1,1]$, matching standard deviations, or something else? If it is absmax, in what sense is Step (3) "equivalent" to rescaling $\sigma$?
Answer:
Short answer: NF4 uses block-wise absolute-maximum (absmax) rescaling in practice. The paper’s statement that “Step (3) is equivalent to rescaling the standard deviation” is a heuristic/explanatory remark: for (approximately) Gaussian, zero-mean weight blocks the block absmax is proportional to the block standard deviation, so dividing by the absmax has the same role as dividing by $\sigma$ up to a block-dependent multiplicative constant. In other words, absmax-scaling removes the per-block scale (the $\sigma$ factor) and therefore “matches” the scale that the NFk table expects.

Why this is true (quick derivation). Write a weight block as $W = \sigma Z$ with $Z \sim N(0,1)^B$ ($B$ = block size). Let $M = \max_i |W_i|$ be the block absmax; then
$$M = \sigma \cdot S_B,\quad S_B := \max_i |Z_i|.$$
The NF4 implementation uses the quantization constant
$$c = 1/M = 1/(\sigma S_B),$$
and the scaled block is
$$c W = \frac{W}{M} = \frac{Z}{S_B}.$$
So after absmax normalization the scaled block has standard deviation $1/S_B$ (a constant that depends on the particular sample of unit normals in the block) and no longer depends on the original $\sigma$. If instead you explicitly divided by $\sigma$ (i.e. standardize to unit normal) and then multiplied by whatever fixed factor is needed so the NFk levels sit in $[-1,1]$, you would be doing the same kind of multiplicative rescaling — the only difference is whether the multiplier is estimated via the block max (absmax) or via $\sigma$.

What the paper means by “equivalent” is therefore operational: both procedures align the block’s scale to the fixed scale that the NFk (quantiles computed from $N(0,1)$ and normalized into $[-1,1]$) assumes. The identity is not exact elementwise (absmax produces a block-dependent scale $1/S_B$, whereas dividing by $\sigma$ would give a deterministic mapping to unit variance), but under the normal-weight assumption $\mathrm{absmax} \propto \sigma$ so the two approaches have the same effect in expectation.

Why absmax in practice? The paper explicitly chooses absmax/blockwise range-matching (same pattern as common k-bit quantization) because it guarantees the full dynamic range $[-1,1]$ is used and is cheap to compute. Block size controls the behavior: $S_B$ (and hence the post-scale standard deviation $1/S_B$) depends on $B$ — for large $B$ the expected max grows like $\sqrt{2\log B}$ — so the choice of block size is part of the design tradeoff between robustness to outliers and effective use of bins.

So: NF4’s implemented first-level quantization = blockwise absmax-to-$[-1,1]$. The “equivalent to rescaling $\sigma$” sentence is a correct conceptual shorthand under the normality assumption, meaning absmax-scaling removes the per-block multiplicative scale (the $\sigma$) so the block’s variance is put on the fixed scale that NFk expects.

### How are NF4 bin centers $q_i$ computed in practice, and how is an exact zero included with $k=4$?
Question:
The paper defines $q_i = \tfrac{1}{2}(Q_X(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1}) + Q_X(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1}))$ for a standard normal $N(0,1)$, then mentions creating an asymmetric data type so that zero is an exactly representable value. Could someone spell out the exact procedure for $k=4$? Specifically: (1) which quantiles on the negative and positive sides are used, (2) how the duplicate zero is handled, and (3) what the final ordered list of 16 level values $\{q_i\}$ is after normalization to $[-1,1]$? A small numeric table (or a pointer to Appendix values) would be helpful.
Answer:
Short answer: NF4 centers are computed from the inverse CDF (quantile) of a standard normal, taken as midpoints of equal‑probability quantile intervals, and then the quantization is made asymmetric so that $0$ is exactly representable. Concretely for $k=4$ the authors (1) form two sets of quantile‑midpoint centers (one for the negative side and one for the positive side) with counts $8$ and $9$ respectively, (2) union those two sets and remove the duplicate $0$, and (3) rescale the whole $16$‑entry table so the largest magnitude center becomes $\pm 1$. The paper gives the exact numeric table in its appendix; the bitsandbytes/QLoRA implementation has the same constants in code.

Longer, but still concise, explanation (so you can reproduce it exactly)

1) How the raw centers are computed. Let $Q(p)$ be the quantile (inverse CDF) of $N(0,1)$. The generic quantile‑quantization midpoint for a single bin with probability interval $(p,p')$ is computed in value space as
$$
\frac{1}{2}\big(Q(p)+Q(p')\big).
$$
For a symmetric, equal‑mass $2^k$-level quantile quantizer one would normally partition the CDF into $2^k$ equal pieces and take mid‑values of adjacent quantile boundaries. The QLoRA paper writes this midpoint formula explicitly (their Equation for $q_i$).

2) Why and how zero is made exact. Taking the symmetric equal‑mass partition yields $2^k$ centers but none of them typically equals exactly $0$ (because $0$ corresponds to $\text{CDF}=0.5$ and the midpoint probabilities do not land exactly at $0.5$). Because exact $0$ is very useful in practice (padding, sparse values, exact zero point), the authors make a small asymmetric tweak: they compute two sets of midpoints separately and then merge them so that one of the computed midpoints is exactly $Q(0.5)=0$ and thus becomes an exact representable level. Concretely for $k=4$ they compute one set of $8$ centers for the negative side and one set of $9$ centers for the positive side (these come from two different equal‑partitionings of the CDF so that one of the positive‑side midpoints falls exactly at $0.5$). They then take the union of those $8+9=17$ raw centers and drop the duplicated $0$, leaving $16$ distinct centers with $0$ represented exactly once.

3) Final normalization. After computing the $16$ centers (values in normal‑distribution units) the whole vector is scaled (divided) by the maximum absolute center value so that the largest magnitude center becomes exactly $+1$ (and the smallest becomes $-1$). That produces the NF4 level set with range $[-1,1]$ which the quantizer stores as the $k$-bit codebook; tensors are rescaled into $[-1,1]$ by their absolute max then quantized to the nearest NF4 center and stored.

Putting that together for $k=4$ (how you would implement it)

- Compute the two sets of raw centers from the standard normal quantile function as described in the paper (the paper’s text gives the counting: $2^{k-1}$ centers for the negative part and $2^{k-1}+1$ for the positive part).  
- Merge and remove the duplicate $0$.  
- Divide every center by the maximum absolute center to map into $[-1,1]$.

Exact numeric values
The paper publishes the exact NF4 center table in the Appendix (Appendix referenced as "app:nf4" in the paper). That appendix contains the canonical $16$ values after normalization; the same numbers are shipped with the bitsandbytes / QLoRA code (so you can inspect the implementation to see the exact floats). If you want to avoid re‑implementing the quantile math you can copy the listed $16$ constants from the paper’s Appendix (or from the bitsandbytes source) and use them directly.

If you want, I can (a) reproduce the appendix numeric table here verbatim, or (b) give a tiny runnable snippet (pseudo‑code) showing exactly which CDF arguments to call and in which order to produce the $8$ and $9$ intermediate centers for $k=4$ so you can recompute the table yourself. Which would you prefer?

### How does QLoRA backpropagate through quantized weights without using a straight-through estimator?
Question:
Backpropagating through quantization is usually tricky because rounding is non-differentiable. The paper says QLoRA dequantizes the 4-bit weights to BF16 and then performs the matrix multiply, and that only LoRA adapter parameters receive gradients. Can someone clarify the gradient path mathematically? Given $\mathbf{Y}^{\text{BF16}} = \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}} \cdot \text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}) + \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}} \mathbf{L}_1^{\text{BF16}} \mathbf{L}_2^{\text{BF16}}$, are $c_1$, $c_2$, and $\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}$ treated as constants so that gradients do not flow through the dequantization at all, and the only trainable path is through $\mathbf{L}_1, \mathbf{L}_2$?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — in QLoRA the quantized storage (the NF4 integers) and the quantization constants (the double-quantized c's) are treated as frozen constants. The dequantization is used to produce a BF16 weight matrix for the numerical forward/backward computations, but the autograd graph does not propagate parameter gradients back into the quantized storage or into the quantization constants. Only the LoRA adapter parameters receive gradients and are updated.

Why, mathematically. Write the forward for one linear layer (paper's Eq. (5)) as
$$
\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\,W_{\mathrm{deq}} + \mathbf{X}\,L_1 L_2,
\qquad W_{\mathrm{deq}}=\text{doubleDequant}(c_1,c_2,W^{\text{NF4}}).
$$
Let the scalar loss be $E(\mathbf{Y})$ and define $G=\partial E/\partial \mathbf{Y}$ (shape $b\times o$). Standard matrix calculus gives the gradients needed for updating the LoRA factors:
$$
\frac{\partial E}{\partial L_2} = (X L_1)^\top G,\qquad
\frac{\partial E}{\partial L_1} = X^\top (G L_2^\top).
$$
The backward also produces the activation gradient
$$
\frac{\partial E}{\partial X} = G W_{\mathrm{deq}}^\top + G (L_1 L_2)^\top,
$$
which uses the numerical values of the dequantized weights.

If one were to update the base weights, the gradient (w.r.t. the dequantized weights) would be
$$
\frac{\partial E}{\partial W_{\mathrm{deq}}} = X^\top G,
$$
and then a chain rule through the dequantization mapping would be required to get gradients for $W^{\text{NF4}}$, $c_1$, $c_2$. That chain would hit non‑differentiable rounding steps and would need an approximation (e.g. a straight‑through estimator or another differentiable surrogate). QLoRA does not do that because the base model is frozen.

Practical/implementation point. In frameworks such as PyTorch the quantized tensors and quantization constants are stored as non‑trainable buffers; dequantization is executed to produce a BF16 tensor that is used in matmuls but is not a leaf requiring gradients. Autograd therefore computes gradients only for tensors marked requires_grad=True (the LoRA parameters and any activations), and it does not try to backpropagate into the quantized storage or into the double‑quantization ops. In other words, the dequantization affects the numeric values used in the backward (it enters formulas like the ones above), but it is not itself a differentiable path that updates the quantized representation.

Intuition / bigger picture. QLoRA gets away without an STE because it never tries to change the quantized representation: the frozen 4‑bit model is only "read" (dequantized) for computation, and the small LoRA adapters absorb the necessary corrections. If you wanted to finetune the quantized base weights themselves, you would need a differentiable treatment of quantization (or an STE or special kernels like SwitchBack) — QLoRA avoids that complexity by design.

### During QLoRA finetuning, are the 4-bit base weights and their quantization constants fixed or updated?
Question:
The paper repeatedly states the base model is "frozen" and quantized to 4-bit, with only LoRA adapters being trained. Does "frozen" here mean that both $\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}$ and the associated quantization constants (the first-level $c_2$ and the second-level $c_1$) are kept fixed throughout training and not recomputed? Or are the scales re-estimated at any point during finetuning (e.g., per-epoch)? Clarifying exactly which quantities remain constant would help me reason about how the adapters compensate for quantization error.
Answer:
Short answer: In QLoRA as presented the quantized base (the 4‑bit stored codes) and all quantization constants are fixed once you quantize the pretrained weights. You precompute and store $ \mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}} $ and the double‑quantization constants (the per‑block first‑level scale $c_2$ and the second‑level constants $c_1$) and do not re‑estimate or update them during finetuning — only the LoRA adapter parameters are learned.

Why — technical grounding from the paper: the forward for a linear layer is written as
$$
\mathbf{Y}^{\text{BF16}} = \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\;\text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}},c_2^{\text{k‑bit}},\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}})+\mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_1^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_2^{\text{BF16}},
$$
and doubleDequant is the deterministic, precomputed dequantization that reconstructs a BF16 weight from the stored 4‑bit codes and their stored scales (Eqns (5)–(6) in the paper). QLoRA dequantizes those stored buffers to the computation dtype at every forward/backward, and backpropagates through the dequantized values only so that gradients flow into the adapter parameters $\mathbf{L}_i$. The paper explicitly notes that “for parameter updates only the gradient with respect to the error for the adapters weights $\partial E/\partial \mathbf{L}_i$ are needed, and not for 4‑bit weights $\partial E/\partial \mathbf{W}$.” In practice the quantized weights and both levels of quantization constants are kept as fixed buffers (not trainable parameters) and are not recomputed per epoch.

Intuition about compensation: because the base model is stored in a fixed, quantized form, the adapters learn an additive, low‑rank correction: the effective linear map becomes
$$
\underbrace{\mathbf{W}_{\text{dequant}}}_{\text{fixed}} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2}_{\text{learned low‑rank delta}}.
$$
LoRA therefore can absorb systematic bias or precision loss introduced by quantization by learning a (usually small) low‑rank residual that corrects the dequantized weights. This is different from quantization‑aware training (QAT), where one would update weights or scales while modeling quantization during optimization; QLoRA keeps the quantization fixed and relies on adapters to recover fidelity.

Practical note: nothing in the QLoRA paper describes re‑estimating $c_1$ or $c_2$ during finetuning. If you wanted a variant that adapts scales, you would be moving toward a QAT variant (you would need to recompute scales and/or update underlying weights and stored codes), which is not what the authors evaluated.

### Deriving the 0.373 bits/parameter saving from Double Quantization with $B_W=64$ and $B_{c_2}=256$
Question:
In the Double Quantization section, the paper claims a reduction from $32/64 = 0.5$ bits/parameter (first-level scales stored in FP32 per 64 weights) down to $8/64 + 32/(64\cdot256) = 0.127$ bits/parameter, i.e., a savings of 0.373 bits/parameter. Could someone walk through this derivation step by step? My current understanding is: (1) $\mathbf{W}$ is grouped in blocks of 64, each block uses one scale $c_2$; (2) those $c_2$ values are themselves grouped in blocks of 256 and quantized to FP8 with one FP32 scale $c_1$ per 256 $c_2$ values; (3) total overhead becomes $8$ bits for each $c_2$ amortized over 64 weights ($8/64$) plus $32$ bits for each $c_1$ amortized over $256\times 64$ weights ($32/(256\cdot64)$). Is this the intended logic, and does any additional metadata need to be accounted for?
Answer:
Yes — your understanding is exactly the intended logic in the paper. Here is the step‑by‑step derivation (with the small implementation caveats at the end).

Start from the usual single‑level setup: weights are quantized in blocks of size $B_W=64$ and each block stores one FP32 scale. That is 32 bits per $B_W$ weights, so the scale overhead (before double quantization) is
$$
\frac{32}{B_W} = \frac{32}{64} = 0.5\ \text{bits/parameter}.
$$

With Double Quantization the first‑level scales $c_2$ themselves are quantized to 8 bits (FP8) and the $c_2$ values are grouped in blocks of $B_{c_2}=256$ to share a single second‑level FP32 constant $c_1$. So the amortized overhead per weight has two terms:

1. one FP8 $c_2$ per weight‑block: $\dfrac{8}{B_W}=\dfrac{8}{64}=0.125$ bits/parameter, and  
2. one FP32 $c_1$ per $B_{c_2}$ of those $c_2$ values, i.e. per $B_{c_2}\times B_W$ weights:
$$
\frac{32}{B_W\cdot B_{c_2}}=\frac{32}{64\cdot 256}=\frac{32}{16384}=\frac{1}{512}\approx 0.001953125\ \text{bits/parameter}.
$$

Add them:
$$
\frac{8}{64}+\frac{32}{64\cdot256}=0.125+0.001953125=0.126953125\approx 0.127\ \text{bits/parameter}.
$$

The saving vs the original single‑level overhead is
$$
0.5 - 0.126953125 = 0.373046875 \approx 0.373\ \text{bits/parameter},
$$
(which as exact fractions is $ \tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{65}{512}=\tfrac{191}{512} $).

A quick sanity check in bytes for a 65B parameter model: saved bits are $0.373046875\times 65\times 10^9$ bits $\approx 2.4248\times 10^{10}$ bits, i.e. $\approx 3.03\times 10^9$ bytes $\approx 3.03$ GB — matching the paper’s “about 3 GB” statement.

Implementation caveats / metadata: the paper’s arithmetic counts only the stored scale words (FP8 for the $c_2$s and FP32 for the $c_1$s). In practice you might also need to store tiny extra metadata per block: e.g. a per‑group mean/offset (if you subtract the mean per group and don’t fold it into the stored scale), explicit zero‑points, padding/alignment, or layer/block shape headers. If you did have to store an extra FP32 mean per $B_{c_2}$ of $c_2$s, that would add another $\dfrac{32}{64\cdot 256}=\dfrac{1}{512}$ bits/parameter (the same magnitude as the $c_1$ term), but the paper’s formula assumes those extra items are not stored separately or are folded into the same FP32 values being counted. Those metadata costs are therefore implementation‑specific and very small compared to the total model size.

Summary: your three bullet points are correct; the arithmetic in the paper follows exactly that logic and yields the quoted $0.373$ bits/parameter saving.

### What FP8 format and dequantization rule are used for $c_2$, and where is the mean stored after mean-centering?
Question:
The paper says the first-level quantization scales $c_2$ are mean-centered (since they are positive) and then quantized to 8-bit float with block size 256, and that $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$ is used to dequantize $c_2^{\text{FP8}}$. Which FP8 format is used (e.g., E4M3, E5M2), and what is the exact dequantization formula? Also, after subtracting the mean from $c_2$ values to make them symmetric around zero, where is that mean stored so it can be added back during dequantization? Is that mean included in $c_1$, stored separately per block, or is it a single global mean?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not specify which FP8 layout (E4M3 vs E5M2) is used — it only says “8‑bit Floats with a blocksize of 256” for the second-level quantization. The dequantization is exactly the two-step inverse used in the paper (their doubleDequant): first recover the first-level scale(s) $c_2$ from the FP8 representation using the second-level scale $c_1$, then use those recovered $c_2$ to dequantize the 4‑bit (NF4) weights. Because the authors mean‑center the $c_2$ values before FP8 quantization, you must add the stored mean back after the first dequant step; the natural (and memory-consistent) interpretation is that the mean is stored per second‑level block (i.e. per 256 $c_2$ values), not as a single global scalar.

More explicitly (following the paper’s notation and Eqns. in the text): group the weight blocks (each weight block has its first‑level scale $c_{2,b}^{\text{FP32}}$). For a second‑level block $g$ (size 256) compute the block mean $\mu_g$ and define centered constants $\tilde c_{2,b} = c_{2,b}^{\text{FP32}} - \mu_g$. Those centered values are quantized to FP8 producing $c_{2,b}^{\text{FP8}}$, and a second‑level scale $c_{1,g}^{\text{FP32}}$ is stored for the group. At dequantization you do

$$\tilde c_{2,b} = \text{dequant}\big(c_{1,g}^{\text{FP32}},\, c_{2,b}^{\text{FP8}}\big)$$   (recover centered $c_2$)  
$$c_{2,b}^{\text{FP32}} = \tilde c_{2,b} + \mu_g$$   (add the mean back)  
$$W_{b}^{\text{BF16}} = \text{dequant}\big(c_{2,b}^{\text{FP32}},\, W_{b}^{\text{4bit}}\big)$$   (recover block weights)

The paper uses the same dequant semantics as their earlier simple linear example, i.e. for linear/symmetric scale quantization the dequant is division by the scale. Concretely, in the simple linear case you can write the inner steps as (illustrative scalar form)

if $q_{2,b} = \text{round}\big(s_{1,g}\cdot (c_{2,b}^{\text{FP32}}-\mu_g)\big)$ with $s_{1,g}=c_{1,g}^{\text{FP32}}$, then
$c_{2,b}^{\text{FP32}} = q_{2,b}/s_{1,g} + \mu_g$,

and if the first quantization used a scale $s_{2,b}=c_{2,b}^{\text{FP32}}$ so that $q_{W,b} = \text{quant}(s_{2,b}\cdot W_b)$, then
$W_b = \text{inv\_quant}(q_{W,b})/s_{2,b}$.

Two practical notes: (1) for NF4 the “inv_quant” step is the NF4 lookup/inverse mapping rather than a plain integer divide, but the paper’s doubleDeQuant abstraction covers that (they write $doubleDeQuant(c_1,c_2,W) = dequant(dequant(c_1,c_2),W)$). (2) because the paper never documents the FP8 layout choice, if you need the exact bit layout used by their implementation you should consult their released code/kernels; the paper itself just says “8‑bit Floats” and gives the blocksize and the double‑dequant formula but not E/M parameters.

### Why choose block size 64 for weights and 256 for quantization scales? What are the accuracy vs. memory trade-offs?
Question:
QLoRA uses a block size of 64 for quantizing $\mathbf{W}$ and 256 for quantizing the first-level scales $c_2$. Could someone explain the empirical or theoretical reasoning behind these choices? For example: (1) How does the block size affect sensitivity to outliers and quantization error, (2) at what point does decreasing the block size for $\mathbf{W}$ cease to improve accuracy, and (3) how much does increasing the block size for $c_2$ hurt reconstruction fidelity vs. memory saved?
Answer:
Short answer: the QLoRA choices (blocksize 64 for the weight blocks, grouping/second-quantization blocksize 256 for the first-level scales) are a practical trade‑off chosen to keep the per‑weight dynamic range small (so 4‑bit NF4 can represent the block accurately) while keeping the extra memory for per‑block scales tiny. Small weight blocks reduce outlier-driven dynamic range and thus quantization error; the quantization constants themselves are low-dimensional and smooth enough that they can be coarsely (8‑bit) double‑quantized in groups of 256 with essentially no observed accuracy loss, yielding substantial memory savings (about $0.373\ \text{bits/parameter} \simeq 3\ \text{GB}$ on a 65B model).

Why small blocks for W help (intuition + math). In blockwise k‑bit quantization each contiguous group of $B$ weights gets a single scale $c$ and then $k$ bits per weight. Ignoring double quantization, the storage cost per parameter is
$$\text{bits/param} = k + \frac{32}{B}$$
because each block contributes one 32‑bit scale. The quantization step size inside a block is proportional to the block absolute max (or equivalent scale); if a block contains an outlier the step becomes large and most values in that block are rounded coarsely. Reducing $B$ makes it much less likely that a given block contains a large outlier, so the effective per‑weight dynamic range shrinks and the k‑bit bins are used more effectively. In other words, smaller $B$ typically reduces quantization error (MSE) because the local scale (and hence quant step) decreases.

Why 64 specifically (practical reasoning in the paper). The paper uses NF4 (a distribution‑aware 4‑bit data type) which already reduces quantization error for normally distributed weights; on top of that a modest block size (they chose $B=64$) is a good compromise: it is small enough to control outliers and keep quantization error low, but not so small that the 32‑bit scale overhead ($32/B$ bits/param) becomes prohibitive. With $k=4$, $B=64$ the simple one‑level cost per parameter is $4 + 32/64 = 4.5$ bits/param. The authors found this to give high fidelity in practice.

When does shrinking $B$ stop helping? The paper does not give a formal threshold sweep, but the general answer is: there are diminishing returns once the block is small enough that blockwise dynamic range is already controlled and the dominant remaining error is the finite k‑bit representation (the NF4 quantization noise) rather than blockwise scale mismatch. Each time you halve $B$ you double the number of scales and therefore double the scale storage term $32/B$. At some point the small improvement in per‑block dynamic range is outweighed by the memory cost of storing many more scales. In practice QLoRA uses $B=64$ as the operational sweet spot; to find the elbow for a new architecture you would sweep $B$ (e.g. $256\to128\to64\to32$) and plot reconstruction error or downstream validation accuracy versus bits/param to find where improvements saturate.

Why double‑quantize the scales and why group them by 256. Double Quantization treats the first‑level scales $c_2$ (one per weight block) as a vector that can itself be quantized. If you quantize $c_2$ to $q$ bits and group $G$ of those $c_2$ values under a single 32‑bit second‑level scale $c_1$, the extra cost per weight becomes
$$\text{scale overhead (bits/param)} = \frac{q}{B} + \frac{32}{B\cdot G}.$$
With the paper’s choices $q=8$, $B=64$, $G=256$ this is
$$\frac{8}{64} + \frac{32}{64\cdot 256} \approx 0.125 + 0.00195 \approx 0.127\ \text{bits/param}.$$
Compare that to the single‑level overhead $32/64 = 0.5$ bits/param: double quantization reduces the scale overhead by about $0.373$ bits/param, which the paper notes is $\approx 3\ \text{GB}$ on a 65B model.

How double quantization affects fidelity. Errors in the quantized scale $c_2$ propagate linearly (to first order) into the reconstructed weights because dequantization is multiplicative: if the reconstructed scale is $c_{2,\text{est}} = c_{2}(1+\epsilon)$ then reconstructed weights are $w_{\text{est}}\approx w_{\text{true}}/(1+\epsilon)\approx w_{\text{true}}(1-\epsilon)$. Thus the relative error on $c_2$ roughly equals the relative multiplicative error induced on weights. Because the $c_2$ values are low‑variance and are stored in an 8‑bit floating format (after mean‑centering, per the paper), their relative quantization error is small in practice; the authors report “no performance degradation is observed for 8‑bit quantization” of $c_2$ with grouping 256, consistent with prior work. So grouping $G=256$ buys a large memory reduction for a tiny increase in relative scale error, and that tiny error has negligible downstream effect in their experiments.

Practical takeaways and how to think about tradeoffs. If you reduce $B$ for weights you improve robustness to outliers and reduce per‑block dynamic range (better fidelity) but pay more memory for scales roughly proportional to $1/B$. If you increase grouping $G$ or quant bits $q$ for $c_2$ you reduce memory for scale storage but increase relative error on the scales and therefore induce proportional relative error on the reconstructed weights. In QLoRA the operating point $B=64$, $q=8$, $G=256$ was chosen because it gives high fidelity and large memory savings; the paper reports that this configuration recovers 16‑bit finetuning performance. If you need to tune for other models, the standard empirical approach is to sweep $B$, $q$, and $G$, plotting reconstruction MSE and downstream validation accuracy versus bits/parameter to find the elbow where extra memory no longer buys meaningfully better accuracy.

In short: blocksize 64 on weights is a pragmatic sweet spot to control outliers without exploding scale storage; blocksize 256 (grouping 256) for the quantized scales plus 8‑bit FP for those scales gives large memory wins with negligible fidelity loss because scale quantization error is small and its effect on reconstructed weights is multiplicative (hence controllable). The paper gives the concrete memory arithmetic above and reports that using these choices produced no observable degradation in their finetuning results.

### Are blocks formed by flattening tensors or per-row/per-channel? Does block orientation affect accuracy?
Question:
The background section says to flatten the tensor and slice contiguous blocks of size $B$, which implies orientation-agnostic grouping of parameters. In practice, does QLoRA (or bitsandbytes) use purely contiguous memory blocks, or a structured scheme like per-output-channel or per-row blocks for linear layers? If blocks are just contiguous, could reordering to per-channel blocks improve accuracy due to better local statistical homogeneity?
Answer:
Short answer: the QLoRA paper describes and (in its experiments) uses flattened, contiguous block-wise quantization — you take the weight tensor, flatten it to a 1D sequence and slice that sequence into contiguous blocks of size $B$. The paper does not report using a structured per-row / per-channel blocking scheme or comparing different block orientations.

Why that matters and the intuition behind possible alternatives. Equation (1) in the paper shows the usual per-block rescale quantization idea:
$$
\mathbf{X}^{\text{Int8}} = \mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{127}{\operatorname{absmax}(\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}})}\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}}\right),
$$
and the paper formalizes block formation by flattening a tensor $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times h}$ and slicing it into $n=(b\times h)/B$ contiguous blocks. The critical point is that every block gets its own scale/quantization-constant $c$. If a block contains an outlier (a very large element), that block’s scale grows and the remaining values in the block get fewer effective quantization bins — so quantization error increases. This is exactly why small block sizes and methods that isolate outliers can help.

Would reordering to per-channel (per-row / per-output-channel) blocks help? In principle yes: grouping parameters that share similar statistics (e.g., weights belonging to the same output channel) tends to reduce within-block dynamic range and therefore quantization error. Per-channel quantization is a common remedy in the quantization literature because it exploits that channels often have more homogeneous distributions than an arbitrary contiguous slice of flattened memory. So, reordering/reshaping so that each block corresponds to one channel (or a few channels) can reduce the impact of outliers and improve fidelity.

Why the paper still uses contiguous flattening. The authors choose the flattened contiguous blocking for simplicity and efficiency, and they pair it with two practical mitigations that reduce the need for more structured grouping: (1) NF4, a data type tuned for normally-distributed weights, which gives better quantization bins for typical weight statistics; and (2) small blocksize (they use $B=64$) plus Double Quantization to keep constant overhead low. Empirically these choices let QLoRA + NF4 recover 16-bit performance on the benchmarks reported, so any gain from reordering might be marginal in those setups.

Practical trade-offs to keep in mind. Implementing per-channel blocks is not free: you either need to store weights in a memory layout where each channel’s weights are contiguous (which may require transposing/copying before quantization), or you need quantize/dequantize kernels that can operate efficiently on strided memory. Both can increase implementation complexity, GPU memory traffic and runtime cost. Also, the benefits depend on the model and layer: some layers (e.g., embeddings, certain attention projections) may exhibit stronger per-channel heterogeneity and therefore benefit more than others.

How to test this yourself (recommended experiments). Quantize the same model two ways: (A) flatten-and-slice contiguous blocks (the QLoRA default) and (B) reorder so blocks align with channels (or use per-channel blocksize) then apply the same NF4 + double-quantization pipeline. Measure simple diagnostics first (per-block $\operatorname{absmax}$, per-block reconstruction MSE or L2 error between original and dequantized weights), then downstream metrics (perplexity or a small held-out evaluation and, if you want the full test, LoRA finetuning + MMLU/Vicuna-style evaluation). Also time memory and throughput — a tiny reduction in quantization error that costs a lot of runtime/memory may not be worth it in practice.

Bottom line: the paper uses contiguous flattened blocks and shows that NF4 + small blocksize + double quantization is enough to recover 16-bit performance on the tasks they test. Per-channel blocking is a plausible way to further reduce block heterogeneity (and therefore quantization error) and is worth experimenting with on layers that show large per-block dynamic ranges, but it brings engineering trade-offs (layout/copy cost, kernel complexity) and was not evaluated in the QLoRA paper itself.

### If weights are dequantized to BF16 on every forward pass, why is there "no runtime degradation"?
Question:
The paper claims QLoRA does not degrade runtime versus a 16-bit baseline, even though dequantization is performed before the matmul. Is this because the CUDA kernels fuse dequantization with the GEMM (so no intermediate BF16 buffer is materialized), or because the dequant cost is negligible compared to GEMM at large sizes? A brief description of the kernel strategy (e.g., on-the-fly dequantization into registers vs. explicit BF16 tensors) would clarify how the overhead is avoided.
Answer:
Short answer: both — QLoRA achieves “no runtime degradation” because the released implementation uses specialized CUDA kernels that dequantize on the fly inside the matmul path (so you do not materialize a full BF16 weight tensor), and because the arithmetic of dequantization is tiny compared to the cost of the large tensor‑core GEMM used by LLM layers. The paper itself emphasizes that weights are stored in 4‑bit (NF4 + double quant) and “whenever a QLoRA weight tensor is used, we dequantize the tensor to BFloat16, and then perform a matrix multiplication in 16‑bit,” and the authors ship CUDA kernels for 4‑bit training — the practical way to make that statement true is to fuse dequant into the kernel so the dequant cost is amortized and does not add a separate memory/compute pass.

Why fused on‑the‑fly dequantization matters (intuitive + technical). A naive implementation that first expands every 4‑bit block into a full BF16 matrix would pay a large extra memory write and then read (write BF16 results to GPU DRAM, then read them back into the GEMM), which is expensive because it increases memory bandwidth and causes extra allocation/transfer overhead. In contrast, a fused microkernel reads the packed 4‑bit words and the per‑block quantization constants, decodes those few elements into registers (or a small shared‑memory tile), and immediately feeds them into the tensor‑core matmul. That avoids the extra global memory roundtrip and lets the kernel reuse the decoded tile across the tile of inputs it multiplies against, so the per‑weight dequant cost is amortized.

Why the arithmetic cost is small. Dequantization for NF4 + double quant is essentially a small per‑block reconstruction: recover the FP32 block scale (double quant: dequantize the quantization constant, then apply it), and apply a simple scaling / codebook lookup for each 4‑bit code. This is a few cheap operations (shifts/masks + multiply/add or table lookup) per packed element. By contrast, the GEMM does many fused‑multiply‑adds on tensor cores and has very high arithmetic intensity; for large LLM matmuls the tensor‑core work dominates wall‑clock time and is what the GPU is optimized for. So even if you add a couple of extra instructions per weight, that overhead is small and can be overlapped with loads and compute inside the kernel.

How the paper’s design enables this in practice. QLoRA uses blockwise 4‑bit storage (paper uses blocksize $B=64$), so the quantization metadata is tiny (the paper shows how double quant reduces the constants down to about $8/64 + 32/(64\cdot256) = 0.127$ bits/param on average). The small per‑block metadata means the kernel only needs a tiny extra read per $B$ weights. Implementations that achieve the paper’s “no runtime degradation” therefore (a) decode per‑block scales quickly, (b) unpack 4‑bit codes into registers/shared memory for the microtile being multiplied, and (c) immediately execute the BF16/GEMM compute — i.e., dequantize into registers/shared memory on‑the‑fly rather than writing a full BF16 tensor to global memory.

A practical caveat: this good behavior depends on using the specialized fused kernels the authors released (and on being in the large‑GEMM regime where tensor‑core compute dominates). If you implement dequantization as a separate full expansion step into BF16 (or run on very small matrices / tiny batches), you will see a slowdown because you lose the memory/work overlap and the amortization benefits.

If you want to confirm the kernel strategy, the paper points to the released code/CUDA kernels (the authors’ QLoRA + bitsandbytes repositories); those kernels implement the fused on‑the‑fly dequant + matmul approach that makes the “no runtime degradation” claim empirically true.

### What causes gradient-checkpointing memory spikes, and how exactly do Paged Optimizers mitigate OOMs?
Question:
The paper says long-sequence minibatches can cause memory spikes with gradient checkpointing and that Paged Optimizers (using CUDA unified memory) address this by paging optimizer states between CPU and GPU. Could someone explain: (1) which tensors are responsible for the spikes (activations, temp buffers, parameter grads, or optimizer states), (2) why checkpointing in particular leads to spikes, and (3) how paging optimizer states specifically prevents OOMs during the backward pass?
Answer:
Short answer: the spikes come mainly from activation-related memory (saved activations, recomputed activations and temporary operator/workspace buffers) that briefly coexist with the other persistent GPU allocations (model storage, parameter gradients and optimizer state). Gradient checkpointing reduces steady activation storage but causes short-lived recomputation allocations that can push the running peak above the GPU limit. Paged Optimizers fix the practical OOM by placing optimizer state in CUDA managed (unified) memory so those state tensors can be evicted to host RAM during the backward/recompute phase and only paged back for the optimizer.update step, creating enough free GPU headroom to avoid the spike.

Details and intuition (grounded in the paper)

Which tensors actually create the spikes. QLoRA emphasizes that most of the *memory footprint* in LLM finetuning is activation / activation-gradient related, not the small adapter parameters: their example shows LoRA parameters ~26 MB whereas (pre-checkpointing) LoRA *input gradients* can be hundreds of MB (they report 567 MB in one setup) and the 4‑bit base model storage is ~5,048 MB. In practice the things you should watch are (a) activations that must be present or are recomputed during backward, (b) short-lived temporaries and operator workspaces created during forward/recompute (attention score matrices, matmul workspaces, softmax buffers, dequantized BF16 copies used for computation), and (c) the persistent arrays that remain resident throughout backward: parameter gradients and optimizer states (e.g., Adam moments). Activations and recomputation temporaries are usually the dominant contributors to a spike; parameter grads and optimizer state add a constant floor that can make a transient spike cross the OOM threshold.

Why checkpointing can lead to *spikes*. Checkpointing trades stored activation memory for extra compute: instead of keeping every intermediate activation, you save only a subset of “checkpoints” and re-run parts of the forward during backward to recreate activations. That reduces steady memory usage but increases transient allocations: when you recompute a block you re-allocate the block’s activations and any large temporary buffers that the operators need. If those recomputation buffers must coexist (even briefly) with other persistent allocations (saved checkpoints, parameter grads, dequantized weight copies, optimizer states), the instantaneous peak can be larger than the non‑checkpointed peak and thus cause an OOM—this is especially acute for long sequence lengths because activations and attention temporaries scale with sequence length. A compact way to think of peak GPU memory at recompute time is:

$$
M_{\text{peak}} \approx M_{\text{model (resident)}} + M_{\text{saved checkpoints}} + M_{\text{recompute temporaries}} + M_{\text{grads}} + M_{\text{opt\_states}},
$$

and checkpointing reduces the saved-checkpoints term but can raise the recompute-temporaries term; if the sum exceeds GPU RAM you get an OOM.

How Paged Optimizers stop these OOMs in practice. The key observation used in QLoRA is that optimizer state (the large arrays that optimizers keep, e.g., Adam’s first/second moments) are not needed during the recomputation and gradient accumulation phase; they are only needed when you perform the optimizer step. Paged Optimizers allocate these optimizer-state tensors in CUDA unified (managed) memory so the CUDA runtime can migrate pages to host RAM when the GPU needs that address space during backward/recompute. That reduces the GPU-resident value of the last term in the sum above (effectively removing or shrinking $M_{\text{opt\_states}}$ at recompute time), creating enough ephemeral headroom for recomputation temporaries to fit. Only at optimizer.step() do those pages get migrated back to the device. QLoRA describes this as using NVIDIA unified memory so optimizer states “are automatically evicted to CPU RAM when the GPU runs out-of-memory and paged back into GPU memory when the memory is needed in the optimizer update step.”

Important practical notes (from the paper / consistent with it). Paged Optimizers don’t change the algorithmic memory complexity: they move some memory to host and rely on demand paging to hide it. This avoids rare OOMs caused by long-sequence minibatches but can incur host↔device transfer overhead if paging happens frequently (the authors report that for a 65B model on a 48GB GPU with batch size 16 they saw similar speed to a regular optimizer, but they also note they don’t provide exhaustive measurements since paging only happens in those rare long‑sequence batches). Also, QLoRA’s base weights are stored at 4‑bit and dequantized to BF16 for compute; those dequantized copies and the recompute temporaries are part of the transient pressure that the paged optimizer is helping to accommodate.

In short: activations and the recomputation buffers are the direct cause of checkpointing spikes; optimizer states are a persistent allocation that can tip an otherwise-safe recomputation over the OOM threshold; Paged Optimizers solve that by letting optimizer state live in managed ( pageable) memory so it can be evicted during the backward/recompute peak and paged back only for the update.

### Do Paged Optimizers affect determinism, optimizer math (e.g., Adam moments), or throughput? Any best practices?
Question:
Unified memory can introduce page faults and timing variability. When using Paged Optimizers for QLoRA, are there any known effects on determinism, numerical stability of Adam moments (which are typically in FP32), or average/variance of step time? Are there recommended settings (e.g., batch sizes, sequence lengths, pinning strategies) to minimize paging overhead while still getting OOM protection?
Answer:
Short answer: In the QLoRA paper the authors report no change in optimizer math or training quality from using Paged Optimizers, and they found no throughput penalty in their spot-check (65B on a 48GB GPU with batch size 16). However, because Paged Optimizers rely on CUDA unified (managed) memory and on-demand page migrations, they can introduce timing variability (page-fault costs) and — in very rare or contrived cases — small, non‑algorithmic numerical differences (rounding/order differences) compared to keeping all optimizer state permanently on-device. If you want robust, low-jitter training, treat paged optimizers as an OOM-protection safety net and tune batch/sequence sizes and prefetching so paging is rare.

Why this behaves that way (intuition + technical): QLoRA’s Paged Optimizers allocate optimizer state in CUDA managed memory so pages can be evicted to CPU RAM when the GPU needs space and paged back into GPU memory “when the memory is needed in the optimizer update step” (paper). That means the optimizer math (e.g., Adam moments stored in FP32) and the update formulas are unchanged by design: the arithmetic is the same as long as the actual update computation runs on the GPU after the pages are paged back. The main change is that accessing optimizer pages that are not resident on the device causes on-demand page-ins (page faults) and implicit data movement across the PCIe/NVLink bus. Those migrations cost time and create variability in step duration; they do not, in general, change the algebraic formulas, but subtle floating point differences can appear if (a) update work ends up being performed on the CPU instead of the GPU, or (b) timing/streaming differences cause different kernel variants or reduction orders to be used. The QLoRA implementation pages states back to the GPU before updates, so (a) is avoided in normal runs and the paper reports no observed instability.

What the paper actually reports: the authors explicitly introduce Paged Optimizers to handle memory spikes (e.g., long sequences) and note that paging only typically happens for rare long-sequence mini-batches; they do not give extensive microbenchmarks, but they did run a runtime check for a 65B model on a 48GB GPU and found that at batch size 16 paged optimizers gave the same training speed as regular (non-paged) optimizers. They also say they did not provide hard measurements for all cases and recommend future work to characterize when slowdowns occur.

Practical recommendations / best practices (what to do to minimize paging overhead while keeping OOM protection):

- Treat paged optimizers as a safety mechanism rather than a primary memory-management strategy: size your per-step memory footprint (batch size and max sequence length) so that the common case fits on GPU and paging is only used for rare spikes. In practice the QLoRA authors used configurations (for 65B on 48GB) where a typical batch of 16 did not pay a penalty.

- Use gradient accumulation instead of very large per-step batch sizes when you need a large effective batch size but must keep peak memory low. Accumulation trades wall-clock compute for reduced per-step memory pressure and greatly reduces paging triggers.

- Cap or bucket sequence lengths (dynamic padding / bucketing) so that occasional long examples don’t create big spikes. If you have very long outliers, drop or truncate them during training or handle them in a separate, larger‑memory run.

- If you can modify low-level CUDA behavior, prefetch optimizer pages to the device right before the optimizer step (cudaMemPrefetchAsync / CUDA managed prefetch) so page-ins happen when you schedule them, not implicitly during a kernel that expects data to be resident. Prefetching turns unpredictable page faults into an explicit transfer you can schedule to overlap with other work.

- Consider using pinned host memory (cudaHostAlloc / PyTorch’s pin-memory allocations) for optimizer buffers if you implement explicit host<->device copies: pinned allocations enable faster DMA transfers and reduce the latency of bringing pages back to the GPU compared to unmanaged pageable host memory.

- Monitor and measure. Log per-step time (mean and variance) and watch for spikes, and inspect GPU memory residency and PCIe traffic while you experiment. If you observe frequent page faults or high variance, tighten your memory headroom (smaller batch or shorter sequences) or prefetch more aggressively.

- If you require strict bitwise reproducibility across runs, do not rely on paged managed memory unless you can guarantee the same residency/prefetch schedule every run. The driver’s page-in behavior adds a source of non-determinism in wall-clock ordering; for strict reproducibility, keep optimizer state resident on-device (no paging) or control all migrations deterministically.

Numerical-stability / Adam-moments specifics: the paper stores optimizer moments in FP32 and pages them. Moving FP32 buffers between host and device preserves their bit pattern; if updates are performed on the GPU after the pages are paged-in, the arithmetic (multiplications/additions) is the same code path as a non-paged run, so you should not see systematic numerical instability from paging alone. Tiny rounding differences can occur if the update is done on CPU (different instruction sets, FMA behavior, or fused ops) or if page timing forces different kernel implementations or different floating‑point accumulation orders — but these are subtle and, for practical purposes, the paper’s experiments show no degradation.

Concrete starting points: for very large models the QLoRA paper found batch size 16 on a 48GB GPU gave comparable speed to non-paged training for their 65B experiment — use that as a sanity check if you try similar hardware/scale. If you see paging-related slowdowns, first reduce per-step peak memory (smaller batch, shorter seq, gradient accumulation) and then add prefetch/pinned-memory strategies.

Bottom line: Paged Optimizers in QLoRA are an effective, pragmatic way to avoid OOMs without changing the optimizer math; they introduce possible step-time jitter and (in corner cases) tiny numerical differences from timing/where arithmetic executes. The safest way to use them is to tune your batch/sequence settings so paging is rare, prefetch pages before the update when possible, and reserve paged optimizers as a reliable fallback for occasional spikes.

### Why do LoRA adapters on all linear layers matter more than increasing rank $r$?
Question:
The paper reports that applying LoRA adapters to all linear layers is critical to match full finetuning, whereas increasing the projection dimension $r$ has little effect. Could someone provide intuition or empirical evidence for why coverage (which layers are adapted) dominates over capacity (rank per adapter)? For instance, is there an observed pattern where certain layers contribute disproportionately, or is the benefit roughly uniform across the transformer block?
Answer:
Short answer: because finetuning a deep transformer is fundamentally about changing the network’s computations at many places, not just adding more capacity at a few places. QLoRA’s experiments show that putting small low-rank adapters in every linear projection lets the model implement the distributed, layer‑wise changes that full finetuning does; by contrast, simply increasing the local rank $r$ at a small subset of layers gives more capacity only locally and does not recover the distributed representational changes. The paper reports this empirically (LLaMA 7B on Alpaca) and explains why it is affordable to “cover” all linear layers.

Why coverage beats local capacity (intuitively and mathematically)
When you finetune the whole model you change many weight matrices $W_l$ across layers $l$. LoRA replaces each additive change with a low-rank update $\Delta_l = L_{1,l} L_{2,l}$ (rank $r$). Two simple facts make the coverage argument compelling.

First, composition/amplification: consider a (linearized) deep mapping $y = W_n W_{n-1} \cdots W_1 x$. If you add low-rank updates at many layers, the first‑order change in the end-to-end map is a sum of terms

$$
y' - y \approx \sum_{i} W_n \cdots W_{i+1}\,\Delta_i\,W_{i-1}\cdots W_1\,x.
$$

Even if each $\Delta_i$ is low-rank, the left/right multiplication by the surrounding full matrices can turn each term into a rich (effectively higher-rank) change to the $\text{input}\to\text{output}$ mapping. In plain language: many small low-rank edits in different places can compose into a large, expressive change to the overall function; a single large-rank edit in one layer cannot always make up for missing edits elsewhere.

Second, representation mismatch and signal routing: different layers learn different kinds of features (early layers extract primitives, middle layers remap representations, later layers synthesize answers). If only a few layers are adapted, the rest of the network still produces representations optimized for the original task/data distribution; boosting rank at one layer can’t reliably shift those upstream or downstream representations to where the task needs them. Distributed adapters let the optimizer nudge representations at the points where they matter.

What the paper shows empirically
The authors explicitly tried the common PEFT pattern (LoRA on query/value projections only) and could not match full finetuning; their LLaMA 7B → Alpaca experiments (see Figure in the paper and Appendix~\ref{app:exp-setup}) show that the single most important LoRA hyperparameter was how many adapters were used, and that placing LoRA on all linear transformer block layers was required to match full 16‑bit finetuning performance. By contrast, sweeping the projection dimension $r$ had little effect on final accuracy (the paper reports that “other LoRA hyperparameters, such as the projection dimension $r$, do not affect performance”). That is direct empirical evidence that coverage is the dominant factor in their setups.

Why this is practical (memory + training dynamics)
The paper also examines memory costs and notes that the dominant memory burden during finetuning comes from activation gradients, not from the LoRA parameter tensors themselves. For a 7B LLaMA example they give, LoRA parameters are tiny (~26 MB) compared to input gradients (~567 MB) and the 4-bit base model (~5048 MB). Because adapters are so cheap, it’s affordable to add them to all linear layers; this lets you get the distributed modification power without blowing up memory. The experiments exploit that fact and find coverage recovers full-finetuning performance while simply increasing $r$ (which raises per-adapter size) gives little extra accuracy for the added cost.

About per-layer importance — is any layer “the one”?
The paper’s key empirical claim is that adapting only the usual small set (e.g., query/value) was insufficient; applying adapters to all linear projections was necessary. The authors do not present a narrow, single-layer “most important” winner in the main text — instead the failure of the limited-coverage designs implies that useful adaptation is spread across both attention ($q/k/v/\text{output}$) and MLP linear layers. So the practical takeaway from their results is that contributions appear distributed enough that you cannot get away with only adapting a tiny subset. (If you need a per-layer budgeted strategy, you would want to do a dedicated layerwise ablation — the paper did not present a detailed per-layer importance ranking in the main text.)

Bottom line / practical recommendation
If your goal is to match full finetuning accuracy while staying memory efficient, follow the paper’s finding: prefer placing LoRA adapters across all linear layers rather than trying to squeeze more rank into a few adapters. Small $r$ per adapter typically suffices; distributing those small adapters across layers recovers the distributed changes full finetuning would make, and is affordable because LoRA parameters are tiny compared to activation gradient memory.

### Concrete shape-level definition of doubleDequant: how are $c_1$, $c_2$, and $\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}$ applied per block?
Question:
In the equation $\text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}}) = \text{dequant}(\text{dequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}), \mathbf{W}^{\text{4bit}})$, could someone give a concrete shape-level example? Suppose a linear weight matrix $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{h\times o}$ is partitioned into blocks of 64 along its flattened memory order. Then: (1) what are the shapes and indexing of $c_2$ and $c_1$, (2) how are the $c_2$ values reconstructed from $c_1$ and applied back to each 64-parameter block, and (3) how does the final dequant for $\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}$ proceed per block? A short pseudo-code snippet would be ideal.
Answer:
Short answer: think of double-quantization as a two-stage undoing of two nested scalings. First you un-quantize the small vector of per-block scales $c_2$ (these were themselves quantized in groups) to get one FP32 scale per 64-parameter weight block; then you use those per-block scales to dequantize the 4-bit NF4 codes for each 64-parameter block into BF16 weights. Concretely, with a weight matrix $W\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times o}$ flattened in memory and a blocksize $B=64$:

Let $L=h\cdot o$ and $n=L/B$ (assume divisible for simplicity). QLoRA stores
- $W^{\text{NF4}}$ as the 4-bit codes for each block: conceptually shape $(n,\,B)$ (in practice packed),
- $c_2^{\text{k-bit}}$ as one quantized scalar per 64-element block: shape $(n,)$ (stored in 8-bit float / FP8),
- $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$ as the second-level (FP32) constants that dequantize groups of $c_2$ entries: choose group size $G=256$ so $m=\lceil n/G\rceil$ and $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$ has shape $(m,)$.

Indexing is therefore: block $b\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ corresponds to flattened indices $[bB,(b+1)B)$ of $W$; block $b$ belongs to group $g=\lfloor b/G\rfloor$, so $c_2^{\text{k-bit}}[b]$ is dequantized with $c_1^{\text{FP32}}[g]$.

Reconstruction (exactly the nested dequant in the paper) proceeds in two steps. First, for each group $g$ recover the FP32 per-block scales:
$$
\hat{c}_2[b] \;=\; \text{dequant\_c2}\big(c_1^{\text{FP32}}[g],\; c_2^{\text{k-bit}}[b]\big)
\qquad\text{for } b\in [gG,(g+1)G),
$$
where the implementation detail of dequant\_c2 depends on how $c_2$ were quantized (the paper centers $c_2$ by subtracting a mean before symmetric quantization — that mean is saved and added back here). In plain words: apply the second-level FP32 scale (and add back the group mean if it was subtracted) to turn each stored 8-bit $c_2$ into the original FP32 block scale.

Second, for each 64-parameter block $b$ dequantize the NF4 codes into BF16 weights by mapping the 4-bit code to its NF4 representative (a small lookup table of $2^4=16$ floats, call it $\text{NF4\_lut}$) and applying the recovered per-block scale:
$$
W^{\text{BF16}}_{b, :} \;=\; \text{NF4\_lut}\big[ W^{\text{NF4}}_{b, :} \big] \;\times\; \hat{c}_2[b],
$$
and then place that 64-length block back into the flattened weight vector and reshape to $(h,o)$.

A minimal, explicit pseudo-code (assuming helper functions dequant_c2 and NF4_lut) that follows the paper is:

```
# Inputs (paper defaults): B = 64, G = 256
# W_nf4: shape (n, B) of 4-bit codes
# c2_q : shape (n,)   of quantized c2 (k-bit, e.g. FP8)
# c1_fp32: shape (m,) of FP32 second-level constants, m = ceil(n/G)
# NF4_lut: length-16 array of NF4 representative floats

L = h * o
B = 64
G = 256
n = L // B
m = ceil(n / G)

# 1) recover per-block scales c2_fp32
c2_fp32 = zeros(n, dtype=FP32)
for g in range(m):
    s = g * G
    e = min(n, s + G)
    # dequant the small vector of c2 entries in this group
    # dequant_c2 applies the group FP32 constant and (if used) adds back the mean that was subtracted
    c2_fp32[s:e] = dequant_c2(c1_fp32[g], c2_q[s:e])

# 2) dequant each 64-element NF4 block using its recovered scale
W_flat = empty(L, dtype=BF16)
for b in range(n):
    codes = W_nf4[b]                    # shape (B,)
    vals  = NF4_lut[codes]              # shape (B,) -- float representatives in [-1,1]
    block = vals * c2_fp32[b]           # scale to original magnitude
    W_flat[b*B : (b+1)*B] = cast(block, BF16)

W_bf16 = W_flat.reshape((h, o))
return W_bf16
```

Two practical notes that connect to the paper: (i) the paper’s equation $\text{doubleDequant}\big(c_1^{\text{FP32}},\; c_2^{\text{k-bit}},\; W^{\text{NF4}}\big) = \text{dequant}\big(\text{dequant}\big(c_1^{\text{FP32}},\; c_2^{\text{k-bit}}\big),\; W^{\text{4bit}}\big)$ is exactly the nested pair of steps above (first recover $c_2$ from $c_1$ and $c_2^{\text{k-bit}}$, then recover block weights from $W^{\text{NF4}}$ using those $c_2$). (ii) implementations usually pack 4-bit codes and vectorize both dequant steps; you can also dequantize blocks on-the-fly in the matmul (streaming dequant) to avoid holding a full BF16 copy of $W$ in memory — QLoRA dequantizes to BF16 for computation, as described in the paper.

This is the shape-level indexing and the per-block application the paper uses: one quantized $c_2$ per 64-element block, those $c_2$’s quantized in groups of 256 and reconstructed from one $c_1$ per group, and finally one NF4 lookup-and-scale per 64-element block to produce the BF16 weight block.

### How can 4-bit QLoRA recover 16-bit performance when 4-bit inference alone degrades accuracy?
Question:
Prior work shows that 4-bit quantization for inference reduces accuracy compared to 16-bit. QLoRA reports that after adapter finetuning on top of the frozen 4-bit base, performance matches 16-bit finetuning and 16-bit LoRA. What is the right mental model for this recovery? Is the LoRA path effectively learning a low-rank correction that compensates for quantization error in each layer, thereby restoring the function class the model can represent? Are there tasks where this compensation fails (e.g., very small datasets or distribution shifts)?
Answer:
Short answer: QLoRA recovers 16‑bit performance because the LoRA adapters learn additive, low‑rank residuals that (1) cancel most of the quantization error where it matters (i.e., on the activations seen during finetuning) and (2) implement the normal task-specific fine‑tuning update. Two enabling facts in the paper make that feasible: the NF4 quantizer keeps the stored 4‑bit weights close to the original (small, structured error), and QLoRA dequantizes to BF16 for compute so backprop through the frozen, quantized base is numerically accurate while only the adapters are trained.

A slightly more formal mental model. Let the original (high‑precision) weight matrix be $W$, and the stored 4‑bit dequantized version be $\tilde W$. Write the quantization error as
$$
E = W - \tilde W.
$$
A LoRA adapter implements a low‑rank additive matrix $\Delta = L_1 L_2$ and the layer produces
$$
Y = X(\tilde W + \Delta).
$$
If we could set $\Delta = E$ we would exactly recover the original layer outputs. In practice LoRA is trained to make the outputs match on the finetuning distribution, so what must hold is not the matrix equality everywhere but the weaker condition
$$
\forall X\ \text{seen in finetuning},\quad X\Delta \approx X E,
$$
i.e. LoRA only has to match the action of the quantization error on the subspace of activations that occurs in the task. That is why a low‑rank adapter often suffices: either (a) $E$ itself is structured/low‑rank, or (b) the set of inputs $X$ spans a low‑dimensional subspace so $X E$ is low‑rank/easy to approximate. The paper’s experiments show exactly this is effective: with NF4 + LoRA applied to many linear layers, QLoRA matches 16‑bit LoRA and (on many tasks) full 16‑bit finetuning.

Why 4‑bit inference alone degrades: when you only quantize to 4 bits and do no finetuning the error $E$ remains uncorrected everywhere, so the model’s function changes and accuracy drops. QLoRA’s fix is to learn the compensating residuals via LoRA during finetuning.

When this compensation can fail. The paper itself documents conditions that matter and limitations you should keep in mind. Compensation is likely to break or be incomplete when (a) the required correction is not well approximated by a low‑rank additive term (i.e., $E$ acts in many independent directions on the relevant inputs), (b) the adapters are too small or placed too sparsely — the authors found that applying LoRA only to q/v projections was often insufficient and that adapters at every linear layer were required to match full finetuning, (c) the finetuning data does not cover the test input subspace (distribution shift), because LoRA only learns to fix the effect of quantization on the activation subspace it sees, and (d) the finetuning signal is too weak (very tiny or poor quality datasets) to learn the needed residuals. The paper also shows that NF4 is important: FP4 quantization lags behind NF4 by about 1 percentage point, so a worse quantizer makes the needed correction larger and harder to learn.

Practical implications and how to test this on your problem: view LoRA as a task‑focused residual corrector, not as magically restoring the entire full‑precision weight matrix everywhere. If you see failures, try increasing LoRA capacity (more layers covered, larger rank $r$), use NF4 (the paper’s quantile‑aware 4‑bit type), ensure finetuning data is high quality and representative of your evaluation distribution, and verify that the correction generalizes under your expected distribution shift. The paper’s empirical results — GLUE, Super‑NaturalInstructions, MMLU and instruction tuning at 7B–65B — show that with these choices QLoRA recovers 16‑bit adapter/full finetuning performance in their benchmarks; but they also emphasize dataset suitability and adapter placement as critical factors and explicitly note limitations beyond their experiments.

In one sentence: QLoRA works because LoRA is free to learn the small, structured residuals the quantizer left behind (and the task‑specific adjustments), and it only needs to match the quantization error in the activation subspace that actually matters for the finetuning/test distribution.

### What precisions are used for LoRA parameters, gradients, and optimizer states in QLoRA memory estimates?
Question:
The paper states there is one storage type (usually 4-bit NF) and one compute type (usually BF16), and that only LoRA parameters are trained. For the LoRA path, which precisions are used for: (1) forward/backward compute, (2) parameter storage, (3) gradients, and (4) optimizer moments (e.g., Adam's $m$ and $v$)? Are the optimizer states BF16 or FP32, and are those the tensors that Paged Optimizers page out to CPU RAM?
Answer:
Short answer: QLoRA dequantizes the 4-bit base weights to BFloat16 for all compute; the LoRA adapters themselves are stored and computed in BFloat16 and their gradients are computed in BFloat16 as well. The paper does not explicitly state the numeric precision used for Adam-style optimizer moments, but its description of “Paged Optimizers” strongly implies the (full‑precision) optimizer state tensors are the large per‑parameter tensors being paged to CPU RAM — i.e., the paper assumes conventional full‑precision optimizer states (practically FP32) are the things that get paged.

Why that answer follows from the paper: the authors write the forward rule for a single layer as
$$
\mathbf{Y}^{\text{BF16}}
= \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\;\text{doubleDequant}\big(c_1^{\text{FP32}},c_2^{\text{k-bit}},\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}\big)
+ \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_1^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_2^{\text{BF16}},
$$
and they explicitly state “we dequantize the storage data type to the computation data type to perform the forward and backward pass” and “we only compute weight gradients for the LoRA parameters which use 16‑bit BrainFloat.” From this it follows directly that (1) forward/backward compute happens in BFloat16 (the dequantized base weights and LoRA multiplies are BF16), (2) LoRA parameter storage and updates are in BF16 (the adapter matrices are labeled $\mathbf{L}_i^{\text{BF16}}$), and (3) the gradients you actually need and compute for the adapters $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{L}_i}$ are computed in BF16.

About optimizer moments (Adam’s $m,v$) and paging: the paper’s Paged Optimizers description says they “allocate paged memory for the optimizer states which are then automatically evicted to CPU RAM … and paged back … when the memory is needed in the optimizer update step.” The paper therefore treats the optimizer state tensors as the large auxiliary per‑parameter tensors that get paged. The authors do not explicitly write “optimizer moments are FP32,” but they do treat other control/scale quantities as 32‑bit (e.g. the top‑level quantization constants $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$), and in practice training with Adam keeps the running moments in full precision for numerical stability. So the correct reading of the paper is: optimizer states are the heavyweight tensors that Paged Optimizers pages (i.e., the things moved between GPU and CPU), and they are treated as conventional higher‑precision optimizer state tensors (the paper’s memory‑saving strategy is to page these states out when not in use).

If you need absolute confirmation for an implementation detail (e.g., whether the released code uses FP32 or BF16 for $m,v$ in the particular optimizer implementation), check the QLoRA/bitsandbytes code referenced in the paper; the paper describes the design and intent but the exact dtype used in the shipped optimizer implementation is best verified in the code.

### How robust is NF4 if weight distributions deviate from normality or have outliers?
Question:
NF4 assumes weights are approximately $N(0, \sigma^2)$ and constructs bin centers from a standard normal CDF. In practice, some layers (e.g., close to layer norm or attention projections) can have heavier tails or outliers. Are there observed failure modes where NF4 underperforms Int4/FP4 or 8-bit? Does choosing a smaller block size (e.g., 64) sufficiently mitigate outliers for NF4, or would per-channel blocks be a safer choice in those layers?
Answer:
Short answer: the QLoRA paper does not report systematic failure modes where NF4 is reliably worse than Int4/FP4 or an $8$-bit fallback; across architectures and sizes (125M–65B) NF4 + blockwise quantization (they use blocksize $64$) consistently outperformed FP4/Int4 and recovered $16$-bit LoRA performance. That said, NF4 is explicitly designed for (approximately) zero-centered normal weights, so if a particular layer’s weights strongly deviate from normality or contain dominant outliers you should expect more quantization error unless you localize the scaling (smaller blocks) or use a higher-precision fallback for that layer.

Why NF4 tends to be robust and where it can struggle. NF4 builds its $k$-bit codebook from the theoretical standard-normal quantiles
$$
q_i=\frac{1}{2}\Big(Q_{N(0,1)}\!\big(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1}\big)+Q_{N(0,1)}\!\big(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1}\big)\Big),
$$
so its bins concentrate resolution where a Gaussian mass lies (near zero) and allocate fewer bins into the tails. That is a very good match for the empirical distribution of pretrained weights (the paper points to this as the reason NF4 is information‑theoretically attractive). When the assumption holds, NF4 gives low error and — importantly for training — LoRA adapters can compensate residual distortion so that finetuning quality matches $16$-bit baselines. The main failure mode is therefore mismatch: if a block contains a very large outlier (or a heavy-tailed shape very different from Gaussian), the block’s absmax scaling will be dominated by the outlier and many mid-sized weights in that block get compressed into few representable levels. Quantile-based schemes are optimal for the true empirical CDF but are expensive or noisy to estimate; NF4 chooses a cheaper theoretical CDF—good in practice but not immune to distribution mismatch.

Practical mitigations that the paper endorses and that follow directly from its design. The paper uses block-wise quantization precisely to limit the damage of outliers: smaller blocks localize an outlier’s effect so it doesn’t rescale an entire large tensor. QLoRA defaults to blocksize $B=64$ for weights (and uses Double Quantization to make the per-block constants affordable). Storing a 32-bit scale per block costs on average $32/B$ bits per parameter; with Double Quantization the paper shows this drops (for $B=64$) from $32/64=0.5$ bits/param to
$$
\frac{8}{64}+\frac{32}{64\cdot 256}\approx 0.127\ \text{bits/param},
$$
making small blocks practical. So in practice: start with NF4 and $B=64$ (the paper’s setting) — it worked across many models and layers in their experiments. If you find a layer with noticeably worse quantization error or validation degradation, shrink its blocksize (e.g., $32$, $16$) or apply per-channel / per-row blocks for that layer. Per-channel blocks are the most conservative (they decouple scaling per output/input channel) and will almost always reduce the impact of channel-local outliers, at the expense of more quantization constants (and thus more memory, which Double Quantization helps mitigate). A simpler fallback is to keep a handful of known-problem layers (often attention projections or immediately adjacent to layer‑norm in other papers) in $8$-bit/$16$-bit — QLoRA supports mixed precision and the paper notes that a small number of higher-precision layers is a practical engineering trade-off.

How to detect problematic layers quickly. The paper doesn’t give a per-layer failure catalog, so a practical workflow is to quantize once with NF4, compute per-block or per-layer reconstruction error (e.g., $L_2$ or relative RMSE between original and dequantized weights) or a short validation loss/ppl sweep, and flag layers/blocks with much larger error. If such blocks exist, try (a) reducing block size for those blocks, (b) switching those blocks to per-channel quantization, or (c) quantizing those layers to $8$-bit. In the QLoRA experiments these interventions were usually unnecessary: NF4 + blocksize $64$ + Double Quantization produced high fidelity across the tested models, but the above diagnostic-and-fallback loop is the safe, practical approach for models or layers you suspect are non-Gaussian.

Bottom line: NF4 is empirically robust in the QLoRA paper’s large suite of experiments, and small block sizes (the paper’s $B=64$) plus Double Quantization are the intended way to control outliers. For particularly pathological layers, per-channel blocks or keeping those layers at $8$-bit/$16$-bit are prudent and commonly used fallbacks.

### Why not perform matmuls directly in 4-bit? What exactly is dequantized to BF16 (weights, activations, or both)?
Question:
The paper describes dequantizing 4-bit weights to BF16 for compute. Are the activations also quantized, or are they always in BF16? Could direct 4-bit matmuls (with 32-bit accumulation) reduce overhead further, or does doing the matmul in BF16 already give the best trade-off because the compute dominates and dequantization is fused? Any notes on kernel availability and numerical stability here would help.
Answer:
Short answer: QLoRA stores the pretrained model weights in 4‑bit (NF4) on disk / in GPU memory, but it does not perform the matrix multiply natively in 4‑bit. At runtime the 4‑bit weight blocks are dequantized to the computation dtype (BFloat16) and the GEMM is done in BF16; activations (and all LoRA / adapter params and gradients) are kept in BF16. This is exactly what the paper states (see equations (5)–(6)): 

$$
\mathbf{Y}^{\text{BF16}} = \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\;\text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}) \;+\; \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_1^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_2^{\text{BF16}},
$$

$$
\text{doubleDequant}(\cdot) = \text{dequant}(\text{dequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}), \mathbf{W}^{\text{4bit}}) = \mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}.
$$

Why not run the matmul purely in 4‑bit? there are three practical reasons, all discussed or implied in the paper.

1) hardware / kernel ecosystem and throughput. Modern GPUs and libraries (cuBLAS / tensor cores) are highly optimized for FP16/BF16 matrix multiply kernels. The QLoRA authors choose a single storage dtype (4‑bit) and a single compute dtype (BF16) so they can dequantize and then call very fast BF16 GEMMs. Native 4‑bit GEMMs (packed 4‑bit inputs, integer multiply/accumulate) are not a standard primitive in commodity libraries; implementing them efficiently requires custom kernels or special hardware. The QLoRA codebase therefore provides CUDA kernels that handle the 4‑bit storage format and the dequantization path; the heavy arithmetic work is done by BF16 tensor‑core GEMMs.

2) arithmetic / data‑flow complexity with block scales and double quantization. QLoRA uses blockwise quantization and double quantization of the scale constants: each weight block has its own scale(s) and those scales themselves are quantized. Algebraically you can write a weight as a scaled code, e.g. $w=\text{dequant}(c,q)$, and the layer product is $Y=XW = X\cdot \text{dequant}(c,q)$. To do this natively in integer/4‑bit land you'd need kernels that (a) multiply floating activations by packed integer codes, (b) accumulate in a wider accumulator (e.g. 32‑bit) and (c) apply per‑block scaling (and possibly dequantize the second‑level scale). That bookkeeping and runtime branching offsets much of the bandwidth/memory benefit and makes an efficient implementation considerably more complicated than "dequantize a packed block to BF16 and run a fast BF16 GEMM."

3) numerical stability for training. QLoRA is for finetuning (not just inference). Although the base weights remain frozen, gradients must be backpropagated through the frozen, quantized layers to compute gradients for the LoRA adapters. Using BF16 for the forward/backward compute keeps a wide dynamic range (BF16 has the same exponent range as FP32) and reduces noise in the activations/gradients compared to operating in low‑bit integer arithmetic. The paper’s empirical point is that dequantizing to BF16 and computing there recovers full 16‑bit finetuning performance; keeping the compute in sub‑byte integer domains during training risks extra quantization noise, accumulation saturation, and harder-to-control backward paths.

Could native 4‑bit matmuls still help in principle? Yes — in inference this is already exploited by some methods: performing dot products with very low‑precision weights and accumulating into a wider type can reduce memory bandwidth and latency. In principle a highly optimized 4‑bit GEMM that (1) reads packed codes, (2) accumulates in a wide integer/FP accumulator, and (3) applies per‑block scaling only once per block could reduce memory traffic. But for training on current hardware the practical win is small-to-nil because (a) tensor cores are extremely fast for BF16/FP16 and (b) the dequantization cost (one pass over the stored weights) is small relative to the cost of the GEMM when batch/sequence sizes are non‑trivial — compute dominates. The QLoRA design exploits that tradeoff and ships CUDA kernels that do the packed‑storage ↦ BF16 conversion and then use BF16 matmuls.

Kernel availability and maturity: at the time of the paper the authors released CUDA kernels (and the bitsandbytes tooling) to handle NF4 storage, double quantization, and on‑the‑fly dequantization into BF16 for efficient training. General, high‑quality, drop‑in 4‑bit GEMM primitives (that fully replace BF16 tensor‑core GEMMs for training) were not part of standard GPU libraries; writing them correctly and stably is nontrivial. Also, future hardware (or vendor libraries) that expose native low‑bit dot‑product instructions with floating accumulation might make true 4‑bit matmuls more attractive, but that is outside QLoRA’s scope.

Conclusion: QLoRA deliberately separates storage precision (4‑bit NF4) from compute precision (BF16). Activations and adapter params remain BF16; weights are dequantized to BF16 before GEMM. That choice is driven by the practical tradeoffs above — it lets the authors get the storage/memory savings of 4‑bit while retaining the speed, numerical stability, and ecosystem advantages of BF16 compute for finetuning. Native 4‑bit matmuls are possible in principle and useful for inference, but for this training setup they are not the best off‑the‑shelf trade‑off given hardware and numerical constraints.

### Reproducing the 7B memory numbers (5,048 MB base, 567 MB LoRA input grads, 26 MB LoRA params): detailed assumptions?
Question:
In the "Memory Requirement of Parameter-Efficient Finetuning" section, the paper reports for a 7B LLaMA model (batch size 1, FLAN v2) that the 4-bit base model is 5,048 MB, LoRA parameters are 26 MB (about 0.2% of base), and LoRA input gradients are 567 MB (reduced to 18 MB on average with checkpointing). Could someone outline the precise assumptions for these figures: tensor precisions, layer counts, where gradients are stored, whether activation recomputation checkpoints affect gradient storage, and whether optimizer states are included or excluded?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper’s 7B memory numbers are measured under the QLoRA ingredients described in the text: the base LLaMA weights are stored in 4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4) with Double Quantization (blocksize 64 for weights, blocksize 256 for the second quantization of the scales), all computation (forward/backward and LoRA params/gradients) is in 16‑bit BrainFloat (BF16), LoRA adapters are placed at every linear projection inside every transformer block, batch size is 1 on the FLAN v2 data, and optimizer states for the (tiny) LoRA parameters are not counted on GPU because QLoRA uses Paged Optimizers (they are paged to CPU). The heavy‑hitters in those figures are the quantized weight storage (4‑bit + quantization constants) and the stored layer inputs (activations) needed to compute LoRA gradients; gradient checkpointing removes most of the latter (this is why $567\ \text{MB} \rightarrow 18\ \text{MB}$).

Below I give the exact assumptions (as used or implied in the paper) and short reproducible formulas so you can re‑derive the numbers.

What each reported number is and the assumptions behind it
- “$4$‑bit base model = $5{,}048\ \text{MB}$. ” This is the GPU footprint of the frozen base LLaMA‑7B stored with NF4 + Double Quantization. The implementation details used in the paper are: NF4 for weight storage, blocksize 64 for weight blocks, double quantization of the block scales with c2 in 8‑bit float (FP8) and the top level scale c1 in FP32. Dequantization is performed on the fly to BF16 for compute. The $5{,}048\ \text{MB}$ is the measured storage cost on GPU in their code (quantized weights + quantization constants + small framework/packing overhead). The per‑parameter model for that storage (Double Quantization, blocksize 64) gives an extra constant overhead of about $0.127\ \text{bits/param}$, so the nominal per‑parameter storage is about $4.127\ \text{bits/param}$; the paper’s Appendix contains the exact measured breakdown (see the paper).

- “LoRA params = 26 MB.” LoRA parameters are stored and updated in BF16. The paper uses LoRA sized to be about $0.2\%$ of the original model weights. The memory is therefore
$$
  M_{\text{LoRA}} \approx N_{\text{params}}\times 0.002 \times 2\ \text{bytes},
$$
where the 2 bytes come from BF16. For a ~7B parameter model this evaluates to ~25–30 MB (the paper reports $\approx 26\ \text{MB}$; small differences come from exact param count and MB vs MiB conventions).

- “LoRA input gradients = 567 MB (reduced to 18 MB with checkpointing).” What the paper calls “LoRA input gradients” is the stored layer inputs (activations) that you must keep to compute gradients for the LoRA adapter (LoRA needs the input X for each adapted linear projection). Those are stored in the computation dtype (BF16, 2 bytes/element). If you apply LoRA to every linear projection in every transformer layer, the activation memory for batch size $B$ and sequence length $S$ is approximately
$$
  M_{\text{act}} \approx B \times S \times H \times N_{\text{LoRA\_lin}} \times 2\ \text{bytes},
$$
where $H$ is the model hidden dim and $N_{\text{LoRA\_lin}}$ is the number of linear projections you applied LoRA to (e.g. for LLaMA‑7B you typically have 32 transformer layers and you may apply LoRA to attention q/k/v/o and the MLP projections $\rightarrow$ roughly 6 linear projections per transformer block $\Rightarrow$ $N_{\text{LoRA\_lin}} \approx 32 \times 6 = 192$). Plugging representative numbers ($B=1$, $H \approx 4096$, $N_{\text{LoRA\_lin}} = 192$) gives
$$
  M_{\text{act}} \approx S\times 1{,}572{,}864\ \text{bytes}\,.
$$

To get the paper’s quoted 567 MB you solve for the effective average sequence length used in FLAN v2 (their reported number corresponds to $S \approx 360$ tokens under these assumptions). Gradient checkpointing trades compute for memory by discarding most intermediate activations and recomputing them during the backward pass; storing only one checkpoint per transformer block (or similar checkpointing granularity) reduces $M_{\text{act}}$ roughly by a factor $\approx$ number_of_layers, which is why $567\ \text{MB} \rightarrow \approx 18\ \text{MB}$ in the paper ($567\ \text{MB} \div 32 \approx 17.7\ \text{MB}$). The paper is explicit that the 18 MB figure is an average per sequence when checkpointing is enabled over FLAN v2 sequences.

Where gradients and optimizer states live
- Gradients: only LoRA parameters are trained so only the adapter weights have parameter gradients. Those gradients and the cached inputs needed to form them are stored in BF16 by the QLoRA implementation (the paper states “we only compute weight gradients for the LoRA parameters which use 16‑bit BrainFloat”).
- Base weights: base (4‑bit) model weights are frozen, so there are no base‑weight gradients to store.
- Optimizer states: the paper’s memory numbers are GPU footprints; QLoRA uses “Paged Optimizers” (NVIDIA unified memory paging) to page optimizer state to CPU when necessary. Practically that means the large moment/variance buffers for the optimizer can be evicted to CPU RAM (and are not counted in the GPU numbers in the paper). So the quoted 5,048 / 567 / 26 / 18 MB figures are not including full on‑GPU optimizer state for a full model optimizer (and for LoRA the optimizer state is small and may be paged out).

How to exactly reproduce their reported numbers
1. Use LLaMA‑7B parameter count used by the authors (their code / appendix has the exact $N$). Use NF4 + double quantization with blocksize 64 for weights and blocksize 256 for $c_2$, and store LoRA params in BF16.  
2. For LoRA param memory compute $M_{\text{LoRA}} = N_{\text{params}}\times 0.002 \times 2\ \text{bytes}$. That yields $\approx 26\ \text{MB}$ for the 7B config (differences due to exact $N$ and MB vs MiB conventions).
3. For activation memory compute $M_{\text{act}} = B\times S\times H\times N_{\text{LoRA\_lin}}\times 2\ \text{bytes}$. Use $B=1$ and the FLAN v2 average sequence length (the paper’s $567\ \text{MB}$ corresponds to the average sequence distribution they used). With checkpointing keep only $O(\text{number\_of\_layers})$ checkpoints and divide $M_{\text{act}}$ accordingly (that is how you see $\approx 18\ \text{MB}$ average).
4. For the 4‑bit base size compute per‑param bits $\approx 4 + 0.127$ (the 0.127 bits/param is the Double Quantization overhead for the block sizes above), then convert to bytes: $M_{\text{base}}\approx N_{\text{params}}\times(4+0.127)/8\ \text{bytes}$. The paper reports 5,048 MB as the practical measured GPU footprint for their implementation (the small gap between a strict per‑param calculation and 5,048 MB is due to implementation overheads, embeddings/layernorm params, allocator rounding/alignment and other runtime metadata — see the Appendix for the exact measured breakdown).

Caveats and practical notes
- These numbers are implementation and dataset dependent: average sequence length, blocksize choices, whether you apply LoRA to all linear layers or only Q/V, and whether you page optimizer state to CPU all change the reported MB values.  
- The key takeaway (and what the paper stresses) is that for PEFT on a frozen quantized base model the dominant memory item is per‑token activations needed to compute adapter gradients; adapters themselves are tiny. Checkpointing + freezing the base and paging small optimizer states to CPU are the mechanisms that make the numbers small enough to finetune large models on limited GPUs.  
- For exact byte‑for‑byte reproduction use the QLoRA code + the Appendix memory table; the paper’s Appendix contains the detailed per‑tensor breakdown that yields the quoted 5,048 / 567 / 26 / 18 MB figures.

### Controlling GPT-4 evaluation bias: how were order effects handled and how should relative scores be reported?
Question:
For the Vicuna-style evaluation with GPT-4 scoring, the paper mentions strong order effects (earlier response in the prompt gets higher scores) and recommends reporting the mean over both orders. Concretely, how did you structure the prompt pairs and aggregate scores to mitigate this bias? If I want to compare a new model to ChatGPT, is the recommended protocol to always (1) evaluate both (Model, ChatGPT) and (ChatGPT, Model) orders, (2) average the per-prompt scores across orders, and (3) then average across prompts to get the final relative score?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — do exactly what you describe. In the paper we found a clear “first-response” bias from GPT‑4, so for the Vicuna-style 0–10 scoring we evaluated each prompt in both orderings (Model first / ChatGPT second and ChatGPT first / Model second), averaged the scores for each system across those two orderings, and then aggregated across prompts to produce the final relative number. That averaging (a simple counterbalancing) removes the linear order bias that made the earlier response get systematically higher scores.

Concretely, a safe protocol you can follow (and consistent with the paper) is the following. For each human prompt $i$ produce the two model responses and call GPT‑4 twice: once with the candidate response shown first and once with ChatGPT first. Let the scores returned be $s^{(1)}_{M,i}$ and $s^{(1)}_{C,i}$ for order 1, and $s^{(2)}_{M,i}$ and $s^{(2)}_{C,i}$ for order 2. Compute the order‑corrected per‑prompt scores
$s_{M,i} = (s^{(1)}_{M,i} + s^{(2)}_{M,i})/2$ and $s_{C,i} = (s^{(1)}_{C,i} + s^{(2)}_{C,i})/2$.
Then compute the overall mean scores across your $n$ prompts
$\bar{s}_M = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n s_{M,i},\quad \bar{s}_C = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n s_{C,i},$
and report the relative performance as
$R = 100 \times \frac{\bar{s}_M}{\bar{s}_C}$.
(You can also compute per‑prompt ratios $r_i = 100\cdot s_{M,i}/s_{C,i}$ and report the mean $r_i$; both are defensible but the paper reported a model’s percent of ChatGPT’s score as shown above.)

A few practical points from the paper you should keep in mind. First, always randomize and run both orders rather than relying on a single order: the bias we saw is large enough to change rankings. Second, report uncertainty (bootstrap confidence intervals over prompts) because point estimates can have wide CI’s (the paper notes wide overlap across models). Third, consider complementing numeric scoring with head‑to‑head preference judgments and Elo aggregation (the paper used a 3‑way win/tie/loss judgment for pairwise matches and constructed Elo ratings, repeating the random sampling many times to reduce noise) — Elo avoids anchoring problems of an absolute 0–10 scale and is what we recommend as a robustness check. Finally, remember that GPT‑4 itself has evaluation biases (e.g., it favored its own outputs in our study), so validate with some human judgments if possible.

So yes: (1) evaluate both orders, (2) average per‑prompt scores across orders, and (3) aggregate across prompts (and report CIs). Complement this with pairwise/Elo evaluations for a more robust picture.
