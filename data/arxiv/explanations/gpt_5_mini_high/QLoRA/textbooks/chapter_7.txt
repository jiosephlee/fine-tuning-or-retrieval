\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 7: Instruction Finetuning at Scale with QLoRA and the Guanaco Models

# Eight instruction datasets and their characteristics
The QLoRA study deliberately considered a diverse set of eight instruction-following datasets to explore how finetuning data affects downstream chatbot and language-understanding performance. These eight datasets fall into several broad categories that capture important differences in origin, size, and intended use.

Two datasets come from crowd-sourcing and multi-turn human conversations. OASST1 (Open Assistant) is a crowd-sourced collection of multi-turn dialogs designed to be useful for instruction-following and RLHF-style pipelines; importantly, its collection guidelines explicitly forbid using GPT models to generate the data. The paper highlights that a relatively small OASST1 subsample (about 9k examples in the experiments they report) proved especially effective for chatbot finetuning. HH-RLHF (a human-preference dataset) is another crowd-sourced source that contains human preference signals and multiple candidate responses per turn.

A second group of datasets were produced by distillation from instruction-tuned models. These include Alpaca, self-instruct, and unnatural-instructions. These datasets were created by generating instruction–response pairs from a teacher model and then using those pairs as supervised finetuning data. Because they inherit characteristics of the teacher, these datasets are useful for producing models that mimic the behavior of the source model(s) used to generate them.

FLAN v2 is an example of a corpora-aggregation dataset: it gathers many different tasks and instruction formats together, producing a large, heterogeneous corpus that has been shown in prior work to improve general-purpose reasoning and few-shot performance. In the QLoRA experiments, the authors subsampled FLAN v2 (the example quoted compares OASST1 ~9k to a subsampled FLAN v2 of ~450k) to study how dataset size and quality trade off.

Finally, the authors include two “hybrid” or aggregated datasets: Chip2 (from LAION / OIG) and Longform. These hybrids combine different sources and generation strategies and are intended to support longer, more open-ended responses. Together, these eight datasets provide a spectrum of data origins (human vs. model-generated), sizes, styles (single-turn instruction–response vs. multi-turn dialogs), and therefore varying suitability for downstream tasks such as MMLU-style knowledge tests versus open-ended chatbot conversations.

The paper’s empirical findings emphasize that these differences matter: different datasets excel at different benchmarks (for example, FLAN v2 tending to help MMLU, and OASST1/Chip2 tending to help chatbot-style benchmarks). That is why the authors trained multiple Guanaco variants on different datasets rather than assuming a single large dataset would dominate across tasks.

# Training recipe: supervised cross-entropy without RLHF
A central design choice for the Guanaco family is a simple, consistent training recipe: supervised finetuning with cross-entropy loss only, without any reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). The authors intentionally used cross-entropy to avoid "confounding effects from different training objectives" so that comparisons across datasets, model sizes, and finetuning strategies remain apples-to-apples.

Concretely, every Guanaco finetuning run used the QLoRA setup: the pretrained base model weights were stored in a 4-bit quantized representation (NF4) and kept frozen, while a set of Low-Rank Adapter (LoRA) parameters were learned. Forward and backward computation were performed in a 16-bit computation type (BrainFloat/BFloat16), with dequantization on the fly from the NF4 storage format; only the LoRA parameters received weight updates. In addition to NF4, the production recipe used Double Quantization to compress quantization constants and Paged Optimizers to manage memory spikes during gradient checkpointing. These implementation details are part of the standardized QLoRA pipeline that made training large Guanaco variants feasible on single GPUs.

When the models are evaluated for generation (as in the chatbot benchmarks), the authors used nucleus sampling with $p=0.9$ and temperature $0.7$ consistently. Keeping this decoding configuration and the single training objective (cross-entropy) fixed allows the experimental comparisons to focus on dataset choice, model size, and the QLoRA finetuning mechanics rather than mixing in RLHF or different decoding hyperparameters.

# Response-only vs full conversation finetuning choices
The datasets the authors used vary in whether they present clean instruction→response pairs or multi-turn conversations where the distinction between instructions and responses is blurred. Accordingly, the Guanaco training pipeline applied two different, but principled, treatments depending on dataset structure.

For datasets that present a clear instruction and a corresponding response (the standard instruction–response format), the authors finetuned only on the response text. This “response-only” choice is part of the effort to avoid confounding objectives: it focuses the optimization on mapping the prompt (instruction) to the desired generated content, rather than training the model to reproduce the entire prompt–response formatting or to model dialogue structure in auxiliary ways. The paper notes that ablations were performed (see their Appendix) to validate choices like this.

For multi-response or tree-structured conversational datasets such as OASST1 and HH-RLHF, where multiple candidate responses can exist for a single prompt or where the dialog structure includes intermediate human/assistant turns, the authors followed a different procedure. They selected the top response at every level of the conversation tree and finetuned on the full selected conversation, including the instructions. In other words, they distilled the multi-response conversation down to a single exemplar conversation (the chosen best reply at each turn), and trained on that full context. This choice preserves the multi-turn conversational structure when the dataset is explicitly conversational, while still imposing a single canonical target for supervised learning.

These practical decisions—response-only for single-turn, canonical-conversation for multi-turn—help keep the supervised objective consistent across heterogeneous datasets and make cross-dataset comparisons more meaningful.

# Hyperparameter transfer rules across 7B, 13B, 33B, 65B
A key practical question for scaling finetuning is how hyperparameters found for a small model apply to larger models. The QLoRA experiments establish a simple, empirically supported transfer rule that the authors used across the Guanaco family.

First, the authors conducted a modest hyperparameter search at the 7B scale and found robust settings; they then tested which of these settings generalized to larger models. They report that most hyperparameters generalize from 7B to 13B and 33B—number of epochs, LoRA rank-related choices, and many optimizer choices were transferable. The notable exceptions were learning rate and batch size. Empirically, they found it helpful to reduce learning rate and increase batch size as model size grows. In their practice: the 33B and 65B runs used half the learning rate of the 7B settings and doubled the batch size. This rule (halve LR, double batch size when moving to 33B/65B) was an empirical heuristic that produced stable training across the larger scales they studied.

Another important LoRA-specific finding relates to where adapters are applied. The common practice in some prior work was to apply LoRA only to query and value projection matrices in attention layers. The authors found that to match the performance of full 16-bit finetuning, it was necessary to place LoRA on all linear transformer block layers—i.e., a denser placement of adapters—rather than only a small subset. On the other hand, some LoRA hyperparameters such as the projection dimension $r$ appeared to have a smaller effect on finetuning quality, making them less critical tuning knobs in practice.

For baselines and 16-bit full finetuning comparisons, the authors also searched learning rates over $[1\mathrm{e}{-6}, 5\mathrm{e}{-5}]$ and batch sizes between $8$ and $128$ to ensure robust comparisons. The takeaway is that a modest tuning effort at a small scale, plus the “halve LR / double batch size” rule when moving to the largest sizes, yielded stable transfer of training recipes across 7B, 13B, 33B, and 65B models.

# Hardware and time budgets for Guanaco training
One of the headline contributions of QLoRA is that it reduces the memory barrier to finetuning large models. In standard 16-bit finetuning, a LLaMA 65B model would demand more than $780\,$GB of GPU memory, placing large-scale finetuning out of reach for most research groups. QLoRA reduces this requirement dramatically by storing the base model weights in 4-bit NF4 format, applying Double Quantization to compress quantization constants, and using Paged Optimizers to avoid training-time memory spikes. With these techniques, the average memory requirement for finetuning a 65B model drops to under $48\,$GB, enabling single-GPU finetuning on a 48GB device.

These memory savings translate into practical, time-bound training budgets for Guanaco variants. The authors report that their best Guanaco model (65B, trained on a variant of OASST1) was finetuned in about 24 hours on a single professional 48GB GPU and achieved 99.3% of ChatGPT performance on the Vicuna benchmark. A 33B Guanaco model can be finetuned on a single 24GB consumer GPU in less than 12 hours; the paper highlights that this makes training large, capable models accessible without server-scale infrastructure. The smallest Guanaco 7B model has a very small deployment footprint (5 GB), allowing even phone-level deployment for inference.

Beyond single runs, QLoRA’s efficiency enabled massive experimentation: the authors finetuned more than 1,000 models across model sizes, datasets, and settings to study instruction tuning trends. They also released adapters for 7B, 13B, 33B, and 65B models trained on the eight datasets, amounting to 32 open-source finetuned adapters. In short, the combination of quantization, adapter finetuning, and optimizer engineering lets researchers explore many training conditions within realistic hardware and time budgets.

# Baselines: Vicuna, Open Assistant, GPT-4, ChatGPT, Bard
In evaluating Guanaco, the authors compared against both open-source research models and commercial systems. Understanding these baselines clarifies what Guanaco was measured against.

Vicuna is a community model produced by full finetuning of LLaMA 13B on a large collection of user-shared conversations from ShareGPT; it represents a distilled, open-source effort that inherits behaviors from the proprietary teacher models whose outputs were shared. Open Assistant (as a deployed model in these comparisons) is a LLaMA 33B model finetuned with RLHF on the OASST1 dataset, and thus represents an RLHF-trained open assistant benchmark.

On the commercial side, the authors evaluated against ChatGPT (GPT-3.5-turbo) and GPT-4, as well as Bard, to place Guanaco’s performance in the context of current state-of-the-art proprietary chatbots. GPT-4 was also used as an automated judge: the authors used GPT-4 to rate model responses relative to ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark and to carry out pairwise comparisons between systems. Notably, the paper reports an ordering bias in GPT-4 evaluations (a tendency to favor the response appearing earlier in the prompt), so the authors controlled for this ordering effect by averaging scores over both presentation orders.

To complement automated judgments, they ran human evaluations through Amazon Mechanical Turk. For pairwise comparisons and Elo-style tournament ranking, they combined human and GPT-4 judgments. The tournament setup used standard Elo updating (starting score 1000, $K=32$) and repeated the randomized tournament process 10,000 times to stabilize rankings. Agreement between GPT-4 and human system-level rankings was moderate (Kendall Tau $\tau=0.43$, Spearman $r=0.55$), and sample-level agreement was weaker (Fleiss $\kappa=0.25$), which the authors use to argue both that automated judging is a reasonable proxy in many settings and that it is not a perfect substitute for human evaluation.

# Data quality matters more than size finding
One of the clearest empirical lessons from the QLoRA/Guanaco experiments is that dataset suitability and quality often outweigh sheer size. The authors present direct evidence that a relatively small, high-quality dataset designed for human-style assistant dialogs can outperform much larger aggregated instruction datasets when the evaluation task is chatbot-style conversation.

The paper gives a striking example: a roughly 9k subsample from OASST1 produced better chatbot performance than a 450k subsample of FLAN v2 in the Vicuna-style evaluations. Conversely, the FLAN v2 data tends to improve MMLU-style multiple-choice knowledge and reasoning benchmarks, while conversational datasets like OASST1 and aggregated, dialog-like sources (e.g., Chip2) more directly improve open-ended chatbot behavior. This shows that “bigger” is not automatically “better”: the alignment between the dataset’s distribution (single-turn instruction–response vs. multi-turn conversation, style and language mix, etc.) and the downstream evaluation is the critical factor.

The authors summarize this as dataset suitability: strong performance on one benchmark (e.g., MMLU) does not imply strong performance on another (e.g., Vicuna chat evaluations). Practically, this means that for a target application—say, a conversational assistant—curating or prioritizing high-quality conversational data can be a better use of finetuning resources than indiscriminately enlarging the training set.

# Deployment memory footprints for Guanaco variants
One of the operationally important outcomes of using QLoRA and 4-bit storage is that Guanaco variants have markedly different and, in many cases, modest deployment footprints compared to full 16-bit models.

The smallest Guanaco model, the 7B variant, requires approximately 5 GB of memory for deployment. This is small enough to be feasible on many modern phones and edge devices and is highlighted by the authors as enabling new privacy-preserving and on-device finetuning/deployment possibilities. The 33B Guanaco, because it stores weights in 4-bit format for deployment, occupies about 21 GB of memory; the paper contrasts this with the Vicuna 13B baseline, which takes about 26 GB in the configuration reported. For the very largest 65B case, the paper emphasizes that QLoRA makes single-GPU finetuning possible on a 48GB device by reducing finetuning memory from the 16-bit footprint of over 780 GB down to less than 48 GB. (The exact deployment footprint of a 65B model after quantized storage depends on the runtime and inference configuration; the paper focuses on the training memory barrier that QLoRA overcomes and reports concrete deployment numbers for the more compact 7B and 33B variants.)

These memory characteristics matter for real-world deployment decisions. A 7B model at a 5 GB footprint is compatible with many on-device use cases; a 33B model at ~21 GB can be served on a single high-end GPU or CPU server with compressed weights; and QLoRA itself enables finetuning these models on single consumer or professional GPUs within reasonable time budgets. The combination of competitively high performance (e.g., Guanaco 65B reaching 99.3% of ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark) together with these compact footprints is what makes the Guanaco family notable: strong chatbot capability without requiring cluster-scale finetuning or unwieldy deployment hardware.

--- 

In summary, the QLoRA finetuning recipe and the resulting Guanaco family illustrate a reproducible, resource-efficient path to build high-quality instruction-following chatbots using only supervised cross-entropy finetuning. The experimental program shows how dataset choice and format are as important—if not more important—than nominal dataset size, how modest hyperparameter transfer rules let you scale recipes from 7B to 65B, and how quantized-adapter training makes large-model finetuning accessible on single GPUs with practical deployment footprints. The authors released the trained adapters and code to allow others to reproduce and extend these findings.
