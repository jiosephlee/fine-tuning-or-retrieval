\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 8: Evaluating Chatbots: Benchmarks, Protocols, and Elo Ratings

# Benchmarks: Vicuna prompts and OASST1 validation set
Evaluating chatbots requires different kinds of tests because "understanding" in a multiple‑choice, knowledge‑recall sense is a different capability from producing helpful, conversational, open‑ended responses. In QLoRA the authors separate these concerns by using a standard Natural Language Understanding (NLU) benchmark and two generative, human‑style prompt sets for chatbot evaluation.

For NLU they use the Massively Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) benchmark. MMLU is a multiple‑choice suite that spans 57 domains (elementary mathematics, history, computer science, law, and so on). In the QLoRA experiments the models are evaluated in the 5‑shot setting and performance is reported as accuracy. MMLU therefore serves as a compact, reproducible measure of raw factual and reasoning capability in a constrained format.

For generative, conversational evaluation the paper uses two curated prompt sets. The Vicuna prompt set is a short, diverse set of 80 prompts assembled to probe many categories of interactive behavior; the prompts are used as provided. The second is derived from the Open Assistant / OASST1 validation split: the authors selected every user message in the validation data and included preceding turns from the conversation as context, producing a set of 953 unique user queries. The paper refers to these two generative testbeds as the "Vicuna" and "OA" benchmarks. The OA benchmark captures more realistic, multi‑turn human assistant interactions and is multilingual, while Vicuna is a smaller, more focused prompt suite. Together these two families of tests give complementary perspectives: MMLU tests structured NLU capability, Vicuna/OA test freeform conversational output quality.

# Decoding setup consistency: nucleus sampling $p=0.9$ and temperature $0.7$
When comparing generative outputs across models, decoding decisions (how the model samples tokens at generation time) materially affect both the style and quality of responses. To avoid confounding evaluation by differing sampling behavior, the QLoRA experiments fix decoding hyperparameters across all models and tests. Concretely, every model generation reported in the paper uses nucleus sampling with $p=0.9$ and temperature $0.7$. These choices are applied to the Vicuna and OA benchmarks (and to qualitative examples) so that differences in output reflect model behavior rather than different randomness or sampling regimes.

Fixing the decoding recipe is a practical requirement for fair comparison: changing sampling temperature or other settings can make outputs shorter or more variable and can change human or automated judgments. By holding sampling parameters constant the authors control one important source of variance in generative evaluation and make relative comparisons between models more meaningful.

# GPT‑4 absolute scoring vs pairwise comparisons with ties
The paper uses two automated evaluation modes with GPT‑4: an absolute scoring protocol and a simplified pairwise comparison protocol. Both are run against the same generative benchmarks but they answer slightly different questions.

In the absolute scoring protocol (inspired by prior work on the Vicuna benchmark) GPT‑4 is shown a query and two responses (ChatGPT’s response and the evaluated model’s response) and is prompted to assign a numeric score out of ten to each, together with an explanation. To summarize a model's overall quality, the authors compute the model's aggregate score as a percentage of ChatGPT’s score; that percentage can exceed $100\%$ if the evaluated model outperforms ChatGPT on average. This approach gives an interpretable, magnitude‑based comparison against a reference system.

The pairwise protocol simplifies judgments to a three‑class choice: given the same query and two candidate responses, GPT‑4 is asked to choose which response is better or to declare a tie, and to justify the choice. The authors run these head‑to‑head comparisons across all permutations of system pairs on both Vicuna and OA. These pairwise labels are easier to translate into a tournament structure and are the input the paper uses to build Elo rankings of models.

Each mode has strengths: absolute scoring yields a magnitude relative to a fixed anchor (ChatGPT) but is susceptible to scale and anchoring effects, while pairwise comparisons avoid the need to interpret an absolute scale and are directly compatible with ranking methods like Elo.

# Controlling ordering effects by averaging over both orders
A concrete empirical finding in the paper is that GPT‑4 exhibits an ordering bias: it tends to assign higher scores to the response presented earlier in its prompt. The authors detect a "significant ordering effect" in their absolute scoring experiments. This is an important practical pitfall: if one always shows Model A first and Model B second, GPT‑4’s bias can create a systematic advantage for the first slot.

To mitigate this, the authors recommend and apply a simple control: present each pair of responses in both orders and report the mean of the two scores. Averaging across both presentation orders reduces the impact of positional bias on the final aggregate numbers. For tournament / Elo experiments they go further by randomizing pair orderings and repeating ranking computations many times (see the next section) so that ordering and match sampling effects are averaged out in the final reported ranking.

# Human annotation via AMT and agreement statistics
Automated evaluators are convenient and cheap, but human judgments remain the gold standard. QLoRA complements GPT‑4 ratings with human annotation collected via Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT). Two human annotators are used for comparisons to ChatGPT (absolute scoring against ChatGPT), and three annotators are used for pairwise comparisons. The human labels are used both to compute pairwise outcomes and to validate the behavior of GPT‑4 as a proxy rater.

Inter‑annotator agreement statistics are reported to quantify reliability. The paper reports a Fleiss' $\kappa$ of $0.42$ among human annotators overall, which the authors describe as "moderate agreement." When comparing GPT‑4 to human judgments the agreement is weaker: at the per‑example level the Fleiss' $\kappa$ between GPT‑4 and the human majority vote is $0.25$. At the system (model) ranking level GPT‑4 and human evaluations are more concordant, with Kendall's $\tau = 0.43$ and Spearman's rank correlation $r = 0.55$. These numbers indicate that automated and human judgments align reasonably at the aggregate ranking level but can differ substantially on individual examples.

These statistics carry two practical implications. First, human labeling is subjective: even trained annotators disagree enough that agreement metrics should always be reported. Second, GPT‑4 is an imperfect stand‑in for humans—cheap and informative, but not identical—so combining both sources and reporting agreement helps users understand where automated evaluation may mislead.

# Tournament design and Elo computation with $K=32$ and resampling
To convert pairwise comparisons into a coherent global ranking, the paper uses a tournament-style design aggregated via Elo ratings. Matches are formed by pairing systems on a prompt; the winner of each match is determined by either GPT‑4 or human annotators (or a majority human vote). Elo is used to turn these match outcomes into an ordinal, continuous score for each model.

Operationally, the authors initialize every model with a rating of $1{,}000$ and update ratings after each match using an Elo update rule with $K=32$. The Elo update mechanics (as used in the paper) make rating changes proportional to how surprising an outcome is: an underdog victory produces a larger rating shift than an expected win. To mitigate sampling and ordering effects the authors do not compute a single deterministic Elo run; instead they repeatedly sample from the set of labeled comparisons and recompute Elo many times. Specifically, they repeat the resampling and Elo computation process $10{,}000$ times with different random seeds. This extensive resampling controls for which pairings appear in which order and yields a stable distribution of Elo values from which robust system rankings can be estimated.

The combination of pairwise labeling, randomized sampling, and repeated Elo computations is the paper's practical recipe for converting many noisy, subjective judgments into a comparatively stable ranking.

# Interpreting Elo, uncertainty, and expected win rates
Elo scores are relative quantities that should be read as comparative strengths, not absolute measures. The authors give a concrete example to calibrate intuition: a player (or model) with Elo $1{,}100$ against an opponent with Elo $1{,}000$ has an expected win probability of approximately $65\%$, whereas two players with the same Elo have expected win probability $50\%$ against each other. Thus modest differences in Elo translate into meaningful changes in head‑to‑head expected outcome rates.

Because the tournament data and judgments are noisy, the authors emphasize reporting uncertainty and avoiding overinterpretation of small Elo differences. The paper demonstrates this in practice by repeating Elo computations many times and noting when confidence intervals overlap (they observed wide confidence intervals in some absolute scoring tables). Repeated resampling produces a distribution over Elo scores; practitioners should report means and spread (or confidence intervals) from that distribution. If two models’ Elo intervals overlap substantially, their relative ordering is not robustly determined by the available comparisons.

A further cautionary example in the paper illustrates evaluator bias: GPT‑4 appears to assign higher ratings to its own outputs than human annotators do—GPT‑4’s self‑rated Elo is substantially higher than its Elo under human judgment (the paper cites an example difference in Elo that corresponds to roughly a $20\%$ higher expected win probability). This finding underscores the importance of reporting both human‑based and automated rankings and of treating automated evaluators as imperfect proxies.

# Biases and recommended reporting practices
QLoRA surfaces several practical biases and attendant reporting recommendations:

- Positional bias. Automated raters (GPT‑4) show a measurable ordering effect favoring the response shown earlier. Mitigation: present pairs in both orders and report averages; randomize presentation order in pairwise comparisons.

- Automated‑vs‑human divergence. GPT‑4 and humans agree at the system ranking level in many cases but disagree enough on specific examples that both sources should be reported. Mitigation: collect a modest number of human labels per item, report inter‑annotator agreement (e.g., Fleiss' $\kappa$), and publish both human and automated results.

- Scale grounding and comparability. Absolute numeric scales (e.g., scores out of ten) can be ambiguous across contexts and raters and are susceptible to anchoring. Mitigation: complement absolute scores with pairwise comparisons and Elo aggregation to avoid reliance on a single, possibly ungrounded numeric scale.

- Transparency and resampling. Because match sampling and ordering affect rankings, repeat and resample tournament computations (the authors use $10{,}000$ resamples) and report the resulting distribution of Elo scores rather than a single point estimate.

- Reproducible generation. Fix decoding parameters across models (the paper uses nucleus sampling with $p=0.9$ and temperature $0.7$) and publish model outputs and annotations. The QLoRA authors open‑sourced all model generations with both human and GPT‑4 annotations to make follow‑up analyses possible.

Finally, the paper recommends caution when interpreting benchmark numbers: dataset suitability matters (a finetuning dataset that is similar to a benchmark can inflate scores), and evaluators should be mindful that even "state‑of‑the‑art" automated raters are imperfect. Future work should investigate evaluator biases and study mitigation strategies, drawing on disciplines such as Human–Computer Interaction and psychology to handle subjective preferences more systematically.

Concluding summary
The QLoRA study demonstrates a practical, repeatable evaluation protocol for modern chatbots: pair the standard NLU testbed (MMLU) with curated generative prompts (Vicuna, OA); fix decoding hyperparameters ($p=0.9$, temperature $0.7$) to avoid confounds; collect both automated (GPT‑4) and human judgments; control ordering effects by averaging over orders and randomization; convert pairwise judgments into Elo rankings (start at $1{,}000$, use $K=32$) with heavy resampling ($10{,}000$ runs) to quantify uncertainty; and always report inter‑annotator agreement and both automated and human results. Following these practices gives a more robust, transparent, and interpretable comparison of chatbot quality than single‑measure evaluations.
