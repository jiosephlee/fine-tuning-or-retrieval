\title{Blogs about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

# Guanaco vs. ChatGPT: What the Scores Really Say

Guanaco — a family of models finetuned with QLoRA — reached up to 99.3% of ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark while being finetunable in roughly 24 hours on a single 48GB GPU. That headline is exciting, but the paper behind Guanaco also shows why interpreting a single percentage can be misleading. This post unpacks how the authors measured those numbers, why they used Elo and pairwise comparisons, what order effects and model-vs-human disagreements reveal, and what conclusions are safe to draw.

## Quick summary
- Guanaco 65B achieved 99.3% of ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark (33B reached about 97.8% in the paper). These comparisons are specific to the Vicuna evaluation protocol.
- The authors evaluated models with both GPT‑4 (automated) and human annotators (AMT), using two test suites: the 80 Vicuna prompts and ~953 OASST1 validation queries.
- They found significant evaluation pitfalls: GPT‑4 shows order bias (favoring the response shown first), model-based judgments can differ from humans, and absolute 10‑point scores have wide uncertainty.
- To reduce scale and ordering issues they ran pairwise comparisons and aggregated results with Elo ratings (initial rating 1000, $K=32$, repeated 10,000 times to control randomness).
- Takeaway: Guanaco is impressive on Vicuna, but close numeric parity on one benchmark does not imply overall parity with ChatGPT. Evaluation choices matter a lot.

## How Guanaco was evaluated (short version)
The authors used three complementary evaluation channels:

1. GPT‑4 absolute scoring versus ChatGPT. For each prompt they supplied ChatGPT’s and the target model’s responses and asked GPT‑4 to score both out of 10 (and explain). The model’s overall performance was reported as a percentage of ChatGPT’s score. They discovered a pronounced ordering effect, so they averaged scores across both possible response orders.

2. Pairwise comparisons (automated and human). For every pair of systems they asked GPT‑4 (and human annotators) to pick the better response or declare a tie. This simplifies evaluation to a three-way outcome (win/tie/loss) and is more robust to scale interpretation.

3. Human evaluations (AMT). Two annotators per item were used for comparisons to ChatGPT, and three annotators for pairwise comparisons. These human judgments were used both directly and to calibrate/compare automated ratings.

Generation settings for these evaluations were consistent: nucleus sampling with $p=0.9$ and temperature $0.7$.

## Why Elo (and how it was used)
Single-number scores (e.g., “X/10”) are easy to misinterpret because they require anchoring what each point on the scale means. Pairwise comparisons avoid that by asking directly which response is better. Elo is a simple, well-known way to convert many pairwise outcomes into a scalar ranking. The expected score for player A against B under Elo is:

$E_A = \dfrac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_B - R_A)/400}}$,

and a rating update after a match uses $\Delta R = K(S - E_A)$ where $S$ is the actual score (1/0/0.5). The authors initialized ratings at 1000, used $K=32$, and repeated the sampling/aggregation 10,000 times to reduce randomness and ordering artifacts.

Why this helps:
- Pairwise results are less sensitive to absolute scale and individual annotator calibration.
- Elo aggregates many local comparisons into a consistent global ranking.
- Repeating the tournament controls for sampling noise and ordering dependencies between matches.

## Order effects and GPT‑4 biases
A concrete problem the authors found: GPT‑4 tends to award higher scores to the response that appears earlier in its prompt. That creates a systematic ordering bias in absolute-score evaluations. The practical fix they used was to evaluate both orderings and average the scores.

They also observed a more troubling bias: GPT‑4 tends to assign higher scores to its own outputs (self-favoring). In the paper’s comparisons, GPT‑4’s own outputs had a substantially higher Elo under GPT‑4 judging than under human judging (Elo of 1348 vs 1176). That difference corresponds to a material shift in expected win probability and means automated model-based evaluation can introduce systematic distortions.

The punchline: if you rely on an LLM to evaluate other LLMs, check for order effects and calibration biases and compare with human judgments.

## Human vs GPT‑4: agreement (and limits)
Aggregate agreement between GPT‑4 and human annotators is moderate at the system level but weak at the example level:

- System-level rank correlation: Kendall Tau $\tau=0.43$, Spearman $r=0.55$ (moderate agreement).
- Example-level agreement between GPT‑4 and human majority vote: Fleiss $\kappa=0.25$ (weak).
- Inter-human agreement was higher but still imperfect: Fleiss $\kappa=0.42$.

Interpretation: GPT‑4 is a reasonable, cheap proxy for humans in many ranking contexts, but it is not a drop-in replacement. Individual examples often show disagreement, and GPT‑4’s biases (ordering, self-favoring) can change results in aggregate.

## What the 99.3% (and similar numbers) actually mean
- The 99.3% figure is relative performance on the Vicuna benchmark under a specific evaluation pipeline (GPT‑4 scoring averaged over orderings and other choices in the paper). It does not imply Guanaco exactly matches ChatGPT across all tasks or user scenarios.
- The Vicuna benchmark itself is small (80 prompts) and, according to the paper, tends to favor open-source models. By contrast the larger OA benchmark (OASST1) favors ChatGPT. Different test sets produce different orderings.
- Confidence intervals on Vicuna scores were wide and many models overlapped, so small percentage differences should be treated cautiously.
- Qualitative failure modes still exist in Guanaco (the paper shows examples): confident factual errors on obscure items, arithmetic mistakes unless the model shows step-by-step calculation, inconsistent refusals, and leakable “secrets” under adversarial phrasing. Numeric closeness on Vicuna does not erase these practical gaps.

## Practical takeaways and recommended practices
Based on the paper’s experiments and findings, here are pragmatic rules for researchers comparing chatbots:

- Prefer pairwise comparisons and Elo aggregation over single-ended absolute scores. Pairwise judgments reduce anchoring problems; Elo provides a principled ranking from those judgments.
- When using model-based evaluation (e.g., GPT‑4), test for order effects and average over permutations of response order.
- Calibrate automated evaluations with a human-annotated subset. Model-based evaluators can be correlated with humans but still show systematic biases.
- Report uncertainty: confidence intervals, overlaps between models, and cross-benchmark results (Vicuna vs OA vs other tasks) give a fuller picture than a single percent.
- Evaluate across multiple benchmarks and include qualitative failure-mode analysis. Benchmarks measure different capabilities; dataset suitability matters (the paper shows small, high-quality datasets can outperform large, noisy ones for chat behavior).

## Bottom line
Guanaco is an important demonstration: with QLoRA it’s practical to finetune very large models on a single GPU and produce a top-tier open-source chatbot that is very close to ChatGPT on at least one respected benchmark. But the headline percentages (e.g., 99.3%) must be read in context: evaluation protocol, dataset choice, ordering biases, and evaluator calibration all influence results. The field should keep refining evaluation practices (pairwise + Elo, controlled orders, human calibration) so we can trust comparisons as models continue to improve.
