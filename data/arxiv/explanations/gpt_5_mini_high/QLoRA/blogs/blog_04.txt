\title{Blogs about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

# LoRA That Actually Matches 16‑bit: What Hyperparameters Matter

LoRA is a now-standard way to finetune large models cheaply, but many common LoRA setups fall short of full-model (16‑bit) finetuning. The QLoRA paper shows why that happens and, more importantly, what to change so adapter finetuning reliably hits parity. The short story: where you place adapters (how many layers get them) matters far more than the usual knobs people obsess over (the low-rank dimension r, tiny parameter counts). Below I summarize the paper's empirical findings and give a compact checklist you can apply when finetuning to match 16‑bit performance.

## Quick primer (one equation)
LoRA adds a low-rank update to an existing linear projection. Given $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}$, LoRA injects a factorized update:
$\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + s\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2$,
where $\mathbf{L}_1,\mathbf{L}_2$ are small trainable matrices and $s$ is a scalar. The LoRA parameters are tiny compared to the base model, so the appeal is obvious: minimal extra params, much less memory to store updated weights.

## The surprising empirical fact
When the authors tried the "default" LoRA recipe used in many papers (apply LoRA only to the attention query and value projections), they could not reproduce full-model 16‑bit finetuning performance on large base models. Their systematic investigation shows:

- The most important LoRA hyperparameter is the number of adapters — i.e., how many linear projections in the transformer actually receive LoRA modules.
- To recover full finetuning accuracy you generally need LoRA applied to every linear layer inside transformer blocks (attention projections and MLP projections), not just a couple of attention matrices.
- Other LoRA knobs (projection rank $r$, the exact fraction of parameters added) have much smaller effects on final performance. In short, coverage across layers beats larger adapters in a few layers.
- Baselines must be well tuned. The paper performed LR searches (1e‑6 to 5e‑5) and batch-size searches (8–128) and notes that default 16‑bit baselines are often undertuned — so be careful when comparing.

These conclusions come from experiments such as LLaMA‑7B finetuned on Alpaca where increasing the number of adapter placements was the dominant factor for closing the gap to full finetuning.

## Intuition: why layer coverage beats rank
- A LoRA adapter in a single projection gives the model a limited, local degree of plasticity. If most projections remain frozen, the network cannot reconfigure internal representations that upstream or downstream layers expect.
- Spreading small low‑rank adapters across every linear layer gives the finetuning process many small degrees of freedom throughout the computation path; collectively these small updates can emulate the effect of updating the full model.
- Increasing $r$ in a few layers gives you more capacity, but concentrated capacity cannot substitute for the ability to adapt representations at every stage.

## Memory reality check (why this is practical)
LoRA parameters are small. In the paper's example, commonly used LoRA setups were roughly 0.2% of the original model parameters. The dominant memory cost during finetuning is activations and their gradients, not the adapter weights themselves. This means you can add adapters densely (many layers) without blowing up memory, especially when combined with techniques like gradient checkpointing or QLoRA-style quantized base models.

## Practical checklist to hit 16‑bit parity
- Place adapters broadly. Apply LoRA to all linear transformer block layers (attention projections and MLP layers), not just query/value. This is the single biggest lever.
- Don’t sweat $r$ first. Try a modest $r$ (common practice values are small) and prioritize adapter coverage over increasing $r$ in a few layers.
- Tune optimization hyperparameters for your scale. The authors searched LRs in [1e‑6, 5e‑5] and batch sizes in [8, 128]. They also found that hyperparameters discovered on 7B generalize to larger sizes except for learning rate and batch size; for 33B/65B they halved the LR and doubled the batch size relative to the 7B recipe.
- Make sure your 16‑bit baselines are well tuned before declaring LoRA underperforms — default baselines are often undertuned.
- Use gradient checkpointing / memory-saving tricks so you can afford adapters on every layer. (The adapter parameters are small; it’s the activations that dominate.)
- If you’re using quantized-base approaches (e.g., QLoRA), this makes dense adapter placement even more practical: base-model memory drops and you can afford adapters everywhere.

## Caveats and scope
- These conclusions are drawn from the experiments in the QLoRA paper (LLaMA, T5, several datasets and scales up to 65B in the QLoRA context). The authors explicitly note that reproducing full 16‑bit *full-model* finetuning at 33B/65B scale is expensive and left some questions for future work; however, they do show that dense LoRA finetuning recovers 16‑bit LoRA performance across scales in their experiments.
- The exact best value of $r$, initialization strategies, scaling $s$, and precise learning-rate schedules were not the dominant axis in the experiments; you may still get small gains from tuning them for niche tasks, but they are secondary to adapter placement.

## Bottom line
If your LoRA finetuning doesn't match full-model finetuning, the first thing to change is not the rank $r$ or the tiny adapter size — it's where you place the adapters. Give the model the ability to adapt representations throughout the network by adding adapters to every linear layer in transformer blocks. Then tune learning rate and batch size (and make sure your 16‑bit baseline was properly tuned). Do that and you’re likely to get the 16‑bit parity that many LoRA recipes miss.
