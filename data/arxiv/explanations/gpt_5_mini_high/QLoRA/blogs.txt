\title{Blogs about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

# From 780GB to 48GB: How QLoRA Makes Giant LLM Finetuning a One‑GPU Job

Finetuning a 65B‑parameter language model used to be a multi‑server problem: a standard 16‑bit training run can require well over 780 GB of GPU memory. QLoRA flips that constraint on its head. By storing the pretrained base model in 4‑bit form, freezing those weights, and backpropagating only into small Low‑Rank Adapters (LoRA), QLoRA cuts the runtime memory footprint to under 48 GB — enough to finetune 65B on a single 48 GB GPU with no loss in quality versus 16‑bit training (for the evaluations reported). The paper uses this trick to train the Guanaco family of chatbots, with the 65B Guanaco reaching 99.3% of ChatGPT’s Vicuna score after a single‑GPU finetune.

This post walks through the core idea, the three technical pieces that make it work, and the empirical payoff — all grounded in the QLoRA paper.

## The core idea, in plain terms

Two simple constraints make QLoRA powerful:

- store the expensive pretrained weights at 4‑bit precision (aggressively compress storage), and
- do not change those weights during finetuning; instead, learn tiny LoRA adapter matrices that are responsible for task adaptation.

Crucially, QLoRA still computes gradients correctly: during forward/backward passes the 4‑bit weights are dequantized to a higher‑precision compute type (BFloat16), the model executes in 16‑bit compute as usual, and gradients flow through the frozen dequantized weights into the LoRA parameters alone. That lets the adapters recover whatever precision the quantized base "lost" without storing the base model in full precision.

A compact expression of the single‑layer forward pass used in QLoRA is:

$\mathbf{Y}^{\text{BF16}} =  \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\,\text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}) \;+\; \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_1^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_2^{\text{BF16}}.$

Here the first term is the dequantized frozen base weight product (computed in BF16) and the second term is the LoRA adapter contribution (learned and updated). The nested dequantization looks like

$\text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}}) = \text{dequant}(\text{dequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}), \mathbf{W}^{\text{4bit}}) = \mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}.$

Two design choices make this efficient and high‑quality: a smarter 4‑bit format (NF4) and a compact way to store quantization constants (Double Quantization). A third systems trick (Paged Optimizers) handles occasional memory spikes during training.

## The three technical ingredients that matter

1. 4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4)

Pretrained weight matrices are approximately zero‑mean and normally distributed. NF4 is a small 4‑bit data type that is information‑theoretically optimal for zero‑centered normal data: instead of trying to estimate empirical quantiles for each block, NF4 uses the theoretical normal quantiles for a standard normal and then rescales the tensor into the NF4 range. The NFk quantiles are computed (for $k$ bits) as

$\displaystyle q_i = \frac{1}{2}\left( Q_X\!\left(\frac{i}{2^k+1}\right) + Q_X\!\left(\frac{i+1}{2^k+1}\right)\right),$

where $Q_X(\cdot)$ is the quantile function of $N(0,1)$. NF4 also uses a small asymmetric tweak to ensure an exact discrete zero point (important for padding, etc.). In practice NF4 reduces 4‑bit quantization error compared to simple FP4 or Int4 formats.

2. Double Quantization

Block‑wise quantization needs to store a scale/constant per block. With small blocks that overhead becomes meaningful: for blocksize 64 with 32‑bit constants you pay $32/64 = 0.5$ bits per parameter just for constants. Double Quantization compresses those constants themselves: it quantizes the per‑block constants to a second, coarser quantization (e.g., FP8) with a larger blocksize. Using FP8 for the quantized quantization constants and blocksize choices of 64 for weights and 256 for the constants reduces the constants overhead to

$8/64 + 32/(64\cdot256) = 0.127$ bits per parameter,

a saving of $0.373$ bits per parameter — roughly a 3 GB saving for a 65B model. The paper reports no empirical degradation from this extra compression.

3. Paged Optimizers (system trick)

A lot of finetuning memory comes from optimizer states and activation gradients, and occasional long sequences can cause short memory spikes that lead to OOM. QLoRA uses NVIDIA unified memory to allocate optimizer state memory as "paged": optimizer tensors are automatically moved to CPU RAM when not needed on the GPU and paged back when required. In practice this prevents OOMs from rare spikes; in a runtime analysis the authors report no training‑speed penalty for 65B runs on a 48 GB GPU at a realistic batch size.

## How the pieces fit in the training loop

- Storage data type: 4‑bit NF4 for base weights (cheap).
- Compute data type: BF16 (dequantize to BF16 on the fly).
- LoRA adapters: small BF16 matrices added at each linear layer; these are the only parameters that get updated.
- Gradients: derived by computing derivatives in BF16 through the dequantized base; gradients for the base weights are not stored or applied.

An important practical finding: the usual minimal LoRA placement (only query/value matrices) was not enough to match full finetuning quality. To recover 16‑bit finetuning performance, QLoRA places LoRA adapters in all linear layers; the adapter size hyperparameter $r$ is not very sensitive, but the number and placement of adapters are.

Also note: most of a LoRA finetune's memory is not the adapter parameters themselves (tiny) but activation gradients. Gradient checkpointing and the paged optimizer strategy keep those spikes under control.

## Empirical payoff: Guanaco and benchmarks

Because QLoRA makes large‑model finetuning affordable, the authors trained a large suite of models (over 1,000 experiments across model sizes from ~80M to 65B, multiple architectures and eight instruction datasets). Key empirical takeaways:

- QLoRA with NF4 + Double Quantization recovers 16‑bit full finetuning performance on GLUE and Super‑NaturalInstructions for models up to 3B, and it matches 16‑bit LoRA performance on MMLU from 7B up to 65B.
- Memory: a 65B model finetuned in 16‑bit would need >780 GB; QLoRA drives that down to <48 GB (so you can finetune 65B on a single 48 GB GPU).
- Training time: a 33B Guanaco can be finetuned on a 24 GB consumer GPU in under 12 hours; Guanaco 65B reaches 99.3% of ChatGPT’s Vicuna score after ~24 hours on a single 48 GB GPU. The smallest Guanaco (7B) runs in ~5 GB of memory and outperforms a 26 GB Alpaca model on Vicuna by about 20 percentage points.
- Data matters more than scale: a small high‑quality dataset (OASST1, ~9k samples) outperformed a much larger subsampled FLAN v2 (450k) for chatbot performance, illustrating that dataset suitability beats sheer size for instruction tuning.

## Evaluation: tournaments, GPT‑4, and human raters

The authors evaluated chatbots using pairwise, tournament‑style comparisons aggregated into Elo ratings. Matches were judged either by humans (AMT) or GPT‑4. Results and caveats:

- GPT‑4 and humans largely agree about system rankings, but there are notable disagreements. System‑level agreement between GPT‑4 and humans had Kendall Tau $\tau=0.43$ and Spearman $r=0.55$; sample‑level agreement was lower (Fleiss $\kappa=0.25$).
- The paper documents biases in automated evaluation (e.g., GPT‑4 shows an order bias favoring whatever response appears first, and GPT‑4 gives higher ratings to its own outputs than humans do).
- The recommendation is pragmatic: GPT‑4 is a useful, cheaper proxy for human evaluation but should be used carefully and ideally validated with humans.

## Qualitative failure modes (brief)

The Guanaco models are strong, but not perfect. The paper’s qualitative checks show typical LLM failure modes: confident errors on obscure facts, brittleness in math unless reasoning step‑by‑step, inconsistent refusals, and simple adversarial prompt tricks that can reveal a "secret" the assistant was asked not to disclose. There are also notable strengths, such as reasonable theory‑of‑mind answers and sensible refusals to answer impossible queries.

## Limitations and takeaway

QLoRA is a practical and well‑engineered advance that democratises finetuning of very large LLMs. Important caveats raised in the paper:

- The authors did not fully establish that QLoRA exactly matches *full* 16‑bit finetuning at 33B and 65B scales (resource limits prevented a direct 16‑bit baseline comparison at those scales). They do show parity with 16‑bit LoRA and parity with full finetuning up to smaller scales.
- Evaluation was concentrated on benchmarks in the paper (MMLU, Vicuna, OA) and the authors note that different benchmarks measure different skills; strong MMLU results do not imply strong chatbot performance and vice versa.
- The paper uses LoRA adapters specifically; other PEFT methods were not exhaustively compared at large scale, and more aggressive quantization (e.g., 3‑bit) remains an open area.

## Why this matters

QLoRA turns a memory barrier into a systems/algorithmic solution. By matching 16‑bit performance while storing the base model at 4 bits and training only small adapters, QLoRA makes it feasible for small labs and even single users to finetune very large models and iterate quickly. The authors released their code, CUDA kernels for 4‑bit training, and pretrained adapters and models to encourage reproducibility and further research.

Code and models (from the paper) are available at the project repositories:
- https://github.com/artidoro/qlora
- https://github.com/TimDettmers/bitsandbytes

If you care about practical finetuning of large LLMs, QLoRA is a technique worth trying: NF4 + Double Quantization + LoRA + paged optimizers give you high‑quality adapters for very large models without a huge GPU farm.



# NF4, Demystified: The 4‑bit Data Type That Keeps LLM Quality Intact

## Why a new 4‑bit format matters

Quantizing large language model weights to low precision is one of the fastest ways to cut memory and make huge models usable for training and inference. But naive 4‑bit formats (uniform Int4 or tiny floating variants like FP4) usually lose too much information: most weights lie close to zero and a few are large outliers, so uniformly spaced codepoints waste representational power. QLoRA introduces NormalFloat4 (NF4), a 4‑bit data type specifically built for normally distributed, zero‑centered neural network weights. The result: storage at 4 bits per parameter while preserving model quality in finetuning.

This post strips NF4 down to the essentials — what it is, why it works, and how it plugs into QLoRA.

## The core problem: outliers and wasted bins

A common low‑bit quantization approach rescales a tensor into the target range and rounds, e.g. for Int8:

\[
\mathbf{X}^{\text{Int8}} = \text{round}\!\left(\frac{127}{\operatorname{absmax}(\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}})}\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}}\right)
= \text{round}(c\cdot \mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}}),
\]

then dequantizes by dividing by the stored scale. If a few values in the block are large outliers, that single scale inflates the effective range and most small values collapse into a few quantization bins. The classic fix is block‑wise quantization (split the tensor into blocks and quantize each block independently), but the choice of the data‑type (what codepoints you allow) still matters a lot at 4 bits.

Quantile (or equal‑probability) quantization is information‑theoretically optimal if you know the data distribution: choose codepoints so that each quantization bin contains the same expected fraction of the data. In practice, exact quantile estimation over each block is costly and approximate algorithms (e.g., SRAM quantiles) can mishandle outliers.

NF4 leverages a simple observation: pretrained neural network weights are typically very close to a zero‑centered normal distribution. That lets you build a small, fixed quantization codebook based on the analytic quantiles of a standard normal distribution — no expensive per‑block quantile search.

## NF4 in one paragraph

NFk (NormalFloat with k bits) is a k‑bit quantization codebook constructed from the quantiles of the standard normal distribution. For k=4 (NF4) you get 16 codepoints chosen so that, under a normal model, each code covers an equal probability mass. To quantize a block you scale that block into the range $[-1,1]$ by its absolute maximum, then replace each value with the nearest NF4 codepoint. Dequantization restores a BF16 (or FP16/BFloat16) tensor for computation. An asymmetric tweak guarantees an exact discrete zero codepoint (important for padding and true zeros).

## How the NFk codebook is built (math)

Let $k$ be the number of bits (for NF4, $k=4$). NFk targets an equal expected number of values per bin under $N(0,1)$. One way to place representative codepoints is to take midpoints of the quantile intervals of the standard normal:

\[
q_i \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\!\left(Q\!\left(\frac{i}{2^k+1}\right) + Q\!\left(\frac{i+1}{2^k+1}\right)\right),
\]

where $Q(\cdot)$ is the quantile function (inverse CDF) of $N(0,1)$ and $i=0,\dots,2^k-1$. Those $q_i$ are then normalized into the fixed range $[-1,1]$ so they form the NFk codebook. At quantization time a block of weights is scaled into $[-1,1]$ via an absolute‑max rescaling (one scale constant per block) and each scaled value is mapped to the nearest $q_i$.

This construction gives NFk the information‑theoretic property of equal expected occupancy across bins under the normal model: most codepoints lie near zero (where the density is highest), and the sparse tail bins handle outliers.

## Handling zero exactly: the asymmetric tweak

A symmetric equal‑probability construction does not necessarily include an exact discrete zero, which matters for perfectly zero entries (e.g., padding) and avoids introducing tiny rounding errors for zeros. NFk fixes this by splitting the code allocation between negative and positive sides asymmetrically: estimate quantiles for the negative half with $2^{k-1}$ bins and for the positive half with $2^{k-1}+1$ bins, then unify and remove the duplicated zero so that one code maps exactly to zero while still using all $2^k$ codepoints. For NF4 this ensures a discrete zero code and full use of the 16 available codes.

## Block‑wise scaling and practical blocksize choices

NF4 is applied block‑wise (flatten the weight tensor and quantize contiguous blocks). Smaller blocks reduce the impact of local outliers but increase per‑block scale overhead. In QLoRA the authors use a blocksize of 64 for the model weights as a practical balance: good quantization fidelity while keeping the extra memory for block scales manageable. (Quantization constants themselves can be compressed further — see Double Quantization below.)

## Where NF4 fits in QLoRA (storage vs compute)

QLoRA stores the pretrained model weights in NF4 (4‑bit) on disk/GPU memory. At runtime the NF4 codes are dequantized to a 16‑bit computation format (BFloat16) for the forward and backward pass. LoRA adapters remain full precision (BF16) and are the only parameters updated during finetuning; gradients are backpropagated through the dequantized weights. The QLoRA forward pass for a linear layer with one LoRA adapter can be written as:

\[
\mathbf{Y}^{\text{BF16}} = \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\,\text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}) \;+\; \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_1^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_2^{\text{BF16}},
\]

where the stored NF4 codes for $\mathbf{W}$ plus their block scales are dequantized back to BF16 for matrix multiplies.

## Double Quantization (briefly) and memory tradeoffs

Blockwise quantization requires storing a scale per block; at small blocksize that adds nontrivial overhead (e.g., 32‑bit scales with blocksize 64 cost roughly 0.5 bits/parameter). QLoRA introduces Double Quantization: quantize the per‑block scales themselves (the quantization constants) with an 8‑bit float grid and a larger blocksize (256), which reduces the scale overhead dramatically (paper reports a reduction of about 0.373 bits/parameter on average). NF4 pairs well with Double Quantization: NF4 handles the weight distribution efficiently, while Double Quantization keeps auxiliary overhead small.

## Why NF4 outperforms FP4 and Int4

- Matching the data distribution: NF4 codepoints concentrate where weights actually live (near zero) instead of giving equal spacing (Int4) or tiny floating layout that isn’t tuned to the distribution (FP4).
- Equal‑probability bins minimize the expected quantization distortion under the normal model.
- Exact zero handling avoids tiny rounding artifacts for zeros (useful for padding).
- Avoids expensive per‑block quantile estimation because the normal quantiles are analytic.

Empirically, the QLoRA paper shows NF4 yields markedly better post‑quantization perplexity and zero‑shot accuracy than FP4 and Int4 across multiple architectures and sizes, and NF4 combined with Double Quantization gives the memory savings with no measurable loss in finetuning quality compared to 16‑bit baselines.

## Practical takeaways

- NF4 is a practical, distribution‑aware 4‑bit scheme built for the common case of zero‑centered, approximately normal neural weights.
- Use NF4 with block‑wise scaling (paper uses blocksize 64 for weights) so outliers are contained locally.
- Keep computation in BF16 (or similar) — NF4 is a storage format; dequantize to 16 bits for matmuls and gradients.
- Combine NF4 with Double Quantization for the block scales to avoid scale overhead.
- The asymmetric zero codepoint is important: it preserves exact zeros and uses all 16 codepoints efficiently.

## Bottom line

NF4 is a compact, distribution‑aware 4‑bit codebook that aligns quantization bins to where pretrained weights actually concentrate. That simple idea — build equal‑probability codepoints from the standard normal, add a discrete zero codepoint, quantize blockwise, and dequantize to BF16 for compute — lets QLoRA store massive models at 4 bits without the quality losses that plagued earlier 4‑bit methods. The result: large‑scale finetuning and inference that are far more memory efficient, yet maintain the performance of higher‑precision baselines. For anyone building quantized LLM systems, NF4 is the first 4‑bit format to reach for. 

For exact NF4 codepoint values and implementation details, see the QLoRA paper appendices and the released code and CUDA kernels.



# Squeezing the Last Bits: Double Quantization and Paged Optimizers Explained

QLoRA made finetuning very large models on a single 24–48 GB GPU practicable. Two relatively low‑profile engineering moves are crucial to that result: Double Quantization (DQ), which compresses the quantization scales themselves, and Paged Optimizers, which use NVIDIA Unified Memory to absorb occasional memory spikes from gradient checkpointing. This post walks through what these two tricks actually do, why they matter, and how they fit into QLoRA’s overall design.

## TL;DR
- When you quantize weights blockwise you must also store a per‑block scale (quantization constant). For small blocks that scalar becomes a nontrivial memory tax: bits_per_param = bits_of_constant / blocksize.
- Double Quantization quantizes those quantization constants again (using 8‑bit floats and a larger blocksize), dramatically shrinking that overhead with no observed accuracy loss.
- Paged Optimizers allocate optimizer state in CUDA Unified Memory so optimizer buffers can be evicted to host RAM and paged back on demand, preventing out‑of‑memory (OOM) failures caused by transient gradient‑checkpointing spikes.

Both are small changes in concept but multiply into gigabytes of savings at the 65B scale.

## Quick background: why quantization constants matter
Blockwise k‑bit quantization stores for each block:
- the k‑bit quantized values (e.g., NF4 for the weights), and
- a per‑block scalar quantization constant $c$ (typically stored in 32‑bit float).

That scalar is necessary to dequantize a block back to floating point when you need to compute with it. The per‑parameter overhead from the scale is
$$
\text{bits per parameter for constants} = \frac{\text{bits\_of\_constant}}{\text{blocksize}}.
$$
Example: with 32‑bit constant and blocksize 64, the constants add
$$
32/64 = 0.5
$$
bits per parameter. For very large models and the small block sizes you want for high precision, that overhead is meaningful.

## Double Quantization: compressing the compressors
The core idea of Double Quantization is simple: treat the per‑block quantization constants themselves as data to be quantized. Concretely, QLoRA does two levels of quantization:

1. Quantize the weight blocks to a k‑bit data type (QLoRA uses 4‑bit NormalFloat, NF4), producing per‑block constants $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$.
2. Quantize those constants $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ to a smaller representation (QLoRA uses 8‑bit floats with a larger blocksize), producing quantized constants $c_2^{\text{FP8}}$ plus a second‑level scalar $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$.

Two practical points the paper highlights:
- Because the raw $c_2$ values are positive, QLoRA subtracts their mean before the second quantization to center them around zero and use symmetric quantization, which is simpler and more efficient.
- They use a small blocksize (64) for the actual NF4 weight blocks (for precision) and a larger blocksize (256) for the second‑level quantization of $c_2$ (to reduce overhead).

What happens at runtime is a small nested dequantization step. If you call the nested dequant function doubleDequant, the forward path converts stored NF4 + quantized constants back into a BF16 matrix that can be used for matrix multiplication. In QLoRA notation:
$$
\text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}},\, c_2^{\text{k-bit}},\, \mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}})
= \text{dequant}\big(\text{dequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}), \mathbf{W}^{\text{4bit}}\big)
= \mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}.
$$

Why this helps (numbers)
- Baseline per‑parameter cost of 32‑bit constants with blocksize 64: $32/64 = 0.5$ bits.
- After double quantization, per‑parameter cost becomes
$$
\frac{8}{64} + \frac{32}{64\cdot256} = 0.127\ \text{bits},
$$
because the second quantization uses 8 bits per c2 value at blocksize 256 and stores the top‑level $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$ at full precision but shared across larger groups.
- Net saving: $0.5 - 0.127 = 0.373$ bits per parameter.

At 65B parameters that is roughly
$$
65\cdot10^9 \times 0.373\ \text{bits} \approx 24.25\cdot10^9\ \text{bits} \approx 3.03\cdot10^9\ \text{bytes},
$$
i.e., about a 3 GB reduction from quantization constants alone. The QLoRA paper reports this average saving and also reports no empirical performance drop from this second quantization stage (the authors use FP8 for the constants and observed no degradation).

Bottom line: Double Quantization lets you keep small weight block sizes (high precision) without paying the full penalty of many 32‑bit per‑block scales.

## Paged Optimizers: smoothing runtime memory spikes
Quantization reduces steady‑state storage for weights, but another source of training OOMs is transient memory spikes. Gradient checkpointing is used to trade compute for memory by saving fewer activations and recomputing them in the backward pass; this reduces overall memory but can create temporary peaks (e.g., when processing a long sequence) that briefly exceed GPU memory and trigger OOM.

Paged Optimizers mitigate that by putting optimizer state in CUDA Unified Memory (managed memory). The GPU runtime will automatically move pages between GPU and host RAM as needed. QLoRA’s approach:

- Allocate optimizer buffers (the optimizer state used during the parameter update step) in Unified Memory.
- During the forward/backward pass, when a spike would otherwise exhaust GPU memory, the CUDA runtime can evict some optimizer pages to host RAM to make room.
- When the optimizer update runs, the required pages are paged back to the device.

This is effectively using host RAM as an automatic overflow store for rarely‑touched optimizer state. It’s like OS swapping but handled at the CUDA page level and driven by actual memory access patterns rather than explicit developer bookkeeping.

Practical observations from the paper:
- Paged Optimizers were essential for running 33B/65B QLoRA on a single 24/48 GB GPU because they absorb the occasional, hard‑to‑predict spikes caused by long sequences.
- The authors did not provide comprehensive runtime benchmarks because paging is workload dependent; in one reported analysis, for a 65B model on a 48 GB GPU with batch size 16, paged optimizers showed the same training speed as regular optimizers. That suggests paging cost can be negligible when it happens rarely.
- If paging happens frequently (many page faults), you will see runtime slowdowns because moving pages between host and device has cost. The point is that for gradient‑checkpointing spikes that are infrequent, paging is a practical and easy way to avoid OOM.

## How these two fit into QLoRA’s pipeline
QLoRA combines:
- NF4 storage for weights (very compact, information‑theoretically tuned to normal weight distributions),
- Double Quantization of per‑block scales (to shrink constant overhead),
- LoRA adapters stored and trained in BF16, and
- Paged Optimizers for optimizer state.

At runtime a linear layer with a LoRA adapter looks like:
$$
\mathbf{Y}^{\text{BF16}} = \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\,\text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}})\;+\;\mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_1^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_2^{\text{BF16}}.
$$
Weights stay stored compactly, are dequantized for the multiply, and optimizer state lives in unified memory so rare peaks don’t kill the job. Together these design choices helped QLoRA reduce the average memory requirement for finetuning a 65B model from hundreds of GB down to a single 48 GB GPU, while preserving full 16‑bit finetuning performance.

## Practical tips and caveats
- Use a small blocksize for weight quantization (QLoRA uses 64) for precision, and a larger blocksize for the second quantization of scales (QLoRA uses 256) to cut overhead.
- Center the per‑block scales (subtract the mean) before second quantization so symmetric schemes work well.
- The paper uses 8‑bit floats (FP8) for the second quantization of the scales; they observed no accuracy degradation from this choice.
- Paged Optimizers are a pragmatic fix for infrequent spikes; if your workload causes continuous page thrashing you will pay a runtime penalty. Monitor page fault activity and tune batch size / sequence length accordingly.
- These techniques add a small implementation complexity (nested dequant and managed memory allocation), but the memory wins at large scale are substantial and verified in the paper.

## Conclusion
Double Quantization and Paged Optimizers are not flashy model ideas, but they remove two practical blockers to running large‑scale finetuning: the memory tax of per‑block quantization constants, and unpredictable runtime OOMs from checkpointing spikes. Together with NF4 and LoRA adapters, they helped QLoRA make 33B and 65B finetuning fit on single 24–48 GB GPUs while retaining 16‑bit finetuning quality. If you’re building memory‑tight training pipelines, both tricks are worth considering.



# LoRA That Actually Matches 16‑bit: What Hyperparameters Matter

LoRA is a now-standard way to finetune large models cheaply, but many common LoRA setups fall short of full-model (16‑bit) finetuning. The QLoRA paper shows why that happens and, more importantly, what to change so adapter finetuning reliably hits parity. The short story: where you place adapters (how many layers get them) matters far more than the usual knobs people obsess over (the low-rank dimension r, tiny parameter counts). Below I summarize the paper's empirical findings and give a compact checklist you can apply when finetuning to match 16‑bit performance.

## Quick primer (one equation)
LoRA adds a low-rank update to an existing linear projection. Given $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}$, LoRA injects a factorized update:
$\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + s\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2$,
where $\mathbf{L}_1,\mathbf{L}_2$ are small trainable matrices and $s$ is a scalar. The LoRA parameters are tiny compared to the base model, so the appeal is obvious: minimal extra params, much less memory to store updated weights.

## The surprising empirical fact
When the authors tried the "default" LoRA recipe used in many papers (apply LoRA only to the attention query and value projections), they could not reproduce full-model 16‑bit finetuning performance on large base models. Their systematic investigation shows:

- The most important LoRA hyperparameter is the number of adapters — i.e., how many linear projections in the transformer actually receive LoRA modules.
- To recover full finetuning accuracy you generally need LoRA applied to every linear layer inside transformer blocks (attention projections and MLP projections), not just a couple of attention matrices.
- Other LoRA knobs (projection rank $r$, the exact fraction of parameters added) have much smaller effects on final performance. In short, coverage across layers beats larger adapters in a few layers.
- Baselines must be well tuned. The paper performed LR searches (1e‑6 to 5e‑5) and batch-size searches (8–128) and notes that default 16‑bit baselines are often undertuned — so be careful when comparing.

These conclusions come from experiments such as LLaMA‑7B finetuned on Alpaca where increasing the number of adapter placements was the dominant factor for closing the gap to full finetuning.

## Intuition: why layer coverage beats rank
- A LoRA adapter in a single projection gives the model a limited, local degree of plasticity. If most projections remain frozen, the network cannot reconfigure internal representations that upstream or downstream layers expect.
- Spreading small low‑rank adapters across every linear layer gives the finetuning process many small degrees of freedom throughout the computation path; collectively these small updates can emulate the effect of updating the full model.
- Increasing $r$ in a few layers gives you more capacity, but concentrated capacity cannot substitute for the ability to adapt representations at every stage.

## Memory reality check (why this is practical)
LoRA parameters are small. In the paper's example, commonly used LoRA setups were roughly 0.2% of the original model parameters. The dominant memory cost during finetuning is activations and their gradients, not the adapter weights themselves. This means you can add adapters densely (many layers) without blowing up memory, especially when combined with techniques like gradient checkpointing or QLoRA-style quantized base models.

## Practical checklist to hit 16‑bit parity
- Place adapters broadly. Apply LoRA to all linear transformer block layers (attention projections and MLP layers), not just query/value. This is the single biggest lever.
- Don’t sweat $r$ first. Try a modest $r$ (common practice values are small) and prioritize adapter coverage over increasing $r$ in a few layers.
- Tune optimization hyperparameters for your scale. The authors searched LRs in [1e‑6, 5e‑5] and batch sizes in [8, 128]. They also found that hyperparameters discovered on 7B generalize to larger sizes except for learning rate and batch size; for 33B/65B they halved the LR and doubled the batch size relative to the 7B recipe.
- Make sure your 16‑bit baselines are well tuned before declaring LoRA underperforms — default baselines are often undertuned.
- Use gradient checkpointing / memory-saving tricks so you can afford adapters on every layer. (The adapter parameters are small; it’s the activations that dominate.)
- If you’re using quantized-base approaches (e.g., QLoRA), this makes dense adapter placement even more practical: base-model memory drops and you can afford adapters everywhere.

## Caveats and scope
- These conclusions are drawn from the experiments in the QLoRA paper (LLaMA, T5, several datasets and scales up to 65B in the QLoRA context). The authors explicitly note that reproducing full 16‑bit *full-model* finetuning at 33B/65B scale is expensive and left some questions for future work; however, they do show that dense LoRA finetuning recovers 16‑bit LoRA performance across scales in their experiments.
- The exact best value of $r$, initialization strategies, scaling $s$, and precise learning-rate schedules were not the dominant axis in the experiments; you may still get small gains from tuning them for niche tasks, but they are secondary to adapter placement.

## Bottom line
If your LoRA finetuning doesn't match full-model finetuning, the first thing to change is not the rank $r$ or the tiny adapter size — it's where you place the adapters. Give the model the ability to adapt representations throughout the network by adding adapters to every linear layer in transformer blocks. Then tune learning rate and batch size (and make sure your 16‑bit baseline was properly tuned). Do that and you’re likely to get the 16‑bit parity that many LoRA recipes miss.



# Guanaco vs. ChatGPT: What the Scores Really Say

Guanaco — a family of models finetuned with QLoRA — reached up to 99.3% of ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark while being finetunable in roughly 24 hours on a single 48GB GPU. That headline is exciting, but the paper behind Guanaco also shows why interpreting a single percentage can be misleading. This post unpacks how the authors measured those numbers, why they used Elo and pairwise comparisons, what order effects and model-vs-human disagreements reveal, and what conclusions are safe to draw.

## Quick summary
- Guanaco 65B achieved 99.3% of ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark (33B reached about 97.8% in the paper). These comparisons are specific to the Vicuna evaluation protocol.
- The authors evaluated models with both GPT‑4 (automated) and human annotators (AMT), using two test suites: the 80 Vicuna prompts and ~953 OASST1 validation queries.
- They found significant evaluation pitfalls: GPT‑4 shows order bias (favoring the response shown first), model-based judgments can differ from humans, and absolute 10‑point scores have wide uncertainty.
- To reduce scale and ordering issues they ran pairwise comparisons and aggregated results with Elo ratings (initial rating 1000, $K=32$, repeated 10,000 times to control randomness).
- Takeaway: Guanaco is impressive on Vicuna, but close numeric parity on one benchmark does not imply overall parity with ChatGPT. Evaluation choices matter a lot.

## How Guanaco was evaluated (short version)
The authors used three complementary evaluation channels:

1. GPT‑4 absolute scoring versus ChatGPT. For each prompt they supplied ChatGPT’s and the target model’s responses and asked GPT‑4 to score both out of 10 (and explain). The model’s overall performance was reported as a percentage of ChatGPT’s score. They discovered a pronounced ordering effect, so they averaged scores across both possible response orders.

2. Pairwise comparisons (automated and human). For every pair of systems they asked GPT‑4 (and human annotators) to pick the better response or declare a tie. This simplifies evaluation to a three-way outcome (win/tie/loss) and is more robust to scale interpretation.

3. Human evaluations (AMT). Two annotators per item were used for comparisons to ChatGPT, and three annotators for pairwise comparisons. These human judgments were used both directly and to calibrate/compare automated ratings.

Generation settings for these evaluations were consistent: nucleus sampling with $p=0.9$ and temperature $0.7$.

## Why Elo (and how it was used)
Single-number scores (e.g., “X/10”) are easy to misinterpret because they require anchoring what each point on the scale means. Pairwise comparisons avoid that by asking directly which response is better. Elo is a simple, well-known way to convert many pairwise outcomes into a scalar ranking. The expected score for player A against B under Elo is:

$E_A = \dfrac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_B - R_A)/400}}$,

and a rating update after a match uses $\Delta R = K(S - E_A)$ where $S$ is the actual score (1/0/0.5). The authors initialized ratings at 1000, used $K=32$, and repeated the sampling/aggregation 10,000 times to reduce randomness and ordering artifacts.

Why this helps:
- Pairwise results are less sensitive to absolute scale and individual annotator calibration.
- Elo aggregates many local comparisons into a consistent global ranking.
- Repeating the tournament controls for sampling noise and ordering dependencies between matches.

## Order effects and GPT‑4 biases
A concrete problem the authors found: GPT‑4 tends to award higher scores to the response that appears earlier in its prompt. That creates a systematic ordering bias in absolute-score evaluations. The practical fix they used was to evaluate both orderings and average the scores.

They also observed a more troubling bias: GPT‑4 tends to assign higher scores to its own outputs (self-favoring). In the paper’s comparisons, GPT‑4’s own outputs had a substantially higher Elo under GPT‑4 judging than under human judging (Elo of 1348 vs 1176). That difference corresponds to a material shift in expected win probability and means automated model-based evaluation can introduce systematic distortions.

The punchline: if you rely on an LLM to evaluate other LLMs, check for order effects and calibration biases and compare with human judgments.

## Human vs GPT‑4: agreement (and limits)
Aggregate agreement between GPT‑4 and human annotators is moderate at the system level but weak at the example level:

- System-level rank correlation: Kendall Tau $\tau=0.43$, Spearman $r=0.55$ (moderate agreement).
- Example-level agreement between GPT‑4 and human majority vote: Fleiss $\kappa=0.25$ (weak).
- Inter-human agreement was higher but still imperfect: Fleiss $\kappa=0.42$.

Interpretation: GPT‑4 is a reasonable, cheap proxy for humans in many ranking contexts, but it is not a drop-in replacement. Individual examples often show disagreement, and GPT‑4’s biases (ordering, self-favoring) can change results in aggregate.

## What the 99.3% (and similar numbers) actually mean
- The 99.3% figure is relative performance on the Vicuna benchmark under a specific evaluation pipeline (GPT‑4 scoring averaged over orderings and other choices in the paper). It does not imply Guanaco exactly matches ChatGPT across all tasks or user scenarios.
- The Vicuna benchmark itself is small (80 prompts) and, according to the paper, tends to favor open-source models. By contrast the larger OA benchmark (OASST1) favors ChatGPT. Different test sets produce different orderings.
- Confidence intervals on Vicuna scores were wide and many models overlapped, so small percentage differences should be treated cautiously.
- Qualitative failure modes still exist in Guanaco (the paper shows examples): confident factual errors on obscure items, arithmetic mistakes unless the model shows step-by-step calculation, inconsistent refusals, and leakable “secrets” under adversarial phrasing. Numeric closeness on Vicuna does not erase these practical gaps.

## Practical takeaways and recommended practices
Based on the paper’s experiments and findings, here are pragmatic rules for researchers comparing chatbots:

- Prefer pairwise comparisons and Elo aggregation over single-ended absolute scores. Pairwise judgments reduce anchoring problems; Elo provides a principled ranking from those judgments.
- When using model-based evaluation (e.g., GPT‑4), test for order effects and average over permutations of response order.
- Calibrate automated evaluations with a human-annotated subset. Model-based evaluators can be correlated with humans but still show systematic biases.
- Report uncertainty: confidence intervals, overlaps between models, and cross-benchmark results (Vicuna vs OA vs other tasks) give a fuller picture than a single percent.
- Evaluate across multiple benchmarks and include qualitative failure-mode analysis. Benchmarks measure different capabilities; dataset suitability matters (the paper shows small, high-quality datasets can outperform large, noisy ones for chat behavior).

## Bottom line
Guanaco is an important demonstration: with QLoRA it’s practical to finetune very large models on a single GPU and produce a top-tier open-source chatbot that is very close to ChatGPT on at least one respected benchmark. But the headline percentages (e.g., 99.3%) must be read in context: evaluation protocol, dataset choice, ordering biases, and evaluator calibration all influence results. The field should keep refining evaluation practices (pairwise + Elo, controlled orders, human calibration) so we can trust comparisons as models continue to improve.



# Quality Beats Quantity: Data Lessons from Finetuning 1,000+ Models

The QLoRA paper ran an unusually broad set of experiments — more than 1,000 finetuned models across 8 instruction datasets and model sizes from ~80M to 65B — and pulled one clear lesson about instruction tuning: dataset suitability and quality matter far more than raw size. A small, carefully curated instruction corpus (OASST1, ~9k examples) produced better chatbot behavior than a much larger subsample of FLAN v2 (~450k examples) for conversational benchmarks. Below I summarize the evidence, explain why this happens, and give pragmatic guidance for picking or creating finetuning data depending on your end goal.

## The empirical takeaway (what the paper shows)

- The authors systematically finetuned many models using QLoRA (efficient 4-bit finetuning) and compared performance on both knowledge benchmarks (MMLU) and chatbot-style benchmarks (Vicuna prompts and the OpenAssistant validation set).
- In those experiments, OASST1 — a relatively small, human-curated, multi-turn instruction/chat dataset (~9k samples) — outperformed a much larger FLAN v2 subsample (~450k) on chatbot evaluation (Vicuna / OA). The paper summarizes this as: "data quality is far more important than dataset size."
- Conversely, FLAN v2 tended to yield stronger results on MMLU (a multi-task, knowledge-style benchmark), showing that different datasets help different downstream behaviors.
- The best open models from this work (the Guanaco family) were finetuned on OASST1; Guanaco-65B reached 99.3% of ChatGPT performance on the Vicuna benchmark in their automated evaluation framework.

Those results make two linked points: (1) bigger is not always better, and (2) the right kind of data matters a lot depending on whether you want a knowledge-oriented model or a conversational assistant.

## Why high-quality, task-suitable data often wins

The paper doesn’t claim a single mechanical cause, but the experiments and dataset choices suggest several plausible mechanisms:

- Style and format alignment. Chatbots need multi-turn, instruction–response dialogues and a conversational tone. OASST1 provides human-authored, multi-turn interactions; FLAN-style mixtures are broader but often single-turn and task-diverse. When the finetuning data matches the expected interaction format, the model learns the right behavior more efficiently.
- Signal-to-noise ratio. Large aggregated corpora can contain many low-quality or mismatched examples. A smaller curated set with clear, high-quality responses concentrates learning on the behaviors you care about.
- Human preference signal. Datasets produced or filtered by humans (or that select the “top” responses) implicitly teach the model desirable response patterns. The authors explicitly selected top responses in multi-response datasets (OASST1, HH-RLHF) and then finetuned on the full selected conversation — a deliberate curation step that likely improved outcomes.
- Task coverage vs. depth tradeoff. FLAN v2-like data covers many tasks, which helps on multi-task knowledge benchmarks (MMLU). But breadth doesn’t substitute for depth when you need fluent, helpful, conversational behavior.

Put simply: if you want to build a chatbot, train on high-quality chat data; if you want multi-task exam-style knowledge ability, train on diverse instruction collections that resemble those benchmarks.

## MMLU vs. chatbot benchmarks — they measure different things

A consistent observation in the paper is that performance on MMLU (multiple-choice, knowledge-heavy tasks) does not reliably predict chatbot performance as measured by Vicuna or the OpenAssistant (OA) validation set. In practice:

- FLAN v2 finetuning improved MMLU scores but produced models that performed worse on Vicuna-type conversational prompts.
- OASST1-based finetuning produced strong conversational models (Guanaco) but did not claim to be the best for MMLU.

This matters for dataset selection: pick training data that mirrors your evaluation and deployment environment. If you optimize for a test like MMLU, you’ll likely get better scores there — but you may harm the qualities that make a good chatbot (multi-turn coherence, stylistic appropriateness, safety/ refusal behavior).

## Practical guidance: choosing finetuning data

Below are concrete, paper-supported suggestions for practitioners:

- Define the end goal up front. Knowledge retrieval? Code help? Open-domain chatbot? The finetuning data should look like your target usage.
- Favor curated, high-quality examples over large noisy dumps when the goal is behavioral improvement (e.g., conversation). The OASST1 vs. FLAN v2 comparison in the paper is a direct example.
- For chatbots, use multi-turn dialogues and preserve context. The authors finetuned on full selected conversations (instructions + responses), which helped the assistant learn multi-turn patterns.
- If your dataset contains multiple alternative responses, consider selecting the best/top response(s) rather than indiscriminately training on every variant. The QLoRA experiments explicitly selected top responses for OASST1 and HH-RLHF.
- If you need both factual knowledge and conversational skills, prioritize data that resembles the final interaction style. The paper shows that combining objectives without alignment to the target format can lead to suboptimal outcomes on the desired metric.
- Validate for data leakage. The authors checked for overlap between OASST1 and Vicuna prompts via fuzzy matching and found none — a useful verification step when benchmark proximity is suspected.
- Use efficient finetuning (e.g., QLoRA / LoRA) to run many controlled comparisons. The feasibility of training large models on single GPUs allowed the authors to explore dataset effects across scales and model families.

## Evaluation: avoid relying on a single automatic signal

The paper used both human annotators and GPT-4 for pairwise comparisons and aggregated results with Elo ratings. Important caveats emerged:

- GPT-4 and human judgments mostly agree on system-level rankings, but not perfectly. System-level Kendall Tau was ~0.43 and Spearman ~0.55 between GPT-4 and humans; sample-level agreement was weaker (Fleiss κ ≈ 0.25).
- GPT-4 showed ordering biases (favoring the first response in a prompt). The authors controlled for this by averaging scores over both orderings.
- Human annotators themselves showed only moderate agreement (Fleiss κ ≈ 0.42).
- Recommendation: use pairwise comparisons and Elo aggregation for robust relative ranking, run both automated (cheaper) and human (gold-standard) evaluations when possible, and control for evaluator biases (e.g., ordering).

The bottom line: automatic evaluators are useful and cost-effective, but validate critical claims with human judgment.

## Short checklist before you finetune

- Pick data that matches deployment format (multi-turn dialogue vs. single-turn instructions).
- Prioritize curation: prefer fewer high-quality examples if your target behavior is conversational.
- When using multi-response datasets, select the best responses and finetune on the whole conversation.
- Evaluate with pairwise comparisons and Elo; validate automated judges against humans on a held-out sample.
- If resources are tight, use QLoRA/LoRA-style finetuning to iterate quickly and test data choices at scale.

## Final thought

The QLoRA study makes the practical case that “more data” is not a universal solution. For instruction tuning — especially when your target is a chat-oriented assistant — a small, well-curated dataset that matches the interaction style can produce better downstream behavior than a much larger, generic corpus. Designing or selecting finetuning data with the deployment task in mind delivers far more bang for your compute buck than simply increasing sample counts.



# Your First QLoRA Finetune: A Practical Roadmap

TL;DR — QLoRA is a memory‑efficient recipe that lets you finetune very large pretrained models by storing the base weights in 4‑bit (NF4) and training small Low‑Rank Adapters (LoRA) in full precision. This post walks through the minimal, practical steps to reproduce the approach from the QLoRA paper using open‑source tooling (QLoRA + bitsandbytes + Hugging Face). You’ll learn what to quantize, where to place LoRA adapters, what to watch for with hyperparameters and memory, and how to run quick evaluations.

## Why QLoRA (one paragraph)
QLoRA reduces the memory footprint of finetuning a 65B model from hundreds of GBs to a single 48GB GPU (paper: from >780GB to <48GB) while preserving 16‑bit finetuning performance. It does this by (1) storing the pretrained weights in a 4‑bit NormalFloat format (NF4) tuned for normally distributed weights, (2) double‑quantizing the quantization constants to save more memory, (3) using LoRA adapters to learn task updates, and (4) using paged optimizers to avoid optimizer memory spikes. The authors used this to produce the Guanaco models (e.g., Guanaco‑65B that reaches ~99.3% of ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark).

## Before you start — minimal requirements & pointers
- Hardware: single GPU with enough memory for your target model: 24GB consumer GPUs can finetune 33B with QLoRA (paper example), 48GB GPUs are used for 65B. Smaller models (7B) fit easily (inference ~5GB).
- Software & repos: QLoRA implementation and CUDA kernels (https://github.com/artidoro/qlora) and bitsandbytes (https://github.com/TimDettmers/bitsandbytes). Hugging Face transformers integration is supported in these projects.
- Data: supervised instruction datasets (paper experimented with OASST1, Alpaca, FLAN v2, etc.). The paper found data quality matters more than size: OASST1 (≈9k high quality samples) outperformed much larger datasets in some chatbot evaluations.
- Objective: cross‑entropy supervised finetuning (no RLHF required to reproduce QLoRA results in the paper).

## The one‑line recipe
1. Load a pretrained model into 4‑bit NF4 storage with double quantized scales.
2. Dequantize to BF16 at runtime for computation.
3. Attach LoRA adapters to (almost) every linear layer in the transformer.
4. Train only the LoRA weights (BF16), using gradient checkpointing and paged optimizers.
5. Evaluate with nucleus sampling (p=0.9, temperature=0.7) and either human or GPT‑4 / Elo comparisons.

Below are the practical details for each step.

## Step 1 — choose a base model and dataset
Pick a base model that matches your compute and target: LLaMA 7B/13B/33B/65B were used in the paper. If you want to reproduce the paper’s high‑end results, use the largest model you can fit with QLoRA: the authors show 33B on a 24GB card and 65B on 48GB.

For instruction tuning data, prefer high‑quality human conversational datasets if you target chat behavior. The paper highlights OASST1 as a strong, compact dataset that produced top results for Guanaco.

Note about licenses: verify that the base checkpoint you use is permitted for your use case.

## Step 2 — enable 4‑bit quantization (NF4) + double quantization — what to do and why
QLoRA’s storage format differs from naive 4‑bit quantization in two important ways:

- NF4 (4‑bit NormalFloat): instead of uniform integer bins or a plain 4‑bit float, NF4 uses quantile bins matched to a standard normal distribution, then rescales. Because pretrained weights are approximately zero‑mean normal, this provides an information‑theoretically better 4‑bit mapping and lower quantization error than Int4 or plain FP4.

- Double Quantization: quantization uses per‑block scales (quantization constants). Storing those constants in 32‑bit for many small blocks can be expensive. Double quantization compresses those constants further (the paper uses an 8‑bit float quantization for those constants) to save memory while preserving accuracy. The paper uses blocksize 64 for weight blocks and blocksize 256 for quantized constants as a working point.

What to do in practice:
- Use the QLoRA / bitsandbytes code that implements NF4 + double quantization and the efficient CUDA kernels. That code handles block sizes and the double quantization pipeline for you.
- Set your computation dtype to BF16 (or FP16/BFloat16 variant supported by your hardware). The 4‑bit values are dequantized to BF16 before matmuls.

Why this matters: NF4 + double quantization is what lets you compress the base weights aggressively (storage) without hurting the forward and backward computations needed to backpropagate to LoRA adapters.

## Step 3 — place LoRA adapters (important): cover all linear layers
Central finding from the paper: the single most important LoRA hyperdecision is *where* adapters are applied. Applying LoRA only to a subset (e.g., query/value projections) is not sufficient to match full‑model finetuning for larger models. To replicate the paper’s recovery of 16‑bit performance:

- Put LoRA adapters on essentially every linear projection inside transformer blocks (i.e., the dense layers and attention projections).
- The LoRA update has the familiar form:
  $$
  \mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + s\,\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2
  $$
  where $\mathbf{L}_1\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times r}$ and $\mathbf{L}_2\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times o}$ are the adapter factors and $s$ is a scaling constant.

Implementation pointers:
- Use a PEFT/LoRA library or the QLoRA code. The QLoRA repo already applies adapters at every linear layer in the authors’ experiments.
- You only update the LoRA parameters (they are BF16); base weights remain frozen in 4‑bit storage and are dequantized to BF16 for computation.
- The paper finds the exact projection rank $r$ is not the dominant factor affecting final performance; increasing adapter coverage is far more important than tuning $r$ aggressively.

## Step 4 — training recipe and stable hyperparameter guidance
The paper’s practical guidance for stable training is empirical rather than prescriptive; follow this workflow:

- Objective: supervised cross‑entropy on your chosen instruction data. The paper’s high‑performing Guanaco models were trained with cross‑entropy only (no RLHF).
- Gradient checkpointing: use it — activation memory is the dominant memory consumer. QLoRA uses checkpointing together with paged optimizers to avoid spikes.
- Learning rate and batch size: the authors searched learning rates in the range $1\mathrm{e}{-6}$ to $5\mathrm{e}{-5}$ and batch sizes from 8 to 128 when tuning the 7B baselines. They found that hyperparameters found at 7B generalize to larger models except for learning rate and batch size. For 33B/65B they report halving the learning rate and doubling the batch size relative to the 7B tuned setting.
- Compute dtype: BF16 (computation), storage: NF4 (4‑bit). Dequantization to BF16 happens at runtime for matmuls.
- Optimizers: use optimizers with paged storage for states (the QLoRA repo implements paged optimizers using NVIDIA unified memory). This avoids OOM when batch/seq length induces spikes.
- Epochs / steps: the paper suggests hyperparameters other than LR and batch size generalize. It’s practical to tune on a small model first (7B) and transfer those settings.

Tip: tune your hyperparameters at a small scale (7B) while keeping the same dataset and training loops. Then scale the LR / batch size with the paper’s heuristic for larger models.

## Step 5 — quick, lightweight evaluation to check everything’s working
- Sampling for qualitative checks: nucleus sampling with $p=0.9$ and temperature $0.7$ (used in the paper for chatbot outputs).
- Automated quantitative checks:
  - MMLU 5‑shot accuracy for a quick sense of general knowledge/zero‑shot abilities (if relevant to your task).
  - Vicuna prompt set and OASST1 validation prompts for chatbot‑style checks.
- Human vs model evaluation: the paper used GPT‑4 as a cheap automated judge relative to ChatGPT and also ran human AMT evaluations; they caution GPT‑4 order bias (preference for the response shown first) and recommend averaging over both prompt orderings and using pairwise Elo aggregation for robust rankings.

If you’re reproducing a paper run, use pairwise comparisons and Elo scoring to rank your model against baselines. The authors aggregated pairwise wins into Elo to reduce the problems with absolute scoring.

## Common pitfalls and how to fix them
- “My QLoRA run is OOM”: ensure gradient checkpointing is enabled and use the paged optimizer provided by the QLoRA repo (it uses NVIDIA unified memory). Also reduce batch size or sequence length.
- “My LoRA finetune underperforms the 16‑bit baseline”: check that LoRA adapters are applied across all linear layers (not just attention q/k/v). Confirm that NF4 + double quantization code path is used (FP4 performed worse in the paper). Make sure compute dtype is BF16 so dequantized weights are computed at higher precision.
- “Quantization constants blowing memory”: double quantization (quantize the scales) is a core QLoRA trick. Use blocksize 64 for weights and blocksize 256 for the quantized scales as in the paper’s implementation for a good memory/accuracy tradeoff.
- “Inconsistent evaluation vs paper”: beware that evaluation has noise: the Vicuna style scoring has wide CIs and automated GPT‑4 judgments show order effects. Use Elo and multiple seeds/evaluators.

## Practical checklist before you kick off training
- [ ] Clone QLoRA repo and bitsandbytes; check the CUDA kernels are compiled and compatible with your CUDA driver.
- [ ] Confirm you can load the base model in 4‑bit NF4 mode and that dequantization to BF16 works.
- [ ] Attach LoRA adapters to all linear layers (use the QLoRA default adapter placement).
- [ ] Enable gradient checkpointing and paged optimizer support.
- [ ] Tune LR and batch size at 7B; when moving to 33B/65B halve LR and double batch size per the paper’s guidance.
- [ ] Use cross‑entropy supervised training; checkpoint LoRA weights only.
- [ ] Run a small evaluation: generate examples with nucleus sampling (p=0.9, temp=0.7) and compute quick metrics (MMLU / prompt set).

## Reproducibility resources
- QLoRA implementation and training scripts, including CUDA kernels: https://github.com/artidoro/qlora
- bitsandbytes for 4‑bit support and optimizer ops: https://github.com/TimDettmers/bitsandbytes
- Hugging Face Transformers integration: QLoRA has been integrated with HF pipelines in the official codebase per the paper.

## Final notes and realistic expectations
QLoRA is a practical, well‑engineered point on the compute/precision curve: you get the benefits of larger parameter counts with much lower memory use, while recovering the accuracy of 16‑bit finetuning. The paper’s authors used QLoRA to train Guanaco models that match or nearly match ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark (e.g., Guanaco‑65B ≈ 99.3% of ChatGPT as measured by their pipeline). That said, real‑world finetuning still requires careful validation of data quality, evaluation protocols, and safety checks (the paper stresses that data quality often beats dataset size for instruction tuning).

If you want a ready‑made starting point, clone the QLoRA repo and run the sample scripts. Tune at 7B to find stable hyperparameters, scale the training with the paper’s LR / batch scaling rule, and use NF4 + double quantization + paged optimizers. Happy finetuning — and welcome to a much more accessible way of tuning very large models.



# The Edge Is Calling: What QLoRA Means for On‑Device and Privacy‑First AI

Large language models used to mean large machines: tens or hundreds of gigabytes of RAM, multiple high‑end GPUs, and a cloud bill to match. QLoRA changes that assumption. By combining extreme (4‑bit) quantization with adapter tuning, the QLoRA paper shows you can finetune models that used to require vast server clusters on a single GPU — and push models that rival ChatGPT while keeping the base weights frozen. That has direct implications for private, custom, and on‑device finetuning.

In this post I’ll summarize how QLoRA works at a practical level, why its engineering choices matter for edge and privacy use cases, and what caveats you should keep in mind.

## What QLoRA does, in one paragraph

QLoRA lets you finetune a pretrained language model that is stored in 4‑bit form by backpropagating through the dequantized weights into small low‑rank adapters (LoRA). The base model weights stay frozen on disk in a compact 4‑bit representation; at runtime they are dequantized to a 16‑bit compute type (BFloat16) for the forward and backward pass, and only the adapter parameters are updated. With a combination of (a) a new 4‑bit data type tuned to normally distributed weights (NF4), (b) “double quantization” to shrink quantization metadata, and (c) paged optimizers that avoid memory spikes, QLoRA reduces the memory needed to finetune a 65B parameter model from more than 780 GB to under 48 GB of GPU memory while matching high‑precision finetuning performance.

## The key technical ideas (short and practical)

- Low‑rank adapters (LoRA). Rather than updating the full weight matrix $W$, QLoRA adds a small learned update via two low‑rank factors:
  $$
  Y = XW + s\,X L_1 L_2
  $$
  Only $L_1$ and $L_2$ are trained; $W$ remains frozen. In practice the adapter parameters are tiny compared to the base model, and adapters can be placed in every linear layer to recover full‑finetuning accuracy.

- 4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4). Pretrained weights are roughly zero‑mean and approximately normal. NF4 builds a 4‑bit data type whose levels are the quantiles of a standard normal distribution (then scaled per block). Formally the level centers are computed from the standard normal quantile function $Q_X$ (for $k$ bits):
  $$
  q_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(Q_X(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1}) + Q_X(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1})\bigr).
  $$
  NF4 is information‑theoretically optimal for normally distributed, zero‑centered weights and empirically outperforms FP4 and Int4 in the paper’s experiments.

- Double Quantization. Block‑wise 4‑bit quantization needs per‑block scaling constants, and those constants add nontrivial overhead. QLoRA quantizes those per‑block constants a second time (to an 8‑bit float representation), which reduces the average overhead from $32/64 = 0.5$ bits per parameter to
  $$
  \frac{8}{64} + \frac{32}{64\cdot256} = 0.127 \text{ bits/param},
  $$
  a saving of about $0.373$ bits/param. For a 65B‑parameter model that is roughly
  $$
  0.373\times 65\times10^9 \approx 24.25\times10^9\text{ bits} \approx 3.03\times10^9\text{ bytes} \approx 3.03\text{ GB},
  $$
  i.e., a meaningful memory win just from compressing the metadata.

- Paged Optimizers. QLoRA uses NVIDIA unified memory to page optimizer state between GPU and CPU to avoid out‑of‑memory spikes (for example, due to gradient checkpointing on long sequences). This allows single‑GPU finetuning of very large models without manual sharding of optimizer state.

- Compute/data flow. Storage is a single low‑precision type (NF4). Before multiply–accumulate the stored weights are dequantized to BFloat16, the adapters are in BFloat16, and all math happens in 16‑bit precision. For a single linear layer the computation looks like:
  $$
  Y^{\text{BF16}} = X^{\text{BF16}}\;\text{doubleDequant}(c_1,c_2,W^{\text{NF4}}) + X^{\text{BF16}} L_1^{\text{BF16}} L_2^{\text{BF16}}.
  $$

## Why this matters for on‑device and privacy‑first workflows

The practical upshots reported in the paper are easy to translate into real workflows:

- Single‑GPU finetuning of very large models. QLoRA reduces the memory requirement for finetuning a 65B model from >780 GB to <48 GB of GPU memory. The authors finetuned a 65B Guanaco model in about 24 hours on one 48 GB GPU, achieving 99.3% of ChatGPT’s performance on the Vicuna benchmark. A 33B Guanaco was finetuned in under 12 hours on a 24 GB consumer GPU and reached strong topline performance as well.

- Small footprints for deployment. The smallest Guanaco (7B) fits in about 5 GB of memory and, in the paper’s evaluations, outperforms a larger Alpaca model that required ~26 GB. That makes deployment to constrained hardware (edge servers, small GPUs, and phones) much more feasible.

- On‑device or local personalization. Because the adapter finetuning steps touch only small parameter sets and the base model can live in a quantized form, QLoRA opens up realistic paths to private, local finetuning. The paper even notes an estimate that an iPhone 12 Plus could finetune around 3 million tokens per night while charging (a concrete demonstration of on‑device feasibility suggested by the authors).

- Cost and access. If you can finetune a 33B model on a single 24 GB consumer card in a few hours, you dramatically lower both the monetary and logistical barriers to adapting models to internal data. That means smaller teams can do domain adaptation, personalization, or research experiments without sending sensitive data to cloud providers.

- Faster iteration on private data. Because QLoRA recovers high‑precision finetuning performance while using far fewer resources, you can iterate more quickly on proprietary datasets (fine‑grained customer support behavior, device logs, internal knowledge) without exposing data to third parties.

## Practical ways teams could use QLoRA

- Private assistant customization: a privacy‑sensitive organization could finetune a 33B model on internal transcripts or SOPs on a single 24 GB server and run the resulting adapter locally or in a VPC‑only environment.

- Personalized models on user devices: ship the 4‑bit base model and download a small adapter tuned to a user’s preferences, or run small nightly personalization finetunes on the device itself (the 7B/5 GB footprint and the phone estimate make this plausible).

- Lightweight research experiments: run hundreds of finetuning runs at different hyperparameters or datasets because the memory and cost reduction lets you scale experiments without a GPU cluster.

- Private data governance: keep training data behind company firewalls since the compute can be local, reducing compliance complexity.

## Limitations and cautions

The paper is upfront about limits you should consider before betting your privacy stack on QLoRA:

- Full‑model finetuning parity at the largest scales is not fully settled. The authors demonstrate that NF4 QLoRA recovers 16‑bit LoRA performance up to 65B for the benchmarks they ran, and that QLoRA matches full 16‑bit finetuning on smaller models (125M–3B). However, they did not conclusively establish that QLoRA exactly matches full 16‑bit full‑parameter finetuning at 33B and 65B across every possible task, primarily due to compute constraints. If your application absolutely depends on matching a full‑precision, full‑weight finetune for a particular evaluation, validation on your task is still required.

- Model weaknesses persist. Guanaco (the QLoRA‑tuned family) still exhibits typical LLM issues: math can fail, obscure factual recall can be confidently incorrect, and instruction‑following has edge cases (refusal behaviour can be inconsistent). These are not problems solved by quantization and adapter tuning; they are general model limitations.

- Evaluation issues. The paper highlights that automated evaluators such as GPT‑4 and human raters only moderately agree in the chatbot comparisons they ran, and automatic ratings showed ordering biases. Be cautious about using a single, automated ranking to conclude model supremacy for your use case.

- Responsible‑AI considerations. While QLoRA lowers the barrier for private finetuning (which can be positive), lowering the barriers to create high‑capability chatbots also raises dual‑use concerns. The authors note that finetuning is dual‑use and encourage careful evaluation of biases and safety behaviors.

## Open source and reproducibility

Importantly for practitioners, the authors released their code, CUDA kernels for 4‑bit training, and a collection of adapters: Guanaco adapters for 7B / 13B / 33B / 65B models, trained on eight instruction datasets (32 finetuned adapters total). They also integrated the approach into the Hugging Face ecosystem. That makes it straightforward to try QLoRA in your environment, reproduce results, and adapt the method to your private data.

## Bottom line

QLoRA is not a magic bullet that removes all model problems, but it is a practical and well‑engineered way to bring very large language models into much smaller memory and compute envelopes. For anyone concerned about keeping data in‑house, lowering finetuning costs, or experimenting with on‑device personalization, QLoRA materially changes the calculus: you can reasonably finetune models that previously required server fleets on a single GPU or even explore finetuning on a phone overnight. Do so thoughtfully, validate on your tasks, and account for the usual LLM pitfalls around faithfulness, reasoning, and safety.
