\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Does NF4 use absmax scaling or standard-deviation scaling? Reconciling two statements in the paper
Question:
The NF4 section says to normalize weights into the range $[-1,1]$ with absolute max rescaling, but it also says "Step (3) is equivalent to rescaling the standard deviation of the weight tensor to match the standard deviation of the $k$-bit data type." Absmax scaling is not the same as scaling by standard deviation. Which rescaling does NF4 actually employ in practice for the first-level quantization of $\mathbf{W}$: absmax to $[-1,1]$, matching standard deviations, or something else? If it is absmax, in what sense is Step (3) "equivalent" to rescaling $\sigma$?
Answer:
Short answer: NF4 uses block-wise absolute-maximum (absmax) rescaling in practice. The paper’s statement that “Step (3) is equivalent to rescaling the standard deviation” is a heuristic/explanatory remark: for (approximately) Gaussian, zero-mean weight blocks the block absmax is proportional to the block standard deviation, so dividing by the absmax has the same role as dividing by $\sigma$ up to a block-dependent multiplicative constant. In other words, absmax-scaling removes the per-block scale (the $\sigma$ factor) and therefore “matches” the scale that the NFk table expects.

Why this is true (quick derivation). Write a weight block as $W = \sigma Z$ with $Z \sim N(0,1)^B$ ($B$ = block size). Let $M = \max_i |W_i|$ be the block absmax; then
$$M = \sigma \cdot S_B,\quad S_B := \max_i |Z_i|.$$
The NF4 implementation uses the quantization constant
$$c = 1/M = 1/(\sigma S_B),$$
and the scaled block is
$$c W = \frac{W}{M} = \frac{Z}{S_B}.$$
So after absmax normalization the scaled block has standard deviation $1/S_B$ (a constant that depends on the particular sample of unit normals in the block) and no longer depends on the original $\sigma$. If instead you explicitly divided by $\sigma$ (i.e. standardize to unit normal) and then multiplied by whatever fixed factor is needed so the NFk levels sit in $[-1,1]$, you would be doing the same kind of multiplicative rescaling — the only difference is whether the multiplier is estimated via the block max (absmax) or via $\sigma$.

What the paper means by “equivalent” is therefore operational: both procedures align the block’s scale to the fixed scale that the NFk (quantiles computed from $N(0,1)$ and normalized into $[-1,1]$) assumes. The identity is not exact elementwise (absmax produces a block-dependent scale $1/S_B$, whereas dividing by $\sigma$ would give a deterministic mapping to unit variance), but under the normal-weight assumption $\mathrm{absmax} \propto \sigma$ so the two approaches have the same effect in expectation.

Why absmax in practice? The paper explicitly chooses absmax/blockwise range-matching (same pattern as common k-bit quantization) because it guarantees the full dynamic range $[-1,1]$ is used and is cheap to compute. Block size controls the behavior: $S_B$ (and hence the post-scale standard deviation $1/S_B$) depends on $B$ — for large $B$ the expected max grows like $\sqrt{2\log B}$ — so the choice of block size is part of the design tradeoff between robustness to outliers and effective use of bins.

So: NF4’s implemented first-level quantization = blockwise absmax-to-$[-1,1]$. The “equivalent to rescaling $\sigma$” sentence is a correct conceptual shorthand under the normality assumption, meaning absmax-scaling removes the per-block multiplicative scale (the $\sigma$) so the block’s variance is put on the fixed scale that NFk expects.
