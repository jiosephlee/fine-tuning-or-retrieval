\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 4: From KL-Regularized RL to Closed-Form Optimal Policies

# Optimal policy under KL constraint: $\pi_r(y\mid x) \propto \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$

We begin from the KL-regularized objective that is standard in RL-based preference learning. For a fixed prompt $x$ and a reward function $r(x,y)$, we seek a policy $\pi(\cdot\mid x)$ that maximizes the expected reward while paying a penalty for diverging from a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}(\cdot\mid x)$. Written per-prefix, the objective is
\[
\max_{\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\; \sum_{y}\pi(y\mid x)\,r(x,y)\;-\;\beta\sum_{y}\pi(y\mid x)\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)},
\]
where $\beta>0$ controls the strength of the KL penalty (larger $\beta$ enforces closer adherence to $\pi_\text{ref}$).

Because the optimization for each $x$ is over a probability distribution $\pi(\cdot\mid x)$, we introduce a normalization constraint $\sum_y\pi(y\mid x)=1$ and form the Lagrangian
\[
\mathcal{L}(\pi,\lambda) \;=\; \sum_{y}\pi(y\mid x)\,r(x,y) \;-\; \beta\sum_{y}\pi(y\mid x)\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \lambda(x)\Big(1-\sum_{y}\pi(y\mid x)\Big).
\]
Taking the derivative of $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to $\pi(y\mid x)$, setting it to zero, and solving for $\pi(y\mid x)$ yields
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x)\;=\;\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right),
\]
where $Z(x)$ is the normalization constant that makes the right-hand side a probability distribution. Intuitively, this formula says the optimal policy is an exponential re-weighting of the reference policy: outcomes with higher reward $r(x,y)$ are upweighted, outcomes with lower reward are downweighted, and the scale of reweighting is set by $\beta$.

Two points about this form are important for intuition. First, the mapping is multiplicative relative to the reference: if $\pi_\text{ref}$ assigns zero probability to some $y$ then the optimal policy will also assign zero probability regardless of $r$. Second, $\beta$ controls how strongly reward tilts the policy: small $\beta$ amplifies reward differences (making the policy concentrate on high-reward outputs), while large $\beta$ keeps the policy close to $\pi_\text{ref}$.

# Partition function $Z(x)$: definition and intractability in practice

The normalization constant appearing above is the partition function
\[
Z(x) \;=\; \sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right),
\]
which ensures that $\sum_y \pi_r(y\mid x)=1$. Formally $Z(x)$ depends on the prefix $x$, the reference policy, and the reward values for every possible completion $y$.

In practice $Z(x)$ is problematic because it requires summing (or integrating) the unnormalized weights over the entire space of possible outputs $y$. For language generation this space is astronomically large (all token sequences of varying lengths), so an exact computation of $Z(x)$ is infeasible. Even approximate estimation of $Z(x)$ can be expensive or unstable, which is why the explicit policy form above — although theoretically closed-form given $r$ — is not directly usable for large discrete output spaces without further manipulation. This practical intractability of the partition function motivates a reparameterization that avoids needing to evaluate $Z(x)$.

# Change of variables: $r(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$

The form of the optimal policy can be algebraically inverted to express the reward in terms of a policy and the partition function. Taking logarithms of
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)
\]
and rearranging gives the change of variables
\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \beta\log Z(x).
\]
This identity says that any reward function that induces the optimal policy $\pi_r$ can be written as the scaled log-ratio between that policy and the reference, plus an additive term $\beta\log Z(x)$ that depends only on the prompt $x$. The additive term is exactly what is required to make the exponentiated form integrate (or sum) to one; it is the negative log-normalizer of the exponential tilt.

Two interpretations follow immediately. First, the log-ratio $\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$ is an intrinsic, per-(prefix,completion) scalar that measures how much the optimal policy deviates from the reference on that completion; it plays the role of an implicit advantage or score. Second, the extra term $\beta\log Z(x)$ is a per-prefix baseline: it shifts all reward values for that $x$ by the same amount. Because many preference models and downstream objectives depend only on reward differences across completions, that baseline will often cancel out — which is the key observation exploited next.

# Preference models depend on reward differences; $Z(x)$ cancels in pairwise terms

Human preference datasets are commonly modeled by probabilistic ranking models such as Bradley–Terry (a two-item special case of Plackett–Luce). Under the Bradley–Terry model the probability that completion $y_1$ is preferred to $y_2$ given prefix $x$ is
\[
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)\;=\;\frac{\exp\!\left(r(x,y_1)\right)}{\exp\!\left(r(x,y_1)\right)+\exp\!\left(r(x,y_2)\right)}
\;=\;\sigma\bigl(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\bigr),
\]
where $\sigma(t)=1/(1+e^{-t})$ is the logistic function. Substitute the change-of-variables expression for $r(x,y)$ into the difference $r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$. The per-prefix additive term $\beta\log Z(x)$ appears in both $r(x,y_1)$ and $r(x,y_2)$ and cancels exactly, leaving
\[
r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)
\;=\;\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)} \;-\; \beta\log\frac{\pi(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)}.
\]
Consequently the Bradley–Terry preference probability can be written purely in terms of policy-to-reference log-probability ratios:
\[
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)
\;=\;\sigma\!\Biggl(\beta\Bigl(\log\frac{\pi(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)} - \log\frac{\pi(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)}\Bigr)\Biggr).
\]
The crucial practical payoff is that the intractable partition function $Z(x)$ no longer appears in the model for pairwise preferences. Because human labels are comparisons between completions, and Bradley–Terry depends only on reward differences, we can express the whole preference model in terms of the policy ratios $\pi/\pi_\text{ref}$ without ever needing $Z(x)$.

# Implication: we can fit policies directly to preference data

The cancellation of $Z(x)$ in pairwise preference probabilities enables a major simplification: instead of first fitting a scalar reward network $r_\phi(x,y)$ and then running RL to turn that reward into a policy, we can directly parametrize a policy $\pi_\theta$ and fit it to the preference data by maximum likelihood under the Bradley–Terry model written in policy form.

Concretely, given an offline dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y_w^{(i)},y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$ of labeled comparisons (where $y_w$ is preferred to $y_l$ for prompt $x$), the negative log-likelihood loss becomes
\[
\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\pi_\text{ref})
\;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Biggl[\log \sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\Bigl(\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\Bigr)\Bigr)\Biggr].
\]
This is an ordinary binary cross-entropy (logistic) loss on labeled pairs, where the “score” for a completion is $\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. Training with this loss updates the policy parameters $\theta$ directly; there is no separate reward network to fit and no RL loop to run.

To see what each gradient step does, differentiate the loss with respect to $\theta$ and write the implicit reward estimate
\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y) \;=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}.
\]
The gradient can be written as
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}
\;=\; -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)\Big]\;\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big).
\]
Thus each example increases the likelihood of the preferred completion $y_w$ and decreases the likelihood of the dispreferred completion $y_l$. The update for a given pair is weighted by the logistic term $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))$, which is larger when the current policy incorrectly assigns higher implicit reward to the dispreferred completion; in other words, mistakes are corrected with larger steps. This dynamic, per-example weighting helps avoid the degenerate behavior that can arise from naïvely trying to push up preferred completions and push down dispreferred completions without regard to the model's current ordering.

Putting these observations together, we obtain the central insight behind Direct Preference Optimization (DPO): because the optimal policy under the KL-regularized objective can be written as an exponential tilt of a reference policy, and because pairwise preference models (like Bradley–Terry) depend only on reward differences (so the partition function cancels), we can reparameterize the reward via policy ratios and train the policy directly by a simple binary cross-entropy on preference pairs. This eliminates the need to (i) learn a separate reward model whose outputs must be normalized or (ii) run reinforcement learning to turn a reward into a policy, while remaining faithful to the same KL-regularized objective.
