\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 6: Mechanics of DPO Updates and Their Effects

# Gradient structure: increase log p(y_w|x), decrease log p(y_l|x)

The Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) objective is a pairwise, likelihood-based loss that compares the model's probabilities for a preferred completion $y_w$ and a dispreferred completion $y_l$ for the same prompt $x$. Writing the policy as $\pi_\theta$ and a fixed reference policy as $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, the DPO loss (from the paper) is
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[
\log \sigma\!\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
- \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\right],
\]
where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the logistic function and $\beta$ is the KL-strength parameter from the constrained RL formulation.

To see what a DPO gradient does to model probabilities, differentiate this objective with respect to the policy parameters $\theta$. Define the model's implicit (scaled) reward estimate
\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y) \triangleq \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
\]
Using the chain rule and the derivative of $\log\sigma(\cdot)$, the gradient can be written compactly as (the same expression given in the paper):
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}
= -\beta\,
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\Big[
\sigma\!\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)\,
\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\Big].
\]

Two immediate observations follow from this form. First, the parameter-gradient direction inside the parentheses is $\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$, which is exactly the same direction produced by a supervised pairwise objective that increases the log-probability of the preferred completion $y_w$ and decreases the log-probability of the dispreferred completion $y_l$. In other words, every DPO update pushes the model to increase $\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)$ and to decrease $\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$.

Second, that push is multiplied by two scalars outside the parentheses: the global factor $\beta$ and a per-example scalar $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))$ that depends on the model's current implicit rewards for the pair. These scalars control the magnitude of the increase/decrease. The form is therefore transparent: DPO implements pairwise preference supervision (increase preferred, decrease dispreferred) but scales each pairwise gradient by an importance weight that depends on the current model state.

# Per-example weight: sigmoid of the implicit reward gap r_hat(y_l) - r_hat(y_w)

The per-example weight in the DPO gradient is
\[
w_\theta(x,y_w,y_l) \;=\; \sigma\!\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big),
\qquad\text{where}\quad
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
\]
This weight is a logistic (sigmoid) function of the model's own implicit reward gap between the dispreferred and preferred completions. Because $\sigma(\cdot)\in(0,1)$, the weight smoothly interpolates between zero and one as the model's internal ordering changes.

The weight has three characteristic regimes that are easy to interpret:

- If the model already assigns a substantially higher implicit reward to the preferred completion, i.e. $\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\gg\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)$, then the argument of the sigmoid is strongly negative and $w_\theta\approx 0$. The pair contributes almost no gradient because the model already "agrees" with the label.

- If the model strongly prefers the dispreferred completion, i.e. $\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\gg\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)$, then the sigmoid argument is strongly positive and $w_\theta\approx 1$. The pair receives a near-maximal update to correct the misordering.

- When the model is uncertain or the implicit rewards are close, $\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\approx\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)$, then $w_\theta\approx 0.5$ and the pair receives a moderate update.

Because $\hat r_\theta$ is built from the log-ratio $\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$, the weight depends not just on the model's own probabilities but on those probabilities relative to the reference policy. This ties the per-example importance to the KL-constrained RL interpretation: the model's implicit reward is precisely the quantity that would appear in the constrained-optimal policy, and the sigmoid maps that gap to a soft correction strength.

# Why dynamic weighting targets hard or misordered pairs and stabilizes learning

The dynamic, data-dependent weight is the key stabilization mechanism of DPO. Intuitively, DPO asks "how much does the model itself disagree with the human preference for this pair?" and scales the correction accordingly. There are several ways to see why that stability follows.

First, DPO concentrates learning signal on mistakes and on uncertain examples. If the model already ranks the preferred completion above the dispreferred one by a comfortable margin, the weight is near zero and further pushing on that pair is unnecessary; forcing the model to move even further would risk overfitting and unnecessary drift away from the reference. Conversely, when the model currently prefers the dispreferred completion — a direct contradiction of the label — the weight is near one and the loss applies a strong corrective gradient. This selective focusing reduces wasted updates on "easy" pairs and directs capacity at "hard" pairs where change is needed.

Second, the weighting produces a soft-margin behavior. The per-pair update magnitude decays as the model's implicit margin increases. That is similar to classical margin-based ranking losses where correctly separated pairs beyond a margin produce no loss. DPO's sigmoid gives a smooth version of this idea tied to the model's own implicit reward scale, so the model naturally settles into solutions that respect a margin relative to the reference policy without oscillating or overshooting.

Third, because the implicit reward $\hat r_\theta$ uses the log-ratio with the reference $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, DPO's corrections are aware of how far the model is drifting from the reference policy. This implicit coupling to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ prevents updates that would aggressively push the policy into regions where the reward-model estimate (or human preferences) are unreliable. In practical terms the paper reports that the per-example weighting prevents the model degeneration that occurs with a naive, unweighted probability-ratio objective (see the paper's Appendix where unweighted variants are shown to lead to degenerate generations).

Finally, by avoiding the need to estimate partition functions or value baselines (which actor-critic RL methods must approximate), DPO removes a source of high-variance updates. The per-example weight is computed deterministically from current log-probabilities and reference log-probabilities, so it has low variance compared to Monte Carlo estimates of returns or learned value functions. The result is a simpler, lower-variance update that is less prone to instability in practice.

# Contrast with unlikelihood training and naive probability-ratio losses

A straightforward alternative to DPO is to treat each labeled pair as a pseudo-supervised example and always push up the preferred example while pushing down the dispreferred example with a fixed coefficient. Concretely, a simple "unlikelihood" style loss for a pair might look like
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{UL}}
= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\big[\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)\big] + \alpha\,
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\big[\mathcal{P}(y_l\mid x;\pi_\theta)\big],
\]
where the second term is some penalty applied to the dispreferred completion (and $\alpha$ a fixed coefficient). Equivalently, a naive probability-ratio objective that simply maximizes the difference of log-probabilities,
\[
\max_\theta\; \log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x),
\]
has a gradient proportional to the unweighted direction $\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$ for every pair.

Both of these naive approaches apply a constant-strength correction to every pair regardless of the model's current ordering or margin. Two problems follow:

1. Over-correction and degeneration. Constant pushing on already-correct pairs can drive the model to collapse probabilities into extreme, low-entropy solutions (e.g., mode collapse), especially when the dispreferred examples come from a long-tail of plausible outputs. The paper specifically observes that a naive probability-ratio objective (i.e., without the sigmoid weighting) can cause degeneration of the language model (see the Appendix table referenced in the paper).

2. Ignoring the reference policy. Unweighted objectives typically do not reference $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, so they do not enforce the KL constraint that keeps the learned policy close to the distribution where preferences were collected. Without that tether, the model can drift far from the data distribution used to estimate preferences, which harms both generalization and safety.

DPO avoids both issues simultaneously. Its gradient direction is the same pairwise increase/decrease as the naive methods, but the scaling is adaptive and computed from $\hat r_\theta=\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$. This both prevents unnecessary pushes on easy pairs (stability) and encodes the desired KL-regularization implicitly (safety and constrained optimization equivalence), producing empirically better reward/KL tradeoffs than PPO and better behavior than unlikelihood baselines in the experiments reported in the paper.

# Guidance on tuning beta and learning rates to balance stability and improvement

The scalar $\beta$ in DPO plays a central role: it originates as the KL-penalty coefficient in the constrained RL objective and appears directly in the DPO loss and gradient. Two mathematical facts about $\beta$ are most important for practical tuning:

1. $\beta$ sets the policy–reference tradeoff. In the RL formulation, larger $\beta$ penalizes KL divergence more strongly, biasing solutions to stay closer to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. Thus, choosing $\beta$ is conceptually selecting how conservative you want the policy to be relative to the reference. The experiments in the paper explored values such as $\beta\in\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$ when sweeping conservativeness; this gives a practical small set to try when tuning.

2. $\beta$ rescales the optimization signal. The DPO gradient contains an explicit factor of $\beta$, and $\beta$ also appears inside the sigmoid through $\hat r_\theta$. Concretely,
\[
\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}
= -\beta\,\mathbb{E}\big[\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))(\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_w)-\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_l))\big].
\]
Therefore larger $\beta$ both amplifies gradient magnitudes (global scaling) and sharpens the sigmoid (making the per-example weight more binary). The practical implication is that when you increase $\beta$ you are simultaneously making updates potentially larger and more decisive about misordered pairs; when you decrease $\beta$ updates are gentler and the sigmoid is smoother around zero, so the method behaves more softly.

Because of this dual role, tuning $\beta$ and the optimizer learning rate together is important. A conservative, empirically grounded approach (aligned with the paper's experimental procedure and theoretical expressions) is:

- Decide the desired conservativeness (how much KL you are willing to accept). If you want the policy to remain close to the SFT/reference policy, choose a larger $\beta$. If you want more freedom to move away from the reference to aggressively follow preferences, choose a smaller $\beta$.

- When changing $\beta$, adjust the learning rate so that the effective step sizes remain reasonable. Since the gradient magnitude scales approximately with $\beta$, doubling $\beta$ while leaving the learning rate unchanged will roughly double typical parameter steps; to keep per-step magnitudes comparable reduce the learning rate proportionally. This adjustment follows directly from the gradient expression.

- Start from the SFT initialization for both $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $\pi_\theta$ (when available). The paper recommends initializing the reference policy to the SFT model (or fitting a reference to preferred completions when an SFT is not available) to reduce distribution shift and stabilize early training. With this initialization fewer pairs will be grossly misordered at the start, which reduces the risk of very large initial updates.

- Monitor both preference loss and the sequence-level KL (or other proxy for deviation from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$). In the experiments the authors evaluated methods by plotting the reward vs. KL frontier. Practically, keep an eye on how rapidly KL grows as you change $\beta$ or the learning rate; if KL explodes, increase $\beta$ or reduce the learning rate.

Finally, one of the selling points shown in the paper is that DPO tends to be less sensitive to extensive hyperparameter tuning than PPO-based RLHF: the loss is a simple pairwise cross-entropy with a deterministic per-example weight, avoiding high-variance RL updates and learned value baselines. In consequence, modest sweeps over a few $\beta$ values (e.g., the small set used in the paper) together with standard supervised fine-tuning learning rates often suffice to find good operating points. Still, the mathematical dependence of the gradient on $\beta$ argues for adjusting optimizer step sizes when $\beta$ varies substantially.

Closing summary

Summarizing the mechanics: DPO performs pairwise preference learning by increasing log-probability of preferred completions and decreasing that of dispreferred completions. Crucially, each pair is weighted by a sigmoid of the model's own implicit reward gap $\hat r_\theta=\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$. This dynamic weighting concentrates updates on misordered or ambiguous pairs, enforces an implicit KL tether to the reference, and yields much lower-variance, more stable updates than naive unweighted pairwise updates or actor-critic style RL with Monte Carlo baselines. Because the gradient scale is proportional to $\beta$, practical tuning couples $\beta$ and the learning rate; the paper's experiments show that small sweeps over reasonable $\beta$ values are sufficient for strong performance, and that DPO is robust in practice.
