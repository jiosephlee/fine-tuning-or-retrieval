\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 10: Experimental Design

This chapter describes the experimental setups the authors used to compare Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) with a range of baselines. The experiments are designed to probe different aspects of learning from pairwise preferences: a controlled setting with a known (ground-truth) reward so we can precisely measure the reward / KL tradeoff; real human-preference datasets for summarization and dialogue where ground-truth reward is unavailable so evaluation uses a separate judge; and multiple model families and sizes to test robustness. For each task we review the data and how the preference labels were obtained, the model initialization choices (SFT / reference model), the competing methods, and the evaluation protocols (reward–KL frontier, sequence-level KL computation, win-rate evaluation with GPT-4, and temperature sweeps). The descriptions that follow are grounded in the experiments reported in the paper.

# Controlled sentiment generation on IMDb with classifier-defined preferences

Purpose and high-level design. The controlled sentiment experiment provides a clean setting where the experimenters have access to a ground-truth reward function, namely a pretrained sentiment classifier. This allows direct measurement of how effectively each algorithm trades off expected reward against deviation from a reference policy (the KL term in the RLHF objective). The controlled setup therefore makes it possible to draw precise, quantitative comparisons between DPO and RL-based methods such as PPO, including a PPO oracle that has access to the true reward.

Data, SFT and preference labels. Prompts $x$ are prefixes taken from the IMDb movie-review dataset. The experimenters first construct a supervised-fine-tuned (SFT) model by fine-tuning a pre-trained language model (the paper uses GPT-2-large) on the IMDb training set until convergence; this SFT serves as the reference sampling policy $\pi^\text{SFT}$. To create preference pairs, the authors sample completions $y_1,y_2\sim\pi^\text{SFT}(\cdot\mid x)$ and then label the pair by comparing the outputs of a pretrained sentiment classifier: the completion with the higher classifier-estimated probability of positive sentiment is treated as the preferred completion $y_w$, so that the synthetic preference label satisfies $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. Because the classifier serves as a ground-truth reward $r^*(x,y)$ in this experiment, the authors can compute an exact expected reward for any policy and thus measure the reward–KL frontier precisely.

Evaluation metric: reward–KL frontier. The constrained RL objective that these algorithms approximate is, for a policy $\pi$,
$\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}[r^*(x,y)] - \beta\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x))$.
To assess how well each method optimizes this tradeoff, the authors plot the achieved expected reward against the sequence-level KL divergence to the reference policy. The sequence-level KL is computed per prompt and then averaged; for a prefix $x$ this KL is
$\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr) = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\!\left[\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\right],$
which, when expanded token-by-token, equals the sum of per-timestep KL divergences (the paper explicitly treats sequence-level KL as the sum of per-step KLs). By plotting reward vs. KL for multiple runs of the same algorithm with different conservativeness hyperparameters, the authors obtain a frontier that characterizes the algorithm’s efficiency at exploiting reward while remaining close to the reference distribution.

Hyperparameter sweeps, runs, and schedule. To generate points along each algorithm’s frontier the authors perform a hyperparameter sweep over the policy-conservativeness parameter for each method. In the controlled sentiment experiment these sweeps used the following values (as reported in the paper): target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO; $\beta\in\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$ for DPO; and $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for the unlikelihood baseline (Preferred-FT used different random seeds to generate multiple points). Altogether the sweep included 22 training runs. During training, each run is evaluated on a held-out set of test prompts at regular intervals (the paper reports evaluations every 100 training steps) until convergence; at each evaluation the authors compute the average reward under the sentiment classifier and the average sequence-level KL described above. These (reward, KL) pairs form the curves shown in the paper; DPO produced a substantially better frontier, achieving higher reward at low KL than PPO and even PPO versions that have access to the ground-truth reward function.

Baselines in this controlled setting. The main baselines compared in this experiment include PPO (with a learned reward model) and PPO-GT (a PPO variant that uses the ground-truth sentiment classifier as the reward). The paper reports running two PPO-GT implementations: an off-the-shelf implementation (TRLX) and a modified PPO-GT that normalizes rewards and further tunes hyperparameters to improve performance. The authors also compare to Preferred-FT (supervised fine-tuning on the chosen completions) and Unlikelihood training (increasing likelihood of preferred completions and decreasing likelihood of dispreferred ones, with an unlikelihood coefficient $\alpha$).

# Reddit TL;DR summarization with human preferences

Task and dataset. The Reddit TL;DR dataset contains forum posts and human-written short summaries; the authors reuse the preference comparisons collected and released by earlier work (Stiennon et al.). These comparisons pair two model-generated summaries for the same post and a human label indicating which summary is preferred. The evaluation objective is to improve summary quality according to those kinds of human judgments.

Reference model and preference data. The SFT reference policy for this task is a GPT-J model fine-tuned on human-written summaries (the authors use the CarperAI "openai_summarize_tldr_sft" checkpoint). The preference dataset the authors use was collected by Stiennon et al. on samples from a similarly trained SFT model; the paper reuses this dataset rather than re-sampling completions.

Evaluation protocol: win rate against baselines judged by GPT-4. Because a ground-truth scalar reward is not available for summarization the authors evaluate success by measuring the win rate of a model’s generated summary against a baseline summary on held-out test examples, using GPT-4 as an automated judge. Concretely, for each test prompt $x$ they sample a candidate completion $y$ from the evaluated model (at a chosen sampling temperature) and compare it to the baseline summary (the reference completion from the test set) by asking GPT-4 to indicate which summary is better. The win rate reported is the fraction of test examples for which GPT-4 prefers the model’s output to the baseline.

Sampling-temperature sweep and robustness. The authors sample completions for all methods at a range of decoding temperatures (the paper reports temperatures spanning $0.0$ to $1.0$) and plot win rate as a function of temperature. This sweep makes the evaluation robust to decoding stochasticity and shows how sensitive each method’s performance is to the sampling temperature. In the reported results DPO achieves a win rate of roughly $61\%$ at temperature $0.0$ (greedy decoding), exceeding PPO’s win rate of approximately $57\%$ at its best temperature. DPO is also reported to be more robust to the choice of sampling temperature than PPO, whose performance degrades at higher temperatures in the experiments.

Baselines and Best-of-$N$. The authors compare DPO against several baselines: the SFT model, Preferred-FT, Unlikelihood training, PPO with a learned reward model, and a Best-of-$N$ baseline that samples $N$ completions from the SFT model and selects the highest-scoring summary according to a learned reward function. Best-of-$N$ decouples reward modeling from policy optimization and can score well but is computationally expensive at test time because it requires sampling $N$ completions per query. In summarization experiments DPO also outperforms the Best-of-$N$ baseline at its best reported temperatures.

Generalization test to a new input distribution. The paper additionally evaluates policies trained on Reddit TL;DR on a different summarization distribution, the CNN/DailyMail test set, to probe out-of-distribution generalization. Using the same GPT-4 concise prompt (replace the phrase “forum post” by “news article”) the authors find that the DPO-trained policy continues to outperform the PPO policy on the new distribution, providing initial evidence that DPO generalizes at least as well as PPO in this setup.

# Anthropic Helpful–Harmless single-turn dialogue

Dataset and preference data. The Anthropic Helpful-and-Harmless (HH) dataset consists of multi-step dialogues collected by Anthropic; for the single-turn experiments the authors use the subset with one human–assistant exchange, where each datapoint includes two candidate assistant responses and a human label indicating the preferred response. The HH dataset used in the paper contains on the order of $170\text{k}$ dialogues (as reported in the paper).

Reference model construction (no SFT checkpoint available). Unlike the other tasks, there is no publicly available SFT model that directly corresponds to the HH preference collection. To make DPO applicable, the authors start with an off-the-shelf pretrained Pythia-2.8B model and create a reference model by fine-tuning it on the preferred completions from the preference dataset (this procedure is what the paper calls Preferred-FT used to produce a $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ whose generations are in-distribution with the labeled preferences). DPO is then trained against that $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ on the HH preference dataset.

Evaluation and baselines. For dialogue the authors again use GPT-4 as an automatic judge: for each test prompt they compare the model’s sampled response against the preferred response in the dataset and ask GPT-4 which reply is better. As baselines they include 2-shot prompting of the base Pythia-2.8B model, Preferred-FT, a Best-of-128 Preferred-FT baseline (sampling 128 completions and selecting the best-scoring one), and an RLHF PPO checkpoint trained on HH (the authors reference a public checkpoint in the comparisons). In these experiments DPO is the only computationally efficient method that meaningfully improves over the Preferred-FT baseline and performs similarly to or better than the computationally demanding Best-of-128 baseline. The authors also report that the public PPO HH checkpoint (PPO trained on HH) did not yield better performance than the base Pythia-2.8B model under any prompt / temperature the authors tried.

Convergence behavior. The authors note that DPO converges to strong performance relatively quickly on the single-turn dialogue task, indicating efficient use of the preference data without the need for costly RL loops.

# Model families and sizes (GPT-2 large, GPT-J, Pythia up to 6B)

Models used per task. The experiments use a mix of commonly used open-architecture language models as SFT / base models. Concretely, the controlled sentiment experiment uses a GPT-2-large SFT model; the Reddit TL;DR summarization experiments use a GPT-J SFT model (the CarperAI SFT checkpoint); and the single-turn dialogue work uses Pythia family models, starting from a Pythia-2.8B pretrained checkpoint and (in comparisons) referring to larger public PPO checkpoints. Across the experiments the authors test DPO on models of different sizes and report results on models up to 6B parameters (the experimental comparisons therefore cover a range of model capacities, showing DPO’s applicability beyond toy models).

Role of the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. In every experiment DPO and the baselines measure deviations from a reference distribution $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Where available, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is simply the SFT checkpoint used to sample candidate completions for human labeling. When such an SFT checkpoint is not available (the HH dialogue case), the authors construct $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by supervised fine-tuning on the preferred completions (Preferred-FT) so that $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is consistent with the preference dataset. This design choice is important: DPO’s objective is expressed relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, and using the same $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ that generated the preference data reduces distributional mismatch.

# Reward–KL frontier evaluation and sequence-level KL computation

Motivation. The RLHF-style objective used in this line of work balances two forces: push the policy toward higher reward and keep it close to the reference policy to prevent catastrophic distribution shift. To evaluate methods that claim to solve this constrained optimization problem, it is not enough to report reward alone; one must inspect the tradeoff curve between reward and divergence from the reference. The reward–KL frontier provides that diagnostic.

Formal quantities. The constrained objective is (repeating the equation to make the experiment notation explicit)
$\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}[r(x,y)] - \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr).$
Two quantities are measured for each trained policy in the frontier plots:

- The expected reward under the ground-truth reward function (in the controlled sentiment experiment):
$\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi}[r^*(x,y)].$

- The average sequence-level KL divergence to the reference:
$\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\left[\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr)\right]
= \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi}\!\left[\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\right].$
The paper notes that the sequence-level KL can be implemented as the sum of per-timestep KLs when computing token-level probabilities.

Constructing the frontier. For each algorithm the paper runs multiple training runs while varying the policy-conservativeness hyperparameter (e.g., PPO target KL or DPO’s $\beta$). Each run yields a final policy; the authors evaluate the expected reward and the sequence-level KL for that policy on the held-out test prompts and plot the resulting point. The collection of points from multiple hyperparameter choices forms an empirical frontier that shows how efficiently each algorithm can increase reward for a given allowed divergence from the reference. In the controlled sentiment experiments the authors measured these quantities every 100 training steps in each run to trace the learning trajectory and to compare the methods across the full training curve.

Interpreting the frontier. A curve that is farther up and to the left is better: higher reward at lower KL is superior. The paper reports that DPO’s frontier strictly dominates PPO’s in the controlled sentiment setting and that DPO can even outperform PPO variants that have access to the ground-truth reward (PPO-GT), indicating DPO’s efficiency at solving the constrained optimization in this task.

# Win-rate evaluation against baselines using GPT-4 as a judge

Why use an automated judge. For real-world tasks such as summarization and dialogue we often lack a scalar ground-truth reward that aligns with human preference. The authors therefore evaluate using pairwise comparisons: for each prompt they present two candidate responses and ask a judge to pick the better one. Collecting fresh human judgments at the scale needed for extensive evaluation is expensive, so the authors use GPT-4 as an automated judge and validate this choice with a human study.

Protocol and prompts. The authors experimented with two different GPT-4 prompts for pairwise comparisons: a simple prompt (GPT-4 (S)) that asks which summary better conveys the important information, and a concise prompt (GPT-4 (C)) that asks which summary is both better and more concise. The paper reports that GPT-4 (C) tends to produce win rates more representative of human judgments (GPT-4 (S) sometimes prefers longer or repetitive summaries more often than humans do). For the main reported results the authors use GPT-4 (C).

Human validation study. To justify using GPT-4 as a surrogate for humans the paper includes a human evaluation comparing GPT-4 judgments to human judgments on a subset of summarization comparisons. The outcome is that GPT-4 agrees with humans about as often as humans agree with each other, suggesting that GPT-4 provides a reasonable proxy for human preference in these datasets. Because of limited human rater availability the study concentrates repeated human labeling on a few head-to-head comparisons (e.g., DPO vs PPO). Based on this validation the authors use GPT-4 to compute win rates across the full test sets.

Win-rate definition. For a given test prompt $x$, let $y_{\text{eval}}$ be the model’s sampled completion and $y_{\text{base}}$ be the baseline completion (reference summary or the dataset’s preferred response). The win rate is the fraction of test prompts for which GPT-4 prefers $y_{\text{eval}}$ to $y_{\text{base}}$. The authors report win rates as a function of sampling temperature, and they also report human versus GPT-4 agreement statistics to support the automated-judge choice.

Head-to-head human comparison. In addition to the GPT-4 evaluations, the paper includes a direct human preference comparison between DPO and PPO on summarization. The authors report that DPO samples at temperature $0.25$ were preferred $58\%$ of the time over PPO samples taken at temperature $0.0$ in human judgments, supporting the GPT-4 results.

# Sampling temperature sweeps and protocol standardization

Why sweeping temperature matters. Decoding temperature controls the randomness in sampling from an autoregressive language model. A single policy can produce outputs of varying quality depending on the decoding temperature, and different algorithms can have different sensitivities to temperature. To make fair comparisons the authors therefore evaluate each trained policy across a standardized set of sampling temperatures and report metrics (win rate, reward, KL) as a function of temperature.

Range and specific temperatures. For summarization and many reported experiments the authors sweep temperatures from $0.0$ to $1.0$ and plot results across that range. In some specific comparisons the paper highlights individual temperatures such as $0.0$ (greedy decoding), $0.25$, and $1.0$ to illustrate differences in peak performance and robustness. For example, DPO’s best summarization win rate appears at temperature $0.0$ in the reported experiments, while other methods can peak at different temperatures or degrade more rapidly as temperature increases.

Standardized sampling across methods. The same set of decoding temperatures and the same sampling procedures are applied to all methods being compared. This standardization ensures that differences in reported win rates or reward–KL points are due to the learned policy rather than differing decoding choices. When reporting frontier plots (reward vs KL) the authors also evaluate policies at consistent sampling settings to compute both expected reward and sequence-level KL.

Practical note on Best-of-$N$. Because Best-of-$N$ requires sampling many completions per prompt at test time, its reported performance is reported as a function of $N$ (the authors report Best-of-128 plateauing in the dialogue experiments). The paper emphasizes that although Best-of-$N$ can be a strong baseline, it is computationally costly at inference time relative to a single-policy method like DPO.

# Summary and reproducibility notes

The experimental design in the paper is structured to probe both the theoretical objective (reward maximization subject to a KL constraint) and practical utility on real preference datasets. The controlled sentiment experiment gives a precise, quantitative playground to compare algorithms on the reward–KL frontier because it provides a ground-truth reward function. The Reddit TL;DR summarization and Anthropic HH dialogue experiments test DPO on human-labeled preferences and use GPT-4 (validated by a human study) as an automated judge to measure win rates across sampling temperatures. Across tasks the authors reuse standard SFT checkpoints where available (or build reference models by Preferred-FT when necessary), sweep policy-conservativeness hyperparameters to generate frontiers, and standardize sampling-temperature sweeps so that methods are compared fairly. Hyperparameter grids and implementation details are provided in the paper’s appendices; the experimental choices reported in the paper illustrate how DPO can be evaluated and compared against PPO, preferred supervised fine-tuning, unlikelihood training, and computationally expensive Best-of-$N$ baselines on realistic preference-learning tasks.
