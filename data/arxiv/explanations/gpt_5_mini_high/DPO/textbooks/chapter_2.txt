\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 2: Foundations: Preference Data, Rewards, and Models

# Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) as a starting point for alignment

A common first step when taking a large pre-trained language model toward a practical, aligned system is supervised fine-tuning (SFT). In SFT, a pre-trained unsupervised language model is further trained on a dataset of high-quality input–output pairs for the downstream task (for example, dialogue exchanges, summaries, or instruction–response pairs). The result of that step is a conditional language model we denote by $\pi^\text{SFT}(y\mid x)$: a probability distribution over candidate completions $y$ given a prompt $x$.

SFT plays two distinct but related roles in preference-based alignment pipelines. First, it moves the model distribution closer to the kinds of outputs we want the final system to produce (reducing the gap between pretraining distribution and task distribution). Second, $\pi^\text{SFT}$ commonly serves as the reference policy (often written as $\pi_\text{ref}$) used later in preference learning and constrained optimization. Concretely, many downstream procedures (including reward modeling and RL-based fine-tuning) sample candidate completions from $\pi^\text{SFT}$ when collecting human preferences, and they use $\pi^\text{SFT}$ as a baseline or penalty term to keep later fine-tuning from drifting too far from the distribution where the learned reward model is reliable.

Because SFT is a supervised maximum-likelihood procedure, it is conceptually simple and stable. Nevertheless, SFT alone cannot fully answer which of several plausible completions humans prefer when preferences are relative rather than absolute. That is why SFT is typically followed by a preference-collection phase and reward-based refinement.

# Preference datasets: triplets (x, y_w, y_l) and how they are collected

Preference data are built from human judgments that compare alternative outputs for the same input. The canonical unit in these datasets is a triplet $(x, y_w, y_l)$, where $x$ is a prompt (or context), $y_w$ is the completion preferred by the annotator, and $y_l$ is the dispreferred completion. Collections of such triplets are assembled into an offline dataset
\[
\mathcal{D} = \{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N
\]
which becomes the training data for the reward model or other preference-learning procedures.

The typical collection protocol used in recent alignment pipelines is: for a prompt $x$, sample two (or more) candidate completions from a model such as $\pi^\text{SFT}$, present these completions (often in randomized order) to a human rater, and ask which completion is better according to task-specific criteria (helpfulness, conciseness, safety, etc.). The rater's choice $y_w \succ y_l$ is interpreted as evidence that, under the true but unobserved scoring rule, $y_w$ is better than $y_l$ for that $x$. Because relative judgments ("which of these is better?") are usually easier and cheaper to collect than absolute scalar judgments, many alignment efforts rely on these pairwise labels.

Datasets may remain pairwise (two completions per prompt) or, when raters provide full rankings over several completions, the data are richer and admit more general ranking models. Importantly, preference datasets collected in this way are typically treated as fixed, offline data: learning proceeds by fitting models to $\mathcal{D}$ rather than by continuing to collect labels in an online loop (although hybrid online strategies are possible in practice).

# Reward functions as latent scorers of $(x,y)$

Preference labels are usually modeled as noisy comparisons induced by an underlying (latent) reward or score function. We denote this unknown scalar scorer by $r^*(x,y)$. Intuitively, $r^*(x,y)$ is the scalar value that, if known, would allow us to rank outputs and prefer higher-scoring completions.

A common pipeline built on this view separates two phases. First, fit a parametric reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ to the pairwise preference data $\mathcal{D}$ under a probabilistic model of choices (see the next sections for the Bradley–Terry and Plackett–Luce models). Second, use the fitted reward $r_\phi$ as the objective for policy optimization: find a policy $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ that, for prompts $x$ drawn from the data distribution, produces high-reward completions without deviating excessively from a reference policy. A standard formalization of the latter goal (used in prior RL-from-human-feedback approaches) is
\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\, y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[ r_\phi(x,y)\bigr] - \beta \,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x) \mid\mid \pi_\text{ref}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr],
\]
where $\beta>0$ trades off reward maximization against staying close to a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ (often $\pi^\text{SFT}$). The presence of the KL penalty is important in practice: it prevents the policy from drifting into regions where the reward model $r_\phi$ is unreliable and it moderates mode collapse.

In the reward-modeling stage we therefore treat $r_\phi(x,y)$ as the parametric proxy to the latent $r^*(x,y)$ and estimate $\phi$ from $\mathcal{D}$ under a chosen probabilistic model of how preferences arise from scores.

# Bradley–Terry model for pairwise preferences

To connect pairwise preference labels to an underlying score function, the Bradley–Terry (BT) model is a standard and widely-used choice. Under BT the probability that a rater prefers $y_1$ to $y_2$ for prompt $x$ is a softmax over the two scores:
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) \;=\; \frac{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr) + \exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_2)\bigr)}.
\]
Because the same normalization appears in numerator and denominator, this two-alternative softmax depends only on the score difference. Writing the logistic (sigmoid) function $\sigma(z) = 1/(1+\exp(-z))$, we can equivalently express the BT pairwise probability as
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) \;=\; \sigma\bigl(r^*(x,y_1) - r^*(x,y_2)\bigr).
\]

This formulation is attractive for learning: it reduces preference prediction to a binary classification problem on score differences. The BT model captures the intuitive property that larger differences in the latent scores make one item much more likely to be preferred, while small score gaps make preferences noisy.

Because BT depends only on score differences, it immediately implies an invariance: adding any function $f(x)$ to both $r^*(x,y_1)$ and $r^*(x,y_2)$ leaves the comparison probability unchanged. That invariance is a key identifiability issue discussed below.

# Plackett–Luce model for ranked lists and its relation to Bradley–Terry

When annotators provide full or partial rankings over a set of alternatives rather than binary comparisons, the Plackett–Luce (PL) model is the natural generalization of Bradley–Terry. Under PL, the probability of an ordered ranking $(y_{(1)},y_{(2)},\dots,y_{(k)})$ for a set of $k$ candidates is computed by a sequential softmax: at the first selection step the item $y_{(1)}$ is chosen with probability proportional to $\exp(r(x,y_{(1)}))$ among the $k$ items; after removing that item, the second item is chosen proportionally to $\exp(r(x,y_{(2)}))$ among the remaining $k-1$, and so on. Formally,
\[
p\bigl(y_{(1)},\dots,y_{(k)} \mid x\bigr)
= \prod_{t=1}^{k}
\frac{\exp\bigl(r(x,y_{(t)})\bigr)}
{\sum_{j=t}^{k} \exp\bigl(r(x,y_{(j)})\bigr)}.
\]
The Bradley–Terry model appears as the special case $k=2$ of Plackett–Luce. Thus PL inherits the same basic property that only relative score differences matter for observed ranking probabilities.

PL is particularly useful when the dataset contains full rankings or top-$m$ lists instead of (or in addition to) pairwise labels. In such cases maximum-likelihood estimation under the PL model uses the product-of-softmax likelihood above rather than the two-way logistic used by BT.

Like Bradley–Terry, Plackett–Luce exhibits an under-specification: adding any function $f(x)$ to all scores $r(x,y)$ for fixed $x$ does not change ranking probabilities. This shift-invariance is central to the identifiability issues discussed next.

# Fitting reward models with binary cross-entropy on pairwise differences

Given an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D}$ of pairwise labels, estimating a parametric reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ under the Bradley–Terry assumption reduces to a standard binary logistic regression (binary cross-entropy) on score differences. The maximum-likelihood (negative log-likelihood) objective commonly used is
\[
\mathcal{L}_R(r_\phi; \mathcal{D}) \;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[ \log \sigma\bigl(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l)\bigr)\Bigr],
\]
where $\sigma$ is the logistic (sigmoid) function and $(x,y_w,y_l)$ are sampled from the dataset of labeled comparisons.

Minimizing $\mathcal{L}_R$ makes $r_\phi(x,y_w)$ larger than $r_\phi(x,y_l)$ whenever the labeled preference indicates $y_w$ is better, with the gradient magnitude depending on how confidently the model currently ranks the two items. In practice, a neural reward model $r_\phi$ is often implemented by taking a language model (frequently initialized from the SFT model) and adding a scalar-valued head (for example, a linear projection from the final transformer representation to one real number). This initialization leverages the representations learned by the language model so that the reward head need not learn language structure from scratch.

When labels include rankings rather than just pairwise choices, the appropriate maximum-likelihood objective is the negative log-likelihood under the Plackett–Luce model, which decomposes into a sum of softmax log-probabilities for each selection step in the ranking.

# Reward normalization and identifiability considerations

Two closely related technical issues arise when working with reward models fit from preference data: (1) the model class is under-constrained by the data (non-identifiability of an additive function of $x$), and (2) the numerical scale and mean of the learned rewards affect downstream optimization variance. Both issues are prominent in practice and motivate simple normalization or canonicalization strategies.

First, formalize the identifiability issue. Define an equivalence relation on reward functions: we say two rewards $r$ and $r'$ are equivalent if they differ by a function of $x$ alone, i.e.
\[
r'(x,y) = r(x,y) + f(x)\qquad\text{for some function } f(\cdot).
\]
Under both the Plackett–Luce family (and Bradley–Terry as a special case), such equivalent reward functions induce identical preference probabilities. Intuitively, preference models depend only on differences $r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$, so adding the same offset to every candidate for a given $x$ cancels out.

The paper formalizes two consequences of this invariance. Lemma 1 states that two reward functions from the same equivalence class induce the same preference distribution under Plackett–Luce (and therefore Bradley–Terry). Lemma 2 states that two rewards in the same class induce the same optimal policy for the KL-constrained reward-maximization problem used in RL fine-tuning. Together these lemmas mean that the preference data determine only an equivalence class of rewards; any representative of that class will produce the same preference probabilities and the same optimal constrained policy.

Because multiple reward functions are consistent with the observed preferences, practitioners typically impose an additional normalization or identifiability constraint when they fit $r_\phi$. A common pragmatic choice (used in prior work) is to normalize learned rewards to have mean zero, e.g.
\[
\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigl[r_\phi(x,y)\bigr] = 0,
\]
which reduces variance in downstream policy optimization and gives a canonical scale to the learned scores. (Different papers describe slightly different normalizations; the key point is to fix the additive degree of freedom.)

The paper also gives a constructive canonicalization that ties a reward function to an explicit policy. Starting from the optimal constrained policy corresponding to a reward $r$, the following relation holds (where $Z(x)$ is the partition function)
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)} \,\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Bigr),
\]
and equivalently
\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\]
Because preference models depend on reward differences, the additive term $\beta\log Z(x)$ cancels when forming pairwise differences, which is why the BT likelihood can be expressed entirely in terms of the policy ratio $\pi_r/\pi_\text{ref}$. The paper shows that every reward equivalence class that is consistent with a Plackett–Luce model has a representative of the special form
\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}
\]
for some policy $\pi$. This equality picks a canonical member of the equivalence class by fixing the partition-function degree of freedom, and it underlies reparameterizations that make certain preference-learning objectives more direct and stable.

Second, beyond identifiability, numerical normalization of rewards matters in practice because variance in reward signals propagates into high-variance gradients when optimizing policies (especially with policy-gradient or actor–critic RL algorithms). To mitigate this, practitioners often subtract baselines, normalize rewards to zero mean, or otherwise rescale the learned $r_\phi$ before using it to train a policy. These normalizations are pragmatic fixes to reduce gradient variance and stabilize training.

Finally, note the practical implication of the equivalence and normalization discussion: while the absolute scalar output of a reward model is not uniquely determined by pairwise labels, the learned reward differences are what drive both preference likelihoods and policy updates. Thus, properly handling the additive ambiguity (by normalization or by choosing a canonical representative) is essential to stable downstream optimization and to interpreting reward magnitudes in a principled way.

--- 

This chapter has laid out the mathematical objects and probabilistic models that form the foundation of learning from human preferences: supervised fine-tuning as the typical starting point, triplet-form preference datasets, latent reward functions, the Bradley–Terry pairwise model and its Plackett–Luce generalization, the standard negative-log-likelihood training objective over score differences, and the central identifiability and normalization issues that practitioners must address. These ingredients are the building blocks used in preference-based alignment pipelines and also motivate alternative parameterizations that make policy extraction and stable optimization easier.
