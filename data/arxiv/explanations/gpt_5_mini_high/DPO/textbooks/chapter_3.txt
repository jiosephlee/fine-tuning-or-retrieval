Chapter 3: The RLHF Framework and Its Objective

# Three phases of RLHF: SFT, reward modeling, RL fine-tuning
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is typically organized as a three‑stage pipeline that sits on top of a pre‑trained language model. The first stage is supervised fine‑tuning (SFT). In SFT, one takes a pre‑trained language model and fine‑tunes it with supervised learning on a dataset of high‑quality, task‑specific examples (instructions and human completions for dialogue, summarization, coding, etc.). The result of this stage is a model we denote $\pi^{\text{SFT}}(y\mid x)$, which is used both as an improved baseline generator and commonly as the initialization for later stages.

The second stage is reward modeling (preference learning). To collect training data for a reward model, the SFT model is prompted with prompts $x$ and used to generate pairs of candidate completions $(y_1,y_2)\sim\pi^{\text{SFT}}(\cdot\mid x)$. Human labelers compare these pairs and indicate which completion they prefer, yielding tuples $(x,y_w,y_l)$ where $y_w\succ y_l$ denotes the preferred answer. The preference data are assumed to be generated from an underlying latent scalar quality function $r^*(x,y)$. A common statistical model for pairwise preferences is the Bradley–Terry model, which says that the probability a human prefers $y_1$ over $y_2$ is
$ p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)=\dfrac{\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_2)\bigr)}. $
Given a static dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y_w^{(i)},y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$ of comparisons, practitioners parameterize a reward model $r_{\phi}(x,y)$ (often initializing the model from the SFT weights and adding a scalar head) and fit it by maximum likelihood under this preference model. Writing $\sigma(\cdot)$ for the logistic function, the negative log‑likelihood objective used in practice is
$ \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi},\mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log\sigma\bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_w)-r_{\phi}(x,y_l)\bigr)\bigr]. $
Practitioners also commonly normalize rewards (for example, to have zero mean) to reduce variance in later stages.

The third stage is RL fine‑tuning: the reward model provides scalar feedback, and a policy (initialized from the SFT model) is optimized to produce high‑reward generations while not drifting too far from the SFT distribution. Because language is discrete, the expected reward objective is not directly differentiable, so this stage is formulated and solved with reinforcement learning algorithms.

# Reference policy pi_ref and why we regularize towards it
A central element of modern RLHF formulations is the reference policy, which is most commonly the SFT model and which we denote $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)$ (in practice $\pi_{\text{ref}}=\pi^{\text{SFT}}$). The reference policy plays two linked roles.

First, it is the data‑generation distribution on which the preference dataset was collected. The reward model was trained to be accurate on comparisons between continuations sampled from that distribution, so optimizing the policy far away from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ risks pushing the policy into regions where the learned reward $r_{\phi}$ is an extrapolation and therefore unreliable. Regularizing toward $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ keeps the policy within the support of the data used to learn the reward.

Second, the reference policy acts as a behavioral prior that preserves desirable aspects of the original generative distribution: fluency, diversity, and avoidance of degenerate or repetitive outputs. Without a penalty tying the optimized policy to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, optimization can drive the policy to collapse to a small set of high‑reward but low‑diversity completions (mode collapse) or to produce outputs that exploit flaws in the reward model. For these reasons, RLHF formulations explicitly trade off reward improvement against deviation from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

# KL‑regularized objective: maximize E[r_phi(x,y)] − beta KL[pi || pi_ref]
The standard mathematical way to express the tradeoff between reward improvement and staying near the reference policy is a KL‑regularized objective. Let $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ be the policy we will optimize. The RLHF fine‑tuning objective commonly used is
$ \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x,y)\bigr]\;-\;\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\;\|\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr], $
where $\beta>0$ is a hyperparameter that controls how strongly the optimization penalizes deviation from the reference policy. Expanding the KL, this objective can be written pointwise as an expectation over $(x,y)\sim\pi_{\theta}$ of the per‑example quantity
$ r_{\phi}(x,y) - \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}. $
In practice, prior works implement this by defining an augmented per‑sample reward
$ r(x,y) \;=\; r_{\phi}(x,y) - \beta\bigl(\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr) $
and then running a policy‑optimization algorithm (commonly PPO) to maximize the expected augmented reward.

Intuitively, the KL term is a soft trust‑region: it restricts how far the learned policy may move from the SFT policy in the direction that maximizes the learned reward. Smaller $\beta$ permits larger deviations (more aggressive pursuit of reward), while larger $\beta$ enforces greater conservatism and keeps generated outputs closer to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

# Preventing reward model extrapolation errors and mode collapse
Two failure modes motivate the KL regularizer. The first is reward model extrapolation: the learned reward $r_{\phi}$ is fit to comparisons drawn from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. If the policy moves into areas of output space not well covered by those comparisons, the learned reward can be arbitrarily wrong there; optimizing against such an unreliable signal can produce unwanted behavior. By penalizing divergence from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, the KL term keeps the policy in regions where $r_{\phi}$ is better supported by data.

The second failure mode is mode collapse or loss of generation diversity. A pure optimization for reward can concentrate probability mass on a few highly scored completions (or even a single collapsed completion), harming naturalness and diversity of outputs. The KL penalty counteracts this by explicitly penalizing large changes to the distributional shape of the policy, favoring gradual, conservative shifts in probability mass rather than aggressive concentration.

Because of these roles, the KL term is not merely a numerical convenience but a safety and stability mechanism that enforces a useful inductive bias: improve quality as judged by the learned reward but do so in a way that respects the prior behavior encoded in $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

# PPO and actor‑critic implementations in language modeling
Because language generation is discrete, the KL‑regularized objective is typically optimized with reinforcement learning algorithms. The standard practical approach in recent RLHF work is to use a policy optimizer such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Operationally, practitioners construct the augmented reward $r(x,y)=r_{\phi}(x,y)-\beta(\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x))$ and then apply PPO updates to the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ so as to increase expected augmented reward while constraining per‑update change.

From a theoretical perspective, minimizing the KL between the current policy and the optimal KL‑constrained policy leads to an objective that contains a normalization term coming from the partition function. Rearranging and analyzing this objective makes explicit the term that actor‑critic methods must handle. One convenient form of the objective, written as an expectation under $\pi_{\theta}$, is
$ \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\; \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\Biggl[\,r_{\phi}(x,y) \;-\;\beta\log\!\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x,y')\Bigr) \;-\;\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Biggr]. $
The middle term (the logarithm of the sum over $y'$) acts like a soft value function, or normalization, and does not change the optimal policy but does appear inside the expectation and therefore affects gradients. Actor‑critic style algorithms introduce a learned value function (the critic) to estimate such terms and to provide a baseline that reduces variance in policy gradient estimates. In practice the need to estimate this soft value / normalization term makes learning more complex: the critic must be trained stably and accurately, and errors in value estimation can increase variance and cause instability.

Because of these estimation difficulties, prior works also have adopted heuristics such as reward normalization (e.g., centering the reward distribution) or using a single‑sample human completion baseline as a Monte‑Carlo estimate of the normalization term. PPO itself is designed to limit the size of policy updates (through its clipped surrogate objective), but it still relies on accurately estimated advantages and value targets; when those estimates have high variance or bias, learning can become unstable.

# Practical issues: on‑policy sampling, value baselines, tuning sensitivity, compute cost
RLHF is substantially more complex and computationally expensive than straightforward supervised fine‑tuning. First, it requires training and maintaining multiple models: the SFT model, a reward model $r_{\phi}$ fit to preference data, and a policy model $\pi_{\theta}$ to be optimized. Second, the RL training loop is sampling‑intensive: collecting on‑policy trajectories (samples from the current policy) is required to estimate policy gradients and to compute expected rewards and KLs. Sampling from large language models inside the training loop is costly in wall‑time and GPU resources.

Third, actor‑critic architectures introduce further engineering complexity because they require learning a value function (baseline) to stabilize policy gradient estimates. Training that critic and ensuring low‑variance advantage estimates requires additional hyperparameters and architectural choices; poor choices can destabilize training. As noted above, the normalization/soft‑value term that arises in the KL‑regularized formulation either needs to be estimated by a critic or approximated with ad hoc baselines, each of which carries practical costs and failure modes.

Fourth, RLHF methods tend to be sensitive to hyperparameters. For example, in PPO the target KL (or the clipping/step‑size schedule) and reward normalization choices materially affect the tradeoff between reward improvement and KL deviation. Empirical studies typically run many experiments sweeping conservativeness parameters (target KL values, $\beta$ values, unlikelihood coefficients, etc.) to find an acceptable operating point. This hyperparameter sensitivity increases computational cost and engineering effort.

Finally, some evaluation or alternative baselines illustrate the computational burden: a Best‑of‑$N$ strategy (sample $N$ completions and pick the highest according to a reward model) can yield strong quality by decoupling policy search from reward optimization, but it is often computationally impractical at inference time because it requires generating and scoring $N$ full sequences per prompt.

Taken together, these practical considerations explain why the standard RLHF pipeline — SFT, reward modeling, and RL fine‑tuning with PPO or related actor‑critic methods — while powerful, is complex, expensive, and sometimes unstable. The KL penalty and related design choices are essential stabilizing components, but they do not eliminate the engineering costs of on‑policy sampling, critic estimation, and hyperparameter tuning that characterize modern RLHF workflows.

# Summary
In summary, the standard RLHF framework proceeds by (1) creating an SFT reference policy, (2) learning a scalar reward from human pairwise preferences (often via a Bradley–Terry style likelihood), and (3) optimizing a policy to maximize expected learned reward subject to a KL penalty that keeps the policy close to the reference. The KL term is crucial: it controls deviation from the data distribution used to train the reward, preserves generation diversity, and prevents exploitative or collapsed solutions. Optimizing the KL‑regularized objective requires reinforcement learning methods (commonly PPO), and those methods introduce additional challenges — value estimation, on‑policy sampling, and sensitive hyperparameters — which make RLHF a resource‑intensive and sometimes unstable process.
