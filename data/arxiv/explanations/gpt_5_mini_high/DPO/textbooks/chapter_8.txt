\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 8: Implementing DPO

# Reusing or constructing preference datasets from SFT samples
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) assumes an offline dataset of pairwise preferences
$\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y_w^{(i)},y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$, where for each prompt $x$ a human (or synthetic) labeler indicates a preferred completion $y_w$ and a dispreferred completion $y_l$. The canonical way to obtain such a dataset in the RLHF pipeline is to sample candidate completions from a supervised-fine-tuned model $\pi^\text{SFT}(y\mid x)$ and to have humans label preferences over those sampled candidates. The paper emphasizes reusing publicly available preference datasets when possible; these datasets were typically produced by some SFT sampling policy.

If you are building a dataset from scratch, follow the same recipe used in the paper: (1) sample completions $y_1,y_2\sim \pi^\text{SFT}(\cdot\mid x)$ for a collection of prompts $x$, and (2) collect pairwise preference labels $y_w\succ y_l$ from human raters (or, in controlled experiments, from an automatic scorer such as a classifier). The controlled sentiment experiment in the paper is an example where preferences were synthetically generated by a sentiment classifier; that is a valid way to obtain labeled comparisons when a ground-truth reward is available for controlled evaluation.

When reusing an existing dataset, pay attention to the distributional match between the dataset and any model you later declare as the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. The DPO derivation assumes the preferences were sampled (or are compatible with) the reference policy used in the reparameterization; when the original SFT that produced the dataset is not available, the paper recommends creating a $\pi_\text{ref}$ that matches the dataset (see the next section). The key point is that DPO is an offline algorithm: it learns from a fixed preference dataset rather than requiring on-policy preference collection during training.

# Choosing and freezing $\pi_\text{ref}$ (use SFT; if missing, train $\pi_\text{ref}$ by MLE on $y_w$)
The reparameterization that makes DPO work relies on a fixed reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. The paper’s implementation guidance is explicit: whenever available, set
$\pi_\text{ref}=\pi^\text{SFT}$, i.e., use the supervised-fine-tuned model that was used to sample the preference pairs. When $\pi^\text{SFT}$ is not available (for example, when you reuse a public preference dataset but the exact sampling model is not shared), the recommended strategy is to fit a reference policy by maximum likelihood on the preferred completions from the dataset:
$\displaystyle \pi_\text{ref}=\arg\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w)\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \pi(y_w\mid x)\bigr].$
This MLE projection aligns the reference policy to the distribution of preferred completions and reduces the distribution shift between the dataset and the reference used in the DPO objective.

Two practical points follow from the paper. First, treat $\pi_\text{ref}$ as fixed (frozen) during DPO optimization: the DPO loss is expressed in terms of the ratio between the current model $\pi_\theta$ and this static $\pi_\text{ref}$. Second, initialize your trainable policy $\pi_\theta$ to $\pi_\text{ref}$ (the paper notes that in practice the policy is initialized to the SFT model). Initializing in this way makes the KL constraint implicit and keeps early updates conservative.

# Batching $(x, y_w, y_l)$ and masking token likelihoods for whole responses
DPO’s loss is defined in terms of sequence-level log-probabilities $\log \pi(y\mid x)$. Concretely, for an autoregressive language model,
$\displaystyle \log \pi(y\mid x) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \log \pi(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}),$
where the sum runs over the response tokens of $y$ (including end-of-sequence if you treat it as part of the completion). Implementationally, you will form batches of triplets $(x_i,y_{w,i},y_{l,i})$ and score the responses under both the current model $\pi_\theta$ and the frozen reference $\pi_\text{ref}$.

Because response lengths vary, standard sequence-padding and attention masks are the natural implementation mechanism. A typical scoring forward pass concatenates the prompt $x$ and the completion $y$ (or otherwise provides $x$ as prefix context) and computes token logits; you then convert logits to token log-probabilities and sum only the log-probabilities that correspond to the completion tokens, using a response mask to ignore prompt tokens and padding. Ensure that your masking is precise: the DPO objective compares whole-response sequence log-probabilities, so the mask should include all tokens that belong to the model-generated completion and exclude prompt tokens and padding.

Include both $y_w$ and $y_l$ in each batch so the pairwise comparisons computed in the loss are efficient and vectorized. Vectorized computation of both the $\pi_\theta$ scores and $\pi_\text{ref}$ scores in the same batch minimizes Python overhead and maximizes GPU utilization.

# Efficient computation of sequence log-probabilities for $\pi$ and $\pi_\text{ref}$
Two practical efficiencies the paper exploits (implicitly) are (1) the reference policy is fixed, and (2) DPO does not require on-policy generation during training. Both imply natural engineering optimizations.

Precompute and cache $\log \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)$ once for every pair $(x,y)$ in your dataset when $\pi_\text{ref}$ is available and does not change. Storing these sequence log-probabilities avoids repeated forward passes through the frozen reference model during every training step and is the single most straightforward compute saver. If memory is constrained, you can store per-example scalar sequence log-probabilities rather than token-level logits; if you do need token-level information later (e.g., for per-token diagnostics), store token log-probs or recompute selectively.

For the trainable model $\pi_\theta$, compute $\log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)$ and $\log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$ on the fly in each batch. Tokenization of prompts and completions is deterministic: cache tokenized sequences to avoid re-tokenizing text each iteration. Keep both positive and negative completions in the same batch so the DPO pairwise loss can be computed without extra synchronization.

When dataset size and hardware permit, perform the following vectorization pattern: (1) build a batched input tensor for all prefixes+responses in the batch (including padding), (2) run a single forward pass through $\pi_\theta$ to get logits, (3) compute token log-probabilities, mask by response tokens, and sum to obtain sequence log-prob scalars. This yields $O(1)$ forward passes per batch rather than separate forward passes per example.

# Optimizers, learning-rate schedules, gradient clipping, mixed precision
The paper treats DPO as a supervised, maximum-likelihood-style optimization over a binary classification objective (binary cross-entropy over pairwise comparisons). Because of that reduction, the training loop and numerical stability techniques you would use for SFT also apply to DPO. The paper emphasizes that DPO is stable, computationally lightweight, and typically requires little hyperparameter tuning compared to RLHF; it therefore recommends reusing the same training infrastructure you use for SFT.

Concretely, the paper’s experiments proceed by taking an SFT model, initializing $\pi_\theta$ from it, and then minimizing the DPO loss. The DPO loss is a scalar differentiable objective (no RL gradient estimators required), so any optimizer you would normally use for supervised LM fine-tuning is compatible. The paper does not mandate a specific optimizer or schedule in the main text; detailed hyperparameters are deferred to the appendix. The important high-level guidance from the paper is that you do not need RL-specific machinery (value heads, policy entropy bonuses, or on-policy rollout buffers) for stability: DPO’s gradient is simply a weighted difference of standard $\nabla_\theta\log \pi$ terms (see the gradient expression below), and standard supervised training tricks—appropriate learning-rate scheduling, gradient clipping when necessary, and mixed-precision (fp16) training for large models—are all applicable in the same way they are for SFT.

One DPO-specific hyperparameter you will need to choose is $\beta$, which appears in the DPO objective as the scalar that controls conservativeness (the implicit KL penalty). In the paper’s experimental sweeps (e.g., in the controlled sentiment runs) $\beta$ was varied across values such as $\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$; that gives a sensible starting range. The paper reports that DPO required much less tuning than PPO in their experiments, but you should still monitor the effect of $\beta$ on the reward–KL tradeoff.

# Monitoring: DPO loss, KL to $\pi_\text{ref}$, proxy task metrics
The paper stresses monitoring the same kinds of quantities used in RLHF evaluations, adapted to the DPO setting. Key scalars to log during training include:

- The DPO training loss computed on a validation split of preference pairs:
$\displaystyle \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_\text{ref}) \;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[\log \sigma\bigl(\beta \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\bigr)\Bigr].$
This is the primary training objective and should decrease smoothly if training is behaving well.

- The implicit reward differences and weighting term distribution. From the paper’s gradient expression:
$\displaystyle \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\pi_\text{ref}) = -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[\sigma\bigl(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\bigr)\bigl(\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l\mid x)\bigr)\Bigr],$
where $\displaystyle \hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}.$
Track summaries of $\hat r_\theta$ (mean, std) and of the sigmoid weights $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))$ to detect cases where many pairs are already ranked correctly (small weight) or where the model systematically misranks (large weight). The paper notes that this per-example importance weight stabilizes training and prevents degeneration that can happen with a naive unweighted probability-ratio objective.

- Sequence-level KL to the reference policy. The paper evaluates the reward–KL frontier and reports "sequence-level KL" defined as the sum of per-timestep KLs. In practice you can estimate the KL between policies by sampling responses $y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)$ at evaluation time and computing
$\displaystyle \text{KL}(\pi_\theta\parallel\pi_\text{ref}) \approx \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\bigl[\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)-\log\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr].$
The paper evaluated policies every 100 training steps in some experiments; adopting a similar cadence for your validation runs is reasonable.

- Task-specific proxy metrics. In real applications there is no ground-truth reward. The paper evaluates on proxy metrics such as win rate against a held-out reference using GPT-4 as an automatic judge or task-specific classifiers (e.g., sentiment classifier in controlled experiments). Log these proxies on validation prompts to track real-world utility.

- Human or strong automatic evaluation. For summarization and dialogue the paper used GPT-4 win rates (and validated GPT-4 against humans). For production or higher-stakes evaluation, plan human evaluations, but use automatic proxies for faster iteration.

# Compute profile and stability advantages vs PPO (no on-policy sampling required)
One of DPO’s main practical advantages highlighted in the paper is its computational and stability profile relative to standard RLHF procedures such as PPO. The paper’s empirical findings and theoretical analysis explain why:

- No on-policy sampling loop. PPO-style RLHF requires iterative sampling from the current policy, reward evaluation, and trajectory storage for each update. DPO is entirely offline: training operates on the fixed preference dataset $\mathcal{D}$ and computes gradients from a simple classification loss. This removes the need to generate samples during training, which dramatically reduces wall-clock compute, data-movement overhead, and engineering complexity.

- No actor–critic/value head to train. Actor–critic algorithms introduce a value network (or learned baseline) to reduce gradient variance; this is another source of instability and hyperparameter tuning. The paper’s analysis shows that the normalization term in the actor–critic objective corresponds to a soft value function that must be estimated; DPO’s reparameterization removes that need altogether and trains the policy directly.

- Lower hyperparameter sensitivity. Because DPO reduces the problem to a supervised maximum likelihood problem, it inherits the relative hyperparameter stability of supervised LM fine-tuning. The paper finds that DPO matches or exceeds PPO performance in several tasks with “virtually no tuning” of DPO-specific hyperparameters.

- Better reward–KL tradeoff in practice. The paper’s controlled experiments show that DPO’s frontier of achieved reward versus KL-divergence from the reference policy strictly dominated PPO’s frontier in their runs (even when PPO had access to the ground-truth reward). Practically, this means DPO is often better at improving task reward while staying close to the reference model.

These points translate into concrete engineering consequences: you can implement DPO as a single supervised training loop over the stored preference triples, reusing your SFT training hardware and infra, without extra RL components (rollout engines, advantage estimators, replay buffers).

# Putting it together — a minimal DPO training recipe (as in the paper)
1. Collect or reuse a dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{(x,y_w,y_l)\}$ of pairwise preferences sampled from some SFT model. If you are constructing the dataset, sample candidates from $\pi^\text{SFT}$ and collect human pairwise labels.

2. Choose $\pi_\text{ref}$. If $\pi^\text{SFT}$ is available, set $\pi_\text{ref}=\pi^\text{SFT}$. Otherwise, fit a reference policy by MLE on preferred completions:
$\displaystyle \pi_\text{ref}=\arg\max_{\pi}\;\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\log\pi(y_w\mid x)].$
Freeze $\pi_\text{ref}$ for training.

3. Initialize $\pi_\theta\leftarrow\pi_\text{ref}$ (the paper does this in practice).

4. Precompute and cache $\log\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)$ for all $(x,y)$ in $\mathcal{D}$ if storage permits. Tokenize and cache sequences to avoid repeated tokenization.

5. Training loop: in each batch, compute
   - $\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)$ and $\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$ by summing token log-probabilities for the completion tokens (use masks to exclude prompt/padding),
   - obtain cached $\log\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)$ and $\log\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)$,
   - form the scalar logits $\Delta = \beta\bigl(\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\log\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)\bigr)-\beta\bigl(\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)-\log\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)\bigr)$,
   - minimize the binary cross-entropy loss $-\log\sigma(\Delta)$ averaged over the batch (this is the DPO objective in equation form).

6. Update $\theta$ with standard supervised gradient steps. The paper emphasizes that DPO training is stable and typically does not need the complex RL machinery.

7. Evaluate regularly (the paper evaluated every 100 training steps in experiments): track the DPO loss on a held-out set of preference pairs, the sequence-level KL to $\pi_\text{ref}$, and task-specific proxies (automatic rewards, GPT-4 win rate, or human judgments).

8. When satisfied, use the trained $\pi_\theta$ as the final aligned policy.

# Practical pitfalls noted in the paper
- Do not replace the DPO per-example importance weighting with a naive unweighted log-probability-ratio objective. The paper reports that a naive ratio objective can lead to degeneration of the language model; the sigmoid-weight from the derived gradient is essential to maintain stability.

- Mismatch between the dataset sampling model and your chosen $\pi_\text{ref}$ can harm performance. When the original SFT is not available, fit $\pi_\text{ref}$ by MLE on the preferred completions to reduce this mismatch.

- Although DPO removes the need for on-policy sampling and value baselines, you must still monitor KL drift to ensure the model does not stray too far from desirable generation behavior.

Closing remark
DPO turns preference learning into a single, well-behaved supervised optimization problem. The implementation recipe in this chapter follows the paper’s design: fixed reference policy, pairwise preference dataset, sequence log-prob scoring, and a binary cross-entropy loss over reparameterized reward differences. That reduction enables you to reuse standard supervised LM training infrastructure and to avoid the complexity, variance, and compute cost of RL-style fine-tuning while still obtaining policies that, in the paper’s experiments, match or outperform PPO-based RLHF.
