\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 12: Evaluating with GPT-4 and Validating with Humans

# GPT-4 prompts: simple vs concise and their behavioral differences
In the paper, the authors used GPT-4 as an automated judge to compute win rates between model-generated outputs (summaries or responses) and reference outputs. They experimented with two distinct GPT-4 prompts intended to elicit pairwise preference judgments. The first, called GPT-4 (S) or the "simple" prompt, asks the model which of two candidate texts "better summarizes the important information in the post" (or, in the dialogue setting, which response is more helpful). The second, GPT-4 (C) or the "concise" prompt, asks the same core question but additionally emphasizes concision — it explicitly asks the judge to prefer a summary that is concise in addition to being accurate. The paper refers readers to the Appendix for the exact prompt text.

Empirically, these two prompt formulations produced different behavioral patterns from GPT-4. With the simple prompt, GPT-4 showed a tendency to favor longer, sometimes repetitive summaries. Adding an explicit concision instruction changed that behavior: under the concise prompt GPT-4 preferred shorter, more compact summaries and its judgments aligned more closely with human ratings in the authors' validation study. The main lesson is that the automated evaluator’s behavior is sensitive to the wording of the evaluation prompt. Small, targeted changes to the prompt — for example, adding an explicit desideratum like "be concise" — can substantially alter the evaluator’s preferences and bring them closer to human judgments for the target task.

# Agreement with human raters and comparison to inter-human agreement
Because automated evaluators are approximations of human judgment, the authors validated GPT-4 by running a human study. They selected three model-condition comparisons meant to span a range of sample quality (a high-performing DPO condition, a low-performing PPO condition, and a middle-performing SFT condition) and collected human judgments on a subset of examples. For each comparison they measured how often GPT-4’s choice agreed with human raters and compared that to inter-human agreement (how often two humans agree with each other).

The key empirical finding is that GPT-4 tended to agree with humans about as often as humans agreed with each other. In other words, GPT-4’s rate of agreement with human raters was roughly on par with the natural variability among humans. The authors therefore conclude that GPT-4, when used with a carefully chosen prompt, is a reasonable proxy for human evaluation in their experiments. They also note a practical limitation: human labeling resources were constrained, and multiple independent human judgments were collected only for a subset of comparisons (specifically those contrasting DPO and a PPO condition). This limited scale means the validation is indicative rather than exhaustive; nevertheless, matching inter-human agreement is a useful sanity check for automated evaluation.

When you design an evaluation pipeline, treating inter-human agreement as a benchmark for automated-evaluator reliability is a practical approach. If an automated evaluator agrees with unambiguous human judgments at a rate comparable to inter-human agreement, it is evidence that the automated signal is capturing much of the human preference structure for that task and prompt.

# Biases such as length preference and how prompt design mitigates them
One concrete bias the authors observed was a length (and repetition) preference in GPT-4 when given a simple, unconstrained prompt. Under GPT-4 (S), the model sometimes rewarded longer outputs because they appeared to contain more information, even when humans preferred shorter, less redundant summaries. The concise prompt (GPT-4 (C)) mitigated that bias: asking explicitly for concision changed GPT-4’s internal scoring so that it gave higher preference to shorter, non-repetitive summaries, producing judgments that mapped better to human preferences in the authors’ validation.

This shows two important principles for controlling evaluation biases. First, automated evaluators are not neutral: they amplify whatever objectives are specified in the prompt. Second, you can reduce undesired bias by making the evaluation criteria explicit in the prompt. If a task values concision, instruction to prioritize concision should be included; if a task values coverage over length, the prompt should say so. In practice, the authors adjusted their evaluation prompt and then empirically verified that the changed prompt produced judgments more representative of humans.

A corollary is that you should test alternative prompts and inspect the kinds of errors or preferences they induce (for example, whether a prompt induces a length bias, a fluency bias, or a factuality bias). The paper demonstrates this by comparing two simple prompts with different emphases and selecting the prompt whose judgments better matched human raters.

# When to rely on human evaluations and hybrid evaluation strategies
Human judgments remain the gold standard, but they are expensive and slow at scale. The authors’ approach is a practical hybrid strategy: use a validated automated evaluator (GPT-4 with a chosen prompt) to perform large-scale evaluation, and validate that automated evaluator against human raters on a representative subset. Concretely, the paper’s workflow was to (1) try candidate GPT-4 prompts, (2) collect human judgments on a subset of comparisons, (3) measure agreement between GPT-4 and humans and compare it to inter-human agreement, and (4) select the GPT-4 prompt whose decisions best reflected human preferences for use in the larger experiments.

This hybrid approach trades off cost and coverage: automated evaluation gives large-scale, repeatable win-rate estimates, while a human validation subset checks and calibrates the automated signal. The authors used this pattern to choose GPT-4 (C) for their main results because it matched human judgments more closely than GPT-4 (S). They also point out practical constraints: because human raters were limited, they only collected multiple human judgments for some of the pairwise comparisons; in other settings you may need to collect more human data to increase confidence.

A practical implication is that you should rely on human evaluation (or on increased human sample size) whenever automated-evaluator agreement with humans is insufficient for your tolerance of error, when the task is high stakes, or when you observe systematic biases in the automated metrics that you cannot remove by prompt engineering. Otherwise, a validated automated judge can be an effective and scalable substitute for many experimental comparisons.

# Putting it together: recommended evaluation pipeline (as used in the paper)
Based on the authors’ experiments and validation, a trustworthy, practical evaluation pipeline contains these elements:

- Design candidate automated-evaluator prompts that reflect the task’s priorities (for example, include concision if concision matters).
- Sample model outputs across relevant decoding settings (the authors evaluated a range of temperatures) so that the evaluation reflects how models will actually be sampled at test time.
- Run the automated evaluator (GPT-4) to compute pairwise win rates at scale.
- Collect human judgments on a representative subset of examples and compute:
  - agreement between GPT-4 and human raters, and
  - inter-human agreement on the same subset.
- If GPT-4’s agreement with humans is comparable to inter-human agreement, you can use GPT-4 for large-scale evaluation; otherwise iterate on the prompt or collect more human labels.
- Reserve human evaluation for final model selection, for verifying edge cases, or whenever automated judgments show systematic divergence from human preferences.

The authors followed this pipeline and, based on their validation, used GPT-4 (C) as the automated metric for the main summarization and dialogue win-rate evaluations.

# Limitations and cautions
The paper emphasizes two limitations that bear on how broadly to trust automated evaluators. First, automated judgments depend on prompt wording: small changes can systematically change outcomes (for example, inducing a length bias). Second, validation itself was performed with limited human resources, so conclusions about representativeness should be treated as suggestive rather than definitive. The authors therefore recommend, and practice, a conservative stance: use automated evaluators only after validation, prefer prompts that replicate human judgments, and continue to re-check automated metrics against humans as models or tasks change.

In short, GPT-4 can be a reliable and practical automated judge if (and only if) you carefully choose and validate the prompt against human judgments. The paper’s experiments illustrate both how prompt wording changes evaluator behavior and a concrete hybrid validation strategy that lets you safely scale evaluation while remaining grounded in human preferences.
