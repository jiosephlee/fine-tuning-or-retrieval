\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 5: Direct Preference Optimization: Objective and Algorithm

This chapter explains Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) — the loss, its interpretation, and the practical pipeline for training a language model policy directly from pairwise preference data. The presentation follows the derivation and results in the paper "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model" and keeps the development self-contained: we start from the standard KL-constrained RLHF objective, show the change of variables that turns a reward fit + RL problem into a simple binary cross-entropy over a policy parameterization, and describe how to implement this in practice.

# DPO loss: binary cross-entropy on beta-weighted log-probability ratio differences between y_w and y_l

DPO fits a policy $\pi_\theta$ directly to pairwise human preference data by maximizing the likelihood that the policy-induced ordering matches the observed preferences under a Bradley–Terry preference model. Concretely, given an offline dataset of comparisons
$\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y_w^{(i)},y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$
(where $y_w$ denotes the human-preferred completion and $y_l$ the dispreferred completion for prompt $x$), DPO uses the following objective:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref}})
= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[ \log \sigma\!\Big( \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\right],
\]

where $\sigma(s)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-s)}$ is the logistic function, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is a fixed reference policy (typically the SFT model), and $\beta$ is a scalar hyperparameter (interpretation below). This is simply the binary cross-entropy (negative log-likelihood) of observing the label "$y_w$ preferred to $y_l$" under the Bradley–Terry model expressed in terms of policy log-probability ratios.

Why this particular form? It is the maximum-likelihood objective obtained after reparameterizing the (unknown) reward differences that generate the preferences in terms of the policy itself (Section 2 below gives the reparameterization). The DPO loss is therefore a standard logistic regression / cross-entropy loss whose inputs are the $\beta$-scaled differences of log-probability ratios $\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})$. In implementation, each pair $(x,y_w,y_l)$ produces a scalar score difference and a single scalar cross-entropy loss; optimization proceeds by standard gradient-based methods.

Gradient and update interpretation. Differentiating the DPO loss yields the following form:

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\text{ref}})
= -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\sigma\big(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)\big)\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\Big],
\]

where we define the implicit reward estimate
$\hat{r}_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$.

This gradient has an intuitive interpretation:

- The factor $\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)$ increases the likelihood of the preferred output $y_w$, while $\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$ decreases the likelihood of the dispreferred output $y_l$ (these are the same per-sample log-probability gradients familiar from maximum-likelihood training).

- The multiplicative scalar weight $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))$ downweights updates when the model already ranks $y_w$ above $y_l$, and upweights updates if $\hat r_\theta$ mistakenly prefers $y_l$ over $y_w$. In other words, the more the current implicit reward model is wrong about the pair, the larger the correction.

- The overall factor $-\beta$ is a global scalar affecting step magnitude (and interacts with the $\beta$ inside $\hat r_\theta$).

This per-example, dynamic importance weight is crucial: a na\"ive objective that simply pushed up $\pi_\theta(y_w)$ and down $\pi_\theta(y_l)$ without this probabilistic weighting can lead to instability or degeneration; DPO’s logistic weighting prevents this by tying update strength to current ranking confidence.

# Policy-as-reward reparameterization: implicit reward equals beta * log(pi/pi_ref)

The key theoretical insight behind DPO is an exact change of variables that expresses a class of reward functions (those compatible with the RLHF objective) in terms of the optimal policy. Start from the standard KL-constrained RLHF optimization:

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r(x,y)\big] - \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\mid\mid\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big].
\]

For any fixed reward function $r(x,y)$, the unique optimal policy (soft-Boltzmann form) solving this problem is

\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Big),
\qquad
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Big).
\]

Taking logarithms and rearranging gives an identity that expresses the reward in terms of the optimal policy:

\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x).
\]

Because preference data under the Bradley–Terry (and more generally Plackett–Luce) models depend only on reward differences $r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$, the additive term $\beta\log Z(x)$ cancels when taking differences. That means that, for the purpose of matching pairwise preferences, we can replace an (under-specified) reward function $r$ by the equivalent, canonical reparameterization

\[
\tilde r(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
\]

for some policy $\pi(\cdot\mid x)$. In words: a policy itself defines a reward (up to an $x$-dependent additive constant), and any reward function in the equivalence class that yields the same preferences and same constrained-optimal policy can be written in this form.

Theorem (informal, from the paper). Under mild assumptions, every reward function in the equivalence class compatible with Plackett–Luce/Bradley–Terry preferences can be represented as
$r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$
for some policy $\pi$. The paper gives a short constructive proof: given any $r$, define the projection
$f(r)(x,y)=r(x,y)-\beta\log\sum_y\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$; substituting the optimal policy into the expression shows $f(r)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_r}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$, i.e. the reparameterized form is simply a normalized member of the same equivalence class.

Consequences and intuition:

- “Your language model is secretly a reward model.” The policy that you are training can be read as defining an implicit reward $\hat r_\theta=\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})$, which is precisely the reward under which $\pi_\theta$ would be the constrained RL optimum (up to the additive, per-input constant that cancels in pairwise comparisons).

- This change of variables removes the need to fit a separate scalar reward network and also removes the need to run an RL loop to maximize the learned reward: instead, fitting the policy to preferences by maximum likelihood (the DPO loss) directly yields the optimal policy corresponding to some reward in the representable class.

- The reparameterization does not reduce expressivity in the sense relevant to preference data: reward functions are only identifiable up to additive functions of $x$, and DPO chooses the canonical representative that sets the partition normalization as above.

# Role of beta: strength of KL constraint and control of policy conservatism

The scalar $\beta$ plays a central role tying the original KL-constrained RL objective to the DPO parameterization and practical behavior of the learned policy.

Interpretation in the original RL formulation. In the constrained optimization

\[
\max_{\pi}\ \mathbb{E}[r(x,y)] - \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi\mid\mid\pi_{\text{ref}}],
\]

$\beta$ controls how strongly deviations from the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ are penalized. A larger $\beta$ places a larger penalty on KL-divergence and therefore yields a policy that stays closer to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (more conservative). Conversely, a smaller $\beta$ weakens the KL penalty and allows the optimized policy to deviate more aggressively to increase reward.

Interpretation inside DPO. In the DPO loss the same $\beta$ appears as a multiplicative scale on the log-probability ratios:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} \sim -\log\sigma\!\big(\beta\big(\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)} - \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}\big)\big).
\]

Thus $\beta$ has two linked effects:

- It scales the implicit reward estimate $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ used inside the logistic. Larger $\beta$ magnifies between-output reward differences implied by a given policy, making the model’s ranking predictions more confident for the same relative log-probabilities.

- It also appears as a multiplicative factor in the gradient (see gradient formula), which changes the absolute step magnitude of updates.

Taken together and read against the original RL formulation, these effects mean that tuning $\beta$ changes the trade-off between reward improvement and staying close to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Practically, smaller $\beta$ values allow more aggressive changes away from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (weaker conservatism), while larger $\beta$ make DPO prefer smaller deviations (stronger conservatism). In experiments reported in the paper, $\beta$ was tuned among values such as $\{0.05,\,0.1,\,1,\,5\}$ to control this trade-off; DPO was found to be relatively robust, and a small grid of $\beta$ values typically suffices.

A final practical note: because the $\beta$-scaling appears inside the logistic, the per-example importance weight $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))$ responds to $\beta$ as well. This coupling is part of why DPO’s updates are stable: the logistic weighting modulates update strength in a way consistent with the KL penalty interpretation.

# Extension from Bradley–Terry to Plackett–Luce rankings

The derivation of DPO starts with the Bradley–Terry model because it is the simplest case of pairwise comparisons. Bradley–Terry is a special case of the more general Plackett–Luce (PL) family of ranking models. PL models the probability of an ordered ranking $y_1\succ y_2\succ\cdots\succ y_k$ as a product of softmax-style terms:

\[
p_{\text{PL}}(y_1\succ y_2\succ\cdots\succ y_k\mid x)
=\prod_{i=1}^k \frac{\exp(r(x,y_i))}{\sum_{j=i}^k \exp(r(x,y_j))}.
\]

If you substitute the same policy-as-reward reparameterization
$r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \text{(prefix-only const)}$,
the normalization terms that depend only on the remaining items cancel or reduce in a manner analogous to the pairwise case, so the overall PL probability can be written in terms of policy probabilities and the reference. Consequently, the DPO-style change of variables extends: instead of a single logistic cross-entropy over a pair, the maximum-likelihood objective under Plackett–Luce becomes a (sequence) cross-entropy between observed rankings and the PL probabilities expressed via $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

In practice this means:

- When the preference data consists of full rankings or top-$k$ orderings rather than single pairwise labels, the same idea applies. One can write the PL likelihood of the observed ranking in terms of the policy log-probabilities and minimize the negative log-likelihood with respect to $\pi_\theta$.

- The pairwise Bradley–Terry case (two items) is the simplest PL special case and is sufficient for most RLHF-style datasets that provide pairwise comparisons.

The paper provides formal derivations for the PL generalization in the appendix; the core idea is the same: because PL and BT depend only on differences of the reward function, the additive partition-function constants cancel after the reparameterization, enabling a direct policy-level likelihood.

# DPO training pipeline: gather preferences, choose pi_ref, optimize the DPO loss

The practical pipeline for using DPO mirrors the standard RLHF data collection and SFT initialization but replaces the reward learning + RL fine-tuning phases with a single supervised-like optimization. The steps are:

1. Produce candidate completions and collect preferences. For each prompt $x$ sample two (or more, if collecting rankings) completions from a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)$. Present these to human annotators (or a high-quality annotator) to obtain comparisons of the form $y_w\succ y_l\mid x$. Collect these into a dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{(x,y_w,y_l)\}$.

   - In many RLHF setups this dataset is already available because it was collected by prompting a supervised fine-tuned (SFT) model and human-labelers. When reusing public preference datasets, be mindful that those comparisons were typically generated by a particular SFT model; using that same SFT as $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ reduces distribution shift.

2. Choose the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. The usual choice is the SFT model $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ obtained by supervised fine-tuning on high-quality demonstrations. If an SFT model is not available, the paper recommends fitting a reference policy to the preferred completions:

\[
\pi_{\text{ref}} \;=\; \underset{\pi}{\arg\max}\ \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\log\pi(y_w\mid x)],
\]

i.e., train the reference model by maximum likelihood on the (prompt, preferred completion) pairs. This helps align the support of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ with the dataset and mitigates distribution shift.

3. Optimize the DPO objective. Initialize your policy $\pi_\theta$ (typically from $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$) and minimize $\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\text{ref}})$ by stochastic gradient descent or any standard optimizer. Each minibatch consists of tuples $(x,y_w,y_l)$ and the per-example loss is the logistic cross-entropy in the DPO formula. Key practical points:

   - No reward model is trained separately: the policy itself parameterizes an implicit reward $\hat r_\theta$.

   - No reinforcement learning (policy gradient rollouts, advantage estimation, value head, actor-critic machinery) or on-policy sampling loop is needed during training: training is just supervised gradient steps on the DPO loss.

   - The main hyperparameter to tune is $\beta$, which controls the reward-vs-KL trade-off (typical search is coarse: e.g., $\beta\in\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$ in the experiments). DPO was found to be relatively stable and required little additional tuning compared to PPO-based RLHF.

4. Evaluation and sampling. After training, sample from $\pi_\theta$ as your final policy. As with other fine-tuning methods, sampling temperature and decoding strategy affect sample diversity and quality; DPO-trained policies have been evaluated across temperatures in the paper.

Why this pipeline is attractive:

- Simplicity: the core training loop is a simple supervised loss, easy to implement on top of existing maximum-likelihood training code for language models.

- Stability and compute: because no inner RL loop, value heads, or repeated on-policy sampling are required, DPO is computationally lightweight and empirically more stable than typical PPO-based RLHF pipelines in the experiments reported.

- Reuse of existing data: when you already have preference datasets collected against a known SFT, simply set $\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi^{\text{SFT}}$ and run DPO.

Practical caveats and notes from the paper:

- The DPO derivation depends on a Bradley–Terry/Plackett–Luce preference model for the data. If your labels follow a very different noisy model, then the maximum likelihood interpretation may be less exact (but the method still gives a reasonable supervised objective).

- The per-example importance weight in the gradient is crucial for avoiding degeneration; the paper reports that a naive unweighted "increase preferred, decrease dispreferred" unlikelihood objective can cause the model to degenerate.

- Implementation details and further hyperparameter recommendations (batching, optimization schedules, etc.) are provided in the paper’s appendix; the high-level pipeline above is what is required to reproduce the DPO approach.

Concluding remarks

DPO turns the usual two-stage RLHF pipeline (fit reward, run RL) into a single, well-principled supervised objective by leveraging the identity between constrained-optimal policies and a policy-parametrized reward. The result is a binary cross-entropy loss on $\beta$-weighted log-probability ratios between preferred and dispreferred completions. Practically, this yields an algorithm that is simple to implement, avoids the complications of actor–critic training, and — as shown in the paper’s experiments — can match or exceed PPO-based RLHF across several tasks while being more computationally lightweight and stable.
