\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 9: Baselines and Comparative Methods

This chapter situates Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) among the common alternatives used to align language models (LMs) to human preferences. For each baseline we describe (1) how it works in the RLHF-style pipeline used in the paper, (2) the assumptions it makes about the available data or modeling components, and (3) the practical trade-offs compared with DPO (in particular, complexity, stability, and the kinds of errors each approach is sensitive to). Where helpful we reproduce the equations from the paper so you can see how the methods relate algebraically to the reward, policy, and preference modeling steps that DPO reorganizes.

# SFT and Preferred-FT (supervised on chosen completions)

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is the simple, commonly used first stage in the RLHF pipeline. Starting from a pre-trained language model, one fine-tunes it on a dataset of high-quality, human-written (or otherwise curated) input–completion pairs for the downstream task to obtain a model denoted $\pi^\text{SFT}$. The SFT model has two roles in later preference-learning stages: it is used as a sensible starting policy, and it frequently serves as the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ that later methods use to constrain policy updates and prevent the policy from drifting too far away from the distribution on which downstream models (e.g., reward models) are accurate.

Preferred-FT is a straightforward supervised baseline built from pairwise preference data: when a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y_w^{(i)},y_l^{(i)})\}$ identifies a preferred completion $y_w$ for prompt $x$, one treats the preferred completion as a target and fine-tunes a model to maximize likelihood of those chosen completions. In the paper this is described as “a model fine-tuned with supervised learning on the chosen completion $y_w$.” Preferred-FT is therefore a pseudo-supervised way to use pairwise data: instead of learning from the comparison directly, it treats the winner as a conventional supervised target.

The main practical advantages of SFT and Preferred-FT are simplicity and low computational cost: they are standard supervised learning problems and do not require reward-model training or sampling-based RL loops. The key limitations, which motivate methods like RLHF and DPO, are that supervised methods do not directly exploit the pairwise-information in $\mathcal{D}$ (they throw away the signal that $y_w$ was preferred to $y_l$ and only keep $y_w$), and they lack an explicit mechanism to control how far the fine-tuned policy can move away from the original SFT/reference model. Empirically in the paper, Preferred-FT often fails to substantially improve over the SFT model on some tasks (for example, Preferred-FT did not significantly improve over SFT on TL;DR summarization), showing the limits of naive supervised treatment of preference pairs. The paper also uses Preferred-FT pragmatically as a way to produce a reference model when a canonical $\pi^\text{SFT}$ is not available: they set $\pi_\text{ref}$ by fine-tuning on the chosen completions so that the reference and the sampled completions are in-distribution for the downstream preference learning stage.

# Unlikelihood training and its alpha coefficient

Unlikelihood training is a simple supervised-style baseline for pairwise preferences. Given a winner $y_w$ and loser $y_l$ for prompt $x$, the unlikelihood approach increases the model probability of $y_w$ and decreases the model probability of $y_l$. In the paper this baseline is described concisely as “optimizes the policy to maximize the probability assigned to $y_w$ and \textit{minimize} the probability assigned to $y_l$,” and the implementation used includes an explicit global scaling coefficient $\alpha\in[0,1]$ on the unlikelihood (decrease) term. That coefficient tunes the relative strength of pushing down losers versus pulling up winners.

Intuitively, unlikelihood is attractive because it directly translates pairwise supervision into log-probability adjustments with the same machinery as maximum likelihood training. It is simple to implement and inexpensive to train compared to RL-based approaches. However, the paper emphasizes a critical difference from DPO: unlikelihood uses a fixed, global weighting for the down-weighting of losers (via $\alpha$) and does not introduce the per-example, dynamic importance weighting that arises naturally from the Bradley–Terry preference model once one reparameterizes rewards by the policy. The DPO gradient for a single pair contains the multiplicative factor $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l) - \hat r_\theta(x,y_w))$ (where $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$ and $\sigma$ is the logistic function). That factor automatically focuses learning on pairs that the current implicit reward model orders incorrectly and downweights pairs that are already ordered correctly. A na\"ive unweighted probability-ratio objective (i.e., a version of unlikelihood without any careful weighting) can cause model degeneration; the paper reports that this is an important practical failure mode (see the paper’s Appendix for experimental evidence). In short, unlikelihood trades theoretical grounding for simplicity: it is cheap but can be brittle unless its global coefficient is tuned carefully, whereas DPO derives a principled, per-example weighting from the underlying preference model.

# PPO-based RLHF with learned rewards and its value baseline

The standard RLHF workflow evaluated in the paper follows three phases: (1) SFT to obtain $\pi^\text{SFT}$, (2) reward modeling from pairwise comparisons, and (3) RL fine-tuning of a policy to maximize the learned reward subject to staying close to a reference policy. The Bradley–Terry preference model is used to link latent scalar rewards to pairwise choices:
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\]
Given a parametric reward network $r_\phi(x,y)$, the reward model is trained by maximum likelihood under this model, i.e.
\[
\mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr],
\]
where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the logistic function. This fits a scalar reward that is (by construction) compatible with the observed pairwise preferences.

The RL fine-tuning stage then optimizes the policy subject to a KL penalty that keeps the learned policy near the reference:
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr].
\]
Because language generation is discrete, this objective is usually optimized with policy-gradient / actor-critic methods; in practice, proximal policy optimization (PPO) has been the common choice. The typical implementation used in RLHF constructs a per-sample scalar reward of the form
\[
\tilde r(x,y) = r_\phi(x,y) - \beta\bigl(\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x) - \log \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr)
\]
and then runs PPO to improve $\pi_\theta$ with respect to these rewards.

There are important practical costs and instabilities in this route. First, training a separate reward model $r_\phi$ requires its own training loop and careful validation. Second, the RL stage requires sampling from the policy in the inner loop and often needs substantial hyperparameter tuning. Third, and crucially, the actor–critic style algorithms introduce a value or baseline estimator to reduce gradient variance. In the paper’s analysis this issue appears explicitly: if one rewrites the objective in a form that compares the parameterized policy to the optimal policy induced by $r_\phi$, a normalization term of the form $-\beta \log \sum_y \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr)$ appears. This term behaves like a ``soft value function'' of the reference policy and must be estimated or otherwise handled in the optimization. The paper notes that while a learned value function can accommodate the term, it is an additional learning problem that can be difficult and a source of instability; prior RLHF work instead sometimes normalizes rewards with a human-completion baseline, a single-sample Monte Carlo estimate. DPO eliminates the need for such baselines by reparameterizing the reward in terms of the policy directly, thereby avoiding an actor–critic learner entirely.

Empirically in the paper, the RLHF P PO implementations required hyperparameter sweeps (target KL values, reward normalization, etc.) and still produced worse trade-offs between achieved reward and divergence from the reference model than DPO. In the controlled sentiment experiments, DPO’s reward–KL frontier strictly dominates PPO even when PPO has access to a tuned implementation.

# PPO-GT with access to ground-truth rewards

PPO-GT is a diagnostic oracle: run PPO but give it the true reward function rather than a learned $r_\phi$. In the controlled sentiment experiment the true reward is available (a sentiment classifier) so PPO-GT can be used to evaluate how much of PPO’s practical shortcomings come from imperfect reward learning versus the RL optimization itself.

The paper runs two PPO-GT implementations: an off-the-shelf PPO and a modified PPO that applies reward normalization and extra hyperparameter tuning. Even with oracle rewards, PPO-GT is outperformed by DPO in terms of the reward–KL tradeoff: DPO achieves higher reward for the same or lower KL divergence than PPO-GT. This empirical result highlights that some limitations of PPO in RLHF settings are intrinsic to the actor–critic optimization style (variance, need for baselines, sensitivity to hyperparameters), not only to errors in the learned reward. In other words, providing the best possible reward does not guarantee PPO will be the most sample- or compute-efficient way to reach the constrained optimum; this motivates the DPO approach of directly optimizing a reparameterized policy objective with a stable classification loss.

# Best-of-N reranking with a learned reward model

Best-of-$N$ (also called reranking) is a simple and powerful baseline that decouples policy generation from reward scoring. The procedure is: sample $N$ candidate completions from a base model (e.g., the SFT model), score each candidate with a learned reward model (trained from preferences), and return the highest-scoring candidate. Because it avoids a complex RL optimization step, Best-of-$N$ often achieves strong empirical performance: it leverages a learned reward model while leaving candidate generation to the (well-behaved) base model.

The main drawback of Best-of-$N$ is computational cost at inference time: one must sample and score $N$ completions for every query. For moderately large $N$ this can be prohibitively expensive in real systems. The paper reports that in TL;DR summarization and single-turn dialogue experiments DPO attains as-good-or-better quality than the Best-of-$N$ baseline while being far cheaper at inference. In the dialogue experiments, the authors found Best-of-$N$ performance plateaus around $N=128$; they therefore used Best-of-128 as a rough upper bound / proxy for what a high-quality RLHF or reranking approach could achieve. DPO matched or exceeded that proxy performance in the experiments, despite being computationally far lighter at test time.

Best-of-$N$ is therefore a useful benchmark for raw achievable quality (if you can afford extensive sampling), but as a practical deployment method it forces a trade-off between quality (large $N$) and latency/cost (small $N$). DPO aims to deliver comparable quality with the efficiency of a single fine-tuned policy at inference.

# Zero-/few-shot prompting baselines

Prompting the base model without fine-tuning is the lowest-effort baseline. In the paper the authors include zero-shot prompting with GPT-J (for summarization) and 2-shot prompting with Pythia-2.8B (for single-turn dialogue) as simple baselines. These methods require no additional labeled data or training time; they rely on the base model’s pre-trained capabilities and on prompt engineering to coax desired behavior.

The trade-offs are clear: prompting is cheap and immediate, but it generally provides less precise steering toward human preferences compared with methods that explicitly train on preference data (SFT, RLHF, DPO). In the presented experiments DPO outperforms the prompting baselines: for example, in TL;DR summarization DPO obtained a higher win rate in GPT-4 judgments than the zero-shot GPT-J baseline. Prompting remains a useful baseline and a practical option when labeled preference data is unavailable or when low latency is prioritized, but it lacks the direct, data-driven control over model behavior that preference-based fine-tuning provides.

Concluding comparison

Table-like intuition (non-mathematical): the methods span a spectrum of simplicity, computational cost, and principled use of pairwise information.

- SFT and Preferred-FT: simple supervised training; cheap to run; do not directly exploit pairwise comparisons and do not explicitly constrain divergence from a reference unless you use regularization.
- Unlikelihood: simple pairwise loss that pushes winners up and losers down; cheap but relies on a global coefficient $\alpha$ and lacks the per-example, preference-model-derived weighting that stabilizes updates in DPO.
- PPO-based RLHF (with learned $r_\phi$): principled two-stage approach (reward learning + RL) that directly optimizes the constrained objective, but introduces a learned reward model, requires sampling in the RL loop, depends on value/baseline estimation, and tends to be more fragile and hyperparameter-sensitive.
- PPO-GT: a diagnostic showing that even with perfect rewards the RL optimizer can be suboptimal vs. DPO, implicating optimization and variance issues in actor–critic methods.
- Best-of-$N$: strong quality by reranking, but computationally expensive at inference time since it requires sampling many candidates per prompt.
- Zero/few-shot prompting: immediate and cheap, but typically less able to match human preference data than fine-tuned approaches.

DPO’s design targets the middle ground: it uses the Bradley–Terry preference model but reparameterizes rewards in terms of the policy so that the optimal policy can be fit directly by solving a binary cross-entropy problem. The resulting loss,
\[
\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right],
\]
is simple to optimize, avoids sampling in the training loop, does not require a separate critic/value baseline, and introduces an adaptive per-example weighting in the gradient that focuses updates where the current implicit reward ordering is wrong. Empirically, DPO combines the practical efficiency of supervised and reranking approaches with the principled objective of RLHF, producing stable, high-quality policies across the tasks evaluated in the paper.
