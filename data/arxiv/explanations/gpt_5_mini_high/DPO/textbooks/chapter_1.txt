\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 1: Motivation: Steering Large Language Models with Human Preferences

# Capabilities vs. desired behavior in large language models
Large unsupervised language models trained at scale acquire a wide range of capabilities: factual knowledge, pattern recognition, and some reasoning skills. These abilities emerge because the models are trained on broad corpora containing text written by many different people for many different purposes. The same training process that produces useful knowledge and skill also encodes a mixture of goals, priorities, and mistakes present in the data. As a result, a model's raw generations reflect the diversity of its training distribution rather than a single, carefully chosen behavior we want it to exhibit.

This distinction between what a model can do and what we want it to do is crucial. The paper gives two illustrative examples. A coding assistant should "understand" common programming mistakes (so it can diagnose and correct them), but when asked to generate working code we prefer the model to produce the relatively rare, high-quality code examples that exist in its corpus. Likewise, a model may be aware that a misconception is widely believed; we may want it to be aware of that fact but not to present the misconception as true in its answers. In short, we must select and steer the model's behavior — choosing which parts of its knowledge and skill to express — if we are to build systems that are safe, helpful, and controllable.

This need for steering motivates methods that sit on top of unsupervised pretraining and reshape the model's output distribution so that it favors the behaviors humans prefer. The work in the paper studies one such approach: learning from pairwise human preferences and using that signal to fine-tune a language model so its generations match what humans consider better.

# Why pairwise human preferences are scalable and accessible
Collecting full, expert demonstrations of desired outputs can be costly. In many settings it is much easier for humans to compare two model-generated responses and say which one they prefer than to craft an ideal response from scratch. Pairwise preference judgments therefore scale more readily: labelers can evaluate many short comparison tasks, and non-experts can often provide reliable comparative judgments even when they could not produce a perfect demonstration.

To convert pairwise judgments into a learning signal we need a statistical model of how preferences arise from underlying "scores" or rewards. A common and simple choice is the Bradley–Terry model (a special case of the Plackett–Luce family). If there is an unobserved scalar reward function $r^*(x,y)$ that scores a completion $y$ in context $x$, the Bradley–Terry model assumes the probability that humans prefer $y_1$ to $y_2$ is
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)
= \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr) + \exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_2)\bigr)}.
\]
Given a dataset of labeled comparisons $\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y_w^{(i)},y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$ (where $y_w$ is the human-preferred completion and $y_l$ the dispreferred one), we can fit a parametric reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ by maximum likelihood. Framing this as binary classification yields the negative log-likelihood (cross-entropy) loss
\[
\mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D})
= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[\log \sigma\bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_w)-r_{\phi}(x,y_l)\bigr)\Bigr],
\]
where $\sigma$ is the logistic function. This converts many simple pairwise judgments into a learned scalar reward that can be used downstream.

Pairwise preference data has been successfully applied across tasks (translation, summarization, dialogue, etc.) because it focuses labeling effort on relative judgments that are often faster and cheaper to collect than demonstrations, while still providing a signal strong enough to alter model behavior.

# Limitations of instruction tuning alone
Instruction tuning — supervised fine-tuning on datasets of instructions paired with human-written completions — substantially improves an LLM's usability. It teaches the model to follow prompts and produce more helpful responses, and it can generalize to unseen instructions to a surprising degree.

Despite its benefits, instruction tuning alone does not solve the steerability problem in general. The instruction-tuned model still reflects the mixture of behaviors present in its training examples, and collecting high-quality demonstrations for every desirable behavior remains expensive. The paper emphasizes that relative human preferences are often easier to obtain than full demonstrations and can be used to refine model behavior in directions that instruction tuning cannot, such as emphasizing particular stylistic or safety constraints that are hard to encode in a demonstration dataset. Consequently, many successful systems add a preference-learning stage after instruction-tuning to more precisely align model outputs with human judgments.

# Complexity and instability of RLHF pipelines
The dominant practical approach to turning preference data into a controllable policy is the three-stage RLHF pipeline described in the paper: (1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to obtain a baseline policy $\pi^\text{SFT}$, (2) reward modeling to fit $r_\phi$ to pairwise data, and (3) reinforcement learning to optimize a policy under the learned reward while staying close to a reference policy.

Concretely, the RL fine-tuning step commonly optimizes
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x,y)\bigr]
\;-\; \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\big\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr],
\]
where $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is typically the SFT model and $\beta>0$ controls how strongly the policy is penalized for deviating from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. The KL term is important: without it the policy can drift to regions where the reward model is unreliable or collapse to degenerate high-reward modes.

Although this formulation is intuitive, it is difficult to optimize in practice. Because generation is discrete, the objective is not directly differentiable, so practitioners use policy-gradient methods such as PPO (proximal policy optimization) or REINFORCE. These RL algorithms introduce several engineering and statistical challenges: they require sampling from the policy during training (which is computationally expensive for large LMs), they need careful hyperparameter tuning, and they typically require learned baselines or value functions to reduce gradient variance. Moreover, actor–critic style methods can be unstable: the reward model $r_\phi$ must be robust, and the learned value estimator or baseline must track normalization terms that depend on the reward; poor estimates here increase variance and can destabilize training.

A further technical difficulty arises when one reasons about the optimal policy for the constrained objective. It can be shown (as in the paper) that the optimal policy corresponding to a reward function $r$ has the Boltzmann form
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
\]
with partition function $Z(x)=\sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr)$. In practice, that partition function is expensive to compute over the combinatorial space of text sequences, so typical RLHF avoids direct computation but must still deal with the consequences of this normalization in the form of value-function or baseline estimation. All these factors make RLHF pipelines complex, computationally intensive, and sometimes unstable.

# DPO at a glance: align with preferences via a simple classification loss
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is a rethinking of the RLHF pipeline that eliminates the separate reward-fitting and RL stages by reparameterizing the reward model so that its optimal policy is expressible in closed form. The core idea is a change of variables: instead of fitting a reward $r_\phi$ and then using RL to obtain a policy, DPO directly parameterizes the target policy $\pi_\theta$ and interprets it as encoding an implicit reward.

Start from the Boltzmann-form relation between a reward and its optimal policy:
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr).
\]
Taking logs and rearranging gives an identity for the reward in terms of a policy:
\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \beta\log Z(x).
\]
Crucially, preference models like Bradley–Terry depend only on differences of rewards between two completions, so the $+\beta\log Z(x)$ term cancels when considering a pair $(y_1,y_2)$. Substituting the reparameterized rewards into the Bradley–Terry preference probability yields an expression that depends only on the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy:
\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)
= \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}
- \beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_1\mid x)}\Bigr)}.
\]
Because of this identity, we can frame maximum likelihood directly over policies instead of over reward functions. DPO optimizes the policy parameters $\theta$ by minimizing the simple cross-entropy loss
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}
\Bigl[\log \sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
- \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Bigr)\Bigr].
\]
This is an ordinary binary cross-entropy objective: for each labeled pair $(y_w,y_l)$ the model is trained to make the implicit reward ratio (expressed via the policy ratio) favor the preferred completion.

What does a DPO gradient step do? Writing $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y) \equiv \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$, the gradient can be inspected as
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}
= -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}
\Bigl[\sigma\!\bigl(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)\bigr)
\bigl(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\bigr)\Bigr].
\]
Thus each example increases the log-probability of the human-preferred completion and decreases the log-probability of the dispreferred one. The factor $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w))$ acts as a per-example, dynamic importance weight: examples on which the model currently predicts the wrong ordering receive larger weight, and examples that the model already ranks correctly receive smaller weight. The paper emphasizes that this weighting is important in practice; a naive objective based only on raw probability ratios (without this logistic weighting) can lead to degeneration.

The DPO training pipeline is simple in practice. One gathers a dataset of pairwise preferences $\mathcal{D}$ by sampling completions $y_1,y_2\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)$ and collecting human preferences. Then one trains $\pi_\theta$ (initialized from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, typically an SFT model) to minimize $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}$. When an SFT model that generated the paired samples is available, it is natural to set $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}=\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$. If $\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$ is not available, the paper recommends estimating a reference policy by maximum likelihood on the preferred completions in $\mathcal{D}$ (i.e., fit a model that maximizes $\mathbb{E}_{x,y_w\sim\mathcal{D}}[\log\pi(y_w\mid x)]$), which helps mitigate distribution shift between the dataset and the reference.

In short, DPO converts preference learning into a single-stage, stable, classification-style optimization that is conceptually and practically much simpler than fitting a reward and running RL to optimize it.

# Contributions and high-level claims of the paper
The paper makes the following main contributions and claims.

First, it introduces Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a new and simple algorithm for learning language model policies directly from pairwise preference data. DPO replaces the separate reward-modeling plus RL stages of the standard RLHF pipeline with a single cross-entropy-style loss on policy ratios, exploiting an analytical mapping between rewards and their KL-constrained optimal policies.

Second, the paper provides theoretical justification for the reparameterization. Preferences modeled by Plackett–Luce (and Bradley–Terry) are invariant to adding a prefix-dependent offset to the reward, so reward functions partition into equivalence classes. The paper proves (Theorem 1) that, under mild assumptions, every reward class consistent with the preference model contains a representative of the form
\[
r(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}
\]
for some policy $\pi$. Equivalently, restricting attention to this parameterization does not lose expressivity for the purpose of matching the observed preferences, and it makes the optimal policy analytically tractable.

Third, the paper argues and demonstrates that DPO is simpler, more stable, and more computationally lightweight than RLHF variants based on actor–critic methods like PPO. Because DPO eliminates the RL loop and avoids learning a separate value baseline to account for partition-function-like normalization terms, it sidesteps a major source of variance and instability in standard RLHF pipelines.

Fourth, the empirical results reported in the paper support DPO's effectiveness. On controlled sentiment generation, summarization (Reddit TL;DR), and single-turn dialogue (the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dataset), DPO matches or exceeds performance of PPO-based RLHF models and other baselines, including "best of $N$" sampling baselines, while working with models up to 6B parameters and with little hyperparameter tuning. The paper highlights that DPO notably outperforms PPO for sentiment control and matches or improves response quality in summarization and dialogue.

Finally, the paper is candid about limitations and avenues for future work. Open questions include how DPO policies generalize out of distribution compared to policies optimized via explicit rewards, how reward over-optimization manifests in this direct parameterization, how DPO scales to very large models beyond those studied (e.g., models orders of magnitude larger), and how to best elicit reliable automatic evaluators like GPT-4 (which the paper used as a proxy for human evaluation). The overall claim is that DPO substantially lowers the practical and conceptual barrier to training aligned language models from preferences, while preserving the objectives and benefits of the RLHF formulation.
