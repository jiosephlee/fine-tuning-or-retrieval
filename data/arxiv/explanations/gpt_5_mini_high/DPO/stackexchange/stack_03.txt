\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### In DPO, is log \(\pi(y\mid x)\) computed over entire sequences or per-token, and how is length bias handled?
Question:
The DPO loss uses terms like \(\log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}\). For an autoregressive LM, this sequence probability is a product over tokens, so the log-probability is a sum over time steps. Does the implementation use the total sequence log-probability or a length-normalized average (e.g., per-token)? If it is the total, how does DPO avoid a bias toward shorter responses, since longer sequences accumulate more negative log-probability? If it is normalized, where is that stated and how is EOS handled in the log-prob calculation?
Answer:
Short answer: DPO uses full sequence probabilities (i.e. the usual autoregressive sequence log-prob, a sum of per-token log-probs) — not a length-normalized average. The paper’s equations and implementation notes treat $\pi(y\mid x)$ as the probability of the whole sampled completion $y$, and the experimental KL is explicitly the sequence-level KL (the sum of per-timestep KLs). EOS (or whatever termination token is part of the sampled completion) is therefore included in that sequence probability.

Why that is the case (intuitively and technically). The DPO objective is written in terms of the sequence-level ratio
$$\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$$
and the loss
$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\Big[\log\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\big)\Big].$$
For an autoregressive LM the sequence log-prob is
$$\log\pi(y\mid x)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\pi(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}),$$
so the DPO reward is the sum over token-level log‑prob differences between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. The paper’s gradient formula also uses $\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y\mid x)$, which is the usual sum of per-token gradients under teacher‑forcing.

Why this does not collapse to “always prefer the shortest sequence” in practice. Two points are important. First, DPO is a pairwise method: it compares the relative log‑prob ratios of two completions, so constant length-dependent factors that affect both $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ in the same way tend to cancel in the difference $\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)$. Second, the reference model acts as a baseline / regularizer: DPO does not maximize raw sequence probability but the ratio to the reference, and the algorithm’s effective conservativeness is controlled by $\beta$. This is the same KL-constrained objective that RLHF methods use, reparameterized; that constraint and the per-example weighting in the DPO gradient (the $\sigma(\cdot)$ term) prevent trivial degeneracies (the paper shows that a naive unweighted ratio objective can cause degeneration). In short, DPO inherits whatever length tendencies the reference and label data imply, but it has no implicit “shorter-is-better” objective beyond the usual fact that shorter sequences can have higher raw likelihoods — that effect is moderated by the reference baseline and the pairwise form of the loss.

About EOS and practical computation. The paper treats a sampled completion $y$ as the full sequence produced by the sampling procedure, so you compute $\log\pi(y\mid x)$ by summing the per-token log-probs up to the sequence termination (EOS or truncation). The experimental note that they report “sequence-level KL (that is, the sum of the per-timestep KL‑divergences)” confirms this implementation choice.

If you explicitly want a length‑normalized objective (e.g. average log-prob per token) you would be changing the reward parameterization: dividing $\log\pi(y\mid x)$ by length is not equivalent to the reparameterization the paper uses and would require a different derivation and would alter the theoretical guarantees (the DPO derivation and Theorem 1 rely on the sequence‑level log‑prob reparameterization). The paper does not make that modification.
