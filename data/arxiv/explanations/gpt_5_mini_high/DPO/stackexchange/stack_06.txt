\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Is DPO truly equivalent to PPO-based RLHF on the same Bradley–Terry objective, or only in the limit of infinite data?
Question:
The paper claims DPO "implicitly optimizes the same objective as existing RLHF algorithms (reward maximization with a KL-divergence constraint)." However, DPO trains on a fixed offline set of pairs, while PPO learns on-policy with respect to the evolving \(\pi_\theta\). Under what conditions do the solutions of DPO and PPO coincide? Is the equivalence exact only at the population level (infinite preference data drawn from the optimal policy’s preference distribution), or does it hold for finite datasets? How does off-distribution bias from the fixed dataset affect the equivalence?
Answer:
Short answer: DPO is exactly equivalent to the KL‑constrained RLHF objective only in the population / well‑specified limit. Concretely, if (1) human preferences follow the Bradley–Terry (or more generally Plackett–Luce) model induced by some latent reward $r^*$, (2) the offline pairwise dataset is effectively the population distribution (infinite data or a consistent estimator), (3) the reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and KL weight $\beta$ used by DPO are the same as in the RL formulation, and (4) your parametric family has enough capacity and optimization is perfect, then the DPO MLE recovers the same optimal policy that the KL‑constrained RL problem (the PPO target) admits. In finite data or under model/misspecification/support mismatch the equivalence is only approximate and practical differences (including off‑distribution bias) matter.

Why the population equivalence holds (the algebraic intuition). For the constrained RL problem the optimal policy for a reward $r$ takes the softmax form
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x)\propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr).
$$
Rearranging gives a representation of any reward (up to an additive function of $x$) in terms of the optimal policy:
$$
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\text{const}(x).
$$
Because Bradley–Terry preferences depend only on reward differences, the additive constant cancels and the pairwise preference probability can be written purely as a function of the optimal policy:
$$
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)
=\sigma\Bigl(\beta\bigl[\log\frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_1\mid x)}
-\log\frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}\bigr]\Bigr).
$$
DPO simply treats this as a likelihood for $\pi_\theta$ and fits $\pi_\theta$ by maximum likelihood on the preference pairs. In the population limit (infinite data, correct BT model, perfect optimization and sufficient model class) the MLE $\pi_\theta$ equals $\pi^*$, the unique policy that maximizes the original RL objective for the corresponding reward class. The paper’s Lemmas and Theorem formalize this: the BT family is under‑identified up to an additive function of $x$, but any reward in the same equivalence class yields the same preferences and same optimal policy, and every such class has a representative of the DPO form.

Where finite‑data / practical deviations appear. In practice we never have infinite data or perfect optimization, so the two procedures are different estimators of the same underlying object:

- DPO is a single‑stage estimator that directly fits a policy $\pi_\theta$ to maximize the likelihood of observed pairwise labels under the BT reparameterization (Eq. 8 of the paper). Its statistical error is the usual MLE finite‑sample error: bias/variance from limited labeled pairs and model misspecification.

- PPO (or RLHF with a learned reward) is a two‑stage procedure: first fit a reward model $r_\phi$ to the pairs, then use RL (PPO) to maximize expected reward minus $\beta$ KL. Finite‑sample errors occur both in reward estimation and in the RL optimization (actor/critic variance, imperfect convergence). Those two approximation steps create different finite‑sample bias than DPO’s single MLE step.

Because of these different error pathways, finite datasets generally yield different learned policies. Empirically (and as argued in the paper) DPO often wins in stability and sample efficiency because it avoids the extra reward‑fitting + RL loop and the high variance/value‑function issues that actor‑critic methods face.

Off‑distribution bias / support mismatch — what to watch for. In realistic RLHF data the labeled pairs are collected by sampling completions from some fixed policy (typically the SFT model), so the dataset only covers outputs in the support of that sampling policy. That induces two important practical effects:

1) Lack of information off the dataset support. Neither the reward model nor DPO has labeled information about completions that were never sampled. If either algorithm (PPO) moves the policy into regions not represented in the dataset, the reward model’s extrapolation can be wrong; PPO, by sampling on‑policy, is more likely to encounter and exploit such extrapolation errors unless the KL term strongly constrains it. DPO, being trained offline on the fixed pairs, will not generate new labels by itself and so cannot correct for reward exaggeration outside the dataset support, but it also cannot “discover” and then overfit to spurious high rewards off‑support.

2) Implicit regularization via $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. DPO’s objective explicitly contains the log ratio to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (through the reparameterization), so the reference distribution appears in the loss and acts like a built‑in regularizer toward the sampling policy. That helps keep the learned policy near the distribution where labels exist, but it does not magically add information about unseen outputs. The paper suggests initializing $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to the SFT model that generated the pairs (or fitting $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to preferred completions when SFT is unavailable) to reduce distributional mismatch.

Practical upshot / guidance. The formal equivalence is an asymptotic statement: if your preference model is correctly specified (Bradley–Terry), you have enough labeled pairs, and you can optimize perfectly, DPO and the solution of the KL‑constrained RL objective coincide. With realistic finite datasets, they are different estimators: DPO tends to be simpler, more stable and often more sample efficient because it avoids reward‑fitting and RL instability (the paper’s experiments show this), but it is also inherently offline and limited by the support of the labeled pairs. If you plan to collect labels online, explore beyond the SFT support, or need to actively acquire new comparisons in regions the SFT never covered, an on‑policy loop (PPO + relabeling) can be necessary; conversely, if you have a good offline preference dataset sampled from a known reference and want a stable, cheap procedure, DPO is often the better choice.

In short: exact equivalence = population / well‑specified limit (the paper’s algebraic results); finite datasets, model error, support mismatch and optimization approximations break exact equivalence and determine practical differences (and why the paper emphasizes how DPO avoids the instabilities of actor‑critic RL in practice).
