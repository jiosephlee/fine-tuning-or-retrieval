\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why does the DPO gradient include a sigmoid weighting term, and what breaks if we omit it (i.e., use naive unlikelihood)?
Question:
The gradient sketch shows a weight of \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))\) multiplying the difference of score functions \(\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l\mid x)\). Intuitively this upweights pairs the model currently misorders. What precisely goes wrong if we drop the sigmoid weight and simply push up \(\log\pi(y_w\mid x)\) and push down \(\log\pi(y_l\mid x)\) (unlikelihood)? Can someone give a simple example or argument showing how the unweighted objective encourages degeneration (e.g., collapsing to short answers or suppressing diversity) that the DPO weighting avoids?
Answer:
Short answer: the sigmoid weight is essential because DPO is the gradient of a proper Bradley–Terry (logistic) likelihood under the reparameterization $r = \beta \log\left(\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\right)$; that logistic factor makes the update focus on the “hard” (misordered) pairs and causes the step size to vanish as a pair becomes confidently correct. Dropping it turns the update into an unbounded, constant-magnitude push that keeps driving probabilities to extremes (reducing entropy and diversity, often collapsing to short/high‑probability answers). The paper observed exactly this degeneration in the naive unlikelihood variant (see Appendix).

Why that sigmoid appears. The DPO loss is
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\Big[\log\sigma\big(\hat r(y_w)-\hat r(y_l)\big)\Big],
\qquad
\hat r(y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)},
$$
so its gradient (paper Eqn.) is
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}
= -\beta\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\sigma\big(\hat r(y_l)-\hat r(y_w)\big)\,\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\Big].
$$
The multiplicative factor $\sigma(\hat r(y_l)-\hat r(y_w))=\sigma(-\Delta)$ (where $\Delta=\hat r(y_w)-\hat r(y_l)$) is near 1 when the model currently misorders the pair ($\Delta$ negative) and near 0 when the model already strongly prefers the correct answer ($\Delta$ large positive). In other words, DPO focuses learning on hard/misordered examples and automatically attenuates updates for already-correct pairs.

What you get if you drop it (the “naive unlikelihood”). Removing the sigmoid produces the update
$$
\nabla_\theta \widetilde{\mathcal L}
= -\beta\,\mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big],
$$
which is exactly the gradient of the simple surrogate objective
$$
\widetilde{\mathcal L} = -\beta\,\mathbb{E}\big[\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big].
$$
This objective has two problematic properties. First, it is unboundedly improved by pushing the preferred answers’ probabilities to 1 and the dispreferred ones to 0; even a single pair admits the trivial “collapse” solution $\pi(y_w\mid x)\to 1,\ \pi(y_l\mid x)\to 0$. Second, the gradient magnitude does not decay as the pair becomes already well ordered, so SGD will continue to exert pressure to make the margin arbitrarily large. Those two facts together encourage the model to drive probability mass away from large parts of the support, and because parameters are shared across many contexts the mass ends up concentrated on a small set of outputs that are easiest to upweight (in practice this often correlates with short, high‑prior responses), i.e. reduced diversity and mode collapse.

A tiny concrete illustration. For a single comparison (one prompt, two completions), $\widetilde{\mathcal L}=\log \frac{\pi(y_l)}{\pi(y_w)}$. Minimizing this pushes $\pi(y_w)\to1$ and $\pi(y_l)\to0$ — nothing prevents this, and the gradient magnitude stays large until the extremes. By contrast DPO’s loss $-\log\sigma(\hat r(y_w)-\hat r(y_l))$ saturates: once $\hat r(y_w)$ is sufficiently bigger than $\hat r(y_l)$ the sigmoid term goes to 0 and further pushes vanish, so the model stops changing that pair and does not keep draining probability from other answers.

How this ties to the intended RL/KL view. DPO is derived from the KL‑constrained reward maximization and is the MLE under the Bradley–Terry reparameterization $r=\beta\log\left(\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\right)$. The sigmoid weight is therefore not an ad‑hoc stabilizer but the derivative of that likelihood; it implicitly enforces the soft margin and preserves the “don’t move if already correct / don’t move too far from the reference” behavior that the original constrained-RL objective gives you. The naive unlikelihood objective severs this link to a probabilistic preference model and to the implicit KL regularizer, so it can (and empirically does, per the paper’s Appendix) push the model to degenerate solutions.

Practical takeaway: the sigmoid weight is the mechanism that (a) focuses learning on the mistakes the model still makes and (b) causes updates to fade as pairs become correct, thereby preventing runaway suppression of many plausible generations and the resulting collapse to short or repetitive answers. Omitting it removes that protection and encourages the degeneration you observed.
