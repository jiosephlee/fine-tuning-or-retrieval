\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Without on-policy sampling, how does DPO avoid overfitting to a fixed set of pairs and discover better responses?
Question:
Since DPO trains on an offline fixed dataset of preference pairs, how does it avoid overfitting to those particular responses and missing higher-quality responses that were never shown? Is the idea that reweighting log-ratios relative to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) coupled with generalization of \(\pi_\theta\) suffices, similar to supervised fine-tuning? In what settings might we need online data collection or self-play (e.g., generating new candidate pairs from the current \(\pi_\theta\)) to keep improving, and can DPO be used in such a loop without abandoning its simplicity?
Answer:
Short answer: DPO does not magically invent answers that never appeared in the training pairs; what prevents overfitting to a fixed set of pairs is (1) the LM’s inductive generalization, (2) a pairwise/log-ratio training signal that moves probability mass across the whole conditional distribution rather than memorizing tokens, (3) the implicit KL “anchor” to a reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (controlled by $\beta$), and (4) the DPO per-example weighting that focuses learning on the informative (mis-ranked) pairs. If the offline preference data and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ already cover or imply better completions, DPO will generalize to them; if they do not, you need extra candidate generation (on-policy / self-play or active data collection) and DPO can be slotted into that loop without becoming RL—the paper notes this as a natural extension but treats it as future work.

Technical intuition (grounded in the paper). DPO parameterizes an implicit reward as
$$
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)},
$$
and directly fits the policy with the pairwise likelihood
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
- \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\Big].
$$
The gradient is
$$
\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}
= -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\Big[\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\Big].
$$
Two points follow. First, updates are contrastive: they increase probability of the preferred response and decrease probability of the dispreferred one, i.e. they change the whole conditional distribution rather than only “copying” $y_w$. Second, the multiplicative weight $\sigma(\cdot)$ concentrates learning on pairs the current model mis-ranks (large weight when the model believes the dispreferred $y_l$ is better). That behaves like a margin-weighted logistic loss and reduces useless overfitting to already-correct pairs.

Role of $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $\beta$. The reference $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (usually the SFT model used to generate the pairwise dataset) acts as an anchor: DPO’s implicit reward is always a log-ratio against $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ so the optimization is regularized away from extreme, unsupported shifts. $\beta$ controls how conservative that anchor is (larger $\beta$ → stronger penalty / more conservative behavior). The paper recommends initializing $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}} = \pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$ (or fitting $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to the preferred completions when SFT is unavailable) to reduce distribution mismatch. This anchoring plus the pairwise, weighted loss is why DPO often generalizes better than naive supervised fine-tuning on $y_w$ alone (Preferred-FT) and why the authors observed that DPO dominates PPO on the reward/KL frontier in experiments.

When you need on-policy / self-play. If the static preference dataset (and the $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ that generated it) never contains — and does not imply by interpolation — higher-quality responses, DPO cannot reliably invent them: it only pushes probability mass toward better responses that the model can reach via its parameters and the learned signal. This is the same “coverage / exploration” limitation seen in offline RL and batch dueling-bandit learning: to discover genuinely new high-quality modes you must generate and label candidates outside the original support. Examples include tasks where the initial SFT is weak, the dataset is tiny or narrow, or the desired behavior is far from anything $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ produces.

Can you keep DPO simple while doing that? Yes. The natural, simple loop is: sample new candidate completions from the current policy (or a mixture of $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $\pi_\theta$), have them labeled (human or trusted synthetic annotator), append those labeled pairs to your offline dataset, then re-run DPO (same cross-entropy/pairwise objective). You do not need RL machinery—just supervised pairwise updates—so the simplicity of DPO is preserved. Two practical cautions from the paper’s theory and discussion: keep an eye on which $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ you use (keeping the original SFT anchor is a conservative choice to avoid runaway drift), and label quality matters (noisy labels will misdirect updates). The paper frames this iterative / self-labeling idea as an open question for future work and presents empirical evidence that DPO already generalizes well in several benchmarks (including an out-of-distribution test) when $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and the preference data have reasonable coverage.

Bottom line: DPO reduces overfitting risk via its contrastive, weighted objective and KL anchoring to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ plus the LM’s inductive bias. But if you want to discover genuinely new, better responses not implied by your offline data, you’ll need to collect (or synthesize) labels for candidates sampled from the new policy—something DPO can absorb straightforwardly while keeping the training step simple.
