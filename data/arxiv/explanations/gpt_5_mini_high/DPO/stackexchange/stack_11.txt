\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What happens if \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is chosen to be uniform—does DPO reduce to standard logistic regression on log-likelihoods?
Question:
Suppose I take \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\) to be uniform over a fixed finite candidate set for each \(x\). Then the DPO objective reduces to a logistic loss on \(\beta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\) differences. Is this interpretation correct? If so, does using a uniform reference make DPO more prone to over-optimization or distribution shift, since the implicit reward \(\hat r_\theta=\beta\log\pi_\theta\) has no baseline anchor to the original model? Are there reasons to avoid a uniform reference in practice, especially with open-ended language generation where the candidate set is huge?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — algebraically DPO collapses to a logistic loss on log-probabilities when the reference is uniform, and that choice removes the usual SFT “anchor”, which in practice makes the method behave very differently (and usually worse) for open‑ended generation. The paper itself recommends using the SFT model as the reference for precisely these reasons.

Why the math is that simple. The DPO loss in the paper is
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) =
- \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
- \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\Big].
$$
If $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)$ is uniform on a finite candidate set of size $K(x)$, then $\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x) = -\log K(x)$ is the same for every $y$ and cancels in the difference. You get
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = - \mathbb{E}\Big[\log\sigma\big(\beta(\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x))\big)\Big],
$$
so the pairwise probability depends only on differences of $\log\pi_\theta$. Equivalently the implicit reward becomes
$$
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)+\text{(const wrt y)},
$$
so pairwise comparisons are logistic on the log-likelihoods.

Why this matters practically. The paper motivates and uses a nonuniform $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (typically the SFT model) for two linked reasons: the KL term in the original RL objective
$$
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[r]-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}[\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}]
$$
acts as an anchor that keeps the fine‑tuned policy near the reference distribution where the preference data (and any learned reward) are reliable. If $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is uniform then
$$
\mathrm{KL}[\pi_\theta\|u] = -H(\pi_\theta) + \log K,
$$
so the KL penalty becomes (up to a constant) an entropy bonus $+\beta H(\pi_\theta)$ rather than a penalty that discourages drifting away from the SFT distribution. That means you lose the intended regularization that prevents the policy from moving into regions where the human preference signal is uncalibrated. The paper explicitly notes the importance of this constraint for preventing model degeneration and distribution shift, and recommends setting $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}=\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$ when available (or fitting a reference to the preferred completions if not).

Concrete consequences and when uniform is (not) sensible. If your candidate set is truly small, closed, and you legitimately want to rank items with no prior (e.g., re‑ranking ten fixed hypotheses per prompt), a uniform reference is a valid, simple choice and reduces DPO to a logistic contrast over log-probabilities. But for open‑ended language generation the candidate set is astronomically large and (a) you cannot sensibly sample uniformly from it, (b) the preference data are almost always collected from an SFT or other nonuniform policy, and (c) optimizing an implicit reward equal to $\log\pi_\theta$ risks pushing the model toward whatever sequences the model can easily increase probability for (short/generic/high‑probability sequences, reward hacking, mode collapse), or into regions where the human labels do not generalize. In short, uniform $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ removes the natural “baseline” anchoring the learned policy to the model that produced the preference data and so increases the chance of over‑optimization and harmful distribution shift. The paper therefore recommends using the SFT/reference model as $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (or fitting one to the preferred completions) to mitigate these problems.

If you do experiment with a uniform reference, treat it as a special re‑ranking/regression regime (finite candidate set), and add explicit regularization (e.g., an explicit KL to an SFT baseline or other constraints) if you want to avoid the drift problems the paper warns about.
