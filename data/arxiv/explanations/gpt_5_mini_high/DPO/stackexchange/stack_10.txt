\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How does DPO generalize to k-way Plackett–Luce rankings, and what is the resulting loss?
Question:
The paper mentions that the derivation extends to Plackett–Luce models when we have ranked lists, not just pairs. What is the explicit DPO loss when the data provide a total order \(y_{(1)}\succ y_{(2)}\succ\dots\succ y_{(k)}\) under the Plackett–Luce assumption? Is it the negative log-likelihood of a cascade of softmaxes over the policy-to-reference log-ratios, i.e., terms like \(\log\tfrac{\exp(\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{(j)}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{(j)}\mid x)})}{\sum_{m\ge j}\exp(\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{(m)}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{(m)}\mid x)})}\)? Any computational or stability differences compared to the pairwise case?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — DPO extends exactly to the Plackett–Luce (PL) ranking model. If you observe a full ordered list $y_{(1)}\succ y_{(2)}\succ\cdots\succ y_{(k)}$ the DPO objective is the negative log‑likelihood of the PL cascade where the PL “scores’’ are the policy-to-reference log‑ratios scaled by $\beta$. Concretely, define
$$
s_{(j)}(x)\;=\;\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{(j)}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{(j)}\mid x)}.
$$
The PL probability of that ranking under these scores is
$$
\prod_{j=1}^k \frac{\exp\big(s_{(j)}(x)\big)}{\sum_{m\ge j}\exp\big(s_{(m)}(x)\big)},
$$
so the DPO loss for one ranked example is the negative log‑likelihood
$$
-\sum_{j=1}^k \log\frac{\exp(s_{(j)})}{\sum_{m\ge j}\exp(s_{(m)})}
\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^k\Big(\log\!\sum_{m\ge j}\exp(s_{(m)}) - s_{(j)}\Big).
$$
(This reduces to the pairwise Bradley–Terry / logistic DPO loss when $k=2$, which is the form shown in the main text. The paper states this PL extension in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.)

Practical / numerical notes and how it compares to the pairwise case. The dominant work is scoring each candidate under $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (getting the sequence log‑probs); that cost grows linearly with $k$. Once you have the $s_{(j)}$ values, the PL loss can be computed stably and in $O(k)$ time by a back‑to‑front log‑sum‑exp pass: compute $\text{lse}_k=s_{(k)}$ and $\text{lse}_j=\log\!\operatorname{logsumexp}(s_{(j)},\text{lse}_{j+1})$, then accumulate $\sum_j(\text{lse}_j-s_{(j)})$. Using the log‑sum‑exp trick avoids overflow/underflow; this is the standard stable implementation trick for softmax/PL and is no more delicate than the pairwise logistic implementation (which ordinarily uses a numerically stable log‑sigmoid).

Statistical / optimization differences versus converting a ranking into pairwise comparisons. You could turn a $k$-ranking into many pairwise Bradley–Terry comparisons (all $\binom{k}{2}$ pairs) and train with the pairwise DPO loss on those pairs. That is a different objective: it overweights low‑rank pairs and costs more (up to $O(k^2)$ pairwise terms). The PL cascade is the canonical MLE for full rankings and uses only $k-1$ nontrivial terms (the last step contributes zero), so it is generally more statistically efficient and computationally attractive for true ranked lists. The gradient interpretation is natural: each cascade step compares the chosen item to the remaining pool via a softmax, so the learning signal is distributed across all remaining alternatives (rather than as many independent pairwise signals).

Implementation caveats. Make sure the reference model has support on the candidates (or add tiny smoothing) so the log‑ratio is finite. Large values of $\beta$ amplify the logits and can make the softmax numerically sharp (again, handled by log‑sum‑exp but may affect optimization dynamics). Other than these usual concerns, there is no special instability introduced by using the PL loss — just use log‑domain arithmetic and the cumulative log‑sum‑exp trick.

In short: your guess was correct — the DPO loss for a $k$-way ranked list under Plackett–Luce is exactly the negative log of the cascade of softmaxes over the scores $\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\rm ref})$. It is naturally more informative than a single pairwise term and, if implemented with standard log‑sum‑exp numerics and by caching sequence log‑probs, is both computationally and numerically straightforward.
