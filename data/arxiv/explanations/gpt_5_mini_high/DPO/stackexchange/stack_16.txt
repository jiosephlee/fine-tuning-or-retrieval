\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Can DPO be adapted to token-level or step-wise preferences instead of sequence-level pairs?
Question:
DPO is presented for sequence-level preferences between completions \(y_w\) and \(y_l\). If I have feedback at the token level or at intermediate steps (e.g., a human marks certain tokens or spans as better), can the DPO derivation be adapted to operate on partial sequences \(y_{1:t}\)? Would the loss become a sum over steps of Bradley–Terry terms using \(\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t\mid x, y_{<t})}\), or are there pitfalls due to the BT model assuming comparisons at the same "granularity"?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — DPO can be applied to partial / token-level items, but you must be careful about what your “items” in the Bradley–Terry model are. If your human labels are explicit pairwise comparisons between two partial sequences (or between two token choices given the same prefix), you can plug those pairs into DPO exactly as in the paper by treating each partial sequence (or token-choice-with-prefix) as an item y. What you cannot generally do is replace a sequence-level DPO term by a naïve sum of independent per-step Bradley–Terry losses unless your comparisons themselves are per-step and share the same context.

Why this is true (math + intuition). The DPO loss in the paper for a pair (x, y_w, y_l) is
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}( \pi_\theta ) \;=\; -\log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} \;-\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big),
$$
and this formula does not care whether the y's are full sequences, truncated prefixes, or single tokens: any object y for which your model defines a probability $\pi(\,y\mid x)$ is a valid “item” in the Bradley–Terry model. For a partial sequence $y_{1:t}$ the sequence log-probability naturally decomposes into conditionals:
$$
\log\pi_\theta(y_{1:t}\mid x)=\sum_{i=1}^t \log\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x,y_{<i}),
$$
so the DPO inner argument becomes a difference of sums of per-token log‑ratios:
$$
\beta\sum_{i=1}^{t_w}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{w,i}\mid x,y_{w,<i})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w,i}\mid x,y_{w,<i})}
\;-\;
\beta\sum_{i=1}^{t_l}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{l,i}\mid x,y_{l,<i})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l,i}\mid x,y_{l,<i})}.
$$

Two useful special cases follow:

1) Token-level comparisons at the same prefix. If the human label compares two token choices at the same prefix $y_{<t}$ (say tokens $a$ and $b$), then the sums cancel and the DPO term exactly reduces to a per-step Bradley–Terry term over the conditional distributions:
$$
-\log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a\mid x,y_{<t})}
-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(b\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(b\mid x,y_{<t})}\Big).
$$
So if your annotations are pairwise token choices given identical context, you may safely form a dataset of such pairs and minimize the corresponding per-step DPO losses (sum them over labeled steps).

2) General partial-sequence comparisons. If the two partial sequences differ over multiple positions or over different prefixes, the DPO argument is a single logistic over the sum of token‑level log ratios (the expression above). Because $\sigma$ is nonlinear, this is not equal to a sum of independent per-step logistic losses. The supervision couples the tokens: a single sequence-level preference constrains the summed token rewards, not each token independently.

Practical implications and pitfalls. First, always make sure the compared items live in the same “choice set” the annotator intended. Per‑token BT is valid only when the annotator actually compared the two token choices at the same prefix (or you generate comparable alternatives). If an annotator marks a token/span as “better” but did not provide an explicit alternative, you must manufacture alternatives (e.g., replace the span with sampled alternatives or compare post‑hoc continuations) to form pairwise data compatible with BT/DPO.

Second, beware of downstream effects: a token that looks locally better might produce worse continuations later. If your human feedback is meant to capture final-sequence quality, per-token labels that ignore continuations can train the policy to optimize locally at the expense of global quality. To reflect downstream effects you can either (a) collect comparisons of partial sequences that include downstream text (treating those partial/full sequences as items in DPO), or (b) for a token-level label, sample continuations after each alternative token and compare the resulting sequences (so the item compared is a completed sequence).

Third, you can mix granularities in the same DPO training set: treat every labeled object (single-token-with-prefix, truncated sequence, full sequence) as an item y and include its pairwise comparisons in $\mathcal{D}$. DPO’s likelihood is still a valid MLE under the Bradley–Terry / Plackett–Luce assumptions. But mixed-granularity data imposes different (and possibly conflicting) constraints on the model; check consistency and monitor sequence-level metrics you care about.

Finally, implementation notes from the paper that carry over: you still need a $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ that gives the reference probabilities at the same granularity (per-token conditionals or sequence probabilities), and you should tune $\beta$ as usual because the $\beta$ reweights the implicit reward scale and appears inside the sigmoid.

Summary recommendation. If your annotators explicitly compare token choices in the same prefix, implement DPO at the token-conditional level (each label becomes a per-step DPO example). If your labels are spans or partial-sequence judgments that imply downstream effects, either treat the partial sequences as items in DPO or convert each token/span label into completed-sequence comparisons (by sampling or deterministic completion) before applying DPO. Don’t naively replace a sequence-level DPO term by a sum of independent per-step BT losses unless your annotations actually decompose at that granularity.
