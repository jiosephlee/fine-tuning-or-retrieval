\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How is the KL between \(\pi\) and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) computed and controlled in DPO experiments, given DPO has no explicit KL penalty?
Question:
In the experiments, the paper reports a sequence-level KL between \(\pi\) and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) and shows a reward–KL frontier. Since the DPO loss does not include an explicit KL penalty, how is the realized KL controlled—only via the choice of \(\beta\)? Is the KL measured as a sum of per-token KLs over sampled sequences from \(\pi\), or evaluated on a fixed set of prompts with teacher-forced targets? Is there a principled way to select \(\beta\) to achieve a desired KL without sweeping?
Answer:
Short answer: the realized KL is controlled by the DPO temperature parameter $\beta$ (it plays the same role as the KL Lagrange multiplier in the usual RLHF objective), and the paper reports a sequence-level KL computed on held-out prompts (they define it as “the sum of the per‑timestep KL divergences”). In practice that sequence KL is estimated as an expectation under the learned policy (i.e. by generating completions and computing log‑prob differences), and the paper chooses $\beta$ by simple sweeps rather than giving a closed‑form mapping from $\beta$ to a desired KL.

Why $\beta$ controls KL (intuition + formula). DPO is derived from the KL‑constrained objective
$$
\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi}[r(x,y)] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big[\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big],
$$
whose optimal policy has the Boltzmann form
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x)\propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\big).
$$
Reparameterizing the reward as
$$
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)
$$
(and substituting into the Bradley–Terry preference model) produces the DPO loss. Because $\beta$ is the same multiplier that appears in the original constrained problem, changing $\beta$ changes how strongly the method trades off reward vs. staying near $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (larger $\beta$ $\Rightarrow$ stronger penalty $\Rightarrow$ more conservative policy).

How the paper measures KL. The paper explicitly reports “average sequence‑level KL (that is, the sum of the per‑timestep KL‑divergences) with the reference policy.” The sequence KL for a prompt $x$ can be written as
$$
\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)
= \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}\Big[\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Big]
= \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}\Big[\sum_{t}\log\frac{\pi(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}\Big],
$$
which equals the expected sum of per‑step KLs. To estimate this in practice you Monte‑Carlo sample completions $y^{(i)}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)$ and compute the empirical average
$$
\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}(x)\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N\Big(\log\pi(y^{(i)}\mid x)-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{(i)}\mid x)\Big),
$$
then average across prompts. (Equivalently you can compute per‑step KLs at the sampled prefixes and sum them.) This is consistent with the paper’s description that they evaluate each policy on a set of test prompts and report the average sequence‑level KL.

Is there a principled way to pick $\beta$ without sweeping? The paper itself simply sweeps a small grid of $\beta$ values (e.g. $\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$) and reports the reward–KL frontier; it does not present a closed‑form mapping from $\beta$ to a target KL. A principled approach you can use in practice is to treat $\beta$ as the Lagrange multiplier and adapt it during training (exactly the same idea used in KL‑penalized RL): measure the current KL periodically and increase $\beta$ if KL exceeds your target (or decrease it if KL is well below target). Concretely a simple dual update step is
$$
\beta \leftarrow \beta + \eta\big(\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}-\mathrm{KL}_{\text{target}}\big)
$$
(or a positive multiplicative update), with projection to keep $\beta>0$. Alternatively, a cheap practical strategy is to train short pilots for a few $\beta$ values or do a coarse binary search: these are exactly the types of sweeps used in the paper. Note that the mapping from $\beta$ to realized KL depends on model capacity, data, and optimization dynamics, so some empirical calibration is unavoidable.

Practical tips. When you care about generation behavior use KL estimated under sampled generations (not teacher‑forced KL on gold prefixes) because teacher‑forced KL tends to understate divergence that appears when the model self‑generates prefixes. Start with the ranges the paper used, run cheap pilot runs to measure KL, and then either pick the $\beta$ that lies on the desired point of the frontier or run an adaptive dual controller as above.

In short: DPO has no explicit KL term in its loss because the KL constraint is encoded implicitly via the reparameterization; the paper measures sequence‑level KL as the sum of per‑timestep KLs evaluated on test prompts (estimated via sampling), and the practical way to achieve a desired KL is empirical calibration (sweep or short pilots) or an adaptive Lagrange‑dual update — there is no single closed‑form map from $\beta$ to KL given the learned reward/policy.
