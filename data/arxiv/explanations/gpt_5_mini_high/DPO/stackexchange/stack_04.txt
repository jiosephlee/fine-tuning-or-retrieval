\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What exactly does the \(\beta\) hyperparameter control in DPO, and how should I pick it to target a KL?
Question:
The paper describes \(\beta\) as controlling the strength of the KL constraint via the mapping \(r(x,y)=\beta\log\tfrac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)\), and it appears in the DPO loss inside the log-ratio. Practically, how does \(\beta\) affect (1) the achieved KL \(\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi\Vert\pi_{\text{ref}}]\), (2) the gradient magnitudes, and (3) the trade-off between exploiting preferences and staying close to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)? Is there a monotonic relationship between \(\beta\) and the realized KL in practice, so that we can tune \(\beta\) to hit a target KL the way PPO targets a KL?
Answer:
Short answer: $\beta$ is the single Lagrange/temperature knob that sets how strongly DPO (and the underlying KL‑constrained RL objective) penalizes deviation from the reference policy. In the RL view $\beta$ multiplies the KL penalty, and in the DPO parameterization it simultaneously rescales the implicit reward and the classification logits used for learning. Increasing $\beta$ makes the fine-tuned policy more conservative (closer to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$); decreasing $\beta$ makes the policy more aggressive (higher expected reward but larger KL). You can therefore tune $\beta$ to move along the reward–KL frontier, but because DPO is trained with a finite model and finite data the mapping $\beta \to$ realized KL is only approximately monotonic in practice, so the recommended way to hit a target KL is a short sweep (or simple binary search) over $\beta$.

Why this is true (equations + intuition). DPO is derived from the KL‑constrained RL objective
$$
\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi}[r(x,y)] - \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi(\cdot\mid x)\Vert\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)] .
$$
The optimal policy for a fixed reward satisfies
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr),
$$
so the factor $1/\beta$ is an inverse temperature: small $\beta$ $\to$ large $1/\beta$ $\to$ a sharp policy that favors high‑reward outputs (larger KL); large $\beta$ $\to$ $1/\beta \to 0$ $\to$ policy tends to the reference (small KL). Re‑writing reward in terms of the policy gives the DPO reparameterization
$$
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x),
$$
and the DPO training loss (maximum likelihood under Bradley–Terry) becomes
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta)= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\Big[ \log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\Big].
$$
The parameter $\beta$ therefore appears twice: as an overall multiplier of the log‑ratio (it scales the implicit reward) and as an overall multiplier outside the DPO gradient. Differentiating gives the learning signal used in the paper:
$$
\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}
= -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}\!\Big[\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\Big],
$$
where $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$.

How this affects (1) KL, (2) gradients, and (3) the exploitation/conservatism trade-off. For (1): in the idealized, exact solution for a fixed reward, increasing $\beta$ always reduces deviation from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (indeed $\pi_r\to\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ as $\beta\to\infty$ and becomes increasingly concentrated on high‑reward outputs as $\beta\to 0$). Thus $\beta$ is the natural handle to move along the reward–KL frontier. In practice, because DPO trains a parametric policy on finite preference data and uses stochastic optimization, the realized KL for a given $\beta$ may not follow a perfectly smooth monotone curve, especially early in training or when model capacity/data are limited; empirically the paper shows sweeping $\beta$ (they used values like $0.05, 0.1, 1, 5$) produces the expected reward–KL frontier and that DPO can efficiently reach different points on it.

For (2): the gradient magnitude has two opposing $\beta$ effects. There is an explicit linear factor $\beta$ outside the expectation, so larger $\beta$ can amplify parameter updates. But $\beta$ also multiplies the log‑ratio inside the sigmoid via $\hat r_\theta$, which changes the sigmoid weight $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))$ between $0$ and $1$. Concretely, for a pair the gradient weight is $\beta\cdot\sigma(-\Delta\hat r)$ with $\Delta\hat r=\hat r_\theta(y_w)-\hat r_\theta(y_l)$. For pairs the model already ranks correctly ($\Delta\hat r\gg 0$) increasing $\beta$ makes $\Delta\hat r$ larger and $\sigma(-\Delta\hat r)\to 0$, so the update for that pair shrinks; for mis‑ranked pairs ($\Delta\hat r<0$) increasing $\beta$ pushes $\sigma(-\Delta\hat r)\to 1$ and the update magnitude scales roughly like $\beta$. Therefore larger $\beta$ magnifies corrections for currently‑wrong examples but suppresses further updates on already‑corrected examples (and can lead to saturation/confidence and small gradients for those). Very small $\beta$ makes the sigmoid argument small, so the weight $\approx 0.5$ and the explicit $\beta$ multiplier keeps the overall updates small — that is, too small $\beta$ can also reduce effective learning signal. So the effect on optimization speed and stability is non‑trivial and depends on the current state of the model.

For (3): trading exploitation vs staying near $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is exactly what $\beta$ controls. Small $\beta$ lets the implicit reward term dominate and the optimized policy will move farther from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to better match preferences (higher reward, larger KL). Large $\beta$ puts more emphasis on the KL term, keeping the policy closer to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and limiting over‑optimization of the (implicit) reward. Intuitively $\beta$ is the Lagrange multiplier that balances these two forces.

Practical guidance to pick $\beta$ if you want to hit a target KL. Because the mapping $\beta \to$ realized KL is predictable in direction but not exact in magnitude for a particular dataset/model/optimization run, the simplest practical approach is a cheap sweep or binary search: run short DPO fine‑tuning runs on a subset (or with fewer steps) with a few $\beta$ values on a log scale (for example the paper’s grid $0.05, 0.1, 1, 5$ is a reasonable starting point), measure the sequence‑level KL (the paper uses the sum of per‑token KLs) and pick or interpolate the $\beta$ that gives the KL closest to your target. Because DPO training is relatively cheap and stable (the authors emphasize it needs little tuning), this sweep is normally faster and more robust than trying to adaptively tune $\beta$ online. If you do wish to adapt $\beta$ during training you can, in principle, monitor the running KL and increase $\beta$ when KL is too large / decrease $\beta$ when KL is too small, but note that this departs from the fixed‑$\beta$ objective analyzed in the paper and requires its own heuristics; the paper does not evaluate adaptive $\beta$ schedules.

A few extra practical points grounded in the paper. First, because $\beta$ changes gradient scaling you may want to adjust learning rates when you change $\beta$ (very large $\beta$ can produce large updates on mis‑ranked pairs). Second, measure KL the same way the paper does (sequence‑level KL) so your target matches how you evaluate. Third, expect diminishing gradients on well‑classified pairs with large $\beta$ (this can help stability near a solution but can also slow fine‑grained improvements), so moderate $\beta$ values often work well as a compromise.

Summary. $\beta$ is the KL‑penalty/temperature that simultaneously (a) controls how strongly the policy is tied to the reference and (b) rescales the implicit reward and DPO learning signal. The direction of its effect on KL is clear ($\beta$ up $\to$ KL down), but because of finite data/model and the nonlinear gradient dependence on $\beta$, the practical way to hit a desired KL is by a short sweep (or binary search) over $\beta$ rather than assuming a guaranteed closed‑form monotonic mapping as is sometimes used in PPO.
