\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How do reward equivalence classes \(r(x,y)\sim r'(x,y)+f(x)\) affect identifiability and the induced optimal policy?
Question:
The paper defines an equivalence relation on rewards that differ by an \(x\)-only term \(f(x)\), and states that (i) Bradley–Terry/Plackett–Luce preferences are invariant to this and (ii) the KL-constrained optimal policy is also invariant. Could someone give a concrete example illustrating why adding \(f(x)\) leaves the optimal policy \(\pi_r\) unchanged? Also, how does this under-specification relate to practical issues like reward normalization used in RLHF (e.g., zero-mean rewards)?
Answer:
Short answer: adding any x-only function $f(x)$ is just a per-context baseline shift of the reward. Bradley–Terry / Plackett–Luce preferences depend only on reward differences, and the KL‑constrained optimum is a softmax over rewards relative to the reference policy, so the common multiplicative factor introduced by $f(x)$ cancels. Concretely, this means the reward learned from preference data is identifiable only up to such an $f(x)$, and common “reward normalization” heuristics (subtracting a mean or baseline) are just choosing a representative from that equivalence class — they do not change the optimal policy, only the numerical baseline used during training.

Why the preferences are invariant (simple algebra). The Bradley–Terry model gives
$$p(y1 \succ y2 \mid x) = \sigma(r(x,y1) - r(x,y2)),$$
so if
$$r^{\prime}(x,y) = r(x,y) + f(x)$$
then
$$r^{\prime}(x,y1) - r^{\prime}(x,y2) = r(x,y1) - r(x,y2),$$
hence $p$ is unchanged. Intuitively: pairwise preference judgments only see differences, so adding the same number to every candidate for a fixed $x$ does nothing.

Concrete numeric illustration. Take $r(x,y1)=2$ and $r(x,y2)=1$ and let $f(x)=-1$. Under Bradley–Terry
$$p(y1 \succ y2 \mid x) = \frac{e^{2}}{e^{2}+e^{1}} = \frac{e}{e+1}.$$
With $r^{\prime} = r + f$ we have $r^{\prime}(x,y1)=1$, $r^{\prime}(x,y2)=0$ and
$$p^{\prime}(y1 \succ y2 \mid x) = \frac{e^{1}}{e^{1}+e^{0}} = \frac{e}{e+1},$$
the same number.

Why the KL‑constrained optimal policy is invariant. The paper shows the optimal policy for reward $r$ is
$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr),$$
where
$$Z(x)=\sum_y\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr).$$
If $r^{\prime}(x,y)=r(x,y)+f(x)$ then
$$\exp\!\bigl(r^{\prime}(x,y)/\beta\bigr)=\exp\!\bigl(f(x)/\beta\bigr)\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr),$$
and the same factor $\exp\!\bigl(f(x)/\beta\bigr)$ appears in every term of $Z^{\prime}(x)$, so it cancels in the ratio. Thus $\pi_{r^{\prime}}=\pi_r$. Intuitively this is the same fact as “adding the same constant to every logit of a softmax does not change the softmax probabilities.”

Concrete policy example (continuing the numbers, with $\beta=1$ and uniform $\pi_{\text{ref}}$): $\pi_r(y1) \propto 0.5\cdot e^{2}$ and $\pi_r(y2) \propto 0.5\cdot e^{1}$, giving the same probabilities as when using $r^{\prime}$ because the common multiplicative factor cancels.

What this means for identifiability and reward normalization in RLHF. Preference data and the Plackett–Luce family identify reward functions only up to an x-only function $f(x)$. In practice people therefore impose an identifiability constraint (for example, subtracting a mean or forcing a per‑context mean of zero) so the learned reward network is unique and numerically stable. Those normalizations are exactly adding an $f(x)$ (or choosing a particular $f(x)$ to subtract), so they do not change the induced preference probabilities or the KL‑constrained optimum policy. The paper formalizes this: Lemma 1 says preferences are invariant under $r \mapsto r + f(x)$, Lemma 2 says the optimal policy is invariant, and Theorem 1 shows you can always pick the representative
$$r(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$$
(up to the additive x-only term coming from the partition function), which is the canonical choice DPO uses.

Practical caveat. Although adding $f(x)$ does not change the theoretical optimal policy, the particular baseline you pick can affect optimization dynamics in finite‑sample / gradient‑based training (variance of policy gradients, critic learning, numerical stability, and the effective learning rates). That is why practitioners still normalize rewards (reduce variance, stabilize actor‑critic updates). DPO sidesteps explicit reward fitting and the need to hand‑tune such baselines because it directly parameterizes the policy and implicitly fixes a canonical baseline (the partition‑function normalization) that makes the mapping between rewards and policy analytic.

Takeaway: baseline shifts $r(x,y) \mapsto r(x,y) + f(x)$ are harmless for both the BT/PL preference model and the KL‑constrained optimal policy — they are an identifiability degree of freedom. Reward normalization in RLHF is simply a choice of that baseline to improve learning dynamics; it does not change the final theoretical policy.
