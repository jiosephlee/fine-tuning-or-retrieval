\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why does the partition function Z(x) cancel when deriving the DPO preference probability?
Question:
In the DPO paper, they start from the optimal policy form for the KL-constrained objective, namely \(\pi_r(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)\). Rearranging gives \(r(x,y)=\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)\), where \(Z(x)\) is the partition function. Plugging this into the Bradley–Terry model \(p(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)=\sigma(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2))\) yields the DPO objective where \(Z(x)\) cancels. I understand that the difference \(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\) removes any \(f(x)\)-type additive terms, but I am confused about the precise conditions: does the cancellation rely only on the two responses sharing the same prompt \(x\)? Do variable-length completions or EOS handling make \(Z(x)\) implicitly depend on length in a way that could fail to cancel? Finally, are there support assumptions (e.g., \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)>0\) for all logged \(y\)) needed so that the log-ratios are well-defined?
Answer:
Short answer: $Z(x)$ cancels because it is an additive constant that depends only on the prompt $x$, and the Bradley–Terry (and Plackett–Luce) preference model depends only on reward differences. Concretely, from the optimal-policy form
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x)\propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr)
$$
you get
$$
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x),
$$
where $Z(x)=\sum_y\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr)$ depends only on $x$. Plugging this into the Bradley–Terry probability
$$
\sigma\bigl(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\bigr)
$$
gives
$$
\sigma\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_1\mid x)}
-\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}
+\beta\log Z(x)-\beta\log Z(x)\Bigr),
$$
and the $\beta\log Z(x)$ terms cancel exactly because they are identical for both $y_1$ and $y_2$.

What this relies on (precise conditions)
- Same prompt $x$: yes — cancellation requires that both completions are compared under the same conditioning context $x$. If you compared completions from different prompts $x$ and $x'$ then the partition functions $Z(x)$ and $Z(x')$ are different and would not cancel.
- Same reference / same $\beta$: the definition of $Z(x)$ uses the same $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)$ and the same temperature $\beta$. If different reference distributions (or different $\beta$) are used for the two items being compared, the additive terms would not match and would not cancel.
- Preference model uses only score differences: Bradley–Terry and Plackett–Luce fall into this family, so the algebra works. A preference model that depended on absolute reward values (not differences) would not let the partition cancel.

Variable-length completions and EOS handling
Autoregressive LMs factorize sequence probabilities by tokens, e.g. $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)=\prod_{t}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})$ with an EOS token marking termination. That only changes how individual sequence probabilities are computed and how $Z(x)=\sum_y\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)$ is formed (the sum implicitly runs over sequences of all lengths). Crucially $Z(x)$ is still a function of $x$ only, so two different-length completions $y_1,y_2$ for the same prompt share the same $Z(x)$ and it still cancels. The only way length/EOS would break cancellation is if the two completions were produced or interpreted under different conditioning conventions or different reference models (for example, if one completion were treated as a truncated prefix with a different normalization than the other). In practice the paper avoids this by using the same reference/SFT model and the same definition of a complete sequence for every pair.

Support / finiteness assumptions
To form the log-ratio $\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ you need the denominator $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)$ to be positive for any logged $y$ in your dataset (otherwise the log is $-\infty$ and the reparametrization breaks). You also need $Z(x)$ finite so $\pi_r$ is a valid distribution — this is the “mild assumptions” the paper refers to. In practice autoregressive softmax LMs assign strictly positive mass to every finite token sequence (within the vocabulary), so these conditions are normally satisfied; when the original SFT that produced the logged data is unavailable the paper explicitly sets $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to a model trained to give mass to the observed preferred completions to avoid support mismatch.

Summary
Algebraically the partition cancels because it is an additive per-prompt constant and the BT model depends only on reward differences. That cancellation is robust to variable sequence length and EOS handling as long as both outputs are completions for the same prompt and both are in the support of the same reference distribution; if those conditions fail (different prompts, different references, zero support) the cancellation no longer holds and the derivation breaks.

### How is the closed-form optimal policy for the KL-constrained objective derived in the DPO paper?
Question:
The paper states that the solution to maximizing \(\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[r(x,y)]-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi(\cdot\mid x)\Vert\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)]\) has the Boltzmann form \(\pi_r(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)\). Could someone walk through the derivation step by step for the discrete sequence case? In particular: (1) how is the Lagrangian set up for the constraint that \(\sum_y \pi(y\mid x)=1\), (2) how does one take functional derivatives to obtain the exponential form, and (3) is the KL the sequence-level KL or a per-token KL, and does that choice matter for the derivation?
Answer:
Short answer: you set up a Lagrangian for the finite-dimensional probability vector $\pi(\cdot\mid x)$, take ordinary partial derivatives (not a fancy calculus of variations step for discrete $y$), use the identity $d(\pi\log\pi)/d\pi = \log\pi + 1$, and solve. The stationarity condition gives the Gibbs/Boltzmann reweighting
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x)\;=\;\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr)}{Z(x)},
\qquad Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr).
$$

Below are the steps and a few clarifying remarks that connect this to the paper.

Derivation (discrete sequences). Fix a prompt $x$ and write $\pi(y)$ for $\pi(y\mid x)$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)$ for $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)$. The objective to maximize (for this $x$) is
$$
J(\pi)=\sum_y \pi(y)\,r(x,y)\;-\;\beta\sum_y \pi(y)\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)},
$$
subject to the normalization constraint $\sum_y\pi(y)=1$ (and $\pi(y)\ge 0$). Introduce a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ for the normalization and form the Lagrangian
$$
\mathcal{L}(\pi,\lambda)
=\sum_y\pi(y)r(x,y)-\beta\sum_y\pi(y)\Big(\log\pi(y)-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\Big)
+\lambda\Big(\sum_y\pi(y)-1\Big).
$$
Differentiate with respect to each coordinate $\pi(y)$. For the entropy-like term use the standard derivative $d(\pi\log\pi)/d\pi = \log\pi + 1$. Thus the stationarity condition $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\pi(y)=0$ gives
$$
r(x,y)-\beta\big(\log\pi(y)+1\big)+\beta\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)+\lambda=0.
$$
Rearrange to isolate $\log\pi(y)$:
$$
\log\pi(y)=\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)+\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)+\frac{\lambda}{\beta}-1.
$$
Exponentiating and absorbing the constant $\exp\!\big(\frac{\lambda}{\beta}-1\big)$ into a normalizer yields exactly
$$
\pi(y)=\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\,\exp\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr)}{Z(x)},
\qquad Z(x)=\sum_y\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\exp\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr).
$$
This is the Boltzmann form quoted in the paper. One can also rearrange that equation to express the reward in terms of the optimal policy
$$
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x),
$$
which is the change-of-variables used in DPO (the additive $\beta\log Z(x)$ depends only on $x$ and cancels out when taking reward differences, which is why the Bradley–Terry model only needs the difference of rewards).

About “functional derivatives” vs. ordinary derivatives. For a discrete set of $y$ you are optimizing a finite-dimensional vector $\pi$ and you use ordinary partial derivatives as above (plus KKT conditions to handle boundaries). In continuous action spaces or infinite-dimensional settings one would use functional derivatives / calculus of variations and obtain the same exponential Radon–Nikodym form (the Gibbs family)—the underlying math is analogous.

Which KL is used and does it matter? The paper’s ELBO-like objective uses the KL between the full sequence distributions, i.e. $\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi(\cdot\mid x)\Vert\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr]$ where $y$ denotes the whole generated sequence. That is exactly the KL that appears in Eq. (3) of the paper and in the derivation above. For autoregressive language models the sequence-level KL factorizes as an expected sum of per-token conditional log-ratios:
$$
\mathrm{KL}\bigl(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\Vert\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr)
= \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}\Big[\sum_t \log\frac{\pi(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}\Big],
$$
so in practice people often compute a “sequence-level KL” by summing per-token KLs. The closed-form Boltzmann solution above treats entire completions $y$ as atomic objects; it remains valid whether $y$ is a whole sequence or a single-token action. If instead you change the constraint from a single sequence-level KL to many separate per-prefix (per-token-state) KL constraints enforced independently at every prefix, the optimization problem changes and the optimal policy will satisfy a local per-prefix Boltzmann relation (this is the usual soft Q / control-as-inference view where the per-step conditionals depend on soft value terms). But that is a different formulation; the DPO paper uses the sequence-level KL (and in experiments measures sequence-level KL as the sum of per-step KLs).

Practical caveats. The derivation assumes $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)>0$ on the support considered (otherwise the ratio is ill-defined). In practice, autoregressive LMs normally give nonzero mass to all finite sequences valid under the tokenizer, so this is not an issue. Also the normalization constant $Z(x)$ can be intractable to compute for very large discrete sequence spaces, which is why DPO uses the algebraic inversion (expressing $r$ in terms of $\pi$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$) and the Bradley–Terry preference model where $Z$ cancels out from pairwise comparisons.

Intuition. The KL penalty acts like an entropic regularizer that prevents drastic deviation from the reference policy; the optimizer therefore reweights the reference by an exponential factor of the reward, producing the familiar Gibbs/Boltzmann distribution that trades off reward (energy) against closeness to the reference (prior). This is the same structure that appears in maximum-entropy RL and control-as-inference frameworks, and is precisely what DPO leverages when it inverts the mapping to express rewards in terms of policy log-ratios.

### In DPO, is log \(\pi(y\mid x)\) computed over entire sequences or per-token, and how is length bias handled?
Question:
The DPO loss uses terms like \(\log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}\). For an autoregressive LM, this sequence probability is a product over tokens, so the log-probability is a sum over time steps. Does the implementation use the total sequence log-probability or a length-normalized average (e.g., per-token)? If it is the total, how does DPO avoid a bias toward shorter responses, since longer sequences accumulate more negative log-probability? If it is normalized, where is that stated and how is EOS handled in the log-prob calculation?
Answer:
Short answer: DPO uses full sequence probabilities (i.e. the usual autoregressive sequence log-prob, a sum of per-token log-probs) — not a length-normalized average. The paper’s equations and implementation notes treat $\pi(y\mid x)$ as the probability of the whole sampled completion $y$, and the experimental KL is explicitly the sequence-level KL (the sum of per-timestep KLs). EOS (or whatever termination token is part of the sampled completion) is therefore included in that sequence probability.

Why that is the case (intuitively and technically). The DPO objective is written in terms of the sequence-level ratio
$$\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$$
and the loss
$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\Big[\log\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\big)\Big].$$
For an autoregressive LM the sequence log-prob is
$$\log\pi(y\mid x)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\pi(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}),$$
so the DPO reward is the sum over token-level log‑prob differences between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. The paper’s gradient formula also uses $\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y\mid x)$, which is the usual sum of per-token gradients under teacher‑forcing.

Why this does not collapse to “always prefer the shortest sequence” in practice. Two points are important. First, DPO is a pairwise method: it compares the relative log‑prob ratios of two completions, so constant length-dependent factors that affect both $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ in the same way tend to cancel in the difference $\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)$. Second, the reference model acts as a baseline / regularizer: DPO does not maximize raw sequence probability but the ratio to the reference, and the algorithm’s effective conservativeness is controlled by $\beta$. This is the same KL-constrained objective that RLHF methods use, reparameterized; that constraint and the per-example weighting in the DPO gradient (the $\sigma(\cdot)$ term) prevent trivial degeneracies (the paper shows that a naive unweighted ratio objective can cause degeneration). In short, DPO inherits whatever length tendencies the reference and label data imply, but it has no implicit “shorter-is-better” objective beyond the usual fact that shorter sequences can have higher raw likelihoods — that effect is moderated by the reference baseline and the pairwise form of the loss.

About EOS and practical computation. The paper treats a sampled completion $y$ as the full sequence produced by the sampling procedure, so you compute $\log\pi(y\mid x)$ by summing the per-token log-probs up to the sequence termination (EOS or truncation). The experimental note that they report “sequence-level KL (that is, the sum of the per-timestep KL‑divergences)” confirms this implementation choice.

If you explicitly want a length‑normalized objective (e.g. average log-prob per token) you would be changing the reward parameterization: dividing $\log\pi(y\mid x)$ by length is not equivalent to the reparameterization the paper uses and would require a different derivation and would alter the theoretical guarantees (the DPO derivation and Theorem 1 rely on the sequence‑level log‑prob reparameterization). The paper does not make that modification.

### What exactly does the \(\beta\) hyperparameter control in DPO, and how should I pick it to target a KL?
Question:
The paper describes \(\beta\) as controlling the strength of the KL constraint via the mapping \(r(x,y)=\beta\log\tfrac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)\), and it appears in the DPO loss inside the log-ratio. Practically, how does \(\beta\) affect (1) the achieved KL \(\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi\Vert\pi_{\text{ref}}]\), (2) the gradient magnitudes, and (3) the trade-off between exploiting preferences and staying close to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)? Is there a monotonic relationship between \(\beta\) and the realized KL in practice, so that we can tune \(\beta\) to hit a target KL the way PPO targets a KL?
Answer:
Short answer: $\beta$ is the single Lagrange/temperature knob that sets how strongly DPO (and the underlying KL‑constrained RL objective) penalizes deviation from the reference policy. In the RL view $\beta$ multiplies the KL penalty, and in the DPO parameterization it simultaneously rescales the implicit reward and the classification logits used for learning. Increasing $\beta$ makes the fine-tuned policy more conservative (closer to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$); decreasing $\beta$ makes the policy more aggressive (higher expected reward but larger KL). You can therefore tune $\beta$ to move along the reward–KL frontier, but because DPO is trained with a finite model and finite data the mapping $\beta \to$ realized KL is only approximately monotonic in practice, so the recommended way to hit a target KL is a short sweep (or simple binary search) over $\beta$.

Why this is true (equations + intuition). DPO is derived from the KL‑constrained RL objective
$$
\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi}[r(x,y)] - \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi(\cdot\mid x)\Vert\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)] .
$$
The optimal policy for a fixed reward satisfies
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr),
$$
so the factor $1/\beta$ is an inverse temperature: small $\beta$ $\to$ large $1/\beta$ $\to$ a sharp policy that favors high‑reward outputs (larger KL); large $\beta$ $\to$ $1/\beta \to 0$ $\to$ policy tends to the reference (small KL). Re‑writing reward in terms of the policy gives the DPO reparameterization
$$
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x),
$$
and the DPO training loss (maximum likelihood under Bradley–Terry) becomes
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta)= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\Big[ \log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\Big].
$$
The parameter $\beta$ therefore appears twice: as an overall multiplier of the log‑ratio (it scales the implicit reward) and as an overall multiplier outside the DPO gradient. Differentiating gives the learning signal used in the paper:
$$
\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}
= -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}\!\Big[\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\Big],
$$
where $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$.

How this affects (1) KL, (2) gradients, and (3) the exploitation/conservatism trade-off. For (1): in the idealized, exact solution for a fixed reward, increasing $\beta$ always reduces deviation from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (indeed $\pi_r\to\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ as $\beta\to\infty$ and becomes increasingly concentrated on high‑reward outputs as $\beta\to 0$). Thus $\beta$ is the natural handle to move along the reward–KL frontier. In practice, because DPO trains a parametric policy on finite preference data and uses stochastic optimization, the realized KL for a given $\beta$ may not follow a perfectly smooth monotone curve, especially early in training or when model capacity/data are limited; empirically the paper shows sweeping $\beta$ (they used values like $0.05, 0.1, 1, 5$) produces the expected reward–KL frontier and that DPO can efficiently reach different points on it.

For (2): the gradient magnitude has two opposing $\beta$ effects. There is an explicit linear factor $\beta$ outside the expectation, so larger $\beta$ can amplify parameter updates. But $\beta$ also multiplies the log‑ratio inside the sigmoid via $\hat r_\theta$, which changes the sigmoid weight $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))$ between $0$ and $1$. Concretely, for a pair the gradient weight is $\beta\cdot\sigma(-\Delta\hat r)$ with $\Delta\hat r=\hat r_\theta(y_w)-\hat r_\theta(y_l)$. For pairs the model already ranks correctly ($\Delta\hat r\gg 0$) increasing $\beta$ makes $\Delta\hat r$ larger and $\sigma(-\Delta\hat r)\to 0$, so the update for that pair shrinks; for mis‑ranked pairs ($\Delta\hat r<0$) increasing $\beta$ pushes $\sigma(-\Delta\hat r)\to 1$ and the update magnitude scales roughly like $\beta$. Therefore larger $\beta$ magnifies corrections for currently‑wrong examples but suppresses further updates on already‑corrected examples (and can lead to saturation/confidence and small gradients for those). Very small $\beta$ makes the sigmoid argument small, so the weight $\approx 0.5$ and the explicit $\beta$ multiplier keeps the overall updates small — that is, too small $\beta$ can also reduce effective learning signal. So the effect on optimization speed and stability is non‑trivial and depends on the current state of the model.

For (3): trading exploitation vs staying near $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is exactly what $\beta$ controls. Small $\beta$ lets the implicit reward term dominate and the optimized policy will move farther from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to better match preferences (higher reward, larger KL). Large $\beta$ puts more emphasis on the KL term, keeping the policy closer to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and limiting over‑optimization of the (implicit) reward. Intuitively $\beta$ is the Lagrange multiplier that balances these two forces.

Practical guidance to pick $\beta$ if you want to hit a target KL. Because the mapping $\beta \to$ realized KL is predictable in direction but not exact in magnitude for a particular dataset/model/optimization run, the simplest practical approach is a cheap sweep or binary search: run short DPO fine‑tuning runs on a subset (or with fewer steps) with a few $\beta$ values on a log scale (for example the paper’s grid $0.05, 0.1, 1, 5$ is a reasonable starting point), measure the sequence‑level KL (the paper uses the sum of per‑token KLs) and pick or interpolate the $\beta$ that gives the KL closest to your target. Because DPO training is relatively cheap and stable (the authors emphasize it needs little tuning), this sweep is normally faster and more robust than trying to adaptively tune $\beta$ online. If you do wish to adapt $\beta$ during training you can, in principle, monitor the running KL and increase $\beta$ when KL is too large / decrease $\beta$ when KL is too small, but note that this departs from the fixed‑$\beta$ objective analyzed in the paper and requires its own heuristics; the paper does not evaluate adaptive $\beta$ schedules.

A few extra practical points grounded in the paper. First, because $\beta$ changes gradient scaling you may want to adjust learning rates when you change $\beta$ (very large $\beta$ can produce large updates on mis‑ranked pairs). Second, measure KL the same way the paper does (sequence‑level KL) so your target matches how you evaluate. Third, expect diminishing gradients on well‑classified pairs with large $\beta$ (this can help stability near a solution but can also slow fine‑grained improvements), so moderate $\beta$ values often work well as a compromise.

Summary. $\beta$ is the KL‑penalty/temperature that simultaneously (a) controls how strongly the policy is tied to the reference and (b) rescales the implicit reward and DPO learning signal. The direction of its effect on KL is clear ($\beta$ up $\to$ KL down), but because of finite data/model and the nonlinear gradient dependence on $\beta$, the practical way to hit a desired KL is by a short sweep (or binary search) over $\beta$ rather than assuming a guaranteed closed‑form monotonic mapping as is sometimes used in PPO.

### Why does the DPO gradient include a sigmoid weighting term, and what breaks if we omit it (i.e., use naive unlikelihood)?
Question:
The gradient sketch shows a weight of \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))\) multiplying the difference of score functions \(\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l\mid x)\). Intuitively this upweights pairs the model currently misorders. What precisely goes wrong if we drop the sigmoid weight and simply push up \(\log\pi(y_w\mid x)\) and push down \(\log\pi(y_l\mid x)\) (unlikelihood)? Can someone give a simple example or argument showing how the unweighted objective encourages degeneration (e.g., collapsing to short answers or suppressing diversity) that the DPO weighting avoids?
Answer:
Short answer: the sigmoid weight is essential because DPO is the gradient of a proper Bradley–Terry (logistic) likelihood under the reparameterization $r = \beta \log\left(\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\right)$; that logistic factor makes the update focus on the “hard” (misordered) pairs and causes the step size to vanish as a pair becomes confidently correct. Dropping it turns the update into an unbounded, constant-magnitude push that keeps driving probabilities to extremes (reducing entropy and diversity, often collapsing to short/high‑probability answers). The paper observed exactly this degeneration in the naive unlikelihood variant (see Appendix).

Why that sigmoid appears. The DPO loss is
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\Big[\log\sigma\big(\hat r(y_w)-\hat r(y_l)\big)\Big],
\qquad
\hat r(y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)},
$$
so its gradient (paper Eqn.) is
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}
= -\beta\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\sigma\big(\hat r(y_l)-\hat r(y_w)\big)\,\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\Big].
$$
The multiplicative factor $\sigma(\hat r(y_l)-\hat r(y_w))=\sigma(-\Delta)$ (where $\Delta=\hat r(y_w)-\hat r(y_l)$) is near 1 when the model currently misorders the pair ($\Delta$ negative) and near 0 when the model already strongly prefers the correct answer ($\Delta$ large positive). In other words, DPO focuses learning on hard/misordered examples and automatically attenuates updates for already-correct pairs.

What you get if you drop it (the “naive unlikelihood”). Removing the sigmoid produces the update
$$
\nabla_\theta \widetilde{\mathcal L}
= -\beta\,\mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big],
$$
which is exactly the gradient of the simple surrogate objective
$$
\widetilde{\mathcal L} = -\beta\,\mathbb{E}\big[\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big].
$$
This objective has two problematic properties. First, it is unboundedly improved by pushing the preferred answers’ probabilities to 1 and the dispreferred ones to 0; even a single pair admits the trivial “collapse” solution $\pi(y_w\mid x)\to 1,\ \pi(y_l\mid x)\to 0$. Second, the gradient magnitude does not decay as the pair becomes already well ordered, so SGD will continue to exert pressure to make the margin arbitrarily large. Those two facts together encourage the model to drive probability mass away from large parts of the support, and because parameters are shared across many contexts the mass ends up concentrated on a small set of outputs that are easiest to upweight (in practice this often correlates with short, high‑prior responses), i.e. reduced diversity and mode collapse.

A tiny concrete illustration. For a single comparison (one prompt, two completions), $\widetilde{\mathcal L}=\log \frac{\pi(y_l)}{\pi(y_w)}$. Minimizing this pushes $\pi(y_w)\to1$ and $\pi(y_l)\to0$ — nothing prevents this, and the gradient magnitude stays large until the extremes. By contrast DPO’s loss $-\log\sigma(\hat r(y_w)-\hat r(y_l))$ saturates: once $\hat r(y_w)$ is sufficiently bigger than $\hat r(y_l)$ the sigmoid term goes to 0 and further pushes vanish, so the model stops changing that pair and does not keep draining probability from other answers.

How this ties to the intended RL/KL view. DPO is derived from the KL‑constrained reward maximization and is the MLE under the Bradley–Terry reparameterization $r=\beta\log\left(\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\right)$. The sigmoid weight is therefore not an ad‑hoc stabilizer but the derivative of that likelihood; it implicitly enforces the soft margin and preserves the “don’t move if already correct / don’t move too far from the reference” behavior that the original constrained-RL objective gives you. The naive unlikelihood objective severs this link to a probabilistic preference model and to the implicit KL regularizer, so it can (and empirically does, per the paper’s Appendix) push the model to degenerate solutions.

Practical takeaway: the sigmoid weight is the mechanism that (a) focuses learning on the mistakes the model still makes and (b) causes updates to fade as pairs become correct, thereby preventing runaway suppression of many plausible generations and the resulting collapse to short or repetitive answers. Omitting it removes that protection and encourages the degeneration you observed.

### Is DPO truly equivalent to PPO-based RLHF on the same Bradley–Terry objective, or only in the limit of infinite data?
Question:
The paper claims DPO "implicitly optimizes the same objective as existing RLHF algorithms (reward maximization with a KL-divergence constraint)." However, DPO trains on a fixed offline set of pairs, while PPO learns on-policy with respect to the evolving \(\pi_\theta\). Under what conditions do the solutions of DPO and PPO coincide? Is the equivalence exact only at the population level (infinite preference data drawn from the optimal policy’s preference distribution), or does it hold for finite datasets? How does off-distribution bias from the fixed dataset affect the equivalence?
Answer:
Short answer: DPO is exactly equivalent to the KL‑constrained RLHF objective only in the population / well‑specified limit. Concretely, if (1) human preferences follow the Bradley–Terry (or more generally Plackett–Luce) model induced by some latent reward $r^*$, (2) the offline pairwise dataset is effectively the population distribution (infinite data or a consistent estimator), (3) the reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and KL weight $\beta$ used by DPO are the same as in the RL formulation, and (4) your parametric family has enough capacity and optimization is perfect, then the DPO MLE recovers the same optimal policy that the KL‑constrained RL problem (the PPO target) admits. In finite data or under model/misspecification/support mismatch the equivalence is only approximate and practical differences (including off‑distribution bias) matter.

Why the population equivalence holds (the algebraic intuition). For the constrained RL problem the optimal policy for a reward $r$ takes the softmax form
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x)\propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr).
$$
Rearranging gives a representation of any reward (up to an additive function of $x$) in terms of the optimal policy:
$$
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\text{const}(x).
$$
Because Bradley–Terry preferences depend only on reward differences, the additive constant cancels and the pairwise preference probability can be written purely as a function of the optimal policy:
$$
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)
=\sigma\Bigl(\beta\bigl[\log\frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_1\mid x)}
-\log\frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}\bigr]\Bigr).
$$
DPO simply treats this as a likelihood for $\pi_\theta$ and fits $\pi_\theta$ by maximum likelihood on the preference pairs. In the population limit (infinite data, correct BT model, perfect optimization and sufficient model class) the MLE $\pi_\theta$ equals $\pi^*$, the unique policy that maximizes the original RL objective for the corresponding reward class. The paper’s Lemmas and Theorem formalize this: the BT family is under‑identified up to an additive function of $x$, but any reward in the same equivalence class yields the same preferences and same optimal policy, and every such class has a representative of the DPO form.

Where finite‑data / practical deviations appear. In practice we never have infinite data or perfect optimization, so the two procedures are different estimators of the same underlying object:

- DPO is a single‑stage estimator that directly fits a policy $\pi_\theta$ to maximize the likelihood of observed pairwise labels under the BT reparameterization (Eq. 8 of the paper). Its statistical error is the usual MLE finite‑sample error: bias/variance from limited labeled pairs and model misspecification.

- PPO (or RLHF with a learned reward) is a two‑stage procedure: first fit a reward model $r_\phi$ to the pairs, then use RL (PPO) to maximize expected reward minus $\beta$ KL. Finite‑sample errors occur both in reward estimation and in the RL optimization (actor/critic variance, imperfect convergence). Those two approximation steps create different finite‑sample bias than DPO’s single MLE step.

Because of these different error pathways, finite datasets generally yield different learned policies. Empirically (and as argued in the paper) DPO often wins in stability and sample efficiency because it avoids the extra reward‑fitting + RL loop and the high variance/value‑function issues that actor‑critic methods face.

Off‑distribution bias / support mismatch — what to watch for. In realistic RLHF data the labeled pairs are collected by sampling completions from some fixed policy (typically the SFT model), so the dataset only covers outputs in the support of that sampling policy. That induces two important practical effects:

1) Lack of information off the dataset support. Neither the reward model nor DPO has labeled information about completions that were never sampled. If either algorithm (PPO) moves the policy into regions not represented in the dataset, the reward model’s extrapolation can be wrong; PPO, by sampling on‑policy, is more likely to encounter and exploit such extrapolation errors unless the KL term strongly constrains it. DPO, being trained offline on the fixed pairs, will not generate new labels by itself and so cannot correct for reward exaggeration outside the dataset support, but it also cannot “discover” and then overfit to spurious high rewards off‑support.

2) Implicit regularization via $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. DPO’s objective explicitly contains the log ratio to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (through the reparameterization), so the reference distribution appears in the loss and acts like a built‑in regularizer toward the sampling policy. That helps keep the learned policy near the distribution where labels exist, but it does not magically add information about unseen outputs. The paper suggests initializing $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to the SFT model that generated the pairs (or fitting $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to preferred completions when SFT is unavailable) to reduce distributional mismatch.

Practical upshot / guidance. The formal equivalence is an asymptotic statement: if your preference model is correctly specified (Bradley–Terry), you have enough labeled pairs, and you can optimize perfectly, DPO and the solution of the KL‑constrained RL objective coincide. With realistic finite datasets, they are different estimators: DPO tends to be simpler, more stable and often more sample efficient because it avoids reward‑fitting and RL instability (the paper’s experiments show this), but it is also inherently offline and limited by the support of the labeled pairs. If you plan to collect labels online, explore beyond the SFT support, or need to actively acquire new comparisons in regions the SFT never covered, an on‑policy loop (PPO + relabeling) can be necessary; conversely, if you have a good offline preference dataset sampled from a known reference and want a stable, cheap procedure, DPO is often the better choice.

In short: exact equivalence = population / well‑specified limit (the paper’s algebraic results); finite datasets, model error, support mismatch and optimization approximations break exact equivalence and determine practical differences (and why the paper emphasizes how DPO avoids the instabilities of actor‑critic RL in practice).

### How does DPO handle reference-policy mismatch when preference data were generated by a different model?
Question:
In practice, the paper suggests initializing \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) by maximizing the likelihood of the preferred responses \(y_w\) if the original SFT model used to collect pairs is unavailable. Why train \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) only on \(y_w\) and not also on \(y_l\)? Does this choice introduce a bias in the implicit reward \(\hat r_\theta=\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\) that could inflate the reward scale? Are there diagnostics to tell if this reference-policy retrofit sufficiently mitigates distribution shift, or should we also include negatives or regularize toward a base LM?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper’s recommendation to fit the missing reference policy by maximum likelihood on the *preferred* completions $y_w$ is a pragmatic fix to a support / numerical-stability problem, not a claim that it is the unique “correct” reference. Theoretically the Bradley–Terry preference model (and Theorem 1 in the paper) says only reward *differences* matter, so changing the additive baseline does not change the underlying preferences or the representable optimal policy in the infinite-data limit. In practice, however, a badly chosen $\pi_{ref}$ (e.g. one that assigns near-zero probability to dataset answers) will distort the numeric values of the implicit reward
$$\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$$
and can cause instability or saturation in training. Fitting $\pi_{ref}$ on $y_w$ is a simple way to guarantee support on the favored outputs and to avoid extreme denominators; it is a heuristic that trades matching the original sampler for numerical and behavioural safety.

Why winners-only makes sense (and what it does to $\hat r$): DPO relies on the fact that the preference model depends only on reward *differences*. From the DPO reparameterization we have for any pair
$$\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)
= \beta\Big(\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}
- \log\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big).$$
So the reference enters as a *shift* of the pairwise log-odds. If the true SFT that generated the pairs is unavailable, and you pick a $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ that gives tiny mass to winners, the subtraction term can be large negative and the logistic inputs will be huge, causing saturation and unstable / overly strong gradients. Training $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ on $y_w$ increases $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)$ (relative to alternatives) and therefore tends to *reduce* the magnitude of that subtraction term, making the implicit reward differences more moderate and the optimization more stable. That is likely why the authors recommend the winners-only MLE as a retrofit in practice.

Does that introduce bias or “inflate” rewards? In the population (infinite-data) sense it does not change which policies are compatible with the observed pairwise preferences because adding any function $f(x)$ to $r(x,y)$ cancels in pairwise differences (Lemma 1 / Theorem 1). In finite-data, though, the choice of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ affects the numerical values of $\hat r$ and thus the optimization dynamics and the learned policy: fitting $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ on winners will bias the KL anchor toward higher-quality outputs (which is usually desirable), whereas fitting $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ to losers or to a very different model could either weaken the KL prior or create extreme log-ratios that disrupt training. So it’s not a theoretical bias in identifiability, but it is a finite-sample / optimization bias that you should be aware of.

Practical diagnostics and checks you can run
1. Check support and likelihoods: compute log-probabilities $\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)$ and $\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)$ on your dataset. If many preferred examples have extremely low log-probability under $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, you have a support mismatch and the retrofit is necessary (or you should change the fit).
2. Inspect the DPO training signal: histogram the per-pair input to the sigmoid,
$\Delta(x):=\beta\big(\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_\theta(y_l)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}\big)$.
If $\Delta$ clusters at huge magnitudes (so $\sigma(\Delta)$ is near 0 or 1), the objective is saturated and learning will be either tiny or unstable. That indicates a bad choice of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ or an inappropriate $\beta$.
3. Monitor KL and performance frontiers: sweep $\beta$ and measure achieved reward vs sequence-level $KL(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})$. If the frontier looks pathological (very high reward at huge KL), that suggests reference mismatch / over-optimistic scaling.
4. Ablate reference fits: compare training $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ on winners-only vs winners+losers vs the original base LM (or a mixture). If policies / evaluation metrics differ substantially, that signals sensitivity.

When you might want to include negatives or regularize toward a base LM
If you have strong reason to recover the original SFT sampling distribution (e.g., you want $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ to reflect exactly the generator used to produce the labeled pairs), then fitting $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ on both $y_w$ and $y_l$ (or on a larger set of samples from the original generator) is sensible. But if the original SFT is low-quality, including losers will make the KL anchor keep the policy closer to undesirable outputs. A middle ground is to train $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ on winners but regularize it toward the base pretrained LM (or make $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ a mixture of the winner-finetuned model and the original base LM). Regularization prevents overfitting to winners (so the anchor remains broad) and reduces the risk that $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ collapses and removes any meaningful KL constraint.

Other practical knobs to control scale and bias: tune $\beta$ (smaller $\beta$ reduces the absolute magnitude of $\hat r$), weight or downsample $y_l$ if you include them when fitting $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, or add a small temperature/smoothing to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ logits so no dataset completion has vanishing probability. Finally, always validate with held-out preference judgments or human/GPT-4 evaluations rather than relying only on implicit reward magnitudes.

Summary recommendation: use the winners-only MLE for $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ as the paper does if the original SFT is unavailable—it’s a pragmatic way to ensure support on desirable outputs and avoid denominator-explosion. But treat this as a heuristic: run the simple diagnostics above (log-probs, $\Delta$ histograms, reward–KL frontiers), tune $\beta$, and if you see sensitivity, try fitting $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ on a broader set (or mix with the base LM) rather than blindly trusting winners-only.

### What assumptions are needed for the theorem that any reward class can be written as \(\beta\log \pi/\pi_{\text{ref}}\)?
Question:
Theorem 1 asserts that, under mild assumptions, every reward function class (modulo an \(f(x)\) baseline) consistent with Bradley–Terry/Plackett–Luce can be represented as \(r(x,y)=\beta\log\tfrac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\) for some \(\pi\). What are the precise assumptions? For example, do we require (1) \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)>0\) on the support of the optimal policy, (2) a finite action space (finite set of sequences) or at least countable support, and (3) measurability or boundedness of \(r\)? Also, does the result depend on the reward being only identifiable up to an additive \(f(x)\) term?
Answer:
Short answer: The “mild assumptions” in Theorem 1 are exactly the ordinary integrability / absolute‑continuity hypotheses needed to make the Gibbs (Boltzmann) reweighting well‑defined. Concretely, for each context $x$ you must have a reference probability $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)$ and a temperature $\beta>0$, and the reward $r(x,\cdot)$ must be measurable and such that the partition function
$$
Z(x)\;=\;\sum_{y}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Big)
$$
(or the analogous integral if the action space is uncountable) is finite and strictly positive. Under these conditions the construction in the paper is valid: define
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x)=\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\big(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\big)}{Z(x)},
$$
and the projected reward
$$
f(r)(x,y)=r(x,y)-\beta\log Z(x)
$$
satisfies
$$
f(r)(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
$$
This is exactly the statement of Theorem 1: every reward equivalence class (where two rewards are equivalent iff they differ by an additive function of $x$) has a representative of the form $\beta\log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}$.

A few clarifying remarks addressing your numbered items and common edge cases.

1. Support / positivity of $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. The representation uses the ratio $\pi(y\mid x)/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)$, so you need absolute continuity of the target policy $\pi$ w.r.t.\ $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$: whenever $\pi(y\mid x)>0$ you must have $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)>0$. If $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)=0$ but the desired $\pi$ places positive mass there, no finite $r$ will produce that $\pi$ via the Gibbs reweighting. In practice for language models $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (an SFT LM) usually has full support over finite sequences so this condition holds.

2. Finite / countable action space vs. general measurable space. The paper writes sums $\sum_y$ and therefore is clearest in the finite or countable case. The same statement extends to general measurable action spaces if you replace sums by integrals and require measurability and integrability: the key technical requirement is that $\exp\big(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\big)$ be integrable w.r.t.\ $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)$ so that $Z(x)\in(0,\infty)$. Thus finite or countable $Y$ makes everything trivial; uncountable $Y$ is allowed but you must check the integrability/measure conditions.

3. Measurability / boundedness of $r$. Measurability (so the sums/integrals make sense) and the integrability condition above are what matter. Boundedness of $r$ is a sufficient but stronger-than-necessary condition that guarantees integrability. The theorem does not require boundedness per se, only that $Z(x)$ be finite.

Finally, yes — the result depends on the well‑known identifiability up to an additive baseline $f(x)$. The Plackett–Luce / Bradley–Terry preference model depends only on reward differences, so rewards are only identified modulo additions of functions of $x$. Theorem 1 is precisely about equivalence classes under that relation: it says each equivalence class contains a representative of the log‑ratio form $\beta\log(\pi/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$ (the representative is obtained by subtracting the $\beta\log Z(x)$ partition term).

Intuition / connection: the theorem is nothing mystical — it is the statement that any policy $\pi$ absolutely continuous w.r.t.\ a reference $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ can be written as a Gibbs reweighting of $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, and the corresponding reward is just $\beta$ times the log density ratio (plus an $x$-dependent constant). The “mild assumptions” are exactly those that make that Gibbs transform well‑defined (positivity of $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ on the required support and finiteness of the partition function).

### How do reward equivalence classes \(r(x,y)\sim r'(x,y)+f(x)\) affect identifiability and the induced optimal policy?
Question:
The paper defines an equivalence relation on rewards that differ by an \(x\)-only term \(f(x)\), and states that (i) Bradley–Terry/Plackett–Luce preferences are invariant to this and (ii) the KL-constrained optimal policy is also invariant. Could someone give a concrete example illustrating why adding \(f(x)\) leaves the optimal policy \(\pi_r\) unchanged? Also, how does this under-specification relate to practical issues like reward normalization used in RLHF (e.g., zero-mean rewards)?
Answer:
Short answer: adding any x-only function $f(x)$ is just a per-context baseline shift of the reward. Bradley–Terry / Plackett–Luce preferences depend only on reward differences, and the KL‑constrained optimum is a softmax over rewards relative to the reference policy, so the common multiplicative factor introduced by $f(x)$ cancels. Concretely, this means the reward learned from preference data is identifiable only up to such an $f(x)$, and common “reward normalization” heuristics (subtracting a mean or baseline) are just choosing a representative from that equivalence class — they do not change the optimal policy, only the numerical baseline used during training.

Why the preferences are invariant (simple algebra). The Bradley–Terry model gives
$$p(y1 \succ y2 \mid x) = \sigma(r(x,y1) - r(x,y2)),$$
so if
$$r^{\prime}(x,y) = r(x,y) + f(x)$$
then
$$r^{\prime}(x,y1) - r^{\prime}(x,y2) = r(x,y1) - r(x,y2),$$
hence $p$ is unchanged. Intuitively: pairwise preference judgments only see differences, so adding the same number to every candidate for a fixed $x$ does nothing.

Concrete numeric illustration. Take $r(x,y1)=2$ and $r(x,y2)=1$ and let $f(x)=-1$. Under Bradley–Terry
$$p(y1 \succ y2 \mid x) = \frac{e^{2}}{e^{2}+e^{1}} = \frac{e}{e+1}.$$
With $r^{\prime} = r + f$ we have $r^{\prime}(x,y1)=1$, $r^{\prime}(x,y2)=0$ and
$$p^{\prime}(y1 \succ y2 \mid x) = \frac{e^{1}}{e^{1}+e^{0}} = \frac{e}{e+1},$$
the same number.

Why the KL‑constrained optimal policy is invariant. The paper shows the optimal policy for reward $r$ is
$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr),$$
where
$$Z(x)=\sum_y\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr).$$
If $r^{\prime}(x,y)=r(x,y)+f(x)$ then
$$\exp\!\bigl(r^{\prime}(x,y)/\beta\bigr)=\exp\!\bigl(f(x)/\beta\bigr)\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr),$$
and the same factor $\exp\!\bigl(f(x)/\beta\bigr)$ appears in every term of $Z^{\prime}(x)$, so it cancels in the ratio. Thus $\pi_{r^{\prime}}=\pi_r$. Intuitively this is the same fact as “adding the same constant to every logit of a softmax does not change the softmax probabilities.”

Concrete policy example (continuing the numbers, with $\beta=1$ and uniform $\pi_{\text{ref}}$): $\pi_r(y1) \propto 0.5\cdot e^{2}$ and $\pi_r(y2) \propto 0.5\cdot e^{1}$, giving the same probabilities as when using $r^{\prime}$ because the common multiplicative factor cancels.

What this means for identifiability and reward normalization in RLHF. Preference data and the Plackett–Luce family identify reward functions only up to an x-only function $f(x)$. In practice people therefore impose an identifiability constraint (for example, subtracting a mean or forcing a per‑context mean of zero) so the learned reward network is unique and numerically stable. Those normalizations are exactly adding an $f(x)$ (or choosing a particular $f(x)$ to subtract), so they do not change the induced preference probabilities or the KL‑constrained optimum policy. The paper formalizes this: Lemma 1 says preferences are invariant under $r \mapsto r + f(x)$, Lemma 2 says the optimal policy is invariant, and Theorem 1 shows you can always pick the representative
$$r(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$$
(up to the additive x-only term coming from the partition function), which is the canonical choice DPO uses.

Practical caveat. Although adding $f(x)$ does not change the theoretical optimal policy, the particular baseline you pick can affect optimization dynamics in finite‑sample / gradient‑based training (variance of policy gradients, critic learning, numerical stability, and the effective learning rates). That is why practitioners still normalize rewards (reduce variance, stabilize actor‑critic updates). DPO sidesteps explicit reward fitting and the need to hand‑tune such baselines because it directly parameterizes the policy and implicitly fixes a canonical baseline (the partition‑function normalization) that makes the mapping between rewards and policy analytic.

Takeaway: baseline shifts $r(x,y) \mapsto r(x,y) + f(x)$ are harmless for both the BT/PL preference model and the KL‑constrained optimal policy — they are an identifiability degree of freedom. Reward normalization in RLHF is simply a choice of that baseline to improve learning dynamics; it does not change the final theoretical policy.

### How does DPO generalize to k-way Plackett–Luce rankings, and what is the resulting loss?
Question:
The paper mentions that the derivation extends to Plackett–Luce models when we have ranked lists, not just pairs. What is the explicit DPO loss when the data provide a total order \(y_{(1)}\succ y_{(2)}\succ\dots\succ y_{(k)}\) under the Plackett–Luce assumption? Is it the negative log-likelihood of a cascade of softmaxes over the policy-to-reference log-ratios, i.e., terms like \(\log\tfrac{\exp(\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{(j)}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{(j)}\mid x)})}{\sum_{m\ge j}\exp(\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{(m)}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{(m)}\mid x)})}\)? Any computational or stability differences compared to the pairwise case?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — DPO extends exactly to the Plackett–Luce (PL) ranking model. If you observe a full ordered list $y_{(1)}\succ y_{(2)}\succ\cdots\succ y_{(k)}$ the DPO objective is the negative log‑likelihood of the PL cascade where the PL “scores’’ are the policy-to-reference log‑ratios scaled by $\beta$. Concretely, define
$$
s_{(j)}(x)\;=\;\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{(j)}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{(j)}\mid x)}.
$$
The PL probability of that ranking under these scores is
$$
\prod_{j=1}^k \frac{\exp\big(s_{(j)}(x)\big)}{\sum_{m\ge j}\exp\big(s_{(m)}(x)\big)},
$$
so the DPO loss for one ranked example is the negative log‑likelihood
$$
-\sum_{j=1}^k \log\frac{\exp(s_{(j)})}{\sum_{m\ge j}\exp(s_{(m)})}
\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^k\Big(\log\!\sum_{m\ge j}\exp(s_{(m)}) - s_{(j)}\Big).
$$
(This reduces to the pairwise Bradley–Terry / logistic DPO loss when $k=2$, which is the form shown in the main text. The paper states this PL extension in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.)

Practical / numerical notes and how it compares to the pairwise case. The dominant work is scoring each candidate under $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (getting the sequence log‑probs); that cost grows linearly with $k$. Once you have the $s_{(j)}$ values, the PL loss can be computed stably and in $O(k)$ time by a back‑to‑front log‑sum‑exp pass: compute $\text{lse}_k=s_{(k)}$ and $\text{lse}_j=\log\!\operatorname{logsumexp}(s_{(j)},\text{lse}_{j+1})$, then accumulate $\sum_j(\text{lse}_j-s_{(j)})$. Using the log‑sum‑exp trick avoids overflow/underflow; this is the standard stable implementation trick for softmax/PL and is no more delicate than the pairwise logistic implementation (which ordinarily uses a numerically stable log‑sigmoid).

Statistical / optimization differences versus converting a ranking into pairwise comparisons. You could turn a $k$-ranking into many pairwise Bradley–Terry comparisons (all $\binom{k}{2}$ pairs) and train with the pairwise DPO loss on those pairs. That is a different objective: it overweights low‑rank pairs and costs more (up to $O(k^2)$ pairwise terms). The PL cascade is the canonical MLE for full rankings and uses only $k-1$ nontrivial terms (the last step contributes zero), so it is generally more statistically efficient and computationally attractive for true ranked lists. The gradient interpretation is natural: each cascade step compares the chosen item to the remaining pool via a softmax, so the learning signal is distributed across all remaining alternatives (rather than as many independent pairwise signals).

Implementation caveats. Make sure the reference model has support on the candidates (or add tiny smoothing) so the log‑ratio is finite. Large values of $\beta$ amplify the logits and can make the softmax numerically sharp (again, handled by log‑sum‑exp but may affect optimization dynamics). Other than these usual concerns, there is no special instability introduced by using the PL loss — just use log‑domain arithmetic and the cumulative log‑sum‑exp trick.

In short: your guess was correct — the DPO loss for a $k$-way ranked list under Plackett–Luce is exactly the negative log of the cascade of softmaxes over the scores $\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\rm ref})$. It is naturally more informative than a single pairwise term and, if implemented with standard log‑sum‑exp numerics and by caching sequence log‑probs, is both computationally and numerically straightforward.

### What happens if \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is chosen to be uniform—does DPO reduce to standard logistic regression on log-likelihoods?
Question:
Suppose I take \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\) to be uniform over a fixed finite candidate set for each \(x\). Then the DPO objective reduces to a logistic loss on \(\beta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\) differences. Is this interpretation correct? If so, does using a uniform reference make DPO more prone to over-optimization or distribution shift, since the implicit reward \(\hat r_\theta=\beta\log\pi_\theta\) has no baseline anchor to the original model? Are there reasons to avoid a uniform reference in practice, especially with open-ended language generation where the candidate set is huge?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — algebraically DPO collapses to a logistic loss on log-probabilities when the reference is uniform, and that choice removes the usual SFT “anchor”, which in practice makes the method behave very differently (and usually worse) for open‑ended generation. The paper itself recommends using the SFT model as the reference for precisely these reasons.

Why the math is that simple. The DPO loss in the paper is
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) =
- \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
- \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\Big].
$$
If $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)$ is uniform on a finite candidate set of size $K(x)$, then $\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x) = -\log K(x)$ is the same for every $y$ and cancels in the difference. You get
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = - \mathbb{E}\Big[\log\sigma\big(\beta(\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x))\big)\Big],
$$
so the pairwise probability depends only on differences of $\log\pi_\theta$. Equivalently the implicit reward becomes
$$
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)+\text{(const wrt y)},
$$
so pairwise comparisons are logistic on the log-likelihoods.

Why this matters practically. The paper motivates and uses a nonuniform $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (typically the SFT model) for two linked reasons: the KL term in the original RL objective
$$
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[r]-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}[\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}]
$$
acts as an anchor that keeps the fine‑tuned policy near the reference distribution where the preference data (and any learned reward) are reliable. If $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is uniform then
$$
\mathrm{KL}[\pi_\theta\|u] = -H(\pi_\theta) + \log K,
$$
so the KL penalty becomes (up to a constant) an entropy bonus $+\beta H(\pi_\theta)$ rather than a penalty that discourages drifting away from the SFT distribution. That means you lose the intended regularization that prevents the policy from moving into regions where the human preference signal is uncalibrated. The paper explicitly notes the importance of this constraint for preventing model degeneration and distribution shift, and recommends setting $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}=\pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$ when available (or fitting a reference to the preferred completions if not).

Concrete consequences and when uniform is (not) sensible. If your candidate set is truly small, closed, and you legitimately want to rank items with no prior (e.g., re‑ranking ten fixed hypotheses per prompt), a uniform reference is a valid, simple choice and reduces DPO to a logistic contrast over log-probabilities. But for open‑ended language generation the candidate set is astronomically large and (a) you cannot sensibly sample uniformly from it, (b) the preference data are almost always collected from an SFT or other nonuniform policy, and (c) optimizing an implicit reward equal to $\log\pi_\theta$ risks pushing the model toward whatever sequences the model can easily increase probability for (short/generic/high‑probability sequences, reward hacking, mode collapse), or into regions where the human labels do not generalize. In short, uniform $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ removes the natural “baseline” anchoring the learned policy to the model that produced the preference data and so increases the chance of over‑optimization and harmful distribution shift. The paper therefore recommends using the SFT/reference model as $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (or fitting one to the preferred completions) to mitigate these problems.

If you do experiment with a uniform reference, treat it as a special re‑ranking/regression regime (finite candidate set), and add explicit regularization (e.g., an explicit KL to an SFT baseline or other constraints) if you want to avoid the drift problems the paper warns about.

### How is the KL between \(\pi\) and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) computed and controlled in DPO experiments, given DPO has no explicit KL penalty?
Question:
In the experiments, the paper reports a sequence-level KL between \(\pi\) and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) and shows a reward–KL frontier. Since the DPO loss does not include an explicit KL penalty, how is the realized KL controlled—only via the choice of \(\beta\)? Is the KL measured as a sum of per-token KLs over sampled sequences from \(\pi\), or evaluated on a fixed set of prompts with teacher-forced targets? Is there a principled way to select \(\beta\) to achieve a desired KL without sweeping?
Answer:
Short answer: the realized KL is controlled by the DPO temperature parameter $\beta$ (it plays the same role as the KL Lagrange multiplier in the usual RLHF objective), and the paper reports a sequence-level KL computed on held-out prompts (they define it as “the sum of the per‑timestep KL divergences”). In practice that sequence KL is estimated as an expectation under the learned policy (i.e. by generating completions and computing log‑prob differences), and the paper chooses $\beta$ by simple sweeps rather than giving a closed‑form mapping from $\beta$ to a desired KL.

Why $\beta$ controls KL (intuition + formula). DPO is derived from the KL‑constrained objective
$$
\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi}[r(x,y)] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big[\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big],
$$
whose optimal policy has the Boltzmann form
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x)\propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\big).
$$
Reparameterizing the reward as
$$
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)
$$
(and substituting into the Bradley–Terry preference model) produces the DPO loss. Because $\beta$ is the same multiplier that appears in the original constrained problem, changing $\beta$ changes how strongly the method trades off reward vs. staying near $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (larger $\beta$ $\Rightarrow$ stronger penalty $\Rightarrow$ more conservative policy).

How the paper measures KL. The paper explicitly reports “average sequence‑level KL (that is, the sum of the per‑timestep KL‑divergences) with the reference policy.” The sequence KL for a prompt $x$ can be written as
$$
\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)
= \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}\Big[\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Big]
= \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}\Big[\sum_{t}\log\frac{\pi(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}\Big],
$$
which equals the expected sum of per‑step KLs. To estimate this in practice you Monte‑Carlo sample completions $y^{(i)}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)$ and compute the empirical average
$$
\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}(x)\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N\Big(\log\pi(y^{(i)}\mid x)-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{(i)}\mid x)\Big),
$$
then average across prompts. (Equivalently you can compute per‑step KLs at the sampled prefixes and sum them.) This is consistent with the paper’s description that they evaluate each policy on a set of test prompts and report the average sequence‑level KL.

Is there a principled way to pick $\beta$ without sweeping? The paper itself simply sweeps a small grid of $\beta$ values (e.g. $\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$) and reports the reward–KL frontier; it does not present a closed‑form mapping from $\beta$ to a target KL. A principled approach you can use in practice is to treat $\beta$ as the Lagrange multiplier and adapt it during training (exactly the same idea used in KL‑penalized RL): measure the current KL periodically and increase $\beta$ if KL exceeds your target (or decrease it if KL is well below target). Concretely a simple dual update step is
$$
\beta \leftarrow \beta + \eta\big(\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}-\mathrm{KL}_{\text{target}}\big)
$$
(or a positive multiplicative update), with projection to keep $\beta>0$. Alternatively, a cheap practical strategy is to train short pilots for a few $\beta$ values or do a coarse binary search: these are exactly the types of sweeps used in the paper. Note that the mapping from $\beta$ to realized KL depends on model capacity, data, and optimization dynamics, so some empirical calibration is unavoidable.

Practical tips. When you care about generation behavior use KL estimated under sampled generations (not teacher‑forced KL on gold prefixes) because teacher‑forced KL tends to understate divergence that appears when the model self‑generates prefixes. Start with the ranges the paper used, run cheap pilot runs to measure KL, and then either pick the $\beta$ that lies on the desired point of the frontier or run an adaptive dual controller as above.

In short: DPO has no explicit KL term in its loss because the KL constraint is encoded implicitly via the reparameterization; the paper measures sequence‑level KL as the sum of per‑timestep KLs evaluated on test prompts (estimated via sampling), and the practical way to achieve a desired KL is empirical calibration (sweep or short pilots) or an adaptive Lagrange‑dual update — there is no single closed‑form map from $\beta$ to KL given the learned reward/policy.

### Can you give a simple example showing why unlikelihood training can cause degeneration while DPO does not?
Question:
The paper cautions that a naive objective that increases \(\log\pi(y_w\mid x)\) and decreases \(\log\pi(y_l\mid x)\) (unlikelihood) can lead to degeneration, while DPO’s weighting avoids this. Could someone provide a minimal illustrative example (e.g., a toy vocabulary with two or three tokens) showing how unlikelihood can push the model toward pathological solutions (such as always outputting very short answers or collapsing modes), and how the DPO logistic weighting prevents that by downweighting already-correctly-ordered pairs?
Answer:
Short answer: unlikelihood-style updates apply the same fixed “push” on every (preferred, dispreferred) pair, even when the model already ranks the pair correctly; those repeated, unnecessary pushes (across many pairs) reallocate probability mass to whatever outputs were not being punished (often short/generic responses), producing collapse. DPO multiplies each pair’s update by a logistic factor that is near zero when the model already prefers the winner, so DPO effectively only updates the hard / mis-ordered pairs and avoids the over-correction that causes degeneration.

Below I give the elementary math (softmax logits) and a tiny numerical toy (3 tokens, 2 prompts) that makes this behavior concrete.

Why the difference appears (short derivation). Let the model parametrize a discrete distribution by logits $l(y)$ and softmax probabilities $\pi(y)=\exp(l(y))/Z$. The derivative of a log-probability w.r.t. the logits is
$$
\frac{\partial \log\pi(y_i)}{\partial l_j}=\mathbf{1}\{i=j\}-\pi(y_j).
$$
A “naive unlikelihood” pairwise update that increases $\log\pi(y_w)$ and decreases $\log\pi(y_l)$ has (in logit space) the per-pair update direction
$$
\Delta_{\text{unlike}} \propto \nabla\log\pi(y_w)-\nabla\log\pi(y_l).
$$
Because the $-\pi(\cdot)$ terms cancel, this simplifies to the constant vector
$$
\Delta_{\text{unlike}} \propto e_{y_w}-e_{y_l},
$$
i.e. raise the logit of the winner and lower the logit of the loser by the same fixed amount, independent of how already-correct the model is for that pair.

DPO’s gradient (Eq. in the paper) inserts a multiplicative weight
$$
\sigma\bigl(\hat r(y_l)-\hat r(y_w)\bigr),
\qquad\text{where }\hat r(y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)},
$$
so the per-pair contribution becomes
$$
\Delta_{\text{DPO}} \propto \sigma\bigl(\hat r(y_l)-\hat r(y_w)\bigr)\,\bigl(e_{y_w}-e_{y_l}\bigr).
$$
If the model already gives the winner much higher probability than the loser, then $\hat r(y_l)-\hat r(y_w)$ is very negative and $\sigma(\cdot)$ is near zero — the update is downweighted (almost ignored). That is the key stability mechanism.

A minimal numeric toy showing the effect. Vocabulary = $\{A,B,S\}$ (A = desired, B = undesired, S = short/generic). Two prompts $x_1,x_2$. We model logits as $l(y)=w_y+b_{x,y}$ where $w$ are shared base logits (what training updates) and $b_x$ are context biases (fixed). Initialize $w=(0,0,0)$ and choose context biases so $x_1$ strongly prefers $A$ and $x_2$ strongly prefers $B$:
$$
b_{x_1}=(2,0,-1),\qquad b_{x_2}=(-1,2,0).
$$
Compute initial softmax probabilities (exact numbers below rounded):
for $x_1$: $\pi_{1}\approx(0.844,0.114,0.042)$ for $(A,B,S)$;
for $x_2$: $\pi_{2}\approx(0.042,0.844,0.114)$.

Dataset: humans prefer $A$ over $B$ for both prompts (so one pair per prompt). One pair is already “easy” (correct and confident) — the $x_1$ pair — the other pair $x_2$ is mis-ordered.

Apply one training step with learning rate $\eta=0.8$.

1) Naive unlikelihood: each pair contributes $e_A-e_B$; summing two pairs gives $2(e_A-e_B)$. Update the shared base logits:
$$
w \leftarrow w + 0.8\cdot 2(e_A-e_B) = (1.6,-1.6,0).
$$
Recompute $x_2$ probabilities after this single global update: $\pi_2$ becomes approximately
$$
\pi_2^{\text{unlike}}\approx(0.423,0.346,0.232).
$$
Notice the short/generic token $S$ rose from $0.114$ to $0.232$ in $x_2$ after one step — the global equal pushes (from also updating the already-correct $x_1$ pair) reallocated mass in a way that significantly raised the short token’s probability.

2) DPO-weighted update: compute per-pair weights $s_i=\sigma(\log\frac{\pi_i(B)}{\pi_i(A)})$. For $x_1$ (already-correct) this is small ($\approx0.12$); for $x_2$ (mis-ordered) it is large ($\approx0.95$). The total effective update is $(s_1+s_2)(e_A-e_B)\approx1.07(e_A-e_B)$, so
$$
w\leftarrow w + 0.8\cdot 1.07(e_A-e_B)\approx(0.857,-0.857,0).
$$
Recompute $x_2$ probabilities: $\pi_2^{\text{DPO}}\approx(0.173,0.627,0.200)$.
You can see DPO’s step is gentler: $S$ still rises but less (to $0.20$ vs $0.232$ under unlikelihood) and the overall change is smaller because the already-easy pair contributed little.

Why this matters in practice. In a real preference dataset you typically have many pairs, and many of those pairs are already correctly ordered by the model (easy negatives). The unlikelihood update treats each pair equally and therefore accumulates many unnecessary pushes that change shared parameters and reallocate mass onto tokens or sequences that were not explicitly punished (often short/generic completions). Those aggregated, repeated pushes are what lead to the degenerations (mode collapse, overly-short replies). DPO’s logistic weight focuses learning on the hard / inverted pairs and attenuates updates for easy pairs; once a pair becomes correctly ordered the weight tends to zero, so DPO avoids the runaway global drift that unlikelihood can create.

Connection to the paper. This is exactly the mechanism the paper points out: the DPO gradient (see the paper’s gradient expression) contains the multiplicative $\sigma(\hat r(y_l)-\hat r(y_w))$ factor that downweights “already-correctly-ordered” examples; the naive probability-ratio or unlikelihood objective lacks that per-example importance weight and therefore can drive pathological redistribution of probability mass.

Takeaway: if you naively push down every dispreferred completion you observe, you risk over-correcting and collapsing probability into cheap/generic outputs. The DPO logistic weight is a simple, principled fix that focuses updates where they’re needed and prevents that collapse.

### Is there a principled relationship between DPO’s \(\beta\) and the inference-time sampling temperature?
Question:
Both \(\beta\) in DPO and temperature at inference rescale logits in different places: \(\beta\) scales the implicit reward/log-ratio during training, while sampling temperature rescales the model logits at decode time. Is there any theoretical relationship between these two scalings? For example, if \(\beta\) is set too low or too high, can an appropriate choice of sampling temperature compensate at inference, or do they affect qualitatively different trade-offs (e.g., preference alignment vs. diversity)?
Answer:
Short answer: no — there is no general, principled one-to-one substitution of DPO’s training-scale parameter $\beta$ by the decoding temperature. They both “rescale logits” but at different places and with different consequences: $\beta$ is a training-time hyperparameter that changes the trained policy (the balance between reward and KL-to-reference), whereas the sampling temperature only reshapes the already-trained policy at decode time (changing stochasticity/diversity). Except in special, degenerate cases (e.g. a uniform reference policy) you cannot recover the effect of a different $\beta$ simply by changing temperature.

Why, mathematically. The paper shows the KL-constrained optimal policy has the Boltzmann form
$$
\pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Big),
$$
and DPO parameterizes the implicit reward as
$$
\hat r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\text{const}.
$$
At inference you usually apply a temperature $T$ by sampling from
$$
\pi_{T}(y\mid x)\;\propto\;\pi_\theta(y\mid x)^{1/T} \;=\; \exp\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{T}\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\Big).
$$
If the trained model exactly equals the Boltzmann solution $\pi_\theta=\pi_r$, then applying temperature gives
$$
\pi_{T}(y\mid x)\propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)^{1/T}\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta T} r(x,y)\Big).
$$
This is not of the same canonical form $\propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\exp(r/\tilde\beta)$ unless the extra factor $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)^{1/T-1}$ is harmless — for example if $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is uniform (or constant across the candidate outputs), in which case temperature simply rescales the effective $\beta$ by $T$ (so $\tilde\beta=\beta T$). In the general case the temperature injects an extra $ \big(\tfrac{1}{T}-1\big)\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)$ term, so you change more than just the reward/KL tradeoff.

Intuition / consequences. $\beta$ is part of the training objective and therefore changes which policy the optimizer converges to: it directly controls the reward vs KL trade-off (smaller $\beta$ $\to$ more reward-seeking, larger $\beta$ $\to$ more conservative w.r.t. $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$). The sampling temperature only reshapes the already-learned distribution pointwise at decode time; it cannot change the learned ordering of probabilities (for $T>0$ the map $p\mapsto p^{1/T}$ is strictly monotone so rankings and argmax are preserved). Thus if a mis-set $\beta$ caused the model to learn the “wrong” ranking of candidate completions, no choice of sampling temperature will recover the intended ranking.

Practical guidance consistent with the paper. Set and tune $\beta$ during training to achieve the desired alignment vs. deviation from the reference (or to hit a target KL frontier). After training, use decoding temperature to trade off determinism vs. diversity when sampling from the fixed policy. The paper’s experiments also note DPO is comparatively robust to sampling temperature (empirically the trained DPO policies retained good win-rates across a range of temperatures), but that robustness does not imply that temperature can replace proper choice of $\beta$.

Summary. $\beta$ and temperature are related in that both appear as “scale” parameters in exponential/softmax expressions, but they act at different stages (training vs. decoding) and are not generally interchangeable. Only in special cases (notably a flat reference $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$) does a change in temperature correspond to a simple rescaling of the effective $\beta$. Otherwise they control qualitatively different trade-offs (preference alignment / KL penalty vs. sampling diversity).

### How exactly does the control-as-inference view lead to the objective with the normalization term in Eq. (14)?
Question:
The paper rewrites the RLHF optimization as minimizing \(\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\Vert\pi^*(\cdot\mid x)]\), which expands to an expectation of \(f(r_\phi,\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta)-\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\), where \(f\) contains a \(-\beta\log\sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(r_\phi/\beta)\) term. Could someone derive this decomposition in detail and explain the interpretation of this term as a soft value (or partition function) baseline? How does dropping or misestimating this term increase variance in actor–critic training, and why does the DPO reparameterization circumvent the need to estimate it?
Answer:
Short answer: the normalization term is exactly the log-partition (a “soft value”) that appears when you write the KL-to-optimal-policy objective in closed form. Algebraically it comes from plugging the soft-optimal policy
$$\pi^*(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(r_\phi(x,y)/\beta\bigr)$$
into the KL, and intuitively it is the state-dependent baseline (the soft log-sum-exp of rewards under the reference policy) that turns the plain reward into an advantage. Dropping or poorly estimating it makes the policy gradient a high-variance REINFORCE estimator (or forces you to learn a noisy critic); the DPO reparameterization avoids this whole problem because the partition function cancels in the pairwise-preference likelihood, so you never need to estimate it. Below I show the short derivation and explain the interpretation and consequences.

Derivation (step-by-step). Start from the control-as-inference form: the optimal “soft” policy for a fixed reward $r_\phi$ and KL weight $\beta$ is
$$\displaystyle \pi^*(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x,y)\Big),$$
with $Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\big(r_{\phi}(x,y)/\beta\big)$ (this is Eq. (6) / Eq. (op_policy) in the paper). Consider minimizing the KL
$\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\big[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\Vert\pi^*(\cdot\mid x)\big] = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\big[\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)-\log\pi^*(y\mid x)\big].$
Substitute $\log\pi^*(y\mid x)=\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)+\frac{1}{\beta}r_\phi(x,y)-\log Z(x)$ to get
$$
\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi_\theta\Vert\pi^*]
= \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\Big[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}-\frac{1}{\beta}r_\phi(x,y)\Big] + \log Z(x).
$$
Multiply by $-\beta$ and move terms to the maximization form (this is the algebra the paper refers to) to obtain
$$
\max_{\pi_\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\Big[\,r_\phi(x,y)-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}-\beta\log Z(x)\,\Big].
$$
Defining the function
$$\displaystyle f(r_\phi,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}},\beta)(x,y):=r_\phi(x,y)-\beta\log Z(x)
= r_\phi(x,y)-\beta\log\!\sum_{y'}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y'\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(r_\phi(x,y')/\beta\bigr)$$
gives exactly the paper’s Eq. (14) (written as an expectation of $f-\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$).

Interpretation: why is $-\beta\log Z(x)$ a “soft value” (baseline)? The quantity
$$\displaystyle V(x):=\beta\log Z(x)=\beta\log\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\!\big[e^{r_\phi(x,y)/\beta}\big]$$
is the log-partition (a log-sum-exp) of the exponentiated reward under the reference distribution. In maximum-entropy / control-as-inference language $V(x)$ is the soft state-value: as $\beta\to 0$ it approaches $\max_y r_\phi(x,y)$, while for larger $\beta$ it is a smooth average of rewards. Defining $f=r_\phi-V$ makes $f$ the soft “advantage” of action $y$ under reward $r_\phi$ relative to the soft value of the prefix $x$. In the policy-gradient identity (REINFORCE) a per-state baseline reduces variance because the gradient of $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[f(x,y)]$ is
$\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\big[f(x,y)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\big]$
when $f$ does not depend on $\theta$. Subtracting $V(x)$ centers the scalar that multiplies $\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta$ for all actions of the same $x$, which reduces variance of the Monte‑Carlo estimator of the policy gradient.

Why high variance / instability arises if you drop or misestimate $V(x)$. If you drop $V(x)$ you are left with the plain REINFORCE estimator using $r_\phi(x,y)$ as the scalar multiplier; this has larger variance because rewards across actions (and across prefixes) are not centered per-state. In practice you cannot compute $V(x)$ exactly because it sums over exponentially many sequences; actor–critic methods therefore try to learn a critic to approximate $V(x)$, or prior RLHF work used single-sample “human completion” baselines (a Monte Carlo estimate of $V$). Both approaches introduce problems: a learned critic can be noisy, lagging, or biased (and learning the critic jointly with policy can cause instability); single-sample Monte Carlo estimates are high-variance and provide a poor baseline. The paper points out precisely that this normalization term is the hard-to-compute soft value whose absence or misestimation is a major source of variance and instability in RLHF actor–critic training (this is the content of the subsection “Instability of Actor-Critic Algorithms”).

Why DPO avoids estimating it. DPO reparameterizes the reward class by writing any representative reward in the equivalence class as
$$\displaystyle r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)$$
(which is Eq. (7) / Eq. (main_eq) in the paper). The Bradley–Terry preference model depends only on reward differences $r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$, so the additive term $\beta\log Z(x)$ (which is the same for both $y_1$ and $y_2$) cancels out. Substituting the parametrization with $\pi_\theta$ into the pairwise preference likelihood yields a cross-entropy loss that depends only on the log-probability ratios $\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$ and never requires $Z(x)$. Therefore DPO never needs to compute or learn the soft value / partition function: the baseline has been eliminated algebraically by working with policy parameters directly and by exploiting that preference labels are invariant to adding the same $x$-dependent constant to all rewards. Concretely, the DPO loss (Eq. (optimum_model)) is
$$\displaystyle -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)$$,
and its gradient is a stable, classification-like update that implicitly reweights likelihood gradients by an advantage-like quantity but never requires estimating $V(x)$.

Bottom line: the normalization term in Eq. (14) is exactly the state-dependent soft value (log-partition) that makes the optimal policy a normalized “softmax” over exponentiated rewards. That term acts like the canonical baseline/soft-value in policy gradients and must be estimated in actor–critic RLHF, which is a key source of variance/instability. DPO sidesteps the problem by parametrizing policies so that this additive term cancels in the pairwise-preference model, converting the whole pipeline into a low-variance, supervised (binary‑cross‑entropy) training problem.

### Can DPO be adapted to token-level or step-wise preferences instead of sequence-level pairs?
Question:
DPO is presented for sequence-level preferences between completions \(y_w\) and \(y_l\). If I have feedback at the token level or at intermediate steps (e.g., a human marks certain tokens or spans as better), can the DPO derivation be adapted to operate on partial sequences \(y_{1:t}\)? Would the loss become a sum over steps of Bradley–Terry terms using \(\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t\mid x, y_{<t})}\), or are there pitfalls due to the BT model assuming comparisons at the same "granularity"?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — DPO can be applied to partial / token-level items, but you must be careful about what your “items” in the Bradley–Terry model are. If your human labels are explicit pairwise comparisons between two partial sequences (or between two token choices given the same prefix), you can plug those pairs into DPO exactly as in the paper by treating each partial sequence (or token-choice-with-prefix) as an item y. What you cannot generally do is replace a sequence-level DPO term by a naïve sum of independent per-step Bradley–Terry losses unless your comparisons themselves are per-step and share the same context.

Why this is true (math + intuition). The DPO loss in the paper for a pair (x, y_w, y_l) is
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}( \pi_\theta ) \;=\; -\log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} \;-\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big),
$$
and this formula does not care whether the y's are full sequences, truncated prefixes, or single tokens: any object y for which your model defines a probability $\pi(\,y\mid x)$ is a valid “item” in the Bradley–Terry model. For a partial sequence $y_{1:t}$ the sequence log-probability naturally decomposes into conditionals:
$$
\log\pi_\theta(y_{1:t}\mid x)=\sum_{i=1}^t \log\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x,y_{<i}),
$$
so the DPO inner argument becomes a difference of sums of per-token log‑ratios:
$$
\beta\sum_{i=1}^{t_w}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{w,i}\mid x,y_{w,<i})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w,i}\mid x,y_{w,<i})}
\;-\;
\beta\sum_{i=1}^{t_l}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{l,i}\mid x,y_{l,<i})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l,i}\mid x,y_{l,<i})}.
$$

Two useful special cases follow:

1) Token-level comparisons at the same prefix. If the human label compares two token choices at the same prefix $y_{<t}$ (say tokens $a$ and $b$), then the sums cancel and the DPO term exactly reduces to a per-step Bradley–Terry term over the conditional distributions:
$$
-\log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a\mid x,y_{<t})}
-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(b\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(b\mid x,y_{<t})}\Big).
$$
So if your annotations are pairwise token choices given identical context, you may safely form a dataset of such pairs and minimize the corresponding per-step DPO losses (sum them over labeled steps).

2) General partial-sequence comparisons. If the two partial sequences differ over multiple positions or over different prefixes, the DPO argument is a single logistic over the sum of token‑level log ratios (the expression above). Because $\sigma$ is nonlinear, this is not equal to a sum of independent per-step logistic losses. The supervision couples the tokens: a single sequence-level preference constrains the summed token rewards, not each token independently.

Practical implications and pitfalls. First, always make sure the compared items live in the same “choice set” the annotator intended. Per‑token BT is valid only when the annotator actually compared the two token choices at the same prefix (or you generate comparable alternatives). If an annotator marks a token/span as “better” but did not provide an explicit alternative, you must manufacture alternatives (e.g., replace the span with sampled alternatives or compare post‑hoc continuations) to form pairwise data compatible with BT/DPO.

Second, beware of downstream effects: a token that looks locally better might produce worse continuations later. If your human feedback is meant to capture final-sequence quality, per-token labels that ignore continuations can train the policy to optimize locally at the expense of global quality. To reflect downstream effects you can either (a) collect comparisons of partial sequences that include downstream text (treating those partial/full sequences as items in DPO), or (b) for a token-level label, sample continuations after each alternative token and compare the resulting sequences (so the item compared is a completed sequence).

Third, you can mix granularities in the same DPO training set: treat every labeled object (single-token-with-prefix, truncated sequence, full sequence) as an item y and include its pairwise comparisons in $\mathcal{D}$. DPO’s likelihood is still a valid MLE under the Bradley–Terry / Plackett–Luce assumptions. But mixed-granularity data imposes different (and possibly conflicting) constraints on the model; check consistency and monitor sequence-level metrics you care about.

Finally, implementation notes from the paper that carry over: you still need a $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ that gives the reference probabilities at the same granularity (per-token conditionals or sequence probabilities), and you should tune $\beta$ as usual because the $\beta$ reweights the implicit reward scale and appears inside the sigmoid.

Summary recommendation. If your annotators explicitly compare token choices in the same prefix, implement DPO at the token-conditional level (each label becomes a per-step DPO example). If your labels are spans or partial-sequence judgments that imply downstream effects, either treat the partial sequences as items in DPO or convert each token/span label into completed-sequence comparisons (by sampling or deterministic completion) before applying DPO. Don’t naively replace a sequence-level DPO term by a sum of independent per-step BT losses unless your annotations actually decompose at that granularity.

### Under what conditions is DPO’s MLE consistent with the true preference distribution (as cited to Bong 2022)?
Question:
The paper notes that, as a reparameterized Bradley–Terry model, DPO enjoys consistency under suitable assumptions (citing Bong 2022). What are the exact conditions for consistency here? For example, do we require independent and identically distributed pairwise comparisons for each \(x\), sufficient coverage of the response space, and a correctly specified link function (logistic)? How does function approximation error from using a neural \(\pi_\theta\) instead of a linear model affect consistency claims?
Answer:
Short answer: DPO’s MLE is consistent under essentially the same structural assumptions that give MLE consistency for Bradley–Terry / Plackett–Luce models. Concretely those are (i) the Bradley–Terry/logistic preference model is correctly specified for the data-generating preferences, (ii) the comparison triples $(x,y_w,y_l)$ are (asymptotically) i.i.d. draws from that preference distribution and standard regularity conditions hold, (iii) the comparisons provide enough connectivity/coverage to identify the relative scores, and (iv) the reference policy used in the reparameterization has support on any response the optimal policy might place mass on. If these hold and the parametric policy class can represent the true optimal policy, the DPO MLE converges to that policy. If any assumption fails you instead converge to the best-fitting Bradley–Terry representation (the usual “pseudo‑true” limit) or to the best approximation inside a misspecified function class.

Why these conditions are the right ones (short explanation tied to the paper). DPO is just a reparameterized Bradley–Terry fit: under the BT model
$$p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)=\sigma\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)\bigr),$$
and DPO uses the change of variables
$$r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x),$$
so the BT likelihood depends only on differences of the left-hand side and the partition term cancels. Thus consistency of the DPO MLE reduces to the usual consistency of MLE for BT/Plackett–Luce: you need the model to contain the truth (or you accept convergence to the projection), you need enough independent data to invoke a law of large numbers, and you need identifiability of the score differences.

A few important, practical clarifications you asked about:

1. i.i.d. comparisons / per-$x$ repetition. Standard asymptotic consistency proofs assume the dataset of triples $(x,y_w,y_l)$ are i.i.d. (or satisfy an ergodic law). If you want pointwise consistency of the estimated conditional policy $\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)$ for a fixed $x$ you need that $x$ is observed many times (so you get many comparisons conditioning on that $x$). In practice, when the domain of $x$ is large the required “many observations per $x$” may fail; then consistency is only assured for the shared‑parameter model as a whole (i.e., the estimator converges to a function that best fits the aggregated data), and per‑$x$ error depends on how well the parametric model generalizes across contexts.

2. coverage / connectedness of responses. To identify relative scores you must observe comparisons that connect the candidate responses. In the finite‑alternatives setting this is the standard graph condition: the comparison graph (nodes = responses, edges = observed pairwise comparisons) must be connected so differences can be recovered. In the language setting the natural analogue is that the sampling procedure that produced $\mathcal{D}$ must explore the response space sufficiently (and the reference policy used to generate responses must put nonzero probability on the behaviors you want to learn). Concretely you need
$$\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x) > 0 \quad\text{whenever}\quad \pi^*(y\mid x) > 0,$$
otherwise the reparameterization $ \log(\pi/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) $ is undefined and DPO cannot represent that optimal policy.

3. correct link (logistic). If the human/preferences truly follow a Bradley–Terry/logistic model, then the MLE is consistent for that truth. If the logistic link is misspecified (humans generate preferences by some other stochastic rule), then the DPO estimator converges to the parameter that maximizes the expected BT log‑likelihood under the true distribution — i.e. the pseudo‑true BT model. So “correct link” is required for exact recovery; without it you still get the best BT approximation.

4. function approximation / neural $\pi_\theta$. The statistical claim (consistency) separates two things: (A) what the population‑level MLE does, and (B) whether your parametrization can reach the population optimum. If your neural family contains the true optimal policy $\pi^*$ (or can approximate it arbitrarily well as data grows), then the DPO MLE is consistent for $\pi^*$. If the neural family is misspecified, the estimator converges to the best approximation in that family (the solution of
$$\theta^\star = \arg\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim p^*}\Big[\log\sigma\big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\!\mid\! x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\!\mid\! x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\!\mid\! x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\!\mid\! x)}\big)\Big].$$
) In addition, practical issues (finite data, optimization error, local minima, regularization, early stopping) can prevent reaching that limit; these are optimization/statistical tradeoffs rather than failures of the asymptotic theory.

To summarize in one sentence: DPO inherits the standard MLE consistency guarantees of Bradley–Terry/Plackett–Luce (as cited in Bong 2022 and noted in the paper) provided preferences are generated by a BT model (or you accept convergence to the BT projection), the data are i.i.d. with sufficient coverage/connectedness of compared responses, the reference policy has the needed support, and your parametric policy class is rich enough to express the population optimum; otherwise you get the usual pseudo‑true / approximation limits. For the formal technical assumptions and proofs you can consult the Bradley–Terry / generalized BT consistency literature (Bong 2022) and the DPO appendix where the change‑of‑variables and identifiability (up to additive $f(x)$) are discussed.

### What if \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)=0\) for a logged sample—how are the log-ratios and gradients defined in DPO?
Question:
The DPO loss uses \(\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\). If the logged dataset includes a completion \(y\) such that \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)=0\) (e.g., due to truncation, tokenization issues, or a reference model with limited support), the log-ratio is undefined. What assumptions or safeguards ensure this cannot happen (e.g., \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) must have full support on the logged pairs)? In practice, is smoothing or adding a small \(\epsilon\) to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) used, and how would that affect the theoretical equivalence to the KL-constrained objective?
Answer:
Short answer: DPO (and the paper’s derivation of its equivalence to the KL‑constrained RL objective) implicitly assumes the reference policy used in the derivation assigns positive probability to every logged completion. In practice the recommended safeguard is to choose or fit a reference model that has support on the dataset (the paper explicitly recommends using the SFT model that generated the pairs, or if that is not available to fit a model by MLE to the preferred completions). Adding a tiny $\epsilon$ to zero probabilities is a pragmatic fix that makes the loss well‑defined, but it changes which reference policy you are regularizing toward — so the theoretical equivalence holds only with respect to the modified (smoothed) reference, not the original one.

Why the support assumption matters. DPO’s loss uses the sequence log‑ratio
$$\hat{r}_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$$
inside a logistic. If $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)=0$ for a logged $y$ then $\hat{r}_\theta$ is undefined (formally $-\infty$ or $+\infty$ depending on numerator), the logistic argument is ill‑defined and gradients are meaningless or numerically unstable. The paper avoids this by assuming the logged completions were sampled from the reference (they write that they “sample completions $y_1,y_2\sim\pi^{\text{SFT}}$” and in practice set $\pi_{\text{ref}}=\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ when available). If the dataset was not produced by a saved SFT, the paper explicitly recommends initializing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by maximizing likelihood on the preferred completions $\{(x,y_w)\}$, which guarantees the fitted $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ assigns non‑zero mass to those sequences (barring tokenization / OOV problems).

Practical fixes and their theoretical effect. If you encounter zeros you have three realistic options: (1) use the original SFT (or otherwise the same model) that generated the logged pairs so $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)>0$ by construction; (2) fit a reference model on the logged completions (MLE on $(x,y_w)$) so the model has support on those sequences; or (3) apply explicit smoothing (e.g. replace $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by $\tilde{\pi}_{\text{ref}}=(1-\epsilon)\pi_{\text{ref}}+\epsilon q$ where $q$ is an everywhere‑positive filler distribution, or add $\epsilon$ at token level and renormalize). The key point is that any of these remedies makes the DPO loss well‑defined. However, smoothing is not a free choice from a theory perspective: the DPO derivation and its equivalence to the KL‑constrained objective hold relative to the specific $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ that appears in the formulas. If you replace $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by $\tilde{\pi}_{\text{ref}}$ you have simply changed the reference distribution that appears in the KL penalty, so the algorithm is still optimizing the analogous KL‑constrained objective but measured against $\tilde{\pi}_{\text{ref}}$, not the original (unsmoothed) reference. For example, with smoothing
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta; \tilde{\pi}_{\text{ref}}) 
= -\mathbb{E}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\tilde{\pi}_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\tilde{\pi}_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right],
$$
and the corresponding KL penalty in the equivalent RL objective is $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\Vert\tilde{\pi}_{\text{ref}})$.

Implementation notes. In practice you should (a) ensure consistent tokenization and vocabulary between the logged data and the reference model (out‑of‑vocab tokens are a common practical cause of zero probability), (b) compute sequence log‑probs in log‑space (sum token log‑probs) to avoid floating‑point underflow, and (c) if you do apply smoothing or clipping for numerical safety, keep $\epsilon$ very small and be explicit that you have changed the reference — the change is usually minor but it is a change to the regularizer in the underlying RL view.

Takeaway. The natural, theory‑clean condition is: $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)>0$ for every logged $y$. The paper’s practical advice (use the SFT that generated the pairs, or fit the reference to the dataset) is exactly how you enforce that. Smoothing is acceptable as an engineering fallback but it replaces the reference in the KL constraint, so the theoretical equivalence should be understood as holding with respect to the smoothed reference you actually use.

### Without on-policy sampling, how does DPO avoid overfitting to a fixed set of pairs and discover better responses?
Question:
Since DPO trains on an offline fixed dataset of preference pairs, how does it avoid overfitting to those particular responses and missing higher-quality responses that were never shown? Is the idea that reweighting log-ratios relative to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) coupled with generalization of \(\pi_\theta\) suffices, similar to supervised fine-tuning? In what settings might we need online data collection or self-play (e.g., generating new candidate pairs from the current \(\pi_\theta\)) to keep improving, and can DPO be used in such a loop without abandoning its simplicity?
Answer:
Short answer: DPO does not magically invent answers that never appeared in the training pairs; what prevents overfitting to a fixed set of pairs is (1) the LM’s inductive generalization, (2) a pairwise/log-ratio training signal that moves probability mass across the whole conditional distribution rather than memorizing tokens, (3) the implicit KL “anchor” to a reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (controlled by $\beta$), and (4) the DPO per-example weighting that focuses learning on the informative (mis-ranked) pairs. If the offline preference data and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ already cover or imply better completions, DPO will generalize to them; if they do not, you need extra candidate generation (on-policy / self-play or active data collection) and DPO can be slotted into that loop without becoming RL—the paper notes this as a natural extension but treats it as future work.

Technical intuition (grounded in the paper). DPO parameterizes an implicit reward as
$$
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)},
$$
and directly fits the policy with the pairwise likelihood
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
- \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\Big].
$$
The gradient is
$$
\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}
= -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\Big[\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\Big].
$$
Two points follow. First, updates are contrastive: they increase probability of the preferred response and decrease probability of the dispreferred one, i.e. they change the whole conditional distribution rather than only “copying” $y_w$. Second, the multiplicative weight $\sigma(\cdot)$ concentrates learning on pairs the current model mis-ranks (large weight when the model believes the dispreferred $y_l$ is better). That behaves like a margin-weighted logistic loss and reduces useless overfitting to already-correct pairs.

Role of $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $\beta$. The reference $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (usually the SFT model used to generate the pairwise dataset) acts as an anchor: DPO’s implicit reward is always a log-ratio against $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ so the optimization is regularized away from extreme, unsupported shifts. $\beta$ controls how conservative that anchor is (larger $\beta$ → stronger penalty / more conservative behavior). The paper recommends initializing $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}} = \pi^{\mathrm{SFT}}$ (or fitting $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to the preferred completions when SFT is unavailable) to reduce distribution mismatch. This anchoring plus the pairwise, weighted loss is why DPO often generalizes better than naive supervised fine-tuning on $y_w$ alone (Preferred-FT) and why the authors observed that DPO dominates PPO on the reward/KL frontier in experiments.

When you need on-policy / self-play. If the static preference dataset (and the $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ that generated it) never contains — and does not imply by interpolation — higher-quality responses, DPO cannot reliably invent them: it only pushes probability mass toward better responses that the model can reach via its parameters and the learned signal. This is the same “coverage / exploration” limitation seen in offline RL and batch dueling-bandit learning: to discover genuinely new high-quality modes you must generate and label candidates outside the original support. Examples include tasks where the initial SFT is weak, the dataset is tiny or narrow, or the desired behavior is far from anything $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ produces.

Can you keep DPO simple while doing that? Yes. The natural, simple loop is: sample new candidate completions from the current policy (or a mixture of $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $\pi_\theta$), have them labeled (human or trusted synthetic annotator), append those labeled pairs to your offline dataset, then re-run DPO (same cross-entropy/pairwise objective). You do not need RL machinery—just supervised pairwise updates—so the simplicity of DPO is preserved. Two practical cautions from the paper’s theory and discussion: keep an eye on which $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ you use (keeping the original SFT anchor is a conservative choice to avoid runaway drift), and label quality matters (noisy labels will misdirect updates). The paper frames this iterative / self-labeling idea as an open question for future work and presents empirical evidence that DPO already generalizes well in several benchmarks (including an out-of-distribution test) when $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and the preference data have reasonable coverage.

Bottom line: DPO reduces overfitting risk via its contrastive, weighted objective and KL anchoring to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ plus the LM’s inductive bias. But if you want to discover genuinely new, better responses not implied by your offline data, you’ll need to collect (or synthesize) labels for candidates sampled from the new policy—something DPO can absorb straightforwardly while keeping the training step simple.

### How are EOS and variable-length completions handled when computing \(\log \pi(y\mid x)\) for DPO?
Question:
When computing sequence log-likelihoods for \(y_w\) and \(y_l\), do we include the EOS token probability as part of \(\pi(y\mid x)\)? If \(y_w\) and \(y_l\) have different lengths, is there any normalization or padding strategy, or does DPO simply sum the log-probabilities over the exact tokenization of each completion? Could differences in EOS handling systematically bias the preference loss (e.g., preferring shorter sequences due to fewer EOS penalties)?
Answer:
Short answer: treat $\pi(y\mid x)$ as the ordinary sequence-level likelihood of the completion (the product of the autoregressive token probabilities) and compute $\log\pi(y\mid x)$ as the sum of the token log‑probabilities for the exact tokenization of the completion (including an EOS token if the completion is terminated by one). DPO’s loss uses those sequence log‑likelihoods directly (no extra length normalization or padding correction in the objective), so inconsistent handling of EOS or tokenization can produce systematic biases; however many length effects partially cancel because DPO always uses the ratio against the reference policy $\pi_{\rm ref}$.

Why this is the right interpretation. The paper writes the DPO objective in terms of full sequence probabilities (for example,
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\rm ref})
= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_w\mid x)}
-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\Big],
$$
)
and an autoregressive policy defines $\pi(y\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^T \pi(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})$, so
$$
\log\pi(y\mid x)=\sum_{t=1}^T \log\pi(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}).
$$
If your completion includes an explicit end‑of‑sequence token, that token is part of $y$ and its log‑probability belongs in the sum. In batched implementations you should pad for vectorization but mask out pad positions so they do not contribute to the summed log‑prob.

Does length / EOS produce bias? It can, in principle. Because the DPO objective uses full sequence log‑likelihoods (not a per‑token average), longer sequences accumulate more log terms and usually have much smaller absolute sequence probability, so naive comparisons of raw sequence log‑probs can reflect length differences. The important mitigating factor in DPO is that the loss uses the log ratio with a reference policy $\pi_{\rm ref}$: the implicit reward is $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y\mid x)}$, so any length‑dependent behavior that both $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\rm ref}$ share will largely cancel out. The paper therefore recommends using the same reference policy that generated the data (typically the SFT model) or fitting $\pi_{\rm ref}$ on the preferred completions so that reference/model biases are aligned. If $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\rm ref}$ have different length biases (or if your dataset was collected with a different tokenization/EOS convention than you use at training time), then DPO updates can indeed systematically prefer shorter (or longer) completions.

Practical recommendations grounded in the paper. Evaluate $\log\pi(y\mid x)$ as the sum of token log‑probs for the exact tokenization of $y$, and make sure to (a) use the same tokenizer and EOS convention for $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\rm ref}$, (b) include the EOS token when the completion is a terminated sequence (or consistently omit it for all completions, but consistency is the key), and (c) mask pads when summing in batches. Do not apply a length normalization (e.g. average log‑prob per token) if you want to preserve the theoretical correspondence and guarantees in the paper; that would change the implicit reward parameterization $r=\beta\log(\pi/\pi_{\rm ref})$ and so break the derivation. If you observe undesired length bias in practice, diagnose it (compare average lengths of preferred vs dispreferred completions, ablate including/excluding EOS, check $\pi_{\rm ref}$ fit) and address it by fixing tokenization/EOS mismatch or by engineering the dataset (e.g. collect comparisons that control for length) or by explicitly adding a length term to the model’s implicit reward with the conscious understanding that you are changing the objective.

In short: include EOS consistently, sum the exact token log‑probs (mask pads), and rely on the $\pi_\theta/\pi_{\rm ref}$ ratio to cancel many length effects—but watch out for mismatches between model and reference or between generation and evaluation conventions, because those are the real source of systematic length/EOS bias.
