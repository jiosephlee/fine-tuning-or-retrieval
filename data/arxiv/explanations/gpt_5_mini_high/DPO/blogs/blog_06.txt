# Theory Corner: Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is more than an engineering shortcut — it exposes a tight mathematical identity between policies and rewards in the common Bradley–Terry / Plackett–Luce preference models used in RL from human feedback. In this note I walk through the core theory from the DPO paper: why reparameterizing rewards via the policy loses no expressivity, how that gives an exact, RL‑free objective for training from pairwise preferences, and why this avoids the variance and baseline headaches that make actor–critic RL unstable in practice.

## Setup: preferences, constrained RL, and the usual pipeline

The standard RLHF pipeline goes like this: start from a reference language model (usually a supervised fine‑tuned model) $\pi_\text{ref}$, collect pairwise human preferences over model completions, fit a scalar reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$ to those comparisons (e.g. via Bradley–Terry), and finally optimize a policy $\pi_\theta$ to maximize expected reward subject to staying close to $\pi_\text{ref}$. The constrained objective is typically written as

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta} \; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[r_\phi(x,y)\bigr] - \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_\text{ref}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr],
\]

where $\beta>0$ controls conservativeness. Because sampling through $\pi_\theta$ makes this nondifferentiable, people normally optimize it with RL (PPO/actor–critic), and they also have to learn a reward model from preferences first. That pipeline works, but it is complex and often brittle.

Two simple facts are the keys to DPO.

1. Under the KL‑constrained objective above, the optimal policy for a fixed reward $r$ has a closed form:

\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
\]
with partition function $Z(x)=\sum_y \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr)$.

2. The Bradley–Terry model for pairwise preferences depends only on reward differences:
\[
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x) \;=\; \sigma\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)\bigr),
\]
where $\sigma$ is the logistic function.

These two facts make it possible to reparameterize the preference model directly in terms of a policy.

## Reward equivalence classes in Bradley–Terry / Plackett–Luce

There is a basic under‑specification that people sometimes overlook: in any Plackett–Luce family (Bradley–Terry is the pairwise case), adding any function of the input prefix $x$ to a reward does not change the induced preferences. Formally, define an equivalence relation on reward functions by

\[
r \sim r' \quad\text{iff}\quad r(x,y) - r'(x,y) = f(x)\ \text{for some } f.
\]

Two simple lemmas follow directly.

- Lemma (same preferences). If $r\sim r'$ then the induced Plackett–Luce preference distribution is identical, because $r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$ is unchanged.

- Lemma (same optimal policy). If $r\sim r'$ then the optimal KL‑constrained policy induced by $r$ and by $r'$ is the same. Intuitively, adding $f(x)$ multiplies every $\exp(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y))$ by the same factor for fixed $x$, and that cancels out with the partition function.

So rewards are only identified up to an $x$‑dependent additive constant in this model class. Any practical pipeline that recovers a reward must either fix such an identifiability constant or accept that it can only recover a reward class.

## The key reparameterization: policy ↔ reward (no loss of expressivity)

Start from the closed‑form optimum for a reward $r$,

\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr).
\]

Solve for $r$ (take logs) to get

\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \beta\log Z(x).
\]

That is, any reward in the equivalence class corresponding to an optimal policy $\pi_r$ can be written as a log‑ratio of $\pi_r$ and $\pi_\text{ref}$ plus an $x$‑dependent offset. Crucially, in the Bradley–Terry model the offset $\beta\log Z(x)$ cancels when taking reward differences, so the pairwise preference probability can be expressed purely in terms of the policy log‑ratio:

\[
p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)
=
\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)} \;-\; \beta\log\frac{\pi(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)}\Bigr).
\]

This is the DPO change of variables: instead of fitting $r_\phi$ and then running RL, parameterize your reward class by a policy $\pi_\theta$ and fit $\pi_\theta$ directly to the observed pairwise labels via maximum likelihood. Concretely, the DPO loss is the binary cross‑entropy

\[
\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\pi_\text{ref})
= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Biggl[\log \sigma\Bigl(
\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)}
-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}
\Bigr)\Biggr],
\]

where each datum is a triple consisting of a prompt $x$, a human‑preferred completion $y_w$, and a dispreferred completion $y_l$.

Two points to emphasize:

- The reparameterization picks a canonical representative of the reward class: it enforces the particular additive constant that makes the partition function satisfy the normalization identity used to derive $\pi_r$.

- The DPO objective is exactly the (reparameterized) Bradley–Terry MLE: we have not restricted the family of expressible preference models relative to the original Plackett–Luce/Bradley–Terry class — Theorem 1 in the paper shows that every reward class consistent with the preference model has a representative of the form $r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$.

So DPO loses no expressivity relative to the class of rewards that matter for preferences and for the constrained RL optimum.

## What the DPO gradient is actually doing

A useful way to understand DPO is to inspect the gradient. Define the implicit reward induced by current policy $\pi_\theta$:

\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y) \;=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}.
\]

Then

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}
= -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}
\Bigl[\sigma\bigl(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\bigr)\bigl(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\bigr)\Bigr].
\]

Interpretation:

- The term $\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$ is exactly a push to increase probability of the preferred completion $y_w$ and decrease probability of the dispreferred $y_l$.

- The multiplicative weight $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))$ focuses learning on examples where the implicit reward $\hat r_\theta$ currently ranks the pair incorrectly (or not strongly enough). If the model already gives $y_w$ a much higher implicit reward than $y_l$, the weight is small; if it misorders them the weight is close to 1/2 or larger.

This dynamic per‑example weighting is what prevents the degeneration you often see with naive "unlikelihood" objectives (which uniformly push down dispreferred completions regardless of current model beliefs).

## Why this avoids actor–critic variance and baseline traps

If you instead follow the reward‑then‑RL route, you end up optimizing an objective equivalent to

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\Bigl[
r_\phi(x,y) - \beta\log\sum_{y'}\pi_\text{ref}(y'\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r_\phi(x,y')\bigr)
- \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}
\Bigr].
\]

The middle term,
\[
f(r_\phi,\pi_\text{ref},\beta)(x) := \beta\log\sum_{y'}\pi_\text{ref}(y'\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r_\phi(x,y')\bigr),
\]
is the log‑partition / soft value term for the reward model; it depends only on $x$ but it must be estimated (or cancelled) in a policy gradient. Actor–critic methods typically handle this term by training a separate value head or subtracting a baseline (Monte‑Carlo estimate, learned critic, etc.). Those approximations introduce variance and bias, and learning a stable critic can be difficult in practice — contributing to the well‑known instability of RLHF implementations based on PPO.

DPO avoids this whole problem by baking the normalization into the policy parameterization: we never need to estimate $f(\cdot)$ because it cancels in preference differences, and we optimize a cross‑entropy objective that is differentiable in policy parameters and does not require on‑policy sampling or a learned critic. In short, the reparameterization turns a high‑variance RL problem into a stable supervised learning problem (binary classification on preference labels).

## Practical remarks and limitations

- The reference policy matters. DPO requires a choice of $\pi_\text{ref}$ (commonly the SFT model used to generate the comparison pairs). The implicit reward is the log‑ratio against that reference, and $\beta$ controls how strongly the learned policy can diverge from the reference.

- DPO is derived under Bradley–Terry / Plackett–Luce preference assumptions. The paper shows the same change‑of‑variables works for the more general Plackett–Luce family as well.

- Expressivity is not lost. Theorem 1 in the paper proves that for any reward in a given equivalence class there exists a policy‑parameterized representative of the form $r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$, so DPO can represent any reward class that matters for preferences and the constrained RL optimum.

- Empirically (reported in the paper), DPO produces a better or comparable tradeoff between reward and KL than PPO, and matches or improves performance on summarization and single‑turn dialogue tasks while being simpler and more stable to train. The paper also discusses remaining questions: out‑of‑distribution generalization, behavior of reward over‑optimization in the direct setting, and scaling to very large models.

## Takeaway

DPO is not a hack — it is an algebraic identity plus a modeling choice. Because preferences are invariant to $x$‑dependent additives in reward, one can choose the canonical representative of each reward class that makes the KL‑constrained optimum analytically tractable. Reparameterizing rewards by the policy log‑ratio yields a simple, exact MLE objective for pairwise preferences that (a) preserves the expressive power of the original preference model, and (b) removes the need for high‑variance actor–critic machinery. The result is a direct, stable, and computationally cheaper route for aligning language models to human preferences.
