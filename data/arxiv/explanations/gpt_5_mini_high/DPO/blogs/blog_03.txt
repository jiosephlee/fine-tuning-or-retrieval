# How It Works: Turning Policies Into Rewards (and Back Again)

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is a neat re-framing of the usual RLHF pipeline that gets rid of two expensive pieces: an explicit reward model and the reinforcement learning loop. The core idea is simple: use a classic preference model (Bradley–Terry), rewrite the reward in terms of the optimal policy, and then directly train the policy with a binary classification loss. In practice this means the language model itself implicitly defines the reward via log‑probability ratios relative to a reference model, and updates are automatically weighted by how “wrong” the model currently is. Below I walk through the main steps, the math that makes it work, and the intuition for why it’s both stable and effective.

## The setup: RLHF in two lines

Standard RLHF proceeds in three stages: (1) supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) produces a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, (2) collect pairwise human preferences over pairs of model completions $(y_1,y_2)$ for prompts $x$, and (3) fit a reward model and run RL to optimize the policy. The RL objective typically targeted in RLHF is

$ \displaystyle \max_{\pi_\theta} \; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[ r_\phi(x,y) \big] - \beta \, \mathrm{D_{KL}}\!\big[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big], $

where the KL term prevents the policy from drifting too far from the reference and $\beta$ controls that conservativeness. Training a separate reward model $r_\phi$ and then an RL fine‑tuning stage introduces sampling, value‑baseline estimation, and optimization instability.

## Preferences: Bradley–Terry and what it buys us

A very common model for pairwise human comparisons is Bradley–Terry. If $r^*(x,y)$ is the (unknown) latent score that drives human choices, Bradley–Terry says

$ \displaystyle p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) \;=\; \frac{\exp\big(r^*(x,y_1)\big)}{\exp\big(r^*(x,y_1)\big)+\exp\big(r^*(x,y_2)\big)} \;=\; \sigma\!\big(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)\big), $

where $\sigma$ is the logistic function. The key property: preferences depend only on differences of $r^*$, so $r^*$ is only identified up to adding any function of $x$.

## The critical identity: optimal policy has a closed form

For the KL‑constrained objective above, the optimal policy for a given reward $r$ admits a closed form:

$ \displaystyle \pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Big), $

with $Z(x)=\sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\big(r(x,y)/\beta\big)$ the normalizer. Rearranging this gives a reparameterization of the reward in terms of the policy:

$ \displaystyle r(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \beta \log Z(x). $

Because Bradley–Terry depends only on reward differences, the $\beta\log Z(x)$ term cancels when we form $r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$. Substituting yields a neat expression of the human preference probability in terms of the optimal policy:

$ \displaystyle p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x) \;=\; \sigma\!\Big(\beta\big[\log\frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} - \log\frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}\big]\Big). $

So — preferences can be written directly as a function of the policy (and the reference), no explicit $r$ needed.

## DPO: optimize the policy directly with a classification loss

The change of variables above turns preference learning into a straightforward maximum likelihood problem for the policy. Given a dataset of preference triples $\mathcal{D}=\{(x,y_w,y_l)\}$ (where $y_w$ is preferred to $y_l$), DPO minimizes the binary cross‑entropy loss

$ \displaystyle \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\text{ref}}) \;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\Big[\log \sigma\!\big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\big)\Big]. $

That is: score each completion by its log‑probability ratio relative to the reference, multiply the difference by $\beta$, and treat it as the logit for the pairwise preference. Train the policy to maximize the likelihood of observed pairwise outcomes. No explicit reward model, no RL updates, no sampling from the policy during training.

## What each update actually does (and why the weighting matters)

Differentiate the DPO loss to see the update direction. Define the implicit model score

$ \displaystyle \hat r_\theta(x,y) \;=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}. $

Then

$ \displaystyle \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} \;=\; -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\Big[\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\Big]. $

Two points to take away:

- The gradient increases probability of the preferred completion $y_w$ and decreases probability of the dispreferred $y_l$ via the usual log‑prob gradients.
- Crucially, each pair is weighted by $\sigma(\hat r(y_l)-\hat r(y_w))$, which is large when the current implicit score ranks the dispreferred higher than the preferred (i.e., the model is “wrong” about that pair) and small when the pair is already correctly ordered.

That adaptive, per‑pair weight is the stabilizing ingredient. A naive unlikelihood-style objective that simply pushes down $y_l$ and up $y_w$ with fixed coefficients can cause degeneration (mode collapse or overcorrection). DPO focuses effort where the implicit reward (the log‑ratio) disagrees with human feedback and peters out on pairs already handled well.

## The practical pipeline, in plain steps

- Start with a reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Usually this is an SFT model; if SFT is not available, the paper recommends fitting $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by maximizing likelihood on the preferred completions in the preference dataset.
- Collect (or reuse) a dataset of preference pairs $(x,y_w,y_l)$ where $y_w\succ y_l$.
- Minimize $\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}$ over the policy parameters $\theta$ with your favorite supervised optimizer (no RL loop, no on‑policy sampling).
- At inference, sample from the tuned policy; the hyperparameter $\beta$ controls the reward vs KL tradeoff (larger $\beta$ means more conservative — the policy stays closer to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$).

Because training is just maximum likelihood with a special per‑pair score, DPO is simpler to implement and much cheaper to run than full RLHF.

## Why this is theoretically sound

Preferences only determine reward up to addition of a function of $x$ (because only differences matter). The paper shows that every reward class consistent with Bradley–Terry has a representative of the form

$ \displaystyle r(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} $

for some policy $\pi$. In other words, DPO’s reparameterization does not shrink the class of representable reward functions (it just picks a convenient representative where the partition function is normalized). Consequently, optimizing the DPO loss recovers the same optimal policy that you would obtain by first fitting a reward and then solving the KL‑constrained RL problem — but without the two‑stage hassle.

## How this compares to actor‑critic / PPO style RLHF

Actor‑critic methods (including PPO variants used in RLHF) have to estimate a value/baseline or otherwise handle the partition function / soft value. That introduces variance and instability: many tricks (reward normalization, learned value function, baselines) are used to stabilize training. DPO sidesteps those issues: by parameterizing the reward in terms of the policy and writing the preference likelihood in closed form, there is no separate reward estimation or value learning stage, and the training objective reduces to a well‑behaved cross‑entropy loss.

## Empirical highlights (from the paper)

- In a controlled sentiment task (IMDB), DPO produced a strictly better reward vs. KL frontier than PPO, even outperforming PPO that had access to ground‑truth rewards.
- On Reddit TL;DR summarization (human preference data), DPO achieved about a 61% win rate against reference summaries (at a good sampling temperature), compared to ~57% for PPO at its best temperature; DPO was also more robust to sampling temperature.
- In single‑turn dialogue (Anthropic HH), DPO improved over simple supervised fine‑tuning on preferred completions and performed comparably to computationally expensive Best‑of‑N baselines.
- DPO converged quickly in practice and scaled to models up to 6B parameters in the experiments reported.

(These are the empirical findings as reported in the paper; see the original work for full plots and experimental setup.)

## Limitations and open questions

The paper notes several places for further work. Initial OOD experiments suggest DPO generalizes at least as well as PPO in some settings, but more study is needed. Questions remain about reward over‑optimization in the direct parameterization, scaling to much larger models, and how to best elicit automated evaluations (the paper used GPT‑4 as a proxy and validated it against human judgments). Also, DPO still relies on collecting pairwise preferences (human or synthetic), so front‑end data collection remains important.

## Takeaways

- DPO converts the RLHF problem into a supervised maximum likelihood problem by using Bradley–Terry plus the closed‑form relationship between reward and optimal policy.
- The policy implicitly encodes a reward via $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$, and preference likelihoods depend only on differences of these log‑ratios.
- Training is a simple binary‑cross‑entropy over pairs, with a dynamic per‑pair weight that concentrates updates where the model disagrees with humans — which improves stability and prevents degeneration.
- Empirically, DPO matches or exceeds PPO‑based RLHF on several tasks while being far simpler and cheaper to run.

If you want a compact experiment to try: take an SFT model as $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, collect or reuse a small preference dataset, implement the DPO loss above, and fine‑tune the policy with standard supervised optimizers. No rollout buffers, no value networks, and no PPO hyperparameter search.
