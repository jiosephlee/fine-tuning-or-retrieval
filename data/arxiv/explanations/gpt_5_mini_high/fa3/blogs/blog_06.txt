\title{Blogs about the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

# Do the Numbers Back It Up? FlashAttention-3 vs FlashAttention-2, cuDNN, and Triton

## TL;DR
FlashAttention-3 (FA3) delivers the headline claims in the paper: on an NVIDIA H100 (Hopper) GPU it gives roughly 1.5–2.0× speedup over FlashAttention-2 (FA2) in FP16 forward (up to 740 TFLOPs/s, ~75% of H100 peak), and about 1.5–1.75× in the backward pass. With FP8 Tensor Cores enabled, FA3 reaches close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s. For medium and long sequences (≈1k tokens and above) FA3 is competitive with — and for many FP16 cases faster than — an H100-optimized cuDNN attention; compared to a standard attention implementation FA3 can be 3–16× faster. On accuracy, FP16 FA3 matches FA2 (and improves on a baseline standard attention) because softmax/rescaling is kept in higher precision; FP8 FA3 with block quantization + incoherent processing cuts FP8 error by about 2.6× vs. a per-tensor FP8 baseline in outlier settings.

## What they measured (short)
Benchmarks were run on an H100 80GB SXM5 GPU. Typical experimental setup in the paper:

- Sequence lengths swept from 512 up to 16k (batch size adjusted so total tokens ≈16k).
- Hidden dim = 2048; head dimensions tested = 64, 128, 256 (so 32, 16, 8 heads respectively).
- Forward FLOPs computed as $4 \cdot \text{seqlen}^2 \cdot \text{head\_dim} \cdot \text{nheads}$, halved for causal masking. Backward FLOPs multiplied by 2.5 (accounting for recomputation and additional matmuls).
- Comparisons included: a standard (HBM-materializing) attention implementation, FlashAttention-2 (PyTorch), FA2 implemented in Triton (H100-specific), and cuDNN’s H100-optimized attention.
- FP8 forward-pass timings were reported as well.

Numerical error was measured as RMSE against an FP64 reference on inputs sampled to simulate outliers: each entry drawn from $\mathcal{N}(0,1) + \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.001)\cdot\mathcal{N}(0,100)$.

## Headline performance results
- FP16 forward: FA3 is about 1.5–2.0× faster than FA2 across many settings, reaching peaks of ~740 TFLOPs/s (about 75% of the H100 matmul peak reported in the paper).
- FP16 backward: FA3 shows ~1.5–1.75× speedups over FA2.
- FP8 forward: FA3 can reach close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s on H100 in favorable settings.
- Compared to a standard attention implementation FA3 can be 3–16× faster (depends on sequence length / head size).
- Compared to cuDNN: for medium and long sequences (≥1k) FA3 FP16 often outperforms cuDNN; FP8 is competitive but depends on head dimension and masking — e.g., for head dim 64 FA3 FP8 tends to be ahead, for 128/256 it is at-par for non-causal and behind with causal masking (paper’s reported nuance).

These are aggregate statements from the paper’s benchmark suite; individual config curves (seqlen × headdim) determine the exact crossover points.

## Where the wins come from (quick, practical explanation)
FA3 combines three algorithmic/hardware-aware moves that together produce the measured gains.

1. Producer–consumer asynchrony (warp-specialization and ping-pong scheduling)
   - FA3 splits the warps in a CTA into producer warps (issuing asynchronous TMA loads into shared memory) and consumer warps (issuing asynchronous WGMMA Tensor Core matmuls). This lets data movement (TMA) and compute (WGMMA) run concurrently and improves occupancy and latency hiding.
   - Ping-pong-style scheduling staggers warpgroups so softmax work from one group gets naturally scheduled while another group’s Tensor Cores run, using barriers to steer the schedule.

2. Interleaving block-wise GEMMs and softmax (intra-warpgroup pipelining)
   - The inner loop is turned into a small pipeline: while one WGMMA computes the next $\mathbf{S}$ block ($\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$), the implementation overlaps the softmax and the $\tilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{V}$ WGMMA from the previous block.
   - This 2-stage (and optionally 3-stage) pipelining reduces the impact of lower-throughput components like exponentials and rescales by letting them execute concurrently with Tensor Core matmuls. The trade-off is extra register buffering (e.g., holding $\mathbf{S}_{\text{cur}}$ and $\mathbf{S}_{\text{next}}$) and more careful barrier placement; the compiler also must preserve the intended order.
   - In the paper’s ablation, these asynchronous/pipelining changes raised throughput in one tested config from ~570 TFLOPs to ~661 TFLOPs, demonstrating that the overlap pays off.

3. Hardware-accelerated low-precision GEMM (FP8) plus layout and accuracy fixes
   - On Hopper the FP8 Tensor Cores can double matmul throughput versus FP16, but they require operands in a k-major layout and place constraints on accumulator layouts.
   - FA3 implements in-kernel tile transposes (LDSM/STSM-based) and register-level permutations to meet these layout needs without extra global-memory transposes.
   - To address FP8 quantization error, FA3 uses block quantization (one FP8 scale per block) and incoherent processing (multiply $\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K}$ by a cheap random orthogonal transform so outliers are “spread out”). The orthogonal transform satisfies $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top = I$ so attention scores are unchanged, and it can be implemented cheaply (Hadamard × diagonal) and fused with other pre-attention ops.
   - The net result: near-doubling of matmul throughput from FP8 and a workable accuracy/latency trade-off for many long-context cases.

## Accuracy summary
- FP16: FA3 matches FA2 in numerical error and improves over a standard attention implementation in the paper’s outlier tests. The main reason is that FA3 (like FA2) keeps intermediate softmax/rescaling accumulators in higher precision (FP32), which reduces numeric drift. The paper reports around 1.7× lower RMSE (relative to the standard HBM-materializing implementation) for FA2/FA3 in FP16 in the outlier input regime.
- FP8: a naive per-tensor FP8 quantization baseline suffers significant error in the presence of outliers. FA3’s block quantization + incoherent processing reduces that FP8 numerical error by about 2.6× relative to that baseline in the paper’s tests. FP8 still has larger absolute rounding/error risk than FP16, but the proposed mitigations materially close the gap while delivering big throughput gains.

Numerical comparisons used RMSE to an FP64 reference on synthetic inputs designed to include rare large-magnitude outliers (as described above).

## Limitations and caveats
- FP8 FA3 is powerful for long sequences, but the FP8 kernel in the paper is not fully persistent / load-balanced; the authors note this partly explains why FP8 FA3 is not always faster than cuDNN for small sequences or with causal masking.
- The pipelined async design increases register usage; deeper pipelining hurts tile size choices and may force trade-offs.
- The approach relies on Hopper-specific async primitives (WGMMA and TMA) and FP8 constraints; the general ideas transfer to other accelerators that expose comparable async/memory/matrix units, but the exact engineering must be adapted.
- FP8 still requires careful tuning to avoid accuracy regressions in training or sensitive inference workloads; the paper focuses on forward-pass timing and numerical RMSE under a particular outlier model.

## Bottom line / practical takeaways
- If you care about long-context Transformer attention on Hopper/H100, FA3 is a substantial improvement over FA2 in both forward and backward speed, and it meaningfully narrows the gap to vendor-optimized kernels (and in many long-sequence FP16 cases overtakes cuDNN).
- The biggest wins come from treating data-movement and Tensor Core matmuls as asynchronous producers/consumers and overlapping comparatively slow elementwise/softmax work with high-throughput GEMMs.
- FP8 offers a large throughput win but needs layout engineering and quantization strategies (block scales + incoherent transforms) to be practical; FA3 provides an effective recipe for that trade-off.
- The implementation details matter: register pressure, compiler instruction ordering, and in-kernel transpose/permutation strategies are central to hitting the reported TFLOPs.
