\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

Introduction and Motivation

# Quadratic cost of attention with sequence length and implications for LLMs and long-context tasks
The self-attention layer at the core of the Transformer computes interactions between every pair of tokens in a sequence. Formally, for a single attention head with query, key, and value matrices $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ (sequence length $N$, head dimension $d$) the forward computation is
\[
\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top,\qquad
\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S}),\qquad
\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V},
\]
where $\alpha$ is typically $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{d}$ and softmax is applied row-wise. The matrix $\mathbf{S}$ is $N\times N$, and computing it requires $O(N^2 d)$ arithmetic (the dominant cost) while storing intermediate attention matrices incurs $O(N^2)$ memory. Because both compute and memory scale quadratically with sequence length, attention quickly becomes the dominant cost as $N$ grows.

This quadratic dependence means that naively increasing context length (larger $N$) rapidly increases runtime and memory requirements, which is a hard limit for both training and inference of large language models (LLMs) and other Transformer-based systems. The quadratic bottleneck is therefore the primary engineering and algorithmic challenge when pushing toward long-context workloads: any practical solution must either reduce the constant factors and memory traffic of exact attention or change the algorithmic scaling.

# Opportunities unlocked by long-context: multi-document reasoning, codebases, high-resolution modalities
Despite the cost, enabling long contexts is highly desirable because it unlocks new modeling capabilities and applications. With longer context windows, models can reason over multiple long documents at once, enabling tasks such as multi-document comparison, synthesis, and long-form question answering. In code modeling, longer contexts permit reasoning across large codebases and many files, which is critical for program understanding, refactoring, and agentic workflows involving many interdependent files. For modalities beyond text, long-context attention is the foundation for handling high-resolution images, long audio snippets, and multi-minute video, all of which require processing many spatial or temporal tokens to capture global structure.

Practically, these opportunities create strong incentives to push exact attention to larger $N$: approximation techniques can be useful, but many applications demand the fidelity and stability of exact attention. Thus improving the throughput and memory efficiency of exact attention is a high-leverage target for enabling richer capabilities in LLMs and multimodal systems.

# Landscape of speedups: approximate attention, software optimizations, and alternative architectures
Researchers and engineers have pursued three broad strategies to address the attention bottleneck.

One approach is approximation: design attention variants whose computational complexity is sub-quadratic (for example by low-rank approximations, kernelization, sparsity, or locality-based schemes). These methods can drastically reduce costs for very long sequences but trade off exactness and may introduce modeling or numerical issues.

A second approach is software and kernel-level optimization that preserves exact attention while dramatically reducing IO and runtime constants. FlashAttention and its successors exemplify this path: they keep the exact math of attention but restructure computation to minimize slow global memory (HBM) traffic and to exploit the GPU execution model, producing large practical speedups with the same mathematical result.

A third direction is alternative architectures that replace the attention layer with structures that natively scale better (for instance recurrence-inspired designs or other token-mixing primitives). These can achieve different scaling properties but imply architectural changes and different empirical behavior.

FlashAttention-style work sits in the second category: it seeks to get the best possible performance from exact attention by matching algorithm design to hardware realities.

# From FlashAttention to FlashAttention-2: exact, IO-aware, fused kernels
The original FlashAttention introduced a key idea: fuse the steps of attention into a single kernel and perform a local softmax reduction so that the algorithm never materializes the full $N\times N$ intermediate matrices to slow global memory. Concretely, FlashAttention tiles the $Q,K,V$ tensors and computes attention block-by-block while keeping intermediate results in on-chip memory (shared memory and registers), thereby avoiding the expensive reads and writes to HBM that dominate runtime when $N$ is large.

FlashAttention-2 extended this approach by parallelizing also over the sequence-length dimension and structuring the inner loop to run over blocks of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$. That redesign improved occupancy and the distribution of work across GPU threads, increasing utilization relative to naïve implementations. In both FlashAttention and FlashAttention-2 the goal is the same: preserve exact attention while minimizing global-memory IO and mapping the computation to the GPU in an IO-aware, tiled fashion.

# Gap on modern GPUs: low utilization of FlashAttention-2 on H100 despite high GEMM utilization in optimized kernels
Even with the IO-aware design of FlashAttention-2, a significant utilization gap remained on newer GPU architectures. On NVIDIA Hopper H100 hardware, FlashAttention-2 was observed to achieve only about 35% of the peak Tensor-Core throughput, whereas highly optimized general matrix–matrix multiplication (GEMM) kernels can reach 80–90% utilization on the same device. That discrepancy indicates untapped performance potential.

Two classes of reasons explain this gap. First, implementation-level choices matter: FlashAttention-2 (as originally implemented) did not exploit some Hopper-specific instructions and tiling idioms that newer optimized kernels use. Work from other teams (for example vendor kernels and recent projects) shows that using Hopper-specific primitives can significantly speed up attention-like computations.

Second, and more fundamentally, FlashAttention-2 was designed in a synchronous style that does not leverage new hardware trends: modern GPUs provide asynchronous specialized units for matrix multiplication (Tensor Cores exposed via WGMMA) and for high-throughput memory movement (Tensor Memory Accelerator, TMA). They also expose much faster low-precision math (FP8) on Tensor Cores. FlashAttention-2 did not explicitly exploit asynchrony between these units, nor did it design the computation to take advantage of low-precision Tensor Cores—both of which are essential to close the utilization gap on Hopper.

# FlashAttention-3 at a glance: asynchrony, GEMM–softmax overlap, and FP8 with higher accuracy
FlashAttention-3 is an algorithmic and implementation redesign that targets these two sources of unrealized performance. It contributes three complementary ideas that together raise utilization while preserving (or improving) numerical fidelity:

Producer–consumer asynchrony via warp-specialization. Modern GPUs include asynchronous hardware for data movement (TMA) and for matrix multiply (Tensor Cores / WGMMA). FlashAttention-3 partitions the warps inside a CTA into specialized roles: producer warps that stream blocks of $\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}$ from HBM into a circular shared-memory buffer using TMA, and consumer warps that perform the blockwise GEMMs on Tensor Cores. By separating producers and consumers and coordinating access via a small pipeline buffer, producers can issue asynchronous loads without stalling the consumers, while consumers can proceed with WGMMA when data arrives. This producer–consumer software pipelining hides memory latency and instruction-issue stalls more effectively than a synchronous loop.

Hiding softmax under asynchronous block-wise GEMMs. The softmax-related computation (row-wise maximum, exponentials, and scaling) is comparatively low-throughput on current hardware: special functions such as exponential are handled by different units with far less FLOP capacity than Tensor Cores. FlashAttention-3 rearranges and pipelines the inner loop so that the expensive blockwise GEMMs (high-throughput, on Tensor Cores) can run asynchronously while softmax work for other blocks is being performed on the regular CUDA cores or multi-function units. Concretely, a two-stage (or optionally three-stage) pipeline computes $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ for the next block while completing the $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{V}$ multiply for the current block, and uses a “ping-pong” schedule across warpgroups to interleave softmax computation with GEMMs. This overlapping is especially powerful because on H100 the Tensor-Core matmul throughput (e.g., hundreds of TFLOPs) dwarfs the throughput of special-function units (a few TFLOPs), so hiding softmax under GEMMs recovers a large fraction of otherwise idle cycles.

Hardware-accelerated FP8 GEMM with accuracy safeguards. Hopper exposes FP8 Tensor Cores, which can roughly double matmul throughput versus FP16/BF16. To take advantage of FP8, FlashAttention-3 adapts the fused attention kernel so the GEMMs target FP8 Tensor Cores; this requires careful attention to operand layout constraints (FP8 WGMMA accepts only k-major layouts in shared memory) and to accumulator/operand layout conversions inside registers. On the accuracy side, FP8 quantization can introduce substantial error, especially in the presence of outlier activations common in large models. FlashAttention-3 uses two complementary techniques to mitigate this: block quantization (per-block scale factors instead of a single per-tensor scale) and incoherent processing (pre-multiplying $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a fixed random orthogonal transform $\mathbf{M}$ so that entries are “spread out” before quantization). Block quantization is naturally compatible with the blockwise structure of the fused kernel and can be fused with preceding memory-bound operations (e.g., rotary embedding), while incoherent processing can be implemented efficiently as a product of cheap transforms (random sign diagonals and a Hadamard matrix) that cost $O(d\log d)$ and can also be fused into prior steps.

Together, these ideas let FlashAttention-3 both improve raw Tensor-Core utilization and use FP8 hardware without sacrificing numerical robustness.

# Headline results: speedups, utilization, and improved FP8 accuracy
Empirically, FlashAttention-3 demonstrates substantial gains on Hopper-class hardware. In FP16, FlashAttention-3 attains a forward-pass speedup of roughly $1.5$–$2.0\times$ over FlashAttention-2, reaching up to about $740$ TFLOPs/s on an H100 SXM5 (roughly $75\%$ of the device’s theoretical matmul peak in that metric). The backward pass also benefits, with speedups of about $1.5$–$1.75\times$. Targeting the FP8 Tensor Cores yields even larger raw arithmetic throughput, approaching $1.2$ PFLOPs/s in the best cases.

Beyond throughput, FlashAttention-3 maintains or improves numerical fidelity. FP16 FlashAttention-3 keeps the same numerical error as FlashAttention-2 and is more accurate than a standard attention implementation because intermediate results related to softmax rescaling are retained in higher precision (FP32). For FP8, the combined block quantization and incoherent processing strategy reduces numerical error significantly: in the reported experiments FlashAttention-3’s FP8 implementation had about $2.6\times$ lower root-mean-squared error than a baseline FP8 attention that uses per-tensor scaling.

Some additional experimental observations are noteworthy. For medium and long sequences (roughly 1k tokens and above), FlashAttention-3 can even outperform a vendor-optimized library (cuDNN) on H100 for FP16 workloads. FP8 performance is more sensitive to head dimension and causal masking: for example, FlashAttention-3’s FP8 kernel outperforms or matches cuDNN in some head-dimension regimes (it can be ahead for head dimension $64$, and at par for larger head dimensions without causal masking; with causal masking the results depend on the configuration).

Finally, the authors open-sourced FlashAttention-3 and plan to integrate it into common frameworks (PyTorch, Hugging Face) so the broader research and practitioner community can use these gains.

# Summary and practical takeaways
The central message is that preserving exact attention is compatible with very high GPU utilization — but doing so requires matching algorithm design to modern hardware trends. FlashAttention-3 shows that three targeted strategies—(1) exploiting asynchrony with warp-specialization, (2) overlapping softmax work with block GEMMs through pipelining and ping-pong schedules, and (3) safely leveraging FP8 Tensor Cores with blockwise quantization and incoherent transforms—yield large practical speedups and maintain numerical robustness.

For a practitioner or student, the key intuitions are:

- The quadratic scaling in $N$ makes any constant-factor or IO reduction extremely valuable for long-context workloads; attention kernels that minimize HBM traffic and keep work on-chip have a big advantage.
- On modern GPUs, specialized asynchronous units for memory (TMA) and matmul (Tensor Cores / WGMMA) can be used in parallel; exposing that asynchrony in the software (producer/consumer warp roles and circular shared-memory buffers) unlocks otherwise wasted cycles.
- Softmax and other non-matmul parts of attention are low-throughput relative to GEMMs and are best hidden under large matmuls via pipelining across blocks.
- FP8 Tensor Cores offer substantial peak speedups but must be combined with algorithmic measures (per-block scaling, incoherent transforms, and careful layout handling) to avoid large numerical degradation.

FlashAttention-3 demonstrates that a careful, hardware-aware redesign of the fused attention kernel yields large practical wins and brings exact attention much closer to the peak capabilities of modern accelerators, enabling more efficient long-context LLMs and multimodal models. The chapter that follows develops the hardware background and the algorithmic building blocks that make these gains possible.

Attention Fundamentals and Numerical Stability

# Definitions: $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, $\mathbf{V}$, $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$ (row-wise), $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$

The basic building block for attention in a Transformer head is three matrices of input projections, commonly called queries, keys, and values. We write these as $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ where $N$ is the sequence length (number of tokens) and $d$ is the head dimension. Each row of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ corresponds to one token's query, key, and value vectors respectively.

From these we form the pairwise score matrix $\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}$ by taking scaled dot-products between queries and keys:
$\mathbf{S} = \alpha\,\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$,
so the scalar score between query $i$ and key $j$ is $S_{ij} = \alpha \langle q_i, k_j\rangle$. The scalar $\alpha$ is typically set to $1/\sqrt{d}$ in practice; this scale is included in the definition above.

The raw scores are converted row-wise to probabilities with the softmax function: for each row $i$,
$P_{ij} = \dfrac{\exp(S_{ij})}{\sum_{j'} \exp(S_{ij'})}$.
We denote the row-wise softmax as $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$, and each row of $\mathbf{P}$ acts as an attention distribution over the value vectors.

The attention outputs for each query row are then the weighted sums of values:
$\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$,
so row $i$ of $\mathbf{O}$ is $O_i = \sum_j P_{ij} v_j$. Intuitively, attention lets each query gather information from the sequence by computing similarity scores (via $\mathbf{S}$) and then forming a weighted mixture of values.

In multi-head attention (MHA), independent triplets $\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}$ are maintained per head and the same formulas apply per head in parallel. All of these operations (score computation, softmax, and output accumulation) are the fundamental arithmetic and data-access patterns we must understand and optimize.

# Scaling by $1/\sqrt{d}$ and the role of subtracting per-row maxima for stability

The choice of the scalar $\alpha$ matters for numerics. In practice one typically sets $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{d}$. Using a scale factor keeps the magnitudes of dot-product scores in a range more suitable for the subsequent exponential in softmax; without such scaling the values in $\mathbf{S}$ can become large in magnitude as $d$ grows, which makes the exponentials numerically unstable.

Beyond scaling, an important implementation detail for numerical stability when computing softmax is to subtract a per-row maximum before taking exponentials. Concretely, for each row $i$ we compute
$\mathrm{rowmax}_i = \max_j S_{ij}$
and replace the row by $S_{ij} \leftarrow S_{ij} - \mathrm{rowmax}_i$ prior to exponentiating. This shift does not change the normalized softmax outputs because it removes the same constant from every entry in the row; it prevents overflow of $\exp(\cdot)$ on very large positive inputs and reduces underflow for very negative inputs. In practice implementations keep track of the per-row maxima (often denoted $m_i$ in fused or streaming code) and use them both to stabilize the forward computation and to compute a compact log-sum-exp quantity useful to the backward pass.

# Streaming (local) softmax and log-sum-exp tracking without materializing $\mathbf{S}$ or $\mathbf{P}$

A straightforward implementation of attention first forms the full score matrix $\mathbf{S}$ and the full softmax matrix $\mathbf{P}$ in global memory (HBM). For long sequences this is expensive in memory bandwidth and capacity because $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ are $N\times N$ matrices. FlashAttention-style algorithms avoid writing these full $N\times N$ intermediates to global memory by performing a blockwise, streaming computation of softmax and of the final output $\mathbf{O}$. The key is to maintain, for each query row, only a small running state that summarizes all processed key/value blocks so far: a running maximum $m_i$ and a running (unnormalized) accumulator $\ell_i$ (a log-sum-exp helper), together with the partial numerator for $\mathbf{O}$.

The blockwise update used in the streaming (local) softmax proceeds as follows. Process the keys and values in blocks (indexed by $j$). For a particular query block or row $i$, suppose after processing blocks $0,\dots,j-1$ we have stored a running maximum $m_i^{\mathrm{old}}$, a running scalar $\ell_i$ equal to the sum of exponentials under the current base, and a partial numerator $\mathbf{O}_i$ representing the contribution accumulated so far. When a new score block $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}$ (the scores between the fixed query block and the $j$-th key block) becomes available, we compute the blockwise updates:

1. Update the running maximum
$m_i = \max\bigl(m_i^{\mathrm{old}},\,\mathrm{rowmax}(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)})\bigr)$.

2. Form the block unnormalized probabilities relative to the updated base:
$\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} = \exp\!\bigl(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} - m_i\bigr)$
(where the exponential is applied element-wise).

3. Update the running denominator (unnormalized sum)
$\ell_i \leftarrow \exp\!\bigl(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i\bigr)\,\ell_i \;+\; \mathrm{rowsum}\bigl(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}\bigr)$,
and update the numerator (the partial contribution to $\mathbf{O}$)
$\mathbf{O}_i \leftarrow \exp\!\bigl(m_i^{\mathrm{old}} - m_i\bigr)\,\mathbf{O}_i \;+\; \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}\,\mathbf{V}_j$.

The scaling factors $\exp(m_i^{\mathrm{old}}-m_i)$ perform a change of base when the running maximum increases. They convert all previously accumulated terms that were stored relative to $m_i^{\mathrm{old}}$ into the new base $m_i$, so that after the update the invariant is
$\mathbf{O}_i = \sum_{j'\le j} \exp\!\bigl(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j')} - m_i\bigr)\,\mathbf{V}_{j'}$
and $\ell_i = \sum_{j'\le j} \exp\!\bigl(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j')} - m_i\bigr)$.
After processing all key/value blocks, the normalized attention output is obtained by dividing element-wise:
$\mathbf{O}_i \leftarrow \ell_i^{-1}\,\mathbf{O}_i$,
and one can also record the final log normalizer $L_i = m_i + \log(\ell_i)$ (a compact summary of the row-wise log-sum-exp).

This blockwise scheme computes exactly the same outputs as forming $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ explicitly, but it only keeps a few vectors per query row in fast on-chip storage (shared memory/registers) while streaming blocks of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ through the kernel. In the paper's pseudocode these variables are named $m_i$, $\ell_i$, $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$, and $\mathbf{O}_i$, and the local-softmax update corresponds directly to the lines that update $m_i$, form $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$, update $\ell_i$, and rescale $\mathbf{O}_i$.

Because only small per-row summaries are kept, this strategy eliminates the costly global-memory reads/writes required to materialize $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ and enables a single fused kernel that produces $\mathbf{O}$ directly from streamed $\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}$ tiles.

# Backward pass structure: $\mathbf{dV}$, $\mathbf{dP}$, $\mathbf{dS}$ via the softmax Jacobian, $\mathbf{dQ}$, $\mathbf{dK}$; the need to recompute statistics

When the attention output $\mathbf{O}$ is used in a loss $\phi$, backpropagation requires gradients with respect to the inputs $\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}$. Using the notation $\mathbf{d}(-) = \partial \phi / \partial(-)$, the backward pass for a single head is expressed by the following chain of matrix operations (these are the exact formulas given in the paper):

- $\mathbf{dV} = \mathbf{P}^\top \mathbf{dO}$,

- $\mathbf{dP} = \mathbf{dO}\,\mathbf{V}^\top$,

- $\mathbf{dS} = \mathrm{dsoftmax}(\mathbf{dP})$,

- $\mathbf{dQ} = \alpha\,\mathbf{dS}\,\mathbf{K}$,

- $\mathbf{dK} = \alpha\,\mathbf{dS}^\top \mathbf{Q}$.

The function $\mathrm{dsoftmax}(\cdot)$ applies the Jacobian of the (row-wise) softmax to each row of $\mathbf{dP}$. For a single vector $p=\mathrm{softmax}(s)$ and a perturbation $\mathrm{d}p$, the corresponding change in $s$ is
$\mathrm{d}s = \bigl(\mathrm{diag}(p) - p\,p^\top\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}p$,
and this same formula is applied row-wise to produce $\mathbf{dS}$ from $\mathbf{dP}$.

A practical consequence of running a fused forward that does not materialize $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ is that the backward pass typically needs to recompute some of the intermediate quantities it would otherwise read from memory. The paper notes this explicitly in the benchmarking setup: the forward pass uses two matrix-multiply operations (the two GEMMs corresponding to computing $\mathbf{S}$ and then $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$), while the backward pass—because of either additional gradient matmuls or recomputations needed when intermediates were not stored—implements approximately five matmuls in total. Thus the backward computational work is modeled as roughly $2.5\times$ the forward FLOPs in their benchmarks. In practice, implementations such as FlashAttention trade storage (writing $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ to HBM) for recomputation of these matrices during the backward pass; fused algorithms choose recomputation to avoid the large memory traffic of storing full $N\times N$ intermediates.

The forward fusion still leaves a small piece of state to be written out per query block: for example the final log-normalizer $L_i = m_i + \log(\ell_i)$ is typically written to global memory so it can be used (or recomputed cheaply) during the backward pass.

# Causal masking and its effect on FLOPs and dataflow

Causal masking enforces a strict autoregressive structure in which token $i$ may only attend to tokens $j\le i$. This makes the attention matrix $\mathbf{P}$ (and prior to normalization, $\mathbf{S}$) lower-triangular rather than dense. One immediate practical effect is that the number of score/value entries to compute is reduced: empirically and in the paper's benchmarking conventions, causal masking reduces the number of computed attention entries by roughly a factor of two, and therefore the counted forward FLOPs are divided by two to reflect that fewer pairwise interactions are needed.

From an algorithmic/dataflow point of view, causal masking changes which blocks of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ contribute to a given query block, and implementations that stream in blocks must ensure they skip or mask out the future blocks for each query. This changes tiling decisions and can affect performance because the load balance and the shape of the per-iteration work change when the triangular mask is applied.

# Variants: multi-head, multi-query attention (MQA), grouped-query attention (GQA)

The single-head formulas above are the primitive building blocks for several commonly used attention variants. In multi-head attention (MHA), the model runs the same attention computation in parallel over several independent heads; each head maintains its own $\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}$ and produces head-specific outputs which are later concatenated. The matrix computations for different heads are independent and naturally parallelize across batch and head dimensions.

Multi-query attention (MQA) and grouped-query attention (GQA) are memory-conscious variants that reduce the number of distinct $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ projections. In MQA, for example, multiple query heads may share a single set of keys and values; in GQA keys and values are shared within groups of heads. Implementations that avoid duplicating shared $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ in global memory must adjust tensor indexing and tiling so the shared tensors are streamed and reused efficiently. The paper follows the approach of FlashAttention-2 for these variants: one modifies indexing rather than duplicating $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ in HBM, thereby saving memory bandwidth and capacity.

# Complexity: $O(N^2 d)$ compute and memory-traffic considerations in standard implementations

Attention is dominated by pairwise interactions between all queries and keys, which yields quadratic scaling in the sequence length $N$. The core arithmetic work scales as $O(N^2 d)$ because for each of the $N^2$ query-key pairs one must perform a $d$-dimensional dot product (or, viewed at matrix level, $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ and then $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$ each cost on the order of $N^2 d$ operations).

For benchmarking the forward pass the paper uses a practical FLOP count formula:
$\text{FLOPs}_{\text{forward}} = 4 \cdot \text{seqlen}^2 \cdot \text{head\_dim} \cdot \text{num\_heads}$,
and with causal masking they divide this number by $2$ to account for the triangular sparsity. For the backward pass they multiply the forward FLOPs by $2.5$ (this accounts for the additional matmuls and recomputation needed when intermediates are not stored).

Memory traffic is the other critical resource. A naïve ("standard") implementation materializes $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ in global memory, which means a very large $O(N^2)$ read/write working set that stresses HBM bandwidth and capacity. FlashAttention-style methods are explicitly designed to avoid these intermediate writes/reads by streaming tiles of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ into fast on-chip memory (shared memory and registers), performing the necessary computations and softmax normalization locally, and writing only the final outputs (and a compact per-row log-sum-exp) back to global memory. This fusion reduces global memory traffic and is the central practical technique used to push attention performance for long-context models.

Closing summary

The formulas and invariants above summarize the mathematics and the key numerical considerations behind single-head and multi-head attention. Two implementation-level principles are central for efficient and stable attention: (1) scale dot-products appropriately (the $\alpha$ factor) and subtract per-row maxima before exponentiation to preserve numerical stability; and (2) avoid materializing $N\times N$ intermediates by using a streaming (local) softmax that maintains per-row statistics ($m_i$, $\ell_i$) and partial numerators, thereby trading a small amount of on-chip state and some recomputation in the backward pass for large reductions in global-memory traffic. These are the ideas used directly by FlashAttention-style kernels to enable attention at long sequence lengths.

GPU Architecture Primer for Attention (Hopper-Centric)

# Memory hierarchy: GMEM/HBM, L2 cache, SMEM (shared memory), registers; bandwidth and latency implications

Modern GPUs organize data storage in multiple levels whose capacities and bandwidths trade off against one another. At the top (largest capacity, lowest bandwidth) is global memory (GMEM, often HBM) — the off‑chip DRAM visible to all streaming multiprocessors (SMs). GMEM is backed by an on‑chip L2 cache that opportunistically holds recently used lines. Closer to the compute units, each SM provides a programmer‑managed on‑chip scratchpad called shared memory (SMEM). Finally, the finest and fastest storage is the register file (per‑thread registers, sometimes called RMEM).

A useful way to remember these relationships is that capacity and bandwidth move in opposite directions: large memories (GMEM) have high capacity but relatively low bandwidth/latency, while small memories (registers) have tiny capacity but very high bandwidth and low latency. In symbols (informal): $\text{cap}(\text{GMEM}) \gg \text{cap}(\text{L2}) \gg \text{cap}(\text{SMEM}) \gg \text{cap}(\text{Reg})$, and the bandwidth ordering is the reverse. This hierarchical structure matters for attention algorithms because the quadratic nature of attention in sequence length makes any HBM read/write of intermediate $N\times N$ matrices extremely costly. FlashAttention‑style algorithms therefore try to keep intermediate results in SMEM or registers and avoid writing large temporaries back to GMEM.

SMEM is not a cache in the hardware‑managed sense; it is explicitly controlled by the programmer and is highly banked, which gives predictable and high aggregate bandwidth when accessed correctly. Registers are private to threads and are the fastest storage; the paper notes the convention that each thread has at most 256 registers available on Hopper (an implementation limit relevant to register pressure and spilling). Because SMEM and registers are on‑chip, moving data between GMEM and SMEM (and filling SMEM from GMEM) becomes a critical activity to schedule efficiently — both for performance and for reducing latency that would otherwise break the deliberate locality of attention kernels.

# Thread hierarchy: threads, warps (32), warpgroups (4 warps), CTAs (threadblocks), clusters, grids

GPU execution is organized as hierarchies of parallelism that map to the hardware scheduler. The smallest execution unit is a thread. Threads are grouped into warps (32 threads per warp on NVIDIA GPUs). On Hopper there is one additional level commonly used by certain Tensor Core instructions: warpgroups, which are sets of 4 contiguous warps (so a warpgroup covers 128 threads). Warps/warpgroups are assembled into cooperative thread arrays (CTAs or threadblocks), CTAs are placed onto SMs, and collections of CTAs form a grid. Hopper also exposes threadblock clusters (CTAs in the same cluster are co‑scheduled on the same GPC), which has implications for certain on‑chip resources and locality.

Two relationships from this hierarchy are especially important for attention kernels. First, SMEM is accessible to all threads in the same CTA; that means all warps within a CTA can read/write the same shared tiles without going back to GMEM. Second, warps assigned to the same CTA are co‑scheduled on the same SM, and CTAs in the same cluster get co‑scheduled in a way that can enforce locality for higher‑level synchronization. These constraints guide how attention tiling maps queries, keys, and values to CTA‑local work.

Because some GPU instructions and hardware units operate at warpgroup granularity (for example, the Hopper WGMMA Tensor Core instruction), kernel authors commonly organize work so that warpgroups cooperate on a tile and use SMEM to share operands. The practical upshot is that a kernel designer must balance the CTA size, warpgroup usage, and how many registers each thread consumes (per‑thread registers are limited) in order to avoid register spilling and to attain high SM occupancy.

# Specialized hardware: Tensor Cores (WGMMA), CUDA cores, multi-function units (exp/log), TMA for async GMEM↔SMEM copies

Hopper exposes several specialized functional units that accelerate different parts of machine‑learning workloads; each has different performance characteristics and programming interfaces.

Tensor Cores are the matrix‑multiply‑accumulate engines. On Hopper these are accessed via a warpgroup‑wide instruction called WGMMA: it is invoked by a warpgoup and can source inputs directly from SMEM. WGMMA delivers extremely high throughput for dense matrix multiplications (the paper reports device‑level FP16 matmul throughput on H100 SXM5 on the order of hundreds of TFLOPs).

CUDA cores (the general FP/INT ALUs) handle fine‑grain scalar and arithmetic work not suited to Tensor Cores. Special functions (e.g., exponential, logarithm) are handled by multi‑function units (MFUs or special function units). These units are essential for softmax, which requires exponentials and reductions, but they have far lower aggregate throughput than Tensor Cores.

For moving blocks of data between GMEM and SMEM, Hopper provides the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA), a dedicated hardware unit that performs asynchronous bulk copies. TMA allows a kernel to launch a GMEM→SMEM transfer and continue executing other instructions while the transfer proceeds in the background (subject to synchronization when the data is actually needed).

There are also warp‑level SMEM↔register collective instructions, LDSM (load matrix from SMEM to RMEM) and STSM (store matrix from RMEM to SMEM). These operate at warp granularity and can move tiles efficiently with low register overhead; the PTX documentation describes them as copying $8\times 8$ matrices with 16‑bit entries, and on Hopper they can be adapted (by packing) to work with FP8 operands as two 8‑bit entries per 16‑bit slot.

Taken together, these specialized units form a pipeline: TMA brings tiles into SMEM asynchronously, WGMMA consumes tiles in SMEM with very high throughput, LDSM/STSM let a warp rearrange tiles between SMEM and registers efficiently, while MFUs carry out the exponentials and other special operations, but at much lower peak throughput. Effective kernels align algorithmic structure to this heterogeneous hardware mix.

# Asynchronous execution and warp‑specialization: producers for data movement, consumers for compute

A central observation exploited by FlashAttention‑3 is that Hopper’s TMA and WGMMA are both asynchronous relative to the thread scheduler: you can issue data transfers and matrix multiplies that proceed independently, and then later synchronize before using their results. This hardware asynchrony opens the door to warp‑specialized software pipelining: partition the warps within a CTA into producer warps (whose primary role is to issue TMA loads and perform any necessary SMEM bookkeeping) and consumer warps (whose primary role is to run WGMMA Tensor Core operations and the heavier compute).

Producer warps can use only a few registers (they mainly launch DMA‑style transfers), while consumer warps need many registers to keep accumulators, temporary tiles, and to schedule back‑to‑back WGMMA instructions. By assigning responsibilities this way, a CTA becomes a small producer‑consumer pipeline: producers stream blocks of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ into a circular buffer in SMEM, and consumers pull tiles from that buffer and perform the dense operations and reductions necessary for attention. Because TMA and WGMMA run asynchronously, the producer warps can be filling future stages of the SMEM buffer at the same time consumer warps compute on already‑loaded tiles.

This division of labor has two practical advantages. First, it improves the ability of the kernel to hide both memory latency and instruction‑issue latencies: while the Tensor Cores are grinding on one block, TMA can be bringing the next block into SMEM. Second, it simplifies register allocation and instruction scheduling: warps that are only moving data do not consume registers reserved for the numerical pipeline, and compiler scheduling can produce tighter instruction streams for compute warps. FlashAttention‑3 uses synchronization points (e.g., barrier instructions like bar.sync) to coordinate the pipeline — for example, employing a “ping‑pong” scheduling pattern that purposefully orders GEMMs from one warpgoup before the GEMMs of another so that the exponentials and reductions from the former are overlapped with Tensor Core work from the latter.

# Dynamic register reallocation with setmaxnreg to favor compute warps

Hopper provides a mechanism to change how the physical register file is partitioned among warpgroups within a CTA. The PTX primitive setmaxnreg lets the kernel specify a maximum number of registers assigned to warpgroups so that threads performing Tensor Core work can acquire more RMEM while data‑movement warps can be given fewer registers. The paper highlights this as a practical lever: producers issuing TMA need only a small number of registers (often one thread per producer warp is enough), whereas consumer warps issuing many WGMMA operations benefit from a larger register allotment.

Why does this matter? Register pressure matters for performance because if a thread requires more registers than allotted it will spill to SMEM (or L1), incurring extra traffic and latency. By dynamically reallocating the register budget so that rich compute warps have a larger share of the register file, FlashAttention‑3 reduces spilling in the compute path and improves sustained Tensor Core utilization. In short, setmaxnreg is an important knob to tailor the physical resource layout to the producer/consumer partitioning the kernel uses.

# Throughput contrast: matmul TFLOPs vs special function unit TFLOPs and why softmax becomes a bottleneck

Hopper’s Tensor Cores deliver orders of magnitude more peak FLOP throughput than the multi‑function units that implement exponentials and other special functions. The paper gives a concrete illustration on H100 SXM5: FP16 Tensor Core matmul throughput is reported around 989 TFLOPs, while special function throughput (exponentials, etc.) is around 3.9 TFLOPs. This enormous gap has direct implications for attention.

Softmax involves a mix of operations: the bulk of attention’s arithmetic is in the two matrix multiplies ($\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}$ and $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$), which map well to Tensor Cores. But softmax also requires pointwise exponentials and reductions (row‑wise maximums, normalizations), which are handled by MFUs at much lower throughput. The paper provides a practical example: for a head dimension of $d=128$, there are roughly $512\times$ more matmul FLOPs than exponential operations in the forward pass computation, while the exponential MFU has about $256\times$ lower throughput than the Tensor Core capacity for matmul in that configuration. The combination of many more required exponentials and far lower exponential throughput implies that exponentials can occupy a significant fraction of the execution time (the paper estimates exponentials could consume about 50% of the cycles in that example).

The situation can get worse with FP8: Tensor Core throughput roughly doubles compared to FP16 on Hopper, but the MFU throughput for exponentials does not change. That increases the imbalance, making the softmax (the non‑GEMM part) a more severe bottleneck unless its work is overlapped with GEMM execution. This is precisely why FlashAttention‑3 designs its pipelines to hide the low‑throughput softmax work under the asynchronous GEMM schedule provided by WGMMA and TMA.

# Low precision formats on Hopper: FP8 Tensor Cores vs FP16/BF16; operand layout constraints (k‑major for FP8)

Hopper introduces hardware support for FP8 Tensor Core operations, which in favorable cases can deliver about $2\times$ the matmul throughput per SM compared to FP16/BF16. However, using FP8 at high performance and with correct numerical behavior requires attention to operand layouts and to quantization errors.

Layout constraints. Consider a GEMM that computes $A \times B^\top$ where $A$ has shape $M\times K$ and $B$ has shape $N\times K$. We say an operand is mn‑major if it is contiguous along its outer dimension (the $M$ or $N$ dimension), and k‑major if it is contiguous along the inner $K$ dimension. The paper points out a critical difference between FP16 and FP8 WGMMA on Hopper: FP16 WGMMA accepts both mn‑major and k‑major inputs when operands are in SMEM, but FP8 WGMMA only accepts k‑major inputs in SMEM. In attention kernels this matters because the usual layout in memory for $\mathbf{V}$ and the layout expected by the FP8 Tensor Core may not match — for example, $\mathbf{V}$ tiles are typically contiguous in the head dimension, whereas the k‑major requirement demands contiguity in the $K$ dimension. That mismatch forces either a preprocessing transpose in GMEM (costly in memory bandwidth) or an in‑kernel transpose after loading into SMEM.

Accumulator/operand layout clash. When chaining GEMMs (as attention does: first compute scores, then use softmaxed scores times $\mathbf{V}$), the FP32 accumulator layout produced by an FP8 WGMMA can be different from the register layout expected by a subsequent FP8 WGMMA input. This incompatibility can block a straightforward fusion of back‑to‑back FP8 WGMMAs. FlashAttention‑3 handles this by performing register permutations (byte permutes) to transform the accumulator ordering into the operand ordering expected by the next WGMMA and by coordinating an in‑kernel transpose of $\mathbf{V}$ so that the logical multiplication still computes the correct output.

In‑kernel transpose and LDSM/STSM. To avoid an expensive GMEM transpose, FlashAttention‑3 performs the transpose in‑kernel after loading tiles into SMEM. It uses LDSM/STSM instructions, which move 128‑byte blocks collectively for a warp, and which are described as copying $8\times 8$ matrices with 16‑bit entries. For FP8 this is implemented by packing two 8‑bit entries into a 16‑bit slot. There are some caveats: the transpose versions of LDSM/STSM cannot split packed 8‑bit entries, so FlashAttention‑3 needs a small sequence of register moves between LDSM and STSM to complete a tile‑wise transpose. This approach is register‑efficient and can be scheduled by producers so that a transpose of the next $\mathbf{V}$ tile executes while the compute path consumes the previous tiles.

Numerical format and accuracy. The typical FP8 encoding used on Hopper (e.g., e4m3) gives only 3 bits of mantissa and 4 bits of exponent, which increases quantization error relative to FP16/BF16. Large language models and attention inputs can contain outliers that are much larger than typical values, making naive per‑tensor FP8 quantization brittle. FlashAttention‑3 mitigates this via two algorithmic techniques: block quantization (one scale factor per tile/block rather than one per tensor) and incoherent processing (multiply $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ to spread out outliers before quantization). The orthogonal transform can be implemented as products of random diagonal $\{\pm1\}$ matrices and Hadamard transforms, which are computable in $O(d\log d)$ and can be fused with other preprocessing (e.g., rotary embeddings), avoiding extra memory bandwidth. Together, these methods reduce the FP8 numerical error substantially compared to simple per‑tensor FP8 quantization.

# Summary: how these features shape attention kernels

Putting these hardware characteristics together clarifies why FlashAttention‑3 adopts the design choices it does. The memory hierarchy encourages keeping intermediates in SMEM and registers to avoid costly GMEM round trips. The thread/warpgoup organization and asynchronous units (TMA and WGMMA) permit a producer/consumer split that streams tiles into a circular SMEM buffer while Tensor Cores perform heavy matmuls. Because softmax’s exponentials are serviced by low‑throughput MFUs, overlapping those operations with Tensor Core work via careful warp scheduling (ping‑pong and intra‑warpgoup pipelining) is essential to avoid leaving the Tensor Cores starved. FP8 Tensor Cores offer large throughput upsides but impose k‑major layout constraints and create accumulator/operand ordering mismatches; the solution is a combination of in‑kernel transposes (LDSM/STSM with packing), register byte permutations, and numerical techniques (block quantization and incoherent transforms) to preserve accuracy.

Understanding these constraints — the hierarchical memories, the warpgoup granularity of WGMMA, TMA’s asynchronous copying, the low throughput of MFUs, and FP8 layout/quantization issues — is the key to reading and reasoning about FlashAttention‑3 and similar high‑performance attention kernels on Hopper‑class GPUs.

From Standard Attention to FlashAttention-2

# Standard attention on GPU: materializing S and P, excessive GMEM reads/writes

The attention layer that sits at the core of Transformer models is simple to state but expensive to compute. For a single head, given query, key, and value matrices $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$ (where $N$ is the sequence length and $d$ the head dimension) the forward pass is usually written as
\[
\mathbf{S} = \alpha\, \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top,\qquad
\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S}),\qquad
\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V},
\]
with $\alpha=1/\sqrt{d}$ a common scaling and the softmax applied row-wise. A standard, straightforward GPU implementation follows these mathematical steps directly: materialize the full score matrix $\mathbf{S}$ in global device memory (HBM), compute $\mathbf{P}$ from $\mathbf{S}$ (typically subtracting the rowwise maximum for numerical stability before exponentiation), materialize $\mathbf{P}$, and finally multiply by $\mathbf{V}$ to produce $\mathbf{O}$.

The cost of that strategy is dominated by memory traffic. The intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ are size $N\times N$, so materializing them incurs $O(N^2)$ storage and repeated reads/writes to global memory. On contemporary GPUs this is costly because of the memory hierarchy: registers and shared memory (SMEM) are fast but small, while global memory (HBM) is large but much slower. Repeatedly materializing $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ forces many passes over the same $O(N^2)$ entries through HBM, creating a memory-bound workload that limits throughput and makes scaling to long contexts (large $N$) expensive. In other words, the naive implementation trades compute locality for simplicity and becomes bottlenecked by global memory I/O rather than by raw arithmetic capability.

# FlashAttention (the original): on‑chip tiling, fused softmax via streaming reductions, avoiding intermediate I/O

FlashAttention (the original algorithm) was introduced to attack precisely that I/O bottleneck by rethinking how attention is computed on a GPU. The core idea is to avoid materializing the large intermediate matrices in HBM by fusing the three logical steps (score computation, softmax, and output accumulation) into a single GPU kernel that streams tiles through fast on‑chip storage and performs local reductions to implement softmax. In short: compute attention blockwise on‑chip and only write the final outputs once.

Concretely, FlashAttention breaks the query matrix into row blocks $\mathbf{Q}_i$ of size $B_r\times d$ and the key/value matrices into column blocks $\mathbf{K}_j,\mathbf{V}_j$ of size $B_c\times d$. For a fixed query block $\mathbf{Q}_i$ the algorithm streams over $j=0,\dots,T_c-1$ and for each block computes the partial scores
\[
\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i\,\mathbf{K}_j^\top,
\]
all held transiently in registers or shared memory. Rather than forming the full rowwise softmax across all $N$ keys at once, FlashAttention maintains a running rowwise maximum $m_i$ and a running row-sum accumulator $\ell_i$ that summarize the contributions of the blocks seen so far. When a new block $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}$ is computed the kernel forms the stabilized exponentials
\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} = \exp\!\big(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} - m_i\big),
\]
and updates the normalization and output accumulators with numerically correct rescalings so that, after all blocks have been processed, the exact softmax outputs and the final $\mathbf{O}_i$ can be obtained. The register/shared‑memory updates in the inner loop follow the pattern implemented in the code (in pseudo-equation form):
\[
m_i \leftarrow \max\big(m_i,\,\mathrm{rowmax}(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)})\big),
\]
\[
\ell_i \leftarrow \exp(m_i^{\mathrm{old}}-m_i)\,\ell_i + \mathrm{rowsum}\big(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}\big),
\]
\[
\mathbf{O}_i \leftarrow \mathrm{diag}\big(\exp(m_i^{\mathrm{old}}-m_i)\big)^{-1}\mathbf{O}_i \;+\; \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}\,\mathbf{V}_j,
\]
and at the end an epilogue rescales $\mathbf{O}_i$ by $\ell_i$ and writes the final block out. The derivation that these streaming updates are algebraically equivalent to computing the usual softmax and $\mathbf{O}$ is given in the FlashAttention literature; intuitively, the running maximum and the multiplicative rescaling keep previously accumulated partial contributions consistent with the normalization used after all blocks are seen.

Because FlashAttention never writes the full $\mathbf{S}$ or $\mathbf{P}$ to global memory and reads them back, it dramatically reduces global-memory I/O: each input block is read once (or a small constant number of times) into fast on‑chip storage and the output block $\mathbf{O}_i$ is written exactly once at the end. This on‑chip tiling plus fused softmax transforms attention from an I/O-dominated kernel into one that better leverages on‑chip bandwidth and compute, enabling much larger effective sequence lengths for the same hardware footprint.

# FlashAttention-2: parallelizing across sequence blocks, improved occupancy and load balancing

FlashAttention removed the dominant intermediate I/O, but there were still opportunities to better match the GPU execution model. FlashAttention-2 reorganized the computation to increase parallelism across the sequence-length dimension itself, rather than only across output query blocks. The key restructuring is to make the inner loop of the forward pass—the loop that iterates over key/value blocks—explicitly parallelizable across the GPU so that more warps and CTAs can be active concurrently. In practice this is done by dividing both the query and key/value dimensions into blocks and scheduling the work so that multiple compute groups operate on different $(\mathbf{Q}_i,\mathbf{K}_j,\mathbf{V}_j)$ tiles at the same time.

This reorganization improves occupancy (the fraction of GPU resources actively doing useful work) and achieves a better distribution of work across SMs. By operating on blocks of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ in a way that allows multiple CTAs to progress through different parts of the sequence at once, FlashAttention-2 reduces idle time and balances load when sequence lengths are long or when the mapping from blocks to warps would otherwise be imbalanced. Practically, FlashAttention-2 preserves the fused single-kernel, on‑chip streaming softmax idea of the original FlashAttention, but changes the parallel decomposition so that both the query-block dimension and the sequence-block dimension contribute to parallelism.

The effect is that smaller tile sizes, more concurrent CTA scheduling, and careful staging of key/value tiles into shared memory can be used to keep the GPU busy over a wider range of sequence lengths and head dimensions than the original design. FlashAttention-2 therefore represents an important engineering evolution: it keeps the same algebraic strategy (no materialized intermediates) while improving how that work maps to the GPU's parallel-execution hierarchy.

# Limitations on H100: synchronous execution model, low Tensor Core utilization (~35%) vs GEMM kernels (80–90%)

Despite these improvements, FlashAttention-2 was observed to underutilize Hopper-generation GPUs such as the NVIDIA H100. Empirically, FlashAttention-2 achieves poor Tensor Core utilization on H100 (around 35\%) compared to highly optimized GEMM kernels which can reach 80–90\% utilization on the same hardware. The paper highlights two classes of causes for this shortfall.

First, some of the gap is implementation-level: FlashAttention-2 implementations were not always using Hopper-specific instruction sequences in place of earlier-Ampere-oriented idioms. On Hopper, newer instructions and tile-based programming models can extract higher raw throughput from the Tensor Cores; failing to adopt these instruction patterns can leave performance on the table.

Second, and more fundamentally, FlashAttention-2 was designed under a simplified, effectively synchronous execution model that did not exploit newer hardware features exposed by Hopper. Hopper introduces stronger hardware asynchrony (an asynchronous Tensor Core instruction WGMMA and an asynchronous tensor memory accelerator TMA) and supports warp-specialized scheduling and dynamic register allocation across warpgroups. FlashAttention-2's synchronous inner loop—compute a GEMM, wait, run softmax, wait, run the next GEMM—creates explicit dependencies and synchronization points that prevent the hardware from overlapping data movement and Tensor Core compute efficiently. In short, FlashAttention-2 minimized global-memory I/O, but it did not explicitly coordinate with the hardware's asynchronous execution primitives, which limited how effectively it could keep the Tensor Cores busy on Hopper.

# Why softmax and data movement serialize GEMMs without explicit asynchrony-aware design

At the heart of the serialization is the dependency chain inside the attention inner loop. For a given query block the typical sequence is:

1. Compute the block scores $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i\mathbf{K}_j^\top$ (a GEMM on the Tensor Cores).
2. Run the softmax-related elementwise reductions and exponentials on $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}$ to produce $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$ and update running normalizers.
3. Multiply the resulting (stabilized) probabilities by the corresponding $\mathbf{V}_j$ block to accumulate into $\mathbf{O}_i$ (another GEMM).

Because step (2) consumes the output of step (1) and step (3) consumes the output of step (2), a straightforward implementation issues a GEMM, waits for completion, runs the softmax arithmetic (rowwise maxima, exponentials and sums), waits for those scalar/vector updates to finish, and only then issues the next GEMM. The GPU is therefore forced into a sequence of blocking waits that prevent useful overlap.

This serialization is especially harmful because of a large imbalance in the raw throughputs of the hardware units involved. The Tensor Cores deliver very high GEMM throughput (on H100 the FP16 matmul peak is reported at roughly 989 TFLOPs), whereas the special-function units that perform exponentials and similar elementwise operations have orders-of-magnitude lower throughput (the paper cites about 3.9 TFLOPs for special functions on H100). To build the intuition the paper gives: for FP16 attention with head dimension 128, the total number of matmul FLOPs in the forward pass is roughly 512 times the number of exponential FLOPs, while the exponential unit has about 256 times lower throughput; put together this means the exponential computations alone can occupy a non-negligible fraction of time that could have been used by the Tensor Cores if the two were not overlapped. In a synchronous implementation the Tensor Cores sit idle while the softmax step executes; conversely, when the Tensor Cores run, the special-function units are idle. Either way, the end-to-end utilization falls well below what the GPU could deliver if these streams were overlapped.

Likewise, data movement from global memory into shared memory (or vice versa) can be asynchronous (on Hopper, TMA exposes this capability), but a synchronous kernel that issues loads and then waits for them to finish serializes the producer/consumer relationship and prevents overlapping of memory transfer with computation. Without an explicit asynchrony-aware scheduling and a producer–consumer decomposition, both movement and computation become serialized bottlenecks.

# Lessons learned: I/O-aware algorithm design must match modern hardware execution

The evolution from standard attention to FlashAttention and then to FlashAttention-2 illustrates a key theme: removing global-memory I/O is necessary but not sufficient for optimal performance on modern accelerators. FlashAttention demonstrated that fusing and tiling to avoid materializing $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ eliminates the dominant I/O costs of classical implementations and enables attention to scale to longer contexts. FlashAttention-2 showed that reorganizing the parallel decomposition across the sequence length can improve occupancy and load balance. But the Hopper observations make clear that algorithmic structure must also be aligned with the newer execution model of the hardware: asynchronous Tensor Core execution, asynchronous DMA-like memory transfers (TMA), warp-specialization, and low-precision tensor cores all change what the kernel must do to extract throughput.

Practically, this means several design principles: (1) preserve the fused, on‑chip nature of computation to limit HBM traffic; (2) design the parallel decomposition so multiple warps/CTAs can work on different sequence blocks concurrently to improve occupancy; and (3) make the kernel asynchrony-aware so that independent hardware units (Tensor Cores, special-function units, and memory accelerators) can be kept busy simultaneously rather than serially. The last point requires changing the canonical inner-loop dependency pattern (GEMM → softmax → GEMM) into a pipelined, producer–consumer form that can overlap softmax computations and data movement with other GEMMs, as well as carefully handling low-precision execution and its layout constraints.

In short: minimizing global-memory I/O (the FlashAttention insight) unlocked a large fraction of the potential speedups; to reach the peak arithmetic bandwidth of modern GPUs one must now also match the kernel's synchronization and data-layout patterns to the hardware's asynchronous execution model and its low-precision capabilities. The work described in the paper follows precisely this trajectory by taking FlashAttention-2 as the starting point and then introducing asynchrony-aware scheduling and FP8-aware layout/quantization strategies to close the utilization gap on Hopper.

Design Principles and Contributions of FlashAttention-3

This chapter explains the key ideas and performance rationale behind FlashAttention-3 (FA-3), the redesign of the attention kernel targeted at Hopper-class GPUs. FA-3 is organized around three tightly coupled knobs: exploiting hardware asynchrony via warp-specialization and circular shared-memory buffers, interleaving block-wise GEMMs with the softmax pipeline to hide slow non-GEMM work, and safely targeting the FP8 Tensor Cores by engineering layout-conformant dataflows together with quantization techniques that preserve numerical accuracy. The discussion below ties these ideas to the attention math, the CTA-level tiling parameters that FA-3 uses, and concrete hardware characteristics (TMA, WGMMA, SFUs) that motivate the design choices. Wherever useful, I indicate the algorithmic structure used in the paper (consumer/producer roles, $B_r, B_c$, $T_r, T_c$, and the $s$-stage circular buffer), and summarize the observed performance and numerical benefits reported for H100 GPUs.

# Producer–consumer asynchrony via warp-specialization and circular SMEM buffers

At the algorithmic level attention for a single head computes
$S = \alpha Q K^\top$, $P = \mathrm{softmax}(S)$ (row-wise), and $O = P V$. FA-3 computes this in tiles: a CTA works on a query tile $Q_i$ of size $B_r \times d$ to produce an output tile $O_i$. The sequence (length $N$) is partitioned into query blocks of size $B_r$ and key/value blocks of size $B_c$, giving
$T_r = \lceil N / B_r \rceil$ and $T_c = \lceil N / B_c \rceil$ blocks respectively. FA-3 maps producers and consumers to distinct warpgroups in each CTA and uses a circular shared-memory (SMEM) buffer with $s$ stages to hold the $K$ and $V$ tiles that the consumer will use.

Why split warps into producers and consumers? Modern GPUs (Hopper in particular) provide dedicated asynchronous mechanisms for memory movement and matrix multiplication: the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) moves data between global memory (HBM) and SMEM asynchronously, and the warpgroup-wide WGMMA instruction drives Tensor Cores asynchronously from shared memory. By making some warps specialize as producers that issue TMA loads into SMEM and other warps specialize as consumers that issue WGMMA GEMMs from SMEM, FA-3 decouples the two dominant activities and exposes more asynchrony to the hardware. Practically this specialization is implemented by (a) having producer warpgroups issue TMA loads of $Q_i$ and then of $K_j, V_j$ into the $\bigl(j \bmod s\bigr)$-th stage of the circular buffer and commit/notify the consumer, and (b) having consumer warpgroups wait for those notifications and then execute the compute-heavy WGMMA GEMMs on the shared-memory tiles.

The circular buffer with $s$ stages implements a simple producer–consumer semaphore: the producer waits for a stage to be released before overwriting it, and the consumer releases a stage after it has consumed the $K_j, V_j$ tiles. This structure is visible in the CTA-level pseudocode in the paper (Algorithm 1): the producer issues asynchronous TMA loads and commits them; the consumer waits on those commits and then runs GEMMs and local-softmax logic. The asynchronous TMA calls do not block the producer, so the producer can keep feeding SMEM while consumers consume older stages. Because producers do little arithmetic and consumers do heavy Tensor Core work, FA-3 uses the Hopper capability to reallocate registers between warpgroups (via setmaxnreg) so consumer warps get the larger register allotment they need for WGMMA, while producer warps keep a minimal register footprint.

This warp-specialization + circular-SMEM design has two direct benefits. First, it increases overlap between data movement and compute: TMA can be issuing loads while WGMMA runs. Second, because different warpgroups have distinct roles, the compiler and the hardware can schedule those roles more effectively and give the compute warps more register resources and the memory warps fewer, improving instruction-level efficiency and occupancy.

# Hiding softmax under asynchronous WGMMA using interleaved block-wise GEMMs

A critical bottleneck in attention is the softmax step: it requires exponentials and reductions that are handled by lower-throughput functional units (multi-function units or SFUs) compared to Tensor Cores. On H100 the paper reports roughly $989$ TFLOPs/s of FP16 matmul throughput but only about $3.9$ TFLOPs/s available for special functions such as exponential. This large imbalance means that if softmax is done serially between two GEMMs, the SFUs will occupy a large fraction of cycles and limit overall Tensor Core utilization.

FA-3 addresses this by pipelining GEMMs and softmax work across blocks so that the slow non-GEMM softmax steps are executed while other GEMMs are running on Tensor Cores. The essential idea is to break the strict per-iteration dependency between the two GEMMs in the inner loop (the "compute $S = Q K_j^\top$" GEMM and the "accumulate $O \mathrel{+}= \widetilde{P} V_j$" GEMM) by keeping a small amount of state in registers and issuing asynchronous WGMMA calls for different roles in an overlapped manner.

Concretely, FA-3 implements a two-stage overlapped pipeline at the consumer level (see Algorithm 2 in the paper). At the start of the main loop the consumer computes $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}} = Q_i K_0^\top$ using WGMMA, then derives $\widetilde{P}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ (the local exponentials and normalization accumulators) from it. In each subsequent iteration $j$ the consumer:

1. Issues an asynchronous WGMMA to compute $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}} = Q_i K_j^\top$ and does not wait for its completion immediately.
2. Issues an asynchronous WGMMA to compute the RS-GEMM $\widetilde{P}_{\mathrm{cur}} V_{j-1}$ to accumulate into $O_i$, again not waiting immediately.
3. Then waits for the first WGMMA to complete, uses $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ to compute the next softmax quantities and rescaling terms, and finally waits for the pending RS-GEMM to finish to apply the precise rescaling to $O_i$.

Because both WGMMA instructions are asynchronous, the exponential/multi-function work involved in computing $\widetilde{P}_{\mathrm{next}}$ (the softmax for the next block) can be scheduled and executed while the Tensor Cores are busy with the WGMMA of the next or previous iteration. This hides the low-throughput softmax work behind high-throughput GEMMs and reduces idle time on Tensor Cores.

Two practical considerations appear in the paper. First, compilers often reorder instructions; FA-3 authors examined generated SASS to ensure that the intended overlapped ordering survives compilation. Second, the 2-stage pipeline increases register pressure: keeping an extra $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ in registers requires space proportional to $B_r \times B_c$ per CTA (in floats), so the chosen tile sizes must balance register usage and pipeline depth. The authors also note a 3-stage variant is possible—further overlapping work at the cost of still more registers—so there is a tradeoff between pipeline depth (and potential Tensor Core utilization) and register pressure / occupancy.

Beyond intra-warpgroup pipelining, FA-3 uses a pingpong scheduling across warpgroups: by arranging barrier synchronizations so that the GEMMs of warpgroup A are scheduled before those of warpgroup B, the softmax of warpgroup A runs while warpgroup B's GEMMs execute and vice versa. This inter-warpgroup pingpong further increases the chance that softmax (SFU work) will be masked by WGMMA activity. In experiments cited in the paper, these overlapping strategies raise measured FP16 forward throughput from around $570$ TFLOPs/s to the $620$–$660$ TFLOPs/s range in representative settings (for example with head dimension $128$ and sequence length $8192$), demonstrating significant improvement from hiding softmax under GEMMs.

# Targeting FP8 Tensor Cores with layout-conformant dataflows

Hopper's FP8 Tensor Cores offer roughly 2× the matmul throughput of FP16 Tensor Cores, so invoking FP8 WGMMA is an obvious lever for raising TFLOPs/s. However, FP8 introduces two practical obstacles that FA-3 addresses carefully: layout conformance and accumulator/operand layout mismatches when chaining GEMMs.

Layout conformance: FP8 WGMMA in SMEM accepts only k-major inputs (i.e., contiguous in the inner $K$ dimension) for its operands, whereas FP16 WGMMA is more flexible. In standard attention tensors the contiguous stride is typically the head dimension $d$ (the "inner" dimension), not the sequence dimension, so the tiles of $\mathbf{V}$ as laid out in global memory are not in the k-major format required by FP8 WGMMA for the second GEMM. FA-3 considers two families of solutions: (1) pre-transpose the $\mathbf{V}$ tensor in global memory (either fused with a previous epilogue op like rotary embeddings or as a separate transpose kernel), or (2) perform an in-kernel transpose of each loaded $\mathbf{V}$ tile after it is brought into SMEM.

The authors reject a standalone pre-transpose in most cases because it is wasteful in a memory-bound context, and they find fusing a transpose into preceding library code is difficult for general deployment. Instead FA-3 implements the in-kernel transpose (option 2) using the LDSM/STSM warp-local register<->SMEM copy instructions. Because LDSM/STSM naturally move $8\times 8$ chunks (described in PTX as 16-bit entries), FA-3 packs two 8-bit FP8 elements into 16-bit lanes and then uses a small sequence of register moves to effect a tile transpose while staying register-efficient. The in-kernel transpose runs in the producer warpgroup and is scheduled so that transposing the next $\mathbf{V}$ tile happens in the shadow of WGMMA activity on the current tiles, preserving overlap.

Accumulator vs operand layout mismatch: when chaining two FP8 WGMMA operations in a fused kernel, the FP32 accumulator layout produced by the first WGMMA does not match the operand layout expected by the second WGMMA. FA-3 addresses this by performing a deterministic byte-permute of register-held accumulators to reorder them into the format expected by the next WGMMA. The paper gives a representative reordering pattern: for an 8-element register sequence they change the order to $\{d_0, d_1, d_4, d_5, d_2, d_3, d_6, d_7\}$ and replicate that permutation across every 8-byte group. Logically, this permutes columns of the $\mathbf{P}$ tile; to maintain correctness the in-kernel transpose of the corresponding $\mathbf{V}$ tile is arranged to implement the complementary row permutation so that the second WGMMA still computes the right output.

Two important practical benefits result from these choices. First, by doing the transpose in-kernel and using register byte-permute rather than cross-thread shuffles, FA-3 avoids expensive thread-to-thread register ownership changes and keeps the dataflow local to warps. Second, because the transpose is executed in the producer warpgroup it can be overlapped with consumer WGMMA work, ensuring the additional cost of layout conversion does not become a performance bottleneck. These changes unlock the FP8 Tensor Core throughput for fused attention while preserving correctness and reasonable register/SIMT behavior.

# Accuracy in low precision: block quantization and incoherent processing

Raw FP8 quantization (per-tensor scaling) is often insufficient for attention because large models exhibit "outlier" activations: rare entries whose magnitude is far larger than the typical element. Outliers make a single per-tensor scale factor ill-suited and can generate large quantization error.

FA-3 adopts two complementary strategies to reduce FP8 error while retaining the throughput benefits of FP8 WGMMA: block quantization and incoherent processing.

Block quantization splits $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ into the same block shapes FA-3 already uses for compute—$B_r \times d$ blocks for queries and $B_c \times d$ blocks for keys/values—and computes one FP8 scale per block. Because FA-3 naturally streams blocks into the kernel, this per-block scaling can be fused with a pre-attention operation (e.g., rotary embedding) without incurring additional memory passes. Furthermore, the algorithm accounts for per-block scaling in the block-wise computation of $\mathbf{S}$ and the softmax reductions, so using block quantization adds no extra algorithmic complexity.

Incoherent processing is a lightweight statistical trick to dampen the effect of outliers when quantizing. FA-3 multiplies $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ on the right by an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ prior to quantization and execution: $(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ because $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top = I$. Multiplying by a random orthogonal transform spreads a single outlier across many coordinates, making the per-block quantizers see smaller-magnitude values and reducing quantization loss. In practice FA-3 uses an $\mathbf{M}$ built as a product of random sign-diagonal matrices and a Hadamard matrix; such transforms can be applied in $O(d \log d)$ time and can be fused with rotary embeddings at essentially no extra cost.

The paper validates these techniques on synthetic outlier data (each entry sampled from $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ plus, with probability $0.001$, an additional $\mathcal{N}(0,100)$ term) and reports that FP8 FA-3 with block quantization plus incoherent processing yields about a $2.6\times$ reduction in RMSE compared to a baseline FP8 attention implementation that uses per-tensor scaling. Thus, FA-3 balances the performance advantage of FP8 Tensor Cores with concrete engineering to keep numerical error under control.

# CTA tiling parameters: Br (query block), Bc (key/value block), Tr and Tc counts

FA-3's dataflow and the performance/accuracy tradeoffs are shaped by the CTA-level tiling parameters $B_r$ and $B_c$. By definition, a CTA works on a single query block $Q_i$ of shape $B_r \times d$ and iterates over $T_c = \lceil N / B_c \rceil$ key/value blocks of shape $B_c \times d$. The CTA produces $O_i$ after accumulating contributions from every $V_j$ via the pipeline described earlier.

Choosing $B_r$ and $B_c$ affects several interacting factors:

- Register pressure and occupancy. Larger $B_r$ or $B_c$ increase the temporary storage (for example, the extra $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ in the two-stage pipeline) that must be kept in registers, potentially causing spills or reducing the number of CTAs that can be resident on an SM. As explained earlier, the pipeline depth (2-stage vs 3-stage) further multiplies this register requirement.

- SMEM usage and circular buffer sizing. Each loaded $K_j, V_j$ tile occupies one stage of the $s$-stage circular buffer. Larger $B_c$ means fewer buffer entries fit in SMEM, so $s$ or $B_c$ must be tuned so the producer can stay sufficiently ahead of the consumer.

- TMA load efficiency and layout costs. TMA loads are most efficient when they can read contiguous regions that match the memory layout; for FP8 the in-kernel transpose is required if the global layout does not match the k-major constraint of WGMMA. Larger tiles typically amortize the fixed overhead of setting up TMA and LDSM/STSM transfers, but they raise register/SMEM costs.

- Fine-grained latency hiding. Smaller $B_r, B_c$ give finer granularity to the pipeline (more frequent opportunities to overlap softmax with GEMMs), but this increases the number of WGMMA and TMA invocations and the synchronization overhead.

FA-3 therefore selects tile sizes experimentally as a tradeoff between these constraints. The algorithmic structure—producer/consumer warpgroups, the $s$-stage circular SMEM buffer, and the two-stage/three-stage pipelining—gives the implementation knobs to balance occupancy, register usage, and the amount of computation available to hide slow softmax work.

# Performance motivation: matching high Tensor Core throughput while keeping SFUs busy

The core engineering motivation of FA-3 is the huge asymmetry between the very high throughput of Tensor Cores and the much lower throughput of the special-function units that compute exponentials and other softmax-supporting operations. The paper quantifies this asymmetry on H100: about $989$ TFLOPs/s for FP16 matmul versus $\sim 3.9$ TFLOPs/s for special functions. Consider the FP16 attention forward pass with head dimension $d=128$. The matmul FLOPs per token pair are far larger than the exponentials required for softmax: in the example given the matmul FLOPs are about $512\times$ the exponential FLOPs, while the exponential hardware is about $256\times$ slower than the matmul hardware; combining these facts leads to exponentials consuming a non-negligible fraction of cycle time unless their work is overlapped with GEMM.

FA-3's three levers are motivated by this imbalance:

1. Warp-specialization and a circular SMEM buffer explicitly allow TMA loads to be issued concurrently with WGMMA execution, exposing memory/computation overlap that FA-2 did not fully exploit.

2. The intra-CTA two-stage (or possible three-stage) GEMM-softmax pipelining hides softmax work behind asynchronous WGMMA calls so that the SFUs are busy while Tensor Cores execute GEMMs, rather than serializing them.

3. Targeting FP8 WGMMA doubles the Tensor Core matmul throughput on Hopper. To benefit from FP8 without unacceptable loss of accuracy, FA-3 incorporates the in-kernel transpose and accumulator reordering to be layout-conformant, and block quantization plus incoherent processing to control quantization error.

These design choices produce measurable improvements. On H100 SXM5 the paper reports that FP16 FA-3 attains up to $740$ TFLOPs/s (about $75\%$ of theoretical FP16 Tensor Core throughput) and FP8 FA-3 reaches close to $1.2$ PFLOPs/s in favorable cases. Relative to FlashAttention-2, FA-3 achieves roughly $1.5$–$2.0\times$ speedup in the forward pass and $1.5$–$1.75\times$ in the backward pass in the reported benchmarks. Ablations show that both the warp-specialization and GEMM-softmax pipelining are necessary contributors to these gains; for instance, pipelining alone moved a canonical experiment from about $570$ TFLOPs/s to the $620$–$660$ TFLOPs/s range.

Taken together, these strategies are an instance of a broader principle: when one hardware resource (Tensor Cores) is far more powerful than another (SFUs and memory), algorithm and kernel design should be shaped to feed the fast resource continuously and hide slow work behind asynchronous operations. FA-3 demonstrates how software can restructure attention into a producer/consumer tiled pipeline that matches a modern GPU’s heterogeneous execution model and low-precision capabilities while controlling the numerical consequences of aggressive quantization.

Concluding remark. FlashAttention-3 is a practical synthesis of three ideas—warp-specialized asynchrony, GEMM/softmax pipelining, and FP8-aware dataflow plus quantization—that together deliver substantial throughput gains on Hopper GPUs while retaining acceptable numerical fidelity. The CTA tiling parameters ($B_r$, $B_c$, $T_r$, $T_c$, and the circular buffer stage count $s$) are the knobs that trade off register pressure, SMEM usage, and pipeline granularity; the hardware features (TMA, WGMMA, LDSM/STSM, setmaxnreg) make these transformations efficient and implementable in a single fused kernel.

Producer–Consumer Asynchrony: Warp-Specialization with Circular SMEM

This chapter explains the CTA-level forward-pass design of FlashAttention-3 without intra-consumer GEMM/softmax overlap. The central idea is to split a CTA's warps into distinct roles—producers that move data from global memory (HBM) into shared memory (SMEM) using the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA), and consumers that perform the Tensor‑Core matmuls (WGMMA) and the row-wise softmax logic on those tiles. A small, s-stage circular buffer in SMEM mediates the handoff between producers and consumers: it lets the producer stream many blocks ahead without overwriting in-use stages, while consumers pull tiles as they become available. This producer–consumer arrangement uses asynchronous TMA and WGMMA primitives to hide memory and instruction latencies and to keep the Tensor Cores busy.

# Roles: producer warpgroup issues TMA loads; consumer warpgroups issue WGMMA and softmax

Warp-specialization is the practice of assigning different warps in a CTA to focused roles. In FlashAttention-3, a producer warpgroup is responsible solely for copying tiles of inputs from HBM to SMEM using TMA; consumer warpgroups read those tiles from SMEM and perform the linear-algebra and elementwise operations that produce the final output tile. This separation of concerns takes advantage of Hopper's asynchronous hardware units: the TMA unit can stream blocks into SMEM without stalling the warps that will later perform computation on those blocks, and the Tensor Core WGMMA instruction can source operands directly from SMEM and execute without blocking the producer.

At the CTA level the work is embarrassingly parallel across query blocks. Fix a query tile size $B_r$ and a key/value tile size $B_c$. For a sequence length $N$ the query matrix is partitioned into $T_r=\lceil N / B_r\rceil$ blocks $\mathbf{Q}_1,\dots,\mathbf{Q}_{T_r}$, and the key/value matrices into $T_c=\lceil N / B_c\rceil$ blocks $\mathbf{K}_0,\dots,\mathbf{K}_{T_c-1}$ and $\mathbf{V}_0,\dots,\mathbf{V}_{T_c-1}$. A single CTA takes responsibility for one query block $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and computes the corresponding output block $\mathbf{O}_i$. Within that CTA, the producer warpgroup streams the required $\mathbf{Q}_i$ (once) and the sequence of $\mathbf{K}_j,\mathbf{V}_j$ tiles into the circular SMEM buffer, and one or more consumer warpgroups repeatedly compute matmuls and softmax updates using the tiles as they arrive.

# Circular SMEM buffer (s stages): pipeline object, commit, wait, release semantics

The shared memory buffer is organized as an $s$-stage ring: each stage is a region of SMEM sized to hold one $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$ tile (and possibly the $\mathbf{Q}_i$ tile when it is staged). A small software pipeline object provides three basic synchronization operations for each stage:

- commit: the producer signals that it has completed loading a tile into a particular stage and that consumers may start using it;
- wait: a consumer (or producer) waits until a given stage is in the desired state (loaded or consumed);
- release: a consumer signals that it is finished with a stage so the producer may reuse it.

These semantics are implemented with barrier-like synchronization between the producer and consumer warpgroups. The circular buffer enforces that at most $s$ stages are outstanding at any time; when the producer reaches the stage that was used $s$ loads ago it must wait for that stage to be released before overwriting it. Conversely, the consumer will block if it tries to read a stage before the producer has committed it.

The s-stage buffer gives two practical benefits. First, it lets the producer queue many TMA loads ahead so the hardware TMA unit can stream data without fine-grained handshakes between loads; in particular, the producer usually issues no waits for the first $s$ iterations while the buffer is being filled. Second, it bounds memory residency in SMEM: with $s$ stages the algorithm trades off SMEM footprint and the maximum number of in-flight blocks that hide TMA latency.

# Loading $\mathbf{Q}_i$, $\mathbf{K}_j$, $\mathbf{V}_j$ with TMA; overlapping TMA latency across iterations

All global-memory-to-shared-memory transfers are issued via the asynchronous Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA). The producer warpgroup issues a TMA load for $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and then, in a loop, issues TMA loads for each pair $(\mathbf{K}_j,\mathbf{V}_j)$ into the $j\!\bmod\! s$ stage of the circular buffer. Because TMA is asynchronous, the producer can issue multiple loads back-to-back; a single TMA load does not stall the producer warpgroup or the consumer warpgroups. Once a TMA load completes the producer executes a commit to notify consumers that the stage is ready.

This asynchrony is the mechanism by which TMA latency is overlapped across iterations. The producer only has to wait when it would otherwise overwrite a stage that consumers have not yet released. Therefore, for the first $s$ issued loads there is no waiting: the buffer is being filled. After that the producer's loop contains a wait against the $(j\bmod s)$th stage being consumed before issuing the next load into that stage. On the consumer side, warps wait for the committed availability of the $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and each $\mathbf{K}_j$ or $\mathbf{V}_j$ tile as they begin the corresponding computation. In this way the movement of data (producer TMA loads) and the computations that consume that data (consumer WGMMA and softmax) proceed concurrently and are regulated by the circular buffer's commit/wait/release protocol.

# Compute steps per K/V block: $ \mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$ (SS‑GEMM), local softmax update ($m_i,\ell_i$), $\mathbf{O}_i$ accumulation with $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$ (RS‑GEMM)

Each consumer warpgroup processes one $\mathbf{Q}_i$ tile through all $T_c$ key/value tiles. The inner iteration for block index $j$ follows three conceptual steps.

First, form the score tile
\[
\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top,
\]
computed as a WGMMA matmul where the first operand is read from SMEM (hence the SS‑GEMM variant). The consumer waits for the producer to have committed $\mathbf{K}_j$ into SMEM before issuing this WGMMA.

Second, perform a numerically stable local softmax update across the rows of the new score tile using running maxima and normalization accumulators. Let $m_i$ denote the per-row running maximum (initialized to $-\infty$) and let $\ell_i$ denote the per-row running denominator accumulator (initialized to $0$). After computing $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}$ the algorithm updates
\[
m_i \leftarrow \max\!\big(m_i,\, \mathrm{rowmax}(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)})\big),
\]
then forms the exponentiated, shifted probabilities for the new block,
\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} = \exp\big(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} - m_i\big),
\]
and updates the running normalization
\[
\ell_i \leftarrow \exp\big(m_i^{\text{old}} - m_i\big)\, \ell_i + \mathrm{rowsum}\big(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}\big),
\]
where $m_i^{\text{old}}$ is the previous value of $m_i$. These algebraic manipulations implement the usual numerically-stable softmax reduction across blocks without ever materializing the full $N\times N$ matrix $\mathbf{S}$ or $\mathbf{P}$ to HBM.

Third, once the corresponding $\mathbf{V}_j$ tile is available in SMEM, accumulate the contribution of this block to the output
\[
\mathbf{O}_i \leftarrow \operatorname{diag}\big(\exp(m_i^{\text{old}}-m_i)\big)^{-1}\,\mathbf{O}_i \;+\; \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}\,\mathbf{V}_j.
\]
This second matmul is issued as a WGMMA where the first operand (the small probability tile $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$) lives in registers and the second operand ($\mathbf{V}_j$) is in SMEM, i.e., the RS‑GEMM variant. After finishing all $T_c$ blocks the consumer performs the epilogue rescaling
\[
\mathbf{O}_i \leftarrow \operatorname{diag}(\ell_i)^{-1}\,\mathbf{O}_i,\qquad
L_i \;=\; m_i + \log(\ell_i),
\]
and writes $\mathbf{O}_i$ and the per-row log-normalizer block $L_i$ back to HBM.

These three steps (SS‑GEMM to get $\mathbf{S}$, softmax bookkeeping to produce $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$, and RS‑GEMM to accumulate into $\mathbf{O}$) are repeated for each key/value block; their ordering is enforced by the consumer's wait/commit logic so correctness is maintained while avoiding global-materialization of $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$.

# SS‑GEMM vs RS‑GEMM: operand residency (SMEM vs registers) and implications

The SS and RS prefixes in the pseudocode indicate the memory residency of the first operand to the WGMMA instruction. SS‑GEMM denotes that the first operand is sourced from SMEM; RS‑GEMM denotes that the first operand is sourced from registers (the second operand remains in SMEM). In our CTA-level flow this distinction maps directly to the two matmuls per block:

- The score matmul $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}=\mathbf{Q}_i\mathbf{K}_j^\top$ is an SS‑GEMM because $\mathbf{Q}_i$ (and typically $\mathbf{K}_j$) live in SMEM when the instruction is issued.
- The output accumulation $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}\mathbf{V}_j$ is an RS‑GEMM because the freshly computed, small probability tile $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$ is formed in registers during the softmax update and is best left there for the subsequent matmul while $\mathbf{V}_j$ is read from SMEM.

These variants have practical implications. Bringing a small tile into registers and using an RS‑GEMM avoids reloading the same small operand from SMEM for each row or column; it reduces SMEM traffic and also makes the subsequent matmul a better match for the Tensor Core's expected data layout. On the other hand, keeping intermediate results in registers increases register pressure, which must be balanced against occupancy and tile sizes. The circular SMEM buffer and the SS/RS usage together let FlashAttention-3 keep the heavy, high‑throughput Tensor Core operations fed while minimizing slow global memory accesses.

# Register allocation strategy with setmaxnreg for producers vs consumers

Hopper exposes the ability to dynamically alter how registers are apportioned across warpgroups via the compiler/runtime directive \texttt{setmaxnreg}. FlashAttention-3 uses this feature to reduce contention between the producer and consumer warpgroups and to assign register-rich contexts to the warps that need them most.

Producer warps, which primarily issue TMA loads, require relatively few registers: their job is to orchestrate asynchronous copies of tiles into SMEM and to perform the commit signaling. In contrast, consumer warps executing WGMMA and the softmax bookkeeping use many registers to hold accumulators, register-staged tiles like $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$, and WGMMA accumulators. To reflect this difference, the CTA performs a register (de)allocation step at kernel start: the producer warpgroup "deallocates" a predetermined number of registers, making them available to the rest of the CTA; consumer warpgroups then "reallocate" registers according to the number of consumer warps. In practice this is implemented by calling \texttt{setmaxnreg} appropriately so that warps doing MMAs obtain a larger share of the register file than the TMA-only warps.

This controlled rebalancing reduces register spilling for the compute-heavy warps and preserves the lightweight footprint of the producer warps. The net effect is higher Tensor Core utilization (fewer stalls caused by register pressure) together with efficient, asynchronous data movement.

# Ping‑pong scheduling across two consumer warpgroups to hide softmax under GEMMs using bar.sync

Beyond producer/consumer specialization, FlashAttention-3 further leverages multi‑warpgroup consumers by using a ping‑pong scheduling pattern that interleaves GEMMs and softmax work across two consumer warpgroups. Conceptually, split the consumer warps into two warpgroups, A and B. The implementation uses barrier synchronization (for example, \texttt{bar.sync}) to force the WGMMA matmuls of a given iteration in warpgroup A to be scheduled before the corresponding matmuls in warpgroup B. Because the hardware WGMMA and TMA are asynchronous, this enforced ordering causes the softmax operations in warpgroup A to be scheduled while warpgroup B is performing its WGMMA matmuls. After a small number of iterations the roles swap: warpgroup B's softmax runs while warpgroup A performs matmuls, and so on—hence the "ping‑pong" terminology.

This explicit orchestration yields a richer overlap between the expensive, high‑throughput Tensor Core matmuls and the comparatively low‑throughput elementwise softmax computations (exponentials, row reductions). The synchronization points serve to serialize the GEMM issuance in a way that maximizes concurrency between GEMMs of one warpgroup and the softmax of the other, rather than allowing the compiler or hardware to interleave them suboptimally.

Practically, this ping‑pong scheme is implemented by ordering \texttt{bar.sync} uses so that GEMM1 (the $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\,\mathbf{V}$ matmul) of iteration $j$ and GEMM0 (the $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ matmul) of iteration $j+1$ in the same warpgroup are scheduled before the corresponding GEMMs in the other warpgroup. In experiments reported in the paper, this strategy improved measured TFLOPs for the FP16 forward pass with head dimension $128$ and sequence length $8192$ from about $570$ TFLOPs to roughly $620$–$640$ TFLOPs.

# Why ping‑pong helps: SFU throughput ($\sim$3.9 TFLOPs) vs FP16 matmul ($\sim$989 TFLOPs) and the FP8 gap widening

The performance motivation for ping‑pong scheduling is a throughput imbalance between the Tensor Core matmuls and the scalar/vector special-function work needed by softmax. On the H100 SXM5 the peak FP16 Tensor Core matmul throughput is on the order of $989$ TFLOPs, while the throughput available for special functions (exponentials, etc.) is much lower: roughly $3.9$ TFLOPs according to the CUDA programming guide. Those two numbers imply that per‑operation throughput for exponentials is several orders of magnitude lower than for matmul.

To make the intuition concrete, for an FP16 forward pass with head dimension $128$ the algorithm carries many more matmul floating-point operations than exponential operations (the paper notes roughly $512\times$ more matmul FLOPs than exponential FLOPs for this setting). But because exponentials can be issued at only about $1/256$ the throughput of matmuls, their time share becomes significant: the lower SFU throughput and the cost per exponential mean that softmax work, if executed serially on the same warps as Tensor Core matmuls, can consume a substantial fraction of the execution cycles and reduce Tensor Core utilization.

Ping‑pong scheduling hides these softmax cycles under the matmul cycles of the other consumer warpgroup. The problem is even more acute with FP8: Tensor Core matmul throughput roughly doubles when running FP8, while the SFU throughput for exponentials remains the same. That widens the mismatch and makes overlapping softmax under matmul via ping‑pong scheduling even more valuable for keeping Tensor Cores saturated.

# Handling MQA/GQA efficiently by indexing to avoid duplicating K/V in GMEM

Multi‑query attention (MQA) and grouped‑query attention (GQA) are attention variants where multiple heads share the same key/value pairs or use grouped queries. In a naive implementation one might physically duplicate the needed $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ tiles in global memory to match the per-head layout expected by the kernel. FlashAttention-3 avoids extra memory duplication by following the approach used in FlashAttention-2: the kernel's tensor indexing is adapted so that a single set of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ tiles in HBM can be reused across the multiple heads or query groups that need them.

Concretely, the producer issues TMA loads of the unique $\mathbf{K}_j,\mathbf{V}_j$ tiles and places them into the circular SMEM buffer once; consumer warpgroups use the adjusted indexing logic to read the same SMEM-resident tiles for all heads or groups that require them. This saves global memory bandwidth and HBM capacity, and fits naturally into the producer–consumer model: the producer never needs to duplicate writes for every head, and the consumers simply map the requested head/group to the appropriate stage in the buffer.

---

Taken together, these pieces—warp-specialization, an s-stage circular SMEM buffer with commit/wait/release semantics, asynchronous TMA-driven loads, SS/RS variants of WGMMA matmuls, register repartitioning via \texttt{setmaxnreg}, and ping‑pong scheduling between consumer warpgroups—implement the CTA‑level FlashAttention-3 forward pass without intra‑consumer GEMM/softmax overlap. The design keeps the Tensor Cores busy while minimizing global memory traffic and avoiding the need to materialize intermediate $N\times N$ matrices, achieving high utilization on modern asynchronous GPU hardware.

Overlapping GEMMs and Softmax Within a Warpgroup

# Sequential dependency problem: GEMM0 → softmax → GEMM1 within one iteration
In the standard block-wise fused attention inner loop, each iteration performs three operations in strict sequence on a given pair of blocks (for a CTA operating on a query tile $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and a key/value block index $j$). First, a block matmul computes the score block
$\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$ (call this GEMM0). Second, a local softmax (with the usual row‑wise numerically stable rescaling) transforms $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}$ into an unnormalized probability block $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$ and updates the running normalization accumulators $m_i,\ell_i$. Finally, the algorithm uses that probability block to update the output accumulator via another matmul, $\mathbf{O}_i \mathrel{+}= \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$ (call this GEMM1). Writing the softmax bookkeeping explicitly,
\[
m_i \leftarrow \max(m_i^{\text{old}},\, \mathrm{rowmax}(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)})),\qquad
\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} = \exp\!\big(\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} - m_i\big),
\]
and
\[
\ell_i \leftarrow \exp(m_i^{\text{old}} - m_i)\,\ell_i + \mathrm{rowsum}\big(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}\big),
\]
after which the $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$ GEMM occurs.

These three steps constitute a hard, per-iteration dependency chain: GEMM0 → softmax → GEMM1. On modern GPUs such as Hopper this is problematic because the devices contain specialized, high-throughput matrix‑multiply units (Tensor Cores / WGMMA) and separate units for scalar and special functions (e.g., exponentials). In practical terms the matmul hardware can deliver orders of magnitude more raw throughput than the special-function units used by softmax. The paper reports, for example, H100 FP16 matmul throughput on the order of hundreds of TFLOPs while exponentials are provided at only a few TFLOPs. The result is that forcing the softmax to sit in the middle of the matmul workstream prevents the high-throughput Tensor Cores from being fully utilized: GEMMs must wait for the comparatively slow softmax to finish before proceeding, leaving the most powerful compute resources idle during the softmax stage.

# Pipelining across iterations: buffering Scurrent and Snext in registers
The central idea to break this intra-iteration serialization is to pipeline the dependency chain across iterations: compute the next GEMM0 while you perform the softmax for the current iteration, and compute the current GEMM1 in parallel with softmax for the next iteration. To make this practical at warpgroup granularity, FlashAttention-3 holds one additional block of scores in fast storage (registers), so the warpgroup always carries two score blocks in flight: $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ and $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$.

Concretely, before entering the steady-state loop the kernel computes an initial $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_0^\top$ and reduces it to initialize $m_i,\ell_i$ and $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}}$. Inside each loop iteration the kernel then launches an asynchronous WGMMA to compute $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$ and stores the partial result in registers (not in shared memory), while simultaneously launching the asynchronous WGMMA that performs the $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}}\mathbf{V}_{j-1}$ update. Once $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ is ready, the warpgroup performs the softmax updates (which transform $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ into $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{next}}$ and update $m_i,\ell_i$). Because both $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ and $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ are resident in registers, the softmax sees its input without extra shared/global-memory traffic.

Storing $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ in registers is what enables the overlap: registers are the fastest storage and do not serialize accesses the way reads/writes to shared memory or global memory would. The cost of this choice is additional register usage proportional to the score‑block size (roughly $B_r\times B_c$ floats per threadblock, partitioned across threads), which must be balanced against the kernel's register budget and occupancy constraints.

# Two-stage loop structure: WGMMA for Snext (commit, no wait), WGMMA for O update (commit, no wait), softmax for Snext, rescale O
The 2-stage pipeline implemented in FlashAttention-3 rearranges the inner loop so that two asynchronous WGMMA calls are in flight each iteration and the softmax work sits between their issue and their completion. A readable description of the steady-state iteration (for $1\le j < T_c-1$) is:

1. Wait until the key tile $\mathbf{K}_j$ is resident in shared memory, then launch an asynchronous WGMMA to compute
   $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$. Issue the WGMMA and "commit" it, but do not wait for completion immediately.

2. Wait until the value tile $\mathbf{V}_{j-1}$ is resident, then launch an asynchronous WGMMA to compute the output update
   $\mathbf{O}_i \mathrel{+}= \widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}} \mathbf{V}_{j-1}$. Again, commit the WGMMA and do not wait.

3. Now explicitly wait for the WGMMA that produced $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ to complete, and perform the softmax-style reductions on $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ to produce $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{next}}$ and update $m_i,\ell_i$.

4. After softmax completes, wait for the WGMMA that computes the $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}}\mathbf{V}_{j-1}$ result to finish, then finish the rescaling step of the output accumulator $\mathbf{O}_i$ (applying the diagonal scaling that comes from the running normalization) and release the corresponding circular‑buffer stages.

5. Slide the window: copy $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ into the role of $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ and proceed to the next iteration.

Two points deserve emphasis. First, both WGMMA calls are dispatched with "commit but do not wait", so the Tensor Core work begins and runs independently of the thread's scalar flow. Second, the scalar softmax work is performed only after the $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ WGMMA finishes, but crucially while the other WGMMA (the output update) is still executing; this places the slow softmax operations on the GPU's special-function/single‑thread units concurrently with the Tensor Core matmuls, enabling hardware parallelism that the naive per-iteration ordering cannot exploit.

# Commit and wait points and their effect on true concurrency
"Commit" and "wait" points in this pipeline are the mechanism that converts the algorithmic overlap into real hardware concurrency. A commit (issue-without-wait) hands the WGMMA instruction to the device and allows the issuing warp to continue executing subsequent scalar instructions immediately; the hardware tensor cores then carry out the matmul asynchronously. A subsequent explicit wait (or synchronization) is necessary before any code uses the matmul's outputs; the wait enforces the data dependency.

The scheduling pattern in the two-stage loop places waits strategically: the kernel waits first for the $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ WGMMA to complete (so the softmax has a valid input) and only later waits for the $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}}\mathbf{V}_{j-1}$ WGMMA to complete (so the rescaling can be applied). This ordering is critical: it ensures that when the softmax runs (which is bound to the special-function units), the tensor cores are busy finishing the output update or computing the next $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$, so the expensive matmul units are not idle. In practical terms, commit/no-wait followed by a later wait turns a sequence that was serial into one with overlapped execution intervals on different hardware engines, increasing aggregate utilization.

It is important to remember correctness constraints: any use of a WGMMA result must be preceded by a wait for that WGMMA. The algorithm carefully sequences the waits so that correctness is preserved while keeping them as late as possible to maximize overlap.

# Register pressure trade-offs: additional storage for Snext ($B_r\times B_c$) vs larger tiles for throughput
Holding $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ and intermediate context in registers is the enabler of the two-stage overlap, but registers are a scarce resource. The extra register footprint is roughly proportional to the score‑block shape, i.e., $B_r\times B_c$ floating-point numbers (distributed across threads in the warpgroup). In addition each thread must retain other per‑thread temporaries (accumulators, indices, $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}$ fragments, and softmax scalars like the running maxima and sums). On GPUs there are fixed limits on registers per thread and per SM; the paper points out that register allocation can be manipulated between warpgroups (e.g., via \texttt{setmaxnreg}) to favor the warps performing WGMMA.

This creates a concrete tuning trade-off. Larger tile sizes ($B_r$, $B_c$) usually improve the amortized efficiency of WGMMA (fewer kernel overheads and better utilization of Tensor Cores), but they also blow up the register footprint required to hold $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ and other temporaries. If register usage becomes excessive, the compiler may lower occupancy (fewer concurrent CTAs per SM) or spill registers to shared memory, both of which can negate the expected throughput gains. Thus optimizing the 2-stage pipeline means balancing tile sizes and pipeline depth so that register pressure remains within practical limits for the target SM while still delivering high tensor‑core utilization.

# Compiler reordering risk and verifying SASS to confirm overlap
A practical complication is that the high‑level kernel code is compiled by NVCC and the backend optimizer, which may reorder instructions in pursuit of local optimizations. If the compiler moves waits earlier or re-sequences instructions across the points where you intended to commit and continue, the intended hardware overlap can be lost. For example, if a wait that you intended to defer is hoisted by the compiler to immediately follow the commit, the WGMMA will run but the thread will not perform the intervening softmax until the wait completes; this destroys the benefit you sought.

The paper therefore emphasizes the need to validate the generated binary at the SASS (assembly) level. Inspecting the emitted SASS lets you confirm that the WGMMA issue instructions (and accompanying commit semantics) are present in the order you expect and that waits/barriers remain placed so that the softmax code is emitted between the commit of one WGMMA and the wait for another. The authors report that such an analysis of their SASS confirmed the expected overlapped instruction stream for the 2-stage version. If reordering is observed, remedies include restructuring the code to expose the intended dependencies more rigidly or using explicit synchronization primitives so the compiler cannot legally transform the sequence.

# Three-stage pipeline variant: potential benefits and increased register demand
The 2-stage pipeline is a balanced compromise between added parallelism and register cost. Extending the idea to a 3-stage pipeline can in principle hide even more of the softmax work behind matmuls: one would keep additional future score blocks (e.g., $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$, $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$, $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next2}}$) and have three asynchronous WGMMA slots staggered across iterations. This increases the window of concurrency so that more of the special‑function and scalar work can execute while multiple Tensor Core matmuls are in flight.

The downside, however, is steeper register demand. Each extra stage requires retaining another $B_r\times B_c$ score block (or other intermediates) across iterations, so the register budget grows linearly with pipeline depth. At some point the marginal benefit of an additional stage is outweighed by the loss of occupancy or the need to spill to shared memory, which reintroduces latency. The paper notes this trade-off and provides a 3-stage variant description and evaluation elsewhere; in practice the optimal pipeline depth is an empirical balance driven by the kernel's register usage, target tile sizes, and the hardware register limits.

# Relationship to SMEM ring buffer depth ($s$) and choosing pipeline depth
The shared-memory circular buffer used to shuttle key/value tiles from the producer warps to the consumer warps has $s$ stages. The algorithm's ability to keep multiple iterations outstanding is bounded by this buffer depth: each in-flight block of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ occupies one stage of the ring until the consumer releases it. Thus the number of logical pipeline stages you can sustain without forcing the producer to stall is upper-bounded by $s$.

That said, the pipeline depth for WGMMA‑softmax overlap need not equal $s$; it must simply be no greater than $s$ to avoid buffer contention. In practice you choose the pipeline depth by jointly considering (a) the ring buffer depth $s$ (which determines how many key/value tiles can be outstanding from the producer), (b) the register budget (which limits how many score blocks you can keep across iterations), and (c) the desired tile sizes for efficient WGMMA. A deeper ring buffer gives more freedom to let the producer lead the consumer and thus supports deeper pipelining, but it consumes more SMEM and increases synchronization bookkeeping. The empirical tuning that produced FlashAttention-3 chooses these parameters to maximize Tensor Core utilization without exceeding the register and SMEM resources of the target SM.

In summary, the two-stage GEMM–softmax pipeline breaks the hard per-iteration GEMM0→softmax→GEMM1 dependency by staging an extra score block in registers and issuing asynchronous WGMMA operations with deferred waits. This carefully sequenced commit/wait pattern lets the slow softmax operations execute concurrently with high-throughput matmuls on the Tensor Cores, substantially improving utilization on architectures that expose asynchronous tensor and memory engines. The design requires careful tuning of tile sizes, pipeline depth, and register allocation, and must be validated at the SASS level to ensure the compiler preserves the intended instruction ordering.

FP8 Efficiency: Layout Constraints and In-Kernel Transpose

# FP8 WGMMA constraints: only k-major operands accepted from SMEM
On Hopper-class GPUs the high-throughput FP8 path through the Tensor Cores is exposed by the warpgroup-wide WGMMA instruction. WGMMA implements a tiled matrix multiply of the form $A \times B^{\top}$, where $A$ is an $M\times K$ matrix and $B$ is an $N\times K$ matrix (so that $A\times B^{\top}$ yields an $M\times N$ output). For correct and efficient operation WGMMA imposes layout expectations on how the two input operands are laid out in shared memory (SMEM). We say an operand is mn-major if it is contiguous in the outer ($M$ or $N$) dimension, and k-major if it is contiguous in the inner ($K$) dimension.

A crucial constraint for FP8 WGMMA (distinct from the FP16 case) is that when an operand is sourced from SMEM it must be provided in k-major format. In other words, the contiguous, fastest-varying memory dimension for the operand must be the inner K dimension. By contrast, FP16 WGMMA accepts either mn-major or k-major operands from SMEM, so code written for FP16 can be more permissive about how tiles are laid out. This FP8 restriction is fundamental: it limits which memory layouts can be fed directly into the FP8 Tensor Cores, and therefore drives how upstream data must be arranged before invoking the FP8 WGMMAs.

# Why V must be contiguous in sequence dimension for the second GEMM
To see why this layout constraint matters for attention, follow the shapes used in FlashAttention-3. For a given query tile $\mathbf{Q}_i$ of shape $B_r\times d$ and a key/value tile $\mathbf{K}_j,\mathbf{V}_j$ of shape $B_c\times d$, the score tile is computed as
$\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^{\top}$, which is $B_r\times B_c$. After softmax we obtain $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$ with the same $B_r\times B_c$ shape, and the second GEMM in the inner loop computes
$\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)} \mathbf{V}_j$, an operation with shapes $(B_r\times B_c)\times(B_c\times d) \rightarrow (B_r\times d)$.

WGMMA expects inputs in the $A\times B^{\top}$ form, so the second GEMM can be expressed with $A=\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$ (an $M\times K$ matrix with $M=B_r$, $K=B_c$) and $B=\mathbf{V}_j^{\top}$ (an $N\times K$ matrix with $N=d$, $K=B_c$). For FP8 WGMMA the k-major requirement therefore refers to the K dimension equal to $B_c$ — the sequence-length block size. Concretely: the operand corresponding to $B$ (here $\mathbf{V}_j^{\top}$) must be contiguous along its inner (K) dimension, which in the original, natural layout of $\mathbf{V}_j$ means that the tile of $\mathbf{V}$ stored in SMEM must be contiguous in the sequence-length dimension (the $B_c$ axis), not in the head dimension $d$.

This is at odds with the standard memory layout used by most frameworks, where tensors $Q,K,V$ are stored with the head dimension as the fastest-varying (contiguous) dimension. That conventional layout makes each row (corresponding to a token or sequence position) contiguous across the head features, i.e., memory-major in $d$. But FP8 WGMMA requires the tile fed in to be contiguous in the $B_c$ direction. Thus, to invoke FP8 WGMMA for the second GEMM we must present $\mathbf{V}_j$ (or equivalently $\mathbf{V}_j^\top$) with the contiguous dimension swapped — in short, a transpose of the $[B_c,d]$ tile is required before or during the kernel.

# Why not pre-transpose V in GMEM: memory bandwidth cost and integration issues
Given the need for $\mathbf{V}$ tiles that are contiguous in the sequence dimension, one could imagine preprocessing $\mathbf{V}$ once in global memory (GMEM) so that all subsequent loads already have the desired layout. The paper sketches two ways of doing that: (1a) fuse the transpose into an upstream operation (for example, the rotary-embedding epilogue) so the upstream write produces the transposed layout directly; or (1b) run a standalone global-memory transpose kernel prior to attention.

Both options have practical drawbacks. Fusing a transpose into a preceding library operation (1a) makes the primitive harder to adopt as a general-purpose attention operator: it creates coupling between attention and upstream layers and complicates library integration. Running an extra global-memory transpose kernel (1b) costs an additional full pass over the tensor in HBM. In inference and other memory-bandwidth-bound regimes this extra pass is expensive: even a highly-optimized transpose kernel approaches device bandwidth but still consumes precious memory bandwidth and increases end-to-end latency. In short, a GMEM pre-transpose either causes integration headaches or incurs a costly extra global memory traffic step, so neither option is attractive for a general high-performance attention primitive.

# In-kernel transpose of V tiles using LDSM (ldmatrix) and STSM (stmatrix) with FP8 packing
Because a GMEM transpose is undesirable, FlashAttention-3 chooses to transpose tiles of $\mathbf{V}$ inside the attention kernel after they are loaded into SMEM. The key GPU-level primitives used for this in-kernel transpose are the warp-scoped matrix load/store instructions LDSM (ldmatrix) and STSM (stmatrix). These instructions move a collective block of data between SMEM and the register file (RMEM) for the warp at a fine-grained tile granularity. In the PTX documentation LDSM/STSM are described as operating on an $8\times 8$ tile of 16-bit elements; for FP8 we exploit packing so that two 8-bit FP8 values occupy a single 16-bit lane. By packing pairs of FP8 elements into 16-bit lanes we can therefore use LDSM/STSM as an efficient warp-level mechanism to load, permute, and store the data needed for an in-kernel transpose.

There is an important caveat: the transpose variants of LDSM/STSM cannot themselves split the packed 16-bit lanes into separate 8-bit components. That means an in-kernel transpose sequence must include additional register-level movements between the LDSM load and the STSM store to rearrange the packed bytes correctly. The FlashAttention-3 implementation uses those additional register permutations to realize the logical transpose of the $B_c\times d$ tile while keeping the operation register-efficient. Because LDSM/STSM move a 128-byte region for a warp and are implemented with minimal register overhead, they are well suited to execute this per-tile transpose inside the producer warpgroup.

# Executing transposes in the producer warpgroup and overlapping them under consumer WGMMAs
LDSM/STSM are register-efficient and amenable to execution by the producer warps in the CTA. FlashAttention-3 leverages this by assigning the in-kernel transpose to the producer warpgroup so that the producer both issues the TMA loads and executes the LDSM/STSM-based transpose before the consumer needs the transposed tile. Critically, the transpose work can be scheduled so it runs in the "shadow" of the consumer's tensor-core work: after the first iteration, the kernel arranges that the transpose of the next $\mathbf{V}$ tile is performed while the consumer is executing the two WGMMAs that involve the preceding $\mathbf{V}$ tile and the current $\mathbf{K}$ tile.

This producer-side, overlapped transpose schedule has two practical advantages. First, it hides most of the transpose cost behind asynchronous tensor-core matmuls, so the extra CPU/GPU cycles required for in-kernel rearrangement do not add a separate global synchronization point or a standalone memory pass. Second, keeping the transpose inside the CTA means the layout transformation remains local to the attention kernel: there is no need to change how other library primitives produce or consume $V$ in global memory, preserving modularity and easing integration.

# FP8 accumulator vs operand-A register layout mismatch for back-to-back FP8 WGMMAs
Beyond the SMEM layout requirement of k-major operands, another low-level obstacle appears when attempting to chain FP8 WGMMAs: the register-file layout used by the FP32 accumulator produced by one FP8 WGMMA is not the same as the register ordering that the next WGMMA expects for its operand A. In other words, after a first FP8 WGMMA produces partial or full FP32 accumulator fragments across the warp's registers, those registers are arranged in a different per-thread sequence than the per-thread register ordering required to treat the accumulator as the A operand of a subsequent FP8 WGMMA.

This mismatch is an artifact of how WGMMA packs inputs and accumulators across threads and lanes in the warp for FP8 precision. The result is that a back-to-back sequence of FP8 WGMMAs cannot be invoked directly: the accumulator values must be rearranged in registers into the operand format expected by the next WGMMA. If left unaddressed, this causes either incorrect results or the need for expensive cross-thread moves (e.g., warp shuffles) to change register ownership.

# Resolving with byte-level register permutation (e.g., d0 d1 d4 d5 d2 d3 d6 d7 pattern) replicated across fragments
The resolution in FlashAttention-3 is to perform a deterministic byte-level permutation of the accumulator registers immediately after the first WGMMA, using byte-permute and other register-level instructions. Concretely, the implementation reorders the per-thread byte sequence in each register fragment from the accumulator's native ordering into the operand-A ordering required by the subsequent WGMMA. The paper gives an example target permutation sequence:
\[
\{\,\texttt{d0}\ \texttt{d1}\ \texttt{d4}\ \texttt{d5}\ \texttt{d2}\ \texttt{d3}\ \texttt{d6}\ \texttt{d7}\,\},
\]
and this small permutation pattern is replicated across every 8-byte fragment in the register block. Applying this byte-level permute to the accumulator transforms its layout so that it can be consumed as an operand by the next FP8 WGMMA without requiring cross-thread shuffles.

From a logical viewpoint this register permutation effects a column permutation of the corresponding $\mathbf{P}$ tile (the softmaxed scores). For example, columns that were originally ordered as $[0,1,8,9,\ldots]$ may become the first four columns after permutation. Once the accumulator registers are permuted into the correct operand-A layout, the chain of FP8 WGMMAs can proceed directly and the Tensor Core hardware will compute the intended matrix products.

# Coordinated row permutation when writing V tiles in-transpose to avoid cross-thread shuffles
Permuting the accumulator registers alone is not sufficient to guarantee correct results: the other input to the second GEMM, the $\mathbf{V}$ tile, must be laid out in SMEM with a row ordering that matches the column permutation introduced by the accumulator permutation. FlashAttention-3 exploits the fact that the in-kernel transpose of $\mathbf{V}$ gives full control over how the tile is written into SMEM. During the in-kernel transpose step the producer warp writes the $\mathbf{V}$ tile with a coordinated row permutation that exactly complements the accumulator permutation applied in registers.

By producing a matching row permutation in the transposed $\mathbf{V}$ tile, the kernel ensures that the permuted accumulator and the permuted $\mathbf{V}$ align so that the second WGMMA yields the correct result. This coordinated rearrangement removes the need to do cross-thread shuffle instructions (which exchange register ownership across threads and are less efficient or more complex), because the entire permutation is handled locally: accumulator bytes are permuted inside each thread's registers, and $\mathbf{V}$ rows are permuted when writing into SMEM. The net effect is a coherent reordering visible to the WGMMA inputs without inter-thread communication.

# TMA cannot change contiguous dimension: implications for the load–transpose schedule
A final practical constraint drives the decision to perform the transpose in-kernel: TMA (the Tensor Memory Accelerator used for asynchronous GMEM-to-SMEM copies) does not change which dimension of the tile is contiguous. In other words, a TMA load copies the data from global memory into SMEM preserving the same contiguous (fastest-varying) dimension as it had in GMEM. Because the default framework layout for $V$ is contiguous in the head dimension $d$, a direct TMA load will not produce the k-major layout required by FP8 WGMMA.

Consequently, the kernel must explicitly perform a local transpose after the TMA load. The load–transpose schedule therefore becomes: TMA loads the $[B_c,d]$ tile into SMEM in the original layout (contiguous in $d$); the producer warp then executes LDSM to pull register fragments into RMEM, performs the required packed-byte register permutations to effect the tile transpose (and to produce the row permutation complementary to the accumulator layout permute, if needed), and then uses STSM to write the transposed (and possibly permuted) tile back into SMEM in the k-major, FP8-ready format. Because these steps are executed by the producer warps and can be overlapped under asynchronous FP8 WGMMAs executed by the consumers, the extra work to change contiguous dimension is hidden and does not require an additional global-memory pass.

# Summary
Taken together, these layout and scheduling techniques let FlashAttention-3 invoke the high-throughput FP8 Tensor Cores for both GEMMs in the attention inner loop without paying a global-memory transpose cost or resorting to expensive cross-thread shuffles. The kernel 1) recognizes that FP8 WGMMA demands k-major SMEM operands (forcing $\mathbf{V}$ tiles to be contiguous in the sequence dimension), 2) avoids a separate GMEM transpose by performing a warp-level in-kernel transpose with LDSM/STSM and packed-FP8 handling, 3) repairs the accumulator-versus-operand register-layout mismatch with deterministic byte-level register permutations, and 4) writes $\mathbf{V}$ tiles with a coordinated row permutation so the permuted accumulator and permuted $\mathbf{V}$ line up for the subsequent WGMMA. These measures preserve correctness, respect the TMA constraint that it cannot change the contiguous dimension, and enable the large FP8 TFLOPs/s gains reported for FlashAttention-3.

FP8 Accuracy: Block Quantization and Incoherent Processing

# FP8 e4m3 format: limited mantissa and dynamic range vs FP16/BF16

Modern GPUs (for example, NVIDIA Hopper) offer FP8 (e4m3) as a low-precision option that can significantly raise peak matmul throughput: the hardware WGMMA path can target FP8 and deliver roughly 2× the matmul throughput per SM compared to FP16/BF16. That gain in raw throughput, however, comes at the cost of much coarser numeric representation. In the e4m3 variant of FP8, only 3 bits are used for the significand (mantissa) and 4 bits for the exponent. With so few mantissa bits, FP8 represents numbers only roughly — small relative differences are lost — and with only 4 exponent bits the dynamic range is also restricted compared to FP16 or BF16. The net effect is higher numerical error in arithmetic and a much greater sensitivity to value distributions (especially to large, infrequent values) than in the wider formats.

Because attention computes many inner products and then applies a softmax, the representation quality for the inputs $\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}$ matters: small relative errors in dot products can change the softmax weights appreciably, and those errors can propagate to the final output. Thus, making FP8 practical for attention requires algorithmic strategies that control quantization error even while enjoying the increased throughput.

# Outlier-heavy activations in LLMs and pitfalls of per-tensor scaling

Large models and modern LLM activations are often “heavy tailed”: most activations are moderate, but a tiny fraction of elements can be much larger in magnitude than the rest. Such outliers are well documented in practice and were explicitly simulated in the paper’s numerical tests by sampling each entry of $\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}$ as
$\mathcal{N}(0,1) + \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.001)\cdot \mathcal{N}(0,100)$, i.e., normally distributed values with a 0.1% chance of an additional large Gaussian term. This kind of distribution produces rare but very large entries that dominate per-tensor statistics.

A common and simple FP8 strategy is per-tensor scaling: one scalar scale is chosen for an entire tensor so that values fit into FP8’s representable range before rounding. Per-tensor scaling is cheap and convenient, but it is also brittle in the presence of outliers: a single very large element forces the global scale to be large, which effectively compresses all the remaining elements toward zero relative to that scale. With only 3 bits of mantissa, most of the representational precision for the many typical elements is lost, producing large quantization error. In other words, a single outlier can force most entries to be represented with very few effective significant bits, harming downstream dot products and softmax computations.

# Block quantization: per-block scales for Q, K, V (B_r x d or B_c x d) aligned with algorithmic tiles

Block quantization addresses the limitation of per-tensor scaling by using one scale factor per block rather than one per whole tensor. FlashAttention-3’s computation already operates on tiles: queries come in blocks of size $B_r \times d$ (we denote the query block size by $B_r$ and the head dimension by $d$), and keys/values naturally come in blocks of size $B_c \times d$. Block quantization simply matches the quantization granularity to those tiles: for each query block and for each key/value block, keep a separate scalar scale and quantize those $B_r\times d$ or $B_c\times d$ blocks independently into FP8.

Because each block typically has a much smaller dynamic range than an entire tensor, a per-block scale lets the quantized block use FP8’s exponent and mantissa more effectively; the result is substantially lower quantization error than a single global scale. The storage overhead is minimal: one scalar per block (the scale) must be remembered, but the blocks and their scales align directly with the algorithm’s tiling so the bookkeeping is simple. Conceptually, if a block’s quantized representation is denoted $\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_i$ together with its scale $\gamma_{Q,i}$ (so the dequantized block is approximated as $\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_i \approx \gamma_{Q,i}\,\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_i$), then each block is rescaled only by its own factor rather than by a tensor-wide scalar. This localizes the effect of outliers to the few blocks that actually contain them.

# Fusing block quantization with rotary embedding without extra runtime

In practice, the values that feed attention often pass through preprocessing steps such as rotary position embeddings (or other token-wise transforms) immediately before entering the attention kernel. FlashAttention-3 exploits this pipeline structure: the block quantization step can be fused into the same memory-bound pass that computes rotary embeddings (or any similar pre-attention per-token transform). Because rotary embedding is memory-bandwidth bound, doing the per-block scaling/quantization in the same read/write pass does not meaningfully increase runtime; the memory traffic dominates and the extra arithmetic for computing a block’s scale and applying it is cheap in comparison.

Operationally, fusing means that when we visit a block of $\mathbf{Q}$ or $\mathbf{K}/\mathbf{V}$ to apply the rotary embedding, we also compute the block’s scale and write out the FP8 representation and the scalar scale. The result is that block quantization costs essentially the same wall-clock time as applying rotary embedding alone, i.e., it is performed “at no additional slowdown” in real runs, as observed in the paper.

# Adjusting S blocks to account for per-block scales at no extra compute

When both operands are quantized blockwise, their dot-products inherit simple multiplicative corrections that are easy to apply. Suppose a query block and a key block are quantized to $\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_i$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_j$ with dequantization scales $\gamma_{Q,i}$ and $\gamma_{K,j}$ so that $\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_i \approx \gamma_{Q,i}\,\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_i$ and $\hat{\mathbf{K}}_j \approx \gamma_{K,j}\,\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_j$. Then the mixed-precision block of scores satisfies
\[
\mathbf{S}_{ij} \;=\; \hat{\mathbf{Q}}_i\,\hat{\mathbf{K}}_j^\top
\;\approx\; \bigl(\gamma_{Q,i}\gamma_{K,j}\bigr)\,\bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_i\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_j^\top\bigr)
\;=\; \bigl(\gamma_{Q,i}\gamma_{K,j}\bigr)\,\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{ij},
\]
where $\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{ij}$ denotes the dot-product computed from the quantized blocks. The correction from quantization is thus a single scalar multiplier per block: multiply each entry of $\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{ij}$ by $\gamma_{Q,i}\gamma_{K,j}$ to recover the approximate full-precision block $\mathbf{S}_{ij}$.

Because FlashAttention-3 already computes and reduces scores block-by-block inside the kernel, this scalar correction is trivial to accommodate: the scalar product $\gamma_{Q,i}\gamma_{K,j}$ can be applied to a block of $\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{ij}$ in the same register/local arithmetic context where the block is already being processed. In other words, the correction is a per-block scalar multiply and can be folded into existing rescaling and softmax steps so that it does not require additional global memory reads/writes or extra heavyweight operations. The algorithmic tiling therefore makes block quantization a natural, low-overhead fit.

# Incoherent processing: multiply Q and K by the same random orthogonal matrix M

Block quantization reduces error by shrinking the per-block dynamic range, but outliers can still dominate individual blocks. Incoherent processing further reduces quantization error by mixing coordinates before quantization so that outliers are spread rather than concentrated. Concretely, FlashAttention-3 multiplies both $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ on the right by the same orthogonal matrix $M$ prior to quantization and tile-wise FP8 encoding. The key algebraic fact is that if $M$ is orthogonal (so $M M^\top = I$), then the attention scores are invariant under this common transform:
\[
(\mathbf{Q}M)(\mathbf{K}M)^\top \;=\; \mathbf{Q}\,M\,M^\top\,\mathbf{K}^\top \;=\; \mathbf{Q}\,\mathbf{K}^\top.
\]
Hence, in exact arithmetic the attention computation is unchanged by this pre-multiplication. But because quantization is applied after multiplication by $M$, the distribution of entries seen by the quantizer is different: each coordinate of $\mathbf{Q}M$ or $\mathbf{K}M$ is a linear combination of many original coordinates, so a single extreme value in one coordinate becomes spread out across many coordinates. After blockwise quantization, this “incoherency” reduces the peak-to-typical ratios that cause large quantization errors, improving numeric fidelity.

The paper refers to this idea as “incoherent processing,” and emphasizes that multiplying by the same $M$ on both sides preserves the exact attention result in the unquantized case while making the subsequent quantization far less harmful.

# Using Hadamard with random signs for O(d log d) cost and fusion with rotary

A naive dense orthogonal $d\times d$ matrix costs $O(d^2)$ time to apply, which would defeat the performance gains from using FP8. FlashAttention-3 uses a structured orthogonal transform that is both effective at spreading out outliers and cheap to apply: the product of random diagonal sign matrices (diagonals of $\pm 1$) and the Hadamard matrix. This construction gives an orthogonal $M$ that can be applied using the fast Walsh–Hadamard transform in $O(d\log d)$ time rather than $O(d^2)$.

Because the Hadamard transform and diagonal sign multiplications are simple elementwise or structured operations, they can also be fused with other per-token transforms (for example, rotary embedding) at the memory-bound stage immediately before quantization. In practice, therefore, applying $M$ does not meaningfully raise the runtime: FlashAttention-3 can combine the Hadamard-based mixing with other pre-attention operations to keep the wall-clock cost negligible compared to memory access time.

# Invariance of QK^T under common orthogonal transforms and intuition for outlier spreading

It is helpful to restate the invariance and the intuition behind why mixing helps. The algebraic invariance is immediate from orthogonality:
\[
(\mathbf{Q}M)(\mathbf{K}M)^\top
= \mathbf{Q} M M^\top \mathbf{K}^\top
= \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top.
\]
This identity guarantees that any improvements in quantized accuracy come purely from better-conditioned inputs to the quantizer, not from changing the mathematical object the attention would compute in full precision.

Intuitively, consider a block that contains an element that is orders of magnitude larger than its peers. If we quantize that block with a single scale, the presence of the outlier forces a large scale, making the typical entries small relative to that scale and thus poorly represented. If instead we first multiply by $M$, that single extreme value contributes in small amounts to many coordinates after the transform; each block now sees a milder dynamic range, and the blockwise scale can be chosen to preserve more information across entries. The Hadamard-based $M$ achieves this mixing efficiently and deterministically (given the random signs), turning localized extremes into incoherent, distributed contributions that quantize much better.

# Empirical result: up to 2.6× lower RMSE vs FP8 per-tensor scaling baseline

The combined effect of block quantization and incoherent processing was validated empirically in the paper. The baseline FP8 implementation used per-tensor scaling (with matmul accumulators in FP32 and intermediate softmax results kept in FP16). On the outlier-heavy synthetic test described earlier (each entry sampled as $\mathcal{N}(0,1) + \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.001)\cdot \mathcal{N}(0,100)$), FlashAttention-3 using block quantization together with incoherent processing reduced the root mean squared error (RMSE) by a factor of about $2.6\times$ relative to that per-tensor FP8 baseline. This shows that the two ideas — blockwise scaling aligned with FlashAttention tiles, and inexpensive orthogonal mixing before quantization — are effective in making FP8 a practical option for attention with much lower numerical degradation than a naive per-tensor quantization scheme.

Summary

Putting the pieces together: FP8 promises large throughput gains but must be handled carefully because of its very limited mantissa and exponent. Block quantization aligns natural algorithmic tiles with per-block scales so each block keeps much of its own precision. Incoherent processing (mixing by an orthogonal $M$ built from Hadamard and random signs) spreads rare outliers so they no longer dominate individual blocks. Both techniques are designed to be low-overhead in practice: block quantization is fused with pre-attention memory-bound transforms like rotary embedding, and the Hadamard-based mixing is $O(d\log d)$ and can also be fused. Empirically these ideas together produced a substantial reduction in FP8 numerical error (about $2.6\times$ RMSE improvement) compared to a simple per-tensor FP8 baseline, making low-precision attention both faster and more accurate in practice.

Backward Pass Algorithms in FlashAttention-3

This chapter explains how FlashAttention-3 (FA-3) computes the backward pass efficiently. We focus on the algorithmic structure, the numerical considerations for the row-wise softmax Jacobian, and how FA-3 reuses the forward execution model (warp-specialization, circular shared-memory buffers, asynchronous WGMMA/TMA) to avoid storing the full quadratic intermediates \(S\) and \(P\). Where appropriate we use the same tiling notation as in the forward algorithm: query blocks \(Q_i\) of size \(B_r\times d\) with \(T_r=\lceil N/B_r\rceil\) and key/value blocks \(K_j,V_j\) of size \(B_c\times d\) with \(T_c=\lceil N/B_c\rceil\). All math is written in LaTeX.

# Gradients: dV = P^T dO, dP = dO V^T, dS = dsoftmax(dP), dQ = \alpha dS K, dK = \alpha dS^\top Q

Start from the forward definitions used throughout the paper:
\[
S = \alpha\, Q K^\top,\qquad P=\mathrm{softmax}(S)\ (\text{row-wise}),\qquad O = P V,
\]
where \(Q,K,V\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}\), \(P,S\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}\), and \(\alpha\) is the usual scaling \(1/\sqrt{d}\). For a scalar loss \(\phi\) and the notation \(\mathbf{d}(\cdot)=\partial\phi/\partial(\cdot)\), the chain rule gives the following matrix-level backward relations that FA-3 must implement:

- dV (gradient wrt values):
  \[
  \mathbf{dV} = \mathbf{P}^\top \mathbf{dO}\qquad(\text{shape }N\times d).
  \]

- dP (gradient wrt softmax outputs):
  \[
  \mathbf{dP} = \mathbf{dO}\,\mathbf{V}^\top\qquad(\text{shape }N\times N).
  \]

- dS (gradient wrt attention logits), obtained by applying the row-wise softmax Jacobian to \(\mathbf{dP}\):
  \[
  \mathbf{dS} = \mathrm{dsoftmax}(\mathbf{dP}).
  \]

- dQ and dK (gradients wrt queries and keys) are ordinary GEMMs:
  \[
  \mathbf{dQ} = \alpha\,\mathbf{dS}\,\mathbf{K},\qquad
  \mathbf{dK} = \alpha\,\mathbf{dS}^\top \mathbf{Q}.
  \]

Each of these matrix equations translates to one or more block-wise GEMMs when executed with FA-3's tiling (e.g., computing \(\mathbf{dP}=\mathbf{dO}\,\mathbf{V}^\top\) is implemented as a sequence of small GEMMs between \(\mathbf{dO}_i\) and \(\mathbf{V}_j\) blocks). Notice that \(\mathbf{dS}\) is not a pure GEMM; it is formed by applying the softmax Jacobian row-wise (elementwise multiplies, row dot-products and broadcasts). The central challenge that FA-3 addresses is computing all of these quantities without materializing the full \(N\times N\) matrices \(S\) and \(P\) to global memory.

# Row-wise softmax Jacobian and stability considerations in FP32

The softmax Jacobian for a single row is the standard compact form. If for a row vector \(s\in\mathbb{R}^m\) we write \(p=\mathrm{softmax}(s)\) and an upstream gradient \(u\in\mathbb{R}^m\) (a row of \(\mathbf{dP}\)), then
\[
\mathrm{dsoftmax}(u) = J\,u,\qquad J=\mathrm{diag}(p)-p p^\top.
\]
A convenient and numerically stable way to evaluate this product is to compute the scalar
\[
\beta \;=\; p^\top u \quad(\text{a single dot-product per row}),
\]
and then
\[
\mathrm{dsoftmax}(u) \;=\; p \odot (u - \beta \mathbf{1}),
\]
where \(\odot\) is elementwise multiplication and \(\mathbf{1}\) is the all-ones vector. This formulation uses one dot-product and a vector-scale-and-subtract, which maps well to blockwise streaming implementations.

Numerical stability: the forward pass already applies the standard row-max trick to compute \(p\) (compute \(m=\max s\), then \(\tilde p=\exp(s-m)\) and normalize by \(\sum \tilde p\)). FA-3 preserves the same stability conventions and — critically — performs the softmax exponentials, normalization and the dsoftmax computations in higher-precision accumulators (FP32) even when the matmuls run in lower precision (FP16 or FP8). Keeping softmax and the dsoftmax dot-products in FP32 avoids catastrophic cancellation and overflow/underflow that would otherwise arise if exponentials or the Jacobian operations were done in narrow types. This choice is an important reason FA-3 matches or improves numerical error versus earlier implementations.

# Recomputation strategy to avoid storing S and P; streaming statistics reuse

Storing the full \(S,P\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}\) is prohibitive for long sequences. FA-3 follows the same memory-efficient philosophy as FlashAttention: it recomputes small blocks of \(S\) and \(P\) on demand in the backward pass and never writes the full matrices to HBM. The key ingredients are (1) tiling in query and key dimensions, (2) saving compact streaming statistics during the forward pass that enable reconstruction of \(P\) blocks without running a numerically-sensitive multi-block reduction, and (3) a two‑phase backward sweep that computes quantities in a streaming fashion with a small number of temporaries.

What FA-3 saves in forward: for each query block \(i\), the forward local-softmax procedure accumulates a per-row log-normalizer
\[
L_i = m_i + \log(\ell_i),
\]
where \(m_i\) is the running row-max and \(\ell_i\) the accumulated sum of exponentials relative to \(m_i\). FA-3 writes the block-wise \(L_i\) to HBM at the end of forward. These \(L_i\) are sufficient to reconstruct softmax probabilities for each block during backward: for any recomputed score block \(S_{ij}=\alpha Q_i K_j^\top\) we can form
\[
P_{ij} = \exp(S_{ij} - L_i),
\]
which yields correctly normalized entries (row-wise) because \(L_i\) is the log of the full row denominator used in the forward softmax.

High-level backward recomputation procedure (block-wise, per query-block \(i\)):

1. Initialize \(\mathbf{dP}_i \leftarrow 0\) (a \(B_r\times B_c\) or aggregated per-row structure depending on tiling), and initialize a per-row scalar accumulator \(\gamma_i\leftarrow 0\) that will hold \(p^\top \mathbf{dP}_i\) (the \(\beta\) above) as we build \(\mathbf{dP}_i\).

2. For each key block \(j\):
   - Load (via the producer/TMA) the key and value tiles \(K_j,V_j\) into SMEM.
   - Recompute the score block
     \[
     S_{ij} = \alpha\,Q_i K_j^\top
     \]
     using an asynchronous WGMMA GEMM.
   - Form the softmax block in FP32 using the saved \(L_i\):
     \[
     P_{ij} \;=\; \exp(S_{ij}-L_i).
     \]
   - Compute the two GEMMs that consume this block:
     a) Accumulate partial \(\mathbf{dV}_j\) contribution:
        \[
        \Delta \mathbf{dV}_j \;=\; P_{ij}^\top \,\mathbf{dO}_i,
        \]
        and add it into a running \(\mathbf{dV}_j\).
     b) Accumulate partial \(\mathbf{dP}_i\) contribution:
        \[
        \Delta \mathbf{dP}_i \;=\; \mathbf{dO}_i\,V_j^\top,
        \]
        and add into \(\mathbf{dP}_i\).
   - While \(\Delta\mathbf{dP}_i\) is present, compute the per-row inner product contribution
     \[
     \gamma_i \;+=\; \mathrm{rowsum}(P_{ij}\odot \Delta\mathbf{dP}_i),
     \]
     accumulating the scalar \(p^\top \mathbf{dP}_i\) for every row in the query-block \(i\).

3. After the loop over \(j\) completes, the block \(\mathbf{dP}_i\) is fully accumulated and the scalar vector \(\gamma_i\) equals \(p^\top \mathbf{dP}_i\) (row-wise). Compute the block-wise dsoftmax:
   \[
   \mathbf{dS}_i \;=\; P_i \odot (\mathbf{dP}_i - \gamma_i),
   \]
   where the operations are carried out row-wise in FP32.

4. Finally, to obtain \(\mathbf{dQ}_i\) and the contributions to \(\mathbf{dK}_j\) for all \(j\), run a second pass over key blocks \(j\):
   - For each \(j\), form GEMMs
     \[
     \Delta \mathbf{dQ}_i \;=\; \alpha\,\mathbf{dS}_i \,K_j,\qquad
     \Delta \mathbf{dK}_j \;=\; \alpha\,\mathbf{dS}_i^\top Q_i,
     \]
     accumulating into \(\mathbf{dQ}_i\) and \(\mathbf{dK}_j\), respectively.

This scheme requires two passes over the key/value blocks: the first pass recomputes \(S_{ij}\) and builds \(\mathbf{dV}\) and \(\mathbf{dP}\) while accumulating the scalar \(\gamma_i\); the second pass produces \(\mathbf{dQ}\) and \(\mathbf{dK}\) once \(\mathbf{dS}_i\) is known. Crucially, neither the full \(S\) nor the full \(P\) is ever written to global memory — only the compact per-query-block log-normalizers \(L_i\) are read from HBM and small temporary blocks are staged in SMEM or registers.

# Adapting warp-specialization and ring buffers for backward

FA-3 carries the forward execution model (producer/consumer warp-specialization and an \(s\)-stage circular SMEM buffer) over to backward. Conceptually the division of labor is the same: some warps (producer warpgroups) issue asynchronous TMA loads of \(Q_i\), \(K_j\), \(V_j\), and \(\mathbf{dO}_i\) into the shared SMEM circular buffer; other warps (consumer warpgroups) execute asynchronous WGMMA GEMMs and the non-GEMM softmax/dsoftmax logic. The ring buffer provides the same decoupling: while the consumer is finishing computations on stage \((j-1)\), the producer can already load stage \(j+s\).

Two practical adaptations for backward are worth emphasizing.

First, the consumer path now carries additional temporaries: besides \(\mathbf{O}_i\) and the running softmax statistics used in forward, the backward consumer must keep \(\mathbf{dP}_i\), the scalar per-row accumulators \(\gamma_i\), and partial accumulators for \(\mathbf{dV}_j\) and \(\mathbf{dK}_j\). FA-3 balances these extra live values between registers and SMEM with the same register reallocation trick (\verb|setmaxnreg|) used in forward: compute-heavy warps doing WGMMA get a larger register allocation, while the producer warps issuing TMAs are cheap in register use.

Second, the backward pipeline naturally maps to a ping‑pong or multi-stage schedule across warpgroups. The first pass (recompute \(S\), evaluate \(P_{ij}\), accumulate \(\mathbf{dV}_j\) and \(\mathbf{dP}_i\)) contains both GEMM and non-GEMM (exponential) work. By splitting producer and consumer roles across warpgroups and inserting barriers analogously to the forward pingpong schedule, FA-3 can overlap the low-throughput exponential and dsoftmax operations of one warpgoup with WGMMA execution of another. The same caveats apply as in the forward case: the compiler may reorder instructions unless care is taken, and adding pipeline stages increases register pressure and constrains tile sizes. In practice FA-3 reuses the same circular-buffer synchronization and warpspecialization primitives so that the backward pass benefits from asynchronous TMA and WGMMA while preserving correctness.

# GEMM count: 5 matmuls in backward vs 2 in forward; FLOPs factor (~2.5x of forward)

Counting the large matrix products clarifies the compute cost of recomputation. A straightforward accounting (and the one used in FA-3's benchmarking) is:

- Forward: two primary matmuls per full attention evaluation (per head):
  1. \(QK^\top\) (to build scores \(S\)) and
  2. \(P V\) (to build outputs \(O\)).
  That is 2 GEMMs.

- Backward (with recomputation, avoiding materializing \(S\) and \(P\)):
  0. Recompute \(QK^\top\) blocks to obtain \(P\) on the fly (GEMM).
  1. Compute \(\mathbf{dV} = P^\top \mathbf{dO}\) (GEMM).
  2. Compute \(\mathbf{dP} = \mathbf{dO}\,V^\top\) (GEMM).
  3. Compute \(\mathbf{dQ} = \alpha\,\mathbf{dS}\,K\) once \(\mathbf{dS}\) is formed (GEMM).
  4. Compute \(\mathbf{dK} = \alpha\,\mathbf{dS}^\top Q\) (GEMM).

That totals 5 matmuls. Hence the backward computational FLOPs are approximately \(5/2 = 2.5\) times the forward FLOPs. This is the same multiplier used in the paper when reporting backward FLOP counts and runtimes (the paper explicitly multiplies forward FLOPs by 2.5 to obtain backward FLOPs for benchmarking). The extra cost is the price of recomputation, paid in order to avoid writing or reading the quadratic \(S\) and \(P\) tensors from global memory.

# Opportunities and constraints for GEMM–softmax overlap in backward

FA-3 leverages hardware asynchrony (as in forward) to hide low-throughput softmax-like operations under Tensor Core GEMMs. However, the backward data dependencies place both opportunities and constraints on how much overlap is possible.

Constraints coming from data dependencies:
- For a fixed query block \(i\), the dsoftmax that produces \(\mathbf{dS}_i\) cannot be computed until \(\mathbf{dP}_i\) is fully accumulated across all key blocks \(j\). Thus the second-phase GEMMs that consume \(\mathbf{dS}_i\) (to form \(\mathbf{dQ}_i\) and per-key contributions to \(\mathbf{dK}_j\)) must be scheduled after the first phase completes for that query block.
- The necessity to compute \(P_{ij}=\exp(S_{ij}-L_i)\) in FP32 (for accuracy) forces an exponential evaluation in the first phase that is expensive in throughput relative to WGMMA.

Opportunities for overlap:
- Cross-block pipelining (ping‑pong): while one warpgroup is finishing the expensive row-wise exponentials and accumulation for query block \(i\), another warpgroup can be performing the WGMMA calls to recompute \(S\) and/or do the GEMMs for a different query block \(i+1\). FA-3's warp-specialization and circular staging make this overlap practical. This is directly analogous to the forward pass overlap that hides exponentials under GEMMs.
- Within a pass, the two GEMM-type operations associated with a given block (e.g., computing \(\Delta\mathbf{dP}_i\) and \(\Delta\mathbf{dV}_j\)) can be arranged so that one is issued as an asynchronous WGMMA and the softmax-related pointwise work (computing \(P_{ij}\) and partial inner products) is performed while the WGMMA is progressing on the next micro-task.
- The second pass (forming \(\mathbf{dQ}_i\) and \(\mathbf{dK}_j\)) is purely GEMM-heavy: it can be overlapped with remaining non-GEMM reductions from earlier query blocks if the pipeline has multiple active blocks.

Practical trade-offs: the more overlap you try to expose, the more live temporaries you must keep in registers or SMEM (e.g., saved \(\mathbf{S}_{\text{next}}\) or partial \(\mathbf{dP}\)), increasing register pressure and potentially causing spills that hurt performance. FA-3 therefore tunes depth of pipelining (2‑stage or 3‑stage variants) together with tile sizes to find the best trade-off for a given GPU (Hopper/H100 in the paper).

# Masking and sequence tiling in backward

Masked attention (for example causal masking) changes which \(S_{ij}\) and \(P_{ij}\) blocks were computed in the forward pass and therefore which blocks must be recomputed in backward. FA-3 follows the same tiling and loop boundaries in backward as were used in the forward kernel so that the backward recomputation reproduces exactly the same pattern of computed entries.

Two practical points follow:

1. Loop bounds per query block: for causal attention, a query block \(Q_i\) only attends to key blocks \(K_j\) with \(j\) up to some per-block limit (typically \(j\leq i\) when blocks align). FA-3 adapts the inner loop bounds so the backward first pass only recomputes \(S_{ij}\) and \(P_{ij}\) for those key blocks that were active in the forward pass. This reduces wasted computation and ensures the backward result matches the forward.

2. Partial blocks and intra-block masking: when a block is partially outside the allowed triangular region (e.g., the block straddles the diagonal), FA-3 computes and applies the same per-entry mask during both recomputation and accumulation (treating masked logits as \(-\infty\) so their \(\exp\) yields zero probability). The ring buffer and TMA loading handle partial tiles by still copying the full tile to SMEM but only performing softmax/GEMM work on the active subset; the load-balancing considerations discussed for forward carry over: causal masking reduces arithmetic intensity and complicates warp load balancing, so FA-3 uses the same CTA partitioning and balancing heuristics from forward.

In short, masking requires careful alignment between forward and backward tiling, but does not fundamentally change the recompute-and-stream strategy; it only restricts some loop bounds and requires attention to partial-block handling.

# Practical summary and trade-offs

- Memory versus compute: FA-3 avoids O(N^2) storage of \(S\) and \(P\) by recomputing score blocks on demand. The cost is an increase in computation in the backward pass (5 GEMMs vs 2 in forward, i.e., ~2.5× FLOPs). For long sequences the memory savings are essential and the compute cost is acceptable because GEMMs are highly optimized on GPU tensor cores.

- Numerical stability: FA-3 intentionally keeps softmax exponentials, normalizations, and dsoftmax dot-products in FP32 regardless of lower-precision GEMMs (FP16/FP8). This yields accuracy comparable to or better than earlier FlashAttention variants, and is particularly important when block quantization and FP8 operand types are used in forward.

- Execution model reuse: the same producer/consumer warp-specialization, circular shared-memory buffering, asynchronous TMA loads and asynchronous WGMMA execution that speed up the forward pass are reused and extended in the backward. This lets FA-3 hide non-GEMM softmax work under matrix-multiply activity via ping‑pong scheduling across warpgroups, at the cost of extra register pressure which must be tuned.

- Implementation complexity: compared to a naive backward that materializes \(S\) and \(P\), FA-3's approach is more complex — it needs careful tiling, correct use of saved forward block statistics \(L_i\), two-phase passes over key/value blocks, and tuned pipelining to obtain the throughput benefits. The design choices and implementation details (register balancing, number of pipelining stages, how partial blocks are handled) are the source of the empirical gains reported in the paper.

By streaming recomputed score blocks, reusing the compact forward log-normalizers, and mapping each algebraic gradient relation to the same asynchronous, warp-specialized execution model used in the forward pass, FlashAttention-3 computes the exact backward pass with minimal global-memory traffic while exploiting Hopper GPU asynchrony and tensor-core throughput.

Empirical Evaluation and Ablation Studies

# Hardware and software: H100 SXM5 80GB, CUTLASS primitives (WGMMA, TMA), FA-3 implementation
All experiments described in the paper were performed on NVIDIA Hopper H100 hardware (SXM5, 80GB). The implementation of FlashAttention-3 (FA-3) is built on top of CUTLASS primitives, in particular the asynchronous warpgroup GEMM primitive (WGMMA) and the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) primitives for bulk GMEM-to-SMEM transfers. These two hardware/software primitives are central to FA-3: WGMMA exposes high-throughput Tensor Core matmuls that can be issued asynchronously, and TMA provides an asynchronous path for loading and storing tiles between HBM (global memory) and SMEM (shared memory). FA-3 wires these primitives together in a single kernel that uses warp-specialization and software pipelining to overlap data movement, GEMMs, and the lower-throughput special-function work required by softmax (exponentials, row reductions).

The codebase for FA-3 was implemented using these CUTLASS building blocks and is open-sourced. Using vendor-provided low-level primitives (WGMMA/TMA) allows FA-3 to target Hopper-specific capabilities (asynchrony, FP8 Tensor Cores) and thus realize much higher measured utilization of the Tensor Cores than earlier implementations.

# Benchmark protocol: sequence lengths 512–16k; batch sized to 16k tokens; hidden 2048; head dims 64/128/256; causal vs non-causal
The experiments sweep sequence lengths from 512 up to 16,384 (denoted 16k in the paper). For each sequence length the batch size was chosen so that the total number of tokens across the batch was fixed at 16k tokens; this keeps aggregate work roughly constant while varying the per-sample sequence length to probe tiling and occupancy effects. The transformer hidden dimension in the benchmarks was fixed to 2048, and head dimension was varied across 64, 128, and 256. These head dimensions correspond to number of attention heads equal to 2048/64 = 32, 2048/128 = 16, and 2048/256 = 8, respectively. Benchmarks were run with and without causal masking to measure both autoregressive and full-attention regimes.

The paper reports both forward- and backward-pass timings for FP16 and forward-pass timings for FP8 (the full FP8 benchmarking results are provided in the appendix of the paper; one representative plot in the main text shows head-dimension 256). Runtime measurements are converted to throughput in TFLOPs/s using the FLOPs-accounting convention described below.

# FLOPs accounting: forward $4 \cdot \text{seqlen}^2 \cdot \text{head\_dim} \cdot \text{nheads}$ (half with causal); backward $\approx 2.5\times$ forward
To report throughput (TFLOPs/s), the paper uses a fixed FLOPs model for the attention forward pass. If we denote the sequence length by $N$, head dimension by $d$, and the number of heads by $H$, the forward-pass FLOPs are computed as
\[
\mathrm{FLOPs}_{\mathrm{forward}} \;=\; 4 \cdot N^2 \cdot d \cdot H.
\]
When causal masking is enabled (autoregressive attention), the effective number of score entries that must be computed is approximately half of the full $N \times N$ matrix, so the forward FLOPs are divided by 2 for causal runs.

The backward-pass FLOPs are approximated as a constant multiple of the forward cost. Empirically (and by accounting the number of matmuls), the paper multiplies the forward FLOPs by $2.5$ to obtain the backward FLOPs:
\[
\mathrm{FLOPs}_{\mathrm{backward}} \approx 2.5 \cdot \mathrm{FLOPs}_{\mathrm{forward}}.
\]
This factor arises because the forward pass contains about two matmuls (the $QK^\top$ and the $PV$-style operation) while the backward pass contains more matmuls when gradients are computed and some recomputation is performed; the 2.5 number summarizes that asymmetry.

Measured runtime (wall-clock seconds) is converted to TFLOPs/s by dividing the FLOPs above by the measured runtime. Percent utilization relative to device theoretical peak is the ratio of measured FLOPs/s to the device peak FLOPs/s for the corresponding precision (the paper reports the commonly quoted utilization number, e.g., FA-3 FP16 reaching 740 TFLOPs/s corresponding to roughly 75% of H100 theoretical).

# Comparisons: PyTorch standard attention, FlashAttention-2, FA-2 Triton (H100-optimized), cuDNN
FA-3 is compared to four baselines in the benchmarks:

- A "standard" attention implementation (PyTorch-style) that materializes the intermediate score matrix $\mathbf{S}$ and the softmax matrix $\mathbf{P}$ in high-bandwidth memory (HBM), thereby performing extra global-memory reads/writes.

- FlashAttention-2 (FA-2), the earlier exact fused-attention kernel that avoids materializing the full $N\times N$ intermediates but follows a more synchronous execution pattern.

- FlashAttention-2 implemented in Triton and tuned to use H100-specific instructions (an H100-optimized Triton variant of FA-2).

- The vendor (NVIDIA) implementation exposed through cuDNN, which is a closed-source highly optimized library targeting H100.

These comparisons measure how much of the benefit comes from algorithmic changes (FA-3's asynchrony-aware pipeline and FP8 support) vs. micro-architectural tuning (H100 instruction choices in Triton/cuDNN).

# Results: FA-3 FP16 $1.5$–$2.0\times$ faster forward and $1.5$–$1.75\times$ faster backward than FA-2; up to 740 TFLOPs/s (≈75%)
The main empirical findings are:

- FlashAttention-3 in FP16 achieves between $1.5\times$ and $2.0\times$ speedup over FlashAttention-2 in the forward pass, and between $1.5\times$ and $1.75\times$ in the backward pass, across the range of sequence lengths and head dimensions tested.

- Against the Triton H100-optimized FA-2, FA-3 obtains about a $1.5\times$ speedup, showing that algorithmic asynchrony and pipelining provide benefits beyond instruction-level tuning alone.

- Compared to a standard attention implementation that materializes intermediates, FA-3 is dramatically faster (reported up to $3\times$–$16\times$ faster depending on regime).

- FA-3 attains peak measured FP16 throughput up to 740 TFLOPs/s, which the paper reports as about 75% utilization of the H100 FP16 Tensor Core peak.

- For medium and long sequence lengths (roughly $1\text{k}$ tokens and up), FA-3 surpasses the performance of the vendor's cuDNN implementation in many regimes.

These results show that making the attention kernel aware of asynchronous hardware units and designing a warp-specialized, pipelined kernel yields substantial end-to-end reductions in runtime and substantially improved Tensor Core utilization.

# FP8 results: near 1.2 PFLOPs/s; competitive with cuDNN across settings
FA-3 is also adapted to exploit Hopper FP8 Tensor Cores. The FP8-enabled FA-3 forward pass reaches throughput close to $1.2$ petaFLOPs/s (1.2 PFLOPs/s) on H100 in favorable settings. The paper highlights two accuracy and performance points about FP8 FA-3:

- Performance: FP8 nearly doubles matmul throughput compared to FP16 on the same hardware; FA-3's FP8 kernels therefore achieve very high measured FLOPs/s. However, FP8 FA-3 does not always dominate for small sequence lengths or for causal masking because, at present in the reported implementation, the FP8 kernel is not configured as a persistent kernel and lacks the same load-balancing and persistent-kernel advantages present in the FP16 FA-3 kernel. This explains why cuDNN's FP8 kernels can be faster in those narrow regimes.

- Accuracy and comparison to cuDNN: FP8 FA-3 is competitive with cuDNN across many settings. More specifically, the paper notes that for head dimension $64$, FP8 FA-3 is ahead of cuDNN. For head dimensions $128$ and $256$, FA-3 FP8 is at parity with cuDNN in non-causal runs and is behind cuDNN when causal masking is used. These head-dimension and masking sensitivities reflect interactions between kernel tiling, register pressure, and the different strategies libraries use to reach high single-GPU efficiency.

To operate in FP8 efficiently FA-3 performs in-kernel layout transforms (tile transposes) and register permutations so that FP8 WGMMA operand layout constraints are satisfied. On the accuracy side FA-3 applies block quantization and incoherent processing (see the numerical accuracy section below) to reduce quantization error while still benefiting from the higher FP8 matmul throughput.

# Ablation: warp-specialization and 2-stage overlap improve from ~570 TFLOPs to ~661 TFLOPs (example setting)
The paper contains an ablation probing the contribution of the two principal FA-3 algorithmic ideas: warp-specialization (producer/consumer warps, ping-pong scheduling) and intra-warpgroup GEMM–softmax overlap via a 2-stage pipeline. The ablation was performed on a representative non-causal FP16 workload with parameters:
\[
\{\text{batch},\,\text{seqlen},\,\text{nheads},\,\text{hdim}\} = \{4,\,8448,\,16,\,128\}.
\]
In this setting the ablation shows that enabling FA-3's asynchronous design increases measured throughput from about $570$ TFLOPs/s to about $661$ TFLOPs/s. This confirms that both warp-specialization and the GEMM–softmax pipelining materially contribute to the overall speedup.

Intuitively, warp-specialization separates the roles of moving data (TMA) and doing matmuls (WGMMA) onto disjoint warps so that the hardware's asynchronous units can be kept busy without frequent context switching. The 2-stage pipelining overlaps softmax-related special-function work (exponentials, row reductions — performed on special function units) with Tensor Core matmuls from neighboring iterations. Since the special functions have far lower peak throughput than Tensor Cores, hiding them under matmuls yields significant end-to-end gains.

The paper also discusses practical constraints revealed by the ablation: the compiled instruction ordering can change how well the planned overlap manifests in the generated SASS, and increasing pipeline depth (e.g., to 3 stages) can increase overlap but at the cost of higher register pressure and potential spilling. Those tradeoffs limit the practical pipeline depth and tile sizes chosen in the implementation.

# Numerical error: FP16 FA-2/FA-3 $\approx 1.7\times$ lower RMSE vs standard; FP8 FA-3 with block quantization + incoherent processing $\approx 2.6\times$ lower RMSE than FP8 baseline
The paper evaluates numerical accuracy by comparing single-precision implementations to an FP64 (double-precision) reference implementation. To stress-test quantization robustness, inputs ($\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}$) were synthesized with occasional large outliers using the following distribution:
\[
\mathcal{N}(0,1) + \mathcal{N}(0,100)\cdot \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.001).
\]
That is, each entry is normally distributed with $\sigma=1$, and with probability $0.001$ an additional independent $\mathcal{N}(0,100)$ term is added to create a small fraction of large-magnitude outliers.

Root-mean-squared error (RMSE) relative to the FP64 reference is reported. The key numerical findings are:

- FP16: Both FlashAttention-2 and FlashAttention-3 achieve about $1.7\times$ lower RMSE than the standard attention implementation. The reduction in error is attributed to FA-2/FA-3 keeping intermediate softmax-related reductions and rescalings in higher precision (FP32), which avoids some of the rounding and dynamic-range issues that arise when softmax normalization and exponential are performed in lower precision.

- FP8: The baseline FP8 attention (per-tensor scaling) uses per-tensor FP8 scaling with FP32 accumulators and softmax results kept in FP16. FA-3 augments FP8 with two targeted techniques: (1) block quantization, where each tile/block of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ gets its own scale factor (instead of a single per-tensor scale), and (2) incoherent processing, where $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ are multiplied by a cheap random orthogonal transform (implemented as products of diagonal $\{\pm 1\}$ matrices and Hadamard matrices so that multiplication costs $O(d \log d)$). Because the orthogonal transform spreads out large outliers across coordinates, quantization error is reduced and, crucially, $(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ so the attention result is mathematically unchanged.

With these two techniques FA-3 in FP8 achieves about $2.6\times$ lower RMSE than the FP8 baseline that uses per-tensor scaling, demonstrating that careful block-wise scaling and incoherent preprocessing can make FP8 attention both fast and much more numerically robust in the presence of outliers.

# Interpreting gains: hiding SFU latency under Tensor Cores; sequence length and head-dimension effects; causal vs non-causal differences
Putting the empirical observations together gives a coherent picture of why FA-3 improves utilization and throughput:

- Asynchrony is the key. Hopper provides two asynchronously operable units central to attention: (1) Tensor Cores accessible via asynchronous WGMMA for matrix multiply work, and (2) TMA for asynchronous block transfers between GMEM and SMEM. FA-3 explicitly assigns producer/consumer roles to warps, issuing TMA loads independently from WGMMA matmuls, and uses software pipelining to overlap the lower-throughput softmax special-function work with matmuls. This reduces idle cycles on the Tensor Cores and thereby increases measured TFLOPs/s.

- The largest gains manifest for larger sequence lengths because the per-CTA compute work grows (the $N^2$ dependence) and the overheads per block or per CTA become relatively smaller. For short sequences the kernel launch overhead, register pressure, and load-balancing subtleties make it harder to fully amortize the pipelining benefits; this is one reason why FA-3's FP8 kernel (which in the reported implementation is not persistent) may lag cuDNN for small $N$ or causal masking.

- Head-dimension interacts with FP8 advantages. Exploiting FP8 Tensor Cores doubles peak matmul throughput compared to FP16 on Hopper; that gain is most visible in regimes where the rest of the pipeline (softmax, layout transforms, TMA) can be kept hidden. The paper observes that for head dimension 64, FA-3's FP8 kernels outperform cuDNN; for head dimensions 128 and 256 they are on par in non-causal runs and fall behind when causal masking is used. These sensitivities reflect a mix of factors: how well the in-kernel transposes and register permutations match the FP8 operand layout constraints, the relative share of special-function work (which does not accelerate in FP8), and the kernel's persistency and load-balancing strategy.

- Limitations highlighted by the experiments include the current lack of a persistent FP8 kernel design and additional load-balancing work needed to make FP8 FA-3 uniformly superior across all short-sequence and causal scenarios. The authors note these as directions for future engineering work.

# Summary
The experimental study in the paper demonstrates that architecting attention kernels around hardware asynchrony and careful low-precision handling yields substantial and practical benefits. FlashAttention-3 attains $1.5$–$2.0\times$ forward speedups over FlashAttention-2 in FP16, reaches up to 740 TFLOPs/s (roughly 75% H100 utilization) in FP16, and pushes FP8 forward throughput close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s while reducing FP8 numerical error significantly through block quantization and incoherent preprocessing. Ablations confirm that both warp-specialization and GEMM–softmax pipelining are necessary to realize these gains, and numerical experiments show the proposed FP8 mitigation techniques reduce RMSE in outlier-prone inputs by a factor of about $2.6\times$ compared to a naive FP8 baseline. The results convincingly illustrate that matching attention algorithms to the asynchronous, low-precision features of modern accelerators is a highly effective way to speed up long-context and high-throughput Transformer workloads.

Practical Implementation and Integration

# Kernel structure: persistent kernels and load balancing (FP16) vs current FP8 non-persistent status
Designing a production-quality FlashAttention-3 kernel begins with choosing whether the kernel will be persistent (a single long-running kernel that owns SM resources and iterates over many tiles) or non-persistent (many short-lived kernel launches). The paper reports that the FP16 FlashAttention-3 implementation uses a persistent kernel and an associated load-balancing strategy, while the FP8 implementation at the time of the paper does not have a persistent-kernel design. This distinction has practical consequences: persistent kernels amortize kernel launch overhead, allow finer-grained load balancing across SMs, and are particularly important for small-to-medium sequence lengths where launch overhead and latency otherwise dominate. The FP16 persistent kernel used in FlashAttention-3 is one reason it achieves consistently high utilization across a variety of sequence lengths; the lack of a persistent FP8 kernel helps explain why FP8 performance falls behind for small sequences or heavily masked workloads in some cases.

From an implementation perspective, a persistent kernel tends to organize each CTA (threadblock) as a long-running worker that pulls work items (query blocks) from a centralized work queue or follows a deterministic schedule, keeping the SM occupied across the whole invocation. Load balancing inside a persistent design typically assigns multiple query blocks to CTAs and lets them loop locally over the key blocks; work is partitioned to keep SMs busy without remapping resources on every block. By contrast, a non-persistent FP8 kernel launches many kernels, each servicing a small amount of work; this is simpler but incurs launch overhead and less opportunity to hide latency via long-lived asynchrony.

When implementing both FP16 and FP8 variants in the same codebase, separate kernel entrypoints are common: one persistent kernel for FP16 that uses the full suite of warp-specialization, ping-pong scheduling, and register reallocation, and one or more non-persistent kernels (or a future persistent FP8 kernel when implemented) for FP8. The persistent FP16 kernel should include the load-balancing logic and keep per-CTA state (counters, ring-buffer pointers, etc.) in registers/SMEM. For FP8, because of extra layout and accuracy work (in-kernel transpose, block quantization, incoherent transforms), the implementation may begin with a simpler non-persistent kernel while the persistent design is developed and tuned.

# Choosing $B_r$, $B_c$, and ring-buffer depth $s$: occupancy vs register pressure trade-offs
Choosing the query block size $B_r$, the key/value block size $B_c$, and the number of stages $s$ in the circular SMEM ring buffer is a central tuning knob for FlashAttention-3. These parameters control arithmetic granularity, on-chip buffering, and register/shared-memory pressure, and their choice involves balancing two competing resource dimensions: per-thread (and per-warpgroup) register usage and overall CTA occupancy.

Larger tile sizes ($B_r$, $B_c$) increase arithmetic intensity: each WGMMA produces more useful flops per load, improving Tensor Core utilization. However, larger tiles also increase register requirements, both for holding accumulators and (in the pipelined versions) for storing pipeline temporaries such as $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$. The two-stage GEMM–softmax pipeline explicitly requires holding an extra $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ in registers; the paper notes this costs on the order of $B_r \times B_c \times \text{sizeof(float)}$ per threadblock in register bytes. Increasing $B_r$ or $B_c$ therefore reduces the number of CTAs that can be resident on an SM because of the finite register file (each thread has at most 256 registers in the target hardware) and finite SM-wide resource limits, lowering occupancy and potentially reducing the ability to hide latency.

The ring-buffer depth $s$ trades off producer/consumer decoupling against SMEM usage. A larger $s$ lets the producer prefetch more $\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}$ tiles into shared memory and makes it less likely that the consumer must wait for a load, which is useful when TMA latency is significant or when producers and consumers execute at different speeds (e.g., due to warp-specialization). But each stage consumes shared memory proportional to the tile footprint, so increasing $s$ reduces the SMEM available to other data and can again reduce occupancy or limit the maximum tile sizes you can pick.

Because these trade-offs are workload- and hardware-dependent, the recommended approach is empirical tuning guided by the following principles that are explicit in the FlashAttention-3 design: (1) start with moderate tile sizes that are known to fit with the chosen pipeline depth and hardware register limits, (2) when enabling the 2-stage (or 3-stage) pipeline, reduce tile sizes to accommodate the extra register temporaries, and (3) choose $s$ only large enough to decouple producer/consumer bursts (often small values suffice) and avoid increasing $s$ until you measure stalls caused by TMA latency. The paper documents that the 2-stage pipeline gives measurable gains but requires careful register budgeting, and that a 3-stage pipeline, while theoretically advantageous, further increases register demand and thus tightens the choice of tile sizes.

# Synchronization primitives: bar.sync, WGMMA commit and wait, TMA barriers
Synchronization in FlashAttention-3 orchestrates three asynchronous resources: TMA (asynchronous GMEM→SMEM copies), WGMMA (asynchronous Tensor Core GEMMs), and ordinary CUDA scheduling across warps/warpgroups. The algorithm relies on a small set of synchronization primitives and careful use of "commit" versus "wait" semantics to implement overlap.

At the CTA/warpgroup level, the implementation uses a lightweight pipeline object that coordinates circular-buffer stages and exposes operations to (a) issue TMA loads and (b) notify or wait on the completion of those loads. Since TMA operations are asynchronous, the producer issues a load and "commits" it; the consumer later "waits" until that stage of the ring buffer has a completed load before using the data. The pseudocode uses explicit "commit" and "wait" pairs around GEMMs as well: a WGMMA invocation can be issued and allowed to proceed without stalling the issuing warpgroup (a non-blocking "commit"), and a later "wait" ensures the results are ready before they are consumed by subsequent code.

To realize the ping-pong scheduling across warp-specialized groups, the implementation injects barriers such as PTX-level bar.sync to force a particular ordering among warpgroups: for example, ensuring GEMMs of warpgroup 1 are scheduled before those of warpgroup 2 so that while warpgroup 2 executes its GEMMs the softmax of warpgroup 1 will be scheduled and executed, thereby using otherwise idle MFFU cycles. In the consumer loop, the pattern is typically: issue WGMMA for $\mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$ and commit (no wait), issue (or allow) the second WGMMA for the previous iteration's $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}} \mathbf{V}$ and commit, then explicitly wait for the first WGMMA before computing the softmax on the produced $\mathbf{S}$ data. The exact commit/wait instruction names are device-specific (the paper uses the higher-level terms commit and wait in the pseudocode), but the intended effect is to exploit the asynchrony of the Tensor Cores while preserving correctness by waiting just before a result is needed.

Finally, because many of the synchronization actions cross the producer/consumer boundary, all waits should be paired and bounded: a producer should not overwrite the $j \bmod s$ stage until consumers release it, and consumers should release a stage only after the RS-GEMM that uses the corresponding $\mathbf{V}$ tile has completed. The pipeline object and simple state variables per-stage (e.g., "loaded", "consumed", "released") are the natural way to encode these semantics.

# Dynamic register management with setmaxnreg for producers vs consumers
One of the enabling implementation techniques of FlashAttention-3 on Hopper is dynamic reallocation of registers across warpgroups using the PTX-level setmaxnreg mechanism. The intuition is that producer warps (which mainly issue asynchronous TMA loads and perform light control tasks) require far fewer registers than consumer warps (which perform WGMMA Tensor Core work and keep accumulators, temporaries, and pipeline state). By dynamically shrinking the register allotment for producer warps and enlarging it for consumer warps, the kernel reduces register waste and provides the heavy compute warps with more RMEM to avoid spills and maximize WGMMA efficiency.

In practice the kernel performs two register configuration steps at CTAs or warpgroup boundaries: (1) in the producer warpgroup, the kernel deallocates a predetermined number of registers so that only a minimal register set remains (the paper explicitly notes that only a single thread is actually needed to issue TMA loads in many cases), and (2) in the consumer warpgroup, the kernel calls setmaxnreg to allocate a larger share of RMEM proportionate to the number of consumer warps so that the warps executing WGMMA can keep larger accumulators and temporaries in registers. Because register allocation affects occupancy, the choice of how many registers to grant to consumer warps must be tuned: granting too many registers per warp reduces the number of warps that can be resident on an SM, lowering SM-level parallelism and possibly hurting latency hiding, while granting too few may cause spills that degrade performance and increase memory traffic.

The practical guidance is to use setmaxnreg to provide a modest but meaningful increase to consumer warps compared to producers, eliminate as many unnecessary registers from producers as possible, and then profile to find the sweet spot where spills disappear without excessively reducing occupancy. The paper emphasizes the value of this dynamic reallocation in enabling warp-specialized kernels and improving overall throughput.

# Implementing in-kernel V transpose with LDSM/STSM and scheduling it under GEMMs
FP8 WGMMA on Hopper requires that operand tiles be k-major when read from shared memory, but the natural packing of tensors in higher-level frameworks is typically contiguous in the head dimension. Because TMA cannot change the contiguous stride when loading tiles, FlashAttention-3 implements an in-kernel tile transpose of $\mathbf{V}$ after it has been loaded into SMEM. Doing this in-kernel avoids a costly pre-processing transpose in global memory (which would be memory-bandwidth-bound and wasteful in inference scenarios) and lets the transpose be scheduled so that its cost is hidden under the arithmetic of the Tensor Cores.

The key building blocks used for this in-kernel transpose are LDSM (ldmatrix) and STSM (stmatrix) instructions. These are warp-collective register↔SMEM copy instructions that operate at a fine-grained tile granularity (128 bytes in the paper's description). Although LDSM/STSM are formally described for $8\times 8$ matrices with 16-bit elements, FlashAttention-3 packs FP8 (8-bit) entries two at a time into 16-bit words so they can be moved by LDSM/STSM. This packing requires additional register permutations between the LDSM and STSM calls because the transpose-aware versions of these instructions cannot split the packed bytes; the implementation therefore issues small register-level shuffles to rearrange the packed pairs before the STSM write-back so that the final tile in SMEM is in the desired k-major layout.

To hide the cost of the in-kernel transpose, FlashAttention-3 schedules it in the producer warpgroup and arranges for the transpose of the next $\mathbf{V}$ tile to execute "in the shadow" of the two asynchronous WGMMA calls that involve the previous $\mathbf{V}$ and current $\mathbf{K}$ tile. In other words, after issuing the WGMMA operations for the current iteration, the producer begins the LDSM/STSM-based transpose for the next iteration; since WGMMA and LDSM/STSM use different hardware resources and are both asynchronous to the main thread execution, this overlapping reduces end-to-end cost. The paper also points out that doing the transpose in-kernel gives additional freedom to match the column/row permutations that arise from the accumulator register layouts of FP8 WGMMA: a small register-level permutation of the accumulator order can be made consistent with a matching permutation in the in-kernel $\mathbf{V}$ transpose so that dependent FP8 WGMMAs compose correctly without expensive cross-thread shuffles.

# Causal masking and boundary handling in block loops
Processing causal masks in a blocked, tiled algorithm requires carefully constraining which key tiles are allowed to contribute to a given query tile and handling partial tiles at sequence boundaries. In FlashAttention-3 the fundamental loop structure iterates over key/value blocks indexed by $j$ for each query block $i$, but with causal masking the inner loop upper bound must be truncated so that keys corresponding to indices greater than the query positions are not used. Concretely, when computing the $i$-th query block (covering queries in a certain index range), the consumer loop should restrict $j$ so that only key blocks with indices that correspond to keys at or before those queries are processed; this means the inner loop becomes variable-length across $i$ and some ring-buffer stages may not be used for some iterations.

Because the local softmax reduction for a query block is computed incrementally over the key blocks actually processed, the same rescaling and accumulation logic applies but with the set of contributing blocks restricted. The pseudocode in the paper already shows how the softmax reduction accumulates $m_i$ and $\ell_i$ across processed blocks; with causal masking the set of processed blocks simply excludes those that are masked. From an implementation standpoint, this requires the producer/consumer synchronization logic to be aware of variable per-query work and a work-stealing or bookkeeping mechanism to avoid releasing or overwriting buffer stages that will still be used by late queries.

At boundaries where the sequence length is not a multiple of $B_r$ or $B_c$, the implementation must support partial tiles. The GEMM invocations, WGMMA layout assumptions, and the softmax exponentiation/rowsum must all be guarded by tile-size parameters that handle the last partial row/column. This typically means masking unused lanes in the WGMMA or using specialized tail-GEMM codepaths to handle smaller-than-full tiles. Because these edge cases are occasional, they should be implemented carefully and tested, but they do not change the core pipeline or synchronization model.

Finally, remember the FLOP accounting used in benchmarking: the paper counts forward FLOPs as
\[
4 \cdot \text{seqlen}^2 \cdot \text{head\_dim} \cdot \text{num\_heads},
\]
and divides this by $2$ when applying causal masking to reflect the triangular half of the attention matrix that is actually computed. This is useful when interpreting profiler numbers and utilization in masked workloads.

# Numerical stability: keep softmax and rescaling in FP32; combining with block quantization scales
Numerical stability is a central concern when moving to low precision. FlashAttention-3's FP16 implementation preserves numerical stability by keeping intermediate softmax reductions and rescalings in higher precision (FP32) even when the GEMMs operate in FP16; this is why the paper reports lower RMSE for FA-3 versus standard attention implementations that materialize more intermediate results in lower precision. In the forward pass, the max per-row $m_i$, the intermediate exponentials, the running normalizer $\ell_i$, and the accumulator $\mathbf{O}_i$ are all kept in FP32 precision so that the exponential and rescale steps do not suffer undue rounding error.

When moving to FP8, FlashAttention-3 uses two complementary techniques to reduce quantization and rounding error without sacrificing most of the throughput advantage: block quantization and incoherent processing. Block quantization keeps one FP8 scaling factor per block (the natural blocks used by the algorithm, e.g., $B_r \times d$ for queries and $B_c \times d$ for keys/values) rather than a single per-tensor scale. Because attention operates naturally on blocks, this quantization can be fused with preceding operations (for example, rotary embeddings) so that quantization does not add a separate memory-bound pass. At compute time, the block scaling factors are accounted for by scaling the corresponding contributions to $\mathbf{S}$; logically the algorithm multiplies quantized values back by their inverse block scale (or equivalently scales $\mathbf{S}$) before applying the softmax in higher precision.

Incoherent processing multiplies $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ prior to quantization; because $\mathbf{M}$ is orthogonal we have $(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, so exactness is preserved apart from quantization. The practical choice for $\mathbf{M}$ in the paper is a product of random diagonal $\pm 1$ matrices and a Hadamard matrix, which can be applied in $O(d\log d)$ time and fused with rotary embedding without extra memory traffic. This spreads out outlier entries so that each quantized entry is an incoherent sum over many original entries, reducing the magnitude disparity and lowering quantization error. The paper demonstrates that combining block quantization with incoherent processing reduces FP8 numerical error substantially (about $2.6\times$ improvement over a per-tensor baseline in their experiments).

Putting all of this together in a kernel requires carefully mixing precisions: run the WGMMA operands in low precision where supported, keep accumulators and softmax computations in higher precision (FP32), and incorporate block-scale factors into the per-block scaling of $\mathbf{S}$ so that the softmax receives properly scaled inputs.

# Integration with PyTorch and Hugging Face; MQA/GQA tensor indexing
FlashAttention-3 is intended to be consumable by the broader ML ecosystem; the paper explicitly states plans to integrate with PyTorch and Hugging Face and to support attention variants such as multi-query attention (MQA) and grouped query attention (GQA). Integration has two main components: an efficient and compatible kernel ABI, and a thin framework wrapper that maps high-level tensor layouts and metadata to the kernel's expected inputs.

On the ABI side, the kernel should accept pointers to $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, and $\mathbf{V}$ (either in FP16 or quantized FP8 blocks), per-block scaling factors (for FP8 block quantization), and optional metadata such as causal-mask flags, head counts, and block sizes. It is beneficial to expose both a persistent kernel entrypoint (for FP16) and a non-persistent one (or a persistent FP8 variant when implemented) so that callers can pick the best-performing path for their workload.

On the wrapper side, the paper follows FlashAttention-2's approach for MQA/GQA: adjust tensor indexing to avoid duplicating $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ in HBM. That is, instead of replicating keys/values per query group, the wrapper should present reshaped and strided views so that the kernel sees the appropriate per-head (or per-group) slices without duplicating memory. Because FlashAttention-3 uses block-based processing, the wrapper must be able to present blocked tensors in contiguous memory on the head dimension (or request an in-kernel transpose for FP8), and it should also be able to fuse pre-processing operations (rotary embeddings, Hadamard-incoherent transforms) into the quantization pass so extra copies are avoided. Practical integration also includes exposing an autograd-compatible backward kernel (the paper describes that the backward pass is implemented and speeds up too) and using the same dtype semantics as the surrounding framework (FP16 default, FP8 optional with block scales and incoherent transforms).

Finally, integration should supply options and fallbacks for cases where the kernel cannot be used (e.g., unsupported head dimension, unsupported device, or when persistent-kernel assumptions fail). The wrapper can select which kernel variant to call (FP16 persistent, FP8 non-persistent, or vendor cuDNN path) based on size, masking, and desired precision.

# Profiling with Nsight tools, reading SASS to verify overlap, diagnosing bank conflicts and spills
Profiling is an indispensable tool for validating that the designed asynchrony and warp-specialization actually produce the intended overlap on real hardware. The paper reports using SASS inspection to confirm that the compiler (NVCC) generates overlapped code for the 2-stage pipeline; this same technique, together with vendor profiling tools (e.g., Nsight family tools), should be used during development.

Start by measuring top-level kernel metrics: achieved TFLOPs, SM utilization, shared memory bandwidth, and global memory bandwidth. These macro metrics indicate whether GEMM compute is saturated or whether memory/TMA is a bottleneck. Because FlashAttention-3 aims to get high Tensor Core utilization, a good sign is TFLOPs approaching the published hardware fraction (the paper reports FP16 reaching up to 740 TFLOPs/s, about 75% utilization on H100).

For deeper debugging, inspect the generated SASS (the GPU assembly) to verify the order and issuance of key primitives. In particular, check for the presence of WGMMA instructions and their commit/no-wait patterns, paired waits, and the presence of LDSM/STSM sequences for the in-kernel transpose. The existence and ordering of these instructions tell you whether the compiler has preserved the intended commit/no-wait semantics or whether it reordered work in a way that defeats overlap.

The profiler can also report register spills, and these are easy to correlate with SASS: excessive register usage or spills indicate the need to reduce tile sizes, reduce pipeline depth, or tune setmaxnreg allocations. Shared memory bank conflicts will show up as reduced SMEM bandwidth and stalls; these are especially relevant when multiple warps perform LDSM/STSM or when SMEM access patterns are not bank-friendly. Use the profiler to identify which code regions generate stalls and then correlate them with the corresponding high-level steps (TMA issuance, WGMMA commit, softmax non-GEMM work, transpose). The paper notes that the exponential/special function throughput is very low relative to matmul throughput, so the profiler will often point to those units as a bottleneck unless the schedule hides them under GEMMs.

Finally, use these measurements iteratively: change $B_r$, $B_c$, and $s$, re-run the profiler, and watch how TFLOPs and stalls shift. The combination of SASS inspection and metric-driven profiling is the recommended path to ensure the run-time behavior matches the design.

# Performance pitfalls: small sequences, extreme head dims, masking-heavy scenarios
Several practical scenarios commonly produce disappointing performance if left unaddressed. First, small sequence lengths: with short sequences the overheads of kernel launch, TMA setup, in-kernel transpose, and pipeline priming can outweigh the benefits of high Tensor Core throughput. The paper documents that FP8 non-persistent kernels in particular suffer in the small-sequence regime; the FP16 persistent kernel mitigates this. For latency-sensitive inference with short contexts, prefer the persistent FP16 kernel or a vendor-provided small-sequence kernel designed for latency.

Second, extreme head dimensions interact poorly with register pressure. Very large head dimensions (or very large tile sizes chosen to exploit those head dims) can blow past per-thread register limits and either force register spills or reduce occupancy so much that the pipeline cannot hide latencies. The consequence is a sharp drop in measured GFLOPs/Tensor Core utilization. The advice is to back off tile sizes, reduce pipeline depth, or adjust setmaxnreg allocations to keep registers and occupancy within sane ranges.

Third, masking-heavy scenarios (for example, causal masking applied across many tiles or complex attention masks) reduce parallelizability: many entries of the attention matrix are skipped, lowering the arithmetic intensity and impairing the ability to overlap softmax with GEMMs. In causal masking the effective work is about half of the full matrix on average (hence the FLOP division by 2 in the paper), and the algorithm must dynamically adjust loop bounds, which may reduce steady-state throughput. In such cases, tuning for smaller tiles or using the vendor library optimized for masked patterns (if available) can be beneficial.

Finally, for FP8 there are two additional pitfalls: (1) pre-transposing $\mathbf{V}$ in global memory as a separate kernel (option (1b) in the paper) is typically too expensive in a memory-bound scenario and should be avoided in favor of the in-kernel LDSM/STSM transpose, and (2) failing to apply block quantization and incoherent transforms in the presence of outliers will result in large numerical error. To avoid these FP8 pitfalls, fuse quantization with a prior memory-bound op, perform the in-kernel transpose under GEMM, and only enable FP8 when the problem size and masking pattern allow the kernel to amortize its setup costs.

Concluding remarks
This chapter summarized the practical implementation and integration concerns raised by FlashAttention-3. The high-level advice from the paper is consistent: exploit warp-specialization and hardware asynchrony, manage registers dynamically, hide non-GEMM overheads under asynchronous WGMMA and TMA, and use block-aware FP8 quantization plus incoherent transforms to regain numerical fidelity. The final step is always measurement: profile on the target hardware, inspect SASS to confirm the intended overlaps, and tune the tiling/register/scheduling parameters for the workload’s sequence length, head dimension, and masking pattern.

Limitations, Extensions, and Conclusion

# Current limitations: FP8 kernel lacks persistent design; small-seqlen and heavy masking cases vs vendor kernels

FlashAttention-3 makes significant progress on GPU attention by exploiting asynchrony and low-precision, but it is not without concrete limitations exposed in the paper. The most prominent practical shortcoming reported is that the FP8 path of FlashAttention-3 does not yet incorporate a persistent-kernel and load‑balancing strategy, while the FP16 path does. In our benchmarks this design gap partly explains why FP8 FlashAttention-3 performs worse than vendor-optimized kernels (e.g., cuDNN) for small sequence lengths and in heavily masked (causal) regimes. Those workloads are sensitive to kernel-launch overheads, fine-grained load balance, and amortization of work per SM; the persistent kernel and load-balancing choices used in the FP16 implementation help mitigate those effects, and their absence in the FP8 implementation reduces its practical advantage for latency‑sensitive scenarios.

Beyond the missing persistent design, FlashAttention-3 inherits and introduces several implementation-level trade-offs that constrain when and how it achieves its peak benefits. The FP8-enabled path requires additional in-kernel layout transformations: FP8 WGMMA on Hopper accepts only k-major SMEM operands and the FP32 accumulator layout assumed by successive WGMMAs does not match the register layout produced by a previous WGMMA. Correctness and performance therefore depend on in-kernel transposes (using LDSM/STSM) and register-byte permutations to reconcile layouts. These transformations add code complexity, increase register use, and can increase instruction overheads that are relatively more expensive for small tiles or short sequences.

Another limitation is register pressure. Deeper pipelining (for example moving from the 2‑stage to 3‑stage overlapping pipeline) and larger block sizes both raise the register footprint per CTA. Higher register pressure can force spilling or reduce occupancy, which in turn undermines the throughput gains from better Tensor Core utilization. Compiler reordering is also a practical concern: the hand-designed overlap between WGMMA (asynchronous GEMM) and the softmax operations depends on a specific instruction schedule, and the compiler (NVCC) may rearrange instructions in ways that complicate the intended asynchrony. The paper documents that, in practice, the SASS generated still implements the intended overlap, but this fragility is a present engineering concern that limits portability and complicates tuning.

Finally, numerical accuracy is a practical limitation when pushing to FP8. Although FlashAttention-3 introduces two techniques (block quantization and incoherent processing) that substantially reduce FP8 quantization error—achieving a reported 2.6$\times$ reduction in RMSE compared to a baseline FP8 attention with per-tensor scaling—FP8 remains more error-prone than FP16/BF16 unless handled carefully. That means there are cases (especially in training or in models with extreme outliers) where using FP8 without the mitigations described will be undesirable.

# Future work: inference-optimized paths, persistent FP8, deeper pipelines, auto-tuning of tile sizes and registers

The paper identifies and hints at several natural directions to address the limitations above. First, building inference-optimized code paths is an explicit next step. Inference workloads commonly have small batch sizes, low latency targets, and may use causal masking; a successful inference-oriented design often favors persistent kernels and more aggressive load balancing so that SMs are occupied continuously with minimal launch/dispatch overhead. Integrating a persistent kernel design into the FP8 path is therefore a high‑priority extension: it would reduce the observed disadvantage of the current FP8 implementation for small sequence lengths and causal masking by better amortizing fixed costs and by tailoring scheduling across CTAs.

Second, deeper and more flexible pipelining is a concrete extension. FlashAttention-3 already describes a 2‑stage overlapped GEMM–softmax pipeline and discusses a 3‑stage variant. Extending to 3 stages (or more) can further hide non‑GEMM costs such as exponential computation by overlapping them with additional GEMM work. The trade-off is clear: more stages increase register usage and complexity in synchronization and state management. A promising research avenue is to explore the design space systematically (including the 3‑stage variant the authors sketch) to quantify where extra stages yield net gain and where register pressure becomes the limiting factor.

Third, auto‑tuning of tile sizes, pipeline depth, and register allocation is a practical and valuable future direction. The paper repeatedly highlights trade-offs between block sizes ($B_r$, $B_c$), register pressure, and pipeline depth; these choices interact with hardware parameters (SM count, register file size) and workload parameters (sequence length $N$, head dimension $d$). An automated tuner that measures or models these trade-offs and emits kernel configurations tailored to a target GPU and workload would make FlashAttention-3's ideas more robust across contexts. Such an autotuner would choose block sizes and the number of pipeline stages to maximize Tensor Core utilization while staying within register and shared-memory budgets.

Finally, building a persistent FP8 kernel entails specific engineering work: reconciling the FP8 k-major operand requirement, placing in-kernel transposes and register permutations in the producer warpgroup, and adapting the load‑balancing logic to the FP8 layout transforms. This is a clear, implementable extension that the authors indicate as near-term work.

# Portability to other accelerators and future low-precision formats (e.g., FP4 on Blackwell)

Although the empirical results in the paper are reported on NVIDIA Hopper (H100), the authors make explicit that the algorithmic ideas are not inherently tied to Hopper. The core techniques—warp-specialized producer/consumer scheduling, asynchronous overlap of data movement and compute, and block‑wise low‑precision quantization with incoherent processing—rely on two hardware capabilities: (1) asynchronous, specialized units for matrix multiply and data movement that can be overlapped, and (2) hardware support for low-precision arithmetic (FP8 or even lower). Where another accelerator exposes similar asynchronous execution primitives and low-precision tensor units, the same high-level techniques should be applicable.

That said, portability is nontrivial in practice because instruction-level and layout constraints differ across architectures. On Hopper the FP8 WGMMA requires k-major SMEM operands and imposes a particular register-to-accumulator layout that demanded in-kernel reorders; other accelerators will have their own constraints. Porting FlashAttention-3 therefore requires mapping the algorithm onto the target ISA and memory/layout conventions: implementing equivalent SMEM transposes, register permutations, and synchronization primitives. In short, the algorithmic principles are portable, but the implementation details are hardware specific.

Looking forward to future low-precision formats such as FP4 (e.g., on Blackwell), the same accuracy considerations that motivated block quantization and incoherent processing for FP8 will become even more important. Finer formats reduce storage and increase theoretical throughput, but they also increase quantization error and sensitivity to outliers. Thus, methods that (a) quantize at a finer granularity (blocks, not whole tensors), (b) spread out values before quantization (incoherent processing via orthogonal-like transforms), and (c) carefully choose which reductions and intermediates remain in higher precision will be essential for practical, accurate low‑precision attention.

# Interplay with training at scale and quantization-aware methods

Using low-precision attention within large-scale training raises both opportunity and risk. The opportunity is clear: FP8 (and lower) can deliver much higher peak throughput—FlashAttention-3 reports FP8 reaching nearly 1.2 PFLOPs/s on H100 in the forward pass—potentially reducing wall-clock time and energy. The risk is numerical instability and increased gradient noise if quantization error is not controlled.

FlashAttention-3 already incorporates quantization-aware strategies: block quantization (one scale per block) and incoherent processing (multiplying $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by an orthogonal-like transform implemented as products of random sign diagonals and a Hadamard matrix). These techniques reduce the per‑block dynamic range and spread out outliers, respectively; they are carefully chosen so they can be fused with preprocessing steps (e.g., rotary embeddings) at no extra memory bandwidth cost. In practice, the forward FP8 implementation still uses FP32 accumulators (as in the baseline FP8 setups described) and keeps certain intermediate reductions at higher precision to limit error growth. For training, the backward pass and optimizer state handling add further precision concerns: gradients, Adam statistics, and accumulation must be managed with appropriate precision and potentially with selective recomputation.

The paper points out that understanding how low-precision attention affects large-scale training—stability, convergence rate, and final model quality—is a direction for future work. Any practical training system using FP8 attention will need to adopt mixed-precision strategies, carefully choose which tensors remain in higher precision, possibly employ loss-scaling, and validate that block quantization / incoherent transforms remain compatible with gradient flow and do not introduce bias.

# Relation to approximate attention and alternative sequence models

Attention's quadratic cost in sequence length has motivated both approximation algorithms (linearized attention, kernel approximations, sparsity-based methods) and alternative sequence models that avoid full self-attention. In the introduction the paper situates FlashAttention-3 amidst that landscape: instead of approximating the mathematical form of attention, FlashAttention-3 is an example of improving the efficiency of exact attention by aligning algorithm design with modern hardware. This places it in a complementary relationship to approximation and architecture-alternative approaches.

For many applications, faster exact attention removes part of the motivation for approximation—if exact attention can be executed at comparable cost for the target sequence lengths, one can avoid approximation-induced errors or algorithmic complexity. On the other hand, for very long contexts where quadratic memory is prohibitive, approximation methods or architectures specifically designed for ultra‑long sequences (or alternative memory mechanisms) will remain relevant. Similarly, alternative sequence models that replace attention entirely (e.g., retentive or RWKV-like models) are orthogonal; FlashAttention-3 speeds up attention layers and therefore benefits any model that continues to rely on attention, but it does not claim to make alternative models obsolete.

# Takeaways: algorithm–hardware co-design, asynchrony-first thinking, and principled low-precision for accuracy and speed

FlashAttention-3 illustrates a central lesson: tight algorithm–hardware co‑design produces large practical gains. By rethinking the attention kernel with awareness of asynchronous hardware units (TMA for memory movement, WGMMA for Tensor Core matmuls) and by partitioning work into producer and consumer warpgroups, the authors recover much more of the available Tensor Core throughput than earlier implementations. The “asynchrony‑first” mindset—designing the software to expose independent units of work that specialized hardware can run concurrently—lets expensive operations such as exponentials and rescaling be hidden under high-throughput GEMMs via careful pipelining and synchronization. The empirical payoff is substantial: FP16 FlashAttention-3 achieves 1.5–2.0$\times$ speedup over FlashAttention-2 in the forward pass (reaching up to 740 TFLOPs/s, about 75\% utilization on H100), and FP8 reaches throughput close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s.

Equally important is the recognition that low precision must be principled, not blind. FP8 brings potential throughput wins, but naive per-tensor quantization yields large errors in the presence of outliers. FlashAttention-3's block quantization reduces dynamic-range mismatch by quantizing on tiles aligned with the algorithm's blocking, and incoherent processing spreads out outliers via orthogonal-like transforms implemented efficiently (Hadamard times random signs). Together these techniques delivered a measured 2.6$\times$ improvement in numerical error over a baseline FP8 attention with per-tensor scaling.

Lastly, FlashAttention-3 highlights the practical engineering trade-offs anyone building high-performance kernels must manage: layout constraints of low-precision instructions, the tension between pipeline depth and register pressure, compiler optimization and scheduling fidelity, and the need for workload-specific tuning. Addressing these trade-offs—through persistent kernels in FP8, autotuning, and careful pipelining—forms the blueprint for future improvements.

In sum, the paper demonstrates that revisiting core kernels with an asynchrony- and precision-aware lens can unlock substantial gains in both speed and accuracy. These gains make longer-context, multi-modal, and more computationally demanding attention applications more practical today, while pointing to a clear set of engineering and research directions to make these gains robust across workloads, hardware, and training regimes.
