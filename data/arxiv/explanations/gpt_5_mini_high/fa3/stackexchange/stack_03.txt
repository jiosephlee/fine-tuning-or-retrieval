\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### How does pingpong scheduling actually overlap softmax with GEMMs across warpgroups?
Question:
The paper says bar.sync is used to force GEMMs in warpgroup 1 before GEMMs in warpgroup 2, so that softmax in one warpgroup runs while Tensor Cores are busy in the other. How is this ordering sufficient to induce concurrent MFU (for exp) and Tensor Core usage on Hopper’s scheduler? Is there a minimal number of consumer warps per warpgroup needed for this to be effective, and what ensures the softmax instructions are not delayed until after the GEMMs complete anyway?
Answer:
Short answer: it works because Hopper exposes two largely independent execution pipelines (Tensor Cores via the warpgroup-wide WGMMA and the MFU that implements exponentials/special functions) and the kernel explicitly issues WGMMA asynchronously (``commit but do not wait'') and only waits when a result is actually needed. The cross‑warpgroup barrier (bar.sync) is used not to make the hardware magically run things in parallel, but to shape the *issue order* of WGMMA vs. softmax work across warpgroups so the hardware scheduler actually has ready MFU work to schedule while the Tensor Cores are busy. In practice you need the warpgroup unit (and at least two warpgroups co‑resident on the SM for the ping‑pong effect to be most effective), and you must avoid creating data dependencies or implicit waits that would force the softmax to stall until GEMMs complete.

Why ordering + asynchrony is sufficient
The paper’s key hardware facts are the ones you should keep in mind: (1) on Hopper the WGMMA instruction is asynchronous and is issued at the warpgroup granularity, and (2) exponentials/special functions execute on a separate multi‑function unit (MFU). Because WGMMA is asynchronous the kernel can issue a GEMM and then continue executing instructions in that warpgroup without stalling for the Tensor Core to finish, provided those instructions do not depend on the GEMM result. That “continue” is what the paper takes advantage of.

bar.sync is used to control *when* each warpgroup issues its WGMMA instructions relative to the other warpgroup(s). By forcing an order of GEMM issue (WG1 issues its GEMMs first, then WG2), the kernel makes sure that at any given time there is a warpgroup that has recently issued a WGMMA (so the Tensor Cores are busy) and another warpgroup that has ready MFU work (softmax/exponentials) to execute. The hardware warp scheduler interleaves warps that are ready; since MFU and Tensor Core are separate pipelines, MFU instructions from one warpgroup can be executed while the Tensor Cores are processing a previously issued WGMMA from another warpgroup. In short, bar.sync shapes issue order; WGMMA’s asynchronous semantics + the separate MFU pipeline let the scheduler physically overlap MFU execution with Tensor Core execution.

What actually prevents the softmax from getting serialized
Two practical kernel-level ingredients prevent the softmax from being deferred until after GEMMs complete: (a) explicit non‑blocking WGMMA usage (``commit but do not wait'') so the code does not implicitly wait for Tensor Core completion, and (b) a two‑stage pipelining of the inner loop so the softmax for one block does not depend on the RS‑GEMM that was just issued. Concretely, the implementation issues the next SS‑WGMMA and also issues the RS‑WGMMA for the previous $P\cdot V$ while only waiting for the specific WGMMA result that the softmax needs. The code waits only when it must (PTX/SASS wait instructions are inserted only where a dependency exists). Because the softmax’s expensive exponentials do not depend on the still‑in‑flight RS‑GEMM, they can be scheduled immediately and execute on the MFU while the RS‑GEMMA/Tensor Core work completes. The authors checked the generated SASS and confirmed the compiler did produce the expected overlapped instruction stream (they also note compiler reordering is a practical risk).

Practical minimums / occupancy considerations
Architecturally a WGMMA is a warpgroup‑wide primitive (the paper defines a warpgroup as 4 contiguous warps), so you need a full warpgroup to issue a WGMMA. For the ping‑pong effect you want at least two warpgroups co‑resident/active on the SM so one group can be in the phase that issues/keeps the Tensor Cores busy while the other group executes softmax on the MFU. In other words, the mechanism requires at least the warpgroup unit and benefits from having two warpgroups (ping/pong) so work can alternate across them. You can get some overlap even within a single warpgroup via the 2‑stage intra‑warpgroup pipelining described in the paper, but that is generally less effective than true ping‑pong across multiple warpgroups because of register pressure and limited ability to keep pipelines fed.

Caveats and why the effect is empirical, not absolute
The paper explicitly warns the ping‑pong picture is idealized — real behavior depends on occupancy, register pressure, compiler scheduling, and the exact placement of waits/barriers. If registers spill or if the compiler introduces waits/reorders code, softmax could be delayed and overlap reduced. That is why the implementation uses setmaxnreg, careful register management, explicit commit/no‑wait patterns, and validation via SASS to make the overlap reliable. Empirically those measures raised TFLOPs from the baseline (e.g., the paper reports improvements such as $570 \rightarrow 620-640$ TFLOPs in their example).

Summary
The bar.sync ordering is effective because it controls *issue order* across warpgroups while WGMMA is asynchronous and the MFU is a separate pipeline. With non‑blocking WGMMA, a pipeline that avoids needless data dependencies, and at least two warpgroups (and good occupancy/register management), the scheduler has ready MFU work to run while Tensor Cores are busy, producing the observed MFU+TC overlap.
