\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### In LongRoPE's unified RoPE formula, what exactly is being scaled: positions or frequencies?
Question:
In the paper "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens", Eq. (1) writes RoPE as \(\cos(n\theta_i), \sin(n\theta_i)\) with \(\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}\), and then the unified interpolation (Eq. (2)) looks like \(\cos\big(n/(\lambda (\beta)^i)\big)\) with \(\beta = \theta^{2/d}\). Later, the indicator-based Eq. (3) multiplies the angle by \(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\) to get either \(n/\beta^i\) or \(n/(\lambda_i\beta^i)\). I’m confused about the notational consistency: are we scaling the frequencies \(\theta_i\) or the positions \(n\)? Are Eqs. (1)–(3) mathematically equivalent under \(\beta = \theta^{2/d}\), and is \(\lambda\) a per-dimension value (\(\lambda_i\)) or a global one?
Answer:
Short answer: LongRoPE is always scaling the RoPE *angle* (the argument of the sine/cosine). Because that angle is the product $n\theta_i$, you can equivalently view the change as scaling the position $n$ or scaling the frequency $\theta_i$. Equations (1)–(3) are consistent with each other under the paper's notation ($\beta=\theta^{2/d}$); the difference is whether the paper writes the multiplicative factor on $n$ or on $\theta_i$, and whether that factor is a single global scalar or a per-dimension value.

A bit of algebra from the paper makes this explicit. The paper defines
$\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}=\beta^{-i}$, so the original rotary angle is $n\theta_i=\dfrac{n}{\beta^i}$. The unified interpolation in Eq. (2)
is written as
$\cos\!\big(\dfrac{n}{\lambda\beta^i}\big)$. But
$$
\cos\!\big(\tfrac{n}{\lambda\beta^i}\big)
= \cos\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\cdot\tfrac{n}{\beta^i}\Big)
= \cos\!\big(n\cdot(\theta_i/\lambda)\big)
= \cos\!\big((n/\lambda)\cdot\theta_i\big).
$$
So dividing the angle by $\lambda$ is algebraically identical to replacing $n$ with $n/\lambda$ (scaling positions) or replacing $\theta_i$ with $\theta_i/\lambda$ (scaling frequencies). That is why the paper sometimes talks about “downscaling rotation frequencies” and sometimes about “position interpolation” — both viewpoints describe the same multiplicative change to the angle.

Eq. (3) is the position-aware, per-dimension generalization LongRoPE uses. It applies an indicator
$\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})$ which equals $1$ for early tokens ($n<\hat{n}$) and $1/\lambda_i$ for later tokens ($n\ge\hat{n}$). Thus for $n\ge\hat{n}$ the angle becomes
$$
\mathbb{I}\cdot\frac{n}{\beta^i}=\frac{1}{\lambda_i}\cdot\frac{n}{\beta^i}=\frac{n}{\lambda_i\beta^i},
$$
exactly the form used in Eq. (2) but with a per-dimension $\lambda_i$ (and with an exemption for the first $\hat{n}$ tokens). Note the paper chooses to *search* the parameter $\lambda_i$ (they allow $\lambda_i\ge 1$ in the search space) and they sometimes denote the actually-applied multiplicative factor as $\hat{\lambda}_i=1/\lambda_i$ — that is the source of the apparent notation flip.

So: (1) mathematically the paper is altering the angle $n\theta_i$; you may think of that as scaling positions or frequencies interchangeably, (2) Eqs. (1)–(3) are consistent under $\beta=\theta^{2/d}$ when you interpret $\lambda$ as the divisor of the angle, and (3) $\lambda$ in the unified Eq. (2) can be a global placeholder (PI uses $\lambda=s$ globally), while LongRoPE generalizes to per-dimension $\lambda_i$ (with the early-token exception $\hat{n}$) and searches those $\lambda_i$ (subject to the monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i\le\lambda_{i+1}$).

### Why does the paper call \(\lambda=1.0\) "direct extrapolation" in the search space?
Question:
In the search-space description, \(\lambda_i\) ranges from 1.0 to \(1.25\,s\), and 1.0 is described as "direct extrapolation". But with your unified form \(\cos\big(n/(\lambda_i\beta^i)\big)\), \(\lambda_i=1\) looks like "no scaling" (neither interpolation nor extrapolation). Shouldn’t extrapolation correspond to \(\lambda_i<1\) (i.e., increasing effective angle per step)? Is this a terminology issue, or is there a different definition of "extrapolation" being used?
Answer:
Short answer: it’s a terminology/semantics choice in the paper. The authors call $\lambda=1.0$ “direct extrapolation” because $\lambda=1$ means “use the original (pre‑trained) RoPE formula without any rescaling” and thus you are literally extrapolating that learned positional mapping to larger token indices. The paper’s “interpolation” means “compress the angles” (i.e. $\lambda>1$), not “any change away from the original” — so “direct extrapolation” = no change ($\lambda=1$).

Why that matches the math. The original RoPE rotation angle for dimension $i$ is
$$
\phi_i(n)=\frac{n}{\beta^i},
$$
and the paper’s rescaled angle is
$$
\phi'_i(n)=\frac{n}{\lambda_i\,\beta^i}.
$$
The per‑token phase increment is $\Delta\phi_i=1/(\lambda_i\beta^i)$. If $\lambda_i>1$ you reduce the per‑token increment (angles are compressed) — that is interpolation (tokens get “closer” in angle space); if $\lambda_i=1$ you keep the pretrained increment exactly (no rescaling) — that is the authors’ “direct extrapolation” of the pretrained RoPE to new, larger $n$. Formally $\lambda_i<1$ would increase per‑token increments (an “expansive” change), but the paper does not explore that regime; its search space starts at $\lambda_i=1$ and only allows $\lambda_i\ge1$.

This usage is consistent with prior work cited in the paper (e.g. YaRN/NTK discussions), where “extrapolation” often meant “leave some dimensions unscaled ($\lambda=1$) so the pretrained mapping is applied directly,” while “interpolation” meant scaling angles down to fit new positions into the pretrained range. Also note the paper sometimes writes the rescale as a factor $1/\lambda_i$ in places (so watch the notation); it’s the same operation but can look inverted depending on where they define $\hat\lambda_i=1/\lambda_i$.

So the confusion comes from two different intuitive readings of “extrapolation”: one could imagine “making angles larger per step” (which would be $\lambda<1$), but the authors use “extrapolation” to mean “apply the original (unscaled) RoPE formula to new positions” (which is $\lambda=1$).

### Does \(d\) denote per-head dimension in RoPE, and are \(\lambda_i\) shared across heads and layers?
Question:
RoPE is typically applied per attention head. In LLaMA2-7B, \(d_{\text{model}}=4096\), \(n_{\text{heads}}=32\), so \(d_{\text{head}}=128\). In Eq. (1), is \(d\) meant to be \(d_{\text{head}}\) (so there are \(d/2=64\) sine-cosine pairs per head)? When you search per-dimension \(\lambda_i\), are these \(\lambda_i\) values shared across all heads and all layers, or are they distinct per head/layer? Implementation-wise, what exactly is the tensor shape over which \(\lambda_i\) is broadcast?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — in the paper $d$ is the per‑head RoPE dimension (so for LLaMA2‑7B with $d_{\text{model}}=4096$ and $n_{\text{heads}}=32$ you get $d_{\text{head}}=128$ and $d/2=64$ cosine–sine pairs), and the searched $\lambda_i$ are a single vector over those RoPE dimensions that is shared across heads and layers (i.e. one $\lambda_i$ per RoPE frequency index, not per head or per layer). The paper’s equations and search description treat the rescale factors as a $d/2$‑length array that is broadcast across batch/sequence/head/layer when applied.

Why: the paper explicitly formulates the search over each RoPE dimension index $i=0,\dots,d/2-1$ and repeatedly uses $d/2=64$ in its examples and complexity counts (for example they write $s=4\times$ search space size as $400^{128/2}\times 14$, which shows they are using $d=128$). the monotonicity constraint is described as "$\lambda_i\le\lambda_{i+1}$ … $i=0,\dots,63$", again making $d=128$ (head dim). There is no per‑head or per‑layer indexing in the search formulation, so the paper’s method learns one $\lambda_i$ per RoPE frequency index and applies it uniformly to all heads and all layers.

Implementation/shape practicalities: in code you can think of the searched vector as an array of length $D_h/2$ (with $D_h$ the head dimension). Typical attention tensors have shape $(B, T, H, D_h)$ (batch, seq_len, n_heads, head_dim). You compute the RoPE angles for pairs ($\cos/\sin$) with a base angle tensor of shape $(T, D_h/2)$ and then apply the scale factors broadcast over batch and heads. Concretely, let $\lambda$ be shaped $(D_h/2,)$; compute

angles = positions[:, None] * inv_freq[None, :]    # shape $(T, D_h/2)$

scale = 1.0 for positions < $\hat{n}$, else scale = 1.0 / $\lambda$  # shape $(T, D_h/2)$

angles = angles * scale                             # shape $(T, D_h/2)$

then expand to $(T, 1, 1, D_h/2)$ to multiply with $Q/K$ components (which you typically split into even/odd pairs so the final $\cos/\sin$ tensors broadcast to $(B, T, H, D_h/2)$ or interleaved back to $(B, T, H, D_h)$). In short, the searched $\lambda_i$ is stored as a 1‑D vector of length $D_h/2$ and broadcast over batch, time, head and layer axes when forming the rotary $\cos/\sin$ matrices.

If you wanted per‑head or per‑layer $\lambda$ you could implement that by making the vector shape $(H, D_h/2)$ or $(L, H, D_h/2)$, but the paper does not do this (and doing so would explode the search space and defeat the paper’s search/monotonicity assumptions).

### What is the theoretical or empirical justification for the monotonic constraint \(\lambda_i\le \lambda_{i+1}\)?
Question:
The evolution search enforces a non-decreasing \(\lambda_i\) across RoPE dimensions, motivated by NTK intuitions that high-frequency (lower-\(i\)) dimensions should interpolate less. Do you have empirical ablations showing performance with and without this monotonicity? Could the optimal schedule be locally non-monotonic for some models/tasks, and if so, does the constraint prevent reaching better solutions?
Answer:
Short answer: the monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ in LongRoPE is a pragmatic inductive prior motivated by NTK/frequency reasoning and by search-efficiency concerns; the paper uses it to reduce the combinatorial search cost and does not report an explicit ablation that compares constrained vs unconstrained searches. It therefore cannot prove that a locally non‑monotonic schedule could never be better, but the authors argue (and it is plausible) that monotonicity is both theoretically well‑aligned and practically helpful. Below I unpack why, what the paper actually did, and how you could test the risk that the constraint blocks better solutions.

Why monotonicity is a natural prior. RoPE dimensions are ordered by effective frequency (the paper writes the rotary angle for dimension $i$ as $n/\beta^i$ with $\beta=\theta^{2/d}$), so the lowest indices encode higher-frequency phase components and the highest indices encode lower‑frequency, slowly varying components. NTK‑based arguments (and prior works cited in the paper) recommend treating different frequencies differently when stretching/extrapolating positional encodings: you want to preserve high‑frequency components (they distinguish nearby token positions) and can afford more rescaling (interpolation) for low‑frequency components. A monotone non‑decreasing $\lambda_i$ (smaller rescale on high‑freq dims, larger on low‑freq dims) directly implements that intuition. Intuitively in signal‑processing terms, heavy interpolation is like low‑pass filtering; you don’t want to low‑pass the high‑frequency RoPE dims that encode fine positional differences.

What the paper actually does and why. In Section 3.2 the authors explicitly state they impose $\lambda_i\le\lambda_{i+1}$ during evolution search. Their two stated motives are (1) NTK/frequency theory as above and (2) a huge search space (they give example combinatorics), so enforcing monotonicity prunes vast numbers of candidate solutions and reduces wasted perplexity evaluations. The empirical gains the paper reports for LongRoPE (better perplexity and very large extensions) were obtained using this constrained search, but the paper does not include a direct ablation that compares search with and without this monotonicity constraint.

Could the optimal schedule be non‑monotonic, and does the constraint prevent better solutions? In principle yes: because RoPE interacts with transformer layers, attention patterns, tokenizer boundaries, dataset statistics and fine‑tuning dynamics, there is no formal theorem in the paper that the best per‑dimension $\lambda_i$ must be monotone. Local non‑monotonicities could be useful in pathological cases (e.g., if certain mid‑frequency channels happen to be especially important for a task or a model’s learned weights). In practice, however, two points argue that strict monotonicity is unlikely to be a major blind spot here: first, NTK/frequency theory gives a strong, cognitively simple prior that matches many prior interpolation approaches (PI/NTK/YaRN). Second, the search in the paper was run on a very small validation sample (few sequences) so unconstrained search would have large variance and a high risk of overfitting spurious non‑monotonic bumps; monotonicity acts as a useful regularizer. But because the paper did not ablate this choice, it remains an empirical question whether allowing local violations yields consistent gains.

How you could test it (practical ablation recipes). If you want to know for your model/task, try progressively relaxing the constraint rather than an all‑or‑nothing experiment; that keeps compute reasonable and isolates effects. For example, (a) run an unconstrained evolution but only on a reduced parameterization such as grouping RoPE into $G$ groups (e.g., $8$–$16$ groups) and searching one $\lambda$ per group; (b) use a soft monotonic penalty in the fitness: minimize perplexity + $\alpha\sum_i\max(0,\lambda_i-\lambda_{i+1})$ and sweep $\alpha$ from large (near hard constraint) to $0$ (no constraint); (c) seed the population from the monotone solution and allow small random residuals (so you explore local non‑monotonic deviations only); or (d) run the unconstrained search for a few generations but keep the top-$k$ survivors projected to the nearest monotone sequence to measure how much raw unconstrained winners violate monotonicity. Measure held‑out perplexity on the same long sequences used in the paper (PG19/Proof‑pile/Books3) and task metrics (passkey retrieval, short‑window recovery). If unconstrained or weakly‑constrained runs produce consistent improvement beyond noise, that is evidence the hard constraint was limiting; if not, the monotone prior is justified.

Practical recommendation. For limited compute and a very large search space, the monotonic constraint is a reasonable inductive bias: it matches NTK/frequency intuition, regularizes against overfitting to small validation samples, and dramatically reduces search cost. If you suspect your model or task has atypical frequency needs (or you have resources), use one of the relaxation ablations above to test whether local non‑monotonicity helps — but expect any gains to be modest and to come at a high search cost unless you reduce the dimensionality of the search.

Summary: the paper’s justification is theoretical (NTK/frequency ordering) plus practical (search efficiency). There is no explicit in‑paper ablation that removes the monotonicity constraint, so it remains an open empirical question whether strict monotonicity ever meaningfully hurts final performance; the experiments and theory suggest it is a sensible and low‑risk design choice in the LongRoPE pipeline.

### How is the initial-token threshold \(\hat{n}\) applied with sliding windows and streaming evaluation?
Question:
You keep the first \(\hat{n}\) tokens un-interpolated (\(\lambda_i\) not applied) because early tokens are heavily attended. But for perplexity on long documents you use sliding windows (e.g., size 256 or 256k). Are the "first \(\hat{n}\) tokens" measured relative to the global document start, or relative to each window’s local start? If it is global, how do you handle windows that start well beyond \(\hat{n}\)? If it is local, then each window reintroduces an unscaled prefix, which seems different from the intended global behavior.
Answer:
Short answer: the paper treats $\hat{n}$ as a global, document-level threshold. In LongRoPE the test/dev samples are considered as full documents and the paper's notation uses the absolute token index $n\in[0,|\mathbf{x}|)$; the rescaling mask is
$$
\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})=\begin{cases}
1 & n<\hat{n},\\[4pt]
1/\lambda_i & n\ge\hat{n},
\end{cases}
$$
so the “keep-first-$\hat{n}$” rule applies to those absolute positions in the original document (see the problem formulation / Eq. in the paper).

Why this matters for sliding windows: when you evaluate a long document with sliding windows you should still supply RoPE with the absolute position indices of each token. Concretely, if a window starts at document offset $o$ and the j-th token inside that window has local index $j$, its RoPE angle is computed with absolute position $n=o+j$. You check the condition $n<\hat{n}$ with that absolute $n$; if the whole window is past $\hat{n}$ then every token in that window will be interpolated. Resetting positions to $0$ at each window (i.e. treating $\hat{n}$ relative to the local window start) would repeatedly reintroduce an “unscaled prefix” for every window and therefore change the model semantics and evaluation in a way the paper explicitly avoids.

Intuition: RoPE encodes absolute position via rotations (angles proportional to $n$), and LongRoPE’s $\hat{n}$ design is intended to preserve only the true early-document positions (which empirically get more attention). If you reset positions per-window you artificially make many tokens look like “early” tokens and would get an optimistic, but not faithful, measure of long-context behavior. Practically, implementers therefore pass a position offset when forming position ids (e.g. $position\_ids = offset + arange(seq\_len)$) or stream tokens incrementally (KV cache with increasing absolute positions) so the threshold test uses the global $n$.

### Does switching the scale at \(n=\hat{n}\) introduce a hard discontinuity, and would a smooth ramp help?
Question:
In Eq. (3), at \(n=\hat{n}\) the factor changes from 1 to \(1/\lambda_i\). This is a hard step. Could this create boundary artifacts in attention around \(\hat{n}\)? Did you try a smooth transition (e.g., a linear or cosine ramp for \(n\in[0,\hat{n}]\)) and compare perplexity or stability?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — the paper’s design uses a hard step at $n=\hat n$, so there is a literal jump in the rotary angle mapping at that token; the authors did not report experiments with a smooth ramp. The step was a deliberate, simple design choice that the paper shows works well in practice (see Finding 2), but a smooth transition is a reasonable idea to try and has trade-offs that I summarize below along with a concrete recipe if you want to test it.

Why it is a discontinuity (what it changes mathematically). In the paper the per‑dimension scaling is applied via the indicator
$$
\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n)=
\begin{cases}
1 & n<\hat n,\\[4pt]
\frac{1}{\lambda_i} & n\ge\hat n,
\end{cases}
$$
so the rotary angle used at position $n$ for dimension $i$ is
$$
\phi_{n,i}=\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n)\cdot\frac{n}{\beta^i}.
$$
Attention between positions $n$ and $m$ depends on the relative rotation $\phi_{n,i}-\phi_{m,i}$. If one index is before and the other after the threshold then the relative angle suddenly changes by a term proportional to $(1-1/\lambda_i)$, i.e.
$$
\Delta\phi_{n,m,i}=\frac{1}{\beta^i}\big(\mathbb{I}_n\,n-\mathbb{I}_m\,m\big),
$$
so the step introduces a phase/angle jump for pairs that straddle the boundary. That jump is real (discrete, token-wise), and can in principle produce local “boundary artifacts” in attention weights for token pairs across $\hat n$.

What the paper reports empirically. The paper explicitly uses this step formulation and searches over $\hat n$ (Finding 2). Empirically they found that keeping the first $\hat n$ tokens completely un-interpolated (i.e., the hard step) improves perplexity and stability compared to applying interpolation uniformly from position 0. The method and the search over discrete $\hat n$ values are central parts of LongRoPE; the paper does not report experiments that replace the step with a smooth ramp. So the hard step is both a design choice and an empirical finding in this work.

Would a smooth ramp help? Intuition says: possibly — smoothing removes the abrupt phase jump and so can reduce sharp local mismatches in angle differences for pairs across the boundary. This could reduce any local attention artifacts and might help training stability when fine‑tuning. However, there are several important trade-offs that explain why the authors used the step:

1. preservation of early tokens. The paper’s motivation for the step is to preserve the original RoPE exactly for the initial tokens (which tend to receive high attention). A ramp that partially scales those tokens would erode that preservation and could reintroduce the “catastrophic values” the interpolation is trying to avoid.

2. search / complexity cost. A hard threshold yields a small discrete search space for $\hat n$. A ramp introduces extra hyperparameters (ramp width, shape, possibly per‑dimension ramps) and greatly enlarges the search/optimization burden the authors already addressed with evolutionary search.

3. empirical sufficiency. The step + search already produced large practical gains (8× non‑fine‑tuning extension and the progressive scheme to 2048k), so the marginal return of a ramp is uncertain.

How to test a ramp (practical recipe). If you want to experiment, you can replace the indicator by a small-width smooth step. For example pick a width $w$ and a smooth interpolant $s(x)\in[0,1]$ (linear: $s(x)=x$; cosine: $s(x)=\tfrac{1-\cos(\pi x)}{2}$). Define
$$
\mathbb{I}_i(n)=
\begin{cases}
1 & n<\hat n,\\[4pt]
1 - \big(1-\tfrac{1}{\lambda_i}\big)\,s\!\left(\dfrac{n-\hat n}{w}\right) & \hat n\le n < \hat n+w,\\[6pt]
\dfrac{1}{\lambda_i} & n\ge \hat n+w.
\end{cases}
$$
This keeps the untouched region $n<\hat n$, then smoothly moves to the scaled regime over $w$ tokens. Start with small $w$ (e.g. $w\in\{4,8,32\}$) and the same monotonicity constraint on $\lambda_i$. Evaluate exactly as in the paper (perplexity on the same PG19/Proof‑pile samples, attention visualizations around the boundary, and the passkey retrieval task). If you fine‑tune, monitor loss stability; if you run search you can include $w$ as a discrete hyperparameter.

What I’d expect and what to measure. A narrow ramp may produce negligible improvement over the hard step because it sacrifices some of the “preserve early tokens” benefit; a wider ramp could smooth attention but at the cost of moving more early tokens away from their pretrained angles. To detect boundary artifacts directly, compare attention mass and per-token loss for tokens in a small neighborhood around $\hat n$ between the step and ramp variants; also compare sliding‑window perplexities used throughout the paper. These diagnostics will show whether smoothing reduces any local instabilities and whether that translates to global perplexity or retrieval accuracy gains.

Bottom line: the hard step is a real discontinuity and could — in principle — create local boundary effects, but in LongRoPE the step was chosen because it preserves the early tokens that are most important and it works well empirically. The paper does not report smooth‑ramp experiments; trying a small, local ramp centered at $\hat n$ (as shown above) is a reasonable next experiment, but be aware it increases the hyperparameter/search burden and may reduce the key benefit of preserving the first $\hat n$ tokens.

### How large is the evolutionary search computationally, and how do you avoid overfitting to a few samples?
Question:
The paper estimates an enormous combinatorial search space (e.g., on the order of \(400^{64}\) for \(d_{\text{head}}=128\) and step 0.01). Practically, how many candidate schedules do you evaluate per target \(L'\)? Roughly how many GPU-hours does a search take for 128k or 256k? Since the fitness is perplexity on only 3–5 long samples (PG19/Books3), how do you reduce overfitting of the found \(\lambda_i,\hat{n}\) to those documents?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not try to enumerate the astronomically large combinatorial space; instead it uses a small, constrained evolutionary budget and a few concrete regularizers so that the search is practical. For targets up to $256\text{k}$ the search evaluates on the order of a few thousand candidate RoPE schedules (roughly $64\times 40 = 2560$ individuals under the paper’s reported hyperparameters), each scored on $5$ long samples; for $>512\text{k}$ the authors halve the population so the budget is roughly half. That yields $O(10^{3}\text{--}10^{4})$ long-sequence forward passes in total, and therefore search cost is dominated by inference throughput on long sequences (tens to a few hundred GPU‑hours in practice, depending on hardware and parallelism) — and overfitting to the small search set is mitigated by several deliberate design choices (seeded initial population, monotonicity and range constraints, averaging over a handful of random long samples, downstream fine‑tuning and held‑out validation).

Concrete accounting from the paper’s reported settings
The paper gives these exact search hyperparameters for targets within $256\text{k}$: population $P=64$, mutation/crossover sizes $N_1=N_2=16$, iterations $T=40$, and evaluates perplexity on $S=5$ random PG19 validation samples (each sample must be at least the target length). So the nominal number of distinct candidates evaluated is
$$\text{\#candidates} = P\times T = 64\times 40 = 2560,$$
and the number of long‑sequence model evaluations (forward passes on whole validation samples) is
$$\text{\#sample\_evals} = \text{\#candidates}\times S = 2560\times 5 = 12{,}800.$$
For windows $>512\text{k}$ they halve population and mutation/crossover sizes, so $\text{\#candidates} \approx 32\times 40 = 1280$ and they score on $S=3$ Books3 samples ($\text{\#sample\_eval} \approx 3840$).

How that maps to GPU‑hours (estimate, not a measured number)
The paper does not report absolute GPU‑hours for search, so you should convert the count above into cost using your measured per‑sample inference time. A compact formula is
$$\text{GPU‑hours} \approx \frac{\text{\#candidates}\times S \times t_{\text{sample}}}{N_{\text{GPUs\_in\_parallel}}},$$
where $t_{\text{sample}}$ is the wall‑clock time to score one long sample (one forward pass that yields next‑token logits for the whole long sequence) on a single GPU, and $N_{\text{GPUs\_in\_parallel}}$ is how many independent evaluations you can run in parallel.

Because attention costs and memory grow (roughly) quadratically with context length, $t_{\text{sample}}$ rises quickly with $L'$. As an illustration (purely an example to show scaling): if a single $128\text{k}$ sample forward takes about $1$ minute on your GPU then $12{,}800$ sample‑evals $\approx 12{,}800$ minutes $\approx 213$ GPU‑hours on one GPU. If the same $256\text{k}$ forward is $\sim 4\times$ slower (quadratic scaling), the cost climbs $\sim 4\times$. In practice you will parallelize many candidate evaluations across GPUs and use implementation/attention optimizations, so wall‑clock search time is much smaller than the single‑GPU number. The important point is: the search is inference‑only (no gradients), so it is significantly cheaper than the fine‑tuning runs the paper also reports (which needed $8$–$16$ A100s for $128\text{k}/256\text{k}$ training).

Why the search is feasible despite the astronomical nominal space
Two parts make the enormous combinatorial space practical to search in the paper:

1) Strong constraints and priors. The search restricts each $\lambda_i$ to a limited interval $[1.0,\, s\times 1.25]$ at step $0.01$, forces monotonicity $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$, and restricts $\hat{n}$ to a small set of candidate thresholds. These are powerful inductive priors (motivated by NTK theory and prior work) that collapse the effective search volume by many orders of magnitude.

2) Evolutionary engineering. The initial population is seeded with PI/NTK/YaRN solutions (so search starts near known good points), mutation/crossover respect the monotonic constraint so many bad candidates are never evaluated, and the top‑k selection concentrates compute on promising regions. All of this keeps the number of heavy, long‑sequence model evaluations to a few thousand rather than trillions.

How the paper avoids overfitting to the few search samples
The paper is explicit about this tradeoff (small S to keep search cheap) and uses multiple mechanisms to avoid overfitting:

- The fitness signal is averaged over several random long samples ($S=5$ for $\le 256\text{k}$, $S=3$ for $>512\text{k}$) rather than a single sequence, which reduces variance of the fitness estimate. The samples are chosen randomly from the validation split with a minimum length requirement, so they are not hand‑picked.
- Monotonicity and the bounded, discretized search grid act as strong regularizers that prevent pathological, high‑variance $\lambda$-vectors that would fit idiosyncrasies of a tiny set.
- The initial population seeds (PI/NTK/YaRN) bias search toward robust, previously validated schemes instead of purely data‑fitting random vectors.
- After the search the chosen RoPE initialization is used as an initialization for actual fine‑tuning on large long‑text corpora (RedPajama / Together Long‑Data Collections) for $400+600$ steps in the progressive protocol. That fine‑tuning is the main mechanism that adapts the model to real long‑sequence data and will wash out small overfits coming purely from the cheap search phase.
- Finally, the paper validates the resulting lambdas on held‑out datasets and tasks: large PG19 test split ($100$ docs), Proof‑pile samples, Books3, passkey retrieval, and standard short‑context benchmarks. Empirically those held‑out evaluations show the searched solutions generalize beyond the small fitness set used during search.

Bottom line
You should think of the paper’s evolutionary search as a cheap, regularized way to find good initial RoPE rescale patterns ($O(10^{3})$ candidate evaluations, not $O(400^{64})$). The absolute GPU‑hour cost depends on your per‑sample forward time and how much parallelism you use; converting the paper’s budget (e.g., $2560$ candidates $\times$ $5$ samples) into wall‑clock is straightforward with the formula above. Robustness to overfitting is handled by averaging fitness across random samples, strong monotonic and range priors, seeding with known good solutions, and then relying on downstream multi‑sample fine‑tuning and held‑out validation to produce a model that generalizes.

### Exactly how is perplexity computed during search: what context and masking are used?
Question:
When evaluating a candidate schedule, do you compute token-level negative log-likelihood over the entire long document, or only over suffixes (e.g., beyond 4k)? Are you using a fixed sliding-window length, and if so, what attention/memory is available across windows? Clarifying the exact perplexity protocol would help understand what the search is optimizing.
Answer:
Short answer: the search evaluates standard autoregressive next‑token loss (i.e. $\text{token NLL} \to \text{perplexity}$) on long text segments whose length meets the target context size, with the candidate RoPE rescale schedule applied to positions inside those segments. The paper uses a small set of long validation samples per candidate (5 PG19 samples for targets $\le 256k$, 3 Pile‑Books3 samples for very large targets) and ranks candidates by the averaged perplexity computed on those segments. The model uses ordinary causal attention inside each evaluated window; there is no extra cross‑window memory mechanism during the search/evaluation passes reported.

What the paper actually says (and what that implies)
- The objective being minimized in the search is the next‑token prediction loss (they explicitly write that the search is guided by perplexity / next‑token loss in Eq. (problem) and in the surrounding text). Concretely, this means the usual autoregressive token negative log‑likelihood is computed and averaged (then exponentiated to give perplexity in reporting).
- Candidates are applied to full long inputs $X$ with the constraint that each sample $x$ satisfies $|x|\ge L'$, where $L'$ is the target context length. The RoPE transform used for inference is exactly as defined in the paper (positions $n<\hat n$ keep original RoPE; positions $n\ge\hat n$ use the candidate rescale factor).
- For search cost reasons they do not evaluate on entire corpora: for target windows within $256k$ they compute perplexity on 5 random PG19 validation samples (each at least $L'$ long); for windows $>512k$ they use 3 Pile‑Books3 samples (see the "Search" paragraph in Setup). The paper therefore evaluates candidates by averaging the standard token NLL across those selected long segments.
- For the main perplexity evaluations reported across many context lengths the paper says they use a sliding‑window evaluation with a step of $256$ tokens (they write “sliding window of $256$”), and for the very long Books3 run they say “sliding window of $256k$”. That is the evaluation protocol used to produce the curves/tables; the search itself is described as using the small set of long samples above.

Points the paper does not fully specify
- The paper does not give a line‑by‑line implementation description of how they chop/slide segments during the search (for example, whether a candidate is evaluated on a single contiguous chunk of length $L'$ taken from a sampled document, or on multiple overlapping $L'$ windows from that document and then averaged). It only states the minimum length requirement $|x|\ge L'$ and the small sample counts used for candidate scoring. It likewise does not state any special masking beyond the standard causal attention mask inside the evaluated window.
- The paper does not introduce any persistent hidden‑state / cross‑window memory for search or evaluation. From the text it follows that windows are processed with the model’s normal causal attention over the window (with RoPE modified per the candidate), and different windows are independent evaluation passes.

Intuition / consequence for what the search is optimizing
Because search minimizes the standard next‑token loss on long segments (not just the tail beyond some baseline context), candidate schedules are rewarded for preserving short‑range modeling (tokens early in the window still contribute to loss) while also improving long‑range positional discriminability. The $\hat n$ parameter (keep original RoPE for the first $\hat n$ tokens) explicitly lets the search protect the important early positions; the averaged perplexity over the selected long samples is the scalar the evolutionary search uses to pick better per‑dimension rescale schedules.

If you need exact per‑token bookkeeping (e.g. whether losses in each evaluated window exclude some warm‑up tokens, or exactly how many windows per document were averaged during search) the paper does not publish that low‑level implementation detail; the released code / evaluation scripts (the authors say code will be released) or asking the authors will give the definitive answer.

### How robust are the searched \(\lambda_i\) and \(\hat{n}\) across domains and model families?
Question:
You search on PG19/Books3-like text but evaluate on other datasets and tasks. Do the same schedules transfer well to code/math or dialogue? For Mistral vs LLaMA2, do you need to re-run the full search per model family, or can one family’s schedule initialize the other with minimal loss?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper’s experiments and ablations indicate that the per-dimension rescale factors $\lambda_i$ and the preserved‑prefix threshold $\hat{n}$ are not strictly universal — they depend on the target extension ratio and on the model / fine‑tuning regime. The authors therefore perform (and recommend) an explicit search per target length and per model; the paper does not claim that a single schedule found on PG19/Books3 will be optimal for very different domains such as code, math or dialogue. That said, the search is intentionally cheap (small validation sets, seeded initialization and a monotone prior) so adapting a schedule to a new model or domain is practical.

Why (intuitively and technically): LongRoPE’s $\lambda_i$ rescales RoPE’s rotation angles per RoPE dimension (in the paper the RoPE entry for dimension $i$ at token position $n$ becomes $\cos(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\times \frac{n}{\beta^i})$, $\sin(\cdot)$ with $\mathbb{I}=1$ for $n<\hat{n}$ and $\mathbb{I}=1/\lambda_i$ otherwise). Different models and corpora distribute positional information (the “spectral” energy across RoPE dimensions) differently: tokenizers, pretraining data statistics and architectural choices change which RoPE dimensions are “important” and how attention is used over tokens. The paper’s ablations explicitly show that non‑uniform $\lambda_i$ and a nonzero $\hat{n}$ help, but the optimal pattern depends on the extension ratio and the model/fine‑tuning setup (e.g., LLaMA2 benefited more from a 256k fine‑tune whereas Mistral in the paper performed better when fine‑tuned at 128k). That empirical difference is the paper’s direct evidence that schedules are model/setting dependent.

What the paper evaluated (so what is supported): searches were run on LLaMA2-7B and Mistral-7B (separately), using PG19 / Books3-like long text for search and validation (the search objective is perplexity on a few held‑out long samples — the paper uses 5 PG19 validation samples for many searches, and 3 Pile‑Books3 samples for very large windows). The final LongRoPE models were evaluated on multiple natural‑language long‑context tasks (Proof‑pile, PG19, Books3 sliding windows, passkey retrieval and standard short‑context benchmarks). The paper does not report experiments on code‑only, math‑heavy, or dialogue datasets, nor does it report cross‑model transfer where a schedule found on model A is applied unchanged to model B.

Practical takeaways / recommendations (grounded in the paper):

- If your target domain is similar to the search corpus used in the paper (natural long text like Books3 / PG19), the paper’s schedules and priors (monotonic $\lambda_i$ increasing with dimension, and a small preserved-prefix $\hat{n}$) are already shown to transfer across several natural‑language evaluations. The monotone constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ is a useful, theory‑backed prior (NTK) that reduces search cost and appears broadly sensible.

- If you move to a substantially different domain (code, mathematical text, chat transcripts), treat the published schedules as a strong seed but validate and re‑search. The authors’ search is lightweight: they seed the initial population with PI/NTK/YaRN schedules, enforce monotonicity, evaluate perplexity on just a few long examples and run evolution. So you can cheaply run a short adaptation search (few validation examples, small population / few iterations) to adapt $\{\lambda_i\}$ and $\hat{n}$ to your domain.

- For moving between model families (e.g., LLaMA2 $\leftrightarrow$ Mistral) the paper’s workflow is to search per model. The empirical differences reported (different best fine‑tune length and different perplexity curves) argue for re‑running at least a small search per model. If compute is tight, initialize the search population with the other model’s schedule (the paper’s “optimized initial population” strategy is exactly this idea) and run a reduced evolution to adapt it; that is likely much cheaper than a full cold search and is consistent with the paper’s design.

- If you only need modest extension (the paper shows up to a $8\times$ extension without fine‑tuning for their LLaMA2 experiments using searched non‑uniform $\lambda_i$), try the non‑fine‑tuned schedule first; for very large extensions follow the progressive pipeline in the paper (search → moderate fine‑tune at a mid‑length like 128k/256k → second search) because the paper shows this is both more stable and much cheaper than attempting a single giant fine‑tune.

In short: the paper demonstrates per‑model and per‑length dependence of the optimal $\lambda_i$ and $\hat{n}$; it does not show automatic, guaranteed transfer to code/math/dialogue. Best practice (consistent with the paper) is to re‑validate and, in most cases, run a short adaptation search per new model or domain; using another model’s schedule as a seed (rather than as a final answer) is a practical shortcut that the paper’s search framework was designed to support.

### How do NTK and YaRN map into your unified notation, and how does LongRoPE differ qualitatively?
Question:
You state that NTK distributes scaling across dimensions (\(\lambda=s^i\) in your text), and YaRN uses three frequency-based groups. In the unified form \(\cos\big(n/(\lambda_i\beta^i)\big)\), could you derive explicitly what \(\lambda_i\) sequences correspond to NTK and YaRN? Qualitatively, are LongRoPE’s learned \(\lambda_i\) closer to a smooth curve (like NTK) or piecewise-constant groups (like YaRN)?
Answer:
Short answer: in the paper’s unified RoPE form
$$\cos\!\Big(\frac{n}{\lambda_i\beta^i}\Big)$$
the NTK and YaRN prescriptions correspond to simple, explicit sequences for $\lambda_i$; LongRoPE instead learns a monotone, per-dimension sequence that is neither the rigid NTK power-law nor the coarse piecewise-constant grouping of YaRN — in practice it looks like a smooth, monotonic curve with local, learned deviations that preserve a few “key” dimensions and early token positions.

Derivations in the unified notation. Using the paper’s notation (original rotary angle $n/\beta^i$, extension ratio $s$):

- PI (baseline linear interpolation) is the trivial constant scaling across all RoPE dimensions:
  $$
  \lambda_i^{\text{PI}} = s\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad
  \cos\!\Big(\frac{n}{s\,\beta^i}\Big).
  $$

- NTK (as described in the paper) distributes scaling across dimensions by a per-dimension power of $s$. The paper writes this schematically as $\lambda = s^{i}$, so in the unified form
  $$
  \lambda_i^{\text{NTK}} = s^{\,i}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad
  \cos\!\Big(\frac{n}{s^{\,i}\beta^i}\Big)=\cos\!\Big(\frac{n}{(s\beta)^{i}}\Big).
  $$
  Intuition: for small $i$ (high-frequency dimensions) $s^{i}\approx 1$ so little scaling (extrapolation); for large $i$ you get much larger $\lambda_i$ (more interpolation). Note that this is a smooth, analytic (exponential-in-$i$) shape.

- YaRN uses three frequency-based groups and therefore a piecewise definition of $\lambda_i$. If you mark two cut indices $i_{\text{high}}$ and $i_{\text{low}}$ that partition the dimension index set, YaRN’s pattern maps to
  $$
  \lambda_i^{\text{YaRN}}=
  \begin{cases}
    1 & i < i_{\text{high}}\quad\text{(high-frequency group: extrapolate)}\\[4pt]
    s^{\,i} & i_{\text{high}}\le i < i_{\text{low}}\quad\text{(middle group: NTK-like)}\\[4pt]
    s & i\ge i_{\text{low}}\quad\text{(low-frequency group: linear PI)}
  \end{cases}
  $$
  So YaRN is a coarse, piecewise-constant / piecewise-prescribed policy across frequency bands (high freq untouched, low freq uniformly scaled, middle uses NTK-like scaling).

Where LongRoPE sits between these two extremes. LongRoPE does a per-dimension search over $\lambda_i$ (with an imposed monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i\le\lambda_{i+1}$ and a bounded search range $[1,\,1.25s]$ as described in the paper). That design point has three immediate consequences that explain its qualitative shape:

- monotonicity bias: the search forces $\lambda_i$ to be non-decreasing in $i$, so the learned sequence is a single ascending curve rather than an arbitrary jagged sequence; this matches the same principle behind NTK (low dimensions require less interpolation than high dimensions).
- per-dimension flexibility: because $\lambda_i$ is optimized per dimension (not by coarse groups), the final sequence can make small, targeted departures from a simple analytic law — e.g. keep particular low- or mid-frequency dimensions very close to 1 to “preserve” them, while increasing others — something neither pure NTK nor YaRN can do.
- boundedness: unlike the analytical NTK formula $s^i$ (which grows very large for big $i$), LongRoPE’s search caps $\lambda_i$ (the paper uses $\lambda_i \le 1.25s$), so the learned curve does not explode for high $i$.

Putting those points together: the empirical description in the paper is that the searched $\{\lambda_i\}$ are “non‑uniform” and differ from PI, NTK and YaRN; because of the monotonic constraint and fine-grained per-dimension search they look much more like a smooth, graded curve (NTK-like in the broad monotone sense) but with learned local deviations and gentler saturation (because of the cap), rather than the coarse three-block pattern of YaRN. In other words LongRoPE learns a monotone, frequency-dependent rescaling function that bridges NTK’s analytic smoothness and YaRN’s intuition about groups, and this learned curve empirically preserves key RoPE dimensions and early token positions (via the separate $\hat n$ threshold) which is why it improves perplexity and enables larger extension ratios.

### How are 2M-token evaluations performed given \(O(n^2)\) attention cost—full context or chunked?
Question:
Naively attending over \(2{,}048\text{k}\) tokens is infeasible for standard dense attention. In the long-sequence experiments you mention sliding windows (e.g., 256k). During Books3 and passkey evaluations, does the model have full attention over the entire \(L'\), or is attention restricted to a window and state is streamed/evicted? If it is chunked/streamed, in what sense is the model truly operating with an effective context of \(\approx 2\text{M}\) tokens?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not run a single dense $O(n^2)$ attention over $2{,}048\text{k}$ tokens in one monolithic forward pass. For long-document perplexity and Books3 evaluation the authors explicitly use a sliding / chunked evaluation (they say “for evaluation efficiency … use a sliding window of $256\text{k}$”), and more generally they compute perplexities with sliding windows rather than trying to allocate an $L'=2{,}048\text{k}$ attention matrix at once. The paper does not give low‑level implementation details for the passkey runs, so it does not claim a single-shot dense $2\,$M-token matmul was performed there either.

Why this matters: dense attention costs $O(n^2)$ memory and compute, so feeding $2\,$M tokens in one shot is essentially infeasible on a single GPU. The standard, practical way (and the one used in the paper for perplexity/Books3) is sliding-window evaluation: you present manageable windows of size $W$ (the paper uses $256\text{k}$ windows in Books3), slide them across the long text with some stride, and accumulate log‑likelihoods. That keeps each forward pass at $O(W^2)$ memory while letting you evaluate behavior over very long documents without ever allocating an $O(L'^2)$ attention matrix for $L'=2\,$M.

What “effective $2\,$M context” means in this work. LongRoPE is primarily about changing the RoPE positional mapping so the model has meaningful rotary encodings up to very large absolute indices. There are two different senses in which a model can “use” a $2\,$M context:

- Positional capacity: after LongRoPE the model can accept position indices up to $\sim 2\,$M and compute sensible relative/absolute encodings for them. That is what the paper demonstrates and what they mean by “extending the context window to $2{,}048\text{k}$.”

- Runtime attention scope: whether the model actually attends to $2\,$M tokens simultaneously depends on how you do inference. If you (a) feed the whole $2\,$M tokens at once, you obtain true full‑context attention semantics but at prohibitive $O(L'^2)$ cost unless you shard KV memory across many GPUs or use other heavy engineering; or (b) stream the text and keep per‑layer key/value caches (incremental caching), in which case the model can in effect attend to the entire past history because every new step attends to the cached keys/values — that yields the same semantics as full attention but is implemented incrementally (memory grows $O(L')$ and total compute is still quadratic overall but distributed over steps). If instead you only evaluate with smaller, disjoint windows, then the model only “sees” and attends within the window (effective context $=$ window size).

Paper facts vs. inference gaps: the paper explicitly documents chunked/sliding-window evaluation for Books3 and uses sliding-window procedures for perplexity experiments (they report different window sizes/strides in the experimental section). It does not give a detailed account of how the passkey runs were implemented at the low level (KV sharding, streaming infrastructure, number of GPUs, etc.). So the safe reading of the experiments is that they tested the model’s positional capability up to $2\,$M and evaluated using practical chunking/streaming strategies rather than attempting an impractical single-shot $2\,$M dense attention on a single device.

If you want to reproduce true full‑history behavior (so that the model literally attends over $2\,$M tokens of content), you will need to run inference with either (1) incremental KV caching (streaming) while ensuring you have enough memory or (2) a distributed/sharded KV implementation that stores keys/values across devices. If you only want to evaluate modeling quality over long texts without full simultaneous attention, sliding-window evaluation (the approach the paper uses for Books3) is the standard, much cheaper option.

### Passkey retrieval at 2M tokens: is the key truly within the active attention window at answer time?
Question:
For the passkey retrieval setup, the key is uniformly placed anywhere up to \(L'=2{,}048\text{k}\). During generation of the answer, does the attention mechanism have access to the entire prefix simultaneously, or do you process the document in chunks and carry some compressed state? If chunked, what guarantees that the key is still represented and not evicted when the question is asked at the end?
Answer:
Short answer: the passkey experiments in the paper are testing the model with the full extended context in the active attention window, not a chunk‑and‑compress pipeline. LongRoPE is a positional‑embedding (RoPE) modification that preserves the standard self‑attention mechanism across an input of length up to $L'=2{,}048\text{k}$, so the passkey (placed uniformly anywhere in that $L'$) is inside the model’s reachable attention scope at answer time.

Why: LongRoPE does not introduce an external memory, summary states, or a streaming/compression mechanism. It changes only how rotary positional angles are scaled (per RoPE dimension and with an optional untouched prefix of size $\hat{n}$), so the model’s queries and keys still exist for every token in the input and standard $QK^T$ attention is computed across the whole prefix. In other words, the model’s attention matrix still includes pairwise interactions between the question token(s) and every token in the $L'$‑length prefix; the RoPE rescaling just attempts to keep those pairwise signals meaningful even at very large distances. The paper’s reported passkey accuracies (e.g. $\gtrsim 90\%$ up to $2{,}048\text{k}$ for LongRoPE‑LLaMA2) are exactly the empirical evidence that the model can attend to and recover a randomly placed five‑digit passkey from the full prompt.

What about chunking / compressed states? The paper explicitly contrasts LongRoPE with retrieval/streaming approaches (which do modify architecture to hold compressed memory or use chunked processing). LongRoPE is described as “retain[ing] the original architecture with minor modifications to the positional embedding” and the experimental setup (prompt template in the appendix, passkeys placed uniformly across the evaluation context length) indicates the full long document was given to the model as context. If you were to implement your own $2M$‑token inference by processing chunks and compressing intermediate chunks, you would be in a different design space and would need to ensure your compressor preserves exact token info (or an index) so the passkey is not evicted — that is not what the authors did or relied on.

Practical caveat: feeding millions of tokens into vanilla self‑attention is extremely costly (quadratic compute/memory). The paper doesn’t change attention complexity; it instead relies on the positional interpolation + fine‑tuning to make those attention computations meaningful at large $L'$, and notes that existing attention optimizations can be reused. In practice reproducing these experiments requires specialized, memory‑efficient attention implementations or distributed execution; but that is an engineering concern separate from the methodological point that the model’s active attention window truly contains the whole $2{,}048\text{k}$ prefix in the paper’s passkey tests.

If you want to be $100\%$ certain for their run: check the appendix / code (they will release it) for the exact inference pipeline they used; but based on the text and reported results, the passkey is inside the active attention window rather than only being represented in some compressed intermediary.

### Why does "position crowding" hurt short-context performance, and how does the 8k readjustment fix it?
Question:
Intuitively, scaling to very large \(L'\) compresses the effective angle changes for small \(n\), making nearby positions harder to distinguish. Could you provide a simple geometric or spectral explanation (e.g., phase increment per token in key dimensions) and show how readjusting to a dedicated 4k/8k schedule restores sufficient angular separation without breaking long-context performance?
Answer:
Short answer: “Position crowding” is just the geometric/spectral consequence of shrinking the per-token rotation (phase) that RoPE uses to tell nearby positions apart. Uniformly down‑scaling all RoPE frequencies by the extension ratio $s$ makes the per‑token phase increments about $\frac{1}{s}$ smaller, so adjacent tokens become almost the same point on each rotary circle and the query–key similarity kernel stops decaying with small position offsets. LongRoPE’s $4\text{k}/8\text{k}$ readjustment fixes this by (a) keeping the high‑frequency RoPE dimensions and the first few token positions much closer to their pretrained angles (so local phase increments remain large enough), and (b) switching to that “short‑length” rescale schedule at inference time for sequences below $8\text{k}$. That restores angular separation for short contexts while still using the long‑context schedule when you actually need million‑token range.

A bit more technically (but still intuitive). RoPE represents position $n$ in each complex pair (dimension) $i$ as a rotation with angle
$$
\phi_{n,i} = n\omega_i\qquad\text{(paper uses }\omega_i=1/\beta^i\text{ so } \phi_{n,i}=n/\beta^i\text{).}
$$
After applying a rescale factor $\lambda_i$ (the paper applies $\frac{1}{\lambda_i}$ to the angle for positions $n\ge\hat{n}$), the angle becomes
$$
\phi'_{n,i}=\frac{n\omega_i}{\lambda_i}.
$$
When attention forms the dot product between the rotated query at position $n$ and key at position $m$ the positional contribution from dimension $i$ is proportional to $\cos\big((n-m)\omega_i/\lambda_i\big)$. Summing over dimensions gives an effective positional kernel
$$
K(n,m)=\sum_i w_i\cos\!\Big((n-m)\frac{\omega_i}{\lambda_i}\Big),
$$
where the positive weights $w_i$ capture per‑dimension magnitudes coming from the content vectors.

For small offsets $\delta=n-m$ we can Taylor expand $\cos(x)\approx 1-\tfrac{x^2}{2}$. That yields
$$
K(n,m)\approx\text{const} - \frac{\delta^2}{2}\sum_i w_i\Big(\frac{\omega_i}{\lambda_i}\Big)^2.
$$
So the “curvature” of the similarity kernel that separates nearby positions is governed by
$$
S\;=\;\sum_i w_i\Big(\frac{\omega_i}{\lambda_i}\Big)^2.
$$
Uniform interpolation (PI) sets $\lambda_i=s$ for all $i$, so $S$ is reduced by a factor $\frac{1}{s^2}$. If $s$ is large (the paper’s progressive pipeline eventually uses a $512\times$ overall extension), $S$ collapses and adjacent tokens produce almost identical positional phases: that is the geometric picture of crowding—many positions collapse into a small arc on each rotary circle so the model loses fine positional resolution and short‑context performance degrades.

The spectral intuition is the same: RoPE dimensions form a band of frequencies. High‑frequency dims ($\omega_i$ large) are what encode fine, local positional differences; low‑frequency dims encode long‑range periodic structure. Uniform scaling is equivalent to compressing the whole frequency bank toward zero (a low‑pass effect), which kills the high‑frequency power needed to resolve nearby tokens.

How the $8\text{k}/4\text{k}$ readjustment undoes this. LongRoPE’s search does two compatible things the paper describes: it makes $\lambda_i$ non‑uniform across dimensions (smaller $\lambda_i$ for high‑frequency dims so they’re preserved), and it optionally preserves the first $\hat{n}$ tokens (no rescaling for $n<\hat{n}$). For short‑length recovery the paper runs a second, constrained evolution search on the extended model but restricts the maximum allowed $\lambda$ (i.e., forces less interpolation). The result is a rescale vector where the dimensions that matter for local discrimination keep much of their original $\omega_i$ (so $\omega_i/\lambda_i$ remains large enough) and the first tokens can be left unmodified. During inference the model uses that $4\text{k}/8\text{k}$ rescale schedule when the sequence length is below $8\text{k}$, and uses the long‑context schedule for large sequences. Thus short‑context angular separation (large $S$) is restored without “breaking” long‑context behavior because you only apply the short‑length rescale when you actually have a short sequence; for long sequences you keep the aggressively scaled schedule that enables million‑token coverage.

So in one sentence: position crowding = per‑token phase increments become too small (geometrically points collapse on the rotary circles; spectrally high frequencies are suppressed), and the $4\text{k}/8\text{k}$ readjustment restores those phase increments for the dimensions and positions that matter for local discrimination (via a targeted, constrained rescale search and dynamic switching), recovering short‑context accuracy while leaving the long‑context schedule intact.

### Clarifying the indicator function in Eq. (3): is it equivalent to dividing by \(\lambda_i\) for \(n\ge\hat{n}\)?
Question:
Eq. (3) defines \(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\) so that the angle is \(\cos(\mathbb{I}\cdot n/\beta^i)\), with \(\mathbb{I}=1\) for \(n<\hat{n}\) and \(\mathbb{I}=1/\lambda_i\) for \(n\ge\hat{n}\). This appears to match your earlier \(\cos\big(n/(\lambda_i\beta^i)\big)\) form. Could you confirm this is the intended behavior and that there is no additional \(\beta\) or \(\lambda\) exponent mixing (e.g., the "\(\lambda(\beta)^i\)" notation in Eq. (2))?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — Eq.(3) implements exactly that. For positions $n\ge\hat n$ the indicator multiplies the original RoPE angle by $1/\lambda_i$, so the angle becomes
$$
\frac{1}{\lambda_i}\cdot\frac{n}{\beta^i} \;=\; \frac{n}{\lambda_i\,\beta^i},
$$
and for $n<\hat n$ you keep the original angle $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$.

To make the mapping explicit: the paper's RoPE angle per dimension is $n\theta_i = n/\beta^i$. Eq.(3) writes
$$
\cos\!\Big(\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n)\cdot\frac{n}{\beta^i}\Big),
$$
with $\mathbb{I}=1$ when $n<\hat n$ and $\mathbb{I}=1/\lambda_i$ when $n\ge\hat n$. Thus algebraically it is
$$
\cos\!\begin{cases}\dfrac{n}{\beta^i}&(n<\hat n),\\[6pt]\dfrac{n}{\lambda_i\beta^i}&(n\ge\hat n).\end{cases}
$$
The slightly awkward notation in Eq.(2) — written as $\lambda(\beta)^i$ — is intended as multiplication with the per-dimension frequency term, i.e. the denominator is $\lambda\cdot\beta^i$ (or, when $\lambda$ varies by dimension, $\lambda_i\cdot\beta^i$). There is no extra exponent mixing: $\lambda$ is a multiplicative rescaling of the denominator (not an exponent of $\beta$ or vice versa).

One small source of notational confusion in the paper: during search they parameterize by $\lambda_i$ (the scale $>1$) but sometimes use a hat to denote the reciprocal; concretely the applied factor in the angle is $1/\lambda_i$. So your interpretation that Eq.(3) recovers the form $\cos\!\big(n/(\lambda_i\beta^i)\big)$ for $n\ge\hat n$ is correct.

### Why restrict \(\lambda_i\ge 1\)? Did you try allowing \(\lambda_i<1\) (true extrapolation)?
Question:
Your search bounds \(\lambda_i\in[1,1.25s]\). Allowing \(\lambda_i<1\) would enlarge the angle increments and could counteract short-context crowding even for large \(L'\). Did you try \(\lambda_i<1\) and find instability, worse perplexity, or other issues? Is the lower bound mainly a search-efficiency or stability choice?
Answer:
Short answer: we did not allow $\lambda_i<1$ in LongRoPE’s reported experiments — the search-space lower bound was $\lambda_i=1.0$ (see the Search Space paragraph: “$\lambda_i$ is allowed to search from a minimum value of 1.0 …”). That choice was intentional and is primarily about stability and preserving the pretrained positional distribution; search-efficiency was a secondary practical reason.

Why that mattered in practice. In the paper we treat the per-dimension rescaling so that for positions $n\ge\hat n$ the RoPE angle becomes
$$
\text{angle}_{i}(n)=\frac{1}{\lambda_i}\cdot\frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}},
$$
so taking $\lambda_i<1$ makes the multiplicative factor $1/\lambda_i>1$ and therefore increases the rotary angle increments compared to pretraining. While that indeed “de-crowds” nearby positions (the intuition you point out), it also pushes the model’s input into a region of angle space that the model never saw during pretraining. The paper repeatedly emphasizes that new/untrained position indices can introduce “catastrophic values” (Introduction) — moving angles outside the pretrained range risks exactly that: out-of-distribution RoPE inputs, faster phase-wrapping (aliasing) of $\cos/\sin$ components, and degraded short-context behavior that the model was originally optimized for. Prior works (NTK, YaRN) use $\lambda=1$ to mean “leave this dimension unchanged (extrapolation)”; LongRoPE follows that conservative convention and only searches toward larger $\lambda_i$ (more interpolation) when needed.

Why stability/generalization dominated the decision. Allowing $\lambda_i<1$ would greatly enlarge the search space and the chance of finding degenerate solutions that look good on a tiny validation set but generalize poorly (the paper already notes the search space is astronomically large). It would also expose the model to stronger OOD shifts that we observed are the main obstacle to extreme context extension (the “catastrophic values” problem). To balance these risks we constrained $\lambda_i\ge1$ and used the monotonicity regularizer $\lambda_i\le\lambda_{i+1}$ plus careful initialization (PI/NTK/YaRN seeds) to keep search focused on stable, plausible rescalings informed by NTK theory (lower-frequency dims can tolerate more interpolation, etc.).

Did we try $\lambda_i<1$ and find it broken? No — the experiments reported in the paper did not explore $\lambda_i<1$ because the search lower bound was set to 1.0. We therefore cannot claim empirical results for that regime in this work.

If you want to explore $\lambda_i<1$ safely (suggestions grounded in the paper’s findings): restrict the deviation from 1 to be small (e.g., test $\lambda_i\in[0.9,1]$) and only for a small subset of dimensions (high-frequency or low-frequency, whichever you hypothesize helps), keep the monotonicity constraint, evaluate with multiple long validation samples (not just a few) to avoid overfitting, and be prepared to fine-tune (even a small number of steps) after applying such extrapolation so the model can adapt to the new angle regime. Also monitor short-context perplexity closely — the paper shows short-context recovery is an important concern and may require a separate readjustment step if you push angles far from pretraining.

In short: the lower bound $\lambda_i\ge1$ in LongRoPE was chosen mainly for stability and to avoid severe OOD / aliasing effects that are known to hurt perplexity and short-context performance; expanding the search to $\lambda_i<1$ is an interesting idea but must be done carefully and validated thoroughly.

### How does the RoPE base \(\theta\) affect LongRoPE, and do schedules transfer across bases?
Question:
In RoPE, \(\theta\) (often \(10^4\)) sets the base frequencies, and \(\beta=\theta^{2/d}\). Some models use larger bases (e.g., \(10^5\) or \(10^6\)). If you learn \(\lambda_i\) for \(\theta=10^4\), can you reuse them for \(\theta=10^6\), or must you re-run the search? Is there a base-invariant way to express the learned schedule (e.g., as a function of normalized frequency)?
Answer:
Short answer: mathematically you can convert a LongRoPE schedule between RoPE bases exactly, so you do not strictly need to re-run the full search — but in practice you should at least validate (and usually re-seed a short search) because of discretization, bounds/monotonicity constraints and empirical interactions with fine‑tuning.

Why and how (derivation). The paper writes each (pre‑rescale) rotary angle as
$\alpha_{i}(n)=n\theta_i = n/\beta^{\,i}$ with $\beta=\theta^{2/d}$. LongRoPE applies a per‑dimension rescale $1/\lambda_i$ so the rescaled angle is
$$\tilde\alpha_{i}(n)=\frac{1}{\lambda_i}\,\frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}.$$
If you have a schedule $\{\lambda_i^{(0)}\}$ that was found for base $\theta_0$ (with $\beta_0=\theta_0^{2/d}$) and you want an equivalent schedule for base $\theta_1$ (with $\beta_1=\theta_1^{2/d}$) you require
$$\frac{1}{\lambda_i^{(1)}}\frac{n}{\beta_1^{\,i}}=\frac{1}{\lambda_i^{(0)}}\frac{n}{\beta_0^{\,i}}\quad\text{for all }n,$$
which gives the closed form conversion
$$\boxed{\;\lambda_i^{(1)}=\lambda_i^{(0)}\left(\frac{\beta_0}{\beta_1}\right)^{i}
=\lambda_i^{(0)}\left(\frac{\theta_0}{\theta_1}\right)^{\!2i/d}\;.}$$
Equivalently you can store the base‑independent per‑dimension angular multiplier
$$a_i=\frac{1}{\lambda_i}\frac{1}{\beta^{\,i}}\;,$$
and recover $\lambda_i$ for any base $\beta$ by $\lambda_i=1/(a_i\beta^{\,i})$.

Intuition. Changing $\theta$ shifts the whole geometric frequency ladder of RoPE; the factor $(\theta_0/\theta_1)^{2i/d}$ rescales that ladder dimension‑by‑dimension. So to keep the *actual* rotary angles (and thus attention geometry) the same you must compensate by an $i$‑dependent factor. In log space the change is linear in $i$: $\log\lambda_i^{(1)}=\log\lambda_i^{(0)}+\frac{2i}{d}\log(\theta_0/\theta_1)$.

Practical caveats (why you might still re-run or refine search). The conversion above is exact only if you want to reproduce the same numerical rotary angles. In practice:

- LongRoPE enforces monotonicity $\lambda_i\le\lambda_{i+1}$ and clamps $\lambda_i$ to a search range; applying the analytic mapping can produce values outside those bounds or slightly violate monotonicity. You should project/clamp and re‑enforce monotonicity (or use the mapped schedule as an initial seed for the evolutionary search).
- The search outputs are discretized (step size $0.01$ in the paper) and optimized for a particular model checkpoint and fine‑tuning state. If the model weights change (different fine‑tuning, different training length, different target extension ratio $s$) the mapped schedule may be suboptimal.
- Large changes in $\theta$ (orders of magnitude) can produce large per‑dimension multipliers for high‑index dims, so empirical validation is important.
- The search also includes the token‑threshold $\hat n$ (how many starting tokens keep original RoPE). That value does not depend on $\theta$ and can normally be reused, but you should validate it too.

Recommended workflow. If you have a schedule learned at $\theta_0$ and want to move to $\theta_1$:

1. Compute $\lambda_i^{(1)}$ by the formula above (or compute/store the base‑independent $a_i$ and reproject).
2. Clamp / project to your allowed range and re‑enforce monotonicity (simple monotone projection).
3. Validate perplexity / a small passkey retrieval test. If performance is acceptable, you can use it directly.
4. Otherwise use the transformed schedule as an initial population for a short LongRoPE evolutionary search (much cheaper than full fine‑tuning) to refine.

Base‑invariant parameterizations. Two natural, more transfer‑friendly ways to represent a schedule are (a) store the absolute per‑dimension angular multiplier $a_i$ defined above (this is exactly base‑independent), or (b) parameterize $\lambda$ as a smooth function of normalized dimension index $i/(d/2-1)$ or of log‑frequency. The paper’s monotone, per‑dimension treatment already implies the schedule is smooth in $i$, so using a low‑dimensional parametric form (e.g. a small set of control points in log‑frequency space) can make transfer across $\theta$ easier and reduce the need for full re‑search.

Bottom line: you do not have to completely re‑run LongRoPE to move between RoPE bases because there is a simple analytic conversion. But for robustness (bounds, monotonicity, discretization and empirical fine‑tuning effects) it is wise to validate and, in many cases, run a short refinement search using the analytically mapped schedule as an initial guess.

### What are the "catastrophic values" at unseen positions, and can you quantify them?
Question:
The introduction mentions that untrained new position indices introduce catastrophic values and out-of-distribution issues. Do you observe exploding/vanishing attention logits or anomalous layernorm/activation statistics at long positions pre-interpolation? Any histograms or gradients showing how interpolation reduces these pathologies?
Answer:
Short answer: in this paper “catastrophic values” means out‑of‑distribution positional encodings and the downstream attention/activation patterns they induce (not numeric infinities). The authors do not show internal histograms or gradient plots in the paper; they quantify the failure modes and the improvement from LongRoPE using external task metrics (perplexity curves, passkey‑retrieval accuracy, and fine‑tuning convergence behaviour). The paper’s empirical evidence for the pathology is the large spikes in perplexity and loss / failures to converge when naive interpolation (or no interpolation) is used, and the much smoother perplexity and high retrieval accuracy after LongRoPE’s non‑uniform rescaling.

Why those “catastrophic values” happen (intuitively + a concise technical description). RoPE encodes position by rotating query/key subspaces, so for dimension $i$ the phase for token position $n$ is
$$
\phi_i(n)=n\cdot \omega_i
$$
where $\omega_i$ is the per‑dimension frequency (the paper writes the RoPE vector as $[ \cos(n\omega_0),\sin(n\omega_0),\dots]$). New, unseen positions (large $n$) are out of the distribution seen during pretraining: either the raw phases $\phi_i(n)$ are far outside the trained range, or a naive interpolation rescales them and causes many tokens to map to very similar phases. After a uniform linear interpolation by factor $\lambda$ the phase increment between adjacent tokens becomes
$$
\Delta\phi'_i=\frac{\omega_i}{\lambda}\,,
$$
so the number of tokens per full $2\pi$ rotation scales as
$$
N'_{\text{cycle},i}=\frac{2\pi}{\Delta\phi'_i}=\frac{2\pi\lambda}{\omega_i}.
$$
Large $\lambda$ (e.g. PI with a huge extension ratio) therefore “stretches” the phase space: many positions collapse into almost the same phase (aliasing / crowding). This does not blow up cos/sin numerically (they stay in $[-1,1]$), but it does change the relative phases that the model learned to rely on, which in turn makes query/key inner products $L_{ij}=q_i^\top k_j/\sqrt{d_k}$ have a distribution very different from training. The downstream effects are (a) attention patterns that are effectively random or overly diffuse (softmax inputs are out of the training distribution), (b) gradients and updates that behave poorly because the model’s implicit positional manifold has been distorted, and (c) fine‑tuning either fails to converge or needs many more steps.

What the paper measures instead of internal histograms. The authors show that naive interpolation or existing non‑uniform heuristics cause perplexity spikes and degraded passkey retrieval beyond modest extensions, and that their searched non‑uniform $\lambda_i$ and initial‑token threshold $\hat n$ significantly reduce perplexity and allow $8\times$ non‑fine‑tuned extension and progressive fine‑tuning up to 2048k. Those external metrics are the paper’s quantitative proxy for the “catastrophic values” problem. The paper does not include plots of attention‑logit histograms, layernorm mean/variance vs position, activation norms, or gradient‑norm traces.

If you want to measure/visualize these pathologies yourself (recommended diagnostics). The following statistics will expose the kinds of OOD effects discussed in the paper:

- Compute attention‑logit histograms per query position. For layer $\ell$, head $h$ and query position $i$ form the logits
  $$
  L_{ij}=\frac{q_{i}^{(\ell,h)\top} k_{j}^{(\ell,h)}}{\sqrt{d_k}}
  $$
  and plot the distribution of $L_{ij}$ across $j$ for $i$ in the trained range vs $i$ in the new (unseen) positions. Large shifts in mean/variance or multi‑modal structure indicate OOD behaviour.

- Plot softmax entropy per query:
  $$
  H(i)=-\sum_j p_{ij}\log p_{ij},\qquad p_{ij}=\mathrm{softmax}_j(L_{ij}).
  $$
  Increased entropy for far positions signals attention dispersion (crowding) while near‑zero entropy signals saturation (one token dominates).

- Track attention concentration (top‑k mass) versus position:
  $$
  M_{k}(i)=\sum_{j\in\text{top-}k(i)} p_{ij}.
  $$
  A fall in $M_k$ with position indicates attention is becoming uniformly spread.

- Measure layernorm pre‑activation statistics per token (mean and variance of the vector before LayerNorm) and activation norm $\|h^{(\ell)}_i\|$ as functions of position. Large systematic shifts indicate distributional mismatch.

- Compute per‑layer and per‑parameter gradient norms during a backward pass from a long sequence and compare for PI vs LongRoPE. Plot $\|\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{L}\|$ vs token‑position or step to see whether gradients vanish/spike.

- Quantify distribution shift with a divergence metric (e.g. KL or Wasserstein) between the training‑range logits histogram and the new‑position histogram.

How interpolation should (and in the paper does) reduce these effects. By choosing non‑uniform $\lambda_i$ and keeping the first $\hat n$ positions unrescaled, LongRoPE preserves higher‑importance frequencies and early token phases so that $\Delta\phi'_i$ is not uniformly tiny across all dimensions. Intuitively this keeps the Q/K relative phases in the “manifold” the model was trained on, so the distributions of $L_{ij}$, softmax entropies, and layer activation statistics stay much closer to in‑distribution. The paper’s quantitative evidence of that is the dramatically reduced perplexity spikes, stable fine‑tuning with far fewer steps, and high passkey‑retrieval accuracy up to million‑scale contexts.

Summary / practical note. The paper calls them “catastrophic values” to describe OOD positional encodings and the downstream distributional shift that makes fine‑tuning hard; it does not document internal histograms/gradient traces. If you want to prove the mechanism empirically, run the diagnostics above on a checkpoint pre‑ and post‑interpolation (PI, NTK, YaRN, LongRoPE) and you will typically see (i) a marked shift in $L_{ij}$ histograms and increased softmax entropy for naive PI at long positions, and (ii) LongRoPE restoring those distributions closer to the pretrained range — which matches the paper’s external metrics (perplexity and retrieval). If you’d like, I can sketch a minimal script (PyTorch pseudocode) that computes the attention‑logit histograms and entropies for a given model and set of positions.

### Why two-stage fine-tuning (128k then 256k) instead of one-stage 256k, and how sensitive is it?
Question:
For LLaMA2 you fine-tune 400 steps at 128k and then 600 at 256k, rather than training 1000 steps directly at 256k. Do you have ablations showing this two-stage approach converges better or reaches better minima? How sensitive are the results to the split, learning rate schedule, and the choice of starting from the 128k-optimized \(\lambda_i\)?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper uses the $128k\to256k$ two-stage because it makes the extension task much easier and more stable in practice — it reduces the per-stage “novel-position” shock the model must absorb, uses a better RoPE initialization for the harder step, and is therefore more efficient than trying to jump straight to $256k$. The paper reports this as a practical choice (and shows related indirect evidence), but it does not include a full numeric ablation that directly compares ($400+600$) to a single 1000-step $256k$ run across all metrics.

Why the two-stage recipe helps (intuitively and technically)
The core practical difficulty when you extend RoPE to a much larger context is that you suddenly introduce a huge number of previously unseen token positions, which produce “catastrophic” out‑of‑distribution positional values and make optimization unstable. Doing the extension progressively reduces the per-step extension ratio the model must adapt to, so fewer new positions are introduced at once and the model’s weights (and attention patterns) can adapt incrementally. In LongRoPE this is combined with a searched, non‑uniform rescale of RoPE dimensions (the per‑dimension $\lambda_i$) that preserves the most informative frequencies/positions; applying the $128k$‑optimized $\lambda_i$ before any gradient updates gives a much better initialization than naive PI/NTK and therefore reduces information loss and optimization difficulty for the next stage.

Concrete facts from the paper that support this
The paper describes the exact workflow: they search rescale factors for $128k$ and $256k$, fine‑tune 400 steps with the $128k$ rescale factors, then swap to the $256k$ rescale factors and continue for 600 steps. The authors state this “proves more efficient than directly fine‑tuning to $256k$” and note that attempting to fine‑tune under very large extension ratios is “challenging” (see the Appendix). Empirical signals in the paper also point to the advantage of smaller per‑stage extension: LLaMA2 LongRoPE-$2048k$ that used the $256k$ fine‑tune as the intermediate stage performs better than the version that only fine‑tuned at $128k$, and the authors explain that this is because the secondary extension ratio becomes smaller ($8\times$ vs $16\times$) when you fine‑tune at $256k$ — i.e., smaller jumps are easier to extend from.

What ablations the paper does and does not provide
The paper includes ablations that are directly relevant: (a) the “effectiveness of the second positional interpolation” shows that performing a second non‑uniform search on the fine‑tuned $256k$ model keeps perplexity low when extending to $512k/1024k/2048k$ compared to PI/YaRN; (b) the analysis of the two non‑uniformities shows how per‑dimension and per‑position choices matter (and that token‑position exemptions help at moderate lengths but are less impactful at the extreme end). However, the paper does not present a table that directly compares ($400@128k + 600@256k$) versus ($1000@256k$) with identical other hyperparameters. The claim that the progressive route is “more efficient” is therefore supported by practical experience and by the indirect empirical results above, but not by a systematic sweep over splits/step allocations/LR schedules in the main text.

Sensitivity to split, LR schedule, and starting from the $128k$ $\lambda_i$
The paper shows that these choices are model‑ and data‑sensitive. For example, LLaMA2 benefited from the $256k$ intermediate fine‑tune (smaller secondary jump) while Mistral did better with $128k$ in the experiments — the difference is attributed to the fine‑tuning setup (Mistral followed YaRN’s $16k$ training length and different LR). The authors list the hyperparameters they used: for LLaMA2 a learning rate of $2\mathrm{e}{-5}$ with linear decay, global batch size 32, 400 steps at $128k$ then 600 at $256k$ ($128k$ training used 8 A100s, $256k$ used 16 A100s); for Mistral they used a constant $1\mathrm{e}{-6}$ and batch 64 with 400 steps (per model) using a $16k$ training length. The differing hyperparameters and dataset/training-sequence-length choices are precisely why the optimal split and schedule can change across models.

In short: (i) initializing the first fine‑tune with the searched $128k$ $\lambda_i$ is helpful because it preserves key RoPE dimensions/positions and gives a far better starting point than naïve interpolation, (ii) the split matters because it determines the per‑stage extension ratio (smaller ratios are easier), and (iii) LR, batch size, and training sequence length interact with the split and with the model architecture — the paper reports reasonable defaults but does not present an exhaustive sensitivity sweep.

Practical takeaways if you want to reproduce or experiment
Treat the progressive strategy as a curriculum: pick an intermediate length so that each stage’s extension ratio is moderate (the paper used $128k$ then $256k$, which corresponds to manageable jumps in their setting). Use the searched non‑uniform $\lambda_i$ for the intermediate stage (this is a key stabilizer). Start with the paper’s reported LRs (LLaMA2: $2\mathrm{e}{-5}$ linear decay; Mistral: $1\mathrm{e}{-6}$ constant) and the same step counts to reproduce their results, then try small adjustments. If you have the budget, test directly comparing (A) single‑stage to (B) progressive with the same total steps and same LR to see which converges faster on your model/data; monitor perplexity on held‑out long segments and on original short windows (the paper also shows that you may need a short‑length recovery search after extreme extension).

Conclusion
The two‑stage procedure in LongRoPE is a pragmatic, stability‑and‑efficiency driven choice: it lowers the effective extension per tuning stage, leverages a better RoPE initialization, and empirically produces better results for the authors’ LLaMA2 experiments. The paper documents these choices and gives indirect empirical support (including comparisons between $ft=128k$ and $ft=256k$ final behaviors), but it does not include a full, systematic ablation that directly compares every possible split/LR/initialization permutation — so you should expect some model‑dependent tuning if you reproduce this on a different model or dataset.

### How should one switch between 8k-tuned and 2048k-tuned schedules during inference without artifacts?
Question:
You propose readjusting RoPE for short contexts and say that during inference, if the sequence length is less than 8k, you update RoPE with the short-length rescale factors. If a generation starts short and later exceeds 8k, should we switch \(\lambda_i\) mid-stream? Would that cause inconsistencies in QK dot products for already cached keys/values? What is the recommended implementation for streaming servers?
Answer:
Short answer: don’t flip the rescale schedule blindly in the middle of generation. Switching the per-dimension scale factors $\lambda_i$ while you keep previously cached keys (and their rotations) will break the $Q\cdot K$ geometry and produce artifacts. If you need to change schedules at runtime, either (a) pick the final schedule at session start and keep it fixed, or (b) make the cache rotatable (store the pre‑RoPE key projections or update cached keys in place by applying the delta rotation) so that all cached keys are represented under the same RoPE mapping that your new queries will use.

Why switching causes artifacts (intuition + maths). RoPE applies an orthogonal $2\times 2$ rotation to each complex pair of dimensions so a cached key at position $n$ typically looks like
$$
K_n^{\text{old}} = R\big(\phi^{\text{old}}_i(n)\big)\,k'_n,
$$
where $k'_n$ is the key projection before RoPE and $R(\phi)$ is the 2D rotation by angle $\phi$ (and $\phi^{\text{old}}_i(n)$ encodes the old $\lambda_i$ mapping). A newly computed query at time $t$ under a new schedule is
$$
Q_t^{\text{new}} = R\big(\phi^{\text{new}}_i(t)\big)\,q'_t.
$$
Their dot product becomes
$$
Q_t^{\text{new}}\cdot K_n^{\text{old}} = q'_t{}^{\!T}\,R\big(\phi^{\text{new}}_i(t)-\phi^{\text{old}}_i(n)\big)\,k'_n,
$$
but valid positional encoding relies on the relative phase difference being the intended function of $(t-n)$. If $\phi^{\text{new}}_i$ and $\phi^{\text{old}}_i$ come from different $\lambda$ schedules, $\phi^{\text{new}}_i(t)-\phi^{\text{old}}_i(n)$ is not the correct relative-angle and attention will interpret wrong distances — producing visible artifacts or broken generation. In short: Q and K must be in the same RoPE coordinate frame.

Practical, safe implementation choices for streaming servers. The paper says it “dynamically adjusts the corresponding RoPE rescale factors” for short vs long contexts, but it doesn’t spell out the streaming implementation. Here are safe options you can implement:

1) Fixed schedule for the session (simplest). Decide up‑front which $\{\lambda_i\}$ you will use for the whole generation session (for example the 2048k schedule if you might grow that large) and never switch. This is lowest-risk and cheapest at runtime.

2) Cache pre‑RoPE projections so you can switch on the fly. At encode time store $k'_n=W_k x_n$ (the key linear projection before applying RoPE) and store $v_n$ as usual. When attending, compute rotated keys on the fly with the currently active $\{\lambda_i\}$:
$$
K_n(\text{active}) = R\big(\phi^{\text{active}}_i(n)\big)\,k'_n.
$$
Because you stored $k'_n$ you can change $\phi$ later without recomputing $W_k x_n$. Memory cost is essentially the same as storing rotated keys; compute cost is the per-step rotation, which is cheap and vectorizable.

3) Update cached rotated keys in place via a delta rotation (if you only stored rotated keys). If you only have $K_n^{\text{old}} = R(\phi^{\text{old}}(n))k'_n$ cached, you can update it to the new frame cheaply by applying the rotation
$$
K_n^{\text{new}} = R\big(\Delta\phi_i(n)\big)\,K_n^{\text{old}},\quad \Delta\phi_i(n)=\phi^{\text{new}}_i(n)-\phi^{\text{old}}_i(n).
$$
On the 2‑D block $[x,y]$ this is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}x'\\ y'\end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix}\cos\Delta\phi & -\sin\Delta\phi\\[4pt]\sin\Delta\phi & \cos\Delta\phi\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}.
$$
This still touches every cached token (cost $O(\text{seq}\times\text{head\_dim})$), but avoids re-running the projection $W_k$ or the whole model.

Notes and tradeoffs. Values $V$ are not affected by RoPE (RoPE is applied to Q and K projections), so you don’t have to touch cached Vs. If your LongRoPE schedules are per-dimension ($\lambda_i$) and position-thresholded ($\hat n$) as in the paper, the delta rotations are dimension- and position-wise; implement them vectorized over sequence and RoPE pairs to be efficient. If you need minimal latency and expect many sessions that stay short, using the short schedule fixed is fine; if you must support arbitrary growth while keeping short‑context fidelity, caching raw projections (option 2) is the most flexible production design.

Bottom line. Yes — switching $\lambda_i$ mid-stream without re-encoding/re-rotating cached keys will produce QK inconsistencies and visible artifacts. The recommended server choices are to either choose and lock a schedule at session start, or store pre‑RoPE projections (or apply a delta rotation to cached rotated keys when switching) so that all cached keys are brought into the same RoPE frame before you continue generating.

### Are there theoretical limits from RoPE periodicity that constrain usable \(L'\) (phase aliasing)?
Question:
Since RoPE uses \(\cos(n\theta_i)\), each dimension has period \(T_i=2\pi/\theta_i\). As \(n\) grows to millions, low-frequency dimensions will wrap many times. Do you risk position collisions or aliasing that degrade uniqueness at \(L'=2{,}048\text{k}\)? How does LongRoPE’s non-uniform scaling affect these periods, and are there principled limits on the maximum safe context length?
Answer:
Short answer: There is no simple, hard-periodicity barrier that suddenly forbids a $2,048k$ context when you use RoPE — periodicity per se only creates a collision if every rotary dimension simultaneously “lines up” (i.e., all phases match modulo $2\pi$). Because RoPE uses many geometrically spaced frequencies and LongRoPE deliberately avoids compressing all of them at once, simultaneous phase-aliasing at the million-token scale is extremely unlikely in practice. The real limits are practical (loss of positional resolution / “crowding”, attention dispersion, and the model’s ability to learn new larger-phase patterns), and LongRoPE’s non‑uniform per-dimension scaling is exactly aimed at avoiding those failure modes.

Why: the math in one short paragraph. The paper writes RoPE angles as (simplified) $n\theta_i$ with $\theta_i=\beta^{-i}$ (where $\beta=\theta^{2/d}$ in their notation). The un-scaled period of dimension $i$ is $T_i=\dfrac{2\pi}{\theta_i}=2\pi\beta^{i}$. After LongRoPE’s per-dimension rescale factor $\lambda_i$ (the paper applies a multiplicative factor $1/\lambda_i$ to the angle), the effective angular frequency becomes $\theta_i'=\theta_i/\lambda_i$ and the period becomes
$$
T_i'=\frac{2\pi}{\theta_i'}=2\pi\,\lambda_i\,\beta^{i}.
$$
Two different token indices $n\neq m$ collide (exactly the same RoPE vector) only if for every dimension $i$
$$
(n-m)\,\theta_i'\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z},
$$
i.e. there exist integers $k_i$ with
$$
d:=n-m=\frac{2\pi k_i}{\theta_i'}=2\pi k_i\,\lambda_i\,\beta^{i}\qquad\text{for all }i.
$$
That last set of equalities is an integer Diophantine constraint: the values $k_i\lambda_i\beta^{i}$ must all be the same integer. For geometrically spaced $\beta^{i}$ and continuous/non‐aligned $\lambda_i$ this simultaneous integer equality is extremely unlikely for small $d$. In short: all dimensions must wrap together for a real collision — one dimension wrapping many times does not by itself cause a collision.

Intuition / why LongRoPE avoids aliasing. Think of RoPE as a bundle of clocks with different periods. A single fast clock can wrap many times, but you still read the joint state of many clocks to know the absolute time. LongRoPE’s search explicitly (i) preserves the high‑frequency (small‑$i$) channels by giving them smaller $\lambda_i$ (the paper enforces the monotonic constraint $\lambda_i\le\lambda_{i+1}$ and seeds the search with PI/NTK/YaRN), and (ii) protects the first $\hat n$ tokens by not rescaling them at all. Preserving some dimensions that still advance significantly per token guarantees local resolution (distinguishing neighboring tokens), while allowing lower‑frequency channels to be stretched so the model can “cover” very long ranges without catastrophic information loss. The paper’s empirical finding is exactly that: uniform downscaling (PI) crowds all channels and causes rapid degradation, while non‑uniform rescaling (LongRoPE search) prevents that and lets them reach $2048k$ with good perplexity and passkey retrieval.

A simple information‑theoretic sanity bound. A useful heuristic is to count how many distinct phase values a single dimension produces over $L'$ tokens: roughly
$$
M_i \approx 1+\left\lfloor\frac{L'\,\theta_i'}{2\pi}\right\rfloor,
$$
so an optimistic upper bound on distinct joint vectors is $\prod_i M_i$. A necessary (not tight) condition to be able to represent $L'$ distinct positions is therefore
$$
\sum_i \log_2 M_i \;\gtrsim\; \log_2 L'.
$$
If you scale every $\theta_i'$ down by a large uniform factor you force many $M_i$ toward 1 and violate this condition; that’s exactly the “crowding” failure PI exhibits. LongRoPE’s per‑dimension tuning keeps enough degrees of freedom (large enough $M_i$ in some dimensions) so the inequality can still plausibly hold for $L'=2{,}048\text{k}$.

Practical takeaway and limits. There isn’t a single RoPE‑periodicity number that says “stop at X tokens.” Rather, the limit depends on (a) number of rotary channels, (b) how aggressively you rescale them, (c) whether you protect initial tokens, and (d) whether you allow some fine‑tuning so the model can adapt. The paper shows empirically that with LongRoPE’s non‑uniform per‑dimension scaling, monotonic constraint, and a small amount of progressive fine‑tuning, you can safely reach $2,048k$ without catastrophic aliasing. Conversely, aggressive uniform scaling will induce practical aliasing/crowding long before that.

What to watch for in practice. Sudden spikes in perplexity or abrupt drop in synthetic retrieval (e.g. passkey) accuracy are good indicators of effective aliasing/crowding. If you must push farther than the paper’s recipe, either add more rotary channels (more dimensions), reduce uniform scaling by protecting more high‑frequency channels (smaller $\lambda_i$ for small $i$), or include additional fine‑tuning on suitably long sequences.

Summary: exact simultaneous phase collisions require very special integer relations across all dimensions and so are astronomically unlikely up to $2\text{M}$ for standard RoPE parameters. The practical failure mode is not a single‑dimension wrap but the loss of combined phase bandwidth when too many channels are uniformly compressed. LongRoPE’s non‑uniform, monotonic scaling + token‑protection is designed to avoid that and that is why the paper succeeds empirically at $2,048k$.
