\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

Why Million-Token Contexts Matter: Motivation and Problem Statement

# Use-cases that demand long context (in-context learning with many examples, tool-use/agents, long-document reasoning)

Large context windows are not a niche requirement: they unlock classes of applications that are difficult or impossible with the usual few-thousand-token limits. First, in-context learning often relies on putting examples, instructions, or structured data directly into the prompt so the model can infer a mapping without gradient updates. Some problems benefit from dozens or hundreds of demonstrations, long instruction sets, or many exemplar documents; these naturally consume tens or hundreds of thousands of tokens when the aim is robust generalization or wide coverage of rare cases. Extending the context window makes it possible to present many more examples and richer task scaffolding in a single interaction.

Second, modern LLM-based agents and tool-using systems accumulate state over long interactions. An agent may keep a history of observations, tool calls, external lookups, or long-form plans that must be consulted to make coherent next-step decisions. When an agent needs to recall early steps of a multi-stage plan, or to combine far-apart facts, a short context window forces lossy truncation or external memory mechanisms; a million-token window avoids or reduces that need.

Third, many real-world reasoning tasks operate over long documents: book-length summarization, legal opinions, scientific papers with extensive appendices, or multi-file codebases. Tasks such as global summarization, long-range cross-referencing, or locating a small piece of information (e.g., a passkey hidden somewhere in a book) require the model to see essentially the whole source material at once. Without a very large context window, systems must rely on lossy chunking, two-stage retrieval, or external index structures; such workarounds often undermine coherence and make some tasks (like a single-pass, global rewrite or reasoning across all chapters) impractical.

These three classes of use-cases—high-capacity in-context learning, agentic memory/tool usage, and long-document reasoning—explain why increasing context windows from a few thousand tokens to the million-token scale is not just an incremental improvement but a qualitative capability extension.

# Limits of pre-trained LLM context windows (e.g., LLaMA2’s 4k) and performance decay beyond the trained range

Most large language models are trained and released with context windows of a few thousand tokens. For example, LLaMA2 is commonly used with a $4\text{k}$ ($4096$ token) limit. That training range matters: the model’s positional encoding and the statistics the model learns about how to use positions are calibrated to the token indices it has seen during pretraining. When you feed tokens whose position indices lie far outside the training range, those positions are effectively out-of-distribution.

The consequence is empirical performance decay as you push beyond the trained window. New position indices (positions the model never saw during pretraining) can introduce what the paper calls “catastrophic values”: signals and rotary angles that the model’s layers treat in ways that were not learned during pretraining, producing high loss or failed generation. This is particularly stark in extreme extensions: going from $4\text{k}$ to more than $1000\text{k}$ (i.e., over a million tokens) introduces a vast number of entirely new positions—more than $90\%$ of the positions in the longer context can be new—so the model faces a highly out-of-distribution input regime.

Because of this mismatch, naive attempts to feed an LLM much longer contexts typically lead to degraded likelihoods, broken in-context behavior, and unstable fine-tuning. That is why extending context windows is not a trivial engineering tweak: it is a distributional-shift problem tied directly to how positional information is encoded and consumed by the model.

# Three core obstacles: catastrophic new positions (out-of-distribution), scarcity/cost of long-text fine-tuning, attention dispersion degrading short-context performance

Achieving reliable million-token contexts requires overcoming three interlocking obstacles.

1) Catastrophic new positions (out-of-distribution). As noted above, extending the token index range produces many position values the model was never trained on. The model’s positional encoding (in the case of many modern LLMs, Rotary Positional Embedding, RoPE) encodes position via rotation angles. New positions map to new angles and new patterns of interactions inside attention. If those angles fall into regions the model has not learned to handle, the result can be catastrophic spikes in loss and failure to learn a stable behavior via further fine-tuning. This OOD problem makes straightforward extension unstable, especially when the extension ratio is large.

2) Scarcity and cost of long-text fine-tuning. A clean solution would be to fine-tune the model on training sequences as long as the desired context window. In practice this runs into two problems. First, sufficiently long contiguous training data are scarce: there are far fewer reliable sources that give many-hundred-thousand or million-token contiguous documents. Second, even if such data exist, training on extremely long sequences multiplies both memory and compute costs. The training time, GPU memory footprint, and engineering complexity grow quickly with sequence length, making direct fine-tuning to million-token windows costly and often infeasible for all but the largest operations.

3) Attention dispersion and short-context degradation. When the context window grows by orders of magnitude, the attention mechanism must distribute its finite representational resources across many more positions. Two interrelated effects occur. One is that attention scores for any given token can become more diffuse, making it harder for the model to concentrate on the few truly relevant positions. The other is that positional interpolation strategies that compress or rescale angles to make new positions numerically “fit” into a pretrained angular range tend to crowd the angular representation of the original short-range positions into a narrower region. That crowding reduces the model’s ability to discriminate nearby tokens in the original short window, degrading performance on tasks designed for the original context length. Thus, naive extension can give up existing short-range capability in exchange for longer reach—an unacceptable trade-off for most applications.

These three obstacles interact: catastrophic OOD positions make fine-tuning unstable, the scarcity and cost of long training data make brute-force fine-tuning impractical, and any naive intervention that reduces OOD risk (for example, uniform interpolation) risks creating attention crowding and short-context loss of fidelity. A practical method must address all three concerns together.

# What LongRoPE achieves: 2048k window, only ~1k fine-tuning steps within 256k, preserved short-context quality

LongRoPE is designed to answer the questions raised by these obstacles. Its headline achievements are concrete and intentionally practical:

- It extends RoPE-based LLMs to a context window of $2048\text{k}$ (i.e., $2{,}048{,}000$ tokens). This is an unprecedented order-of-magnitude growth compared to typical released windows.

- It does so while keeping additional fine-tuning requirements extremely modest. The progressive strategy described in the paper reaches the $2048\text{k}$ target using at most on the order of $1{,}000$ fine-tuning steps, and those fine-tuning steps are run with training lengths bounded by $256\text{k}$. In other words, LongRoPE avoids having to train on million-token sequences directly; instead it uses small, efficient fine-tuning stages plus targeted interpolation searches.

- It preserves performance on the original short context window. LongRoPE includes mechanisms to recover and maintain the model’s behavior on $4\text{k}$–scale tasks (and nearby short ranges) despite the enormous extension. That is important in practice because most downstream tasks and benchmark evaluations still assume short-context behavior; any extension that ruins that baseline capability would be of limited use.

Operationally, LongRoPE achieves this without changing the LLM’s core architecture: it keeps the model weights and layers intact and only modifies how positional information is presented (through carefully chosen rescale factors applied to RoPE). That makes the method lightweight: existing inference optimizations and deployment patterns for RoPE models remain applicable.

# High-level overview of LongRoPE’s three pillars: non-uniform interpolation search, progressive extension, short-context recovery

LongRoPE rests on three interlocking pillars. Each addresses one or more of the obstacles described above, and together they form a practical recipe for large extensions.

Pillar 1 — Non-uniform positional interpolation via targeted search. The key insight is that positional interpolation need not be uniform across all frequency dimensions of RoPE nor across all token positions. Empirically the paper finds two forms of non-uniformity: different RoPE dimensions should be rescaled by different amounts, and the earliest token positions in a sequence often benefit from much less (or no) interpolation. Concretely, LongRoPE searches for a per-dimension set of rescale factors $\{\lambda_i\}$ and a token-threshold $\hat{n}$ so that positions $n<\hat{n}$ use the original RoPE angles while positions $n\ge\hat{n}$ use rescaled angles. The search is cast as an optimization over these discrete choices, guided by held-out perplexity on long samples. Because the full combinatorial space is enormous, LongRoPE uses an evolutionary search with two practical accelerations: (i) the initial population is seeded with known reasonable baselines (uniform linear interpolation and previously proposed non-uniform heuristics), and (ii) a monotonicity constraint is enforced so that $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ across RoPE dimensions (this both matches the NTK intuition that low-dimension/high-frequency components should be changed less and dramatically reduces search waste). The result of this pillar is a much better initialization for the model when moving to longer windows: careful, non-uniform interpolation preserves the critical dimensions and initial positions that carry fine-grained positional information. Practically, this approach by itself enables up to a $8\times$ extension without any fine-tuning.

Pillar 2 — Progressive extension to $2048\text{k}$. The paper avoids direct fine-tuning all the way to $2048\text{k}$ (which would require rare long-doc data and massive compute). Instead, LongRoPE proceeds in stages. First, it applies the non-uniform search on the pre-trained model for intermediate targets (e.g., $128\text{k}$ and $256\text{k}$) and fine-tunes at those intermediate lengths. Empirically the efficient schedule used in the paper fine-tunes for a small number of steps (for example, a 400-step stage using the $128\text{k}$ rescale factors followed by a 600-step stage using the $256\text{k}$ rescale factors) rather than attempting a single long fine-tuning at the final huge length. After producing a stable, fine-tuned $256\text{k}$ model, LongRoPE performs a second non-uniform search on that already-extended model to discover rescale factors that map the $256\text{k}$ model to a $2048\text{k}$ window. Because the fine-tuned model already knows how to interpret a wider range of positional angles, the second search can produce a $2048\text{k}$ extension without further large-scale fine-tuning. Overall this progressive strategy turns a $512\times$ final extension (from $4\text{k}$ to $2048\text{k}$) into a sequence of tractable steps, each requiring modest data and compute.

Pillar 3 — Short-context recovery. Extremely large extensions tend to compress the angle space that corresponds to short token indices, harming discriminability on tasks designed for the original window. To prevent that, LongRoPE performs an additional, targeted readjustment: it runs a constrained search to find rescale factors that are tuned for short lengths (e.g., $4\text{k}$ or $8\text{k}$). The search reduces the maximum allowed $\lambda$ values (encouraging less interpolation) for those short ranges and stores these factors for runtime use. At inference time the model can dynamically select the appropriate RoPE rescale profile: when the sequence length is short (below a threshold such as $8\text{k}$), it applies the short-range profile; otherwise it uses the rescale factors appropriate for the long-range setting. This dynamic adjustment recovers much of the original short-context performance while keeping the ability to operate reliably on million-token inputs.

Taken together, these three pillars address the three core obstacles: non-uniform search reduces catastrophic OOD positional effects by preserving crucial angles and early tokens; progressive extension avoids needing scarce million-token training data and large compute by fine-tuning only at intermediate lengths; and short-context recovery mitigates attention-crowding and preserves the model’s original short-window strengths.

In sum, the problem statement that LongRoPE answers is: how to extend a RoPE-based pretrained LLM from its original short context length $L$ to a very large target $L'$ (for example, from $4\text{k}$ to $2048\text{k}$) while (a) avoiding catastrophic OOD behavior caused by new positions, (b) keeping fine-tuning data and compute feasible, and (c) preserving or recovering the model’s short-context performance. LongRoPE’s combination of targeted non-uniform interpolation, evolutionary search, a progressive fine-tuning schedule, and a short-range recovery step provides an effective, practical solution to that problem, enabling million-token contexts without wholesale architectural changes.

Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE): Foundations

# How Transformers use positional information; RoPE vs. absolute/learned encodings

Transformers compute attention using pairwise interactions among token representations, and by construction those interactions are permutation-invariant. To recover sequence order information the model must be given explicit positional cues. In practice this is done by augmenting token representations with position-dependent information; that general idea is the starting point for all positional encoding schemes. The LongRoPE paper frames this general requirement and then focuses on one particular family of encodings that has become common in modern large language models: rotary positional embeddings (RoPE).

RoPE differs from other position-encoding choices in that it does not insert a separate learned vector per absolute index in the same way a learned absolute embedding would. Instead, RoPE encodes position by modifying (rotating) components of the token representation using sinusoidal functions of a position-dependent angle. This rotation-based construction produces a compact, deterministic representation across dimensions and positions, and it is the specific positional mechanism studied throughout the paper. All derivations and scaling manipulations that follow apply to RoPE as the paper defines it.

# RoPE mechanics: rotary angles, frequency schedule across dimensions, base $\theta=10000$, $\beta=\theta^{2/d}$

RoPE represents the position $n$ of a token via pairs of sine and cosine components applied across the embedding dimensions. If the model embedding dimension is $d$, RoPE produces the following structured vector of length $d$ (grouped into $d/2$ cosine–sine pairs):

$
\left[\cos(n\theta_0),\; \sin(n\theta_0),\; \cos(n\theta_1),\; \sin(n\theta_1),\; \ldots,\; \cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}),\; \sin(n\theta_{d/2-1})\right].
$

Here $n$ is the token position index and each $\theta_i$ is a rotation frequency associated with the $i$‑th cosine–sine pair. The RoPE frequency schedule is defined so that the frequencies decrease across dimension index $i$ according to a geometric law:

$
\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}.
$

In the common default parameterization used by the models in the paper the base value $\theta$ is $10000$. It is convenient to rewrite the per-dimension frequency using the auxiliary constant $\beta$ defined by

$
\beta = \theta^{2/d},
$

so that

$
\theta_i = \beta^{-i},
$

and the rotation angle that appears in the trigonometric functions is

$
\text{angle}_{i}(n) = n \theta_i = \frac{n}{\beta^{i}}.
$

Two points are important about this construction. First, different pairs of dimensions operate at different effective frequencies: as $i$ increases, $\beta^{i}$ grows and $\theta_i$ shrinks, so higher-index pairs encode lower temporal frequency (coarser positional structure) while lower-index pairs encode higher temporal frequency (finer positional differences). Second, the cosine–sine pairs are a periodic parameterization of angle; the embedding for position $n$ is determined by the angle $n\theta_i$ modulo the $2\pi$ cycle of cosine and sine. This per-dimension angular representation is the lever we use when changing the model’s effective context window.

# Extension ratio $s = L'/L$ and its effect on rotary angles

When we attempt to extend a pretrained model’s context window from an original maximum length $L$ to a new target maximum length $L'$, it is convenient to quantify the change as the extension ratio

$
s = \frac{L'}{L}.
$

A common practical approach to make angles for the new, larger positions fall within the range that the pretrained RoPE parameters saw during training is to rescale the rotary angles by a multiplicative factor. In the paper this rescaling is expressed dimension-wise (and, optionally, position-wise) as an operator that multiplies the original angle $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$ by a factor $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})$. Specifically, for a chosen per-dimension scale $\lambda_i$ and a token-index threshold $\hat{n}$, the RoPE encoding used at token position $n$ is written as

$
\text{RoPE}_n \;=\; \big[\ldots,\; \cos\!\big(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\times\frac{n}{\beta^i}\big),\; \sin\!\big(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\times\frac{n}{\beta^i}\big),\ldots\big],
$

with the indicator factor

$
\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n}) =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } n < \hat{n},\\[4pt]
\frac{1}{\lambda_i} & \text{if } n \ge \hat{n}.
\end{cases}
$

Thus for positions beyond the threshold $\hat{n}$ the rotation angles are scaled down by $\lambda_i$ (i.e., replaced by $(1/\lambda_i)\cdot n/\beta^i$). The common, simplest choice—used by linear positional interpolation (PI) in the paper—is to take the same scalar for every dimension, $\lambda_i = s$, so every rotation angle is reduced uniformly by the extension ratio. In that case each angle becomes

$
\text{angle}_i'(n) = \frac{1}{s}\cdot\frac{n}{\beta^i}.
$

A useful immediate observation follows by looking at the per-dimension angular increment between adjacent token positions. For dimension $i$ and positions $n$ and $n+1$ (when the rescaling applies), the angular increment is

$
\Delta\phi_i \;=\; \text{angle}_i'(n+1) - \text{angle}_i'(n) \;=\; \frac{1}{\lambda_i\,\beta^i}.
$

This simple expression makes explicit why changes in $\lambda_i$ matter: increasing $\lambda_i$ shrinks the per-token angular step in that dimension, and decreasing $\lambda_i$ increases it.

# Interpolation vs. extrapolation intuition and the ‘crowding’ problem when compressing many positions into trained ranges

Two broad strategies appear in the RoPE literature (and in the paper’s discussion) for dealing with new token positions beyond the pretrained maximum: interpolation and extrapolation.

- Interpolation (as the paper calls positional interpolation or PI) rescales the rotary angles so that the large new position indices are mapped back into the angular range the model was trained on. Concretely, with interpolation and $\lambda_i>1$ the new angles are smaller than the linear continuation $n/\beta^i$ would be; the model is effectively asked to reuse patterns learned for smaller angles to handle larger positions.

- Extrapolation means avoiding such downscaling and keeping angles at or closer to their original schedule (in the paper’s practical characterization, extrapolation corresponds to $\lambda_i=1$, i.e., not applying interpolation for that dimension or region of the sequence). Extrapolation trusts the model to generalize outside the angular range seen during pretraining.

Interpolation has an intuitive appeal because it prevents producing entirely out-of-distribution angular values when $n$ becomes much larger than during pretraining. But it brings a fundamental trade-off: compressing a long span of new token indices into the smaller angular bandwidth the model has seen makes many distinct positions end up with very similar cosine–sine pairs, especially in dimensions where the per-token angular increment becomes tiny. This is the “crowding” problem emphasized in the paper. Formally, because

$
\Delta\phi_i = \frac{1}{\lambda_i \beta^i},
$

a large interpolation factor $\lambda_i$ (for example $\lambda_i = s$ in PI when $s$ is large) causes $\Delta\phi_i$ to be small across many dimensions. Tokens that were previously separated by meaningful angular differences now have nearly identical angles, so the positional encoding loses its capacity to discriminate nearby indices.

The paper reports (and uses as motivation) the empirical consequence of crowding: when many new positions are compressed into the pretrained angular range, model performance degrades unless fine-tuning is used to adapt weights to the new pattern of encoded positions. PI therefore tends to underperform at very large extension ratios. To mitigate the crowding effect, the paper discusses and builds on prior approaches that use non-uniform scaling across RoPE dimensions:

- NTK-inspired methods distribute the interpolation pressure non-uniformly across dimensions so that high-frequency (lower-index) dimensions are scaled less and low-frequency (higher-index) dimensions are scaled more, preserving fine-grained local distinctions while compressing only the coarser dimensions.

- YaRN groups the RoPE dimensions into frequency bands and applies different strategies per band (extrapolation for the highest frequencies, NTK-like scaling for middle bands, and PI for the lowest frequencies).

These non-uniform strategies reduce crowding of the most information-bearing dimensions. The LongRoPE paper shows that further gains come from searching for per-dimension rescale factors (and per-position thresholds) rather than relying on fixed formulas or fixed groupings, because the best non-uniformity depends on both model and target extension length.

# Impact of positional scaling on attention and token distinguishability

To understand how RoPE scaling affects attention, it is enough to remember that attention weights are computed from dot products between token representations (queries and keys). RoPE injects positional information via the cosine–sine pairs that modulate those representations; therefore changes in the rotation angles alter the relative geometry of token vectors and thus the pattern of attention weights. The key effect of aggressive interpolation is that positional differences produce much smaller changes in the trigonometric components: if the per-token angular increment $\Delta\phi_i$ becomes tiny in the most informative dimensions, then the rotated representations of adjacent tokens look nearly identical across those dimensions. Consequently, the model’s attention mechanism cannot sharply distinguish nearby positions; attention becomes more diffuse or “dispersed” over many tokens rather than sharply focused on a few relevant neighbors.

This dispersion has two practical consequences emphasized in the paper. First, it degrades the model’s ability to exploit local context, raising language-model perplexity and causing failures on tasks that require fine-grained positional discrimination (for example, distinguishing neighboring tokens or retrieving a short passkey buried in long irrelevant text). Second, it can harm performance even within the model’s original short context window: when the model is globally reconfigured to handle very large $L'$, the compression of angles can force the original-range positions into a narrower angular subspace, making the model worse at tasks it previously handled well at short context lengths. The paper documents such short-context degradation and proposes a remedy: after producing a very large extension, perform an additional adjustment or a separate search of rescale factors targeted at short lengths so that the model can dynamically use a less-compressed RoPE for short inputs.

The paper also highlights a second positional non-uniformity: early tokens in an input sequence often attract high attention and are therefore particularly important to keep distinguishable. Empirically they find that leaving the first $\hat{n}$ tokens unrescaled (i.e., using $\mathbb{I}=1$ for those positions) improves performance, and the optimal choice of $\hat{n}$ depends on the target extension. This observation is consistent with the idea that the model relies on a small prefix of highly attended tokens whose relative ordering must be preserved with high fidelity.

Putting the quantitative and qualitative pieces together, the central engineering lesson of the paper is that scaling RoPE for longer contexts should not be applied uniformly. Instead:

- preserve higher-frequency (lower-index) RoPE dimensions by applying smaller $\lambda_i$ there so local distinctions remain sharp;

- consider not rescaling the earliest positions (a threshold $\hat{n}$) to maintain strong local structure where the model relies on it most;

- and treat per-dimension and per-position scaling as a joint design (or search) problem to avoid unnecessary crowding while still keeping newly encountered angles within ranges that the pretrained weights can handle.

These are exactly the principles LongRoPE operationalizes: non-uniform, per-dimension rescale factors $\lambda_i$, an optional untouched initial prefix $\hat{n}$, and monotonicity constraints on $\lambda_i$ (so that $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$) that enforce the intuition of preserving high-frequency dimensions.

# Summary

RoPE encodes position by applying dimension-specific rotation angles $n\theta_i$ realized as cosine–sine pairs. The frequency schedule across dimensions ($\theta_i = \beta^{-i}$ with $\beta=\theta^{2/d}$ and default $\theta=10000$) gives each pair a different sensitivity to positional increments. When extending the model’s context window from $L$ to $L'$ (ratio $s=L'/L$), rescaling the rotation angles is a straightforward way to keep new positions within a familiar angular regime; however, uniform rescaling compresses per-token angular steps, producing a crowding effect that reduces token distinguishability and spreads attention. Non-uniform strategies—scaling dimensions differently and optionally protecting an initial prefix—preserve the most informative dimensions and positions, mitigating crowding and providing a better starting point for either direct inference at larger contexts or for light fine-tuning. LongRoPE builds on these foundations by searching for per-dimension and per-position rescale factors that balance the trade-offs described above.

Prior Context-Extension Techniques via Positional Scaling

Transformers do not have an inherent representation of token order, so modern large language models (LLMs) rely on positional encodings. A widely used choice is Rotary Positional Encoding (RoPE), which encodes position $n$ into a sequence of rotated sine/cosine features across $d$ embedding coordinates. In the simplified form used throughout the paper, RoPE for a token at position $n$ is written as

\[
\left[\cos(n\theta_0),\; \sin(n\theta_0),\; \cos(n\theta_1),\; \sin(n\theta_1),\; \ldots,\; \cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}),\; \sin(n\theta_{d/2-1})\right],
\]

where $d$ is the embedding dimension and each rotary angle is $n\theta_i$ with $\theta_i$ derived from a base frequency (the paper uses the common parameterization $\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}$ with a default base $\theta=10000$). When we attempt to use a pretrained LLM on sequences longer than it was trained for, new position indices produce angles the model has never seen, which can lead to large distribution shifts and degraded behavior. Position interpolation methods address this by rescaling rotation angles for new positions so that their angles lie within a range closer to the pretrained regime.

A compact way to describe positional rescaling is to introduce per-dimension rescale factors. One convenient unified form (used in the paper) is

\[
\text{RoPE}(n) = \biggl[\; \cos\!\bigl(\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_0,\hat n)\cdot \tfrac{n}{\beta^0}\bigr),\; \sin\!\bigl(\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_0,\hat n)\cdot \tfrac{n}{\beta^0}\bigr),\; \ldots,\; \cos\!\bigl(\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_{d/2-1},\hat n)\cdot \tfrac{n}{\beta^{d/2-1}}\bigr),\; \sin\!\bigl(\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_{d/2-1},\hat n)\cdot \tfrac{n}{\beta^{d/2-1}}\bigr)\; \biggr],
\]

with the piecewise factor

\[
\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n) =
\begin{cases}
1 & n < \hat n,\\[4pt]
1/\lambda_i & n \ge \hat n,
\end{cases}
\]

where $\lambda_i$ (or equivalently $\hat\lambda_i=1/\lambda_i$) controls how much we downscale rotation angles for the $i$‑th RoPE dimension, $\hat n$ can control whether the earliest token positions are left unchanged, and $\beta$ is another constant derived from the RoPE base. The extension ratio $s$ is the target context length divided by the original trained length, $s = L'/L$. The different prior methods instantiate different choices for the $\lambda_i$ (and typically take $\hat n=0$).

Below we review the main prior approaches to positional scaling that the paper surveys: Linear Position Interpolation (PI), NTK and dynamic NTK ideas, and YaRN. For each method we give the mechanism (how the $\lambda_i$ are chosen), the intuition for why it helps, and the practical limitations that motivate more expressive treatments.

# Linear Position Interpolation (PI)

Linear Position Interpolation (often abbreviated PI) is the simplest and most direct approach. If you want to extend a model's context from length $L$ to length $L'$, PI rescales every rotation angle uniformly by the extension ratio. In the unified formulation this corresponds to setting

\[
\lambda_i = s \quad\text{for all RoPE dimensions } i,
\]

and typically $\hat n=0$ so every position is rescaled. Concretely, PI replaces angles $n\theta_i$ with approximately $(n/s)\theta_i$ (or equivalently multiplies the angle argument by $1/s$), mapping the larger position indices back into the numeric range seen during pretraining.

Why this is attractive: it is conceptually and implementationally simple. All new positions are mapped into the pretrained angular range, so the model sees inputs that are numerically closer to its training distribution. The method serves as a straightforward initialization for subsequent fine‑tuning on longer sequences.

Why it fails at large extension ratios (intuition and math). Consider the angular difference between adjacent positions in a single RoPE frequency: originally that local phase step is about $\Delta = \theta_i$. After PI with scale $s$ the step becomes $\Delta' = \theta_i / s$. Thus uniformly scaling by a large $s$ compresses angular differences between neighboring tokens. When angles for many adjacent positions all lie close together in angle space, the positional encodings become "crowded" — the model loses the fine-grained capacity to distinguish nearby positions. This crowding makes local ordering signals weaker, harming attention's ability to focus on relative offsets. Empirically, PI tends to work if followed by fine-tuning on long sequences, but its performance degrades as $s$ grows large without such adaptation. The paper reports that PI commonly needs fine-tuning to be usable for large extensions and tends to underperform at high extension ratios because of this crowding.

# NTK and Dynamic NTK

The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) viewpoint motivates a more nuanced, frequency-aware interpolation. The NTK perspective treats RoPE-like features as a family of Fourier-type bases whose contributions to the model's representation vary by frequency. Low-index RoPE dimensions (those with higher rotation frequency) encode fine-grained, short-range positional distinctions; high-index dimensions (lower frequency) encode coarser, longer-range structure.

Mechanism. NTK-based approaches therefore propose unequal scaling across dimensions: scale the higher-frequency (lower-index) dimensions less and allow more aggressive scaling (even interpolation) on lower-frequency dimensions. A stylized way to capture this is to make $\lambda_i$ depend on the dimension index $i$ so that $\lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \cdots$, i.e., a monotonic, dimension-dependent rescale schedule. The paper describes formulations where the effective per-dimension scale varies (a simple illustrative formula reported in the paper takes $\lambda$ to follow a power of $s$ across dimensions). Dynamic NTK further adapts the per-dimension (and sometimes per-position) rescaling based on the current sequence length, allowing the interpolation strategy to change as the sequence grows.

Why this helps. By preserving more of the high-frequency dimensions' angles (less scaling), the model retains the fine-grained positional distinctions that are crucial to discriminate nearby tokens. Letting low-frequency components absorb more of the rescaling provides room for representing longer ranges. This mixed strategy mitigates the crowding problem that uniform PI produces, so in many cases NTK-style methods can extend context length without immediate fine‑tuning.

Practical outcome and limits. NTK-aware rescaling often enables modest non-finetuned extensions and empirically can work up to a few times the original length. The paper notes that NTK-style methods can extend context windows in non-fine-tuning scenarios, but are typically effective only up to roughly a 4× extension; beyond that, performance still degrades and fine-tuning tends to be necessary. NTK therefore captures an important dimension-wise non-uniformity, but it is still a relatively constrained analytic prescription; it uses a fixed functional form derived from theory rather than directly optimizing the per-dimension behavior for a given pretrained model and target length.

# YaRN

YaRN (short for a frequency-aware scheme introduced in prior work and discussed in the paper) takes a pragmatic, hybrid route between PI and NTK. Instead of applying a smoothly varying per-dimension function, YaRN partitions RoPE dimensions into three frequency-based groups and treats each group differently:

- High-frequency group: perform extrapolation (i.e., effectively set $\lambda \approx 1$, leaving those dimensions unchanged). The reasoning is that these dimensions are already encoding short-range detail and should not be compressed.
- Middle-frequency group: apply an NTK-like unequal interpolation/extrapolation scheme.
- Low-frequency group: apply linear interpolation (PI-like scaling), since these components are the ones intended to represent longer-range information.

Why this helps. The multi-group design gives flexibility: high-frequency channels preserve fine-grained position signals, low-frequency channels expand to cover distant positions, and the middle group mediates between them. In many practical settings YaRN provides a stronger baseline than simple PI, because it explicitly recognizes that not all RoPE dimensions should be treated the same.

Limitations. The key limitation of YaRN — and what the paper emphasizes — is that the grouping boundaries and thresholds are human-designed empirical choices. Different pretrained models (different layer/frequency allocations, different training recipes) can have different internal information distributions, so a fixed grouping may be suboptimal. The paper provides empirical evidence that YaRN can underperform in some settings: for example, in their PG19 experiments YaRN did worse than PI and NTK for non-fine-tuned extensions because it failed to reach the effective target context behavior in that regime (they observed perplexity spikes near the target length in some cases). In short, YaRN is more expressive than PI but still constrained by heuristic groupings rather than model-specific optimization.

# Where these methods fall short: missing the full non-uniformity across dimensions and token positions

Although PI, NTK and YaRN progressively increase in sophistication, they share common limitations that the paper highlights. Understanding these limitations helps explain why methods that search for per-dimension and per-position rescale factors can perform better.

1. Partial treatment of per-dimension heterogeneity. NTK and YaRN recognize that different RoPE dimensions carry different kinds of positional information, but they do so via fixed analytic formulas or coarse grouping. Real pretrained LLMs can exhibit more intricate, model‑specific distributions of information across RoPE dimensions: which dimensions are most important, and how much each can be safely compressed, vary with architecture and training. Coarse prescriptions risk over‑compressing dimensions that encode crucial local structure or under‑compressing dimensions that could have safely supported greater extension. The paper’s empirical searches show that allowing a specialized rescale factor per RoPE dimension (rather than a single global scale or three coarse groups) can substantially reduce perplexity for extended contexts.

2. Ignoring token‑position non‑uniformity. The prior methods typically treat all token positions the same (equivalently $\hat n=0$ in the unified formulation). But the paper observes that early positions in a sequence are often attended to more heavily or carry especially important signals, so they should be interpolated less (or even left unchanged). Leaving the first $\hat n$ tokens unscaled preserves the original pretrained behavior for the most attended locations. Existing analytic methods do not incorporate this positional thresholding; the empirical results in the paper show that allowing a token-position threshold (i.e., different treatment for initial tokens versus later ones) improves performance, and that the optimal threshold depends on the target extension length.

3. Fixed heuristics versus model‑specific optimization. PI, NTK, and YaRN are built from theory and human intuition. They provide useful starting points but are not adapted to the idiosyncrasies of a particular pretrained checkpoint, corpora, or target stretching factor. The paper shows that an automated search over per-dimension rescale factors (and optionally a nonzero $\hat n$ for preserving initial tokens) can discover non-uniform rescalings that outperform these heuristics, both with and without fine‑tuning.

4. Practical obstacles beyond interpolation form. Even with a good interpolation rule, the broader challenges remain: a very large fraction of positions will be “new” after a huge extension (for example, extending from 4k to >1000k introduces over 90% new positions), which creates a significant distribution shift and can produce catastrophic values; fine‑tuning to adapt to those positions requires very long documents and substantial compute, which are often scarce or expensive; and aggressive interpolation can "spread" attention over so many positions that performance on the original short context degrades. PI, NTK and YaRN each mitigate some of these issues but do not eliminate them entirely.

Summary. Linear PI is simple but creates crowding and requires fine‑tuning for large $s$. NTK-style methods respect frequency differences across RoPE dimensions and can extend without fine‑tuning up to modest factors (the paper cites roughly a 4× practical limit), but they rely on analytic forms that may not match every model. YaRN blends extrapolation, NTK-like scaling, and PI by grouping dimensions, which works well in many cases but depends on heuristic group boundaries and can be suboptimal for new models or target lengths. Crucially, none of these prior approaches fully exploit the two distinct sources of non‑uniformity identified in the paper: fine-grained, per-dimension variation and the differing importance of early versus later token positions. That gap motivates methods that search or optimize per-dimension and per-position rescale factors to provide better initializations for fine‑tuning and to enable much larger extensions with less degradation.

Empirical Non-Uniformities in Positional Interpolation

# Search-driven discovery using LLaMA2-7B with perplexity on PG19/Proof-pile as fitness

Transformer language models require explicit positional encodings to represent token order. A widely used choice in modern LLMs is Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE). For a token at position $n$ and embedding dimension $d$, the RoPE encoding can be viewed (component-wise) as
\[
\left[\cos(n\theta_0),\ \sin(n\theta_0),\ \cos(n\theta_1),\ \sin(n\theta_1),\ \ldots,\ \cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}),\ \sin(n\theta_{d/2-1})\right],
\]
where the per-dimension rotation frequencies follow $\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}$ (the default base $\theta$ is 10000 in common implementations). When we extend the model's usable context from $L$ to $L'$, a common family of approaches rescales the effective rotation angles so that positions beyond the trained range map back into the pretrained angular range. If $s=L'/L$ denotes the extension ratio, many methods apply a global or structured rescaling that can be written in a unified form as
\[
\left[\cos\!\big(\tfrac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^0}\big),\ \sin\!\big(\tfrac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^0}\big),\ \cos\!\big(\tfrac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^1}\big),\ \ldots\right],
\]
with $\beta$ related to the base frequency and $\lambda$ a rescale parameter (or a family of them). Linear positional interpolation (PI) uses a single $\lambda=s$ for all dimensions; NTK-based and YaRN approaches introduce dimension-dependent or grouped rescaling patterns.

The work behind LongRoPE frames the design of these rescale factors as a search problem and drives the search with a concrete fitness metric: next-token prediction loss (perplexity) on long-document validation samples. Concretely, the search optimizes a per-RoPE-dimension rescale $\lambda_i$ together with a token-position threshold $\hat{n}$ that lets the earliest $\hat{n}$ tokens keep their original RoPE (no rescaling). The RoPE angle used for dimension $i$ and token position $n$ is therefore
\[
\text{angle}_{i}(n) \;=\; \mathbb{I}(\lambda_i,\hat{n}) \cdot \frac{n}{\beta^i},
\qquad
\mathbb{I}(\lambda_i,\hat{n}) \;=\;
\begin{cases}
1 & n < \hat{n},\\[4pt]
\dfrac{1}{\lambda_i} & n \ge \hat{n},
\end{cases}
\]
and the objective is to choose $\{\lambda_i\}_{i=0}^{d/2-1}$ and $\hat{n}$ to minimize perplexity on long sequences:
\[
\underset{\{\lambda_i\},\hat{n}}{\operatorname{arg\,min}}\ \mathcal{L}\big(\text{LLM}(\text{RoPE}_{\lambda,\hat{n}}, \mathbf{X})\big)
\quad\text{for }\mathbf{X}\text{ of length } \ge L'.
\]

Because this search space is enormous (each $\lambda_i$ may take many values and $\hat{n}$ has several choices), LongRoPE employs an evolutionary search with practical optimizations. The search space in experiments permits each $\lambda_i$ to vary from $1.0$ (full extrapolation) up to $s\times 1.25$ (slightly more aggressive interpolation than PI) at a resolution of $0.01$, and $\hat{n}$ is chosen from a discrete set $\{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}$. The evolutionary algorithm seeds its initial population not purely at random but includes PI, NTK and YaRN candidate rescalings as starting individuals, then randomly mutates copies of these seeds. A crucial constraint enforced at mutation/crossover time is monotonicity across dimensions: $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$, i.e., rescale factors are non-decreasing with the RoPE dimension index. This monotonicity both encodes a prior (lower-index dimensions correspond to higher-frequency components that typically should be changed less) and dramatically reduces wasted evaluations.

Practically, the search uses perplexity on PG19 and Proof-pile validation samples as the fitness function. For the smaller target windows (within 256k) the experiments used population $P=64$, mutation/crossover sizes $N_1=N_2=16$, mutation probability $p=0.3$, and ran $\mathcal{T}=40$ iterations, computing perplexity on 5 randomly chosen PG19 validation sequences that meet the length requirement. For windows larger than 512k the population and related sizes were halved and perplexity was measured on a small set of Pile-Books3 validation samples. The search is therefore both empirically driven and tuned explicitly to minimize real language-modeling loss on long-context data.

# Finding 1: Optimal lambdas vary substantially across RoPE dimensions; non-uniform dimension-wise scaling outperforms PI/NTK/YaRN

A central empirical result is that the best rescale factors are not a single global scalar nor a simple hand-crafted grouping: they vary substantially across RoPE dimensions. When the evolutionary search is allowed to pick an independent $\lambda_i$ per RoPE dimension (subject to the monotonicity constraint), the discovered pattern is clearly non-uniform and differs from the simple prescriptions of PI, NTK, or YaRN. In the experiments with LLaMA2-7B, the searched per-dimension lambdas yield significantly lower perplexity at extended context lengths (for example, at 8k and 16k) than any of the baselines that apply uniform or coarse-grained rescaling.

Why does this matter? RoPE dimensions correspond to rotations at different frequencies: the lower-index dimensions (small $i$) are higher-frequency channels, while higher-index dimensions are lower-frequency channels. A single scale applied to every dimension treats these channels equally and tends to "crowd" many token positions into a compressed angular range in dimensions that were encoding fine-grained position distinctions. NTK-style formulas partially address this by suggesting less scaling for some dimensions and more for others, and YaRN groups dimensions into three bands with different behavior; but these prescriptions are coarse. The search-discovered pattern preserves more of the original angle relationships in those dimensions that are most important for distinguishing nearby positions, while allowing stronger rescaling in dimensions that can tolerate it. Empirically, that preservation translates into better next-token likelihoods under the extended context, and the improvement is observed without any fine-tuning of model weights.

An additional empirical note reported in the study is that YaRN—despite being a sophisticated, human-designed grouping approach—can underperform PI and NTK in some settings (e.g., on PG19) when used without fine-tuning because its hand-crafted grouping does not match the specific per-dimension importance profile of the given model. The search-based, fully non-uniform scalings therefore have two advantages: they adapt to the particular model's RoPE geometry, and they find more fine-grained compromises between preservation and rescaling than group-wise heuristics.

# Finding 2: Early tokens (n < \hat{n}) benefit from no or less interpolation; optimal \hat{n} depends on the target length L'

The second empirical non-uniformity concerns token position rather than RoPE dimension. The search includes a scalar threshold $\hat{n}$ so that the first $\hat{n}$ token positions use the original (unrescaled) RoPE angles while later positions are rescaled. Across experiments the authors observed a consistent benefit from keeping a small prefix of the sequence unmodified: performance improves when the earliest tokens are left with their original positional encoding.

The intuition and experimental evidence align. Early tokens in a long sequence often receive large attention weights and act as anchors in attention patterns; prior works (e.g., Streaming LLM, LM-Infinite) have observed that initial positions can attract disproportionate attention mass. If those initial positions are mapped via a rescale that is inconsistent with the pretrained RoPE geometry, the attention computations that rely on those positions produce large, out-of-distribution activations (the paper refers to these as "catastrophic values"). By leaving those early positions untouched—or by applying substantially milder rescaling—the model preserves the expected representations in the parts of the sequence that drive much of the attention, which reduces perplexity.

The experiments made this concrete: the authors extended context windows to 8k and 16k with PI and NTK while systematically varying $\hat{n}$ through values such as $0,2,4,\dots,256$. Two clear observations emerged. First, any non-zero $\hat{n}$ (i.e., keeping some initial tokens unrescaled) tends to improve perplexity relative to always rescaling from position $n=0$. Second, the optimal choice of $\hat{n}$ depends on the target length $L'$. For shorter extensions (e.g., 8k) a relatively small $\hat{n}$ can be sufficient, while larger extensions may benefit from a larger $\hat{n}$ threshold. This dependence reflects a trade-off: not rescaling early positions reduces the interpolation burden for those critical positions but increases pressure on later positions to be brought into the pretrained angular range by larger rescale factors. The search procedure effectively navigates this trade-off and chooses $\hat{n}$ appropriate for each target $L'$.

# Finding 3: Non-uniform scaling improves both non-fine-tuned and fine-tuned settings; provides better initialization and enables 8× non-FT extension

The third finding ties the previous two together in practical terms. The per-dimension non-uniform scalings and the token-threshold mechanism are useful both when the model is left otherwise intact (no fine-tuning) and when the model is later fine-tuned for longer contexts.

On the non-fine-tuning side, the discovered scalings enable substantially larger safe extensions than prior methods. Whereas NTK-style interpolations typically manage at most about a $4\times$ extension without fine-tuning, LongRoPE's search-discovered rescalings permit up to an $8\times$ extension while maintaining reasonable perplexity. In the experiments reported, LLaMA2-7B extended from a 4k base up to 32k without model weight updates by applying the searched per-dimension lambdas and a nonzero $\hat{n}$. This demonstrates that careful, model-adaptive, non-uniform positional rescaling can drastically reduce the "out-of-distribution" effect of new positions even without changing parameters.

For still larger extensions, fine-tuning becomes necessary. Here the searched rescalings play a different but complementary role: they provide an excellent initialization for fine-tuning. If you start fine-tuning from a RoPE setting that already preserves important per-dimension and early-position information, the optimizer is not fighting large, catastrophic activations introduced by naive interpolation. The paper illustrates this by fine-tuning LLaMA2-7B for a longer target (e.g., 64k in their experiments) using the searched RoPE as initialization: the models fine-tuned from LongRoPE initialization achieve better perplexity than models fine-tuned from PI or YaRN initializations. In other words, LongRoPE both raises the ceiling for non-finetuned extension and lowers the optimization cost when fine-tuning is required.

This property underlies the progressive extension strategy used to reach extremely large windows (e.g., 2048k) in the broader LongRoPE pipeline: perform a search and (short) fine-tuning at an intermediate length (e.g., 128k or 256k), then search again on that fine-tuned checkpoint to extend further without full-scale fine-tuning at the final, enormous length. The search-found non-uniform RoPE parameters make both steps tractable and effective.

# Intuition: preserving information in key frequencies and salient early positions reduces interpolation-induced loss

All three empirical findings can be understood from a compact intuition about how RoPE encodes position and how interpolation perturbs that encoding.

RoPE represents positions by multiplying rotation angles with position index $n$. Each pair of dimensions encodes a sinusoidal rotation at a particular frequency. Lower-index dimensions correspond to higher frequencies and thus capture finer-grained differences between neighboring tokens; higher-index dimensions correspond to lower frequencies and capture coarser, long-range position patterns. A uniform downscaling of rotation angles compresses the angular range for all dimensions equivalently; this tends to "crowd" adjacent positions into similar angles in the high-frequency channels and thus destroy the model's ability to distinguish near positions. That crowding is perceived by the model as a distributional shift of its positional inputs and manifests as increased perplexity and unstable attention activations.

Non-uniform, per-dimension rescaling combats this by preserving the angular relationships in those frequencies that matter most for fine discrimination (smaller $\lambda_i$ for low $i$) while allowing larger adjustment where information loss is less harmful (larger $\lambda_i$ for high $i$). Similarly, preserving the original RoPE for early positions (choosing a modest $\hat{n}>0$) keeps the parts of the sequence that attract large attention mass in a familiar, pretrained regime, further reducing out-of-distribution effects that can poison attention computations.

The monotonicity constraint ($\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$) used during search codifies this intuition: it enforces an ordering consistent with frequency semantics and prevents the search from producing jagged, implausible rescaling patterns. Seeding the evolutionary population with PI, NTK, and YaRN candidates both accelerates convergence and ensures that the search explores sensible neighborhoods around known good solutions.

Taken together, these insights explain why a search-driven, non-uniform treatment across RoPE dimensions and across token positions yields superior practical outcomes: it preserves informative components of the positional representation while making only the adjustments that are necessary for new, distant positions. That selective preservation both reduces interpolation-induced loss in zero-shot (no fine-tuning) scenarios and provides a much better starting point for the relatively small amount of fine-tuning required to reach very large context lengths.

# Summary

The experiments with LLaMA2-7B and long-document validation (PG19, Proof-pile) demonstrate two empirical non-uniformities that matter for extending context windows: (1) RoPE dimension-wise non-uniformity (different $\lambda_i$ across dimensions) and (2) token-position non-uniformity (leave a prefix of positions unrescaled). A search-driven approach that optimizes both kinds of non-uniformity simultaneously finds rescalings that outperform uniform or coarse-grained heuristics. Those rescalings enable an $8\times$ extension without fine-tuning, improve fine-tuning convergence when longer extensions are required, and—crucially—do so by preserving the information-bearing parts of the original positional embedding rather than attempting a one-size-fits-all remapping.

Formulating LongRoPE as an Optimization Problem

# Variables: per-dimension rescale factors \{ \lambda_i \} and early-token threshold \hat{n}

LongRoPE builds on the RoPE (rotary positional encoding) representation. For a token at position \(n\), the RoPE encoding over the model's \(d\)-dimensional embedding (organized as \(d/2\) complex-rotation pairs) can be written as
\[
\big[ \cos(n\theta_0),\; \sin(n\theta_0),\; \cos(n\theta_1),\; \sin(n\theta_1),\;\ldots,\; \cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}),\; \sin(n\theta_{d/2-1}) \big],
\]
where the per-dimension rotary angles follow \(n\theta_i\) and \(\theta_i\) are derived from a base frequency parameter (the paper denotes \(\beta=\theta^{2/d}\) in derived formulae). RoPE thus represents position \(n\) by rotating pairs of coordinates with frequencies that vary across embedding dimensions.

LongRoPE introduces two families of search variables to reshape how rotation angles are assigned to extended positions:

- Per-dimension rescale factors \(\{\lambda_i\}_{i=0}^{d/2-1}\). Each \(\lambda_i\) controls how much the rotary angle for the \(i\)-th RoPE dimension is scaled for tokens beyond a certain early-token boundary. Intuitively, \(\lambda_i\) determines how aggressively we "compress" or "downscale" the angle \(n/\beta^i\) for later tokens so that newly introduced large positions can be represented within a pretrained angular range. The paper searches \(\lambda_i\) in a discrete range chosen relative to the extension ratio \(s = L'/L\); empirically the allowed range is from \(1.0\) (direct extrapolation) to \(s\times 1.25\) with step size \(0.01\). The lower bound \(1.0\) corresponds to minimal extra scaling (direct extrapolation), while larger values correspond to stronger interpolation.

- Early-token threshold \(\hat{n}\). This is a non-negative integer that specifies a prefix length of the input sequence whose RoPE values are left unchanged. Tokens at positions \(n<\hat{n}\) keep the original pretrained rotary angles, while tokens at \(n\ge\hat{n}\) are subject to per-dimension rescaling. The set of candidate \(\hat{n}\) values used in the paper is discrete and includes \(\{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}\). The inclusion of \(\hat{n}\) captures the empirical observation that the very early tokens in a sequence often receive large attention and therefore benefit from preserving the pretrained RoPE behavior.

Together, \(\{\lambda_i\}\) and \(\hat{n}\) parameterize a family of RoPE modifications that can vary across embedding dimensions and across token positions, enabling LongRoPE to express rich, non-uniform interpolation strategies.

# Indicator function \(\mathbb{I}(\cdot,\cdot)\): no scaling for \(n < \hat{n}\); apply \(1/\lambda_i\) for \(n \ge \hat{n}\)

LongRoPE applies the per-dimension rescale factors only to tokens at or after the threshold \(\hat{n}\). This is encoded by an indicator-style multiplier. For a given RoPE dimension index \(i\) and a token position \(n\), define
\[
\mathbb{I}(\lambda_i,\hat{n}; n) \;=\;
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } n < \hat{n},\\[4pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_i}, & \text{if } n \ge \hat{n}.
\end{cases}
\]
Using this multiplier, the modified rotary pair for the \(i\)-th RoPE dimension at position \(n\) becomes
\[
\bigg(\cos\!\Big(\mathbb{I}(\lambda_i,\hat{n}; n)\,\cdot\,\frac{n}{\beta^i}\Big),\;
      \sin\!\Big(\mathbb{I}(\lambda_i,\hat{n}; n)\,\cdot\,\frac{n}{\beta^i}\Big)\bigg).
\]
Thus for the prefix \(n<\hat{n}\) we preserve the pretrained rotation angles exactly, and for the suffix \(n\ge\hat{n}\) we scale every rotation angle for dimension \(i\) by the factor \(1/\lambda_i\). Multiplying the original angle by \(1/\lambda_i\) is equivalent to mapping the large position index \(n\) into a smaller effective angle, which is the mechanism positional interpolation methods use to avoid "catastrophic" untrained angles when extending beyond the pretrained window.

Why this two-part indicator? Empirically the earliest tokens are disproportionately important (they often receive strong attention), so leaving them unmodified reduces disruption to pretrained short-context behavior. At the same time, allowing per-dimension scaling on later tokens lets the method treat different frequency channels differently: high-frequency channels (lower index \(i\)) typically require less scaling, while low-frequency channels can tolerate or require more scaling. The indicator formalizes these two forms of non-uniformity in a compact, searchable parametrization.

# Objective: minimize next-token loss (perplexity) over documents with length \(\ge L'\)

The search goal in LongRoPE is to choose the per-dimension rescale factors and the early-token threshold so that the language model performs well (i.e., has low next-token loss) on long inputs of interest. Formally, let \(\mathbf{X}\) be a collection of long documents and let \(L'\) be the target context window (we only consider documents \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X}\) with \(|\mathbf{x}|\ge L'\) so that long-range behavior is tested). The objective can be written succinctly as
\[
\{\lambda_i^\star\},\; \hat{n}^\star \;=\;
\operatorname{arg\,min}_{\{\lambda_i\},\,\hat{n}}
\; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X}:\,|\mathbf{x}|\ge L'} \Big[ \mathcal{L}\big(\mathrm{LLM}\big(\mathrm{RoPE}(\{\lambda_i\},\hat{n})\big),\,\mathbf{x}\big)\Big],
\]
where \(\mathcal{L}\) denotes the next-token prediction loss (cross-entropy) for the sequence and \(\mathrm{RoPE}(\{\lambda_i\},\hat{n})\) denotes the RoPE encoding family obtained via the indicator-based rescaling described above. In practice LongRoPE uses perplexity (the exponential of the average negative log-likelihood) as the scalar evaluation metric to guide search; the search procedure directly compares candidate RoPE parametrizations by their perplexity on a small set of held-out long validation samples.

Two important practical points from the paper underpin this objective choice. First, the focus is on realistic long-document behavior, so the expected loss is evaluated only on examples that exercise the extended window (\(|\mathbf{x}|\ge L'\)). Second, because the objective is evaluated by running the (unchanged) LLM forward with modified RoPE parameters and computing perplexity, the search is model-aware: the chosen \(\{\lambda_i\}\) and \(\hat{n}\) are tuned to the specific pretrained model and to the chosen target length \(L'\).

# Search-space scale and combinatorics (example: \(4\times\) extension yields \(\sim 4\times 10^{167}\) candidates)

The parametrization that allows per-dimension \(\lambda_i\) and discrete \(\hat{n}\) gives LongRoPE the expressive power to capture rich non-uniform interpolations, but it also creates a combinatorially large search space.

Quantitatively, the number of discrete choices per \(\lambda_i\) depends on the discretization and the target extension ratio \(s=L'/L\). If \(\lambda_i\) is allowed to take values in
\[
\{1.00,\,1.01,\,1.02,\,\dots,\,s\times 1.25\},
\]
then the per-dimension count \(M\) is approximately \((s\times 1.25-1.0)/0.01 + 1\). The number of choices for \(\hat{n}\) is the size \(N\) of the discrete candidate set (the paper uses a 14-element set listed earlier). Because there are \(d/2\) independent RoPE rotation pairs (each with its own \(\lambda_i\)), an upper bound on the total number of candidate RoPE parametrizations is
\[
\text{#candidates} \;=\; M^{\,d/2} \times N.
\]

Even for modest values this explodes extremely quickly. For example, with an extension ratio \(s=4\), the paper uses a discretization that yields about \(M\approx 400\) choices per dimension; with \(N=14\) choices for \(\hat{n}\) and \(d/2=64\) rotation pairs this gives
\[
400^{64}\times 14 \;\approx\; 4\times 10^{167},
\]
an astronomically large search space. This simple calculation makes clear that exhaustive enumeration is impossible and naive random search will also be prohibitively inefficient.

# Why an empirical search (vs. closed-form) is necessary given model- and target-specific optima

Given the enormous combinatorial space, why not derive a closed-form or analytic rule for \(\{\lambda_i\}\) and \(\hat{n}\)? LongRoPE's paper explains multiple reasons why an empirical search is the pragmatic and effective choice:

1. The mapping from RoPE rescaling parameters to language-model loss/perplexity is a black-box, highly nonlinear, non-convex function of the parameters. The loss depends on complex interactions between modified positional encodings and the pretrained transformer's weights; there is no available closed-form expression that predicts how a small change in \(\lambda_i\) will affect perplexity.

2. Positional information in a pretrained transformer is distributed non-uniformly across embedding dimensions and across token positions. The paper empirically finds two distinct forms of non-uniformity: (a) different RoPE dimensions require different amounts of interpolation/extrapolation, and (b) the earliest token positions benefit from less or no interpolation. These non-uniform structures are subtle and model- and target-length-dependent; hand-designed heuristics (e.g., uniform linear scaling, simple NTK formulas, or fixed groupings used by prior works) can miss important per-dimension or per-position patterns that substantially affect perplexity.

3. The optimal rescaling pattern depends on the pretrained model and on the target extension ratio \(s\). The same heuristic is not uniformly optimal across models (the paper demonstrates different behavior for LLaMA2 and Mistral) nor across extension lengths. A single closed-form formula therefore cannot be expected to yield near-optimal performance in the general case.

4. An empirical search lets LongRoPE directly optimize the quantity we care about (next-token loss/perplexity) on realistic long examples, and it allows incorporation of domain priors as search constraints. Concretely, the paper uses an evolution-based search that seeds the initial population with known good baselines (PI, NTK, YaRN) and enforces a monotonicity constraint \(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\) to reflect NTK-inspired intuition (lower-index, higher-frequency dimensions should be scaled no more than higher-index, lower-frequency dimensions). These search design choices both reduce the effective search space and exploit available theory and prior practice while still letting the optimizer discover model- and length-specific optima.

Because of these factors, LongRoPE frames the problem as a guided empirical search: it evaluates candidate \(\{\lambda_i\},\hat{n}\) by computing perplexity on held-out long documents and selects values that minimize that empirical loss. This approach directly targets the practical goal (good long-context next-token performance for a particular LLM and target \(L'\)) and — as the paper shows — yields rescale patterns that outperform simple closed-form heuristics in both non-fine-tuned and fine-tuned scenarios.

Summary

Formulating LongRoPE as an optimization problem makes explicit the two degrees of freedom the method exploits: per-dimension rescale factors \(\{\lambda_i\}\) and an early-token threshold \(\hat{n}\). The indicator-based RoPE modification preserves pretrained angles for an initial prefix and applies per-dimension angular compression thereafter. The search objective is to pick \(\{\lambda_i\},\hat{n}\) that minimize next-token loss (perplexity) on long documents. Because the discrete search space is combinatorially huge and the loss landscape is model- and length-dependent, LongRoPE adopts an empirical (evolutionary) search strategy guided by perplexity rather than a single closed-form interpolation rule.

Evolutionary Search for Non-Uniform Rescale Factors

# Search space design: $\lambda_i \in [1.0, 1.25\times s]$ with step $0.01$; $\hat{n} \in \{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}$

The search problem in LongRoPE is to find, for each RoPE dimension, a rescale factor that minimizes language-modeling loss when the model is applied to documents longer than the original training length. Concretely, for each RoPE (complex) dimension index $i$ we search for a scalar $\lambda_i$ and we search jointly for a token-threshold $\hat{n}$ that controls whether the original RoPE is preserved for the first $\hat{n}$ tokens. The rotation angle used at token position $n$ and RoPE dimension $i$ is multiplied by a factor given by the piecewise function

\[
\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_{i},\hat{n}) \;=\;
\begin{cases}
1 & n < \hat{n},\\[4pt]
\frac{1}{\lambda_i} & n \ge \hat{n},
\end{cases}
\]

so that for $n\ge\hat{n}$ the rotation is scaled by $1/\lambda_i$ (smaller angles correspond to more interpolation), while for initial tokens ($n<\hat{n}$) the original RoPE angles are retained. In the implementation LongRoPE searches $\lambda_i$ directly; $\lambda_i=1.0$ therefore encodes "no interpolation" (direct extrapolation), and larger $\lambda_i$ values encode progressively stronger interpolation. For a target extension ratio $s=\frac{L'}{L}$ the allowed search range for each $\lambda_i$ is

\[
\lambda_i \in \left[\,1.0,\; 1.25\times s\,\right]
\]

with a step granularity of $0.01$. The extra $1.25$ factor allows solutions that apply even slightly stronger interpolation than plain linear PI would, which can be useful in practice. The threshold $\hat{n}$ is drawn from a small discrete set

\[
\hat{n} \in \{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\},
\]

chosen to cover the empirical regimes where the first few tokens deserve special treatment (no interpolation). The inclusion of $\hat{n}$ in the search captures the second form of non-uniformity (token-position dependence) discovered in the study.

Because we permit an independent $\lambda_i$ per RoPE dimension and multiple choices for $\hat{n}$, the raw Cartesian search space is astronomically large. For example, with step $0.01$ and a roughly 400-choice range per dimension, a $4\times$ extension leads to on the order of $400^{64}\times 14$ combinations (the paper cites approximately $4\times10^{167}$), which motivates the need for an efficient search procedure rather than brute force enumeration.

# Population initialization seeded with PI, NTK, YaRN solutions; mutation probability $p$

A practical evolutionary search depends critically on its initial population. LongRoPE uses an "optimized initial population" strategy: rather than starting entirely at random, the initial population includes the established interpolation rules as seed individuals — specifically the PI (linear Position Interpolation) solution, the NTK-derived solution, and YaRN’s grouped-solution. These three are included directly because they are known sensible baselines (they encode different biases about how to distribute interpolation across RoPE dimensions), and starting from them gives the evolutionary process strong candidates immediately.

The remainder of the initial population (population size denoted $P$) is generated by randomly mutating these seed individuals. Concretely, after inserting PI, NTK and YaRN into the initial pool, the other $P-3$ individuals are created by applying random perturbations to those seeds with some mutation probability $p$ (the paper uses $p=0.3$ as a default). This initialization both preserves useful existing structure and injects diversity so the search can explore variants that interpolate more or less aggressively dimension-wise or that change the preserved initial token count $\hat{n}$.

For the practical experiments reported in the paper (target window sizes up to 256k), the default population size is $P=64$. This choice balances breadth of exploration against the cost of evaluating candidates (each candidate requires running the model on long sequences to measure perplexity). The initialization scheme significantly accelerates convergence relative to a completely random start because good baseline patterns are already present in the pool.

# Monotonic non-decreasing constraint across dimensions ($\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$); NTK-based motivation

LongRoPE imposes an important structural constraint on admissible candidates: the per-dimension rescale factors are required to be monotonically non-decreasing with the RoPE dimension index,

\[
\lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \cdots \le \lambda_{d/2 - 1}.
\]

This monotonicity constraint is motivated both by theoretical intuition (from NTK and Fourier-like interpretations of RoPE) and by pragmatic search efficiency. Intuitively, lower-index RoPE dimensions correspond to higher angular frequencies; NTK-style analysis and prior work suggest that those high-frequency components encode fine-grained positional distinctions and therefore should be changed less when extending context. Higher-index, lower-frequency dimensions can tolerate — and often require — more interpolation to avoid crowding the large new position range. Enforcing $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ encodes this inductive bias directly in the search space.

From a systems perspective the monotonic constraint is also a powerful pruning mechanism. Many random perturbations of $\lambda$ vectors produce non-physical jagged sequences that violate the frequency ordering assumption and are unlikely to be good models of positional structure; by discarding such candidates early (i.e., before the expensive perplexity evaluation) the algorithm avoids wasting computation. In practice LongRoPE filters mutated or crossed candidates and only computes model perplexity for candidates that satisfy the monotonicity check.

# Mutation and crossover to generate candidates; top-k selection by perplexity

LongRoPE adopts a standard evolutionary loop with two main genetic operators: mutation and crossover, both applied under the monotonicity constraint. The general procedure per iteration is:

1. Evaluate the current population by applying each candidate's rescale factors to the LLM's RoPE and computing perplexity on a held-out long-document sample set (details of the fitness measure follow in the next section).

2. Select a top-k set of parents (the best-performing candidates by perplexity). In the reported experiments the algorithm keeps the top-32 as parents.

3. Generate two pools of offspring:
   - Mutation pool: produce $N_1$ mutants by randomly perturbing parent parameters. Perturbations respect the discrete step size (changes are multiples of $0.01$ in $\lambda$), and the threshold $\hat{n}$ may also be changed by selecting a different value from the allowed set. Each mutated child is accepted only if it satisfies the monotonicity constraint.
   - Crossover pool: produce $N_2$ recombinants by combining pairs of parents (for example by exchanging contiguous blocks of the $\lambda$ vector or mixing $\hat{n}$ values). Again, the monotonicity check filters out invalid recombinations before evaluation.

4. Form the next population as the union of the parents, the accepted mutants, and the accepted recombinants. Repeat for a fixed number of iterations $\mathcal{T}$.

The paper reports practical default values for the inner-loop sizes when searching for windows up to 256k: $N_1=N_2=16$, mutation probability $p=0.3$, population $P=64$, and number of iterations $\mathcal{T}=40$. At each generation the algorithm computes perplexities for the current population, updates the top-k set, and uses that set to spawn the next generation (see Algorithm 1 in the paper). By keeping the top candidates across generations the evolutionary process concentrates search effort on promising regions of the parameter space while still injecting variation through mutation and recombination.

Note that the implementation does not evaluate candidates that violate monotonicity; this is a deliberate optimization to avoid performing expensive model runs on candidates that contradict the NTK-inspired ordering prior.

# Fitness evaluation protocol: long-document perplexity (PG19 / Books3), sliding-window evaluation

The fitness function used to rank candidates is the language-modeling perplexity measured on long-document validation samples. Formally, the search objective (repeating the paper’s notation) is to minimize the next-token prediction loss

\[
\underset{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X},\,|\mathbf{x}|\ge L'}{\operatorname{arg\,min}}\;\; \mathcal{L}\big(\text{LLM}(\text{RoPE},\mathbf{X})\big),
\]

where the RoPE used for evaluation incorporates the candidate $(\{\lambda_i\},\hat{n})$.

Because evaluating a candidate requires running the model on long sequences, LongRoPE carefully selects a small number of representative long-document samples for fitness evaluation. For target context windows up to 256k the paper uses 5 random samples from the PG19 validation set; each selected sample must be at least as long as the target context length. For target windows larger than 512k the evaluation set is switched to a small number (3) of samples drawn from Pile-Books3 (Books3), reflecting the scarcity of extra-long contiguous validation documents and the higher cost of evaluating very long inputs.

Perplexity is computed in the standard way from negative log-likelihood, and — when reporting final model curves across very long documents — the experiments use sliding-window evaluation (e.g., a sliding window stride of 256 tokens when producing per-length perplexity curves in results). During search, the fitness evaluation is stricter: candidates must perform well specifically on sequences that match or exceed the target extension length; the algorithm enforces a minimum length requirement for the chosen samples so the fitness reflects behavior in the intended operating regime.

By minimizing perplexity directly on long-document data, the search is guided toward rescale patterns that preserve the model’s ability to distinguish positions and to avoid catastrophic activations introduced by new positions.

# Scaling to very large $L'$: reducing population/mutation/crossover sizes

A practical consideration is that the cost of evaluating a single RoPE candidate scales with the sequence length $L'$ used for evaluation. When $L'$ becomes very large (hundreds of thousands to millions of tokens), running the model even once for a candidate is expensive. The paper addresses this by adapting the evolutionary hyperparameters as $L'$ grows: for target windows over 512k they halve the sizes of population, mutation pool and crossover pool (i.e., use smaller $P$, $N_1$, and $N_2$). This straightforward scaling-down reduces the total number of candidate evaluations and keeps the search compute within practical limits.

Halving those parameters is a pragmatic trade-off: it reduces exploration breadth but keeps the search feasible. The monotonicity constraint and the optimized initialization (seeding with PI/NTK/YaRN) make the reduced search still effective, because many sensible candidates are already close to those seeds and the monotonic constraint prunes unreasonable variants. In short, LongRoPE adapts search budget to $L'$ by shrinking the per-generation candidate set, rather than attempting an infeasible exhaustive exploration at very large scales.

# Outcome: reliable $8\times$ context extension without fine-tuning; smoother perplexity curves vs PI/NTK/YaRN

The evolutionary search delivers two immediate, empirically validated benefits.

First, it finds non-uniform, dimension-wise rescale vectors and suitable $\hat{n}$ thresholds that preserve key high-frequency RoPE dimensions and the most-attended initial token positions. This preserves discriminative positional information in regimes where simple linear interpolation would crowd many positions into a narrow angular range. The result is a candidate RoPE mapping that the pretrained model can use on much longer inputs without catastrophic degradation.

Second, and strikingly, these searched solutions enable an $8\times$ extension of context length without any fine-tuning: the paper demonstrates that for LLaMA2-7B the search-based RoPE enables moving from a 4k window to 32k while preserving reasonable perplexity (see Fig. 3 in the paper). By contrast, standard PI and other non-uniform analytic schemes such as NTK or YaRN show perplexity spikes much earlier (the paper reports behavior that spikes after about a $2\times$ extension for those methods). The evolutionary method therefore produces much smoother perplexity curves as input length grows, because it keeps the most important positional components intact and only interpolates those components that can tolerate it.

Beyond the non-fine-tuning regime, the searched RoPE vectors also provide superior initialization for short, economical fine-tuning to reach even longer contexts. The paper uses a progressive strategy: search and fine-tune to 128k/256k, then perform a second-stage search on the fine-tuned model to reach 2048k. The first-stage search outcomes make the subsequent fine-tuning both faster to converge and more stable, because the model sees a rescaled RoPE that does not introduce highly out-of-distribution angular values at the new positions.

Ablations reported in the paper confirm that both components of the search space are valuable. Searching only across RoPE dimensions (the $\lambda_i$) provides a large perplexity improvement over PI; including the token-position non-uniformity (the $\hat{n}$ choices) further improves results at moderate extension ratios (e.g., up to tens of thousands of tokens), because retaining the initial tokens without interpolation preserves attention behavior that the model relies on.

Summary and practical guidance

- Parameterization: search per-dimension $\lambda_i$ in $\left[1.0, 1.25s\right]$ with step $0.01$, jointly with $\hat{n}$ chosen from the small discrete set above.
- Initialization: always include the PI, NTK and YaRN solutions in the initial population; fill the rest by mutating these seeds with probability $p$ (default $0.3$).
- Constraint: enforce $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ to encode the NTK-inspired frequency ordering and to prune the search space before costly evaluation.
- Evolution loop: evaluate population by perplexity on long-document samples, keep top-k parents, generate $N_1$ mutants and $N_2$ recombinants with monotonic-filtering, repeat for $\mathcal{T}$ generations. Default hyperparameters used in the paper (for $L'\le 256$k) are $P=64$, $N_1=N_2=16$, top-$k=32$, and $\mathcal{T}=40$.
- Scaling: for very large $L'$ halve $P$, $N_1$, $N_2$ (and/or reduce iterations) to limit compute; use a smaller evaluation set (3 Books3 samples) because very long validation sequences are scarce.

These choices, taken together, form a practical evolutionary procedure that discovers non-uniform RoPE rescale factors which (1) enable up to $8\times$ context expansion without any fine-tuning, (2) provide strong initializations that reduce fine-tuning needs for much larger extensions, and (3) produce smoother, lower-perplexity behavior across extended sequence lengths than prior analytic interpolation schemes.

Progressive Extension to 2048k Tokens

# Stage A: search rescale factors for $128\text{k}$ and $256\text{k}$ on the pretrained model ($32\times$ and $64\times$)

Stage A begins on the pretrained LLM and its purpose is to find a good, non-uniform positional-interpolation initialization prior to any expensive fine-tuning. Concretely, for a model whose original context window is $L$ (e.g., $L=4\text{k}$), we target two intermediate lengths $L'_1=128\text{k}$ and $L'_2=256\text{k}$, which correspond to extension ratios $s_1=\frac{L'_1}{L}=32$ and $s_2=\frac{L'_2}{L}=64$. Rather than applying a single global linear rescale for all RoPE dimensions (as in simple PI) or a fixed hand-crafted grouping (as in YaRN), LongRoPE frames the problem as a search for a per-dimension rescale vector $\{\lambda_i\}$ together with a small-position threshold $\hat{n}$ that leaves the earliest token positions un-rescaled. The rescaled rotary angle for dimension $i$ and token position $n$ thus follows the same functional form used throughout the paper but with $\lambda_i$ allowed to vary across dimensions and the first $\hat{n}$ positions optionally exempted from rescaling.

The search used in Stage A is an evolutionary search tailored for this large discrete space. The search space for each per-dimension scale $\lambda_i$ is taken from a continuous grid from $1.0$ (no interpolation / direct extrapolation) up to about $s\times 1.25$ with fine granularity (the paper uses a step of $0.01$). The token-threshold $\hat{n}$ is chosen from a small discrete set (for example $\{0,1,2,4,8,\dots,256\}$). Two important inductive biases dramatically reduce the effective search cost: (1) the monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$, motivated by NTK and RoPE frequency ordering (lower-index dimensions have higher frequency and thus should be changed less), and (2) seeding the initial population with sensible baselines (PI, NTK, YaRN) and random perturbations of them rather than purely random samples. These constraints preserve the plausible structure of good solutions and dramatically cut the number of candidate evaluations needed.

Why run this search on the pretrained model rather than immediately fine-tuning to the final target? First, the search yields an initialization that preserves important RoPE information (key dimensions and initial-token encodings). Empirically the paper shows that such non-uniform rescale vectors often permit considerably larger non-fine-tuning extensions (e.g., up to $8\times$ without fine-tuning) and provide a much better starting point for any subsequent fine-tuning. Second, searching at the $128\text{k}$ and $256\text{k}$ scales is computationally far cheaper than searching or training at the final $2048\text{k}$ scale because candidate perplexity evaluations can be performed on shorter long-form windows and because the search space stays tractable with the monotonic constraint.

# Stage B: fine-tune to $256\text{k}$ (e.g., LLaMA2: 400 steps @128k factors, then 600 steps with 256k factors)

Stage B executes the actual limited fine-tuning using the rescale factors discovered in Stage A. The key principle is gradual adaptation: the model should be exposed to the new positional regime progressively, rather than being forced in a single jump from $L$ to $L'$ where the majority of positions are previously unseen. For LLaMA2 the paper follows a two-step fine-tuning schedule that reflects this principle. First, the model is fine-tuned for a modest number of steps using the $128\text{k}$ rescale factors discovered in Stage A; the paper reports $400$ steps for this first phase. After that checkpoint stabilizes, the RoPE factors are replaced with the $256\text{k}$ rescale factors from Stage A and the model is fine-tuned for an additional $600$ steps. In total this uses about $1{,}000$ steps of fine-tuning within the $256\text{k}$ training-length regime rather than attempting to fine-tune directly at $2048\text{k}$.

There are multiple reasons this two-phase fine-tuning is effective. From an optimization perspective, each phase introduces far fewer truly “new” position indices at a time, so the model encounters a less severe distribution shift and the gradients are more stable. From a data perspective, long training segments of length $128\text{k}$ or $256\text{k}$ are substantially more common and cheaper to process than $2048\text{k}$ segments, alleviating the scarcity and compute barriers associated with training on extremely long sequences. Practically, the paper reports that fine-tuning in this staged way is empirically more stable and faster to reach satisfactory performance at $256\text{k}$ than a single, direct fine-tuning to the same length.

It is important to stress that the Stage B fine-tuning uses the non-uniform rescale vectors found in Stage A as the RoPE initialization. The better the initialization (i.e., the more it preserves critical RoPE frequencies and initial token encodings), the less the model needs to adapt by changing many internal weights to accommodate new positional indices. That is exactly why Stage A and Stage B are coupled: a carefully searched RoPE setup makes the limited fine-tuning feasible and effective.

# Stage C: second search on the $256\text{k}$ fine-tuned LLM to reach $2048\text{k}$ without further fine-tuning (final $512\times$ total)

Stage C leverages two empirical facts established earlier: (1) non-uniform rescale factors can enable relatively large non-fine-tuning extensions (the paper demonstrates an $8\times$ non-fine-tuning extension in many settings), and (2) a model that has been fine-tuned to some intermediate long length has learned representations and attention patterns that make a further non-fine-tuning extension easier. Accordingly, after Stage B leaves us with a model that is trained and validated at $256\text{k}$, Stage C runs a second evolutionary search for per-dimension $\lambda_i$ and position threshold $\hat{n}$ on that fine-tuned checkpoint, but now with the target context length $L' = 2048\text{k}$. The per-dimension search now aims to exploit the trained representations up to $256\text{k}$ and to find a rescaling that “stretches” them to $2048\text{k}$.

Because the fine-tuned LLM already handles positions up to $256\text{k}$ in a trained way, the second search is able to use the previously observed $8\times$ non-fine-tuning property: it finds a rescale configuration that extends the model another $8\times$ from $256\text{k}$ to $2048\text{k}$ without additional gradient updates. From the perspective of the original model baseline (e.g., $L=4\text{k}$), the total extension across the three stages is $s_{\text{total}}=\frac{2048\text{k}}{4\text{k}} = 512$, i.e., a $512\times$ extension relative to the original context window.

Practical considerations during the Stage C search are important. Because candidate evaluations at $L'=2048\text{k}$ are expensive and require genuinely long samples, the paper adjusts the search budget and validation protocol for very large targets: for windows over $512\text{k}$ the population size and mutation/crossover budgets are reduced to keep overall cost manageable, and perplexity evaluation is performed on long-book validation samples (e.g., from Books3 / Pile-Books3) that actually reach the target length. The outcome of this stage is a RoPE rescale configuration that can be deployed to the Stage B fine-tuned checkpoint to realize $2048\text{k}$ context handling in inference, without further fine-tuning.

# Short-context recovery: additional searches at $4\text{k}/8\text{k}$; use low-length factors dynamically at inference for sequences $<8\text{k}$

One well-known side effect of aggressive positional interpolation is that positions within the original short context window become “crowded” in rotary-angle space after a large global rescale; this can degrade performance on tasks that rely on the original short-range ordering. LongRoPE addresses this by performing an extra recovery step after Stage C. The idea is simple: run an additional targeted evolution search on the extended (Stage C) model but restrict the objective and search range to short-window behavior (for example target lengths $4\text{k}$ and $8\text{k}$). In that short-length search the allowed maximum $\lambda$ is reduced to encourage less interpolation, producing rescale vectors that better preserve the pretraining geometry for short contexts.

During inference the system then dynamically selects which RoPE rescale vector to apply based on the runtime input sequence length. If the sequence length is shorter than the recovery threshold (the paper uses $8\text{k}$ as the switch point), the model uses the short-length rescale factors; otherwise it uses the long-length factors tuned for $2048\text{k}$. In this way LongRoPE offers a practical compromise: when a short sequence is presented, the model behaves much like the original pretrained model (preserving short-range distinctions); when a very long sequence is presented, the model switches to the rescale factors that allow robust long-range operation. The result is that the large-context model retains acceptable or near-original performance on short-window benchmarks while supporting million-token contexts.

# Why progressive works: improved optimization stability, reduced data/compute demands, better final quality

There are three complementary reasons the progressive multi-stage strategy is effective in practice.

Improved optimization stability. Extending from a short window to an extremely long one in a single step creates an enormous distributional shift: most token positions become “new,” the model sees rotation angles it was never trained on, and this often produces catastrophic activations and poor gradients. By splitting the process into intermediate targets ($128\text{k}$, then $256\text{k}$) and by seeding fine-tuning with carefully searched non-uniform RoPE rescale vectors, each adaptation step presents the model with a much smaller effective shift. The early search preserves critical high-frequency (low-index) RoPE dimensions and the initial tokens (via $\hat{n}$), so during fine-tuning weights do not have to simultaneously compensate for wholesale positional re-encoding. This staged transition yields empirically smoother convergence and avoids the instability seen when trying to learn from scratch at enormous extension ratios.

Reduced data and compute demands. Extremely long sequences (hundreds of thousands to millions of tokens) are rare in existing corpora, and training with them is costly both in memory and time. The progressive plan confines the expensive gradient-based learning to $256\text{k}$ sequence lengths where long segments are still manageable and more readily available; it requires only about $1{,}000$ fine-tuning steps in the LLaMA2 example. The final jump to $2048\text{k}$ is accomplished by a search and rescale (no further gradient computation), so the method avoids the need to collect massive numbers of $>1{,}000\text{k}$ segments or to run prohibitively long training jobs.

Better final quality through informed initialization and targeted search. The two-stage search plays a dual role: first it finds RoPE rescale factors that preserve information most important to the pretrained model; second, after modest fine-tuning, it finds a different set of rescale factors that match the fine-tuned weights’ representation geometry up to $256\text{k}$ and safely extrapolate to $2048\text{k}$. This decomposition—search for good interpolation, fine-tune, then search again—combines the benefits of non-uniform interpolation (preserving critical dimensions and initial tokens) and supervised adaptation (allowing internal weights to adjust to longer-range interactions). The net result, validated across LLaMA2 and Mistral experiments in the paper, is a large-context model that achieves low perplexity on long sequences, retains high passkey retrieval accuracy across millions of tokens, and preserves much of the original short-context performance after the short-window recovery step.

In short, the progressive strategy mitigates the two main practical bottlenecks in pushing context windows to the million-token scale: (1) the catastrophic effects of introducing huge numbers of previously unseen positional indices, and (2) the scarcity and cost of training data and compute for extremely long sequences. By combining principled per-dimension searches with staged fine-tuning and a final long-range interpolation, LongRoPE attains $2048\text{k}$ context windows while keeping fine-tuning costs modest and preserving short-context behavior.

Implementation and Engineering Guide

# Integrating per-dimension scaling into RoPE code paths; no architecture changes

LongRoPE is implemented by changing only the positional (RoPE) computation, leaving the model architecture, attention math, and trained weights intact. Conceptually this is a single, localized modification: replace the original rotary-angle computation for each RoPE dimension with a dimension-wise rescaled version that depends on token position. Using the notation from the paper, the original rotary angle for the $i$-th RoPE dimension at token position $n$ is
\[
\frac{n}{\beta^i},
\]
and LongRoPE replaces this by multiplying the angle by a multiplier that depends on the searched per-dimension rescale vector $\lambda=\{\lambda_i\}$ and a token-threshold $\hat{n}$. Concretely, define the multiplier
\[
m(i,n)=
\begin{cases}
1 & n<\hat{n},\\[4pt]
\frac{1}{\lambda_i} & n\ge\hat{n},
\end{cases}
\]
so that the modified RoPE becomes
\[
\mathrm{RoPE}_n = \big[\ldots,\cos\!\big(m(i,n)\cdot\frac{n}{\beta^i}\big),\; \sin\!\big(m(i,n)\cdot\frac{n}{\beta^i}\big),\ldots\big].
\]
Because this change acts only on the rotation angles used to convert queries and keys, there is no need to touch attention kernels, weight tensors, or model dimensions: the rotary transform is computed slightly differently, and the downstream attention math is unchanged. The paper stresses this point repeatedly: LongRoPE retains the original architecture with minor positional-embedding modifications and can therefore reuse existing optimizations and kernels.

Implementation guidance (conceptual, consistent with the paper): implement $m(i,n)$ as a short routine that takes the current token index $n$, the threshold $\hat{n}$, and the precomputed vector $\lambda_i$. Apply $m(i,n)$ when computing per-dimension rotation angles (the cos/sin tables or the angle tensor), then apply the usual rotary mixing to keys and queries. Keep $\lambda$ as a float array of length $d/2$ (where $d$ is the model embedding size) and $\hat{n}$ as an integer. Follow the monotonicity constraint used in the search ($\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$) in any code paths that validate or generate rescale vectors.

# Managing multiple rescale profiles and switching by current sequence length at inference

LongRoPE produces rescale profiles targeted at specific context lengths (for example, profiles discovered for 4k/8k short recovery, for 128k or 256k fine-tuning targets, and for the final extreme 2048k extension). The paper’s workflow stores and uses a small set of such profiles rather than a single global setting. At inference time the model selects the profile appropriate for the sequence length in use: the experiments explicitly adjust RoPE for short lengths (the paper mentions applying searched rescale factors when sequence length is less than 8k) and otherwise use the extended-profile for long inputs.

Practically, keep a small table of rescale profiles keyed by their target context length (for example, entries for 4k, 8k, 128k, 256k, 2048k as produced during the LongRoPE pipeline). At runtime, pick the profile whose search target matches the operating regime of the current request (in the paper’s experiments, short-context requests used their short-length profile while long-context requests used the extended profile). The exact switch policy is an engineering choice; the paper demonstrates the value of at least having a short-profile (4k/8k) to recover short-window performance and a long-profile for million-token inputs.

One practical point emphasized by the paper is to ensure consistency between the profile used to create any cached keys/values and the profile used when those cached items are later consumed. If you switch profiles mid-session, make sure the RoPE application on both cached and newly computed tokens matches the active profile (see the next section for compatibility notes with KV cache).

# Compatibility with optimized attention (e.g., FlashAttention) and KV-caching

Because LongRoPE only changes the rotation angles used to construct the rotary embeddings, optimized attention kernels and KV-caching mechanisms remain compatible in the same way they are with standard RoPE. The attention computation (matrix multiply of queries and keys, softmax, weighted sum of values) and the tensor shapes are unchanged; only the per-token, per-dimension angle inputs to the rotary operation are different. The paper explicitly notes that models extended via LongRoPE "retain the original architecture with minor modifications to the positional embedding, and can reuse most pre-existing optimizations."

Engineering implications you should keep in mind (following the paper’s constraints): compute the scaled rotation angles before invoking your optimized kernel so that queries and keys passed to FlashAttention or other kernels are already transformed by the same RoPE profile. For KV-caching, compute and store the keys/values using the same rescale profile that will be used when those cached entries are consumed; if you allow dynamic switching of profiles at inference time, ensure that cached entries are either invalidated or recomputed with the new profile so that the rotary-phase alignment remains consistent. These are compatibility considerations implied by the paper’s design principle of changing only the RoPE computation while reusing existing attention and caching infrastructure.

# Data preparation for search and evaluation: ensuring documents exceed $L'$; windowing strategies

The paper treats the search and evaluation datasets as collections of long documents and enforces the basic requirement that each sample used for search or evaluation be at least as long as the target context length $L'$. In formal terms the search objective requires samples $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X}$ with $|\mathbf{x}|\ge L'$, and all search/evaluation selections respect that constraint.

Concretely, follow the same policy used in the experiments. For searches targeting context windows up to 256k, the authors used 5 random samples from PG19 validation set, each sample satisfying the minimum length requirement for the target $L'$. For searches or evaluations beyond 512k they used fewer and larger examples (3 random samples drawn from Pile-Books3) to reduce evaluation cost. For sliding-window perplexity evaluation the experiments used different strides depending on dataset and length: for PG19 and Proof-pile they used a sliding window stride of 256 tokens (fine-grained evaluation across the document), while for the billion-token Books3 evaluation they used a very coarse stride (the paper used a “sliding window of 256k”) to make evaluation tractable.

For fine-tuning the extended model, the paper describes chunking long training corpora to the training length: for LLaMA2 fine-tuning they chunked RedPajama into 128k segments bookended with BOS/EOS tokens; for Mistral they used Together’s Long-Data Collections with 16k training length when training the 128k and 256k fine-tuned models (this choice influenced Mistral’s further extension behavior in the experiments). Use the dataset and chunking approach that matches your target fine-tuning length, and ensure the training chunks are large enough to contain the sequence length used by the search/evaluation pipeline.

# Typical search hyperparameters ($P$, $N_1$/$N_2$, $p$, iterations) and cost control for $>512$k

The paper gives concrete, experimentally-validated search defaults for the evolutionary search used to find per-dimension $\lambda_i$ and token threshold $\hat{n}$. For target window sizes within 256k the authors used:
\[
P = 64,\quad N_1=N_2=16,\quad p=0.3,\quad \mathcal{T}=40,
\]
and they selected the top-32 individuals each iteration for mutation/crossover. Perplexity during search was computed on 5 random PG19 validation samples with a minimum length requirement matching the target $L'$. The search space allowed $\lambda_i$ values from $1.0$ up to $s\times 1.25$ in steps of $0.01$ (where $s=L'/L$ is the extension ratio), and $\hat{n}$ was allowed to take values from the set $\{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}$.

To control cost for larger target lengths (windows over 512k), the paper halves the population and the mutation/crossover sizes and also reduces the number of validation samples (they computed perplexity on 3 random Books3 samples for these very large searches). Two additional efficiency measures used in every search are important in practice: (1) initialize the population with the three known baselines (PI, NTK, YaRN) and then randomly mutate them to fill the initial pool, and (2) enforce the monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ to avoid evaluating large numbers of pathological candidates. Both measures substantially lower the number of candidate evaluations required.

Because each perplexity evaluation at extreme $L'$ is expensive, use these levers exactly as the paper does: reduce $P$, $N_1$, $N_2$, the number of validation samples, and prefer more guided initialization (include PI/NTK/YaRN seeds) for very large searches. The paper’s exact settings are a practical starting point for reproducing their results.

# Troubleshooting: detecting perplexity spikes, relaxing/strengthening constraints, adjusting seeds and mutation rates

Detecting problems in an extension typically begins by plotting validation perplexity as a function of evaluation context length and comparing against the known baselines (PI, NTK, YaRN). In the paper, failure modes are visible as abrupt spikes in validation perplexity past a particular length (this was observed for PI and some other baselines when extending beyond small factors). Use the same sliding-window perplexity evaluations as during search to locate the spike point.

If you observe a perplexity spike, the paper suggests several remedies that are consistent with their method and experiments. First, check the role of the token-position non-uniformity (the threshold $\hat{n}$). The authors found that preserving the original RoPE for the initial $\hat{n}$ tokens (i.e., setting $m(i,n)=1$ for $n<\hat{n}$) often improves robustness; increasing $\hat{n}$ can reduce spikes caused by catastrophic positional changes at early token positions. Second, for short-window performance degradation after extreme extension, perform a targeted re-search for short-context rescale factors: the paper demonstrates that reducing the maximum allowed $\lambda$ in the search (encouraging less interpolation) and running an extra evolution search for 4k/8k lengths recovers short-window performance. Third, if the search appears to be getting stuck in poor local minima, increase exploration by raising the mutation probability $p$ or increasing mutation/crossover pool sizes $N_1$, $N_2$, or the population $P$—but be aware that these increases come with linearly more validation work. The paper’s defaults ($p=0.3$, $P=64$, $N_1=N_2=16$) are a practical balance; deviations are legitimate tuning levers if you have more compute.

Two additional practical points used in the paper’s search procedure help avoid common failures. Always include known baselines (PI, NTK, YaRN) in the initial population so the search starts from sensible points; and enforce the monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i\le\lambda_{i+1}$ to remove many implausible candidates before running expensive perplexity evaluations. If you must relax monotonicity to explore more exotic patterns, do so cautiously and expect larger search cost.

Finally, when pursuing the full progressive pipeline (first search and fine-tune at 128k/256k, then run the secondary search on the fine-tuned model to reach 2048k), follow the paper’s fine-tuning schedule as a template. For LLaMA2 the authors fine-tuned 400 steps with the 128k rescale factors and then an additional 600 steps after switching to the 256k rescale factors; for Mistral they used a 400-step schedule with a 16k training length following YaRN’s recipe. If the secondary extension fails to produce stable perplexity, consider using the smaller intermediate extension ratio (e.g., prefer a 256k fine-tune rather than 128k when resources permit) because the smaller secondary rescale jump is easier to bridge in the paper’s ablations.

Summary and practical checklist (aligned with the paper)
Begin by implementing the per-dimension multiplier $m(i,n)$ in your RoPE computation so you can apply a vector $\lambda$ and threshold $\hat{n}$. Prepare long documents (each sample must have length $\ge L'$) and choose sliding-window strides that match evaluation budget (PG19 used stride 256; Books3 used a coarse stride of 256k in the paper). Run the evolutionary search with the paper’s defaults ($P=64,N_1=N_2=16,p=0.3,\mathcal{T}=40$) and the monotonicity constraint; include PI/NTK/YaRN seeds in the initial population. For very large $L'$ (over 512k) halve population/mutation sizes and reduce the number of validation samples. If you need a fully fine-tuned 2048k model follow the progressive pipeline in the paper (search for 128k/256k, fine-tune (400+600 steps used for LLaMA2), then run a secondary search on the fine-tuned checkpoint to produce the final 2048k profile). If short-window performance degrades, run an extra short-length search with a reduced maximum $\lambda$ to recover original behavior.

Experimental Results: Language Modeling and Retrieval

This chapter summarizes the experimental evidence reported for LongRoPE on two representative 7B-scale LLMs (LLaMA2-7B and Mistral-7B). The experiments measure three complementary properties that characterize long-context performance: (1) language-modeling quality (perplexity) across increasing evaluation context lengths, (2) retrieval ability on a synthetic "passkey" task that stresses long-range memory, and (3) preservation of performance on standard short-context benchmarks. Where relevant I repeat the evaluation protocols used in the paper (datasets, sliding-window evaluation, and the main baselines) and then explain the observed trends and their causes as presented by the authors.

# Within 256k: decreasing perplexity trends on PG19 and Proof-pile; outperforming strong baselines

Setup and evaluation protocol. To assess language modeling quality within the intermediate long-range regime, LongRoPE-extended models were evaluated on PG19 and Proof-pile test splits. Perplexity was computed with a sliding-window evaluation (a window stride of 256 tokens for PG19 and Proof-pile). For PG19 the full test set of 100 documents was used; for Proof-pile the paper reports results on 10 long samples (each at least $128\mathrm{k}$ tokens). The paper compares LongRoPE variants against strong baselines that include PI (linear positional interpolation), NTK-based schemes, YaRN, and several open-source long-context models (Together-32k, Code LLaMA, LongLoRA-full-FT-100k, YaRN-LLaMA, YaRN-Mistral).

Observed patterns. Across both datasets, LongRoPE exhibits an overall decreasing perplexity trend as evaluation length increases from the original short window (e.g., $4\mathrm{k}$) up to $256\mathrm{k}$. That is, the models are able to use longer context to reduce uncertainty rather than suffering catastrophic spikes in loss. Importantly, the LongRoPE-extended models outperform the cited state-of-the-art baselines within the same range, even though LongRoPE models are evaluated with context windows that are often much longer than those used during baseline fine-tuning (the paper emphasizes that LongRoPE-2048k models still outperform baselines inside the $256\mathrm{k}$ range).

Why this happens (intuition grounded in the paper). The paper attributes the gains to two key mechanisms in LongRoPE: (1) the search for non-uniform rescale factors across RoPE dimensions and token positions preserves the most informative dimensions and the RoPE encoding of early tokens, which reduces the information loss introduced by naive interpolation; (2) the progressive extension strategy (search → fine-tune at moderate long lengths such as $128\mathrm{k}$/$256\mathrm{k}$ → second search) provides a better initialization for the subsequent extension. Both effects make the extended model better able to exploit long context for language modeling, producing steadily decreasing perplexity across the $4\mathrm{k}\rightarrow256\mathrm{k}$ range.

# Beyond 2M (Books3): LLaMA2 maintains favorable perplexity; Mistral degrades past ~256k due to training setup

Dataset and evaluation. To stress extreme contexts, the authors evaluate on Books3, selecting 20 books each exceeding $2{,}048\mathrm{k}$ tokens, and they measure perplexity using a sliding window of $256\mathrm{k}$ tokens. This setup aims to simulate real documents that are multiple millions of tokens long and to verify robustness well beyond the usual LLM window sizes.

LLaMA2 results. LongRoPE applied to LLaMA2-7B produces models that can be used up to $2{,}048\mathrm{k}$ tokens and maintain favorable perplexity trends. For LLaMA2 the reported pattern is a general decrease in perplexity as more context becomes available, with only marginal increases observed at the largest evaluation points (notably at $1{,}024\mathrm{k}$ and $2{,}048\mathrm{k}$). Between the two progressive fine-tuning choices the LLaMA2-2048k model fine-tuned up to $256\mathrm{k}$ performs better than the model fine-tuned only to $128\mathrm{k}$; the paper attributes this to a smaller secondary extension ratio in the former case ($8\times$ extension from $256\mathrm{k}\rightarrow2{,}048\mathrm{k}$ versus $16\times$ from $128\mathrm{k}\rightarrow2{,}048\mathrm{k}$).

Mistral results and explanation. Mistral-7B extended with LongRoPE shows stronger performance than many baselines at shorter long-context lengths, but its perplexity grows substantially beyond $256\mathrm{k}$ evaluation length (the paper notes Mistral's perplexity exceeding 7 beyond $256\mathrm{k}$). The authors explain this behavior as an artifact of the Mistral fine-tuning setup used in their experiments: for Mistral they followed YaRN's fine-tuning configuration that uses a $16\mathrm{k}$ training length, and this choice limits Mistral's ability to generalize its positional encoding to extremely large windows during the subsequent extension. Consequently, Mistral’s best-performing LongRoPE variant for extreme lengths came from the model fine-tuned to $128\mathrm{k}$ (rather than $256\mathrm{k}$) under the particular training recipe used.

Takeaway. The Books3 results show LongRoPE can realize extremely large context windows in practice for models where the intermediate fine-tuning and training-length choices support that scaling (LLaMA2 being the clear example in the reported experiments). Where the fine-tuning recipe limits exposure to sufficiently long training chunks (as with Mistral under the used configuration), performance degrades beyond the range best represented during fine-tuning.

# Passkey retrieval: high accuracy to million-token scales (≥90% up to 2048k for LLaMA2; Mistral 100% to ~1800k then ~60% at 2048k)

Task and protocol. The passkey retrieval task is a synthetic, probing evaluation that measures a model’s ability to locate and output a short secret (a five-digit number) hidden somewhere inside a long document. For each evaluation length the passkey is placed uniformly at a random position across the context. The paper runs 10 iterations per condition and reports retrieval accuracy (whether the model outputs the exact passkey). The prompt template and exact formatting are described in the paper’s appendix; here we only summarize the core findings the authors report.

Results summary. Off-the-shelf extended models and several baselines show a steep falloff in retrieval accuracy beyond around $128\mathrm{k}$ of context, often dropping to zero. In contrast, LongRoPE-extended LLaMA2-7B (the $2{,}048\mathrm{k}$ model fine-tuned to $256\mathrm{k}$ and then extended) maintains very high retrieval accuracy: the paper reports accuracy at or above $90\%$ from $4\mathrm{k}$ up to $2{,}048\mathrm{k}$. LongRoPE-Mistral-2048k (fine-tuned at $128\mathrm{k}$ in the reported experiments) achieves perfect retrieval up to approximately $1{,}800\mathrm{k}$, but accuracy falls to about $60\%$ at $2{,}048\mathrm{k}$—a decline that is consistent with the perplexity degradation noted above for Mistral at extreme evaluation lengths.

Interpretation. These results demonstrate that the LongRoPE procedure does more than reduce average perplexity: it preserves the model’s capacity to access distant tokens reliably for direct retrieval-style tasks. The paper attributes this to the combination of (i) non-uniform RoPE rescaling that protects key dimensions and early-token encodings (so the model can still attend sharply to important tokens), and (ii) the progressive fine-tuning plus second-search recipe that avoids directly optimizing from $4\mathrm{k}\rightarrow2{,}048\mathrm{k}$ in one step. The close correspondence between retrieval accuracy and perplexity behavior (high accuracy when perplexity remains low and steady; drops when perplexity rises) supports the conclusion that LongRoPE yields usable long-range memory in practice.

# Short-context benchmarks (ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA): largely comparable to base models; small drops, occasional gains

Benchmarks and protocol. To verify that extreme context extension does not catastrophically harm the original, short-context abilities of the models, the paper evaluates LongRoPE-extended models on standard benchmarks drawn from the Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard. The exact evaluation settings used are: 25-shot ARC-Challenge, 10-shot HellaSwag, 5-shot MMLU, and 0-shot TruthfulQA. These tasks probe reasoning, commonsense, factuality, and robustness under few-shot/zero-shot settings that are typical for LLM evaluation.

Reported outcomes. LongRoPE-2048k models remain largely comparable to the original base models on these benchmarks. The authors report only modest performance changes: small accuracy drops in some tasks and occasional improvements in others. A specific example given is that the Mistral LongRoPE variant outperforms the original Mistral on TruthfulQA by about $+0.5\%$. The LongRoPE-LLaMA2-2048k model that was fine-tuned to $256\mathrm{k}$ shows slightly more degradation on some benchmarks compared to smaller fine-tuning targets, but the drop remains within reasonable bounds.

Mechanisms to preserve short-context behavior. The paper explains that positional interpolation at very large extension ratios can “crowd” the original short-range positional region, hurting performance on tasks designed for short contexts. To mitigate this, LongRoPE includes a short-context recovery stage: after the large extension is produced, the authors run an additional evolution-based search that limits the maximum rescale factors (encouraging less interpolation) for short lengths such as $4\mathrm{k}$ and $8\mathrm{k}$. During inference, the model uses the RoPE rescale factors appropriate for the current sequence length (for sequences below about $8\mathrm{k}$ the adjusted factors are used). This dynamic adjustment recovers much of the short-context performance while retaining the extreme-length capability.

# $8\times$ no-fine-tune extension: smooth perplexity vs PI/NTK/YaRN baselines

What “no-fine-tune” means here. One attractive property reported for LongRoPE is that, because the search discovers non-uniform rescalings that preserve influential RoPE components, it can extend a pretrained model’s usable context in some cases without any additional fine-tuning. This is different from approaches that require long-length fine-tuning to avoid catastrophic behavior on new token positions.

Empirical demonstration. On LLaMA2-7B, the LongRoPE search yields RoPE rescale factors that allow extension from $4\mathrm{k}$ up to $32\mathrm{k}$ ($8\times$) without any fine-tuning. In contrast, the paper reports that simpler schemes such as PI, NTK-based interpolation, and YaRN exhibit large perplexity spikes when pushed beyond about $2\times$ their original context length. Figure-level comparisons in the paper show LongRoPE producing a much smoother perplexity curve across the $4\mathrm{k}\rightarrow32\mathrm{k}$ range.

Why this is significant. Extending a pretrained model's context window by $8\times$ without fine-tuning can be a powerful practical option: it avoids the high compute and data requirements of direct long-length fine-tuning while still delivering a usable improvement in context capacity. According to the authors, the key technical reason LongRoPE can do this is that the evolutionary search finds rescale factor patterns that are non-uniform both across RoPE dimensions and across token positions, thereby preserving high-frequency dimensions and early tokens that matter most for attention and local discrimination.

# Summary and practical implications

Across the experiments reported in the paper, LongRoPE demonstrates three complementary strengths:

- It reduces perplexity monotonically or steadily as evaluation length grows up to at least $256\mathrm{k}$ and, for well-configured fine-tuning (LLaMA2 in the experiments), up to $2{,}048\mathrm{k}$. This indicates that the model uses long context effectively rather than being confused by new positions.
- It preserves functional long-range memory demonstrated by the passkey retrieval task: LongRoPE-LLaMA2-2048k sustains $\ge 90\%$ retrieval accuracy up to $2{,}048\mathrm{k}$ in the reported runs, while LongRoPE-Mistral-2048k shows strong performance until training-recipe limits appear.
- It keeps short-context behavior near the original model performance through an explicit short-context recovery search and by dynamically choosing RoPE rescale factors at inference time.

The experiments also highlight a practical caveat: the quality of extreme-length extension depends on the intermediate fine-tuning recipe (sequence lengths used during fine-tuning matter). The Mistral results demonstrate that if the fine-tuning exposure to long chunks is limited (e.g., training length $16\mathrm{k}$ in the reported Mistral setup), then the model’s ability to generalize to multi-hundred-thousand or million-token windows is impaired relative to a model that received longer fine-tuning chunks (as with the LLaMA2 experiments reported).

Taken together, the reported results position LongRoPE as a practical and effective approach to extend existing RoPE-based LLMs to very large contexts (including multi-million-token windows) while preserving short-context competence—provided the intermediate fine-tuning choices are compatible with the final extension target.

Ablations and Deeper Analysis

# Second interpolation vs. PI/YaRN when scaling 256k → 512k/1024k/2048k

When we extend a model's context window progressively, a key step is the secondary positional interpolation performed after a moderate-length fine-tuning stage. In LongRoPE this appears as a second search for non-uniform RoPE rescale factors on a model already fine-tuned at 256k, with the goal of reaching 512k, 1024k, and ultimately 2048k without further gradient steps. The ablation reported in the paper compares three approaches for this second stage: (1) plain linear positional interpolation (PI), (2) YaRN-style grouped interpolation, and (3) LongRoPE's searched, per-dimension (and optionally per-position) rescale factors. The empirical finding is clear: LongRoPE's second interpolation maintains a consistently low perplexity across the secondary extension steps, whereas PI and YaRN exhibit rapidly increasing perplexity as the extension ratio grows (Table~\ref{tbl:secondsearch}).

Why does the searched second interpolation succeed where PI and YaRN fail? There are two interacting reasons grounded in the mechanics of RoPE and the fine-tuning dynamics. First, fine-tuning at a long intermediate length (here 256k reached using 400 + 600 steps in the experiments) adapts the model parameters to a particular positional encoding geometry. A uniform or group-wise rescaling (PI / YaRN) treats many RoPE dimensions the same (or coarsely grouped), which can erase or distort the per-dimension geometry the model learned during fine-tuning. Second, LongRoPE's search finds a per-dimension rescale $\lambda_i$ (and an optional early-token threshold $\hat{n}$) that preserves the most important frequency components and the most sensitive initial token angles, providing an initialization that avoids the "catastrophic values" introduced by crude interpolation. In practice this enables going from a 256k fine-tuned model to a 2048k usable model with no extra fine-tuning, accomplishing a final effective extension ratio of $512\times$ (from a 4k base) while keeping perplexity stable.

Operationally, achieving this requires the search to be computationally tractable. The paper enforces a monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ (motivated by NTK theory) and initializes the evolutionary population with PI/NTK/YaRN candidates to accelerate convergence. These design choices make the secondary search efficient enough to run on the fine-tuned checkpoint and produce rescale factors that are compatible with the model’s learned weights.

# Short-context recovery effectiveness: improved 4k/8k perplexity and average benchmark accuracy

A recurring phenomenon when extending context windows to extreme lengths is that short-context performance can degrade. Mechanistically this is described as "crowding": after large interpolation factors, positional rotations for positions that used to be distinct are mapped into a much tighter region of angle space, making nearby positions harder to distinguish and harming next-token prediction in short windows. LongRoPE explicitly addresses this by running a focused search to recover short-context behavior: the algorithm restricts the maximum allowable $\lambda$ during a re-search for short lengths (e.g., 4k and 8k), encouraging less aggressive interpolation, and produces a small set of RoPE rescale factors specialized for short inputs. At inference time LongRoPE dynamically switches to these short-length rescale factors when the sequence length is below a threshold (the paper uses 8k).

The ablations demonstrate that this targeted recovery step yields substantial improvements in short-length perplexity (Table~\ref{tbl:shortperformance}) and recovers much of the lost accuracy on standard benchmarks that are designed around the original context window. Concretely, LongRoPE-2048k models after short-context readjustment obtain markedly better perplexity at 4k and 8k windows, and maintain comparable zero-/few-shot accuracy on tasks from the Open LLM Leaderboard. The paper further reports a concrete example where LongRoPE models are competitive on short-window benchmarks: the Mistral-based LongRoPE model even slightly improves TruthfulQA performance by approximately $+0.5\%$ relative to the original Mistral under the evaluation protocols used.

Intuitively, the recovery step works because the cost of maintaining a single set of rescale factors for all lengths is too high: the same rescale that helps encode millions of tokens may over-compress short-range position encodings. By constraining the search space (reducing the allowed maximum $\lambda$) and performing a dedicated search for short windows, LongRoPE preserves the fidelity of local position distinctions without changing the model weights. The practical takeaway is that dynamic RoPE selection (long-rescale for very long inputs, short-rescale for inputs below some threshold) is a low-cost and effective strategy to avoid harming the model's original short-context capabilities.

# Contributions of non-uniformities: dimension-only vs. dimension+position; dependence on target length

A central hypothesis of LongRoPE is that positional interpolation should not be uniform: two distinct kinds of non-uniformity matter. The first is across RoPE dimensions (different rotation frequencies should be rescaled differently), and the second is across token positions (the initial tokens may deserve less interpolation). The paper ablates these two effects in two regimes: modest extensions (to 16k and 32k) and the extreme extension chain leading to 2048k.

The ablation results show that the dimension-wise non-uniformity alone already yields a large gain over PI. Searching per-dimension rescale factors $\lambda_i$ reduces perplexity substantially compared to uniform scaling, which aligns with NTK-based intuition that low-index (high-frequency) dimensions encode fine-grained relative positions and thus should be altered less. Adding the token-position non-uniformity (preserving the first $\hat{n}$ tokens by setting their rescale to 1) gives another measurable improvement for the moderate extensions (16k and 32k). This supports the idea that the very first tokens are particularly sensitive: they often attract large attention weights and play outsized roles in attention computations, so perturbing their angular encoding hurts performance more than perturbing later tokens.

However, the ablation also reveals a dependence on the target length. For the extreme final extension to 2048k, preserving just the initial tokens without interpolation does not bring the same benefit and in the experiments becomes "non-useful" (Table~\ref{tbl:searchdimension}). The paper notes this and leaves it to future work to better explain or address why early-token preservation fades at extreme ratios. Possible interpretations consistent with the paper’s observations are that (a) when the extension ratio is enormous, the relative scale of an un-interpolated prefix becomes negligible compared to the entire sequence and (b) the model’s adapted attention patterns after long-length fine-tuning change the sensitivity profile across positions. The empirical prescription follows: always include dimension-wise, per-frequency rescaling in the search; include an early-token preservation parameter $\hat{n}$ when your target extension is moderate, but validate its impact when pushing to million-token scales.

# Effect of fine-tuning window size (128k vs 256k) on final 2048k performance

LongRoPE's progressive strategy splits the total extension into two stages: (i) extend and fine-tune at an intermediate length $L_{\text{ft}}$ (experimented with 128k and 256k), and (ii) perform a second search on the fine-tuned model to reach the final target (2048k). An important ablation concerns the choice of $L_{\text{ft}}$ because that determines the secondary extension ratio $s_{\text{sec}} = \frac{L_{\text{final}}}{L_{\text{ft}}}$ (for example, $256\text{k} \to 2048\text{k}$ is $s_{\text{sec}}=8$, while $128\text{k} \to 2048\text{k}$ is $s_{\text{sec}}=16$).

Empirically, the paper finds that LLaMA2 benefits from choosing $L_{\text{ft}} = 256\text{k}$: the resulting LongRoPE-2048k (ft=256k) model outperforms its (ft=128k) counterpart. The explanation given is straightforward: a smaller secondary extension ratio (8× versus 16×) is easier to realize with a post-finetuning search and produces less catastrophic shifts in the RoPE geometry. For Mistral the pattern is different: the Mistral-based LongRoPE models achieve better final performance when fine-tuned at 128k. The paper attributes this to differences in the fine-tuning regime: in the Mistral experiments the 128k and 256k fine-tuning followed YaRN's settings which used a training sequence length of 16k, and this choice interacted with Mistral's adaptation ability during fine-tuning and thus affected the eventual extendability.

The practical conclusion is that the best intermediate fine-tuning length is not universally fixed: you should attempt to minimize the secondary extension ratio when possible, but also account for your fine-tuning regime (training sequence length, number of gradient steps, learning rate, batch size). In the experiments LongRoPE achieves the 2048k target with as few as 1k fine-tuning steps distributed across the intermediate lengths (400 steps to 128k and 600 steps to 256k for LLaMA2), but which intermediate length gives the best end result depends on model and training choices.

# Observed limitations: early-token preservation less impactful at extreme lengths; model- and training-regime sensitivity

The ablations reveal several practical limitations and sensitivities that are important when using LongRoPE or interpreting its results.

First, as already emphasized, the benefit of preserving an initial prefix of tokens (token-position non-uniformity) shrinks as the target context length becomes extremely large. The search experiments demonstrate that preserving the first $\hat{n}$ tokens helps at low-to-moderate extensions but does not improve, and may become irrelevant, at 2048k. The paper explicitly notes this as an open issue: "preserving only the initial tokens without interpolation becomes non-useful" at extreme lengths, and the authors leave a deeper investigation for future work.

Second, there is model- and training-regime sensitivity. The LLaMA2 and Mistral experiments differ in how much fine-tuning length, learning rate, and training sequence length were used, and this affects final performance. Mistral, for example, outperforms baselines at shorter extended lengths but shows a higher perplexity beyond 256k (perplexity exceeding 7 in the experiments), whereas LLaMA2's perplexity decreases more gracefully with larger context. These differences show that LongRoPE's effectiveness depends in part on how the base model was fine-tuned (number of steps, dataset segmentation, and sequence length used during fine-tuning).

Third, computational and search-space limitations remain. The number of possible per-dimension rescale configurations explodes combinatorially with the number of RoPE dimensions and the granularity of $\lambda$ values; the paper addresses this with (a) an evolution search initialized with PI/NTK/YaRN seeds, (b) a monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ to reduce search overhead, and (c) reduced population sizes for extremely large target lengths (halving population, mutation, and crossover sizes for windows over 512k). These are pragmatic engineering choices that make the search feasible, but they also constrain the exploration space and may miss some better configurations. In addition, scarcity of extremely long texts and limited fine-tuning budget (the experiments use at most about 1k fine-tuning steps within the 256k training length) are practical limits: the method leverages clever initialization and search to avoid massive re-training, but that design choice also leaves residual sensitivity to training data and steps.

Taken together, these ablations and analyses show where LongRoPE's design is essential (per-dimension rescaling, progressive fine-tuning, short-context readjustment) and where practitioners must be cautious (early-token heuristics, model-specific training choices, search constraints). The clear empirical lesson is that per-dimension non-uniform interpolation is the single most robust contributor to successful extension; per-position preservation often helps but must be validated per-target-length; and the intermediate fine-tuning length should be chosen to minimize the subsequent extension ratio while respecting your training regime.

Related Work and Methodological Landscape

# Retrieval-augmented generation and external memory: applicability and trade-offs vs. in-model context

Retrieval-augmented generation and external-memory designs place the burden of long context outside the core Transformer by storing past context or large corpora in an external index and fetching relevant pieces at inference time. As described in the paper, these approaches typically introduce an additional retrieval module and an external memory component that the model consults when producing outputs. The core model usually remains similar, but the overall system requires explicit architectural or pipeline-level modifications to support indexing, retrieval, and integration of retrieved passages into generation.

The paper highlights a key practical contrast between retrieval-based solutions and LongRoPE-style in-model extensions. Retrieval systems explicitly modify system architecture, whereas LongRoPE keeps the model architecture largely intact and only changes how positional information is represented (minor modifications to RoPE positional embedding). In the authors' words, LongRoPE is “more lightweight, with minor positional embedding modifications,” and it is able to address tasks beyond pure retrieval — for example, long-document summarization and few-shot in-context learning — by extending the model’s native context window rather than relying on external lookups.

This distinction implies a trade-off in capabilities and engineering complexity that the paper stresses without making absolute claims about superiority. Retrieval architectures are naturally suited to scenarios where external, indexed knowledge or many distinct documents must be accessed on demand; they decouple storage from the model. By contrast, LongRoPE and other in-model extension methods aim to give the Transformer itself a much larger contiguous workspace without adding separate components. The paper positions LongRoPE as an in-model alternative that is easy to deploy (minor positional changes) and broadly applicable to tasks that demand direct, contiguous access to very long sequences.

# Attention-masking strategies (e.g., StreamingLLM, LM-Infinite, parallel windows) vs. positional scaling

A different family of approaches extends effective context by changing how attention is computed rather than changing positional encodings or adding an external memory. The paper groups these together as attention-based context window extension methods. Their central idea is to exploit attention mask design or attention-scheduling strategies so that a model can process longer inputs while staying within its original architectural and positional-encoding limits. The cited examples (StreamingLLM, LM-Infinite, parallel-window approaches) fall into this category: they manipulate attention to prevent what the paper refers to as attention explosion and to make long inputs manageable within the model’s native attention mechanism.

Positional-scaling approaches (positional interpolation, NTK-aware rescaling, YaRN, and LongRoPE) address a different but related problem: when tokens appear at positions the model was never trained on, their RoPE-based positional encodings can produce out-of-distribution rotary angles that harm next-token prediction. Attention-masking strategies primarily alter which tokens attend to which other tokens and when, whereas positional-scaling methods alter the mapping from absolute or relative position indices to the rotary angles used by attention. The paper emphasizes this complementary relationship explicitly: attention-mask methods mitigate the practical problem of attention spreading thinly across many tokens, while positional interpolation methods mitigate the "catastrophic values" that come from introducing many untrained new positions.

There is additional conceptual overlap emphasized in the paper. For example, prior work such as StreamingLLM and LM-Infinite observed that the earliest tokens in a stream often attract large attention weights and are especially important; LongRoPE incorporates this observation by allowing the first $\hat{n}$ token positions to remain un-interpolated (i.e., to keep their original RoPE angles) when searching for rescale factors. Thus, the attention-focused insights around token importance can inform positional-scaling decisions, and vice versa. The paper frames these two families (attention masks vs. positional scaling) as complementary levers: they tackle different parts of the long-context problem and can be used together in system design even though the paper does not present combined experiments.

# Large-base RoPE and long-context fine-tuning (Code LLaMA, LLaMA2-Long, ScaledRoPE)

A straightforward line of prior work for expanding context is to change the RoPE parameterization before or during fine-tuning. Methods such as Code LLaMA, LLaMA2-Long, and ScaledRoPE fall into this class: they select a large base value for RoPE (changing the frequency schedule of the rotary angles) and then fine-tune the model on longer sequences so that the model learns to operate under the new angular regime. Conceptually, this is direct reparameterization plus supervised adaptation: alter the encoder so positions compress differently across dimensions, and then expose the model to long sequences during fine-tuning so it can learn to use those altered encodings.

The paper points out why this approach is attractive but also why it is challenging at extreme lengths. First, fine-tuning to very long lengths is expensive in compute and requires long-sequence training data that are often scarce. Second, naively changing positional encodings can still introduce “catastrophic values” for many new positions if the reparameterization is not chosen carefully, making convergence or stability during fine-tuning difficult. LongRoPE is presented as an alternative that combines careful initialization (a searched, non-uniform rescaling of RoPE) with a progressive, resource-aware fine-tuning strategy. Instead of directly attempting to fine-tune at the final, enormous target length, LongRoPE searches for per-dimension rescale factors (and an initial token cutoff) that preserve important parts of the original RoPE, fine-tunes to a moderate long length (256k in the paper), and then applies a second non-uniform interpolation step to reach 2048k without further heavy fine-tuning.

Thus, compared to “large-base RoPE + full fine-tuning,” LongRoPE emphasizes better initialization (via evolutionary search over per-dimension $\lambda_i$ and a threshold $\hat{n}$), progressive extension, and short-context recovery procedures to preserve performance on small windows. This combination is what enables LongRoPE to claim very large final windows (2048k) while keeping real fine-tuning effort modest (the paper reports up to only 1k fine-tuning steps within a 256k training length as part of the progressive plan).

# Compute-efficient long-context fine-tuning (LongLoRA, PoSE) and complementarity with LongRoPE

Because training on very long sequences is expensive, a set of recent techniques focuses on making the fine-tuning step itself more efficient. The paper cites LongLoRA and PoSE as representative methods that reduce the overhead of fine-tuning for long contexts. These methods are introduced in the paper as orthogonal to LongRoPE: they address the cost and parameter-efficiency of adapting model weights to long contexts, whereas LongRoPE focuses on smarter positional initialization (per-dimension, position-aware rescaling) and on a progressive extension strategy that reduces the need for direct fine-tuning at the final long length.

“Orthogonal” here means that the two sets of ideas operate on different parts of the pipeline and can be composed in principle. LongRoPE provides a better positional-encoding initialization and a way to reach very large context windows with minimal fine-tuning; LongLoRA and PoSE aim to make whatever fine-tuning is necessary cheaper and more parameter-efficient. The paper explicitly frames LongRoPE as compatible with these approaches: because LongRoPE mainly changes RoPE rescale factors and retains the rest of the model architecture, an efficient fine-tuning adapter (e.g., LoRA-style low-rank updates or other PoSE-style techniques) could be used on top of the LongRoPE-initialized model to further reduce fine-tuning compute. The paper does not report experiments combining them, but it highlights this complementarity as a practical direction: LongRoPE handles position-encoding design and initialization, while LongLoRA/PoSE handle efficient parameter updates.

# Combining LongRoPE with retrieval/memory and advanced attention mechanisms

One of the paper’s recurring themes is that different long-context strategies tackle distinct failure modes of applying a pretrained LLM to ultra-long sequences: untrained positional indices (handled by positional interpolation), attention distribution and scaling issues (handled by attention-mask strategies), and external knowledge or very large document collections (handled by retrieval). The paper explicitly calls positional-interpolation methods and attention-based methods complementary, and treats retrieval as a different engineering choice that typically requires architectural changes.

Because LongRoPE is implemented as a relatively small change to the positional encoding (per-dimension rescale factors and a small runtime choice for short-vs-long windows) and keeps the rest of the model intact, it is positioned as a modular tool that can be combined with other families of methods. The paper notes that LongRoPE “retains the original architecture with minor modifications to the positional embedding, and can reuse most pre-existing optimizations,” which implies that integrating LongRoPE with an attention-scheduling strategy or with a retrieval pipeline is feasible from a systems perspective.

The paper does not present combined empirical studies, so it stops short of claiming specific benefits from particular combinations. However, it motivates the following conceptual point grounded in its results: because LongRoPE reduces the positional-interpolation-induced information loss (by exploiting non-uniformities across RoPE dimensions and token positions), it can provide a more stable in-model backbone on top of which attention-sparsity schemes or retrieval modules can operate. For example, attention-masking strategies that aim to limit where and when tokens attend can focus on computational tractability, while LongRoPE ensures that the tokens that are attended still carry position encodings that remain interpretable to the pretrained model. Likewise, a retrieval component can be used alongside LongRoPE in scenarios where not every piece of external information needs to be present in the contiguous in-model context; LongRoPE would then make the contiguous in-model context more trustworthy for those cases where such a large contiguous span is necessary.

Overall, the paper situates LongRoPE as a practical, RoPE-focused lever in the broader methodological landscape: it is lightweight, capable of extreme single-model context extension (2048k in the paper), and designed to play well with other strategies that address orthogonal bottlenecks (attention scaling and retrieval). The recommended view from the paper is not that any single method is universally best, but that LongRoPE addresses the specific problem of positional extrapolation and initialization effectively, and that its design and modest engineering footprint make it an attractive building block to combine with retrieval and attention-based techniques when designing systems for very long-context applications.

Limitations, Broader Impacts, and Future Directions

# Compute and data considerations for million-token contexts (search and evaluation costs)

LongRoPE’s core technical claim is striking: extending RoPE-based LLMs’ context windows up to 2,048k tokens while using relatively little fine‑tuning (on the order of 1k steps within a 256k training length). That achievement depends on two complementary pieces that are computationally nontrivial in practice: (1) an evolutionary search that identifies non‑uniform rescale factors per RoPE dimension and a starting‑position threshold, and (2) the fine‑tuning and evaluation needed to verify and deploy those factors. The paper highlights several concrete compute and data realities that any practitioner must face.

First, the search space is astronomically large if explored naively. The paper gives an explicit example: even modest extension ratios lead to combinatorially huge choices (for a 4× extension the authors cite a search combinatorics of magnitude ≈4×10^{167}). That motivates the evolution search with constraints (monotonicity across dimensions) and carefully chosen initialization. In concrete terms, LongRoPE’s configuration for searches within 256k used a population size $P=64$ and iteration count $\mathcal{T}=40$, which corresponds to roughly $P\times\mathcal{T}=64\times40=2560$ perplexity evaluations in the canonical search run. For even larger targets (windows beyond 512k) the paper halves those population parameters to keep evaluation work tractable. Each such evaluation is itself nontrivial because, as the authors emphasize, computing perplexity on very long target windows is time‑consuming; the paper therefore evaluates against a small set of long samples (5 PG19 validation samples for within‑256k searches, 3 Pile‑Books3 samples for larger windows). These choices show that LongRoPE treats search as an expensive, sample‑based, approximate procedure rather than an exhaustive optimization.

Second, the fine‑tuning effort—while relatively small in update steps compared to naive direct fine‑tuning at the final target length—is nevertheless heavy in resource requirements because long sequences demand more memory and distributed resources. The progressive strategy in the paper fine‑tunes to 128k (400 steps) and then 256k (an additional 600 steps), i.e., about 1k steps total for the 256k fine‑tuning milestone. However, the hardware footprint is nontrivial: the 128k stage used 8 A100 GPUs and the 256k stage required 16 A100 GPUs for LLaMA2 experiments (the authors used different, smaller setups for Mistral). Thus LongRoPE reduces wall‑clock training steps at the final length but does not eliminate the need for substantial GPU memory and distributed training infrastructure. The paper intentionally favors a progressive path because direct fine‑tuning at 2,048k would be both harder to converge and far more expensive.

Third, data availability and preprocessing are limiting factors. The paper explicitly calls out the scarcity of naturally occurring documents that exceed very large lengths (e.g., >1,000k tokens). LongRoPE copes by chunking corpora (e.g., RedPajama) into long segments for fine‑tuning and by using existing long‑document collections (Together’s Long‑Data Collections, Books3 / Pile‑Books3) for evaluation. The limited supply of truly long documents also motivates the progressive design: one can fine‑tune at logically intermediate lengths (128k / 256k) where data and compute are more manageable and then rely on the searched non‑uniform interpolation to reach 2,048k without direct training at that length.

Finally, the authors give several practical knobs that reduce compute demand in practice: restricting the search range for each per‑dimension rescale factor (the paper searches $\lambda_i$ in $[1.0,\, s\times 1.25]$ with step $0.01$), imposing a monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$, seeding the initial evolution population with PI/NTK/YaRN solutions instead of random starts, and using small numbers of long samples to estimate perplexity during search. All of these are sensible tradeoffs between search thoroughness and wall‑time. In short, LongRoPE is not “free” computationally — it shifts cost from brute‑force long‑length fine‑tuning into a careful search plus a moderate but hardware‑intensive fine‑tuning stage — but it makes the problem tractable on modern GPU clusters by design choices the paper documents.

# Risks: privacy exposure over long inputs, prompt injection across large spans, miscalibrated reliance on distant tokens

The paper’s Broader Impacts statement is compact: it acknowledges that the work aims to advance ML and that there are a variety of potential societal consequences but does not single out particular ones. Extending context windows from a few thousand tokens to multi‑million tokens, however, changes the set of realistic applications and therefore the risk surface. The paper’s technical claims and evaluation results suggest several broad risk directions that practitioners and policy teams should evaluate when deploying extremely long‑context models.

Privacy exposure over long inputs. A model that can process multi‑million token contexts can ingest entire corpora, archives, or combined datasets in a single forward pass. That capability raises straightforward privacy concerns if the long context contains personal data, credentials, or proprietary information. The paper itself motivates LongRoPE by enabling tasks like passkey retrieval and long document summarization; those same applications can inadvertently surface sensitive content if dataset curation and access controls are not strict. Because LongRoPE modifies only positional embeddings and retains the original architecture, it is relatively easy to deploy at scale, which increases the urgency of careful data governance.

Prompt injection and adversarial placement across long spans. With much longer contexts, an adversary can hide malicious prompts or instructions deep in a document or distribute them sparsely across many pages. The model’s ability to attend across extremely long spans makes it plausible that injected instructions placed far from the visible query could still influence generation. The paper demonstrates passkey retrieval across million‑token contexts, which is a useful capability for benign tasks but also shows that the model can find and act on signals buried in vast amounts of text. Defenders should therefore consider adversarial testing, robust prompt filtering, and mechanisms to prioritize or ignore parts of the context for safety‑critical deployments.

Calibration and relevance of distant context. LongRoPE’s experiments make a tradeoff explicit: when the attention budget is stretched over millions of tokens, attention weights can become “dispersed,” and that dispersion can degrade the model’s behavior on the originally intended short context. The paper documents this phenomenon and responds with a short‑context recovery step (search/adjust rescale factors for 4k and 8k) to mitigate the degradation. More generally, a model with an enormous context window can be misled to give undue weight to distant tokens that are tangential or irrelevant, producing overconfident outputs that rely on spurious distant evidence. Appropriate design choices (e.g., dynamic RoPE selection depending on query length, attention masking heuristics, or combining retrieval to surface only highly relevant documents) are practical mitigations the paper points toward indirectly by treating short‑context recovery as necessary.

Because the paper focuses on technical feasibility and shows how to preserve short‑context performance via a re‑search step, it provides partial mitigation directions. However, deploying million‑token LLMs in sensitive settings requires an explicit guardrail strategy: careful data curation, adversarial evaluation (including long‑range prompt injection tests), and conservative policies about what long contexts are permitted and how model outputs are validated.

# Scope: RoPE-based models; extensions to other positional schemes and to layer-/head-wise scaling

LongRoPE is explicitly built around rotary positional embeddings (RoPE). The method operationally searches per‑RoPE‑dimension rescale factors $\lambda_i$ and a token index threshold $\hat{n}$ and applies the rescaling to the RoPE rotary angles. The paper repeatedly emphasizes this dependence: its unified positional interpolation model, the search space, the monotonicity constraint across RoPE dimensions, and the experimental results all assume RoPE as the positional mechanism. Consequently, the scope of the published method is RoPE‑based LLMs; the paper states that LongRoPE “can be applied to any LLMs based on RoPE embedding” and that models extended via LongRoPE “retain the original architecture with minor modifications to the positional embedding.”

That focus is both a strength and a limitation. RoPE is widely used in modern LLMs, so LongRoPE has broad applicability in practice. At the same time, other positional schemes (absolute learned positional embeddings, ALiBi, T5‑style relative encodings, and others) encode position information differently; a per‑dimension angular rescaling approach is not directly meaningful for some of those schemes. Extending the conceptual idea of non‑uniform interpolation to other encodings is possible in principle, but the paper does not attempt or evaluate that adaptation.

Another explicit design choice in the paper is the granularity of rescaling: LongRoPE searches rescale factors per RoPE dimension and a global threshold $\hat{n}$ across all attention layers and heads. The method does not, in the present work, search different rescale schedules per layer or per attention head. That was a deliberate simplification that already produced large gains: exploiting non‑uniformity across RoPE dimensions and token positions proved effective. However, the paper’s findings naturally suggest a next level of granularity as a future direction: layer‑wise or head‑wise rescale factors could capture additional structure in how positional information is used throughout the Transformer stack. Because layer/head granularity would blow up the search space further, the paper’s emphasis on search efficiency and monotonic constraints becomes even more relevant for such extensions.

In short, LongRoPE is a RoPE‑targeted approach that deliberately focuses on dimension‑wise and position‑wise non‑uniformity. Applying similar ideas to non‑RoPE positional encodings or expanding the search to layer/head granularity remains an open, promising extension that would require both algorithmic and compute considerations beyond the present paper.

# Toward analytic or learned schedules to reduce search cost; adaptive per‑sequence $\hat{n}$ and $\lambda_i$

One of the paper’s central engineering contributions is a practical evolution search with two optimizations: seeding the initial population with PI/NTK/YaRN candidates and imposing a monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$. These choices dramatically reduce wasted evaluations in an otherwise intractable combinatorial space. Still, search is approximate and computationally nontrivial, and the paper’s own design and ablations suggest a research path aimed at replacing or augmenting heavy search with faster, possibly analytic or learned mechanisms.

Analytic schedules. The paper builds on NTK intuition and compares PI, NTK, and YaRN analytic or hand‑designed schemes. Those heuristics are relatively cheap but were shown to be suboptimal in many settings compared with the searched non‑uniform factors. A middle ground is attractive: derive refined analytic formulas that parameterize $\lambda_i$ as simple functions of the extension ratio $s$ and dimension index $i$ (for example, smooth functions motivated by the NTK spectral viewpoint). The search outputs from LongRoPE itself could be used to fit compact parametric forms of $\lambda_i(s,i)$; such fitted schedules would be inexpensive to compute at inference time and could serve as good initializers when some limited search or fine‑tuning is still desired.

Learned predictors and meta‑models. Another pragmatic strategy is to learn a small model that predicts effective $(\lambda_i,\hat{n})$ given a few inputs: the base model family, the target extension ratio $s=L'/L$, and perhaps a small set of local statistics about the sequence or task. The paper already demonstrates that PI/NTK/YaRN are useful initializers; a learned predictor could generalize across those families and provide better one‑shot guesses, dramatically reducing or eliminating evolution runs for routine deployments.

Adaptive per‑sequence schedules. LongRoPE already uses different RoPE rescale factor sets depending on inference length (the paper searches and deploys separate settings to recover short‑context performance for 4k and 8k lengths). That is a form of dynamic selection tied to global sequence length. A natural future direction is a more fine‑grained, content‑aware adaptation: choose $\hat{n}$ or even adjust a small subset of $\lambda_i$ dynamically based on per‑sequence signals (for example, attention distributions computed on a short prefix, metadata about the document, or a learned relevance score). Because the paper treats $\hat{n}$ as a scalar threshold and $\lambda_i$ as global per‑dimension factors, moving from global to per‑sequence adaptivity is technically straightforward but must be carefully constrained to avoid run‑time overhead and instability.

Finally, theoretical work that more sharply characterizes the tradeoff between preserving pretrained RoPE information and compressing large index ranges would also be useful. The paper’s empirical findings (e.g., lower RoPE dimensions are more fragile to aggressive interpolation, and initial positions should often be exempted from interpolation) give concrete inductive facts that can guide analytic approximations or small learned models to replace expensive searches.

# Integrating with recurrence/memory, hardware‑aware kernels, and efficient long‑context training recipes

LongRoPE situates itself among several families of approaches for achieving long context. The paper explicitly calls retrieval‑based and attention‑based context extension techniques “complementary” and notes that efficient fine‑tuning works (LongLoRA, PoSE) are orthogonal to their method. This observation suggests several practical integration and systems‑level directions.

Combining with recurrence and memory. The literature contains streaming and recurrence schemes (cited in the paper as Streaming LLM and LM‑Infinite) that avoid quadratic attention over the entire history by maintaining summarized state or recurrent caches. Because LongRoPE modifies only positional embeddings and preserves model weights and architecture, it can in principle be combined with recurrence/memory mechanisms: use LongRoPE to make position encodings meaningful across millions of tokens while using a memory or recurrence module to avoid computing full dense attention across all tokens at every forward pass. That combination can yield more scalable end‑to‑end pipelines where LongRoPE supplies consistent positional interpretation and the memory module supplies computational tractability.

Hardware‑aware kernels and attention implementations. The paper’s experiments use A100 clusters and distributed training infrastructure and explicitly point out the significant memory footprint required by long sequences (128k/256k). Because LongRoPE preserves the model architecture and changes only positional embeddings, it is compatible with software and kernel optimizations that target long sequences: fused attention kernels, tiled or streaming attention implementations, and memory‑slicing strategies. The authors note that LongRoPE “can reuse most pre‑existing optimizations,” which is an explicit invitation to pair the method with hardware‑aware kernels in order to bring practical training and inference costs down.

Efficient long‑context training recipes. The progressive extension recipe in the paper — search for intermediate lengths, fine‑tune at 128k and 256k with modest steps, then perform a second search to reach 2,048k without further fine‑tuning — is itself an efficient training recipe. That recipe can be combined with other techniques the paper references as orthogonal: adapter‑style fine‑tuning (LongLoRA), parameter‑efficient updates, or curriculum schedules that increase sequence length gradually during training. The paper also shows that training choices (for example, Mistral’s use of a 16k training length in YaRN settings) materially affect downstream extension performance; this underlines that training sequence length, batch size, and the exact progressive schedule are all important knobs to tune in practice.

Putting these pieces together, the most practical path to deployable million‑token models is an ecosystem approach: use LongRoPE’s rescaling ideas to make positional encoding robust to huge indices, combine with memory/recurrence or retrieval to limit the number of tokens attended densely at inference, and deploy attention kernels and distributed strategies that are engineered for very long sequences. The paper’s empirical work shows parts of this ecosystem are feasible; system and kernel work remain necessary to make large‑scale, real‑time deployments cost‑effective.

# Concluding thoughts and practical takeaways

LongRoPE expands what is technically feasible for RoPE‑based LLMs: careful, non‑uniform positional interpolation plus a progressive fine‑tuning strategy produces models that work well across a dramatically widened range of context lengths (from canonical 4k to 2,048k in the paper’s results). That capability opens many new research and application paths, but it also shifts where the practical friction lies. The key limitations the paper documents are (1) search complexity and evaluation cost, (2) scarcity of truly long training documents, and (3) the need to preserve short‑context behavior despite extreme extensions.

In terms of near‑term best practices the paper supports: prefer progressive extension rather than direct fine‑tuning at the final target length; seed any search with analytic heuristics (PI/NTK/YaRN) and impose sensible constraints (monotonicity) to reduce wasted evaluations; evaluate search candidates with a small, carefully chosen set of long samples to keep search tractable; and include a short‑context recovery step so that gains at million‑token lengths do not come at the cost of degraded behavior on routine short contexts.

The paper also leaves several promising directions to the community: bringing analytic and learned predictors to replace or accelerate search, investigating per‑layer and per‑head rescaling, adapting the idea to non‑RoPE positional formats, and integrating LongRoPE with memory/retrieval or hardware‑aware attention kernels to make million‑token context LLMs efficient and safe in practice. Finally, because longer contexts change the risk landscape (privacy, adversarial prompts, and reliance on distant evidence), deployments using LongRoPE should be accompanied by governance steps: dataset hygiene, adversarial testing, and run‑time controls that limit what the model can see or act on in sensitive applications.

All of these conclusions follow from the paper’s experiments, ablations, and explicit design choices. For a student or practitioner reading LongRoPE, the paper provides a reproducible and pragmatic blueprint to obtain very long context windows while also signaling the engineering, evaluation, and safety work that must accompany any move toward multi‑million‑token LLMs.
