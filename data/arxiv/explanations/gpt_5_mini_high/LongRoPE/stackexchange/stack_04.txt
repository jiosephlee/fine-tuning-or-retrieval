\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### What is the theoretical or empirical justification for the monotonic constraint \(\lambda_i\le \lambda_{i+1}\)?
Question:
The evolution search enforces a non-decreasing \(\lambda_i\) across RoPE dimensions, motivated by NTK intuitions that high-frequency (lower-\(i\)) dimensions should interpolate less. Do you have empirical ablations showing performance with and without this monotonicity? Could the optimal schedule be locally non-monotonic for some models/tasks, and if so, does the constraint prevent reaching better solutions?
Answer:
Short answer: the monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ in LongRoPE is a pragmatic inductive prior motivated by NTK/frequency reasoning and by search-efficiency concerns; the paper uses it to reduce the combinatorial search cost and does not report an explicit ablation that compares constrained vs unconstrained searches. It therefore cannot prove that a locally non‑monotonic schedule could never be better, but the authors argue (and it is plausible) that monotonicity is both theoretically well‑aligned and practically helpful. Below I unpack why, what the paper actually did, and how you could test the risk that the constraint blocks better solutions.

Why monotonicity is a natural prior. RoPE dimensions are ordered by effective frequency (the paper writes the rotary angle for dimension $i$ as $n/\beta^i$ with $\beta=\theta^{2/d}$), so the lowest indices encode higher-frequency phase components and the highest indices encode lower‑frequency, slowly varying components. NTK‑based arguments (and prior works cited in the paper) recommend treating different frequencies differently when stretching/extrapolating positional encodings: you want to preserve high‑frequency components (they distinguish nearby token positions) and can afford more rescaling (interpolation) for low‑frequency components. A monotone non‑decreasing $\lambda_i$ (smaller rescale on high‑freq dims, larger on low‑freq dims) directly implements that intuition. Intuitively in signal‑processing terms, heavy interpolation is like low‑pass filtering; you don’t want to low‑pass the high‑frequency RoPE dims that encode fine positional differences.

What the paper actually does and why. In Section 3.2 the authors explicitly state they impose $\lambda_i\le\lambda_{i+1}$ during evolution search. Their two stated motives are (1) NTK/frequency theory as above and (2) a huge search space (they give example combinatorics), so enforcing monotonicity prunes vast numbers of candidate solutions and reduces wasted perplexity evaluations. The empirical gains the paper reports for LongRoPE (better perplexity and very large extensions) were obtained using this constrained search, but the paper does not include a direct ablation that compares search with and without this monotonicity constraint.

Could the optimal schedule be non‑monotonic, and does the constraint prevent better solutions? In principle yes: because RoPE interacts with transformer layers, attention patterns, tokenizer boundaries, dataset statistics and fine‑tuning dynamics, there is no formal theorem in the paper that the best per‑dimension $\lambda_i$ must be monotone. Local non‑monotonicities could be useful in pathological cases (e.g., if certain mid‑frequency channels happen to be especially important for a task or a model’s learned weights). In practice, however, two points argue that strict monotonicity is unlikely to be a major blind spot here: first, NTK/frequency theory gives a strong, cognitively simple prior that matches many prior interpolation approaches (PI/NTK/YaRN). Second, the search in the paper was run on a very small validation sample (few sequences) so unconstrained search would have large variance and a high risk of overfitting spurious non‑monotonic bumps; monotonicity acts as a useful regularizer. But because the paper did not ablate this choice, it remains an empirical question whether allowing local violations yields consistent gains.

How you could test it (practical ablation recipes). If you want to know for your model/task, try progressively relaxing the constraint rather than an all‑or‑nothing experiment; that keeps compute reasonable and isolates effects. For example, (a) run an unconstrained evolution but only on a reduced parameterization such as grouping RoPE into $G$ groups (e.g., $8$–$16$ groups) and searching one $\lambda$ per group; (b) use a soft monotonic penalty in the fitness: minimize perplexity + $\alpha\sum_i\max(0,\lambda_i-\lambda_{i+1})$ and sweep $\alpha$ from large (near hard constraint) to $0$ (no constraint); (c) seed the population from the monotone solution and allow small random residuals (so you explore local non‑monotonic deviations only); or (d) run the unconstrained search for a few generations but keep the top-$k$ survivors projected to the nearest monotone sequence to measure how much raw unconstrained winners violate monotonicity. Measure held‑out perplexity on the same long sequences used in the paper (PG19/Proof‑pile/Books3) and task metrics (passkey retrieval, short‑window recovery). If unconstrained or weakly‑constrained runs produce consistent improvement beyond noise, that is evidence the hard constraint was limiting; if not, the monotone prior is justified.

Practical recommendation. For limited compute and a very large search space, the monotonic constraint is a reasonable inductive bias: it matches NTK/frequency intuition, regularizes against overfitting to small validation samples, and dramatically reduces search cost. If you suspect your model or task has atypical frequency needs (or you have resources), use one of the relaxation ablations above to test whether local non‑monotonicity helps — but expect any gains to be modest and to come at a high search cost unless you reduce the dimensionality of the search.

Summary: the paper’s justification is theoretical (NTK/frequency ordering) plus practical (search efficiency). There is no explicit in‑paper ablation that removes the monotonicity constraint, so it remains an open empirical question whether strict monotonicity ever meaningfully hurts final performance; the experiments and theory suggest it is a sensible and low‑risk design choice in the LongRoPE pipeline.
