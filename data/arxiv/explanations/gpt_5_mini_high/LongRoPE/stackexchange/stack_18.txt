\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### Why two-stage fine-tuning (128k then 256k) instead of one-stage 256k, and how sensitive is it?
Question:
For LLaMA2 you fine-tune 400 steps at 128k and then 600 at 256k, rather than training 1000 steps directly at 256k. Do you have ablations showing this two-stage approach converges better or reaches better minima? How sensitive are the results to the split, learning rate schedule, and the choice of starting from the 128k-optimized \(\lambda_i\)?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper uses the $128k\to256k$ two-stage because it makes the extension task much easier and more stable in practice — it reduces the per-stage “novel-position” shock the model must absorb, uses a better RoPE initialization for the harder step, and is therefore more efficient than trying to jump straight to $256k$. The paper reports this as a practical choice (and shows related indirect evidence), but it does not include a full numeric ablation that directly compares ($400+600$) to a single 1000-step $256k$ run across all metrics.

Why the two-stage recipe helps (intuitively and technically)
The core practical difficulty when you extend RoPE to a much larger context is that you suddenly introduce a huge number of previously unseen token positions, which produce “catastrophic” out‑of‑distribution positional values and make optimization unstable. Doing the extension progressively reduces the per-step extension ratio the model must adapt to, so fewer new positions are introduced at once and the model’s weights (and attention patterns) can adapt incrementally. In LongRoPE this is combined with a searched, non‑uniform rescale of RoPE dimensions (the per‑dimension $\lambda_i$) that preserves the most informative frequencies/positions; applying the $128k$‑optimized $\lambda_i$ before any gradient updates gives a much better initialization than naive PI/NTK and therefore reduces information loss and optimization difficulty for the next stage.

Concrete facts from the paper that support this
The paper describes the exact workflow: they search rescale factors for $128k$ and $256k$, fine‑tune 400 steps with the $128k$ rescale factors, then swap to the $256k$ rescale factors and continue for 600 steps. The authors state this “proves more efficient than directly fine‑tuning to $256k$” and note that attempting to fine‑tune under very large extension ratios is “challenging” (see the Appendix). Empirical signals in the paper also point to the advantage of smaller per‑stage extension: LLaMA2 LongRoPE-$2048k$ that used the $256k$ fine‑tune as the intermediate stage performs better than the version that only fine‑tuned at $128k$, and the authors explain that this is because the secondary extension ratio becomes smaller ($8\times$ vs $16\times$) when you fine‑tune at $256k$ — i.e., smaller jumps are easier to extend from.

What ablations the paper does and does not provide
The paper includes ablations that are directly relevant: (a) the “effectiveness of the second positional interpolation” shows that performing a second non‑uniform search on the fine‑tuned $256k$ model keeps perplexity low when extending to $512k/1024k/2048k$ compared to PI/YaRN; (b) the analysis of the two non‑uniformities shows how per‑dimension and per‑position choices matter (and that token‑position exemptions help at moderate lengths but are less impactful at the extreme end). However, the paper does not present a table that directly compares ($400@128k + 600@256k$) versus ($1000@256k$) with identical other hyperparameters. The claim that the progressive route is “more efficient” is therefore supported by practical experience and by the indirect empirical results above, but not by a systematic sweep over splits/step allocations/LR schedules in the main text.

Sensitivity to split, LR schedule, and starting from the $128k$ $\lambda_i$
The paper shows that these choices are model‑ and data‑sensitive. For example, LLaMA2 benefited from the $256k$ intermediate fine‑tune (smaller secondary jump) while Mistral did better with $128k$ in the experiments — the difference is attributed to the fine‑tuning setup (Mistral followed YaRN’s $16k$ training length and different LR). The authors list the hyperparameters they used: for LLaMA2 a learning rate of $2\mathrm{e}{-5}$ with linear decay, global batch size 32, 400 steps at $128k$ then 600 at $256k$ ($128k$ training used 8 A100s, $256k$ used 16 A100s); for Mistral they used a constant $1\mathrm{e}{-6}$ and batch 64 with 400 steps (per model) using a $16k$ training length. The differing hyperparameters and dataset/training-sequence-length choices are precisely why the optimal split and schedule can change across models.

In short: (i) initializing the first fine‑tune with the searched $128k$ $\lambda_i$ is helpful because it preserves key RoPE dimensions/positions and gives a far better starting point than naïve interpolation, (ii) the split matters because it determines the per‑stage extension ratio (smaller ratios are easier), and (iii) LR, batch size, and training sequence length interact with the split and with the model architecture — the paper reports reasonable defaults but does not present an exhaustive sensitivity sweep.

Practical takeaways if you want to reproduce or experiment
Treat the progressive strategy as a curriculum: pick an intermediate length so that each stage’s extension ratio is moderate (the paper used $128k$ then $256k$, which corresponds to manageable jumps in their setting). Use the searched non‑uniform $\lambda_i$ for the intermediate stage (this is a key stabilizer). Start with the paper’s reported LRs (LLaMA2: $2\mathrm{e}{-5}$ linear decay; Mistral: $1\mathrm{e}{-6}$ constant) and the same step counts to reproduce their results, then try small adjustments. If you have the budget, test directly comparing (A) single‑stage to (B) progressive with the same total steps and same LR to see which converges faster on your model/data; monitor perplexity on held‑out long segments and on original short windows (the paper also shows that you may need a short‑length recovery search after extreme extension).

Conclusion
The two‑stage procedure in LongRoPE is a pragmatic, stability‑and‑efficiency driven choice: it lowers the effective extension per tuning stage, leverages a better RoPE initialization, and empirically produces better results for the authors’ LLaMA2 experiments. The paper documents these choices and gives indirect empirical support (including comparisons between $ft=128k$ and $ft=256k$ final behaviors), but it does not include a full, systematic ablation that directly compares every possible split/LR/initialization permutation — so you should expect some model‑dependent tuning if you reproduce this on a different model or dataset.
