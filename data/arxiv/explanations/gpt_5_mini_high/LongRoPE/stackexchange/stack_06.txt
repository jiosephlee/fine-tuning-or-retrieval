\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### Does switching the scale at \(n=\hat{n}\) introduce a hard discontinuity, and would a smooth ramp help?
Question:
In Eq. (3), at \(n=\hat{n}\) the factor changes from 1 to \(1/\lambda_i\). This is a hard step. Could this create boundary artifacts in attention around \(\hat{n}\)? Did you try a smooth transition (e.g., a linear or cosine ramp for \(n\in[0,\hat{n}]\)) and compare perplexity or stability?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — the paper’s design uses a hard step at $n=\hat n$, so there is a literal jump in the rotary angle mapping at that token; the authors did not report experiments with a smooth ramp. The step was a deliberate, simple design choice that the paper shows works well in practice (see Finding 2), but a smooth transition is a reasonable idea to try and has trade-offs that I summarize below along with a concrete recipe if you want to test it.

Why it is a discontinuity (what it changes mathematically). In the paper the per‑dimension scaling is applied via the indicator
$$
\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n)=
\begin{cases}
1 & n<\hat n,\\[4pt]
\frac{1}{\lambda_i} & n\ge\hat n,
\end{cases}
$$
so the rotary angle used at position $n$ for dimension $i$ is
$$
\phi_{n,i}=\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n)\cdot\frac{n}{\beta^i}.
$$
Attention between positions $n$ and $m$ depends on the relative rotation $\phi_{n,i}-\phi_{m,i}$. If one index is before and the other after the threshold then the relative angle suddenly changes by a term proportional to $(1-1/\lambda_i)$, i.e.
$$
\Delta\phi_{n,m,i}=\frac{1}{\beta^i}\big(\mathbb{I}_n\,n-\mathbb{I}_m\,m\big),
$$
so the step introduces a phase/angle jump for pairs that straddle the boundary. That jump is real (discrete, token-wise), and can in principle produce local “boundary artifacts” in attention weights for token pairs across $\hat n$.

What the paper reports empirically. The paper explicitly uses this step formulation and searches over $\hat n$ (Finding 2). Empirically they found that keeping the first $\hat n$ tokens completely un-interpolated (i.e., the hard step) improves perplexity and stability compared to applying interpolation uniformly from position 0. The method and the search over discrete $\hat n$ values are central parts of LongRoPE; the paper does not report experiments that replace the step with a smooth ramp. So the hard step is both a design choice and an empirical finding in this work.

Would a smooth ramp help? Intuition says: possibly — smoothing removes the abrupt phase jump and so can reduce sharp local mismatches in angle differences for pairs across the boundary. This could reduce any local attention artifacts and might help training stability when fine‑tuning. However, there are several important trade-offs that explain why the authors used the step:

1. preservation of early tokens. The paper’s motivation for the step is to preserve the original RoPE exactly for the initial tokens (which tend to receive high attention). A ramp that partially scales those tokens would erode that preservation and could reintroduce the “catastrophic values” the interpolation is trying to avoid.

2. search / complexity cost. A hard threshold yields a small discrete search space for $\hat n$. A ramp introduces extra hyperparameters (ramp width, shape, possibly per‑dimension ramps) and greatly enlarges the search/optimization burden the authors already addressed with evolutionary search.

3. empirical sufficiency. The step + search already produced large practical gains (8× non‑fine‑tuning extension and the progressive scheme to 2048k), so the marginal return of a ramp is uncertain.

How to test a ramp (practical recipe). If you want to experiment, you can replace the indicator by a small-width smooth step. For example pick a width $w$ and a smooth interpolant $s(x)\in[0,1]$ (linear: $s(x)=x$; cosine: $s(x)=\tfrac{1-\cos(\pi x)}{2}$). Define
$$
\mathbb{I}_i(n)=
\begin{cases}
1 & n<\hat n,\\[4pt]
1 - \big(1-\tfrac{1}{\lambda_i}\big)\,s\!\left(\dfrac{n-\hat n}{w}\right) & \hat n\le n < \hat n+w,\\[6pt]
\dfrac{1}{\lambda_i} & n\ge \hat n+w.
\end{cases}
$$
This keeps the untouched region $n<\hat n$, then smoothly moves to the scaled regime over $w$ tokens. Start with small $w$ (e.g. $w\in\{4,8,32\}$) and the same monotonicity constraint on $\lambda_i$. Evaluate exactly as in the paper (perplexity on the same PG19/Proof‑pile samples, attention visualizations around the boundary, and the passkey retrieval task). If you fine‑tune, monitor loss stability; if you run search you can include $w$ as a discrete hyperparameter.

What I’d expect and what to measure. A narrow ramp may produce negligible improvement over the hard step because it sacrifices some of the “preserve early tokens” benefit; a wider ramp could smooth attention but at the cost of moving more early tokens away from their pretrained angles. To detect boundary artifacts directly, compare attention mass and per-token loss for tokens in a small neighborhood around $\hat n$ between the step and ramp variants; also compare sliding‑window perplexities used throughout the paper. These diagnostics will show whether smoothing reduces any local instabilities and whether that translates to global perplexity or retrieval accuracy gains.

Bottom line: the hard step is a real discontinuity and could — in principle — create local boundary effects, but in LongRoPE the step was chosen because it preserves the early tokens that are most important and it works well empirically. The paper does not report smooth‑ramp experiments; trying a small, local ramp centered at $\hat n$ (as shown above) is a reasonable next experiment, but be aware it increases the hyperparameter/search burden and may reduce the key benefit of preserving the first $\hat n$ tokens.
