\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### How robust are the searched \(\lambda_i\) and \(\hat{n}\) across domains and model families?
Question:
You search on PG19/Books3-like text but evaluate on other datasets and tasks. Do the same schedules transfer well to code/math or dialogue? For Mistral vs LLaMA2, do you need to re-run the full search per model family, or can one family’s schedule initialize the other with minimal loss?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper’s experiments and ablations indicate that the per-dimension rescale factors $\lambda_i$ and the preserved‑prefix threshold $\hat{n}$ are not strictly universal — they depend on the target extension ratio and on the model / fine‑tuning regime. The authors therefore perform (and recommend) an explicit search per target length and per model; the paper does not claim that a single schedule found on PG19/Books3 will be optimal for very different domains such as code, math or dialogue. That said, the search is intentionally cheap (small validation sets, seeded initialization and a monotone prior) so adapting a schedule to a new model or domain is practical.

Why (intuitively and technically): LongRoPE’s $\lambda_i$ rescales RoPE’s rotation angles per RoPE dimension (in the paper the RoPE entry for dimension $i$ at token position $n$ becomes $\cos(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\times \frac{n}{\beta^i})$, $\sin(\cdot)$ with $\mathbb{I}=1$ for $n<\hat{n}$ and $\mathbb{I}=1/\lambda_i$ otherwise). Different models and corpora distribute positional information (the “spectral” energy across RoPE dimensions) differently: tokenizers, pretraining data statistics and architectural choices change which RoPE dimensions are “important” and how attention is used over tokens. The paper’s ablations explicitly show that non‑uniform $\lambda_i$ and a nonzero $\hat{n}$ help, but the optimal pattern depends on the extension ratio and the model/fine‑tuning setup (e.g., LLaMA2 benefited more from a 256k fine‑tune whereas Mistral in the paper performed better when fine‑tuned at 128k). That empirical difference is the paper’s direct evidence that schedules are model/setting dependent.

What the paper evaluated (so what is supported): searches were run on LLaMA2-7B and Mistral-7B (separately), using PG19 / Books3-like long text for search and validation (the search objective is perplexity on a few held‑out long samples — the paper uses 5 PG19 validation samples for many searches, and 3 Pile‑Books3 samples for very large windows). The final LongRoPE models were evaluated on multiple natural‑language long‑context tasks (Proof‑pile, PG19, Books3 sliding windows, passkey retrieval and standard short‑context benchmarks). The paper does not report experiments on code‑only, math‑heavy, or dialogue datasets, nor does it report cross‑model transfer where a schedule found on model A is applied unchanged to model B.

Practical takeaways / recommendations (grounded in the paper):

- If your target domain is similar to the search corpus used in the paper (natural long text like Books3 / PG19), the paper’s schedules and priors (monotonic $\lambda_i$ increasing with dimension, and a small preserved-prefix $\hat{n}$) are already shown to transfer across several natural‑language evaluations. The monotone constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ is a useful, theory‑backed prior (NTK) that reduces search cost and appears broadly sensible.

- If you move to a substantially different domain (code, mathematical text, chat transcripts), treat the published schedules as a strong seed but validate and re‑search. The authors’ search is lightweight: they seed the initial population with PI/NTK/YaRN schedules, enforce monotonicity, evaluate perplexity on just a few long examples and run evolution. So you can cheaply run a short adaptation search (few validation examples, small population / few iterations) to adapt $\{\lambda_i\}$ and $\hat{n}$ to your domain.

- For moving between model families (e.g., LLaMA2 $\leftrightarrow$ Mistral) the paper’s workflow is to search per model. The empirical differences reported (different best fine‑tune length and different perplexity curves) argue for re‑running at least a small search per model. If compute is tight, initialize the search population with the other model’s schedule (the paper’s “optimized initial population” strategy is exactly this idea) and run a reduced evolution to adapt it; that is likely much cheaper than a full cold search and is consistent with the paper’s design.

- If you only need modest extension (the paper shows up to a $8\times$ extension without fine‑tuning for their LLaMA2 experiments using searched non‑uniform $\lambda_i$), try the non‑fine‑tuned schedule first; for very large extensions follow the progressive pipeline in the paper (search → moderate fine‑tune at a mid‑length like 128k/256k → second search) because the paper shows this is both more stable and much cheaper than attempting a single giant fine‑tune.

In short: the paper demonstrates per‑model and per‑length dependence of the optimal $\lambda_i$ and $\hat{n}$; it does not show automatic, guaranteed transfer to code/math/dialogue. Best practice (consistent with the paper) is to re‑validate and, in most cases, run a short adaptation search per new model or domain; using another model’s schedule as a seed (rather than as a final answer) is a practical shortcut that the paper’s search framework was designed to support.
