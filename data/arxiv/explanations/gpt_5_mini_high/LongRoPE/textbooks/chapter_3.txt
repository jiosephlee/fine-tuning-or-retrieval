\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

Chapter 3: Prior Context-Extension Techniques via Positional Scaling

Transformers do not have an inherent representation of token order, so modern large language models (LLMs) rely on positional encodings. A widely used choice is Rotary Positional Encoding (RoPE), which encodes position $n$ into a sequence of rotated sine/cosine features across $d$ embedding coordinates. In the simplified form used throughout the paper, RoPE for a token at position $n$ is written as

\[
\left[\cos(n\theta_0),\; \sin(n\theta_0),\; \cos(n\theta_1),\; \sin(n\theta_1),\; \ldots,\; \cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}),\; \sin(n\theta_{d/2-1})\right],
\]

where $d$ is the embedding dimension and each rotary angle is $n\theta_i$ with $\theta_i$ derived from a base frequency (the paper uses the common parameterization $\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}$ with a default base $\theta=10000$). When we attempt to use a pretrained LLM on sequences longer than it was trained for, new position indices produce angles the model has never seen, which can lead to large distribution shifts and degraded behavior. Position interpolation methods address this by rescaling rotation angles for new positions so that their angles lie within a range closer to the pretrained regime.

A compact way to describe positional rescaling is to introduce per-dimension rescale factors. One convenient unified form (used in the paper) is

\[
\text{RoPE}(n) = \biggl[\; \cos\!\bigl(\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_0,\hat n)\cdot \tfrac{n}{\beta^0}\bigr),\; \sin\!\bigl(\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_0,\hat n)\cdot \tfrac{n}{\beta^0}\bigr),\; \ldots,\; \cos\!\bigl(\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_{d/2-1},\hat n)\cdot \tfrac{n}{\beta^{d/2-1}}\bigr),\; \sin\!\bigl(\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_{d/2-1},\hat n)\cdot \tfrac{n}{\beta^{d/2-1}}\bigr)\; \biggr],
\]

with the piecewise factor

\[
\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n) =
\begin{cases}
1 & n < \hat n,\\[4pt]
1/\lambda_i & n \ge \hat n,
\end{cases}
\]

where $\lambda_i$ (or equivalently $\hat\lambda_i=1/\lambda_i$) controls how much we downscale rotation angles for the $i$‑th RoPE dimension, $\hat n$ can control whether the earliest token positions are left unchanged, and $\beta$ is another constant derived from the RoPE base. The extension ratio $s$ is the target context length divided by the original trained length, $s = L'/L$. The different prior methods instantiate different choices for the $\lambda_i$ (and typically take $\hat n=0$).

Below we review the main prior approaches to positional scaling that the paper surveys: Linear Position Interpolation (PI), NTK and dynamic NTK ideas, and YaRN. For each method we give the mechanism (how the $\lambda_i$ are chosen), the intuition for why it helps, and the practical limitations that motivate more expressive treatments.

# Linear Position Interpolation (PI)

Linear Position Interpolation (often abbreviated PI) is the simplest and most direct approach. If you want to extend a model's context from length $L$ to length $L'$, PI rescales every rotation angle uniformly by the extension ratio. In the unified formulation this corresponds to setting

\[
\lambda_i = s \quad\text{for all RoPE dimensions } i,
\]

and typically $\hat n=0$ so every position is rescaled. Concretely, PI replaces angles $n\theta_i$ with approximately $(n/s)\theta_i$ (or equivalently multiplies the angle argument by $1/s$), mapping the larger position indices back into the numeric range seen during pretraining.

Why this is attractive: it is conceptually and implementationally simple. All new positions are mapped into the pretrained angular range, so the model sees inputs that are numerically closer to its training distribution. The method serves as a straightforward initialization for subsequent fine‑tuning on longer sequences.

Why it fails at large extension ratios (intuition and math). Consider the angular difference between adjacent positions in a single RoPE frequency: originally that local phase step is about $\Delta = \theta_i$. After PI with scale $s$ the step becomes $\Delta' = \theta_i / s$. Thus uniformly scaling by a large $s$ compresses angular differences between neighboring tokens. When angles for many adjacent positions all lie close together in angle space, the positional encodings become "crowded" — the model loses the fine-grained capacity to distinguish nearby positions. This crowding makes local ordering signals weaker, harming attention's ability to focus on relative offsets. Empirically, PI tends to work if followed by fine-tuning on long sequences, but its performance degrades as $s$ grows large without such adaptation. The paper reports that PI commonly needs fine-tuning to be usable for large extensions and tends to underperform at high extension ratios because of this crowding.

# NTK and Dynamic NTK

The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) viewpoint motivates a more nuanced, frequency-aware interpolation. The NTK perspective treats RoPE-like features as a family of Fourier-type bases whose contributions to the model's representation vary by frequency. Low-index RoPE dimensions (those with higher rotation frequency) encode fine-grained, short-range positional distinctions; high-index dimensions (lower frequency) encode coarser, longer-range structure.

Mechanism. NTK-based approaches therefore propose unequal scaling across dimensions: scale the higher-frequency (lower-index) dimensions less and allow more aggressive scaling (even interpolation) on lower-frequency dimensions. A stylized way to capture this is to make $\lambda_i$ depend on the dimension index $i$ so that $\lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \cdots$, i.e., a monotonic, dimension-dependent rescale schedule. The paper describes formulations where the effective per-dimension scale varies (a simple illustrative formula reported in the paper takes $\lambda$ to follow a power of $s$ across dimensions). Dynamic NTK further adapts the per-dimension (and sometimes per-position) rescaling based on the current sequence length, allowing the interpolation strategy to change as the sequence grows.

Why this helps. By preserving more of the high-frequency dimensions' angles (less scaling), the model retains the fine-grained positional distinctions that are crucial to discriminate nearby tokens. Letting low-frequency components absorb more of the rescaling provides room for representing longer ranges. This mixed strategy mitigates the crowding problem that uniform PI produces, so in many cases NTK-style methods can extend context length without immediate fine‑tuning.

Practical outcome and limits. NTK-aware rescaling often enables modest non-finetuned extensions and empirically can work up to a few times the original length. The paper notes that NTK-style methods can extend context windows in non-fine-tuning scenarios, but are typically effective only up to roughly a 4× extension; beyond that, performance still degrades and fine-tuning tends to be necessary. NTK therefore captures an important dimension-wise non-uniformity, but it is still a relatively constrained analytic prescription; it uses a fixed functional form derived from theory rather than directly optimizing the per-dimension behavior for a given pretrained model and target length.

# YaRN

YaRN (short for a frequency-aware scheme introduced in prior work and discussed in the paper) takes a pragmatic, hybrid route between PI and NTK. Instead of applying a smoothly varying per-dimension function, YaRN partitions RoPE dimensions into three frequency-based groups and treats each group differently:

- High-frequency group: perform extrapolation (i.e., effectively set $\lambda \approx 1$, leaving those dimensions unchanged). The reasoning is that these dimensions are already encoding short-range detail and should not be compressed.
- Middle-frequency group: apply an NTK-like unequal interpolation/extrapolation scheme.
- Low-frequency group: apply linear interpolation (PI-like scaling), since these components are the ones intended to represent longer-range information.

Why this helps. The multi-group design gives flexibility: high-frequency channels preserve fine-grained position signals, low-frequency channels expand to cover distant positions, and the middle group mediates between them. In many practical settings YaRN provides a stronger baseline than simple PI, because it explicitly recognizes that not all RoPE dimensions should be treated the same.

Limitations. The key limitation of YaRN — and what the paper emphasizes — is that the grouping boundaries and thresholds are human-designed empirical choices. Different pretrained models (different layer/frequency allocations, different training recipes) can have different internal information distributions, so a fixed grouping may be suboptimal. The paper provides empirical evidence that YaRN can underperform in some settings: for example, in their PG19 experiments YaRN did worse than PI and NTK for non-fine-tuned extensions because it failed to reach the effective target context behavior in that regime (they observed perplexity spikes near the target length in some cases). In short, YaRN is more expressive than PI but still constrained by heuristic groupings rather than model-specific optimization.

# Where these methods fall short: missing the full non-uniformity across dimensions and token positions

Although PI, NTK and YaRN progressively increase in sophistication, they share common limitations that the paper highlights. Understanding these limitations helps explain why methods that search for per-dimension and per-position rescale factors can perform better.

1. Partial treatment of per-dimension heterogeneity. NTK and YaRN recognize that different RoPE dimensions carry different kinds of positional information, but they do so via fixed analytic formulas or coarse grouping. Real pretrained LLMs can exhibit more intricate, model‑specific distributions of information across RoPE dimensions: which dimensions are most important, and how much each can be safely compressed, vary with architecture and training. Coarse prescriptions risk over‑compressing dimensions that encode crucial local structure or under‑compressing dimensions that could have safely supported greater extension. The paper’s empirical searches show that allowing a specialized rescale factor per RoPE dimension (rather than a single global scale or three coarse groups) can substantially reduce perplexity for extended contexts.

2. Ignoring token‑position non‑uniformity. The prior methods typically treat all token positions the same (equivalently $\hat n=0$ in the unified formulation). But the paper observes that early positions in a sequence are often attended to more heavily or carry especially important signals, so they should be interpolated less (or even left unchanged). Leaving the first $\hat n$ tokens unscaled preserves the original pretrained behavior for the most attended locations. Existing analytic methods do not incorporate this positional thresholding; the empirical results in the paper show that allowing a token-position threshold (i.e., different treatment for initial tokens versus later ones) improves performance, and that the optimal threshold depends on the target extension length.

3. Fixed heuristics versus model‑specific optimization. PI, NTK, and YaRN are built from theory and human intuition. They provide useful starting points but are not adapted to the idiosyncrasies of a particular pretrained checkpoint, corpora, or target stretching factor. The paper shows that an automated search over per-dimension rescale factors (and optionally a nonzero $\hat n$ for preserving initial tokens) can discover non-uniform rescalings that outperform these heuristics, both with and without fine‑tuning.

4. Practical obstacles beyond interpolation form. Even with a good interpolation rule, the broader challenges remain: a very large fraction of positions will be “new” after a huge extension (for example, extending from 4k to >1000k introduces over 90% new positions), which creates a significant distribution shift and can produce catastrophic values; fine‑tuning to adapt to those positions requires very long documents and substantial compute, which are often scarce or expensive; and aggressive interpolation can "spread" attention over so many positions that performance on the original short context degrades. PI, NTK and YaRN each mitigate some of these issues but do not eliminate them entirely.

Summary. Linear PI is simple but creates crowding and requires fine‑tuning for large $s$. NTK-style methods respect frequency differences across RoPE dimensions and can extend without fine‑tuning up to modest factors (the paper cites roughly a 4× practical limit), but they rely on analytic forms that may not match every model. YaRN blends extrapolation, NTK-like scaling, and PI by grouping dimensions, which works well in many cases but depends on heuristic group boundaries and can be suboptimal for new models or target lengths. Crucially, none of these prior approaches fully exploit the two distinct sources of non‑uniformity identified in the paper: fine-grained, per-dimension variation and the differing importance of early versus later token positions. That gap motivates methods that search or optimize per-dimension and per-position rescale factors to provide better initializations for fine‑tuning and to enable much larger extensions with less degradation.
