\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

Chapter 2: Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE): Foundations

# How Transformers use positional information; RoPE vs. absolute/learned encodings

Transformers compute attention using pairwise interactions among token representations, and by construction those interactions are permutation-invariant. To recover sequence order information the model must be given explicit positional cues. In practice this is done by augmenting token representations with position-dependent information; that general idea is the starting point for all positional encoding schemes. The LongRoPE paper frames this general requirement and then focuses on one particular family of encodings that has become common in modern large language models: rotary positional embeddings (RoPE).

RoPE differs from other position-encoding choices in that it does not insert a separate learned vector per absolute index in the same way a learned absolute embedding would. Instead, RoPE encodes position by modifying (rotating) components of the token representation using sinusoidal functions of a position-dependent angle. This rotation-based construction produces a compact, deterministic representation across dimensions and positions, and it is the specific positional mechanism studied throughout the paper. All derivations and scaling manipulations that follow apply to RoPE as the paper defines it.

# RoPE mechanics: rotary angles, frequency schedule across dimensions, base $\theta=10000$, $\beta=\theta^{2/d}$

RoPE represents the position $n$ of a token via pairs of sine and cosine components applied across the embedding dimensions. If the model embedding dimension is $d$, RoPE produces the following structured vector of length $d$ (grouped into $d/2$ cosine–sine pairs):

$
\left[\cos(n\theta_0),\; \sin(n\theta_0),\; \cos(n\theta_1),\; \sin(n\theta_1),\; \ldots,\; \cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}),\; \sin(n\theta_{d/2-1})\right].
$

Here $n$ is the token position index and each $\theta_i$ is a rotation frequency associated with the $i$‑th cosine–sine pair. The RoPE frequency schedule is defined so that the frequencies decrease across dimension index $i$ according to a geometric law:

$
\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}.
$

In the common default parameterization used by the models in the paper the base value $\theta$ is $10000$. It is convenient to rewrite the per-dimension frequency using the auxiliary constant $\beta$ defined by

$
\beta = \theta^{2/d},
$

so that

$
\theta_i = \beta^{-i},
$

and the rotation angle that appears in the trigonometric functions is

$
\text{angle}_{i}(n) = n \theta_i = \frac{n}{\beta^{i}}.
$

Two points are important about this construction. First, different pairs of dimensions operate at different effective frequencies: as $i$ increases, $\beta^{i}$ grows and $\theta_i$ shrinks, so higher-index pairs encode lower temporal frequency (coarser positional structure) while lower-index pairs encode higher temporal frequency (finer positional differences). Second, the cosine–sine pairs are a periodic parameterization of angle; the embedding for position $n$ is determined by the angle $n\theta_i$ modulo the $2\pi$ cycle of cosine and sine. This per-dimension angular representation is the lever we use when changing the model’s effective context window.

# Extension ratio $s = L'/L$ and its effect on rotary angles

When we attempt to extend a pretrained model’s context window from an original maximum length $L$ to a new target maximum length $L'$, it is convenient to quantify the change as the extension ratio

$
s = \frac{L'}{L}.
$

A common practical approach to make angles for the new, larger positions fall within the range that the pretrained RoPE parameters saw during training is to rescale the rotary angles by a multiplicative factor. In the paper this rescaling is expressed dimension-wise (and, optionally, position-wise) as an operator that multiplies the original angle $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$ by a factor $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})$. Specifically, for a chosen per-dimension scale $\lambda_i$ and a token-index threshold $\hat{n}$, the RoPE encoding used at token position $n$ is written as

$
\text{RoPE}_n \;=\; \big[\ldots,\; \cos\!\big(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\times\frac{n}{\beta^i}\big),\; \sin\!\big(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\times\frac{n}{\beta^i}\big),\ldots\big],
$

with the indicator factor

$
\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n}) =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } n < \hat{n},\\[4pt]
\frac{1}{\lambda_i} & \text{if } n \ge \hat{n}.
\end{cases}
$

Thus for positions beyond the threshold $\hat{n}$ the rotation angles are scaled down by $\lambda_i$ (i.e., replaced by $(1/\lambda_i)\cdot n/\beta^i$). The common, simplest choice—used by linear positional interpolation (PI) in the paper—is to take the same scalar for every dimension, $\lambda_i = s$, so every rotation angle is reduced uniformly by the extension ratio. In that case each angle becomes

$
\text{angle}_i'(n) = \frac{1}{s}\cdot\frac{n}{\beta^i}.
$

A useful immediate observation follows by looking at the per-dimension angular increment between adjacent token positions. For dimension $i$ and positions $n$ and $n+1$ (when the rescaling applies), the angular increment is

$
\Delta\phi_i \;=\; \text{angle}_i'(n+1) - \text{angle}_i'(n) \;=\; \frac{1}{\lambda_i\,\beta^i}.
$

This simple expression makes explicit why changes in $\lambda_i$ matter: increasing $\lambda_i$ shrinks the per-token angular step in that dimension, and decreasing $\lambda_i$ increases it.

# Interpolation vs. extrapolation intuition and the ‘crowding’ problem when compressing many positions into trained ranges

Two broad strategies appear in the RoPE literature (and in the paper’s discussion) for dealing with new token positions beyond the pretrained maximum: interpolation and extrapolation.

- Interpolation (as the paper calls positional interpolation or PI) rescales the rotary angles so that the large new position indices are mapped back into the angular range the model was trained on. Concretely, with interpolation and $\lambda_i>1$ the new angles are smaller than the linear continuation $n/\beta^i$ would be; the model is effectively asked to reuse patterns learned for smaller angles to handle larger positions.

- Extrapolation means avoiding such downscaling and keeping angles at or closer to their original schedule (in the paper’s practical characterization, extrapolation corresponds to $\lambda_i=1$, i.e., not applying interpolation for that dimension or region of the sequence). Extrapolation trusts the model to generalize outside the angular range seen during pretraining.

Interpolation has an intuitive appeal because it prevents producing entirely out-of-distribution angular values when $n$ becomes much larger than during pretraining. But it brings a fundamental trade-off: compressing a long span of new token indices into the smaller angular bandwidth the model has seen makes many distinct positions end up with very similar cosine–sine pairs, especially in dimensions where the per-token angular increment becomes tiny. This is the “crowding” problem emphasized in the paper. Formally, because

$
\Delta\phi_i = \frac{1}{\lambda_i \beta^i},
$

a large interpolation factor $\lambda_i$ (for example $\lambda_i = s$ in PI when $s$ is large) causes $\Delta\phi_i$ to be small across many dimensions. Tokens that were previously separated by meaningful angular differences now have nearly identical angles, so the positional encoding loses its capacity to discriminate nearby indices.

The paper reports (and uses as motivation) the empirical consequence of crowding: when many new positions are compressed into the pretrained angular range, model performance degrades unless fine-tuning is used to adapt weights to the new pattern of encoded positions. PI therefore tends to underperform at very large extension ratios. To mitigate the crowding effect, the paper discusses and builds on prior approaches that use non-uniform scaling across RoPE dimensions:

- NTK-inspired methods distribute the interpolation pressure non-uniformly across dimensions so that high-frequency (lower-index) dimensions are scaled less and low-frequency (higher-index) dimensions are scaled more, preserving fine-grained local distinctions while compressing only the coarser dimensions.

- YaRN groups the RoPE dimensions into frequency bands and applies different strategies per band (extrapolation for the highest frequencies, NTK-like scaling for middle bands, and PI for the lowest frequencies).

These non-uniform strategies reduce crowding of the most information-bearing dimensions. The LongRoPE paper shows that further gains come from searching for per-dimension rescale factors (and per-position thresholds) rather than relying on fixed formulas or fixed groupings, because the best non-uniformity depends on both model and target extension length.

# Impact of positional scaling on attention and token distinguishability

To understand how RoPE scaling affects attention, it is enough to remember that attention weights are computed from dot products between token representations (queries and keys). RoPE injects positional information via the cosine–sine pairs that modulate those representations; therefore changes in the rotation angles alter the relative geometry of token vectors and thus the pattern of attention weights. The key effect of aggressive interpolation is that positional differences produce much smaller changes in the trigonometric components: if the per-token angular increment $\Delta\phi_i$ becomes tiny in the most informative dimensions, then the rotated representations of adjacent tokens look nearly identical across those dimensions. Consequently, the model’s attention mechanism cannot sharply distinguish nearby positions; attention becomes more diffuse or “dispersed” over many tokens rather than sharply focused on a few relevant neighbors.

This dispersion has two practical consequences emphasized in the paper. First, it degrades the model’s ability to exploit local context, raising language-model perplexity and causing failures on tasks that require fine-grained positional discrimination (for example, distinguishing neighboring tokens or retrieving a short passkey buried in long irrelevant text). Second, it can harm performance even within the model’s original short context window: when the model is globally reconfigured to handle very large $L'$, the compression of angles can force the original-range positions into a narrower angular subspace, making the model worse at tasks it previously handled well at short context lengths. The paper documents such short-context degradation and proposes a remedy: after producing a very large extension, perform an additional adjustment or a separate search of rescale factors targeted at short lengths so that the model can dynamically use a less-compressed RoPE for short inputs.

The paper also highlights a second positional non-uniformity: early tokens in an input sequence often attract high attention and are therefore particularly important to keep distinguishable. Empirically they find that leaving the first $\hat{n}$ tokens unrescaled (i.e., using $\mathbb{I}=1$ for those positions) improves performance, and the optimal choice of $\hat{n}$ depends on the target extension. This observation is consistent with the idea that the model relies on a small prefix of highly attended tokens whose relative ordering must be preserved with high fidelity.

Putting the quantitative and qualitative pieces together, the central engineering lesson of the paper is that scaling RoPE for longer contexts should not be applied uniformly. Instead:

- preserve higher-frequency (lower-index) RoPE dimensions by applying smaller $\lambda_i$ there so local distinctions remain sharp;

- consider not rescaling the earliest positions (a threshold $\hat{n}$) to maintain strong local structure where the model relies on it most;

- and treat per-dimension and per-position scaling as a joint design (or search) problem to avoid unnecessary crowding while still keeping newly encountered angles within ranges that the pretrained weights can handle.

These are exactly the principles LongRoPE operationalizes: non-uniform, per-dimension rescale factors $\lambda_i$, an optional untouched initial prefix $\hat{n}$, and monotonicity constraints on $\lambda_i$ (so that $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$) that enforce the intuition of preserving high-frequency dimensions.

# Summary

RoPE encodes position by applying dimension-specific rotation angles $n\theta_i$ realized as cosine–sine pairs. The frequency schedule across dimensions ($\theta_i = \beta^{-i}$ with $\beta=\theta^{2/d}$ and default $\theta=10000$) gives each pair a different sensitivity to positional increments. When extending the model’s context window from $L$ to $L'$ (ratio $s=L'/L$), rescaling the rotation angles is a straightforward way to keep new positions within a familiar angular regime; however, uniform rescaling compresses per-token angular steps, producing a crowding effect that reduces token distinguishability and spreads attention. Non-uniform strategies—scaling dimensions differently and optionally protecting an initial prefix—preserve the most informative dimensions and positions, mitigating crowding and providing a better starting point for either direct inference at larger contexts or for light fine-tuning. LongRoPE builds on these foundations by searching for per-dimension and per-position rescale factors that balance the trade-offs described above.
