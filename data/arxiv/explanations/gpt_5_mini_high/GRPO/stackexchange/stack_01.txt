\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does GRPO compute advantages without a critic, and how is credit assigned across tokens?
Question:
In the paper, PPO uses a value function and GAE to compute token-level advantages. GRPO instead sets per-token advantages from group-normalized rewards: for outcome supervision, \(\hat{A}_{i,t} = \tilde r_i = (r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r}))/\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})\) for all \(t\) in an output \(o_i\), and for process supervision, \(\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{\mathrm{index}(j) \ge t} \tilde r_i^{\mathrm{index}(j)}\). Without a learned value baseline or discounting, how does GRPO handle credit assignment in long chains-of-thought? Does dividing by \(|o_i|\) fully mitigate length bias, and did you observe instability for very long outputs?
Answer:
Short answer: GRPO removes the learned critic by using a group-based baseline and (when available) step-wise rewards so that token-level credit is obtained by simple Monte‑Carlo returns computed across the sampled group. Outcome supervision gives a coarse, whole-output credit (every token in an output shares the same standardized reward); process supervision gives fine-grained, causal credit by summing future step rewards for each token. Dividing the per-output contribution by $|o_i|$ (the length) mitigates the raw gradient-magnitude bias from longer outputs but does not magically remove all length-dependent effects (early tokens naturally get more cumulative return in process supervision). In the paper’s experiments (max length 1024, group size $G=64$) these design choices plus clipping and KL regularization produced stable training and better performance with process supervision; the authors do not report instability for the lengths they used, but very long CoTs beyond their regime could still raise variance and require further remedies.

Why no critic still works (intuitively and technically). A critic in PPO is used to provide a baseline / low‑variance estimate of expected return from a state. GRPO replaces a parametric critic with a Monte‑Carlo baseline computed over a group of outputs for the same question. For outcome supervision the paper computes
$$
\widetilde r_i=\frac{r_i-\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf r)}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\hat A_{i,t}=\widetilde r_i\;\text{ for all }t,
$$
so gradients are proportional to the standardized group-relative reward (this is just REINFORCE with a group baseline and standardization). Because GRPO samples many outputs per question (they use $G=64$), the group mean/std give a fairly low-variance baseline and a scale-normalized advantage, which is a cheap alternative to training a separate value network.

How credit is assigned across tokens (derivation that makes the mechanism explicit). For process supervision the paper scores intermediate reasoning steps and normalizes all those step rewards across the whole group:
$$
\widetilde r_i^{\mathrm{index}(j)}=\frac{r_i^{\mathrm{index}(j)}-\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf R)}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf R)},
\qquad
\hat A_{i,t}=\sum_{\mathrm{index}(j)\ge t}\widetilde r_i^{\mathrm{index}(j)}.
$$
Plugging this into the usual policy‑gradient term inside the GRPO objective and reordering the sums yields
$$
\frac{1}{|o_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|}\hat A_{i,t}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\!\mid\!q,o_{i,<t})
= \frac{1}{|o_i|}\sum_j \widetilde r_i^{\mathrm{index}(j)}\sum_{t\le \mathrm{index}(j)}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\!\mid\!q,o_{i,<t}).
$$
This algebra makes the credit‑assignment explicit: each step reward $\widetilde r^{\mathrm{index}(j)}$ multiplies the sum of log‑prob gradients of tokens up to that step, so a high step reward reinforces the prefix tokens that led to that step. In other words, process supervision implements the usual Monte‑Carlo return (with implicit discount $\gamma=1$) but computed at step boundaries and normalized across the group — giving causal, backward-propagated credit without a learned value function.

What the $|o_i|$ division does (and does not) do. The objective uses the per-output normalization factor $1/|o_i|$ (this is the same per‑token averaging used in many tokenized RL objectives). That factor prevents the total gradient contribution from scaling linearly with sequence length: in outcome supervision, the gradient for output $i$ is essentially $\widetilde r_i\cdot(1/|o_i|)\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_{i,t}$, so averaging keeps long outputs from dominating simply because they have more tokens. However, that normalization does not remove structure-dependent effects: in process supervision earlier tokens accumulate more future step rewards (so their raw advantages are larger), and if raw rewards are correlated with length (e.g., longer answers are more likely to contain the correct final step), normalization across the group will reduce but cannot perfectly eliminate that correlation. Put another way, the $|o_i|$ factor controls per‑output gradient magnitude, not the causal role of tokens in producing future steps; that causal role is intentionally preserved so that earlier reasoning steps receive credit for later good outcomes.

Stability and practical notes from the paper. The paper stabilizes GRPO training using several standard practices: advantage standardization within each sampled group (mean and std), PPO‑style clipping in the surrogate objective, an explicit KL regularizer to a reference policy (estimated per token with an unbiased estimator), and relatively large group sampling ($G=64$) together with a max length of 1024 and single policy update per exploration stage. Empirically these choices made GRPO with process supervision outperform outcome supervision and online RFT on their math benchmarks, and the authors do not report training instability at the lengths they used. They also discuss that GRPO trades off the memory/computation of a critic for higher sampling cost and variance control via group normalization; for much longer chains‑of‑thought you should expect variance to grow and may want to consider (if needed) discounting / GAE‑style smoothing, a small value head, or stronger process rewards — all of which are suggested directions in the paper’s discussion.

Bottom line: GRPO handles token credit without a learned critic by (1) using a group mean/std as a cheap baseline and standardizer, and (2) using process supervision (when available) to form Monte‑Carlo backward returns that assign credit to prefix tokens. Dividing by $|o_i|$ removes the raw gradient magnitude bias from length but doesn’t and shouldn’t erase the causal longer‑horizon credit that earlier tokens legitimately deserve; within the paper’s experimental regime (max length 1024, $G=64$) these choices were effective and stable.
