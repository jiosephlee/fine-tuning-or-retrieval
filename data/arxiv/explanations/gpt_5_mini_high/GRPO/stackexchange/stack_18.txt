\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### What explains the lack of gains from arXiv-only training, and did you control for LaTeX tokenization and boilerplate?
Question:
You observe that training on MathPile or ArXiv-RedPajama yields no improvement or even deterioration on GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-STEM, and miniF2F. Could this be due to LaTeX-heavy tokenization, math symbol coverage in the tokenizer, or residual boilerplate (references, macros) hurting signal-to-noise? Did you test smaller arXiv mixtures or specialized tokenizers?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper tested arXiv-only training (MathPile and a cleaned ArXiv-RedPajama) and found no improvement — sometimes a drop — on GSM8K, MATH, MMLU‑STEM and miniF2F. They did control for some LaTeX boilerplate (ArXiv‑RedPajama had preambles, comments, macros and bibliographies removed), but the paper does not report any experiments with specialized tokenizers or systematic mixes (small arXiv fractions). The authors explicitly list those as open limitations and leave finer-grained tokenization/mixture studies to future work.

What the paper actually did and controlled for: the authors trained on two arXiv-flavored corpora (MathPile — heavily arXiv, $\sim 8.9B$ tokens — and ArXiv‑RedPajama — full LaTeX dump with preambles/comments/macros/bibliographies removed, $\sim 28B$ tokens). They trained DeepSeek‑LLM $1.3B$ for $150B$ tokens and DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5 $7B$ for $40B$ tokens on each arXiv corpus and observed no notable gains (and sometimes degradation) on the listed benchmarks. So they already tried both a heuristically cleaned arXiv aggregate (MathPile) and an arXiv LaTeX set where common boilerplate was removed (ArXiv‑RedPajama), which addresses some of your boilerplate concern.

Why LaTeX/tokenization/boilerplate remain plausible explanations (but not proven in the paper): arXiv papers are stylistically different from the benchmarks and from web math pages used in DeepSeekMath (which come from filtered CommonCrawl). That creates a domain/distribution mismatch: arXiv text is dense, formula-heavy and uses many TeX macros and symbol sequences that may be tokenized into rare pieces. Two intuitive effects follow. First, a tokenizer trained for general code / web text may fragment math notation into lots of low-frequency subwords, making learning slow/noisy for those tokens and reducing signal-to-noise for the downstream chain-of-thought style reasoning benchmarks. Second, arXiv content places heavy mass on compact symbolic expressions and formal exposition rather than the step-by-step natural‑language solutions GSM8K/MATH demand, so an arXiv-only pretraining signal can shift model behavior away from the conversational, chain-of-thought style the benchmarks reward. The paper’s removal of macros/preamble mitigates boilerplate, but it does not by itself rule out tokenization mismatch or that remaining arXiv content is simply the “wrong” style for these downstream tasks.

What the paper did not (but should) test: the authors note they did not study (1) the effect of arXiv tokens when combined with other data (mixture ratios), (2) whether arXiv helps at larger model scales, and (3) task types beyond the chosen benchmarks. They also do not report any experiments with alternative or math‑aware tokenizers, nor any tokenization diagnostics (vocabulary coverage, rare-token counts, or tokenization length for math environments). So the specific hypotheses you raise (LaTeX-heavy tokenization, tokenizer symbol coverage, residual boilerplate still lowering SNR) were not isolated experimentally in the paper.

Practical diagnostic and follow-up experiments you can run (short, actionable):
- Measure tokenization statistics on a representative arXiv sample vs the DeepSeekMath web math sample and the target benchmarks: tokens-per-character, fraction of tokens containing backslashes/LaTeX delimiters, OOV/rare-token frequency, and token entropy. Large systematic differences point to tokenizer mismatch.
- Run small controlled pretraining/fine‑tuning runs (cheap proxies) where you keep total compute fixed and sweep arXiv mixture fraction (e.g., $0\%$, $5\%$, $20\%$, $50\%$, $100\%$). Evaluate GSM8K/MATH and track perplexity on a held-out benchmark-like set to detect distribution shift vs only final accuracy.
- Ablate LaTeX content: detex (replace math with canonical placeholders or MathML), remove math environments entirely, or keep only non‑formula paragraphs. If removing math environments improves downstream accuracy compared to raw arXiv, that supports the “notation noise / tokenizer fragmentation” hypothesis.
- Try lightweight tokenizer hacks: add high-frequency LaTeX macros and common symbol sequences as single tokens (extend vocabulary + random‑init embeddings), or pre-process LaTeX to canonicalize macros before tokenization. Measure whether these help downstream fine-tuning and reduce token fragmentation.
- Compare arXiv-only vs arXiv+web‑math (DeepSeekMath) mixtures. The paper suggests arXiv might be helpful in other tasks or at larger scales, so a mixture sweep can reveal beneficial ratios.
- Finally, evaluate contextual/perplexity diagnostics: compute the model’s perplexity on benchmark prompts before and after arXiv training. An increased perplexity on benchmark-like text after arXiv-only training would quantify harmful distributional drift.

Conclusion: the paper already removed obvious LaTeX boilerplate for one arXiv variant and still found no benefit, so residual boilerplate alone is unlikely to be the whole story. But the paper did not measure tokenizer coverage or test specialized tokenizers or mixture ratios, so LaTeX-heavy tokenization and symbol sparsity remain plausible culprits. The clean next steps are the tokenization diagnostics and small controlled mixture/detex experiments suggested above — they will quickly tell you whether the problem is tokenization/coverage, domain mismatch, or an irrelevance of arXiv-style text for these benchmarks.
