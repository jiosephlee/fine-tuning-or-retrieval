\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### With \(G=64\) samples per question, is GRPO actually more compute-efficient than PPO with a critic?
Question:
You argue GRPO reduces memory by removing the critic, but GRPO requires sampling \(G=64\) outputs per question and scoring them. In practice (for a 7B policy), how do wall-clock time, GPU memory, and tokens processed per second compare between GRPO (no critic, many samples) and PPO (critic of similar size, fewer samples)?
Answer:
Short answer: GRPO with $G = 64$ trades off a large extra generation (inference) cost for substantially lower memory pressure. If you measure pure FLOPs or wall‑clock when both sampling and training are run on the same GPUs, GRPO ($G=64$) will typically require many$\times$ more compute and will process far fewer training tokens per second than PPO with a same‑sized critic. However, GRPO can be far easier to run on small / memory‑constrained clusters because it removes a second value model (and its optimizer states), and that practical memory saving is exactly why the paper chose GRPO.

Why, and how big is the tradeoff (back‑of‑the‑envelope):

Let $F$ be the cost (in time or FLOPs) of one forward pass for one token of the policy, and $B$ the extra cost of doing the backward/update work per token (a commonly used rough rule is $B \approx 2\text{--}3\times F$). Let $L$ be the average generation length per sampled output, $Q$ the number of questions in a training batch, $G$ the group size used by GRPO ($64$ in your question), and let $g_p$ be the number of samples per question used by PPO (many PPO pipelines use $g_p = 1$). If both algorithms perform $k$ training epochs over the collected samples per iteration, a simple model for the per‑iteration compute (ignoring small constants and bookkeeping) is:

- GRPO compute $ \approx Q\cdot L\cdot G\cdot (F + k\cdot (F + B))$
- PPO compute  $ \approx Q\cdot L\cdot g_p\cdot (F + 2k\cdot (F + B))$   (the factor $2k$ arises because PPO must update both the policy and the critic)

Dividing and canceling $Q\cdot L$ gives the ratio

$$
R \;=\; \frac{\text{Compute}_{\text{GRPO}}}{\text{Compute}_{\text{PPO}}}
\;=\; \frac{G\bigl(F + k(F+B)\bigr)}{g_p\bigl(F + 2k(F+B)\bigr)} .
$$

Take the common setting $g_p = 1$ and plug a representative backward/forward ratio $B = 3F$. If both methods do the same number of epochs $k$ (e.g. $k=1$ or $k=4$), this simplifies to

$$
R \;=\; G\cdot\frac{1+4k}{1+8k}.
$$

For $k$ in the $1$–$4$ range this prefactor is about $0.52$–$0.56$, so roughly

$$
R \approx 0.53\; G.
$$

With $G = 64$ this gives $R \approx 34$. In plainer language: with the simple assumptions above GRPO performs on the order of $30\times$–$40\times$ more raw forward/backward work per RL iteration than PPO that uses a similarly sized critic. Even with different epoch counts the break‑even $G$ is small: solving $R = 1$ yields $G \approx 1.8$–$2.0$ for realistic $k$, so any $G \ge 3$ already makes GRPO heavier in pure compute.

What that means for wall‑clock / tokens/sec / memory in practice

1) Wall‑clock time and tokens/sec. If everything (generation and training) runs on the same training GPUs with no special parallelization or inference offload, the extra inference work in GRPO ($G$ times more outputs to generate) will usually dominate and make GRPO much slower wall‑clock per RL iteration and will reduce tokens processed per second by roughly the factor $1/R$ above. In other words, if PPO processes $X$ tokens/s in your setup, GRPO ($G=64$) will often be $O(1/30)$–$O(1/40)$ of that on the same machines unless you overlap or offload sampling.

2) GPU memory. A concrete practical saving is large. A 7B model in 16‑bit weights is on the order of ~14 GB of raw weights ($7\times 10^{9}$ params $\times$ 2 bytes), and adding optimizer/ema states and activation memory increases the per‑model resident memory substantially. If PPO uses a second critic of comparable size, you effectively need space for two full networks plus their optimizer state (unless you aggressively shard or share parameters). Removing the critic therefore removes ~one model worth of weights + optimizer states + activation memory during critic backpropagation; that can easily be tens of GB of GPU RAM saved and is often the limiting resource that forces more complicated sharding or more GPUs. The paper’s motivation is exactly this: GRPO “significantly reduce[s] training resources” by eliminating the extra value model.

3) Implementation and parallelism change the story. The raw FLOP argument above assumes everything is done on the same GPUs and serially. In real systems you can mitigate the GRPO inference burden: generate samples in very large batched decoding (amortizes attention costs), offload generation to inference‑optimized machines, use lower precision / quantized inference, employ speculative decoding, or run sampling in parallel across many cheap instances while the trainer holds the policy weights. With these mitigations GRPO’s wall‑clock penalty can be reduced substantially. The paper’s experimental choice ($G = 64$ with a 7B policy) implicitly relies on such engineering choices: they accept much more sampling work to keep the memory demand low enough to train on available hardware.

Practical guidance

If you have ample memory / GPUs and want the smallest total FLOP cost (and usually best tokens/sec), PPO with a small or same‑size critic is likely more compute/time efficient per training step. If you are memory constrained (single node or limited GPU RAM), GRPO is an attractive practical choice because it avoids the extra model and optimizer states and often makes the RL stage feasible; you then pay the cost in extra inference work, which you can mitigate by offloading/parallelizing generation or by reducing $G$ (e.g., try $G = 8$–$16$ as a knob). Another middle ground is to shrink the critic (lightweight value head or shared encoder) rather than a full separate 7B network.

If you want to decide for your exact hardware, measure two things on your infra and plug into the formula above: (a) generation latency to produce one output of average length $L$ (so you know $F\cdot L$), and (b) cost/time to run one policy+critic update for a batch of samples (so you know the PPO denominator). Then compute the break‑even $G$ from

$$
G^* \;=\; \frac{F + 2k(F+B)}{F + k(F+B)}
$$

and compare to $64$. For most reasonable $k$ and $B$ the break‑even $G$ is $\sim 2$, i.e., $G=64$ is firmly on the side of higher compute but lower memory.

Bottom line: GRPO ($G=64$) is almost certainly more compute‑intensive (and will process fewer tokens/sec if you don’t specially parallelize sampling) than PPO with a same‑sized critic, but it can be far more memory‑efficient and therefore the only practical option (or the faster one in wall‑clock) on memory‑constrained setups or when you can offload/parallelize the heavy sampling work. The paper’s claim is about reducing the memory/training‑resource burden rather than claiming lower pure compute/FLOP cost.
