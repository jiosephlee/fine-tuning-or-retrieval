\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Are GRPO’s loss-level KL penalties equivalent to PPO’s reward-level KL penalties in expectation?
Question:
PPO typically incorporates per-token KL via a penalty in the reward, e.g., \(r_t = r_\varphi(q,o_{\le t}) - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})}{\pi_{ref}(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})}\). GRPO instead adds \(\beta\, \mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_{\theta} \Vert \pi_{ref}]\) directly to the loss. Are these two regularizations equivalent (up to constants) under the clipped objective and sequence-level averaging, or do they lead to systematically different gradient directions? Any empirical observations on stability vs. controllability of KL with GRPO?
Answer:
Short answer: not strictly equivalent as optimization trajectories, but equivalent at the local (first‑order) level where you evaluate the surrogate gradient. Concretely, if you linearize the PPO surrogate at the sampling policy (the usual place where the policy gradient is computed) and you use the same per‑token advantages (i.e. you do not mix KL into the advantage in GRPO), then GRPO’s additive loss‑level KL produces the same expected policy‑gradient contribution as PPO’s reward‑level per‑token KL. However, once you account for clipping, different advantage/baseline schemes (value+GAE in PPO vs. GRPO’s group normalization), the KL estimator choice, and finite updates, the two give different update dynamics and can produce systematically different gradients in practice. The paper’s GRPO design trades the entanglement of the KL with advantage/importance‑ratio for a direct regularizer (and an unbiased KL estimator), which simplifies advantage calculation and reduces memory; empirically GRPO yields stable training and big metric gains on math benchmarks in this work, but the paper does not publish a head‑to‑head plot of KL control vs PPO.

Why they match at the local gradient (math sketch)
Start from the usual (simplified) surrogate without clipping and consider one token/context for clarity. PPO with a per‑token KL added into the reward (paper’s equation for the reward) generates trajectories with rewards computed at the sampling policy $\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}$; write the surrogate (importance‑sampling form

$$
J_{\rm PPO}(\theta)\;=\; \mathbb{E}_{o\sim\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(o)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(o)}\,A_{\rm old}(o)\right],
$$

where $A_{\rm old}$ is the advantage computed from rewards that include the per‑token KL evaluated at sampling time:
$$
A_{\rm old}(o)=A_r(o)\;-\;\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(o)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(o)}.
$$
Differentiating the surrogate at $\theta=\theta_{\rm old}$ gives (using $\nabla_\theta (\pi_\theta/\pi_{\rm old})|_{\theta_{\rm old}} = \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta|_{\theta_{\rm old}}$):

$$
\nabla_\theta J_{\rm PPO}(\theta)\Big|_{\theta_{\rm old}}
= \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\rm old}}\!\big[ A_{\rm old}(o)\, \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o)\big]\Big|_{\theta_{\rm old}}
= \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\rm old}}\!\Big[\big(A_r(o)-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\rm old}}{\pi_{\rm ref}}\big)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o)\Big]\Big|_{\theta_{\rm old}}.
$$

Now GRPO’s surrogate (again removing clipping for this derivation) is the same importance‑weighted policy term but with an additive KL penalty in the loss (paper’s Equation (3) schematic):

$$
J_{\rm GRPO}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\rm old}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(o)}{\pi_{\rm old}(o)}\,\hat A(o)\right] - \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_\theta\Vert\pi_{\rm ref}],
$$

where $\hat A$ is the advantage computed from reward model scores alone (no KL inside). Differentiate the KL term; using the identity (expand $D_{KL}$ and use $\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta=\pi_\theta\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta$ and $\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta]=0$) one gets

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_\theta\Vert\pi_{\rm ref}]
= \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!\Big[\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\rm ref}}\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta\Big].
$$

Evaluate that gradient at $\theta=\theta_{\rm old}$ and combine with the policy term:

$$
\nabla_\theta J_{\rm GRPO}(\theta)\Big|_{\theta_{\rm old}}
= \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\rm old}}\!\big[\hat A(o)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o)\big]
- \beta\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\rm old}}\!\Big[\log\frac{\pi_{\rm old}}{\pi_{\rm ref}}\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o)\Big].
$$

If $\hat A=A_r$ (i.e. the advantage used in GRPO is the reward‑only advantage), the two gradients are identical at $\theta_{\rm old}$. The identity $\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta]=0$ is the simple normalization fact that makes the extra “+1” term that appears in some algebra vanish.

Where practical differences come from
The algebra above shows why the two constructions are not fundamentally contradictory: moving a $-\beta\log\pi$ term from reward into the loss rearranges terms but yields the same first‑order policy gradient at the sampling point. But in practice several things break exact equivalence and make the updates and trajectories different:

1. Clipping / nonlinearity. PPO’s min/clip is a non‑linear operation on the importance ratio. Even though the two objectives agree at $\theta=\theta_{\rm old}$, clipping changes higher‑order behavior, so finite updates can yield different directions and magnitudes.

2. Baseline / advantage computation. PPO normally uses a learned value function and GAE, while GRPO uses group‑relative normalization of sampled outputs (the paper’s group advantage). Different baselines change the per‑token coefficients multiplying $\nabla\log\pi$, so gradients differ beyond the local equivalence.

3. Which distribution KL is evaluated on and estimator variance. GRPO uses an unbiased estimator for $D_{KL}[\pi_\theta\Vert\pi_{\rm ref}]$ (the paper cites a specific estimator) and places the KL explicitly in the loss; PPO’s reward‑KL is evaluated at sampling time (so it appears inside the advantage). That difference affects variance and how the penalty reacts across iterations.

4. Sequence‑level averaging is linear, so it does not break the local equivalence (sums/averages commute with expectations) — but the other points above do.

Empirical behavior reported in the paper
The paper does not publish a side‑by‑side KL‑control curve comparing GRPO vs PPO, so you won’t find a direct plot of “mean KL vs steps” there. What the paper does report is that GRPO (with a KL coefficient $\beta=0.04$, sampling $G=64$ outputs per question, and their unbiased KL estimator) is memory‑efficient (it removes the critic), trains stably, and produces large gains on math benchmarks (DeepSeekMath‑RL 7B: GSM8K increased to 88.2\%, MATH to 51.7\% in their setup). The authors also argue (and show in their unified‑paradigm analysis) that putting KL in the loss decouples it from advantage computation and avoids complicating $\hat A$, which is an implementation and stability advantage.

Practical takeaways
If you want exact first‑order mimicry of PPO’s reward‑KL behavior for small updates and you match the advantage/baseline and sampling policy, GRPO’s additive KL gives the same expected gradient at the update point. If you care about (a) simpler advantage computation, (b) removing the value network and lowering memory, or (c) directly monitoring an explicit $D_{KL}[\pi_\theta\Vert\pi_{\rm ref}]$ term, GRPO’s loss‑level KL is an attractive, practical choice. If you want the exact finite‑step dynamics of PPO (including clipping interactions), you should expect differences and should tune $\beta$ / clipping / learning rate accordingly. The paper demonstrates GRPO is effective and stable for their math‑RL pipeline but does not claim or show that GRPO strictly controls KL better than PPO in every setting — only that it is simpler to compute and works well in their experiments.
