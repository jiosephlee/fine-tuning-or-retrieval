\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does dividing the GRPO objective by sequence length interact with length bias and group normalization?
Question:
The GRPO loss averages per token, i.e., multiplies clipped ratios by \(\hat{A}_{i,t}\) and averages over \(t=1..|o_i|\). With outcome supervision where \(\hat{A}_{i,t}=\tilde r_i\) for all tokens, this seems to make the total update magnitude per sample roughly proportional to \(\tilde r_i\), independent of \(|o_i|\). Does this eliminate incentives for verbosity or terseness, or did you still observe length-related failure modes?
Answer:
Short answer: dividing the GRPO surrogate by sequence length does remove the obvious first‑order incentive to “game” the optimizer by making outputs longer (or shorter) just to increase the total gradient magnitude when you use outcome supervision with a constant per‑token advantage. However it does not guarantee there are no length‑related incentives left — those can reappear indirectly through (a) the reward model’s length sensitivity and group normalization, (b) process (step‑level) rewards, (c) interaction with clipping and the per‑token KL term, and (d) the mechanics of sampling/credit assignment. The paper does not report any catastrophic length collapse caused by GRPO, but it also does not present a focused length‑bias study, so you should monitor length in practice.

Why the obvious cancellation happens (math intuition). Write the per‑output contribution to the GRPO objective (using the paper’s notation) as
$$
J_i \;=\; \frac{1}{|o_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|}\Big(\text{min}\big[ r_{i,t}\,\hat A_{i,t},\;\text{clip}(r_{i,t})\,\hat A_{i,t}\big] - \beta\; \mathrm{D}_{KL,t}\Big),
$$
where $r_{i,t}=\frac{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}$ and $\mathrm{D}_{KL,t}$ is the per‑token KL estimator. Under outcome supervision the paper sets $\hat A_{i,t}=\widetilde r_i$ (a constant across tokens of output $i$), so the advantage factors out:
$$
J_i \;\approx\; \widetilde r_i\;\frac{1}{|o_i|}\sum_{t} \text{min}[r_{i,t},\text{clip}(r_{i,t})] \;-\; \beta\;\frac{1}{|o_i|}\sum_t\mathrm{D}_{KL,t}.
$$
If ratios are near 1 (or clipping is inert) the gradient produced by the first term is roughly
$$
\widetilde r_i\;\frac{1}{|o_i|}\sum_{t}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid\cdot)
\;=\;
\widetilde r_i\;\frac{1}{|o_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o_i\mid q).
$$
Because $\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o_i\mid q)=\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid\cdot)$ typically grows roughly linearly with $|o_i|$ (each token contributes a similar magnitude), the $1/|o_i|$ factor cancels that growth and the per‑sequence update magnitude becomes proportional to $\widetilde r_i$ rather than to $|o_i|$. That algebra is the reason your intuition is correct at the first order: GRPO’s per‑token averaging removes the trivial “longer = bigger gradient” channel.

Why length bias can still appear (mechanisms). The cancellation above only removes a simple scaling effect. Several other mechanisms can reintroduce length dependence in practice. First, the reward model itself may prefer particular lengths (e.g., longer, more detailed CoT answers can systematically score higher); group normalization (subtract mean and divide by std across the G outputs) makes the normalized advantages depend on the distribution of raw rewards across outputs, and that distribution usually correlates with length, so length-related preferences survive in the signed advantage $\widetilde r_i$. Second, process supervision (the paper’s alternate mode) sets token advantages as cumulative sums of future step rewards, $\hat A_{i,t}=\sum_{j\ge t}\widetilde r_i^{\text{index}(j)}$, and then
$$
\frac{1}{|o_i|}\sum_t\hat A_{i,t}=\frac{1}{|o_i|}\sum_j \text{index}(j)\,\widetilde r_i^{\text{index}(j)},
$$
which depends on step positions and thus on how many steps/tokens the output contains; process supervision therefore reintroduces length sensitivity in a structured way. Third, the per‑token KL regularizer and clipping operate tokenwise; although both are averaged by $1/|o_i|$ in the objective, the optimizer’s per‑token updates and clipping mean the path the model takes through parameter space depends on how many tokens are present and where high‑leverage tokens are located. Finally, sampling and decoding biases (temperature, nucleus, beam search) interact with the learned policy in ways that can favor shorter or longer outputs even if the nominal gradient magnitude does not.

What the paper says/observes. The paper describes outcome and process supervision, the group normalization of rewards, and the per‑token averaging in GRPO and reports strong empirical gains from GRPO using these designs. It does not present a focused analysis of length bias or report a systematic failure mode caused by verbosity/terseness. So there is no evidence in the paper of a fatal length pathology, but there is also no detailed ablation that proves length is harmless.

Practical checks and mitigations. In practice you should monitor the post‑RL length distribution and inspect reward vs length plots. If you see undesirable drift, simple mitigations consistent with the GRPO framework are available: compute baselines stratified by length (i.e., normalize rewards within length buckets), add a small explicit length penalty to the reward model, or include length features in the reward model so it can learn to be length‑invariant when appropriate. Using process supervision carefully (designing step rewards that are not simply proportional to chain length) and tuning the KL coefficient $\beta$ are other practical levers. These ideas fit naturally into the paper’s “Data Source / Algorithm / Reward Function” paradigm for making RL more robust.

Bottom line: the per‑token averaging in GRPO removes the obvious per‑sequence scaling incentive under constant per‑token advantages, but length‑related incentives can still enter indirectly via the reward model, normalization, process rewards, clipping/KL interactions and decoding. The paper does not report a severe length failure mode, but it is sensible to monitor and, if needed, use length‑conditioned baselines or explicit length penalties.
