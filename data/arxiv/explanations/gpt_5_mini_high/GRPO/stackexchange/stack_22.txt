\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### What prevents degenerate behaviors (verbosity, hedging, premature EoS) when only final outcomes are rewarded?
Question:
Under outcome supervision, the only reward is at the end of the output. Besides the KL term, do you include any format or extraction rewards to encourage concise, well-structured rationales? Did process supervision measurably reduce verbosity or improve intermediate step correctness compared to outcome-only GRPO?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper relies on three practical mechanisms (rather than an explicit “format” reward) to limit degenerate behaviors under outcome-only reward: (1) a KL regularizer that anchors the policy to the SFT reference (which itself was trained on well‑formed CoT/PoT answers), (2) GRPO’s group‑relative baseline / normalization (large group sampling) which makes rewards comparative and reduces variance, and (3) — when used — process supervision that gives step‑level rewards and therefore much better credit assignment than final‑only signals. The authors do not report any separate “format/extraction” reward term; instead they use the KL term plus the process reward model and iterative reward‑model updates. Empirically the paper reports that process supervision (GRPO+PS) outperforms outcome‑only GRPO (GRPO+OS), i.e., the step‑aware reward improves training outcomes, though the paper does not publish a direct verbosity metric (average length / hedge rate).

Why these mechanisms matter (intuitively + technically). The classical failure mode under final‑only reward is simple credit‑assignment / reward‑hacking: if every token in a run gets the same advantage, the optimizer can “cheat” by producing very short answers, hedging language, or emitting an early EoS that sometimes accidentally yields the correct final answer. GRPO addresses this in two ways that are described in the paper.

First, outcome supervision in GRPO still uses a group relative baseline: for a question you sample a group of outputs and compute normalized rewards across that group. Concretely the paper sets the per‑token advantage for outcome supervision to
$$\hat{A}_{i,t}=\widetilde r_i=\frac{r_i-\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf r)},$$
so tokens in an output are reinforced or penalized relative to other sampled outputs. Because rewards are comparative, obviously poor / degenerate outputs tend to have lower relative reward and are pushed down. The authors use a large group size (e.g., $G=64$) when sampling outputs, which makes the group baseline robust and reduces the chance that the policy can gain by producing trivial but lucky short answers.

Second, the training objective contains an explicit KL regularization term that keeps the learned policy close to the SFT reference model. In GRPO the KL is added directly to the loss (rather than embedded into the scalar reward) as
$$- \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{KL}\big[\pi_\theta\Vert\pi_{\text{ref}}\big]$$
(where the paper uses an unbiased estimator for $\mathbb{D}_{KL}$). Because the reference is the SFT model trained on chain‑of‑thought / program‑of‑thought examples, this term discourages large shifts toward degenerate decoding behaviors (very short answers, random hedging tokens) that diverge strongly from the structured SFT outputs.

Third, and most directly for the credit assignment problem, process supervision supplies fine‑grained step rewards rather than a single terminal score. The paper defines normalized step rewards
$$\widetilde r_i^{\text{index}(j)}=\frac{r_i^{\text{index}(j)}-\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf R)}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf R)}$$
and assigns each token the sum of future step rewards:
$$\hat{A}_{i,t}=\sum_{\text{index}(j)\ge t}\widetilde r_i^{\text{index}(j)}.$$ 
This makes earlier tokens responsible for enabling later correct steps: premature EoS or hedging that halts the chain will stop accruing future step reward, so those behaviors receive negative relative advantage. In short, process supervision is reward‑shaping that provides a strong, fine‑grained learning signal for the intermediate reasoning steps and hence reduces incentives for degenerate shortcuts.

What the paper measured. The authors explicitly compare outcome supervision (OS) and process supervision (PS) variants and report that “GRPO+PS shows superior performance compared to GRPO+OS,” which demonstrates that step‑aware (process) rewards improve final benchmark performance. They also observe that RL tends to improve Maj@K (making the correct output more likely among the top candidates) rather than Pass@K, consistent with RL stabilizing and concentrating probability on better (and usually better‑structured) outputs. The paper does not report a direct, quantitative reduction in "verbosity" (e.g., mean token length, explicit hedging counts), so if you need a measured verbosity metric you would have to compute it from the logs or run an ablation that records output lengths and hedge tokens.

To answer your two explicit subquestions: no — beyond the KL term and the reward models (outcome or process), the paper does not introduce an extra “format/extraction” reward specifically designed to force concise/rationale structure; instead they rely on (i) the SFT reference (which encodes the desirable format), (ii) the KL trust‑region regularizer, (iii) group‑relative normalization, and (iv) optional process supervision which gives stepwise signals that implicitly discourage fluff and premature EoS. And yes — process supervision measurably improved training outcomes relative to outcome‑only GRPO (the paper’s GRPO+PS vs GRPO+OS comparison), even though the paper stops short of reporting direct verbosity statistics.

If you want to further reduce hedging or enforce concise structure in future runs, the most direct extensions (not described in the paper) are explicit length penalties or format classifiers used as auxiliary reward terms, or a stepwise reward model trained to penalize irrelevant tokens; but the paper’s combination of KL anchoring, group normalization and process supervision already addresses the main failure modes associated with terminal‑only rewards.
