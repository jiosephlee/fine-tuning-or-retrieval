\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why use a 10% threshold to flag math-related domains in Common Crawl mining, and what bias might this introduce?
Question:
You label a domain as math-related if over 10% of its pages are recalled by the fastText classifier, then manually annotate math-relevant URLs within that domain. How was the 10% chosen, and did you evaluate the risk of (a) missing sparse math sources or (b) over-including domains where a minority of math pages are highly templated, potentially biasing the corpus?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper treats the $10\%$ rule as a pragmatic heuristic to focus manual URL annotation on domains that are enriched in math pages; it does not report a formal ablation or a principled derivation of that exact cutoff. The authors mitigate both kinds of risk you name (missing sparse sources, and over‑representing templated math pages) through the iterative recall/annotation loop, ranking and (near)‑deduplication, and downstream validation, but they do not present a targeted quantitative analysis that proves the $10\%$ choice is optimal or that those specific biases are fully eliminated.

Why $10\%$ (what the paper actually did)
In Section “Data Collection and Decontamination” the pipeline first runs a fastText classifier over deduplicated Common Crawl and records which pages are “recalled” as math in the first iteration. Domains are grouped by base URL and any domain where more than $10\%$ of its pages were recalled is flagged as “math‑related.” Within those flagged domains the authors then manually annotate math‑relevant URL paths (for example, mathoverflow.net/questions) and add pages under those paths that the classifier missed. This is embedded in an iterative loop: the enlarged seed set retrains the classifier, recall expands, and the process repeats until marginal gains are tiny (they stopped after four iterations, noting that $\sim 98\%$ of the data from iteration 4 had already been collected in iteration 3).

Intuition behind the design
That $10\%$ acts as a practical trade‑off: it biases manual effort toward domains where math content is concentrated (higher precision per human‑annotation hour) while still allowing the iterative classifier to learn and pick up other math pages. In other words, it’s a precision/effort control rather than an attempt to be an optimal statistical cutoff. The pipeline then tries to recover diversity by (a) manually adding URL patterns (which pulls in entire math sections inside a domain), (b) iteratively retraining the fastText model to expand recall, and (c) ranking pages by classifier score and keeping top matches to filter out low‑quality noise.

How the paper addresses (and does not fully eliminate) the two risks you asked about
(a) Missing sparse math sources: the pipeline does not guarantee discovery of math pages that are very sparse and scattered across large general domains (e.g., occasional math blog posts buried on a news site). Such pages could fall below the $10\%$ domain signal and so would not trigger manual URL pattern harvesting. The paper’s mitigation is pragmatic: the classifier still directly recalls many positive pages across domains (so isolated pages can be picked up without domain flagging), and the iterative enrichment makes the classifier more diverse over iterations. But the paper does not present a dedicated measurement of recall on “sparse math” pages or an ablation that sweeps the domain threshold to show how many such pages would be recovered at different cutoffs.

(b) Over‑including templated math pages (template bias): the authors apply URL‑based deduplication and near‑deduplication early in the pipeline (reducing Common Crawl to 40B HTML pages) and they rank pages by classifier score to keep higher quality pages. Those steps help limit raw duplication from large numbers of near‑identical templated pages. Still, manually adding entire URL paths (e.g., all question pages under a /questions/ prefix) can legitimately bring in many similarly structured pages, and even after near‑deduplication a domain’s question pages can dominate the corpus. The paper acknowledges selection bias as a limitation (for example, they note in the Discussion that some geometry / ellipse/triangle problems are not well handled and this “may indicate data selection bias”), but it does not report a quantitative study showing how much templated pages skew token counts or downstream behavior.

What they did instead to validate the whole approach
Rather than reporting a threshold ablation, the paper validates the overall corpus by training models on it and comparing downstream benchmark performance against other math corpora (MathPile, OpenWebMath, Proof‑Pile‑2). Those pretraining experiments show substantially better downstream math performance for models trained on the DeepSeekMath corpus, which is evidence the heuristic pipeline produced useful data at scale. However, that validation is an end‑to‑end performance check and does not isolate the two specific risks you asked about.

Practical diagnostics / mitigations you might run (if you wanted to probe or improve this choice)
If you want to evaluate or harden the pipeline against the two failure modes, useful analyses are: sweep the domain‑flag threshold (e.g., $1\%$, $5\%$, $10\%$, $20\%$) and measure the marginal gain in unique math pages and in downstream validation performance per percent of additional manual annotation cost; sample random pages from large general domains (those with $<10\%$ recall) and estimate how many math pages the classifier missed (estimates of domain‑level recall); measure per‑domain token counts and the distribution of near‑duplicate cluster sizes to detect template dominance and then downsample heavy clusters; detect and filter “template” pages with structural signals (same HTML skeleton, low textual uniqueness) or by clustering embeddings and capping per‑cluster retention; and finally, report domain entropy (how concentrated tokens are among top domains) so you can quantify domain bias in the final corpus.

Bottom line
The $10\%$ cutoff is a practical heuristic used to focus scarce human annotation on concentrated math sources; it is not justified by a threshold sweep in the paper. The pipeline contains sensible mitigations (iterative retraining, URL‑pattern annotation, ranking by classifier score, URL‑based dedup and near‑dedup) and the authors validate the finished corpus by strong downstream gains, but they do not present a dedicated quantitative evaluation of missed sparse sources or of template over‑representation. The paper itself flags selection bias as a limitation and points to future work—so your concerns are precisely the kinds of evaluations the authors suggest as next steps.
