\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How is the reference model updated in GRPO, and does frequently setting \(\pi_{ref} \leftarrow \pi_{\theta}\) weaken KL control?
Question:
The algorithm sets \(\pi_{ref}\) to the current \(\pi_{\theta}\) at each outer iteration. This seems to keep the KL penalty small because the reference keeps chasing the policy. How often do you refresh \(\pi_{ref}\) in practice, and did you compare to a fixed SFT reference? How do you ensure sufficient regularization against drift or verbosity?
Answer:
Short answer: in GRPO the reference policy is re‑set to the current policy at the start of each outer iteration (Algorithm 1: $\pi_{ref} \leftarrow \pi_{\theta}$). In the paper’s experiments the authors run iterative GRPO (they report doing two outer iterations in their ablation) so the reference is refreshed once per outer iteration rather than on every gradient step. Setting the reference this way does reduce the long‑term pull back to the original SFT model (because the KL is measured to a recent snapshot rather than the original SFT), but it does not eliminate KL control — it turns the KL term into a local trust‑region that stabilizes the inner updates of each iteration rather than an absolute anchor to the initial SFT.

Why that still regularizes. GRPO adds a direct per‑token KL penalty to the GRPO objective (Equation (3)) rather than hiding KL in the reward, i.e. the objective contains a term like $-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_{\theta}\Vert\pi_{ref}]$ for the tokens being optimized. Although $\pi_{ref}=\pi_{\theta}$ at the moment you take the snapshot (so instantaneous KL is zero), subsequent inner updates are penalized for diverging from that snapshot. In addition GRPO keeps the usual PPO‑style ratio clipping (the $\varepsilon$ clip) and uses group‑relative advantage normalization (sample groups of size $G$ and normalize rewards) so updates are both variance‑reduced and bounded. The paper also computes the KL with an unbiased estimator
$$
\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_{\theta}\Vert\pi_{ref}]
=\frac{\pi_{ref}}{\pi_{\theta}}-\log\frac{\pi_{ref}}{\pi_{\theta}}-1
$$
evaluated token‑wise, and normalizes/ clips the surrogate objective — all of which together restrain runaway updates that could cause verbosity or degenerate behavior.

Practical details and the trade‑off. The authors give the concrete GRPO settings they used: policy learning rate $1\mathrm{e}{-6}$, KL coefficient $\beta=0.04$, group size $G=64$ (64 samples per question), batch size 1024, max length 1024, and “the policy model only has a single update following each exploration stage.” Those design choices (small LR, single update per exploration, large G, normalization, clipping and an explicit KL term) are how they keep sufficient regularization despite refreshing $\pi_{ref}$ frequently. In short: frequent refreshes weaken a global KL tie to the original SFT model (allowing the policy to drift across iterations), but they preserve a strong local KL / trust‑region that prevents sudden divergence and controls verbosity within each iteration.

Did they compare to a fixed SFT reference? The paper discusses that PPO traditionally uses the initial SFT as $\pi_{ref}$, and it frames GRPO as a different design (dynamic reference, group baseline, no critic). However, the paper does not present a dedicated ablation that replaces the dynamically refreshed $\pi_{ref}$ with a fixed SFT reference and reports that comparison. So the direct fixed‑vs‑dynamic reference ablation is not shown; the experimental results compare GRPO to other methods (RFT, DPO, PPO variants) but do not isolate “refresh frequency” as a reported ablation.

If you are worried about drift or verbosity in your own runs, the knobs you can tune (consistent with the paper’s choices) are: refresh less often (hold $\pi_{ref}$ fixed for more outer iterations) if you want stronger global anchoring to SFT; increase $\beta$ to penalize divergence more; reduce policy LR or the number of inner update steps; use process supervision (per‑step rewards) rather than outcome‑only rewards to shape stepwise behavior; add explicit length/verbosity penalties in the reward if needed; and monitor per‑update KL and output length statistics. The paper’s configuration (refresh per outer iteration + $\beta=0.04$, clipping, large $G$, normalized group advantages, single update per exploration) is their practical recipe to balance improvement and stability.
