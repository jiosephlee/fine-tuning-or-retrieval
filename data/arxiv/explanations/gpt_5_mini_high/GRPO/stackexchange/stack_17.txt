\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### The paper says 40B HTML web pages after deduplication—can you clarify whether this is pages or tokens?
Question:
The data collection section mentions reducing Common Crawl to 40B HTML web pages via URL and near-duplicate deduplication. That number seems very large for pages. Is this a typo for 40B tokens, or does it refer to unique URLs processed before filtering? Could you detail the dedup procedure and the actual page counts?
Answer:
Short answer: that “40B HTML web pages” in the Data Collection paragraph is almost certainly a units/wording error — the paper is talking about tokens in the selection experiments, not 40 billion distinct pages. The rest of the section makes this clear: the authors repeatedly describe selecting the “top 40B, 80B, 120B, and 160B tokens” and they report the final math corpus as 35.5M web pages totaling 120B tokens, so “40B web pages” would be inconsistent with those numbers.

Why I conclude it’s a typo and not literally pages. The pipeline text says they ranked classifier-scored pages and “only preserve the top-ranking ones,” and then explicitly says they tested preserving the top 40B/80B/120B/160B tokens and chose top 40B tokens in the first iteration. Later they report the final output of the iterative pipeline as 35.5M (million) mathematical web pages with 120B tokens. Those three facts cannot all be true if “40B” in the earlier sentence really meant “40 billion pages.” As a sanity check, the final 120B tokens over 35.5M pages implies an average of about
$$
\frac{120\times 10^9}{35.5\times 10^6}\approx 3380
$$
tokens per page, which is plausible for math-heavy HTML pages; by contrast 40 billion pages would be an absurdly large web-page count for a deduplicated Common Crawl subset and would not fit the described downstream token-selection experiments.

What the paper actually says they did (dedup + selection pipeline). They applied URL-based deduplication and “near-deduplication” to reduce Common Crawl (the paper does not give low-level implementation details for the near-dedup step). They trained a fastText classifier using OpenWebMath as seed positives and 500k randomly sampled Common Crawl pages as negatives (fastText hyperparameters: vector dim 256, learning rate 0.1, max word n-gram 3, min word occurrences 3, epochs 3). They ran the classifier over the deduplicated CC, ranked pages by score, and experimented with keeping the top 40B/80B/120B/160B tokens (keeping top 40B tokens in the first iteration). To widen recall they grouped pages by domain, marked domains where over 10% of pages were collected as math-related, manually annotated math-specific URL paths in those domains, added uncollected pages under those paths to the seed, retrained the classifier, and repeated this loop. After four iterations they obtained 35.5M math web pages totaling 120B tokens; by the fourth iteration they noted nearly 98% of the data had already been collected in iteration three. They also decontaminated the corpus by removing any text segment containing a 10-gram that exactly matches any evaluation benchmark substring (and used exact matches for shorter benchmark texts of length 3–9 grams).

If you need absolute confirmation for a paper citation or dataset release, the safest next step is to ask the authors (or check their code/data release) for an erratum or the exact deduplicated page count. A suggested, short question to the authors is: “In Section 2 you write ‘resulting in 40B HTML web pages’ but elsewhere you refer to top 40B tokens and later report 35.5M pages / 120B tokens. Did you mean 40B tokens (or some other unit) instead of 40B pages?”
