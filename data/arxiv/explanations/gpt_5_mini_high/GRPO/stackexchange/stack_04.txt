\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Does z-scoring rewards within each group bias GRPO toward relative rather than absolute quality?
Question:
For a question \(q\), GRPO samples \(G\) outputs, scores them, and uses group-normalized rewards \(\tilde r_i = (r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r}))/\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})\). In standard policy gradient, subtracting a baseline preserves unbiasedness, but dividing by a batch-dependent \(\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})\) changes the effective step size and relative scaling. Does this normalization change the optimal policy or only rescale gradients? How sensitive are results to \(G\) and to the variance of \(r_i\)?
Answer:
Short answer: z‑scoring (subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation) does not behave like the usual baseline-only trick that is guaranteed to leave the policy-gradient estimator unbiased. Subtracting a baseline preserves the expected gradient because the baseline term multiplies an expectation that is zero; dividing by a group-dependent standard deviation, however, introduces a multiplicative factor that depends on the sampled outputs (and on the question), so it reweights per‑question contributions and can change the fixed point (the “optimal” policy) unless the standard deviation is the same across all groups (or independent of the policy). Intuitively, GRPO’s z‑scoring emphasises relative rank within each group and, at the same time, weights questions with small reward spread more heavily than questions with large reward spread.

Why (mathematically) it is not a pure rescale. Let $J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{q}\mathbb{E}_{o\sim\pi_\theta}[r(q,o)]$ and recall the standard policy gradient
$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{q}\mathbb{E}_{o\sim\pi_\theta}\big[r(q,o)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o\mid q)\big].
$$
If you subtract a per‑question baseline $b(q)=\mathbb{E}[r\mid q]$ then
$$
\mathbb{E}_o\big[(r-b(q))\nabla\log\pi\big]=\mathbb{E}_o[r\nabla\log\pi]-b(q)\,\mathbb{E}_o[\nabla\log\pi]
=\mathbb{E}_o[r\nabla\log\pi],
$$
because $\mathbb{E}_o[\nabla\log\pi]=\nabla\int\pi=0$. So subtracting that baseline does not change the expected gradient.

If instead you use the population standard deviation $\sigma(q)=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[r\mid q]}$ and form the normalized advantage $A_{\mathrm{norm}}=(r-b(q))/\sigma(q)$, the gradient becomes
$$
\mathbb{E}_q\mathbb{E}_o\big[A_{\mathrm{norm}}\,\nabla\log\pi\big]
=\mathbb{E}_q\Big[\frac{1}{\sigma(q)}\,\mathbb{E}_o\big[(r-b(q))\nabla\log\pi\big]\Big]
=\mathbb{E}_q\Big[\frac{1}{\sigma(q)}\,\mathbb{E}_o\big[r\nabla\log\pi\big]\Big].
$$
So normalization inserts a multiplicative per‑question weight $1/\sigma(q)$. Unless $1/\sigma(q)$ is constant across $q$, that changes the weighting of different questions in the expected gradient and therefore can change the stationary point / optimal policy.

Two practical complications make the effect stronger in GRPO as implemented in the paper. First, GRPO uses the sample mean and sample standard deviation computed from the group of $G$ outputs, so the denominator $s$ is a random statistic that depends on the particular sampled outputs; for finite $G$ the normalization is noisy and correlated with the sampled actions, which introduces additional bias and variance in the gradient estimator. Second, $\sigma(q)$ itself depends on the current policy (different policies produce different reward distributions), so the reweighting is policy‑dependent and can change the optimization trajectory in substantive ways.

How sensitive this is to $G$ and to the reward variance. The sensitivity is exactly what you would expect from the previous paragraphs. With small $G$ the sample mean/std are noisy: dividing by a small (noisy) sample std can hugely amplify the gradient (instability) and produce high variance and bias; with larger $G$ the sample estimates converge toward the population mean/std, making the per‑question weight $1/\sigma(q)$ more stable. In the paper the authors use a fairly large group ($G=64$) during GRPO (see Section “Training and Evaluating DeepSeekMath-RL”), which reduces the sampling noise and makes the normalization behave like a deterministic per‑question reweighting (rather than a wildly noisy per‑batch multiplier). If reward variance across outputs is small, normalization can blow up the signal (small $\sigma$ $\Rightarrow$ large $1/\sigma$), so implementations must guard against division by (near) zero; if reward variance is large, normalization will shrink the learning signal for that question and slow its impact on the policy. Thus the net effect is that GRPO emphasizes relative ordering inside each group but differentially weights questions according to their reward spread.

Practical implications and remedies. The paper chooses this group‑relative normalization deliberately because reward models are typically trained on comparisons; GRPO’s group normalization therefore aligns the training signal with the comparative nature of the reward model and empirically yields strong gains (the paper reports clear improvements using $G=64$). If you care about preserving the original expected‑reward optimum, do not divide by a group‑dependent std (or use a population/global std that is decoupled from the sampled actions). If you accept a reweighted objective (or want a ranking‑style training signal), z‑scoring is fine but you should (a) use sufficiently large $G$ to reduce sampling noise (the authors’ $G=64$ is a practical example), (b) clamp or floor the std (add small $\varepsilon$) to avoid explosions when variance is tiny, and (c) tune learning rate / KL penalty because normalization changes effective step sizes across questions. Another alternative is to maintain running (policy‑independent) normalization statistics or to use a learned critic (PPO) if you want an unbiased advantage estimator at the cost of memory and compute.

Bottom line: group z‑scoring preserves within‑group ranking (so GRPO is still optimizing relative quality inside the group), but it generally does more than just rescale the global gradient — it reweights questions by $1/\sigma(q)$ and (for finite $G$) introduces additional sampling noise and bias. That reweighting is an intentional design choice in GRPO (to match comparative reward models) and the paper stabilizes it by sampling many outputs per question (they use $G=64$).
