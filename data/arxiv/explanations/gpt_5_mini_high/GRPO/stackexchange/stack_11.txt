\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How are reasoning step boundaries detected for process supervision to compute \(\mathrm{index}(j)\)?
Question:
Process rewards rely on step-end token indices \(\mathrm{index}(j)\) within chain-of-thought text. How are steps detected and token-aligned in free-form CoT? Are steps explicitly annotated with special tags, identified by regex rules (e.g., lines starting with "Step"), or predicted by a separate model? How robust is this across English and Chinese?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not detail a novel boundary‑detector — it treats $\mathrm{index}(j)$ abstractly as “the end token index of the $j$‑th step” (see the “Process Supervision RL with GRPO” subsection) and says process reward models are trained “following \citep{wang2023math}”. The paper therefore implies that step boundaries come from the step segmentation used to build the reward‑model training data (i.e., from the chain‑of‑thought style SFT data), but it does not give an explicit recipe such as “we used regex” or “we trained a separate boundary model.”

What that means in practice and how people usually implement it
- If you have gold CoT / SFT examples where the reasoning is already written in a stepwise form (numbered lines, “Step 1: …”, blank‑line separation, or an explicit special token), you can take those textual step boundaries as canonical and map them to token indices: tokenize the prefix up to each step end and set $\mathrm{index}(j)$ to the length (token index) of that prefix. This is the simplest, most robust approach and is the one implicitly suggested by “process supervision” that scores each step in the CoT outputs used to train the reward model.
- For sampled (model‑generated) outputs that were not gold‑annotated, common options are (a) require the model to emit explicit separators (so the same tokenizer mapping works), (b) apply simple heuristics/regex to detect enumerators or line breaks (fast but brittle, especially across languages), (c) run a trained step‑boundary classifier (sequence tagger) to predict step end positions, or (d) score multiple candidate prefixes with the process reward model and treat high‑score prefix positions as step ends (more compute‑heavy but language‑agnostic). The paper does not commit to any one of these choices; it only states that process rewards are assigned at step ends and normalized inside each group.

Token alignment detail (how $\mathrm{index}(j)$ is used)
Once step end character positions are known, you convert them to token indices with the same tokenizer used for the model. The paper’s advantage calculation uses these indices: the reward for the $j$‑th step is attached to token position $\mathrm{index}(j)$, and each token’s advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ sums the normalized rewards from all step ends with $\mathrm{index}(j)\ge t$ (exact formula in the text). So correct alignment requires the step boundary to be mapped to the last token of that step’s textual prefix.

Robustness across English and Chinese
The paper reports multilingual SFT data and improved Chinese benchmarks, which shows their overall pipeline (pretraining + SFT + GRPO) worked in a multilingual setting. But the paper gives no ablation that isolates “how step boundaries were detected” or compares heuristics versus explicit markers across languages. Practically, heuristics that rely on spaces, ASCII punctuation, or English enumerators are less reliable for Chinese; explicit step markers in SFT data or a learned boundary tagger are the more robust choices for multilingual process supervision.

If you want to reproduce this part (recommended practical recipe)
1. During SFT annotation, include explicit step separators (e.g., one step per line, or insert a special token like [STEP] at the end of each step).  
2. Train the process reward model on those annotated step‑aligned examples (the paper says they “construct the training set of reward models following Wang et al.”).  
3. For sampled outputs, either require the model to emit the same separators or run a lightweight boundary tagger trained on your annotated data.  
4. Map step text prefixes to token lengths with the exact tokenizer used by the policy to obtain $\mathrm{index}(j)$.

Bottom line: the paper defines $\mathrm{index}(j)$ and uses process rewards built from step‑segmented CoT examples, but does not publish the exact detection heuristic. For reliable multilingual process supervision, the safest choice is to use explicit step delimiters in your SFT data or train a boundary detector rather than relying solely on brittle regex rules.
