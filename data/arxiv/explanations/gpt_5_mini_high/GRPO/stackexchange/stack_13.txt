\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why does RL improve Maj@K but not Pass@K, and how exactly are these two metrics computed here?
Question:
You report that RL improves Maj@K but not Pass@K and hypothesize it makes the output distribution more robust rather than increasing fundamental capability. Could you provide your precise definitions and settings (values of \(K\), decoding parameters) and an interpretation: does RL increase the mode probability of correct solutions while reducing the diversity of correct-but-rare solutions?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper’s RL (GRPO) makes the model concentrate probability mass on a few high‑reward outputs (mode amplification) rather than increasing the total probability mass of all correct outputs. $\mathrm{Maj@}K$ (majority voting) benefits from that concentration, while $\mathrm{Pass@}K$ (whether any sample is correct among $K$) depends only on the total mass on correct outputs, so it need not improve. The paper’s experiments (GRPO with group size $G=64$, naive nucleus sampling, max length $1024$) show exactly this phenomenon.

Precise definitions (as used in the paper)
For each problem $q$ the model is sampled $K$ times under the decoding used in the paper (they report using “naive nucleus sampling”; during RL they drew $G=64$ samples per question). Let the $K$ i.i.d. samples be $o_1,\dots,o_K$ and let $\text{answer}(o_i)$ be the canonical final answer extracted from sample $o_i$. Define $\text{correct}(o_i)=1$ if $\text{answer}(o_i)$ equals the gold answer for $q$, else $0$.

- $\mathrm{Pass@}K$ is the fraction of problems for which at least one sampled output is correct:
$$
\mathrm{Pass@}K \;=\; \frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{q\in D}\mathbf{1}\{\exists i\in\{1,\dots,K\}:\; \text{correct}(o_i)=1\}.
$$
- $\mathrm{Maj@}K$ (majority voting / self‑consistency) is the fraction of problems for which the most frequent final answer among the $K$ samples equals the gold answer (ties resolved by whatever tie‑break rule the evaluation uses):
$$
\mathrm{Maj@}K \;=\; \frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{q\in D}\mathbf{1}\{\operatorname{mode}\{\,\text{answer}(o_i)\}_{i=1}^K = \text{gold}(q)\}.
$$

A short probabilistic view that explains the divergence
Let the model’s single‑sample distribution over final answer strings for question $q$ be $\{p_a\}$ (probability assigned to answer string $a$). Let $C$ be the set of correct answer strings and define the total correct mass
$$
p_{\mathrm{corr}}=\sum_{a\in C} p_a,
$$
and the mass on the single most probable correct string
$$
p_{\mathrm{mode}}=\max_{a\in C} p_a.
$$
Under i.i.d. sampling,
$$
\mathrm{Pass@}K(q)=1-(1-p_{\mathrm{corr}})^K,
$$
so $\mathrm{Pass@}K$ depends only on the total correct mass $p_{\mathrm{corr}}$. By contrast $\mathrm{Maj@}K$ is driven by how concentrated that mass is: if a single correct string has probability $p_{\mathrm{mode}}$, the chance that it appears a majority of the $K$ samples is roughly
$$
\sum_{j>\lfloor K/2\rfloor} \binom{K}{j} p_{\mathrm{mode}}^{\,j}(1-p_{\mathrm{mode}})^{K-j},
$$
and if the correct mass is split among many rare correct variants the majority event becomes vanishingly unlikely even if $p_{\mathrm{corr}}$ is non‑trivial.

Toy numeric illustration ($K$ small so intuition is clear): fix $p_{\mathrm{corr}}=0.3$ and compare two cases. If that $0.3$ is concentrated (one correct string with $p_{\mathrm{mode}}=0.3$), then for $K=5$ $\mathrm{Maj@}5 \approx 0.115$ (you can get a majority with moderate chance). If the same $0.3$ is split across three correct variants ($p_{\mathrm{mode}}=0.1$) $\mathrm{Maj@}5$ drops to $\approx 0.008$, yet $\mathrm{Pass@}5$ is identical in the two cases ($\mathrm{Pass@}5 = 1-0.7^5\approx0.832$). So $\mathrm{Maj@}K$ changes strongly with concentration, $\mathrm{Pass@}K$ does not.

Why GRPO / RL produces this effect (mechanism in the paper)
GRPO samples groups of outputs and computes advantages by normalizing rewards inside each group (outcome supervision sets token advantages equal to the group‑normalized final reward; process supervision sums normalized step rewards). That group‑relative normalization makes updates amplify the high‑scoring outputs seen in the sampled group and suppress lower‑scoring ones. Concretely, the policy gradient in GRPO re‑weights token probabilities based on relative reward in the sampled group (Equation (4) in the paper), and a KL penalty to the reference policy is applied to avoid runaway drift. The practical effect is reallocation of probability mass among outputs the model already produces: high‑reward, already‑sampled outputs get pushed up (increasing $p_{\mathrm{mode}}$), while many rare outputs lose mass. Because the reward is applied comparatively within sampled groups and the KL keeps the policy near the SFT reference, GRPO is efficient at amplifying good modes but not at discovering many new, diverse correct modes. That behaviour matches the empirical observation: $\mathrm{Maj@}K$ improves (mode is stronger) while $\mathrm{Pass@}K$ does not (total correct mass $p_{\mathrm{corr}}$ did not substantially increase).

What the paper reports about settings
GRPO used group size $G=64$ during RL (the same count used for the paper’s self‑consistency experiment: “self‑consistency over 64 samples”), naive nucleus sampling for exploration (the paper does not report explicit top‑p or temperature values in the excerpt), maximum generation length $1024$, KL coefficient $0.04$, and one policy update per exploration stage. The $\mathrm{Maj@}K$ / $\mathrm{Pass@}K$ curves shown in the paper were produced by sampling multiple outputs per question (up to the sample counts shown; $G=64$ is the practical sampling count they used in RL/self‑consistency).

How you can test / measure this in your experiments
To confirm whether RL amplified modes and reduced diversity, compute per‑question statistics before and after RL: the histogram of distinct final answers among $K$ samples, $p_{\mathrm{corr}}$ (fraction of samples that are correct), $p_{\mathrm{mode}}$ (fraction for the most frequent correct string), and the entropy of the empirical answer distribution. If RL increases $p_{\mathrm{mode}}$ and lowers entropy while leaving $p_{\mathrm{corr}}$ roughly unchanged, you should see $\mathrm{Maj@}K$ rise while $\mathrm{Pass@}K$ stays flat.

Practical implication and how to increase Pass@K
If your goal is higher $\mathrm{Pass@}K$ (covering more correct variants / increasing total correct mass), you need training or decoding changes that increase $p_{\mathrm{corr}}$ rather than merely concentrating existing mass. Possible directions (some discussed in the paper) include more exploratory decoding during RL data collection (higher temperature, wider top‑p, tree search), reward functions that explicitly reward novelty/coverage or recall of any correct variant, iterative RL with a reward model trained on broader/out‑of‑distribution samples, or stronger process supervision that guides intermediate steps to increase true capability rather than just re‑ranking existing outputs.

Summary
$\mathrm{Maj@}K$ is sensitive to how concentrated the model’s correct answers are; $\mathrm{Pass@}K$ is sensitive only to the total mass on any correct answers. GRPO’s group‑relative updates and KL regularization tend to concentrate probability on high‑reward modes the model already samples, so $\mathrm{Maj@}K$ improves (majority voting becomes more reliable) while $\mathrm{Pass@}K$ does not necessarily increase — this is exactly the effect reported in the paper.
