\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### When you keep the top 40B tokens in iteration 1, how is a token-level cutoff derived from page-level scores?
Question:
Pages are ranked by fastText scores, but selection is described in terms of tokens (e.g., top 40B tokens). Do you keep full pages until a token budget is reached, truncate long pages, or weight by score? Are there per-domain or per-host caps to prevent a few sites from dominating the early iterations?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper ranks whole pages by the fastText score and then keeps the highest‑ranked pages until the cumulative token budget is reached (so the “top $40B$ tokens” is a cumulative token cutoff over ranked pages). The authors do not describe any per‑page weighting scheme or a special “truncate pages at selection time” rule, nor do they report an explicit per‑domain / per‑host cap; instead they rely on URL‑based deduplication / near‑deduplication and a domain‑level augmentation step to reduce redundancy and increase diversity.

Why I read it that way: the Methods text says they “rank the collected pages according to their scores predicted by the fastText model, and only preserve the top‑ranking ones,” and that the preserved volume is measured by the top $40B$, $80B$, $120B$, $160B$ tokens (they chose top $40B$ tokens in iteration 1). That phrasing — rank pages, then preserve the top pages until you hit a token threshold — is the standard interpretation: sort pages by score and accumulate their token counts until the budget is reached. The paper later reports the final corpus size as $35.5M$ pages totalling $120B$ tokens, which implies selection operates at page granularity (average tokens per preserved page $\approx \frac{120\cdot 10^9}{35.5\cdot 10^6} \approx 3380$ tokens/page), consistent with keeping page units rather than chopping every page to a tiny fixed length.

What they do to avoid domination by a small set of sites: the pipeline explicitly applies URL‑based deduplication and near‑deduplication before scoring, which removes identical and near‑identical copies (a very important first step to prevent a few hosts or scrapers from dominating raw counts). They also organize Common Crawl into domains, compute the fraction of pages collected per domain, label domains with $>10\%$ collected pages as math‑related, and manually annotate math‑related URL paths to expand the seed set for subsequent iterations. That domain analysis and manual augmentation is meant to broaden the positive training examples rather than impose numeric per‑host caps. The paper does not state that they imposed an explicit per‑domain cap during selection.

On truncation / training slicing: the paper does specify a model context length during pretraining (4K), so long pages will naturally be chunked at training time into 4K windows. That is a separate concern from selection: selection appears to preserve page‑level units and the tokenizer / data loader will split very long pages into training examples.

If you need exact implementation details you won’t find in the text (for example whether they allow the last added page to overshoot the token budget or whether they ever truncate the very last page to hit the budget exactly), those are not specified in the paper — you would need the dataset release or author code/comments to pin those micro‑decisions down. If you implement a similar pipeline, common best practices are to (a) deduplicate aggressively, (b) accumulate full documents by score until the token budget is reached (optionally allowing a small overshoot), and (c) add per‑domain subsampling or caps if you want a hard diversity constraint — the paper relied on dedup + domain annotation rather than an explicit numeric cap.
