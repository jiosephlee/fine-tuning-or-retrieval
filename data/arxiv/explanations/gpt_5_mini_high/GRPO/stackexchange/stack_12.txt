\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why is there no discount factor in process-supervised advantages, and did you try discounted returns?
Question:
The process advantage is defined as \(\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{\mathrm{index}(j) \ge t} \tilde r_i^{\mathrm{index}(j)}\), without a discount \(\gamma\). Did you experiment with discounted returns to reduce variance or to de-emphasize distant steps, especially for very long chains-of-thought? If so, what was the effect?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not report using a discount factor for the process-supervised advantages — GRPO uses the simple undiscounted sum over future normalized per-step rewards
$$
\hat A_{i,t}=\sum_{\mathrm{index}(j)\ge t}\widetilde r_i^{\mathrm{index}(j)}
$$
by design. The authors chose this for simplicity and because the group-normalized, stepwise rewards already act as a low-variance, comparative baseline; they did not report experiments with discounted returns. Below I explain why that design is reasonable, what trade-offs discounting would introduce, and how you could test discounting if you wanted to.

Why no $\gamma$ (intuitively and technically)
The process reward model in the paper scores explicit reasoning steps, and GRPO is built around a group-relative normalization: all step rewards from the $G$ sampled outputs for the same question are standardized (subtract group mean, divide by group std) so the policy sees comparative, low-variance signals. Summing those normalized step scores produces an empirical Monte‑Carlo return for every token without needing a learned value function. Using an undiscounted sum is a simple, direct way to credit all future step-level signals equally; it keeps the pipeline free of an extra hyperparameter and stays aligned with how the reward model was trained (comparative scores across steps/outputs).

By contrast, classic PPO/GAE uses a discount factor because it learns a value function and trades bias vs variance in advantage estimation. GRPO deliberately avoids training a value network to save memory, so it also avoids the whole machinery of choosing $\gamma$/$\lambda$ and fitting a baseline that is correct at every token. Normalizing across the group already reduces variance, so the authors prioritized a simple and stable MC-style advantage.

Trade-offs if you add discounting
Discounting future step rewards (for example, replacing the sum by a geometrically weighted sum)
$$
\hat A_{i,t}^{(\gamma)}=\sum_{\mathrm{index}(j)\ge t}\gamma^{\mathrm{index}(j)-t}\,\widetilde r_i^{\mathrm{index}(j)}
$$
does two main things. First, it reduces the contribution of distant steps and therefore can reduce variance coming from long noisy tails of reasoning; second, it biases credit assignment toward more immediate step signals (i.e., local corrections). That can help stability for extremely long chains-of-thought if later step rewards are noisy, but it can also harm learning when early steps are crucial for a correct final answer (you may weaken the incentive to produce long, correct derivations). In other words, discounting trades variance reduction for biased credit assignment — the standard RL trade-off.

Practical considerations about normalization and where to put $\gamma$
Because GRPO normalizes step rewards across the whole group, you must decide whether to apply discounting before or after normalization, and that choice matters. Normalizing raw per-step rewards and then discounting mixes units across positions; discounting first and then normalizing discounted returns across the group is usually the cleaner option if you want the group baseline to be comparable across sampled outputs:
1) compute discounted returns at each token from raw step scores,
2) compute group mean/std over those discounted returns,
3) standardize and use those as advantages.

What I would try (experimental recipe)
The paper doesn’t report such experiments, so if you want to explore discounting empirically, start conservatively because mathematical CoTs are often long and later steps matter. Try $\gamma$s close to 1 (e.g., 0.999, 0.995, 0.99, 0.95) rather than tiny values. Measure not only final benchmark accuracy (GSM8K, MATH) but also Maj@K/Pass@K, chain-length distribution, variance of advantages, gradient norms, and whether solutions become shorter/shallower. You can also try an eligibility‑trace / GAE-style factor (a $\lambda$) implemented on top of discounted step rewards to smoothly interpolate bias/variance. Compare two normalizations (discount then normalize vs normalize then discount) — I recommend discount then normalize across the sampled group.

Bottom line
The paper intentionally used undiscounted group-normalized returns because that matches the comparative, stepwise reward setup and avoids extra hyperparameters and a learned value function. Discounting is a reasonable idea and likely to reduce variance at the cost of biasing credit assignment; the authors did not report such experiments, so empirical testing ($\gamma$ very close to 1, normalize after discounting, monitor chain-length and Maj/Pass metrics) is the right way to see whether it helps for long chains-of-thought in your setting.
