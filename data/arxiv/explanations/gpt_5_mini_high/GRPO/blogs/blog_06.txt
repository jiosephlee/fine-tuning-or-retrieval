\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# A Field Guide to Alignment Methods—SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, GRPO—Through One Unifying Lens

Modern alignment work for large language models looks like a crowded toolbox: supervised fine-tuning, rejection-sampling fine-tuning, pairwise preference losses, actor-critic reinforcement learning, and new hybrids. The DeepSeekMath paper offers a clean way to compare all of these methods by asking one question: how does each method turn data and reward signals into per-token gradient pressure on the policy?

This post unpacks that "unifying lens", maps common methods onto it, and highlights a key experimental insight from the paper: reinforcement learning (as applied here) tends to increase Maj@K (stability of correct answers across samples) rather than Pass@K (the single best-of-K chance). I finish with practical takeaways and open directions the paper points to.

## The unified lens: gradients as three knobs

The paper frames every method by how it produces the gradient of the objective. Concretely, the gradient can be written as

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{(q,o)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o|}
GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q,o,t,\pi_{rf})\;\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(o_t\mid q, o_{<t})\right].
$$

Read this as: training changes the model by taking log‑prob gradients for tokens, scaled by a per-token coefficient $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$ that depends on three independent design choices:

- Data source $\mathcal{D}$: where the (question, output) pairs come from (offline dataset vs outputs sampled from an evolving policy).
- Reward function / reference $\pi_{rf}$: what supplies the supervision signal (a rule, a learned reward model, or nothing).
- Algorithm $\mathcal{A}$: how the method converts the reward signal into the gradient coefficient $GC$ (simple filtering, pairwise preference loss, advantage estimation, etc.).

This decomposition is simple but powerful: differences between methods reduce to “which data do you sample?” × “what score do you use?” × “how do you convert scores to per-token forces?”

## Three knobs explained

- Data source: Offline methods use outputs or labels collected before the RL/fine-tuning loop (for example, SFT examples or logs sampled from a frozen SFT model). Online methods repeatedly sample outputs from the current policy during training so the training distribution tracks the policy.

- Reward function: This can be (a) a rule-based binary signal (e.g., correctness), (b) a learned scalar reward model trained on comparisons, or (c) nothing (pure supervised targets). Learned reward models are naturally comparative: they score outputs relative to one another.

- Algorithm: This is how scores become per-token coefficients $GC$. Examples include: filter-and-finetune (binary), pairwise-contrastive losses (DPO), or policy-gradient style objectives that use advantages (PPO, GRPO). The algorithm determines whether incorrect outputs are merely ignored or actively penalized, whether grading is coarse or fine-grained, and whether per-step structure is used.

## Where common methods sit

Below is a compact mapping of the common methods to the three knobs.

- Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)
  - Data: Offline, human-written (gold) input→target pairs.
  - Reward: None (supervised ground truth).
  - Algorithm: Standard maximum likelihood; $GC$ is effectively the supervised signal (teacher tokens get positive weight).

- Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT)
  - Data: Offline outputs sampled from the SFT model (filtered).
  - Reward: Rule-based (e.g., answer-correctness). Filter keeps only “successful” outputs.
  - Algorithm: Fine-tune on the filtered subset. $GC$ acts like a binary gate: correct outputs get reinforced; incorrect ones are ignored (no negative push).

- Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
  - Data: Offline outputs (pairs), often from SFT or human annotations.
  - Reward: Pairwise preferences (human or synthetic).
  - Algorithm: Pairwise loss that directly updates policy to prefer the better output. DPO changes $GC$ in a pairwise contrastive way but stays offline.

- Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
  - Data: Online: sample outputs from the current policy.
  - Reward: Learned reward model (usually trained on pairwise labels); per-token KL penalty is embedded into reward.
  - Algorithm: Actor-critic with a learned value function $V_\psi$ and Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) to produce token-level advantages $A_t$; $GC$ is the clipped importance ratio times $A_t$. PPO typically needs a separate value network (memory + compute heavy).

- Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) — the paper’s proposal
  - Data: Online: sample a group $\{o_1,\dots,o_G\}$ of outputs per question from the current policy.
  - Reward: Learned reward model scores the group; GRPO normalizes scores within the group (comparative by design).
  - Algorithm: No critic. The baseline is the group average reward (and standard deviation is used to normalize). Advantages are computed relative to the group, then fed into a PPO-like clipped objective. GRPO replaces a learned value function with a group-relative baseline and adds a direct KL penalty to the loss. This reduces memory footprint (no critic) and aligns with how preference models are trained.

## GRPO: the mechanics you should remember

GRPO's central trick is to compute advantages inside a sampled group. For a group of $G$ outputs for the same question, the outcome‑supervision advantage is simply the normalized group score:

$$
\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i
\quad\text{where}\quad
\widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf r)}.
$$

For process supervision (reward at intermediate reasoning steps), GRPO accumulates normalized step rewards after token index $t$:

$$
\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{j:\,\mathrm{index}(j)\ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{\mathrm{index}(j)}.
$$

Because the baseline is the group mean, GRPO needs no learned value model; the comparison is internal to each sampled group. The paper also regularizes the policy by adding a KL term to the loss (rather than subtracting per-token KL inside reward), using the unbiased estimator

$$
\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_\theta\Vert\pi_{ref}]
= \frac{\pi_{ref}}{\pi_\theta} - \log\frac{\pi_{ref}}{\pi_\theta} - 1,
$$

evaluated per token. In practice the authors used $G=64$ outputs per question in RL training, which both matches how reward models are trained (comparative) and gives stable group statistics.

The upshot: GRPO gives many of PPO’s stability benefits while cutting memory and better matching comparative reward models.

## Outcome vs. process supervision: coarse vs. fine signal

- Outcome supervision supplies a single scalar at the end of a generated solution (correct/incorrect or a learned scalar). GRPO sets the same normalized reward as the advantage for every token in that output.

- Process supervision scores intermediate reasoning steps (e.g., chain-of-thought steps). These finer signals let you shape the policy at different stages of the generated reasoning. In GRPO, process supervision produces a per-step reward sequence that is summed into per-token advantages; the paper finds this finer-grained supervision improves learning on complex reasoning tasks.

## Online sampling matters (and why)

A recurring empirical point in the paper: online sampling beats offline sampling. Methods that train on outputs generated by the evolving policy (online) can exploit new behaviors that the model is forming; offline methods that stick to a frozen dataset miss those dynamics.

The authors show Online RFT (an online variant of RFT) outperforms the offline RFT in later stages of training. GRPO (online, with graded advantages) outperforms Online RFT (online, but with binary reinforcement), demonstrating that both the data source (online) and the algorithmic shaping of $GC$ (continuous, signed advantages versus binary reinforcement) matter.

## A surprising diagnostic: Maj@K vs Pass@K

One concise finding explains *how* RL is helping the models. The paper measures two metrics across multiple samples:

- Pass@K (best-of-K style metric): does any sample among K solve the problem?
- Maj@K (distributional/stability metric): are the correct outputs well-represented across the K samples — i.e., does the correct answer occupy substantial mass in the sample set?

They observe that RL (GRPO) substantially improves Maj@K but not Pass@K. Put another way, RL makes the model put more probability mass on the correct answers that were already present in the top-K region, rather than discovering novel correct answers that only show up when you sample many tries. The practical interpretation is that RL here stabilizes and concentrates the model’s distribution toward already-good modes (making correct answers more probable and repeatable) rather than fundamentally expanding the model’s base problem-solving capability.

This explains why RL-based alignment often helps real-world reliability (so a single sample is more trustworthy) without necessarily changing the “best-of-many” ceiling.

## Practical takeaways and where alignment should go next

The unified lens helps you reason about trade-offs; here are concrete implications and forward directions the paper emphasizes.

- Make reward models generalize better. If the reward model only knows how to compare outputs in a narrow distribution, RL will overfit the distribution rather than improve capability. Reward models must generalize across out-of-distribution prompts and advanced decoding outputs.

- Reflect reward-model uncertainty. Reward models are themselves fallible. Training algorithms should be robust to noisy or biased reward signals (the paper calls for moving from “weak” to “strong” alignment regimes). This suggests principled ways to incorporate uncertainty into the learning signal or to gradually sharpen trust in the reward model.

- Use smarter sampling/decoding for exploration. The data source matters: sampling regimes that expose diverse, high-quality modes (tree search, structured exploration, or sampling guided by uncertainty) may give the RL signal more useful gradients than naive nucleus sampling.

- Invest in process (stepwise) reward models. Fine-grained, per-step supervision helps shape chain-of-thought and multi-step reasoning. Building scalable, high-quality stepwise reward datasets and models is a promising direction.

- Iterative RL with replay helps. The paper’s iterative RL loop—retraining the reward model on fresh policy outputs and keeping a replay buffer of historical data—improves stability and performance. This is an effective practical pattern to follow.

## Quick summary

The paper’s unified perspective reduces many alignment methods to three choices: which data you sample, which reward you trust, and how you turn that reward into per-token gradient coefficients. That viewpoint makes it easy to see why methods behave differently: offline vs online sampling changes what the model learns about, rule vs learned reward changes whether you get graded or binary feedback, and algorithmic choices (critic vs group baseline, per-step vs end-of-output signal) determine whether you penalize mistakes or only amplify successes.

GRPO is an elegant member of the family: it pairs online sampling with group-relative advantages to avoid a separate value network, aligns the update mechanism to how comparative reward models are trained, and reduces memory cost. Empirically, RL under GRPO made the model’s correct answers more stable across samples (higher Maj@K) rather than increasing the best-of-many peak (Pass@K), which is an important clue about *what* RL buys you in practice.

If you work on alignment, think about the three knobs—data, reward, algorithm—when you design experiments. Improving reward generalization, accounting for reward uncertainty, and inventing better exploration/sampling schemes are the most promising directions to push alignment further.
