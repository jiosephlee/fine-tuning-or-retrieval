\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Mining Math from the Open Web: Inside the 120B-Token DeepSeekMath Corpus

Large language models need lots of high-quality, domain-relevant data to get good at math. DeepSeekMath shows that the public web—Common Crawl—contains exactly that, if you can find it. This post walks through how the DeepSeek team turned Common Crawl into a 120B-token math corpus: the iterative fastText-based classifier, URL/domain-level mining, human-in-the-loop refinement, deduplication, and benchmark decontamination. I’ll also explain why this corpus outperforms prior public math collections (MathPile, OpenWebMath, Proof-Pile-2) in pretraining experiments, and offer practical takeaways if you want to build a domain-specific corpus at scale.

## The problem and the idea in one sentence
Common Crawl is huge and noisy. The challenge is to extract a large, high-quality, multilingual subset of pages that are actually useful for training math reasoning. The DeepSeekMath pipeline does this by: (1) starting from a compact, trusted seed (OpenWebMath); (2) training a fast, scalable classifier; (3) iteratively mining and expanding via domain-level heuristics and human checks; (4) deduplicating and decontaminating benchmarks; (5) validating corpus quality via downstream pretraining experiments.

## Pipeline overview (high level)
The pipeline is iterative and pragmatic:

- Start with a high-quality seed: OpenWebMath pages.
- Train a fastText classifier to score Common Crawl pages for “math-likeness.”
- Reduce Common Crawl size with URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication, then run the classifier to recall candidate pages and rank them by score.
- Keep the top-ranked pages (selection volume tuned by pretraining experiments).
- Find missed math sources via domain-level analysis, manually annotate math-specific URL patterns inside those domains, add them to the seed, retrain the classifier, and repeat.
- Apply human annotation and additional refinement to improve model recall/precision.
- Decontaminate with strict benchmarks-filtering (10-gram exact matching, with a fallback for shorter benchmark texts).
- Iterate until marginal gains vanish. The final product: 35.5M math web pages totaling 120B tokens.

Now let’s unpack the main components.

## fastText classifier: simple, fast, scalable
Why fastText? At web scale you need a classifier that is extremely fast to train and infer over billions of pages. The team trained a fastText model to recognize pages “like” OpenWebMath:

- Positive examples: 500k randomly sampled entries from the OpenWebMath seed.
- Negative examples: 500k random Common Crawl pages.
- Key fastText hyperparameters reported: vector dimension = 256, learning rate = 0.1, max word n-gram length = 3, minimum word occurrences = 3, epochs = 3.

The classifier is used to score and rank deduplicated Common Crawl pages; top-ranked pages are kept for further processing. The team tested preserving different top slices (top 40B, 80B, 120B, 160B tokens) and used pretraining experiments to guide the choice—an important empirical step rather than relying solely on heuristics.

## Deduplication and the initial candidate set
Before classification the crawl was aggressively reduced via URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication; after this step the dataset the classifier worked over was ~40B HTML pages. Ranking those pages by the classifier score lets you prioritize the highest-likelihood math pages without wasting compute on the long tail of noisy content.

## Domain-level mining: find the missed pockets
A single classifier trained on a single seed will miss types of math content not well represented in the seed. To address that, the team:

- Split the entire crawl into disjoint domains (same base URL).
- For each domain, computed the fraction of pages that the classifier collected in the first iteration.
- Marked domains as “math-related” when more than 10% of their pages were collected (example domain in the paper: mathoverflow.net).
- Manually inspected those math-related domains and annotated URL patterns that correspond to math content (e.g., paths like /questions).
- Added pages linked to those annotated URL patterns that the classifier had not yet collected back into the seed corpus.

This domain-level step is a practical way to recover entire subtrees of sites that are highly math-focused but heterogeneous in content, which a single pass of a classifier may miss.

## Human-in-the-loop refinement and iteration
After expanding the seed via domain mining, the pipeline retrains the fastText classifier on the enlarged seed, re-scores Common Crawl, and repeats. The team also injected human annotation to refine the collected candidates. After four iterations the process yielded 35.5M pages (120B tokens). By the fourth iteration nearly 98% of the data collected in that pass had already been collected in the third iteration, so they stopped.

The iterative human+classifier loop is important: it increases diversity of positive examples and steadily improves recall without letting quality drift downward.

## Benchmark decontamination: 10-gram exact matching
To avoid leaking evaluation data into the training set, the team applied a strict decontamination rule:

- Remove any text segment that contains a 10-gram substring that exactly matches any substring from evaluation benchmarks (English: GSM8K, MATH; Chinese: CMATH, AGIEval).
- For benchmark items shorter than 10 tokens but at least 3 tokens long, use exact matching on the full string.

This is conservative by design. It’s computationally straightforward and reduces the risk of subtle contamination that can artificially inflate benchmark numbers.

## What they kept and how they decided
Important empirical choices were guided by pretraining probes. The team experimented with preserving the top 40B/80B/120B/160B tokens after ranking. In the first iteration they kept the top 40B tokens, and after four full iterations the final corpus totaled 120B math tokens. The selection thresholds were validated with pretraining runs rather than hand-waving.

## Why this corpus beats prior public math corpora (and how that was validated)
DeepSeekMath is much larger than prior public math-focused corpora: OpenWebMath is ~13.6B tokens, MathPile is ~8.9B tokens, and Proof-Pile-2 aggregates OpenWebMath, AlgebraicStack and arXiv (with an arXiv:Web:Code ratio used in Llemma). DeepSeekMath at 120B tokens is therefore several times larger—paper notes "almost 7× the math web pages used by Minerva and 9× OpenWebMath."

But bigger is only useful if it’s high-quality and diverse. The paper reports three factors that make their corpus more effective in pretraining experiments:

1. Higher average quality per token (as measured by downstream pretraining probes). When the team trained a 1.3B model for 150B tokens on each candidate corpus, the model trained on DeepSeekMath consistently outperformed models trained on MathPile, OpenWebMath, and Proof-Pile-2 across eight math benchmarks using few-shot chain-of-thought prompting.

2. Multilingual coverage. DeepSeekMath includes both English and non-English pages (predominantly English and Chinese), which translated into stronger performance on Chinese math benchmarks compared to primarily English-only corpora.

3. Scale reduces repetition. Smaller corpora get cycled through many times during long pretraining runs; the paper observed that for the baselines, repeated epochs led to early plateaus in learning. DeepSeekMath’s scale produces a steeper and more sustained learning curve in the pretraining experiments (the paper notes that DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B trained on DeepSeekMath outperforms Proof-Pile-2 at 50B tokens, which is one epoch of Proof-Pile-2).

Concrete downstream results tied to this corpus were reported for the larger model trained on a mix where 56% of tokens were from DeepSeekMath: DeepSeekMath-Base 7B achieved 64.2% on GSM8K and 36.2% on the competition-level MATH benchmark (without tool use), outperforming Minerva 540B in those metrics as reported in the paper. Those model numbers underscore that dataset quality + selection strategy can narrow the gap to much larger models.

## Practical takeaways for building domain-specific corpora
If you want to build a large, high-quality domain corpus from a noisy web crawl, the DeepSeekMath experience suggests a practical recipe:

- Start from a trusted seed. Even a relatively small, high-quality dataset (OpenWebMath) is enough to bootstrap a scalable recall model.
- Use a fast, scalable classifier for initial recall. fastText is a pragmatic choice for billions of pages because it’s cheap to train and run.
- Deduplicate aggressively up front. URL-level deduplication plus near-dedup cuts the crawl to a manageable candidate set.
- Rank and select rather than threshold blindly. Preserve the top-ranked pages by score and validate the preserved volume by pretraining probes (e.g., try multiple preserved volumes).
- Use domain-level heuristics to recover missed sources. Compute how many pages from each domain your classifier selects; for domains with concentrated selection, manually inspect and add math-specific URL patterns back to the seed.
- Keep humans in the loop. Manual URL annotation and spot-checking of candidates materially improve recall and precision.
- Decontaminate with strict, conservative rules. Exact n-gram matching (10-gram here) is simple and effective.
- Validate corpus quality via downstream pretraining experiments, not just heuristics. The right metric is whether a model trained on the corpus learns more and generalizes better.
- Prefer multilingual collection if the target tasks are multilingual; it can improve performance across languages.
- Iterate until diminishing returns. That’s the point of the seed → classifier → expand → retrain loop.

## Caveats and interesting negative results
A few cautionary findings from the paper are worth repeating:

- ArXiv alone did not improve mathematical reasoning on the adopted benchmarks in the team’s experiments. Models trained on arXiv-heavy corpora sometimes showed no improvement or even deterioration on the benchmarks the paper uses (GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-STEM, miniF2F). The authors note limitations: this does not mean arXiv is useless for every math task or at every scale—only that it was not a clear win in their experiments and on these particular downstream tasks.
- Bigger corpus is not the only answer: data selection quality matters. The iterative selection + human refinement appears to be a large part of the gain.
- The pipeline focuses on textual web pages. If your domain requires highly structured sources (e.g., LaTeX-heavy arXiv content, formal proofs), you may need specialized preprocessing.

## Short conclusion
DeepSeekMath demonstrates that public web data contains rich, high-quality mathematical content at scale—and that a pragmatic, iterative pipeline (fast classifier, aggressive deduplication, domain-level mining, human refinement, and conservative decontamination) can turn Common Crawl into a 120B-token math corpus that materially improves pretraining outcomes. The main lesson: combine speed (fastText), empirical validation (pretraining probes), and human judgment (URL annotation and checks) to build domain-specific corpora that actually teach models the behaviors you care about.

If you’re building a domain corpus, you don’t need to reinvent every component—follow the seed → classify → expand → validate loop, and let downstream training curves be your ultimate quality control.
