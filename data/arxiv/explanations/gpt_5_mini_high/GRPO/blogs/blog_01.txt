\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# DeepSeekMath, Explained: How a 7B Open Model Learns to Do Competition-Level Math

Mathematical reasoning is one of the toughest benchmarks for language models: it needs multi-step, structured thinking, symbolic manipulation, and often the ability to call external tools. DeepSeekMath shows that with focused data and a targeted fine-tuning recipe, an open 7B model can reach competition-level performance on the MATH dataset and come surprisingly close to proprietary leaders.

This post walks through what DeepSeekMath is, why it works, and what the results mean for open AI.

## The headline numbers

- DeepSeekMath-RL (7B) — trained without external tool execution or ensemble voting — reaches 51.7% accuracy on the competition-level MATH benchmark. According to the authors, this is the first time an open-source model passes 50% on MATH.
- Using self-consistency with 64 samples pushes MATH to 60.9% (i.e., sample multiple solutions and take the majority/consensus).
- For GSM8K (grade-school math), DeepSeekMath-RL attains 88.2% (up from 82.9% before RL).
- The pre-trained base model (DeepSeekMath-Base 7B) already shows strong gains: 64.2% on GSM8K and 36.2% on MATH — competitive with much larger closed models such as Minerva 540B on some metrics.

Those numbers matter: they show a 7B open model, when trained with the right data and optimization, can approach the math performance of much larger closed models like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra on benchmarks.

## Why math is hard for LLMs (short version)

Math problems typically require:
- Multi-step chain-of-thought reasoning rather than a single-token prediction.
- Symbolic manipulation and precise arithmetic, which is brittle for models not trained on explicit solution traces or code.
- Robustness: a model must reliably produce the correct chain, not just occasionally hit it by chance.

Two practical levers help: (1) exposing the model to massive amounts of math-like text (including worked solutions and code), and (2) using targeted fine-tuning / RL to concentrate probability mass on correct solution traces. DeepSeekMath applies both.

## The training recipe (in plain terms)

DeepSeekMath is built in stages:

1. Initialize from a code-pretrained model: DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 (7B). The authors find a code-initialized start point helps mathematical reasoning, especially for program-aided solutions.
2. Continued pre-training (500B tokens total): the bulk of the new data is a math-focused corpus plus code and natural language. The downstream model is called DeepSeekMath-Base (7B).
   - Data mix for the 7B base: about 56% from the DeepSeekMath Corpus (math), 20% GitHub code, 10% arXiv, 4% AlgebraicStack, and 10% general Common Crawl (English + Chinese).
3. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT): ~776K instruction-tuning examples in chain-of-thought (CoT), program-of-thought (PoT), and tool-integrated formats in English and Chinese. This produces DeepSeekMath-Instruct (7B).
4. Reinforcement learning with GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization): applied to a subset of instruction data (only CoT-format GSM8K and MATH questions, ~144K items) to produce DeepSeekMath-RL (7B).

Key RL detail: during GRPO training the model samples many candidate solutions per question ($G=64$ in their runs), scores them with a reward model, and updates the policy using within-group comparisons (more on GRPO below).

Total math-focused tokens in pretraining: 120B tokens from the web (the "DeepSeekMath Corpus").

## The DeepSeekMath Corpus: how the math data was built

The authors mined Common Crawl for high-quality math pages using an iterative fastText-based classifier seeded with OpenWebMath. The pipeline highlights:

- Start with a curated seed (OpenWebMath) as positive examples and random Common Crawl pages as negatives.
- Train a fastText classifier, recall top-scoring pages from a deduplicated Common Crawl (tens of billions of HTML pages), and keep the best-ranked pages.
- Iterate: identify domains with a high hit-rate (domains where >10% of pages were collected), manually annotate math-related URLs within those domains, add these as extra positives, retrain the classifier, and repeat.
- Decontaminate benchmark overlaps by removing any text segments that exactly match a 10-gram from evaluation benchmarks.
- Final result after four iterations: ~35.5M math web pages, ~120B math tokens.

Why this matters: the DeepSeekMath Corpus is several times larger than existing public math corpora (e.g., OpenWebMath ~13.6B tokens, MathPile ~8.9B). It is also multilingual (substantial Chinese content), which helps performance on Chinese math benchmarks.

## Why starting from code helps

The authors ran ablations showing code pretraining benefits math tasks, especially program-aided reasoning. Code pretraining improves the model’s ability to generate correct Python solutions for math problems (useful for PoT-style prompts) and also speeds or enhances subsequent math pretraining. One practical outcome: initializing from a code model (DeepSeek-Coder) produced better math performance than starting from a general LLM checkpoint.

## What is GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization)?

GRPO is a practical variant of PPO designed for LLM fine-tuning with preference/reward signals, optimized for memory and alignment with comparative reward data.

Simplified intuition:
- Standard PPO uses an actor (policy) and a critic (value model) to compute token-level advantages; the critic is expensive to train and big in memory.
- GRPO drops the critic. Instead, for each question $q$ the policy samples a group of outputs $\{o_1,\dots,o_G\}$ from the old policy, the reward model scores each output producing rewards $\{r_1,\dots,r_G\}$, and these are converted into relative advantages inside the group.
- For outcome supervision (one reward per completed solution), the normalized group reward is:
  $$\widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})}.$$
  GRPO then sets the advantage for every token in output $o_i$ to $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i$.
- For process supervision (reward per reasoning step), the token-level advantage sums the future step rewards:
  $$\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{\text{step}\ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{\text{step}}.$$

Why this is useful:
- No separate value model reduces memory and training complexity.
- The group-relative baseline naturally matches reward models trained on comparisons (pairwise or ranked outputs).
- Normalizing within a group stabilizes advantage estimates and leverages relative signal among sampled outputs.

Practical setup used by the authors: $G=64$ samples per question, iterative RL with replayed reward-model training, KL regularization against a reference policy, and a modest policy learning rate. They show GRPO improves both in-domain (GSM8K, MATH) and out-of-domain math tasks (e.g., CMATH).

## How RL changed the model — what really improved

The authors investigate how RL helps and present two important observations:

1. Online sampling beats offline sampling. Methods that sample in real time from the current policy (online) outperform those using offline samples collected from the initial SFT model.
2. GRPO outperforms online rejection-sampling-style fine-tuning because it uses graded reward signals (not only binary correctness) and process-aware supervision when available.

A key diagnostic: RL improved Maj@K (majority correctness across top-$K$ samples) much more than Pass@K (probability of a single generated sample being correct). In plain terms: RL concentrates correct reasoning traces among the top-ranked outputs, making ensemble or sampling-based methods (like self-consistency) much more effective. That explains why self-consistency with 64 samples pushes MATH from 51.7% to 60.9%.

The authors also provide a unified view showing SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO differ mostly in three ingredients: data source (online vs offline), reward function (rules vs learned models), and the algorithm that turns rewards into gradient coefficients.

## Evaluation highlights beyond raw MATH scores

- Step-by-step reasoning (CoT): DeepSeekMath-Base 7B leads open-source base models on GSM8K and MATH in few-shot CoT prompting.
- Program-of-thought (PoT): For program-aided math (Python + libraries like math/sympy), DeepSeekMath-Base outperforms prior state-of-the-art open models such as Llemma 34B on program-aided tasks.
- Formal mathematics (autoformalization): DeepSeekMath-Base demonstrates strong few-shot informal-to-formal performance on miniF2F using Isabelle, showing the pretraining corpus contains useful formalization cues.
- General reasoning and code: Math pretraining also helps MMLU and BBH performance; including code tokens during continual training preserves coding ability.

## Some ablations & surprising findings

- ArXiv is not a free lunch. The authors found that training on arXiv-only corpora (even large ones) did not reliably improve, and sometimes hurt, performance across several math benchmarks. This suggests web-derived worked solutions and problem pages provide different (and perhaps more useful) signals than raw arXiv content for the benchmarks they evaluated.
- Mixing code and math tokens in a single stage can avoid catastrophic forgetting and preserve both coding and math abilities, but model capacity matters: small models may struggle to absorb both modalities perfectly in a one-stage mix versus a staged approach.

## Limitations and open problems

The authors are explicit about remaining weaknesses:

- Geometry and some theorem-proof capabilities remain weaker than closed models. They noted failures on certain triangle/ellipse problems in dry runs, indicating data selection bias in pretraining or fine-tuning.
- Few-shot generalization is still behind GPT-4: DeepSeekMath shows similar zero- and few-shot performance, whereas GPT-4 improves substantially with few-shot examples.
- Reward models and RL remain fragile: the paper points to future work in reward-model generalization, modeling reward uncertainty, and more robust weak-to-strong alignment methods.
- Scale still helps: some gains may be unlocked only with larger model capacity.

## Bottom line: what this means for open AI

DeepSeekMath is a concrete demonstration of a few practical ideas:

- Heavy investment in a purpose-built, web-mined math corpus (120B math tokens) can give a small model outsized gains.
- Code pretraining is a useful foundation for mathematical problem solving, especially when program-aided solving is part of the evaluation.
- Careful supervised instruction tuning plus an efficient, comparison-aware RL method (GRPO) can concentrate probability mass on correct multi-step solutions, enabling single-sample performance to improve and sampling-based techniques (self-consistency) to pay off even more.

A 7B open model pushing past 50% on MATH is not just a benchmark milestone — it shows that with engineering on the data and training side (not just parameter scale), the open community can close much of the gap to proprietary models in specific, hard domains like math. The DeepSeekMath Corpus and GRPO are concrete pieces other researchers can build on to continue that progress.
