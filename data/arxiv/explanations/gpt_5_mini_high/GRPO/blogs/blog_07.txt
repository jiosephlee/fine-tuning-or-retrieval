\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Surprises, Limits, and What’s Next: ArXiv Isn’t Magic, Geometry Is Hard

DeepSeekMath is a focused attempt to push open models on mathematical reasoning. The headline results are impressive: a 7B model trained on a carefully mined Common Crawl math corpus, instruction-tuned, and reinforced with a new RL recipe reaches competitive scores on hard benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K and the competition-level MATH). But the project also produced several counterintuitive and candid takeaways that matter for anyone building math-specialized LLMs. This post pulls those lessons together, explains why they matter, and sketches a practical roadmap for what’s next.

## The counterintuitive find: arXiv isn’t a silver bullet
A lot of prior math-focused corpora lean heavily on arXiv. MathPile and several related efforts are arXiv-dominated (e.g., >85% in some mixes), so the widely-held intuition has been: more arXiv = better math reasoning.

DeepSeekMath’s experiments challenge that. The team built a 120B-token DeepSeekMath Corpus by mining Common Crawl for math content via an iterative fastText-based classifier and human refinement (ending with ~35.5M math pages). They also ran controlled experiments training models on arXiv-only corpora (variants like ArXiv-RedPajama and arXiv-heavy mixes). The result: arXiv-only or arXiv-heavy training produced no clear improvements — and in some cases produced worse downstream performance — on a range of benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-STEM, miniF2F). In short: raw arXiv scale is not by itself a substitute for carefully selected web math.

Important nuance: the paper cautions this conclusion is limited. The team did not exhaustively test every task (e.g., informalization tasks), every data mix, or larger model scales. Still, the practical lesson is clear: the content and diversity of math data matter more than simply adding more arXiv tokens.

## What actually moved the needle
DeepSeekMath’s wins came from a few concrete engineering choices:

- A large, high-quality math web corpus. Iterative mining of Common Crawl with a classifier seeded from OpenWebMath produced 120B math tokens containing both English and Chinese material. The resulting model (initialized from a code-trained 7B base) showed strong gains across English and Chinese math benchmarks.
- Code pretraining before math training. The team found that code training helps mathematical problem solving both with and without tools. Two-stage schedules (code → math) improved program-aided reasoning and also accelerated subsequent math training.
- Focused instruction tuning and a compact but effective RL phase. With chain-of-thought and program-of-thought instruction tuning followed by reinforcement learning, the model’s numeric scores rose substantially (for example, DeepSeekMath-Instruct → RL moves MATH from ~46.8% to ~51.7%, and GSM8K from ~82.9% to ~88.2% under the reported settings).

These show that carefully selected web math and training recipes can let a relatively small 7B model punch well above what naive scaling on arXiv would suggest.

## Reinforcement learning: GRPO and what it told us
One practical innovation in the paper is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a variant of PPO that removes the separate critic and uses group statistics as a baseline. Concretely, for each question the policy samples a group of outputs $\{o_1,\dots,o_G\}$, scores them with a reward model, and computes advantages relative to the group. This avoids training a heavy value network and reduces memory cost; the authors used groups of size $G=64$ during RL.

Key empirical insights from RL experiments:

- GRPO was effective and efficient: it improved in-domain benchmarks substantially (e.g., MATH and GSM8K) while using less RL memory than standard PPO.
- Process supervision (step-level rewards) generally helps over just outcome-level rewards.
- Iterative training of the reward model (retraining on new policy samples with a replay buffer) boosts performance further.
- Crucially, the team observed that RL raised Maj@K (the probability the majority of a sampled set is correct) more than Pass@K. In practical terms, RL made the model’s probability mass sharper around the correct answers (so majority voting/self-consistency helps more), but did not necessarily change the model’s underlying ability on single deterministic outputs. This suggests RL is often aligning output distributions rather than doing deep capability discovery.

## Current weaknesses: geometry, some theorem-proof tasks, and few-shot gaps
Despite strong numbers on many benchmarks, limitations remain:

- Geometry and theorem-style problems remain weaker than closed-source baselines. The team reports poorer performance on geometry (notably some triangle and ellipse problems in a dry run) and says theorem-proof capability trails the best closed models. They point to likely data selection bias in pretraining/fine-tuning as a partial cause.
- Few-shot is still a gap versus GPT-4. DeepSeekMath shows similar zero-shot and few-shot performance, while GPT-4 gains from few-shot examples. This suggests model scale and/or training diversity still give closed models an edge in few-shot adaptation.

These are honest, practical limits — not one-off lab quirks.

## A pragmatic roadmap: what to try next
The paper lays out several concrete directions that follow from these findings. Below I summarize the most actionable items.

- Improve data coverage and specificity
  - Close geometric gaps by deliberately collecting more geometry-focused materials (problem sets, worked solutions, and variations), including non-English sources. The existing Common Crawl mining pipeline is a good start but needs targeted expansion into missing subdomains.
  - Expand process-level math examples (stepwise solutions, program-of-thought traces) so models see intermediate reasoning steps during pretraining and SFT.

- Better decoding and exploration strategies
  - Move beyond naive nucleus sampling during RL exploration. Tree-search–style decoding and other structured sampling strategies can produce higher-quality candidate traces for reward scoring.
  - Invest in efficient inference techniques (speculative decoding, fast sampling) to make large-sample exploration practical.

- Make RL robust to noisy rewards
  - Reward models are imperfect. The team recommends exploring “weak-to-strong” alignment methods that are robust when reward labels have noise or bias.
  - GRPO is a step (group baselines fit the comparative nature of many reward datasets), but more robust gradient-coefficient schemes are promising.

- Build stronger, uncertainty-aware reward models
  - Reward models should generalize to out-of-distribution prompts and advanced decoding outputs. They should also communicate uncertainty (calibration or explicit posterior estimates) so RL algorithms can down-weight low-confidence signals.
  - Process reward models (fine-grained, step-level scorers) continue to look useful for supervising complex multi-step reasoning.

- Continue to exploit code training
  - The observed benefits from code pretraining motivate further study of mixed or staged code+math schedules, particularly at larger scales where single-stage mixtures sometimes underperformed.

## Bottom line
DeepSeekMath shows that carefully engineered web math data, combined with sensible code pretraining, instruction tuning, and a targeted RL recipe, can push an open 7B model to competitive math performance. But scale and data diversity still matter in subtle ways: arXiv bulk is not a substitute for the right mix of web math, geometry remains a blind spot, and RL often sharpens output distributions more than it fundamentally improves core reasoning.

If you’re building math-specialized LLMs: don’t treat arXiv as “magic dust.” Invest in targeted data coverage (especially for geometry and theorem formats), make exploration and decoding smarter, and treat reward modeling and RL as joint problems — better reward models and RL algorithms that tolerate imperfect supervision are as important as more compute.
