\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 2: Problem Setting, Benchmarks, and Evaluation Modes

# Task definitions: step-by-step reasoning, tool-integrated reasoning, formal proofs
The paper organizes mathematical evaluation around three complementary task families that probe different facets of a model's mathematical competence.

Step-by-step reasoning refers to generating a human-readable chain of intermediate steps that lead from a problem statement to a final answer. In this work that is instantiated by chain-of-thought (CoT) style solutions: the model is prompted (often in a few-shot setting) to produce an explicit sequence of reasoning steps and a final numerical or symbolic answer. Step-by-step reasoning is designed to reveal the model’s ability to plan a multi-step solution, manipulate algebraic expressions and arithmetic, and maintain coherence across intermediate steps. The paper evaluates CoT performance across many benchmarks to measure how well models produce correct, explainable solutions without external computation.

Tool-integrated reasoning covers approaches where the model is allowed to call external computation or produce executable artifacts as part of the solution. The paper treats program-of-thought (PoT) and broader tool-integrated reasoning under this umbrella. In PoT the model writes a program (the paper uses Python in evaluation) that computes the solution; allowed libraries include standard numeric libraries and symbolic tools such as $ \texttt{math} $ and $ \texttt{sympy} $. The evaluation then runs that program and uses its execution result as the answer. Tool-integrated reasoning more generally may interleave natural-language reasoning with tool calls or allow multi-step tool usage (the paper’s instruction-tuning and evaluation sets include examples in a tool-integrated format). This mode is particularly useful when exact arithmetic, numerical stability, or symbolic manipulation are critical and when the model's natural-language arithmetic is error-prone.

Formal proofs form a qualitatively different task: instead of producing informal textual reasoning, the model must produce a formal object acceptable to a proof assistant. The paper studies the informal-to-formal autoformalization task where the model is given an informal statement, its formal counterpart, and an informal proof, and must generate a formal proof in the language of a theorem prover. The generated formal proof is then validated by the proof assistant. In the paper this pipeline uses Isabelle as the prover and Sledgehammer as an automated tactic-filling tool. Formal-proof tasks evaluate a model’s ability to translate intuitive mathematical reasoning into mechanically checkable steps and to respect strict syntactic and semantic constraints that go far beyond what is required for natural-language reasoning.

These three task families probe different capabilities: CoT tests multi-step, explainable reasoning in natural language; PoT and tool-integrated setups test the ability to produce correct executable solutions and to orchestrate external tools; formal proof tasks test the ability to target a formal calculus and to satisfy mechanical verification.

# English benchmarks: GSM8K, MATH, SAT, OCW Courses, MMLU-STEM, BBH
The paper evaluates models on a spectrum of English benchmarks chosen to span difficulty, style, and evaluation protocol.

GSM8K is a grade-school-level quantitative reasoning dataset widely used to measure performance on multi-step arithmetic and reasoning problems. The paper repeatedly uses few-shot chain-of-thought prompting on GSM8K to measure a model’s basic step-by-step reasoning ability and programmatic solving when tools are allowed. For context, the DeepSeekMath-Base model reaches strong scores on GSM8K (the paper reports 64.2% for DeepSeekMath-Base 7B under CoT evaluation), which highlights the effect of math-focused pre-training.

MATH is a competition-level benchmark composed of high-school and early-college competition problems that demand deeper mathematical reasoning and more sophisticated multi-step strategies. The paper emphasizes MATH as a hard test for open models: with no tool use DeepSeekMath-RL 7B reaches 51.7% on MATH after reinforcement learning, and using self-consistency (described later) over 64 samples yields 60.9% for the model—illustrating how sampling and aggregation can markedly affect a model’s evaluated performance on tougher problems.

SAT and OCW Courses are included as additional English sources spanning standardized test questions (SAT) and course-style problems drawn from course materials (OCW Courses, as used in prior math-evaluation work). These datasets provide diverse problem styles and are used to evaluate how pre-training and fine-tuning generalize across curricula and problem formats.

MMLU-STEM and BBH probe broader reasoning beyond pure arithmetic. MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding) has 57 multiple-choice tasks across many subjects; MMLU-STEM is the subset focusing on science, technology, engineering, and mathematics. BBH (BIG-Bench Hard) is a suite of 23 challenging tasks, many requiring multi-step or creative reasoning. The paper uses these benchmarks to show that math-focused pre-training can also benefit general reasoning and multi-topic performance.

Taken together, these English benchmarks let the paper measure performance from simple arithmetic to contest-level mathematics, and from natural-language explanation to tightly-scored multiple-choice tasks.

# Chinese benchmarks: MGSM-zh, CMATH, Gaokao-MathCloze, Gaokao-MathQA
To measure multilingual and language-specific performance, the paper evaluates on several Chinese mathematical benchmarks.

MGSM-zh is the Chinese analog to grade-school quantitative reasoning tasks, designed to test basic multi-step reasoning in Chinese. CMATH is a Chinese benchmark for quantitative reasoning covering a range of difficulties. The paper reports that the DeepSeekMath Corpus is multilingual (chiefly English and Chinese) and that math pre-training on multilingual web pages yields measurable improvements on Chinese benchmarks compared with English-only pre-training used by other works.

Gaokao-MathCloze and Gaokao-MathQA come from the Chinese national college-entrance exam (Gaokao) style problems. These datasets capture problems typical of upper-secondary curricula in Chinese, and often include multiple-choice and cloze-style formats that require precise answer formatting and understanding. The paper’s supervised fine-tuning set also includes annotated Chinese K–12 problems spanning many subtopics (the paper cites 76 subtopics such as linear equations), which contributes to improved Chinese performance after instruction tuning.

Evaluating on these Chinese sets is important because it exposes language-dependent failure modes and tests the ability of a single model and pre-training corpus to handle mathematics expressed in multiple languages.

# Formal mathematics: miniF2F with Isabelle and Sledgehammer
For formal mathematics the paper focuses on the informal-to-formal theorem proving task on miniF2F. miniF2F is a benchmark of competition-style mathematical problems suitable for formalization. The evaluation protocol used in the paper supplies the model with an informal statement, a formal counterpart of the statement, and an informal proof sketch; the model must produce a formal proof in Isabelle’s language.

The paper’s evaluation pipeline is two-stage. First, the model generates a formal proof sketch or structured proof script. Second, the generated formal object is passed to Isabelle. The paper then uses Sledgehammer, an automated prover that attempts to discharge subgoals by calling external proof search tools, to fill in details the model omitted in the sketch. The final measure of success is whether Isabelle accepts the proof. This setting tests different skills than natural-language CoT: the model must produce syntactically correct formal objects and anticipate mechanized proof tactics. The paper reports that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B demonstrates strong few-shot autoformalization performance on this task, showing that math-focused pre-training can improve the bridge between informal reasoning and formal verification.

# Evaluation modes: tool-free CoT vs program-of-thought with Python
The paper uses distinct evaluation modes to reflect the task definitions and practical differences when tools are allowed or disallowed.

Tool-free CoT evaluation requires the model to produce a purely textual chain of reasoning, ending in a final answer that can be checked against the ground truth. The paper typically applies few-shot CoT prompting: a small number of worked examples (CoT-style prompts) are supplied and then the model is asked to produce a solution for each test problem. This mode measures the model’s internal reasoning and its ability to write explanations intelligible to humans and verifiable by an evaluator script (for example, by parsing the final numeric answer).

Program-of-thought evaluation (PoT) asks the model to generate executable code that implements a solution. In the paper’s setup the model is prompted to write a Python program; libraries such as $ \texttt{math} $ and $ \texttt{sympy} $ are permitted to perform arithmetic and symbolic computations. The program is executed and the execution result is taken as the model’s answer. This removes a class of surface-level arithmetic or transcription errors but transfers the burden to producing correct program syntax and the correct algorithmic approach. The paper reports that pre-training on code improves PoT performance and also has beneficial effects on tool-free reasoning.

Tool-integrated evaluation is a hybrid: the model may interleave natural language steps with tool calls, or be tuned on examples that mix CoT and PoT styles. The SFT dataset constructed in the paper contains CoT, PoT, and tool-integrated examples so that models can be evaluated in settings where tools are available at inference time or are simulated during evaluation.

These modes are complementary: CoT emphasizes human-like step explanations and is sensitive to arithmetic mistakes by the model; PoT emphasizes correctness via execution and can be more robust for numerically intensive tasks; tool-integrated modes expose the model’s ability to combine both types of reasoning.

# Self-consistency and majority voting concepts
Self-consistency is a sampling-and-aggregation strategy the paper uses to improve final-answer reliability. The idea is to sample multiple independent reasoning traces (often using a stochastic decoder) for the same question and then aggregate the final answers via majority voting. Concretely, if the model produces $K$ sampled CoT solutions with final answers $a_1,\dots,a_K$, self-consistency returns the majority-final answer as the model’s prediction. The paper reports a concrete result: self-consistency over $64$ samples from DeepSeekMath 7B yields a MATH accuracy of $60.9\%$, substantially higher than single-sample CoT accuracy for the same model.

Majority-based aggregation is formalized in the paper’s analysis as Maj@K. For a single example with $K$ sampled outputs, Maj@K reports whether the majority answer among the $K$ samples equals the ground-truth answer. This contrasts with Pass@K (defined below), which reports whether any sampled output among $K$ is correct. The paper’s reinforcement-learning analysis observes that RL improves Maj@K but not Pass@K: after RL the model’s distribution concentrates probability mass on correct answers so that the majority among the top $K$ is more likely to be correct, while the chance of at least one correct sample among $K$ (Pass@K) does not necessarily increase. This observation supports the interpretation that RL is making the model’s output distribution “more robust” by shifting mass toward correct outputs rather than expanding the model’s raw capability to produce at least one correct trace among many samples.

From a practical perspective, self-consistency and majority voting are powerful evaluation-time techniques: sampling many solutions and aggregating can substantially improve measured accuracy, especially for hard multi-step problems.

# Metrics: accuracy, Pass@K, Maj@K
The paper uses simple but carefully chosen metrics to quantify success across different modes.

Accuracy is the most basic metric: for a dataset of $N$ examples, accuracy is
\[
\text{Accuracy} \;=\; \frac{\#\{\text{examples answered correctly}\}}{N}\,.
\]
For CoT evaluations this means extracting the final answer from the generated chain and comparing it to the ground truth; for PoT evaluations the executed program’s result is compared against the expected output; for formal proofs the metric is the proportion of problems for which the theorem prover accepts the generated formal proof.

Pass@K measures whether at least one of $K$ sampled outputs for a given problem is correct. If for example we sample $o_1,\dots,o_K$ for question $q$, define the indicator
\[
\text{Pass@}K(q) \;=\; \begin{cases}
1 & \text{if }\exists i\in\{1,\dots,K\}\ \text{s.t. } o_i \text{ is correct}\\[4pt]
0 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]
The dataset-level Pass@K is the average of this indicator across questions. Pass@K captures whether the correct answer is present among a collection of stochastic samples.

Maj@K captures whether majority voting among $K$ sampled outputs yields the correct answer. Let $a_1,\dots,a_K$ be the final answers for the $K$ samples, and let $a^\ast$ be the answer with highest frequency among them. Then
\[
\text{Maj@}K(q) \;=\; \begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } a^\ast \text{ equals the ground-truth answer}\\[4pt]
0 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]
Maj@K is the metric used in self-consistency-style aggregation and emphasizes whether the correct answer is supported by the bulk of the model’s stochastic outputs.

The paper uses these metrics to distinguish different improvements: improving Pass@K may indicate the model can produce a correct answer somewhere in its output distribution, while improving Maj@K indicates the distribution concentrates mass on correct answers so that simple aggregation reliably recovers the right solution.

# Common pitfalls: contamination, answer extraction, few-shot vs zero-shot
Evaluating mathematical reasoning is subtle and several common pitfalls must be addressed; the paper explicitly deals with some of them.

Benchmark contamination (data leakage) is a major concern: training on texts that contain copies of evaluation questions or answers will inflate measured performance. To prevent this the paper’s pre-training corpus is decontaminated using a conservative n-gram matching rule: any text segment that contains a 10-gram string exactly matching a substring from an evaluation benchmark is removed; for benchmark texts shorter than 10 grams but at least 3 grams, exact matching is still used. This removes direct copies or near-copies of benchmark questions from the pre-training data and reduces the risk that the model memorized evaluation items.

Answer extraction is a practical evaluation-stage challenge. For CoT outputs, the evaluator must reliably extract the final numeric or symbolic answer from free-form text; small formatting differences or missing “Answer:” markers can cause false negatives. For PoT outputs, the evaluator must execute the generated program and read its execution result; programs can fail to run for syntactic reasons, raise exceptions, or produce ambiguous outputs. The paper’s PoT evaluation treats the execution result as the authoritative answer, which makes the evaluation robust to many arithmetic mistakes but sensitive to code-generation quality and execution reliability.

Few-shot versus zero-shot performance is another important axis. Some large models gain substantial improvements from few-shot prompting, whereas smaller models may show little difference between zero-shot and few-shot evaluation. The paper notes a limitation: DeepSeekMath (at 7B) does not improve substantially from few-shot prompting compared to zero-shot, unlike much larger closed models such as GPT-4. This suggests that few-shot gains depend on model scale and on how the model’s pre-training and fine-tuning predispose it to follow few-shot exemplars.

Finally, data selection bias and coverage gaps can create blind spots. The paper reports that particular capabilities—geometry and some theorem-proof patterns—remain weaker than competing closed models, and the authors attribute this partly to selection bias in pre-training and fine-tuning data. Similarly, the experiments reported in the paper show that naively including arXiv papers did not improve (and sometimes worsened) downstream mathematical benchmarks, indicating that not all “math-like” corpora are equally beneficial; careful curation and evaluation decontamination are therefore essential.

Taken together, these pitfalls underline that high-quality evaluation of mathematical reasoning requires careful corpus construction, thoughtful prompt and output parsing rules, consideration of sampling and aggregation strategies, and awareness that some gains (for instance via reinforcement learning or self-consistency) may reflect distributional sharpening rather than an increase in the model’s latent problem-solving capacity.
