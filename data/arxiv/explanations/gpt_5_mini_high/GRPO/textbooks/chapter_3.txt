\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 3: Engineering a Massive Math Corpus from the Web

# Seed corpus: OpenWebMath as positive examples
Any large-scale, domain-specific data-mining pipeline must begin with a reliable set of positive examples that capture the target distribution. In the DeepSeekMath pipeline the team selected OpenWebMath as that initial seed corpus: a curated collection of high-quality mathematical web texts. The seed plays two roles at once. First, it provides clean exemplars of what the classifier should consider “mathy” content; second, it supplies the labeled positives needed to bootstrap a simple but fast classifier capable of scanning billions of web pages.

Practically, the authors sampled from OpenWebMath to form the positive class used to train the first classifier. Concretely, they randomly selected $500{,}000$ data points from the seed corpus to serve as positive examples during the first round of classifier training. That large, high-quality positive set makes it feasible to learn a compact model that generalizes from the seed to the messy, heterogeneous material in Common Crawl, while keeping the annotation burden low in the early stages.

# fastText classifier setup: positives and negatives, key hyperparameters
To scale recall across the entire Common Crawl, the team used a fastText-based classifier. FastText is an efficient, bag-of-features style classifier that is particularly well suited to web-scale retrieval because it trains and scores very quickly. The training set for the first classifier consisted of the $500{,}000$ positive examples sampled from OpenWebMath and $500{,}000$ negative examples sampled from Common Crawl. Using this balanced, large training set helped the classifier learn robust surface and n-gram cues that distinguish math content from general web text.

The authors reported the explicit fastText hyperparameters they used: a vector dimension of $256$, a learning rate of $0.1$, a maximum word n-gram length of $3$, a minimum word occurrence threshold of $3$, and $3$ training epochs. They employed the open-source fastText implementation for this step. These choices reflect a trade-off between expressivity and speed: modest embedding dimensionality and short n-grams keep memory and compute manageable at web scale while still capturing many mathematical patterns (symbols, formula-like tokens, and domain-specific vocabulary).

# Web-scale deduplication and near-duplication
Common Crawl contains enormous amounts of redundancy: mirrored pages, syndicated content, and near-duplicates are pervasive. Before running any classifier at web scale it is therefore essential to consolidate duplicates so that downstream ranking and retrieval are efficient and do not over-count the same content. The DeepSeekMath pipeline applies URL-based deduplication and near-duplication techniques to collapse the crawl down to a workable set of candidate pages. After these deduplication steps the working set contained roughly $40\ \text{B}$ HTML web pages.

Reducing the crawl in this way does two important things. First, it cuts the computational cost of featurizing and classifying every page. Second, it reduces the risk that a small number of popular pages will dominate the final corpus simply because they are replicated many times. The deduplication stage therefore produces a cleaner, more diverse pool of pages for the subsequent fastText recall and ranking steps.

# Scoring and ranking pages by classifier confidence
With the deduplicated collection in hand, the fastText model is applied to every candidate page to produce a scalar score (a confidence that the page is math-related). The pipeline then ranks pages by that score and retains only the top-ranked pages for further consideration. This ranking-by-confidence is the core mechanism that converts a seed-driven classifier into a controlled corpus extraction: it allows the team to dial the balance between precision and scale by selecting more or fewer top documents.

Deciding how many of the top-ranked pages to keep is an empirical choice. The DeepSeekMath team treated the ranking as a tunable filter and measured downstream effects by running pre-training experiments on the retained subsets (see next section). In the initial iteration they conservatively preserved the top pages corresponding to the highest-confidence slice of the deduplicated crawl and chose to keep the top $40\ \text{B}$ tokens worth of content from those pages.

# Token budgets explored: 40B, 80B, 120B, 160B
Instead of fixing a single corpus size up front, the project explicitly explored a range of token budgets to evaluate the trade-offs between quantity and per-token quality. The team assessed pre-training outcomes when retaining the top $40\ \text{B}$, $80\ \text{B}$, $120\ \text{B}$, and $160\ \text{B}$ tokens from the ranked pages. These experiments let them observe how model learning curves changed as the retained math material grew: whether additional tokens improved final accuracy or merely introduced noise and repetition.

In that first pass they settled on keeping the top $40\ \text{B}$ tokens, a choice driven by the pre-training experiments performed at each budget level. Iteration and subsequent refinements later expanded the retained collection to yield the final $120\ \text{B}$-token DeepSeekMath Corpus, but the initial budget experiments were crucial to understand the marginal value of adding more ranked pages.

# Domain-based mining: high-yield math domains and manual URL annotation
A classifier trained only on the initial seed will miss many math pages whose surface features differ from the seed. To broaden coverage, the team applied a domain-level discovery heuristic. They partitioned Common Crawl into disjoint domains (pages sharing the same base URL) and computed, for each domain, the percentage of that domain’s pages that the classifier had selected in the first iteration. Domains where more than $10\%$ of pages were selected were flagged as math-related; a concrete example is mathoverflow.net.

Flagging high-yield domains made it efficient to focus manual effort. The authors then manually annotated the URL patterns inside these flagged domains that correspond to mathematical content—for example, tagging a path like /questions on mathoverflow.net as math-specific. Any pages under those annotated URL patterns that had not yet been collected were then added back into the positive seed set. This domain-and-URL annotation step injects targeted diversity into the positive examples, which is especially effective at capturing site-specific templates and navigational text that a single seed cannot represent.

# Four iterations to 35.5M pages and 120B tokens
The end-to-end collection process is iterative. Starting from the OpenWebMath seed, the authors retrained the fastText classifier after augmenting the positive set with the domain-level discoveries and human-annotated URLs, then re-scored the deduplicated crawl, re-ranked pages, and again enlarged the seed with newly discovered positives. After repeating this cycle four times the pipeline produced $35.5\ \text{M}$ mathematical web pages totaling $120\ \text{B}$ tokens.

The iterative approach has two virtuous effects. First, each iteration supplies the classifier with a more diverse and representative set of positives, reducing blind spots that arise from a narrow initial seed. Second, by monitoring collection overlap the team could detect convergence: in the fourth iteration nearly $98\%$ of the pages had already been collected in the third iteration, so growth had essentially plateaued and the process was stopped. Human annotation at intermediate steps provided higher-quality labels that improved the classifier’s recall of real math content across sites.

# Multilingual coverage with English and Chinese emphasis
A distinctive property of the DeepSeekMath Corpus is its multilingual character: although a large fraction of material is in English, the pipeline deliberately preserved substantial Chinese content as well. Because Common Crawl already contains pages in multiple languages, the seed-and-classifier approach can surface non-English math pages when the seed and the classifier support them. The authors note that this multilingual coverage—particularly the English and Chinese material—contributed to measurable improvements on Chinese mathematical benchmarks, where earlier math corpora were heavily English-centric.

Including multiple languages also changes the classifier’s operating regime: features and n-grams that signal mathematical content in one language do not necessarily transfer to another, which is one reason why the domain-focused URL expansion and human annotation were important for capturing non-English math pages reliably.

# Decontamination: exact 10-gram and 3-gram filtering against benchmarks
To protect the integrity of benchmark evaluations, the DeepSeekMath pipeline includes explicit decontamination filters that remove pages which contain verbatim fragments of held-out evaluation problems and solutions. Concretely, the team followed a conservative rule: any text segment that contains a $10$-gram string exactly matching any substring from the evaluation benchmarks is removed from the training corpus. For benchmark items shorter than $10$ grams but at least $3$ grams, they applied exact matching at that shorter n-gram length. The benchmarks specifically guarded against include English datasets such as GSM8K and MATH and Chinese datasets such as CMATH and AGIEval.

This exact n-gram filtering is a pragmatic compromise: it catches many potential leakages of benchmark content into the pre-training data without relying on fragile semantic similarity heuristics. It is, however, a blunt instrument and may remove some legitimate pages that coincidentally contain short shared phrases; the authors prefer that conservative culling to the risk of contamination.

# Applicability to other domains (e.g., coding)
The overall design of the DeepSeekMath pipeline is domain-agnostic: start from a high-quality seed, train an efficient classifier to recall similar pages, deduplicate the crawl, rank by classifier confidence, expand positives via domain-level discovery and manual URL annotation, and iterate until coverage converges. The authors explicitly note that the same procedure applies to other specialized domains—coding being an obvious example. To build a code-focused corpus one would swap the seed to a curated code dataset, sample negatives from the crawl, train an analogous fast classifier, and run the same iterative domain discovery and decontamination steps.

Beyond mere transferability, the paper’s experiments suggest that starting a model from code training can interact positively with later domain training (for mathematics in their case), so applying this pipeline to code would both enable a high-quality code corpus and, in multi-stage pre-training regimes, potentially benefit downstream domain adaptation. The key practical takeaway is that careful seed selection, scalable and fast classification, domain-aware expansion, and conservative decontamination together enable construction of very large, high-quality domain corpora from the noisy but abundant Common Crawl.
