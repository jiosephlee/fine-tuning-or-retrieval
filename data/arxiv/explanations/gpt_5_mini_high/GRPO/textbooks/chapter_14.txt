\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 14: Limitations, Responsible Use, and Future Directions

# Weaker geometry and theorem-proving vs closed models

One of the clear limitations reported in the paper is that, despite strong gains on many quantitative-reasoning benchmarks, DeepSeekMath shows relatively weaker capability on geometry and some theorem-proving tasks compared to the best closed-source models. The authors explicitly note that in dry runs the model “cannot handle problems related to triangles and ellipses,” and more generally that the model’s geometry and theorem-proof competence lags behind closed models (Conclusion). At the same time, the paper also shows that DeepSeekMath-Base exhibits strong few-shot autoformalization on some formalization tasks (miniF2F → Isabelle) — so the weakness is specific and not a blanket failure on all formal-math tasks.

Two explanations are emphasized in the paper and are useful to keep in mind when diagnosing this class of failures. First, geometry problems often depend on visual information, specialized diagram conventions, or a style of reasoning that differs from the linear, algebraic text found on many web pages; such material is less likely to be well represented by a web-mined corpus whose classifier is trained on mostly text-like mathematical pages. Second, limitations in the pretraining and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) data selection can create topical gaps: if the seed set and iterative selection pipeline under-represent geometry or geometric proofs, the model will receive far fewer examples of the specialized patterns needed for reliable performance.

From the vantage point of future work, the paper points to data-selection and fine-tuning as the most direct handles to close this gap. Concretely, improving geometry/theorem performance can be approached by (a) augmenting the pretraining corpus with geometry-specific sources (textbook problems, olympiad-style geometry threads, formal geometry libraries), (b) explicitly engineering the data-selection pipeline to recall and retain pages with geometric diagrams or LaTeX constructions common to geometry, and (c) designing SFT / process-supervision data that include stepwise geometric reasoning and formalization examples. The paper also cautions that simply adding arXiv content did not show notable improvements in their experiments, so targeted curation (not just more general scientific text) appears necessary.

# Few-shot limitations at 7B scale

A prominent empirical point in the paper is that model scale matters for few-shot in-context learning. The authors report that, at 7B parameters, DeepSeekMath is substantially improved by math-focused pre-training, instruction tuning, and RL, but it still underperforms GPT-4 on few-shot gains: “GPT-4 could improve its performance with few-shot inputs, while DeepSeekMath shows similar performance in zero-shot and few-shot evaluation” (Conclusion). In other words, the 7B model shows limited improvement when provided with few worked examples at test time, compared to very large closed models.

The paper’s account suggests two interacting causes. First, smaller models have less capacity to internalize and then rapidly adapt to novel patterns presented only in-context. Second, the training distribution and fine-tuning regime matter: if the model has not been exposed during training to many few-shot-like contexts, its in-context learning behavior will remain muted. The experiments in the paper also show that data quality matters a great deal — a well-curated math corpus let a 7B model approach the performance of a much larger closed model on many benchmarks, but not on the few-shot advantage dimension.

Practical and research directions consistent with the paper include (1) studying how to increase few-shot responsiveness at modest parameter scales via targeted training (for example, more SFT examples that explicitly mimic few-shot contexts or chain-of-thought few-shot episodes), and (2) empirically evaluating the trade-offs between investing compute in model scale versus investing compute in higher-quality, more diverse pretraining and SFT data. The paper’s broader message is that data improvements can compensate a lot for smaller scale, but some few-shot behaviors may still require either larger models or specialized training aimed at in-context learning.

# Potential data-selection biases in pretraining and SFT

The paper describes a carefully engineered, iterative pipeline for mining math-relevant pages from Common Crawl. The approach starts from a seed corpus (OpenWebMath), trains a fastText classifier (256-d vectors, learning rate 0.1, word n-grams up to length 3, minimum word count 3, 3 epochs), performs URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication over Common Crawl, and then iteratively recalls and expands positive examples by identifying domains with a high collection rate (>10% of pages collected) and annotating math-related URLs within those domains. After four iterations they obtained 35.5M math web pages (120B tokens). They also applied benchmark-decontamination heuristics removing any text segment containing a 10-gram exact match to evaluation benchmarks (or shorter exact matches for short benchmarks).

Those design choices — every step from the seed set to the classifier algorithm and the domain heuristics — are powerful levers but also sources of bias. The paper itself points out that the geometry shortcomings may “indicate data selection bias in pre-training and fine-tuning.” Concretely, biases can arise because the fastText classifier is trained on examples similar to the seed; if the seed over-represents algebraic, symbolic, or text-heavy math pages, pages that use diagrams, non-standard LaTeX constructs, or pedagogical geometry explanations may be under-recalled. The >10% domain threshold and the URL heuristics tend to privilege domains with a high density of the initially recognized style, which can reinforce coverage gaps.

To mitigate these biases (and the paper recommends further work along these lines), one should diversify seed collections, incorporate targeted retrieval heuristics for underrepresented subtopics (geometry, combinatorics, formal proofs), and use active human annotation to cover modalities that an automated classifier misses. The paper’s iterative approach — expanding the seed set based on domains where the first pass concentrated — is a strong step, but it should be complemented by targeted sampling, stratified selection by subtopic, and explicit checks for topic coverage (for example, measuring how many problems about triangles, conic sections, or formal lemmas exist in the collected corpus). The decontamination rule (10-gram exact matching) is conservative and important for preventing benchmark leakage, but it is also an example of a processing rule that affects what data survives; documenting and reproducing such heuristics (seed lists, classifier hyperparameters, thresholds) is essential for reproducibility and for diagnosing bias.

# Advanced decoding for RL data: tree-search and better exploration

A core message in the paper’s RL discussion is that the quality of the sampled outputs used for RL matters a great deal. Reinforcement learning in this setting uses question prompts and sampled outputs as exploration data; the paper notes that their RL used nucleus sampling but explicitly points to more advanced decoding and sampling strategies as a promising future direction (“advanced sampling (decoding) strategies, like those based on tree-search methods” and “efficient inference techniques” in Section 5.2).

Why does decoding matter? RL algorithms (including GRPO) learn from the outputs that the current policy produces. If those outputs are narrowly concentrated or lack diverse reasoning trajectories, the reward model and subsequent updates will see a limited set of behaviors and may only refine a narrow mode. Tree-search decoding (or other structured exploration strategies) can expose diverse reasoning chains and intermediate states, producing higher-quality trajectories for both reward-model training and policy updates. Efficient decoding techniques (speculative decoding and other approximate decoders) are also important because they increase the number of distinct outputs one can sample for the same compute budget, which enables larger groups per question (the paper’s GRPO experiments used groups of size 64) and, empirically, more stable group-relative baselines.

Concretely, the paper suggests evaluating advanced decoding strategies in the RL loop: compare simple nucleus sampling to structured tree-search exploration and measure the effect on downstream metrics (e.g., GSM8K and MATH accuracy, Maj@K/Pass@K behavior). A careful study would also analyze diversity, reward-model calibration on generated outputs, and throughput (samples per second) because efficient inference is necessary for scaling exploration.

# RL robustness to noisy rewards and weak-to-strong alignment

A central concern raised in the paper is that modern RL-style fine-tuning methods implicitly put a lot of trust in the reward model’s outputs, yet reward models are imperfect and can contain substantial noise. The paper references annotation noise observed in curated datasets (for example, noting that curated preference datasets have non-trivial annotation error rates) and uses that to motivate the need for RL techniques that are robust to noisy reward signals.

The paper articulates a “unified paradigm” in which training methods differ along three axes: Data Source, Reward Function, and Algorithm (Equation (9) in the paper). Under this view, many contemporary methods (RFT, DPO, PPO, GRPO) are variants of direct or simplified RL in which the reward model is treated as a source of supervision. If the reward signal is noisy or miscalibrated, blindly pushing model probabilities up or down can overfit to reward-model artifacts rather than producing real capability improvement.

To address this, the paper recommends exploring “weak-to-strong” alignment pipelines: methods that start from weak or approximate reward supervision and progressively refine the reward signal and the policy in a way that mitigates the effects of noise. One concrete mechanism the paper uses is iterative RL: reward models are periodically re-trained on newly sampled data (with a replay buffer for stability), and the policy is updated against the refreshed reward model (Algorithm 1). This iterative loop helps the reward model adapt to the actor’s changing distribution. Beyond that, the paper suggests pursuing RL algorithms that explicitly account for reward uncertainty and that are robust to noisy labels—research directions the authors highlight as important next steps.

# Reward model generalization, uncertainty calibration, and process rewards

The paper identifies three concrete directions for improving reward-based RL from the reward-model perspective: better generalization, reflecting model uncertainty, and building efficient process (stepwise) rewarders.

1. Generalization. Reward models trained on a restricted set of outputs or prompts may not score out-of-distribution questions or sophisticated decoding outputs correctly. The paper stresses that without generalization, RL can end up “stabilizing the distribution” of the policy (making it conform to whatever the reward model prefers) rather than improving the underlying capability that matters for human evaluation. The practical implication is that reward models should be validated on diverse outputs (different decoding algorithms, out-of-domain prompts) and trained with data that covers the intended exploration regime.

2. Uncertainty calibration. The authors point out that reward models typically return scalar scores but do not communicate uncertainty. Reward uncertainty is useful in the training algorithm: if the reward model is uncertain about a particular output, the RL update should either down-weight that example or treat it differently than high-confidence signals. This is closely tied to the “weak-to-strong” idea: uncertainty-aware methods can treat initial reward signals as tentative and require corroboration before making strong policy updates.

3. Process rewards. The paper empirically compares outcome supervision (a single reward at the end of an entire output) and process supervision (rewards at the end of reasoning steps). Outcome supervision sets the per-token advantage to a normalized terminal reward:
\[
\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})},
\]
where \(\mathbf{r}=\{r_1,\dots,r_G\}\) are the group rewards. Process supervision, in contrast, produces step-level rewards \(r_i^{index(j)}\), normalizes them across the whole batch, and assigns each token the sum of future step rewards:
\[
\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{index(j) \ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}.
\]
Empirically, GRPO combined with process supervision (GRPO+PS) outperformed GRPO with outcome supervision (GRPO+OS), demonstrating the value of fine-grained training signals for complex multi-step reasoning. The practical takeaway is that building high-quality process reward models is a promising direction: such models give RL more informative credit assignment and appear to be more effective for multi-step mathematical reasoning.

# Scaling data and models responsibly and reproducibly

A recurring empirical lesson in the paper is that large, high-quality data can materially improve a relatively small model: DeepSeekMath-Base (7B) trained with extensive math data gets close to or matches much larger prior models on many benchmarks (the paper compares a 7B model favorably with Minerva 540B in several respects). This result carries two important implications for responsible scaling.

First, prioritizing data quality and domain-relevant curation can be a more compute- and resource-efficient route to capability than simply increasing parameter count. The paper’s DeepSeekMath Corpus (120B math tokens) was built by an iterative seed-and-recall pipeline plus human annotation; the careful engineering of that collection was central to the model’s performance gains.

Second, the paper documents concrete practices to support reproducibility and to mitigate risks like benchmark contamination: URL-based deduplication, near-deduplication, a 10-gram exact-match filter against evaluation benchmarks, and thorough reporting of training hyperparameters (AdamW with \(\beta_1=0.9,\ \beta_2=0.95,\ \mathrm{weight\_decay}=0.1\); peak learning rates and learning-rate schedules; batch sizes and context lengths). These implementation details are important to reproduce results and to compare approaches fairly.

On RL and compute-efficiency, the paper also highlights algorithmic choices that reduce resource requirements while maintaining effectiveness. GRPO — by removing an explicit learned value function (the critic) and instead using the group-average baseline — reduces the memory and computational burden associated with typical PPO setups where a separate critic needs to be trained alongside the policy. This form of algorithmic parsimony is an example of responsible engineering: it enables meaningful RL improvements without multiplying the infrastructure footprint.

For responsible release and reproducibility, the paper’s experiments underline the need to publish (or at least document) data-selection scripts, seed corpora, decontamination heuristics, reward-model training data and evaluation splits, and RL sampling parameters (group size, sampling temperature, decoding method). Doing so permits others to reproduce results, to measure bias and coverage, and to extend the work safely.

# Open problems and suggested projects for students

The paper suggests multiple open directions and gives a clear starting point for student projects. Below are project-style suggestions that stay closely tied to the experiments and recommendations in the paper.

1) Measure the utility of arXiv content at larger scales. The authors observe that adding arXiv-only corpora did not improve mathematical reasoning for the model sizes they tried (1.3B and 7B). However, they explicitly leave open the question of whether arXiv tokens become beneficial at larger model sizes or in different mixes. A student project could: (a) replicate the arXiv-only and mixed-corpus experiments at modest scales (1.3B and 7B), (b) vary the arXiv:Web:Code ratios as in prior work, and (c) evaluate on GSM8K, MATH, miniF2F and Chinese math benchmarks to quantify the effects. The deliverable would be a set of controlled ablations and a short paper reporting whether and when arXiv helps.

2) Targeted data collection for geometry and theorem proofs. Given the observed geometry weakness, a concrete project is to design an augmentation pipeline that specifically recovers geometry-oriented web pages and formal-geometry corpora. This would follow the paper’s iterative data-selection philosophy: create geometry-specific seeds (textbook problems, olympiad geometry, diagram-laden pages), train a classifier, and measure how adding those pages affects geometry benchmarks and theorem-proving metrics. Evaluations should include both informal geometry problems and formal autoformalization tasks (miniF2F / Isabelle).

3) Compare GRPO, PPO, and critic-free alternatives at scale and measure resource/use trade-offs. The paper claims GRPO reduces memory usage by avoiding a separate value function while still producing strong improvements. A reproducible student project would re-implement GRPO, PPO with a critic, and a baseline RFT/DPO variant under matched compute budgets, and compare (a) final accuracy on GSM8K and MATH, (b) Maj@K/Pass@K behavior, and (c) runtime and peak GPU memory. This directly probes the core engineering-economic trade-offs the paper highlights.

4) Build and evaluate process reward models with uncertainty estimates. The paper shows process supervision improves outcomes. Students can build a process reward model (trained to score individual reasoning steps), add uncertainty estimates (e.g., via ensembles or simple probabilistic calibration), and run GRPO where update weights are modulated by reward-model uncertainty. The key questions to test are (a) does uncertainty-weighted RL stabilize training, and (b) does it reduce sensitivity to mislabeled or noisy rewards?

5) Advanced decoding and exploration strategies for RL. Task: implement and evaluate tree-search sampling (or other structured decoding) as the exploration mechanism in the RL loop, compare to nucleus sampling, and measure downstream reward-model calibration and policy improvement. The student should also instrument sampling diversity and measure how different samplers affect GRPO’s group-normalized advantage statistics.

6) Study the few-shot behavior as a function of training regimes. The paper reports limited few-shot gains at 7B. A project can systematically vary SFT curricula (e.g., include few-shot-style contexts and chain-of-thought few-shot examples during SFT), compare zero-shot vs few-shot evaluation, and report whether curriculum design can recover few-shot benefits without scaling model parameters. Use the same benchmarks and training recipes from the paper to keep experiments comparable.

For any of these projects, the paper provides useful “how-to” details: the data-selection pipeline, benchmark decontamination rule (10-gram filter), reward-normalization and advantage formulas used in GRPO, and the specific hyperparameters used for pretraining and RL. Following and documenting those choices is important for reproducibility and fair comparison.

Conclusion

The DeepSeekMath paper demonstrates that careful data curation, targeted pretraining, and appropriately designed RL can materially push the mathematical reasoning ability of an otherwise modest 7B model. At the same time, the paper candidly documents remaining limitations — geometry and theorem proving, few-shot responsiveness at small scale, and sensitivity to data-selection and reward-model issues — and it lays out concrete next steps. For researchers and students, the most actionable takeaways are to (a) treat data selection as an experimental variable to be measured and improved, (b) design reward and RL pipelines that account for noise and uncertainty (iterative loops, process supervision, uncertainty-aware updates), and (c) prioritize reproducible reporting of data and training recipes so that observed gains can be independently verified and responsibly built upon.
