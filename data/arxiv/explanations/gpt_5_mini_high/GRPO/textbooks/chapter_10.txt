\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 10: RL Outcomes: Performance Gains and Generalization

# In-domain improvements: GSM8K $82.9\%$ to $88.2\%$, MATH $46.8\%$ to $51.7\%$

One of the clearest empirical fruits of applying Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to DeepSeekMath-Instruct is the substantial in-domain improvement on the two canonical quantitative-reasoning benchmarks used for RL training. The reinforcement learning phase was conducted using chain-of-thought (CoT) formatted questions drawn from GSM8K and MATH (these were treated as the in-domain tasks). After applying GRPO-based RL, performance on GSM8K rose from $82.9\%$ to $88.2\%$, and performance on the competition-level MATH benchmark rose from $46.8\%$ to $51.7\%$.

These gains should be interpreted in the context of the training setup: GRPO collects a group of outputs per question (in our experiments $G=64$) from the current policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, scores them with a learned reward model, and computes advantages relative to the group. The objective (see the paper for the full form) encourages the model to increase probability mass on outputs that are relatively better inside each sampled group while regularizing the policy toward a reference model. Two short intuitions explain why in-domain gains are large. First, RL directly optimizes for outputs that the reward model judges better on the exact tasks used to collect the groups, so the learning signal is concentrated on the problems the model will be evaluated on. Second, the group-relative baseline reduces variance and aligns with how the reward model was trained (comparisons between outputs on the same question), giving more signal per update than a scalar final reward alone.

The absolute increases are larger on GSM8K than on MATH; this is expected because GSM8K contains more grade-school problems where small improvements in procedure or arithmetic robustness produce immediate accuracy gains, whereas MATH is a harder, competition-level set where remaining failure modes are deeper and more structural.

# Self-consistency: 64-sample $60.9\%$ on MATH

Self-consistency is a simple post-hoc ensembling strategy: sample many solution traces from the model and take the majority-voted final answer. With DeepSeekMath, applying self-consistency using $64$ samples yielded an accuracy of $60.9\%$ on MATH. This number is important for two reasons.

First, it shows how sampling + voting can extract substantially more performance than single-sample decoding. Second, it connects directly to the empirical effect of GRPO: the reinforcement learning phase increased the model’s tendency to place the correct answer into a dominant mode of the output distribution. The paper reports that RL improved Maj@K (majority-vote accuracy among $K$ samples) but did not necessarily improve Pass@K (the probability that at least one of $K$ samples is correct). In plain terms, after RL the model makes the correct answer appear more consistently across samples, so taking a majority vote over $64$ drawn solutions strongly amplifies the single-run improvements.

Mechanistically, GRPO's group-relative updates encourage outputs that score better than the group mean to be reinforced and those below the mean to be suppressed. Over training this concentrates mass on higher-quality solution modes, which is precisely what majority-vote self-consistency exploits. Therefore, the $60.9\%$ self-consistency figure is best viewed as the joint product of GRPO shaping the output distribution and the majority-vote decoding extracting the dominant correct mode.

# Out-of-domain improvements: CMATH $84.6\%$ to $88.8\%$

An important question about RL that optimizes on a narrow set of tasks is whether improvements generalize to other tasks not used during RL. In the DeepSeekMath experiments, RL was performed only on CoT-format GSM8K and MATH problems (about $144\text{k}$ questions), yet measurable gains were observed on out-of-domain benchmarks. A representative example is CMATH (a Chinese quantitative reasoning benchmark), where accuracy improved from $84.6\%$ before RL to $88.8\%$ after RL.

This cross-task improvement suggests that GRPO is not merely overfitting to idiosyncratic patterns of the in-domain data. Instead, the RL phase appears to enhance general capabilities that transfer: clearer step-by-step reasoning, cleaner intermediate computations, and a higher likelihood of producing a correct final answer across related math problem formats. The group-relative update rule helps because it emphasizes relative quality across outputs for the same prompt; by consistently reinforcing higher-quality reasoning patterns, the model learns strategies that are useful beyond the exact prompts used in exploration.

That said, the paper frames these out-of-domain gains conservatively: improvements are observable and significant on some benchmarks (like CMATH), but they are not claimed to uniformly fix every generalization shortcoming. The observed transfer demonstrates that RL can change the model’s distribution in ways that benefit related tasks.

# Tool-integrated reasoning performance after RL

DeepSeekMath-Instruct had already shown strong tool-integrated (program-aided) performance prior to RL — e.g., approaching about $60\%$ on MATH under tool-enabled evaluation. The GRPO-based RL phase was run using only CoT-format questions (no explicit tool-generation training data in the RL phase). Nevertheless, the paper reports that DeepSeekMath-RL outperformed the instruction-tuned model across all evaluated metrics, which includes settings where tool use was permitted in evaluation.

Two high-level explanations for this are available from the empirical setup. First, reinforcement learning improved the core reasoning and solution-staging capabilities of the model: even programs or tool-integrated solutions benefit when the underlying plan and intermediate steps are clearer and more consistent. Second, GRPO’s group-based reinforcement favors outputs that not only contain correct final answers, but also exhibit higher reward-model scores (which were built to prefer good solution traces). Because reward models were constructed to value process quality as well as final correctness (process supervision was one explored regime), RL can indirectly improve program-writing behaviors even without tool-specific RL data.

The paper does not provide a detailed per-benchmark table isolating the exact numeric gains for every tool-enabled metric post-RL, but it emphasizes that the RL model outperforms the SFT model across the suite of evaluations, including the tool-integrated regime.

# Comparison vs open-source (7B–70B) and closed-source baselines

One of the striking empirical claims is that DeepSeekMath-RL (a $7\text{B}$ model) attains performance competitive with — and in many cases superior to — much larger or closed-source models on mathematical reasoning benchmarks. Concretely, DeepSeekMath-RL reached $88.2\%$ on GSM8K and $51.7\%$ on MATH. According to the reported evaluations, these scores surpass all open-source models in the $7\text{B}$–$70\text{B}$ range and outperform the majority of closed-source baselines.

The progression of model performance across stages helps to see the effect magnitude. The base continual-trained model (DeepSeekMath-Base) already showed substantial strength (for reference, it achieved $64.2\%$ on GSM8K and $36.2\%$ on MATH prior to instruction tuning). Supervised instruction tuning raised these numbers to about $82.9\%$ (GSM8K) and $46.8\%$ (MATH). GRPO-based RL then lifted them to $88.2\%$ and $51.7\%$. This staged improvement illustrates that data curation, supervision format, and RL are complementary levers: careful pre-training and SFT move the model to a strong starting point, and GRPO refines the output distribution in ways that produce further measurable gains.

The paper also highlights the broader implication: with carefully engineered pre-training data and an efficient RL algorithm like GRPO, a parameter-efficient ($7\text{B}$) model can approach the performance of much larger closed models on rigorous benchmarks.

# Ablations on supervision type and iteration benefits

A significant part of the experimental investigation in the paper compares different supervision styles and training regimes. Three ablation findings are particularly relevant.

First, online sampling (where training data are samples produced by the current policy) outperforms offline sampling (where augmented data come from the fixed SFT model). In experiments, Online RFT (an online variant of rejection-fine-tuning) matched RFT early but outperformed it later in training. Intuitively, as the policy diverges from the SFT model, the online samples capture new failure modes and improvements, making online updates more informative.

Second, the type of supervision — outcome supervision (OS) versus process supervision (PS) — matters. Outcome supervision assigns a single normalized final reward to all tokens of an output: for output $o_i$ with reward $r_i$ the advantage at every token is $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})}$. Process supervision provides stepwise rewards and defines the token advantage as the sum of normalized step rewards from the current position to the end, i.e. $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{index(j)\ge t}\widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$, where $\widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$ is a normalized reward for the $j$-th reasoning step. The experiments show GRPO with process supervision (GRPO+PS) outperforms GRPO with outcome supervision (GRPO+OS). This indicates that finer-grained, step-aware feedback helps the model learn better intermediate reasoning behavior, not just better final answers.

Third, iterative RL — where the reward model is periodically retrained on new samples and a replay buffer is used (the paper uses $10\%$ historical data in replay) — yields additional gains. Empirically, performing one or more iterations of GRPO with reward-model refreshment produced significant improvement, especially after the first iteration. Iterative RL addresses the distribution shift between the policy and the reward model’s training distribution: by retraining the reward model on recent policy outputs, the supervision remains well-aligned with what the policy produces.

Finally, the paper contrasts GRPO with Online RFT and shows GRPO’s key advantage: GRPO converts scalar reward magnitudes into graded gradient coefficients, so it can amplify good outputs and penalize poor outputs proportionally. In contrast, Online RFT (binary filter) only reinforces correct outputs uniformly. This graded signal leads to more efficient learning and better final performance.

# Resource–performance trade-offs

GRPO was designed with practical resource considerations in mind. The central trade-offs observed and described in the experiments are:

- Memory and model-size savings: GRPO removes the need for a learned value function (critic) that PPO typically trains alongside the policy. Since that critic is usually another large model, eliminating it greatly reduces peak memory use and the number of model parameters that need to be updated during RL. The paper emphasizes that GRPO therefore "significantly reduces training resources" relative to PPO.

- Increased sampling and reward-evaluation cost: GRPO requires sampling a group of outputs per question to compute the group-relative baseline and advantages. In the reported RL runs the group size was $G=64$, and the training batch size was $1024$; for each sampled output a reward model must score either the final output (outcome supervision) or stepwise tokens (process supervision). This heavy sampling increases inference-time compute during exploration and increases the number of reward-model evaluations.

- Fewer policy updates per exploration stage: to control optimization stability, the policy model only received a single update after each exploration stage in the reported setting. This design reduces the number of gradient steps per set of sampled outputs and helps control compute, but it also means more exploration rounds may be needed for a given wall-clock time.

- Reward-model training overhead: iterative GRPO retrains the reward model periodically using new sampled data plus a replay buffer (the paper used $10\%$ historical data). This adds extra training work for the reward model, but it keeps supervision aligned with the policy and was shown to yield further performance gains.

In sum, GRPO trades off additional sampling and reward-model compute for substantial memory savings and a simpler RL setup (no critic). Practically, this made RL feasible on a $7\text{B}$ policy model and produced strong empirical gains without the large infrastructure that PPO with a critic would require.

# Takeaways

The empirical story in the paper is consistent and instructive. GRPO-based RL applied to DeepSeekMath-Instruct results in large in-domain gains ($\text{GSM8K}: 82.9\%\rightarrow 88.2\%$, $\text{MATH}: 46.8\%\rightarrow 51.7\%$), meaningful out-of-domain transfer (e.g., $\text{CMATH}: 84.6\%\rightarrow 88.8\%$), and substantial improvements when used with majority-vote decoding (self-consistency with $64$ samples gives $60.9\%$ on MATH). The ablations show that (1) online sampling beats offline sampling as the policy evolves, (2) process supervision yields better learning signals than outcome-only supervision, and (3) iterative reward-model retraining further improves performance. From a resources perspective, GRPO achieves these gains while removing the heavyweight critic used by PPO, at the cost of heavier sampling and reward evaluations during exploration. Finally, analysis of Maj@K vs Pass@K indicates that RL tends to make correct answers more dominant in the model’s output distribution (improving robustness of top-$K$ outputs) rather than simply increasing the chance that a correct answer appears somewhere among many samples. Together, these results demonstrate that carefully designed, resource-conscious RL (GRPO) can meaningfully push the performance and generalization of a compact, instruction-tuned math-specialized model.
