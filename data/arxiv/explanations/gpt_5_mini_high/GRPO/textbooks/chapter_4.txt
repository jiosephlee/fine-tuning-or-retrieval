\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 4: Validating Corpus Quality and Scale

# Controlled pretraining setup on DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B
To compare the different math-focused corpora fairly, the authors ran a controlled pretraining study in which every variable except the corpus itself was held constant. The model family chosen for these comparisons was DeepSeek-LLM with roughly $1.3$ billion parameters (denoted DeepSeek-LLM $1.3\text{B}$). For each corpus under study—DeepSeekMath, MathPile, OpenWebMath, and Proof-Pile-2—a separate DeepSeek-LLM $1.3\text{B}$ instance was trained from the same initialization and under the same training recipe. Each experiment trained the model for the same number of tokens ($150\text{B}$ tokens), which means smaller corpora were repeated multiple times while larger corpora supplied more unique material. This design isolates corpus content as the primary cause of performance differences: any observed gap can be attributed to what the corpora contain and how diverse those contents are, not to differences in model size, optimizer, or training length.

All comparisons used the HAI-LLM training framework, and the evaluations focused on downstream mathematical benchmarks using the same prompting style (few-shot chain-of-thought, discussed below). By matching architecture, total training tokens, and evaluation protocol, the setup gives a clean, empirical test of “corpus quality per token” rather than conflating corpus size with other confounding variables. This controlled setup is important because many public math corpora differ simultaneously in both size and composition; fixing the training recipe lets us examine whether one corpus provides more learning signal per token than another.

# Optimizer and learning-rate schedule used in comparisons
The pretraining comparisons used a consistent optimization configuration to ensure fairness across corpora. The optimizer was AdamW with hyperparameters $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.95$, and $\text{weight\_decay} = 0.1$. The learning-rate schedule was a multi-step schedule with warmup and staged decay. Concretely, the learning rate warmed up to its peak over the first $2000$ steps, then remained or decayed according to the multi-step schedule such that it reached $31.6\%$ of the peak after $80\%$ of the training steps and further decreased to $10.0\%$ of the peak after $90\%$ of the training steps. The peak learning rate used in these controlled runs was $5.3\times 10^{-4}$. Training used a large token batch size of $4\text{M}$ tokens and a context length of $4\text{K}$ tokens.

Keeping the optimization and schedule unchanged across the corpus comparisons is critical: warmup and staged decay are standard mechanisms to stabilize early training and to reduce effective step size as the model converges. By fixing these details, the experiments attribute differences in downstream performance to differences in corpus content and scale rather than to different optimization dynamics.

# Results across eight math benchmarks with few-shot CoT
Evaluation in this study used few-shot chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting across a suite of eight mathematical benchmarks. Few-shot CoT means the evaluator supplies a small set of input–solution exemplars that include step-by-step reasoning, then asks the model to produce a solution with internal reasoning steps for each held-out problem. This evaluation style emphasizes the model’s ability to produce multi-step, explicit reasoning chains, which is exactly the behavior we want to probe for mathematical competence.

Across the eight benchmarks, the model trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus consistently led in performance. The paper reports a “clear performance lead” for the DeepSeekMath-trained DeepSeek-LLM $1.3\text{B}$ over counterparts trained on MathPile, OpenWebMath, and Proof-Pile-2 when evaluated with few-shot CoT prompts. In other words, when everything else is held fixed, pretraining on the DeepSeekMath corpus supplies more usable reasoning signal per token for these mathematical tasks. This result shows that the composition and selection of mathematical web content in DeepSeekMath produce downstream gains that are detectable on practical benchmark suites.

# Learning curves: higher quality at fewer tokens vs Proof-Pile-2
Beyond final scores, the authors examined learning curves (performance as a function of tokens seen during pretraining). One striking observation is that the DeepSeekMath-trained model outperformed the model trained on Proof-Pile-2 already at $50\text{B}$ tokens of training. The paper emphasizes that $50\text{B}$ tokens correspond to roughly one full epoch of Proof-Pile-2 under the experimental setup, so this comparison is a natural way to judge per-token quality: at the same number of tokens seen, the DeepSeekMath model had learned more.

This pattern—better performance after fewer tokens—is a direct indicator of higher average information content or higher “signal per token” in DeepSeekMath. Smaller corpora like Proof-Pile-2 must be repeated many times to reach $150\text{B}$ tokens, and repeated exposure to the same material yields diminishing returns; the learning curve tends to plateau. By contrast, a larger, more diverse corpus that better matches the distribution of downstream math tasks can yield steeper early learning and more sustained gains because each additional token is more likely to expose the model to novel syntactic forms, problem styles, or reasoning patterns relevant to the benchmarks.

# Effect of multilingual data on Chinese benchmarks
A notable property of the DeepSeekMath Corpus is that it is multilingual, with English and Chinese being the two most represented languages. This multilingual composition has measurable benefits: models trained on DeepSeekMath show improved mathematical reasoning on both English and Chinese benchmarks. The paper points out that existing public math corpora are mostly English-centric, and when models are trained on those corpora they show limited gains—or even regressions—on Chinese mathematical benchmarks.

Intuitively, including non-English math pages gives the model exposure to Chinese mathematical vocabulary, notation conventions, problem styles, and idioms. Those exposures are important when the downstream tasks themselves are in Chinese (for example, CMATH and Gaokao-derived tasks), because a model that has never seen many Chinese math pages has to generalize across language-specific surface forms as well as mathematical concepts. By contrast, an English-heavy corpus cannot supply that language-specific signal. The controlled experiments therefore show that multilingual pretraining materials are not merely “nice to have”; for cross-lingual math competence they are a practical necessity.

# Scale advantages: steeper, more sustained gains at 120B tokens
Size matters, but not in a vacuum: the DeepSeekMath Corpus provides scale together with targeted quality. The corpus in the paper is reported as approximately $120\text{B}$ math tokens drawn from filtered Common Crawl pages; this is several times larger than competing math corpora cited in the study, for example MathPile ($8.9\text{B}$ tokens) and OpenWebMath ($13.6\text{B}$ tokens). Proof-Pile-2 is itself a mixture (it includes OpenWebMath, AlgebraicStack material on the order of $10.3\text{B}$ tokens, and an arXiv component around $28.0\text{B}$ tokens), but it remains substantially smaller in unique content than DeepSeekMath.

When the authors trained DeepSeek-LLM $1.3\text{B}$ on DeepSeekMath, the resulting learning curve was both steeper early on and more persistent later in training: performance continued to improve across a wider token range instead of plateauing. The reason is practical: very large, high-quality corpora reduce the need to replay the same examples many times; each training pass introduces more unique mathematical contexts, representations, and problems. Repetition of a small corpus accelerates early learning on its limited content but quickly saturates. The DeepSeekMath experiments therefore illustrate a double advantage of scale plus careful selection: you get both faster per-token learning and longer-lasting improvements as training progresses.

# Takeaways: quality, multilingual benefits, size of corpus
The controlled pretraining experiments yield three compact lessons that are useful for any researcher thinking about math pretraining:

1. Corpus quality per token matters. When two training runs see the same number of tokens, the run exposed to better-selected mathematical web pages learns more. DeepSeekMath’s filtered Common Crawl pages provided more usable signal per token than the smaller, often arXiv-dominated alternatives in this study.

2. Multilingual content extends capability beyond English. DeepSeekMath’s inclusion of Chinese materials led to meaningful gains on Chinese math benchmarks; English-centric corpora cannot substitute for language-specific mathematical data when you care about non-English performance.

3. Scale combined with selection yields sustained learning. A very large, well-curated corpus (here on the order of $120\text{B}$ tokens) lets the model continue to improve across many more tokens without hitting the plateau caused by repeatedly replaying a small dataset.

These findings reinforce the practical point that careful corpus engineering—selection, deduplication, multilingual coverage, and sufficient unique scale—can move the needle on mathematical reasoning more than merely increasing parameter counts or training time on a poor-quality dataset. In short: for math reasoning, invest in better data as well as more data.
