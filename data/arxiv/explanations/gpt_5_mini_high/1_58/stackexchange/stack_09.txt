\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### 71.4x arithmetic energy savings claim: what op-energy constants, accumulator width, and memory costs were assumed?
Question:
You report that on 7 nm, BitNet b1.58 saves 71.4x arithmetic energy for matrix multiplications versus FP16. Can you list the per-op energy numbers used (e.g., FP16 multiply, FP16 add, int8 add, int32 accumulate), the assumed accumulator precision, and the operation mix per MAC? Was memory energy (reads/writes from SRAM/DRAM) included, or is 71.4x compute-only? A worked example for a representative GEMM shape would be very helpful.
Answer:
Short answer first: the  paper’s quoted “$71.4\times$” is an arithmetic-only (compute-only) estimate for matrix–multiply energy on a $7\ \text{nm}$ process using the energy model cited in the paper (they cite the energy model papers but do not publish a private table of per‑op constants or the exact accumulator width or the exact operation mix). The paper states they “estimate the arithmetic operations energy consumption of matrix multiplication” and then report the $71.4\times$ saving; it therefore does not include DRAM/SRAM transfer energy in that $71.4\times$ number. The paper also does not print the per‑operation numbers (e.g., exact pJ values) nor an explicit MAC‑level op mix breakdown, so you cannot find those constants inside the PDF — instead they point to the external energy models they used.

Because you asked for a reproducible explanation, below I (a) write the minimal, transparent algebra one should use to reproduce that kind of ratio, (b) give the typical operation mix that the paper implicitly assumes for FP16 vs. BitNet b1.58, (c) state the accumulator precision the design normally needs, and (d) give a worked numeric example (labelled “illustrative”) that shows how a $71.4\times$ number can arise when you plug in plausible per‑op energies from the literature cited by the paper.

What is being compared (operation mix)
For a dense MAC in a conventional FP16 matrix multiply the inner‑loop per multiply‑accumulate is one floating multiply plus one floating add (in hardware often a fused FMA). I will denote the per‑op energies $E^{FP16}_{mul}$, $E^{FP16}_{add}$. A single FP16 MAC energy (compute part) is then $E^{FP16}_{MAC} = E^{FP16}_{mul} + E^{FP16}_{add}$.
For BitNet b1.58 (ternary weights $\{-1,0,1\}$ with 8‑bit activations) a single inner product element is implemented as “if $w=0$: skip; if $w=+1$: add $a$; if $w=-1$: subtract $a$.” That means the hot inner operation is an integer add/sub and the accumulation must be performed into a wider integer accumulator. The per‑element cost (for a nonzero weight) is therefore dominated by an integer add/sub on the activation type plus the cost of the accumulator update. I will denote these energies $E^{INT8}_{add}$ (cost to add an 8‑bit value into the accumulator path) and $E^{INTacc}_{add}$ (cost of the accumulator update at its working width). If the implementation uses bit‑packing + bitwise/popcount primitives to compute many dot‑products in parallel (typical for binary/ternary kernels), the effective per‑element energy can be even lower — the paper’s references and the Ladder 2‑bit kernel they use point in that direction.

Accumulator width and why int32 is typical
The paper specifies 8‑bit activations and ternary weights. If activations are in the signed 8‑bit range (roughly $\pm 127$) and the dot length (inner dimension) is $K$, the maximum absolute sum is bounded by $K\cdot 127$, so the number of bits required is $\lceil \log_2(127K + 1)\rceil$. For typical Transformer inner‑dims ($K$ in the thousands) this requires $\sim 18$–$22$ bits, so designers ordinarily use a 32‑bit integer accumulator (int32) for headroom and to avoid overflow. The paper does not state a different accumulator width, so int32 is the reasonable assumption and the usual one used in energy comparisons.

How to compute the compute‑only energy for one output dot (transparent algebra)
If $K$ is the dot length and $d$ is the fraction of weights that are nonzero ($d\le 1$; ternary has some zeros which reduce work), the compute energy per output element is

$$
E_{out}^{FP16} = K \cdot E^{FP16}_{MAC} = K\cdot\left(E^{FP16}_{mul} + E^{FP16}_{add}\right),
$$

$$
E_{out}^{BitNet} = K \cdot d \cdot\left(E^{INT8}_{add} + E^{INTacc}_{add}\right) + E_{scale},
$$

where $E_{scale}$ is the small cost of applying the per‑row/column scale (the paper’s quantization uses a scalar $\gamma$ per weight block — that scalar multiply can be hoisted out of the inner loop and is negligible relative to $K$ MACs). The arithmetic‑only speedup/savings ratio is

$$
\text{Savings} = \frac{E_{out}^{FP16}}{E_{out}^{BitNet}} \approx \frac{K\cdot E^{FP16}_{MAC}}{K\cdot d\cdot E^{Bit}_{MAC}} = \frac{E^{FP16}_{MAC}}{d\cdot E^{Bit}_{MAC}},
$$

where I used $E^{Bit}_{MAC} := \left(E^{INT8}_{add} + E^{INTacc}_{add}\right)$. (If you use a bit‑packed popcount implementation, replace $E^{Bit}_{MAC}$ by the amortized per‑element cost of the bitwise/popcount sequence; that is the key lever that can drive the large factors.)

Was memory energy included?
No — the $71.4\times$ number in the paper is explicitly an arithmetic (compute) energy comparison for matrix multiplication. The paper separately discusses memory/bandwidth savings (smaller weights / lower bandwidth) but the $71.4\times$ figure is the compute‑only arithmetic estimate, not an end‑to‑end (DRAM+SRAM+compute) energy number. If you want an end‑to‑end energy ratio you must add the memory transfer energies (weight reads, activation reads/writes, writebacks), and the final savings will generally be smaller than the compute‑only figure (but BitNet also reduces memory energy thanks to much smaller weight size and lower bandwidth).

An illustrative worked example (reconstruction)
The paper does not print the atomic pJ numbers it used, so the following is a reproducible reconstruction (explicitly flagged as illustrative) using plausible per‑op energies taken in the spirit of the cited energy models. Plugging different per‑op constants (from the cited refs or measured silicon) will change the result; this shows how the arithmetic $71.4\times$ can be produced.

Assumptions (illustrative, not verbatim from the paper):
$E^{FP16}_{mul} = 1.20\ \text{pJ}$, $E^{FP16}_{add} = 0.30\ \text{pJ}$ so $E^{FP16}_{MAC} = 1.50\ \text{pJ}$.
$E^{INT8}_{add} = 0.007\ \text{pJ}$, $E^{INT32}_{add} = 0.014\ \text{pJ}$ so $E^{Bit}_{MAC} = 0.021\ \text{pJ}$.
Take $d = 1$ (no zeros) for a worst‑case per‑element cost; as soon as $d<1$ the BitNet cost drops proportionally.
Pick $K = 4096$ (a realistic Transformer inner‑dim for a large FFN).

Compute per‑output energy:
$$
E_{out}^{FP16} = K\cdot 1.50\ \text{pJ} = 4096 \times 1.50\ \text{pJ} = 6144\ \text{pJ} = 6.144\ \text{nJ}.
$$

$$
E_{out}^{BitNet} = K\cdot 1 \cdot 0.021\ \text{pJ} = 4096 \times 0.021\ \text{pJ} = 86.016\ \text{pJ} = 0.086016\ \text{nJ}.
$$

$$
\text{Ratio} = \frac{6.144\ \text{nJ}}{0.086016\ \text{nJ}} \approx 71.4.
$$

So with these per‑op constants and $K=4096$ the arithmetic energy ratio is $71.4$. If you use a nonzero density $d<1$ (i.e., some weights are $0$), replace $E_{out}^{BitNet}$ by $d\cdot(\text{the value above})$ and the ratio grows by $1/d$. If the BitNet kernel is using bit‑packing+popcount primitives the amortized $E^{Bit}_{MAC}$ can be lower than the simple INT8+INT32 add numbers I used here, and that also increases the ratio.

What to do to reproduce the exact paper number
Because the authors rely on the energy models in the cited refs but do not publish a table of the constants they actually plugged in, the only way to get *their exact* constants is to (a) examine the cited energy‑model papers [energycost, pokebnn] and use the same process‑scaling and the same assumptions (FMA counted as one or two ops, whether they amortized a scale multiply outside the inner loop, whether they assumed bit‑packed popcount implementations), or (b) ask the authors for the exact table. The algebra above shows exactly which quantities you need:

$E^{FP16}_{mul}$, $E^{FP16}_{add}$, $E^{INT8}_{add}$, $E^{INTacc}_{add}$, $K$ (inner dim), $d$ (nonzero weight fraction), and whether the BitNet primitive is implemented as scalar INT8 adds or as amortized bit‑packed popcount operations.
