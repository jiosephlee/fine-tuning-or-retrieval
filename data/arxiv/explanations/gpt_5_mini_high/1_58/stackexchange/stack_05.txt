\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Per-token activation quantization to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$: what exactly is "per token", and how are scales computed and applied?
Question:
The paper states that activations are 8-bit, scaled to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ per token, and the zero-point is removed. Does "per token" mean per batch element and sequence position independently, over the hidden dimension of each layer? Is the scale $s$ computed as $s = Q_b/\max(|x|)$ or via an abs-mean/percentile? Is $Q_b=127$ (symmetric int8)? Are scales recomputed at every layer and time step in decoding, and what is the runtime overhead of this dynamic scaling?
Answer:
Short answer: “Per token” means one scale per token vector (i.e., per batch element and sequence position) computed across the hidden dimension at each layer. The paper does not write an explicit arithmetic formula for the activation scale, but it says the activation quantizer “follows the same implementation in BitNet” and that weights used an abs‑mean scale, so the intended/most consistent interpretation is an abs‑mean (or equivalent single‑scale) per token mapped to a symmetric signed 8‑bit range (so $Q_b$ is the symmetric 8‑bit peak, typically $127$). Scales are produced when a token’s activations are computed (so per layer when that token passes through the layer); the runtime cost is just a per‑token reduction plus elementwise rescale/round operations and is small compared to the cost of the layer matrix multiplies (the paper reports this as “negligible”).

More detail and a concrete formula consistent with the paper. Let $x\in\mathbb{R}^d$ be the hidden vector for one token (a particular batch element and sequence position) at a given layer. The paper’s weight quantizer computes an average absolute scale $\gamma$ for a weight matrix and does RoundClip against $\{-1,0,1\}$. Translating that style to activations and an 8‑bit symmetric target gives a natural/consistent recipe:

- compute an abs‑mean for the token: $\gamma_x=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^d |x_i|$ (plus a tiny $\epsilon$ for stability),
- take a scale per token $s=\frac{\gamma_x+\epsilon}{Q_b}$ (equivalently you can view $Q_b/(\gamma_x+\epsilon)$ as the multiplier used before rounding),
- quantize to signed 8‑bit integers by
  $$
  q_i=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{x_i}{s}\right),-Q_b,Q_b\right),
  $$
  and dequantize as $\hat{x}_i = s\cdot q_i$.

This matches the description “scaled to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ per token” and mirrors the paper’s use of an absmean scale for weights. The paper itself does not explicitly say “we used max(|x|) or percentile” for activations; because the weight quantizer used an abs‑mean, the conservative reading is that activations use the same abs‑mean style. (Using max or a percentile is an alternative design choice in other works, but it would be a deviation from “same implementation in BitNet” unless the authors explicitly state it.)

On $Q_b$: since activations are 8‑bit and the paper emphasizes removing the zero‑point by using a symmetric interval $[-Q_b,Q_b]$, $Q_b$ is the symmetric 8‑bit maximum (standard choice is $Q_b=127$). Using $127$ keeps the mapping symmetric around zero and avoids an asymmetric zero‑point.

On when scales are computed and runtime cost: the scale for a given token and layer must be computed when that token’s activations are produced for that layer. In autoregressive decoding that means you compute the scale once when you propagate the new token through the layer. If you quantize and store K/V caches, you then store the quantized bytes plus the single scalar scale for that token+layer so you do not need to recompute the scale for cached keys/values later. The extra work to produce the scale is one reduction across the $d$ hidden dimensions (to get $\gamma_x$), one reciprocal/multiply to form $1/s$ (or one division), $d$ elementwise multiplies and rounds to produce the integer vector $q$, and storing one float scale per token per layer. Relative to the cost of the layer GEMMs (large matrix multiplies), this is typically tiny; the paper characterizes the approach as “more convenient and simple for both implementation and system‑level optimization, while introduces negligible effects to the performance in our experiments.”

If you implement this, a practical checklist is: compute and store one float scale per (batch, position, layer) together with the quantized 8‑bit vector; on attention you can either use integer kernels that fold in scales or dequantize on the fly using the stored scale. The paper doesn’t publish microsecond overhead numbers for the scale computation, only the qualitative “negligible” statement, but the asymptotic cost and the storage/memory tradeoff are as described above.
