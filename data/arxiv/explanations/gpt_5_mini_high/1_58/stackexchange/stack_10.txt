\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### KV cache precision and "lossless compression to 4 bits": is the cache currently 8-bit, and what does "lossless" mean?
Question:
The paper claims 8-bit activations reduce KV cache size and that it can be "losslessly compressed" to 4 bits or lower. Are $K$ and $V$ currently stored as int8 during decoding? By "lossless to 4 bits", do you mean entropy coding that preserves exact int8 values, or an additional quantization that shows negligible accuracy loss (i.e., not truly lossless)? If entropy coding is intended, what compression ratios and runtime overheads do you observe?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper states that BitNet b1.58 uses 8‑bit activations, so the logical K and V activations in the KV cache are quantized to 8 bits in their experiments; the phrase “losslessly compressed to 4 bits (or lower)” is aspirational/future work in the paper and is ambiguous — it could mean true bit‑level lossless compression (entropy coding of the int8 values down to an average of 4 bits/value) or it could be shorthand for “we can further reduce the stored representation to 4 bits in practice without hurting model quality” (which would be a lossy quantization that’s empirically benign). The paper does not present implementation details, compression numbers, or runtime measurements for the 4‑bit claim.

Why I say that: the paper explicitly says the model is trained and run with 8‑bit activations (so K/V are 8‑bit activations), and then adds in the discussion that those 8‑bit activations “can be further losslessly compressed to 4 bits or even lower … which we leave as future work.” Because the compression statement is in the “future work” part, no experimental evidence or method is given.

What “lossless” would mean in the two senses and the implications
There are two different technical readings of “losslessly compressed to 4 bits” and they have very different requirements.

1) True lossless (bit‑exact recovery): this means you store the current 8‑bit quantized K/V values in a compressed form and can recover exactly the original 8‑bit values when needed. Information‑theoretically this is only possible on average if the empirical entropy of the 8‑bit symbols is $\le 4$ bits. If X is the discrete distribution of the quantized activations, the Shannon lower bound is
$$H(X) = -\sum_x p(x)\log_2 p(x),$$
and any lossless coder must use on average at least $H(X)$ bits per symbol. So to get an average of 4 bits/value you need $H(X) \le 4$. Practically this can be achieved by techniques like ANS/arithmetic coding, Huffman coding, delta or run‑length schemes, or packing if the alphabet is small (e.g. if only 16 distinct symbols appear then a fixed 4‑bit packing is exact). The cost of true lossless coding is that general entropy decompressors (ANS, range coder) add compute and branching overhead; a simpler route is block‑wise nibble (4‑bit) packing if the values truly live in a 16‑level alphabet, which is cheap to unpack with vectorized bit ops.

2) “Lossless” as “no observable accuracy loss”: authors sometimes mean “we can quantize further to 4 bits without degrading perplexity/accuracy.” That is not lossless in a bit‑exact sense — it is lossy quantization that happens to preserve downstream metrics. This is typically done by designing a 4‑bit quantization scheme (per‑block/per‑head scaling, bias correction, fine‑tuning/GPTQ style methods) and verifying task performance. The runtime overhead is lower because the representation is native 4‑bit (you can implement kernels that read packed 4‑bit values directly) rather than decompressing an entropy stream.

Practical tradeoffs and what to measure (why the paper is silent)
Because the paper does not give measurements for 4‑bit KV caching, you need to evaluate two things if you want to follow up: (a) compressibility of the existing 8‑bit K/V values and (b) runtime/latency cost to decode/use them during autoregressive decoding. To decide which approach is realistic you should measure the empirical distribution of the quantized K/Vs (compute a histogram and entropy $H(X)$). If $H(X)$ is substantially below 8 bits and near or below 4 bits you can consider lossless entropy coding. If not, evaluate a 4‑bit lossy quantization and measure model quality.

A few implementation notes that affect performance and overheads: if you scale activations per token or per small block (the paper scales per token), you must also store the scale(s) alongside compressed data; those scale bits are overhead amortized over the block size (if a scale uses $s$ bits and you store it every $B$ values then add $s/B$ bits/value to the budget). Bit‑packing to 4 bits (two values per byte) is cheap to implement on GPU/CPU and has very low unpack cost; entropy coding (ANS / Huffman) can achieve lower average bits but requires a heavier decompression step which can add latency unless carefully engineered (blockwise ANS plus parallel decoding can help). Also consider access pattern: autoregressive decoding repeatedly reads small slices of the KV cache (per‑head, per‑token), so decompression cost must be paid for the slice sizes you actually need — decompressing the whole cache per token would kill latency savings.

How to experimentally validate (quick recipe)
Collect a representative dump of quantized K and V tensors from decoding, compute the empirical pmf and $H(X)$. Try three simple experiments: (i) naïve 4‑bit fixed packing (if the alphabet is $\le 16$ or you apply a simple per‑block remapping) and measure accuracy and unpack speed; (ii) ANS/Huffman on blocks of reasonable size (say thousands of elements) and measure compressed size and decode throughput; (iii) a lossy 4‑bit quantization (per‑head or per‑token scale) and measure downstream perplexity/accuracy and decoding throughput with custom kernels. Report average bits/value, end‑to‑end token latency, and extra memory for scales/headers.

Conclusion
In short: the KV cache in the paper is produced from 8‑bit activations (so K/V are 8‑bit in their experiments). The “losslessly compressed to 4 bits” line is a forward‑looking claim without details — it could mean true entropy coding (exact recovery) or further 4‑bit quantization that doesn’t hurt accuracy, and the paper doesn’t report compression ratios or runtime numbers. To resolve this concretely you need to (1) compute the empirical entropy of your K/V tensors and (2) benchmark both fast nibble‑packing and entropy decoders (or direct 4‑bit quant kernels) to trade off memory saving vs. added latency; the Shannon bound $H(X)$ is the right quantitative test for whether a *true* lossless 4‑bit average is even possible.
