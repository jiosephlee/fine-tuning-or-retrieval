\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How does the Ladder 2-bit kernel implement ternary-weight matmuls with int8 activations—adds/subs only, or bit-serial tricks?
Question:
With weights in $\{-1,0,1\}$ and int8 activations, a straightforward approach would use add, subtract, or skip per weight. On GPUs, that could be branchy or bandwidth-bound. How does the Ladder 2-bit kernel realize this efficiently in practice? Do you encode ternary values as two binary masks (nonzero and sign) and fuse them with vectorized additions, or use popcount/XNOR-like techniques adapted from binary networks?
Answer:
Short answer: the BitNet paper itself does not spell out the inner loop of the Ladder 2‑bit kernel, but on modern GPUs the practical, high‑throughput implementations avoid per‑weight branching and fall into one of two families: (A) a packed‑weight + decode/fused GEMM path (i.e., store two 1‑bit masks and expand or fuse them into int8 tiles and run an INT8 GEMM / tensor‑core kernel), or (B) a bit‑serial/popcount path that bit‑packs activations and uses bitwise AND/popcount against weight sign/nonzero masks and accumulates across activation bitplanes. Both achieve “adds/subs only” at the mathematical level (because $w \in \{-1,0,1\}$ means $y = \sum_j w_j x_j = \sum_j 1_{w_j=1} x_j - \sum_j 1_{w_j=-1} x_j$), but they trade off memory traffic, number of passes, and which hardware primitives they lean on.

To make that concrete: write the ternary weight matrix $W$ as two bitmasks
$P = 1_{W=+1}$, $N = 1_{W=-1}$, so
$W = P - N$,
and a matmul $A W^T$ becomes $A P^T - A N^T$. One straightforward, branchless implementation is to keep $P$ and $N$ packed (2 bits per weight or two bitmaps), either issue two masked INT8 GEMMs (compute $A\cdot P^T$ and $A\cdot N^T$) or decode small tiles of $P/N$ into signed int8 $(-1,0,1)$ in shared memory and do a single fused INT8 GEMM that subtracts the negative contributions on the fly. This approach is friendly to NVIDIA’s tensor cores and the highly optimized INT8 GEMM paths in FasterTransformer: it minimizes the number of kernel launches and memory reads for activations (one pass over $A$), avoids branches, and reuses high‑throughput INT8 matrix multiply hardware.

The other canonical approach borrows from binary/bit‑serial techniques: decompose each int8 activation into bitplanes $a = \sum_{b=0}^{B-1} 2^b a^{(b)}$ (and handle the sign bit appropriately), pack many $a^{(b)}$ bits into machine words, then for each bitplane compute $\mathrm{popcount}(P \& A^{(b)})$ and $\mathrm{popcount}(N \& A^{(b)})$. The dot product is
$$\mathrm{dot}(W,a) = \sum_{b} 2^b\big(\mathrm{popcnt}(P \& A^{(b)}) - \mathrm{popcnt}(N \& A^{(b)})\big).$$
This uses very compact storage for weights (2 bits/wt) and very cheap bitwise+popcount ops, but it requires one pass per activation bit (so $B$ passes for $B$‑bit activations). On hardware where bitwise/popcount throughput is extremely high or where tensor cores are absent, bit‑serial can win; on modern GPUs with extremely fast INT8 GEMMs, the $B$‑pass overhead often dominates and makes decode+GEMM faster.

Which one does Ladder actually use? the BitNet paper only says “the 2‑bit kernel from Ladder is also integrated” and measures latency with FasterTransformer; it does not give the kernel source in the text. Given the integration target (FasterTransformer, NVIDIA GPUs) and the large measured speedups, the likely practical choice for Ladder’s GPU kernel is a packed 2‑bit representation with an on‑tile decode or a fused two‑mask computation that feeds high‑throughput INT8 GEMM/tensor‑core code (i.e., the “decode + fused GEMM” style). That design gives a single high‑throughput pass over activations, avoids branchy per‑weight add/sub logic, and maps well to the hardware primitives FasterTransformer exploits. Bit‑serial/popcount variants remain an attractive alternative on some hardware (or for very sparse $W$), but they typically need multiple bit‑plane passes for int8 activations and so are less common for GPU inference kernels tuned for tensor cores.

If you need definitive confirmation for the exact Ladder kernel used here, the quickest way is to inspect the Ladder kernel source or the FasterTransformer integration (the paper points to those codebases). If you want to prototype yourself, implement both: (1) a decode‑into‑int8 + INT8 GEMM fused kernel, and (2) a bit‑serial popcount kernel, and measure on your target GPU — that will show which dominates for your activation bitwidth, density of zeros in $W$, and batch/shape regime.
