\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Can you formalize the claimed "new scaling law"—a function relating quality and cost for 1.58-bit vs FP16?
Question:
Beyond the qualitative equivalences (e.g., 13B 1.58-bit is more efficient than 3B FP16), can you provide fitted relationships such as $\text{PPL}(N,T,b)$ vs parameter count $N$, training tokens $T$, and bit-width $b$, and a cost model $C(N,b)$ for latency/memory/energy? This would enable plotting iso-quality iso-cost curves to plan model sizing. How robust are these fits across datasets and modest architecture variations?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not publish a closed-form $\mathrm{PPL}(N,T,b)$ or a single $C(N,b)$ function, but it supplies enough empirical datapoints (perplexities matching at 3B+, measured latency/memory/energy ratios, and three cross-bit “equivalences”) to build a simple, practical pair of models you can fit and use to draw iso-quality / iso-cost curves. Below I give (1) a compact, literature-consistent functional form for quality that includes a bit-width factor, (2) simple latency / memory / energy cost formulas, (3) a minimal calibration using the numbers reported in the paper, and (4) a recipe to produce iso-quality / iso-cost curves and the caveats about robustness.

1) A compact quality (loss / perplexity) model
Use cross-entropy loss $L = \ln(\mathrm{PPL})$ because scaling laws are usually power-laws in loss. A convenient joint form (Kaplan-style) with a bit-width capacity factor $s(b)$ is

$$
L(N,T,b) \;=\; L_\infty \;+\; A\big(N\,s(b)\big)^{-\alpha} \;+\; B\,T^{-\beta},
$$

and PPL is recovered by $\mathrm{PPL}=\exp(L)$. Intuition: the model-size term decays as a power law in an effective parameter count $N_{\mathrm{eff}}=N\,s(b)$, and training-token effects are a separate power law in $T$. The bit-width enters through $s(b)$: $s(b)<1$ would mean reduced effective capacity per parameter; $s(b)>1$ would mean richer effective capacity (the paper’s main empirical claim is that for $b=1.58$ and $N\gtrsim 3\,$B, $s(1.58)\approx 1$ — i.e., no measurable loss vs FP16).

How to fit this from data: collect several $(N,T)$ points and fit $L_\infty,A,\alpha,B,\beta$ by least-squares on $L$ (or on $\log$–transformed data for the power terms). With those fitted you can invert the model to get the $N$ needed to reach a target loss at a given $T$ and $b$:

$$
N(b) \;=\; \frac{1}{s(b)}\left(\frac{A}{L_{\mathrm{target}}-L_\infty - B T^{-\beta}}\right)^{1/\alpha}.
$$

If you only have the paper’s claims (they trained at $T\!=\!100\,$B and show matching PPL for FP16 vs $b=1.58$ at $N\ge 3\,$B), treat $s(1.58)\approx 1$ as your starting prior; fit $s(b)$ only if you evaluate a few bit-widths.

2) Simple cost (memory / latency / energy) models
Memory: the paper shows that weights and activations both matter (activations/KV cache are non-negligible). A simple per-parameter linear model in bits is

$$
M(N,b) \approx \frac{N\big(b + a\cdot \mathrm{act\_bits}(b)\big)}{8} + M_0 \quad \text{(bytes)},
$$

where $\mathrm{act\_bits}(b)$ is activation bit-width used (paper: 16 for FP16 baseline, 8 for BitNet $b=1.58$), $a$ is the (unitless) ratio that converts activation bits-per-param into the same per-parameter basis, and $M_0$ covers embedding/fixed overhead. This form is intuitive: total memory $\approx$ weight bits + activation/KV bits (both scale with model size for large models).

Latency: a minimal, empirically useful model is

$$
t(N,b) = t_{\mathrm{fixed}} + N\cdot c(b),
$$

i.e., per-token latency = fixed overhead + a per-parameter contribution. Equivalently, normalize to FP16 per-billions-of-parameters units by writing $N$ in billions and

$$
t_{\text{FP16}}(N)=C(\beta + N),\qquad
t_{b}(N)=C(\beta + N\cdot k(b)),
$$

so the speed-up at size $N$ is $r(N)=\dfrac{\beta+N}{\beta+N k(b)}$. Here $k(b)$ is the per-parameter cost of bit-width $b$ relative to FP16 and $\beta$ encodes fixed overhead measured in the same normalized units. This form captures that speed-ups increase with model size (overhead becomes less important).

Energy: separate arithmetic energy and memory-transfer energy. A simple per-parameter model is

$$
E(N,b) \approx N\big( \mathrm{ops\_per\_param}\cdot e_{\mathrm{arith}}(b) \;+\; e_{\mathrm{mem\_per\_bit}}\cdot b \big) + E_0,
$$

where $e_{\mathrm{arith}}(b)$ is energy per arithmetic operation at bit width $b$ and $e_{\mathrm{mem\_per\_bit}}$ is energy to move one bit. The paper reports that 1.58-bit arithmetic for matmul has about $71.4\times$ lower arithmetic energy (on a 7nm chip) than FP16 arithmetic, so $e_{\mathrm{arith}}(1.58)\approx e_{\mathrm{arith}}(16)/71.4$ for the matmul portion.

3) Minimal calibration from the paper (worked example)
The paper reports (paraphrased): at 3B, BitNet $b=1.58$ is $2.71\times$ faster and uses $3.55\times$ less GPU memory than FP16 LLaMA (same $N,T$); at 70B the speed-up grows to $4.1\times$; and arithmetic energy for matmul is $\sim 71.4\times$ lower.

You can use these numbers to solve a couple of parameters in the cost models:

Memory-fit. Let the measured memory ratio be $r_M = M_{1.58}/M_{16} = 1/3.55\approx 0.2817$. With $\mathrm{act\_bits}(16)=16$, $\mathrm{act\_bits}(1.58)=8$ and weight bits 16 vs 1.58, the per-parameter activation coefficient $a$ satisfies

$$
r_M \;=\; \frac{1.58 + a\cdot 8}{16 + a\cdot 16}.
$$

Solving for $a$ gives

$$
a \;=\; \frac{1.58 - 16r_M}{16r_M - 8} \;\approx\; 0.838.
$$

So a rough calibrated memory formula (ignore small fixed overheads) is

$$
M(N,b)\approx \frac{N\big(b + 0.838\cdot\mathrm{act\_bits}(b)\big)}{8}\ \text{bytes},
$$

which yields the $3.55\times$ reduction at 3B when you plug in the paper’s act bit choices. Intuition: activations/KV caches are large relative to just weights, so the measured memory shrinkage is smaller than the raw weight-bit ratio $1.58/16$.

Latency-fit. Model $t_{\text{FP16}}(N)=C(\beta+N)$ and $t_{1.58}(N)=C(\beta + N k)$, use the two speed-ups $r(3\text{B})=2.71$ and $r(70\text{B})=4.1$ (write $N$ in billions: 3 and 70). Solving the two equations yields

$$
k(1.58)\approx 0.237,\qquad \beta\approx 0.627,
$$

so asymptotic speed-up $\approx 1/k \approx 4.22$ and the finite $\beta$ explains why the speed-up at 3B is only $2.71\times$ (fixed overheads matter at small $N$). This gives the simple working latency model

$$
t_{1.58}(N) \;=\; C\big(0.627 + 0.237\,N\big),\qquad t_{16}(N)=C\big(0.627 + N\big),
$$

with $C$ a hardware-dependent constant (time per billion FP16 parameters). To convert to absolute latency, measure $C$ once (e.g., time per token for a known FP16 model).

Energy illustration. Use the arithmetic-energy advantage $e_{\mathrm{arith}}(1.58)\approx e_{\mathrm{arith}}(16)/71.4$ and the memory model above. If $f$ is the fraction of FP16 total energy coming from arithmetic (matmul) vs memory movement, then the end-to-end energy ratio is roughly

$$
\frac{E_{1.58}}{E_{16}}\approx \frac{f/71.4 + (1-f)/3.55}{1}.
$$

If arithmetic dominates ($f\approx 0.8$) you get an end-to-end ratio $\sim 0.07$ ($\approx 14\times$ reduction); if arithmetic is 50% you get $\sim 0.15$ ($\approx 6.7\times$). The paper’s “$71.4\times$ on arithmetic” number is the best-case arithmetic term; the true wall-plug reduction depends on how memory-dominated your end-to-end workload is.

4) How to produce iso-quality / iso-cost curves (practical recipe)
First fit $L(N,T)$ for your dataset and training recipe at FP16 (or use literature exponents as priors): gather PPLs for 3–5 model sizes at fixed $T$ and fit $L_\infty,A,\alpha$ (and $B,\beta$ if you vary $T$). Next estimate $s(b)$ by training/evaluating 1–2 models at alternative $b$ (paper suggests $s(1.58)\approx1$ for $N\gtrsim3\,$B). Measure (or use the paper-calibrated) cost parameters $a$, $k(b)$, $\beta$ and $C$ on your target hardware (measure latencies and peak memory for two model sizes and solve the two-parameter linear model). Then for a desired target loss $L^*$ compute $N(b)$ from the inverted law above and plug into $M(N(b),b)$, $t(N(b),b)$, $E(N(b),b)$ to get iso-quality cost points. Plotting $b\mapsto C(N(b),b)$ gives the iso-quality iso-cost tradeoffs.

5) Robustness and caveats
Scaling laws are empirically robust in broad regimes, but all fitted constants depend on dataset, tokenization, training recipe, small architecture changes (RoPE vs no RoPE, normalization choices), and especially on quantization-aware training tricks. The key empirical points from this paper that make 1.58-bit attractive are: (a) PPL parity with FP16 at the same $N$ for $N\ge 3\,$B (so $s(1.58)\approx1$ in that regime), and (b) large arithmetic-energy and per-parameter time reductions that grow with model size (i.e., $k(1.58)\ll 1$). But you should always re-calibrate $s(b)$ and the hardware constants ($C,\beta,k(b),a,e_{\mathrm{mem}}$) on your data and hardware if you want production-quality provisioning.

Summary (one-liner): use a Kaplan-style loss model with an effective-size multiplier $s(b)$ and simple linear-in-$N$ cost models (memory bits per param + activation coefficient; latency = fixed + per-param cost; energy = arithmetic + memory terms). Calibrate $s(b)$ and the few cost constants on a handful of measured sizes; with the paper’s reported ratios you can initialize $a\!\approx\!0.84$, $k(1.58)\!\approx\!0.237$, $\beta\!\approx\!0.627$, and $e_{\mathrm{arith}}(1.58)\!\approx\!e_{\mathrm{arith}}(16)/71.4$ and then produce iso-quality / iso-cost curves for planning.
