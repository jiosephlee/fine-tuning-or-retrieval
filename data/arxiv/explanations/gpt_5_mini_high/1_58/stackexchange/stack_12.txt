\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How much ternary-induced sparsity do you observe, and is it exploited for speed on current GPUs?
Question:
Allowing $0$ in $\{-1,0,1\}$ should induce weight sparsity. What fraction of weights are zero per layer at convergence (mean, variance across layers), and how does this differ between attention and MLP blocks? Does your kernel actually skip arithmetic for zeros to gain speedups, or are current GPU paths effectively dense so sparsity only helps conceptually (e.g., for future hardware)?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not report per-layer zero fractions, and the measured speedups are not coming from skipping arithmetic on zeros — they come from using dense low-bit kernels (FasterTransformer + a Ladder 2-bit kernel) and lower-bandwidth / integer arithmetic. The ternary zeros are therefore primarily a modeling/representation advantage in this work and a promising lever for future hardware or specialized sparse kernels, but they were not exploited as dynamic zero-skipping on current GPUs in the reported results.

Why the paper doesn’t give a numbers table: BitNet b1.58 enforces
$$\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},-1,1\right)$$
with $\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|$. An entry becomes 0 exactly when $\left|\frac{W_{ij}}{\gamma+\epsilon}\right|<0.5$, i.e. $|W_{ij}|<0.5(\gamma+\epsilon)$.
So the per-layer zero fraction is simply $p_0^{(layer)}=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}\mathbf{1}\!\big(|W_{ij}|<0.5(\gamma+\epsilon)\big)$.
Because the paper does not print these $p_0$ statistics (mean/variance per layer or broken out by attention vs MLP), we cannot give concrete numbers from the paper itself. If you want to measure it yourself from checkpoints, compute $\gamma$ per weight matrix and evaluate that indicator to get exact per-layer means and variances (and then average over attention vs MLP blocks).

Intuition about where zeros are likely to live: the fraction depends entirely on the empirical weight distribution after training. For many heavy‑tailed distributions, absmean ($\gamma$) is a natural scale and a substantial fraction of weights can fall into the small interval that rounds to zero, so nontrivial sparsity is plausible. It is common in practice (outside this paper) for MLP / feed‑forward matrices to admit higher “redundancy” and thus higher zero rates than projection matrices in attention, but that is an empirical claim — the paper does not report that breakdown.

Why the reported speedups do not rely on zero‑skipping: the paper states that latency/memory/throughput were measured using FasterTransformer with an integrated Ladder 2‑bit kernel. Those are packed low‑bit dense GEMM implementations: they reduce memory transfer and use optimized low‑bit integer arithmetic (and special bit‑packing tricks), which is how the paper achieves the 2–4× latency speedups and large throughput/memory gains (for example, 2.71× faster at 3B and 4.1× at 70B as reported). They explicitly note “Both latency and memory were measured with a 2‑bit kernel, so there is still room for optimization,” which signals that no additional zero‑skipping sparse kernel was used for extra wins.

Practical system point of view: current GPUs are massively optimized for dense GEMMs; unstructured (irregular) sparsity is hard to accelerate because index/indirection overhead and irregular memory accesses often erase the theoretical FLOP savings unless sparsity is very high or structured (e.g., block or N:M sparsity with hardware support). So on today’s GPU paths the main payoffs come from lowering bit‑width and packing weights, not from skipping operations for every zero. That is why the paper emphasizes both algorithmic benefits (better modeling via a zero state) and the prospect of new hardware or sparse‑aware kernels (Groq/LPUs and similar) to harvest additional speed/energy gains later.

If you want to exploit the zeros in practice: measure $p_0^{(layer)}$ as above, then (a) if you get very high and structured sparsity consider training with structured sparsity objectives (block / N:M) so existing sparse GEMM paths or hardware primitives can be used; (b) for irregular sparsity you’ll need custom masked GEMM kernels or a specialized accelerator to get net wins; (c) alternatively, treat the ternary weights as packed sign/mask representations and explore bit‑packed sparse formats that a custom kernel or ASIC could exploit (the paper calls out new hardware as future work).

Summary: the paper adds a zero state and thereby induces measurable sparsity in principle, but it does not publish per‑layer sparsity numbers; the speedups reported are achieved by dense low‑bit kernels (2‑bit packed implementations and reduced memory/INT arithmetic), not by dynamically skipping arithmetic on zeros.
