\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### During training, are FP32 "master" weights kept and quantized on-the-fly, or are parameters stored strictly as ternary?
Question:
For quantization-aware training, it is common to maintain high-precision "master" weights and apply $\tilde{W}$ only in the forward pass via an STE. Did you keep FP32/BF16 master weights and optimizer states, or were parameters constrained to ternary at all times? What was the training-time memory footprint and throughput relative to an FP16 LLaMA trained with the same tokens?
Answer:
Short answer up front: the paper does not explicitly state “we kept FP32/BF16 master weights” vs “we constrained parameters to ternary at every step.” However, the way their quantizer is written and their framing (trained from scratch with 1.58‑bit weights, but defining the quantizer on a matrix $W$) strongly implies a quantization‑aware training (QAT) style setup: a real‑valued weight tensor $W$ is present during training and a ternary $\widetilde{W}$ is produced for the forward pass (i.e., the standard pattern where you keep a high‑precision master copy and apply rounding/STE in the forward). The paper does not report training‑time memory or throughput numbers — all of the reported speed / memory / throughput figures are for inference.

Why I read it that way: the paper defines
$$
\widetilde{W}=\mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},-1,1\right)
\quad\text{with}\quad
\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|.
$$
That notation treats $W$ as a real valued tensor from which the ternary $\widetilde{W}$ is computed; $\gamma$ is a smooth functional of $W$. The rounding operation is non‑differentiable, so the usual way to train under that formulation is to propagate gradients through the quantizer with a straight‑through estimator (STE) or a proxy gradient while updating the high‑precision $W$. The text also repeatedly describes the model as “trained from scratch, with 1.58‑bit weights and 8‑bit activations” which describes the target representation, not the optimizer internals. Because the paper omits an explicit sentence like “we stored master weights in FP32 and used STE,” you can’t claim the authors forced weights to be strictly ternary at all times, but the equations and conventions make the QAT / master‑copy interpretation the most likely.

Training memory / throughput: the paper gives several concrete inference numbers (all measured for inference with FasterTransformer and an integrated 2‑bit kernel), for example that BitNet b1.58 “starts to match full precision LLaMA at 3B … while being $2.71\times$ faster and using $3.55\times$ less GPU memory,” that a 3.9B BitNet is “$2.4\times$ faster and consumes $3.32\times$ less memory than LLaMA 3B,” and that BitNet 70B is “$4.1\times$ faster” and (on two 80GB A100s) supports up to $11\times$ the batch size of LLaMA 70B ($\approx 8.9\times$ higher throughput). Those are inference results; the paper does not publish training GPU memory or training throughput comparisons against an FP16 LLaMA trained on the same tokens.

Practical implication: if you reproduce BitNet with the apparent QAT setup (high‑precision master $W$ + quantized forward via STE), you will not get the same dramatic memory savings during pretraining that you see at inference, because you must still store the high‑precision master and optimizer state(s) during training. In a typical setup this means extra bytes per parameter (master copy + Adam moments or similar), so training memory can be larger than a pure FP16 training baseline unless you use memory tricks (ZeRO, gradient checkpointing, low‑precision optimizer states, etc.). Conversely, training with truly ternary‑only parameters (no master copy) is possible but requires different optimizers/projected updates and is less common; the paper gives no evidence the authors used that route.

If you need to reproduce or measure training costs, the safest course is to (a) inspect the released code/config (or ask the authors) to see whether they keep master weights and what datatype the optimizer states use, and (b) run a small-scale experiment comparing: standard FP16 training, QAT with FP32/BF16 masters + STE, and a projected‑ternary training recipe. The paper’s published numbers support large inference wins, but they do not answer the training‑time memory/throughput question directly.
