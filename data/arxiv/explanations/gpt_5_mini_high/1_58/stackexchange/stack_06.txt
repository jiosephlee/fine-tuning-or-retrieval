\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Does per-token scaling disturb attention calibration? How are $QK^\top$ dot products handled with different scales per token?
Question:
If each token's hidden vector is quantized with its own symmetric scale, queries $Q$ and keys $K$ may have token-dependent scales. Before computing $QK^\top$ and softmax, do you dequantize to a common floating-point domain, or carry per-token scales into the dot product and renormalize analytically? Any observed impact on attention score variance or the effective softmax temperature due to per-token quantization?
Answer:
Short answer: you can do either, and they are mathematically equivalent — but in practice you usually keep the per-token scales around and either (A) compute integer dot-products and multiply by the product of the two token scales, or (B) dequantize one side (usually the cached keys) once and then only have a per-query scalar left. Per-token symmetric scaling does change the pre‑softmax logits (it multiplies them), so it can alter the effective softmax “temperature” in principle — but in BitNet’s design this effect is small and was empirically negligible.

Why, and how to think about it precisely. Let the quantized (integer) query and key vectors be $\widehat{Q}_i,\widehat{K}_j$ and their per‑token symmetric scales be $s^Q_i,s^K_j$. Then the true (dequantized) dot product used by attention is
$$
z_{ij}=\frac{Q_i\cdot K_j}{\sqrt{d_k}}
=\frac{(s^Q_i\widehat{Q}_i)\cdot(s^K_j\widehat{K}_j)}{\sqrt{d_k}}
=\frac{s^Q_i s^K_j}{\sqrt{d_k}}\;\big(\widehat{Q}_i^\top\widehat{K}_j\big).
$$
So you can implement this either by computing the integer inner product $p_{ij}=\widehat{Q}_i^\top\widehat{K}_j$ and then multiplying by the scalar factor $s^Q_i s^K_j/\sqrt{d_k}$, or by dequantizing $\widehat{Q}$ and $\widehat{K}$ into floats and computing the usual float matmul. Both give the same numerical logits.

How this affects softmax calibration. The softmax sees
$$
\mathrm{softmax}_j\big(z_{ij}\big)=\mathrm{softmax}_j\!\left(\frac{s^Q_i s^K_j}{\sqrt{d_k}}\,p_{ij}\right).
$$
If you pre‑dequantize and cache keys as $K'_j=s^K_j\widehat{K}_j$ (a common practical choice), then
$$
z_{ij}=\frac{s^Q_i}{\sqrt{d_k}}\;\widehat{Q}_i^\top K'_j,
$$
so each query row is multiplied by the same scalar $s^Q_i$. Multiplying all logits in a softmax row by a positive scalar changes the effective temperature: larger $s^Q_i$ makes the distribution sharper, smaller $s^Q_i$ makes it flatter (because $\mathrm{softmax}(c\cdot x)$ is equivalent to $\mathrm{softmax}(x/(1/c))$). If you do not pre‑dequantize keys, the remaining per‑key factors $s^K_j$ make the effect non‑uniform across keys, which is a more complex distortion of relative scores.

Why BitNet reports negligible effect. The paper explicitly uses per‑token symmetric quantization of activations to the range $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ (no zero point), and uses RMSNorm and training-from-scratch. Symmetric quantization makes the algebra above exact (no pesky zero-point offsets), and RMSNorm / the training procedure keep activation norms reasonably well behaved and let the model adapt to any systematic scaling during training. The authors state that this per‑token scaling “introduces negligible effects to the performance in our experiments,” which matches the intuition that (a) per‑token scale variation is not huge after normalization layers and (b) training from scratch lets the network accommodate the residual temperature shifts.

Practical trade‑offs and recommendations. If you want maximum numerical fidelity to an FP baseline you can dequantize to float before softmax; if you want to stay in integer/math-efficient kernels, compute integer matmul once and apply the scalar scale product $s^Q_i s^K_j$ to the integer results. To avoid per‑pair multiplies you can pre‑dequantize/scale and store keys once (so only a per‑query scalar remains), or use coarser grouping of scales (per‑head or per‑layer) to reduce variance. If you are doing post‑training quantization of an already trained FP model, watch for calibration shifts (temperature changes) and consider light calibration or a small fine‑tuning pass; if you train with quantization (as BitNet does) the model will likely absorb the effect and you will see little degradation.

In short: per‑token symmetric scaling does mathematically multiply logits and therefore can change softmax sharpness, but it is easy to handle exactly (either by dequantizing or by carrying the scale products into the dot product), and in the BitNet setup the practical impact on attention calibration was small.
