\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why does parity with FP16 emerge only around 3B parameters—what breaks at smaller scales?
Question:
Results suggest BitNet b1.58 starts to match FP16 LLaMA around 3B parameters. For 1B or smaller, is the gap driven by representational limits of ternary weights, optimization challenges (e.g., gradient noise with heavy quantization), or architecture hyperparameters tuned for FP16? Any ablations that vary width vs depth at fixed parameter count, or adjust MLP expansion/attention head counts, to isolate the capacity most affected by ternary constraints?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper itself does not run the targeted ablations you ask for, but the text and results strongly point to a combination of (1) representational limits introduced by coarse ternary quantization (especially the per‑matrix absmean scaling), and (2) optimization / robustness effects that large, overparameterized models tolerate much better than small ones. Using LLaMA‑style hyperparameters “as is” for ternary training is a deliberate fairness choice in the paper, but it also means any small‑model mismatch from FP16 hyperparameters is left uncorrected — the authors report no width‑vs‑depth or MLP/attention head ablations to isolate which capacity modes fail first.

Why this is a plausible decomposition (grounded in what the paper reports)

The paper’s quantizer is explicitly coarse: they compute
$$\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|$$
and then map each entry to the nearest element of $\{-1,0,1\}$ after scaling: roughly
$$q_{ij}=\mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W_{ij}}{\gamma+\epsilon},-1,1\right),$$
so the stored pattern is effectively a ternary mask and a single scale per weight matrix (the paper describes this as “scale by average absolute value, then round to $\{-1,0,1\}$”). That design is extremely parameter‑efficient, but it also creates a coarse per‑element approximation error
$$E_{ij}=W_{ij}-\gamma q_{ij},$$
and that error is a larger fraction of the signal when (a) the matrix is small, (b) the per‑matrix scale poorly matches per‑channel amplitudes, or (c) the task requires fine continuous weight magnitudes. The paper also notes that matrix multiplies (i.e. the linear layers) are the dominant computation and thus are the primary place this quantization acts.

Two consequences follow and explain the 3B threshold:

1) Representational limit: small models have fewer parameters and less redundancy, so a three‑level per‑matrix quantizer cannot approximate many useful continuous transforms as well. In larger models there are many more ternary degrees of freedom to combine and approximate finer functions (redundancy + overparameterization acts like a “smoother” that hides coarse quantization). The paper’s empirical curve — the gap shrinking with size and parity appearing around 3B — matches this redundancy explanation.

2) Optimization / robustness: extreme quantization makes training dynamics harder (biased / noisy gradients when using discrete weights or STE variants). Large models are empirically and theoretically more robust to noisy / biased updates because they can find many parameterizations that implement the same function; small models have to be more “precise” and so noisy gradients or quantization bias more easily prevent finding a good solution. The paper trains BitNet b1.58 from scratch and reports that performance gap narrows with scale, which is consistent with an optimization/robustness effect that is ameliorated by scale.

Role of LLaMA hyperparameters: the authors intentionally use LLaMA‑alike components and “the same configuration” for fair FP16 vs 1.58‑bit comparisons. That makes the comparison clean but does not explore whether alternative width/depth/MLP expansion/head counts would be better for quantized small models. The paper does not report ablations that change width vs depth at fixed parameter count, nor MLP expansion or per‑layer micro‑architecture tuning targeted at ternary weights.

What an informative ablation would look like (and what each outcome would imply)

If you run fixed‑parameter experiments that vary width vs depth, vary the MLP expansion ratio, or vary number/size of attention heads, you can separate representational vs optimization causes. If widening (more neurons per layer with fewer layers) at fixed total parameters closes the gap at 1B, that points to a representational / redundancy problem — ternary weights need more parallel degrees of freedom to approximate fine functions. If deeper architectures (more layers, thinner width) become *worse* under ternary quantization, that suggests discretization error compounds through layers or makes optimization harder. If per‑channel or per‑row scaling (instead of a single per‑matrix $\gamma$) fixes most of the gap, that implicates the coarse absmean scaling as the bottleneck. If optimization‑oriented fixes (longer training, different optimizers, distillation from an FP16 teacher, or techniques that reduce gradient bias/noise) help most, that implicates optimization dynamics.

Practical signals you can measure: per‑layer quantization error norms, training loss trajectories (noisy vs smooth), sensitivity of final accuracy to freezing certain layers in FP16, and whether instability is reduced by increasing activation precision (the paper uses 8‑bit activations, which already helps).

Bottom line: according to the paper, parity at ~3B is an empirical scaling phenomenon; the paper provides the quantizer design (per‑matrix absmean + ternary levels), the training recipe (from‑scratch, 8‑bit activations, LLaMA‑alike config), and the scaling results, but it does not provide the ablations needed to conclusively attribute the small‑model gap to only one cause. My best reading of the evidence in the paper is that representational limits of coarse ternary quantization (amplified by per‑matrix scaling) are the primary cause, with optimization/gradient‑noise issues and unchanged FP16 hyperparameters also contributing — and that carefully targeted ablations (width vs depth, per‑channel scaling, keeping critical layers in higher precision, or distillation/optimizer changes) would be the right next experiments to pinpoint the dominant factor for 1B and smaller models.
