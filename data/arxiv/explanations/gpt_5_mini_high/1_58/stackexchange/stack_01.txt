\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### In BitNet b1.58, is the ternary weight matrix rescaled by $\gamma$ after matmul, or is the scale dropped?
Question:
In "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits", the ternary weights are obtained via $\tilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(W/(\gamma+\epsilon), -1, 1)$ with $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$. In many ternary schemes one uses $W_q \approx \gamma \cdot \tilde{W}$ and applies a scalar rescale after the matmul. Here it is unclear whether inference uses only $\tilde{W}$, or whether there is a dequantization multiply by $\gamma$ (per-tensor or per-channel) on the output. If there is a rescale, where does it happen and is that multiply counted in the latency/energy results? If there is no rescale, how is magnitude information recovered elsewhere?
Answer:
Short answer: $\gamma$ is not a throwaway — the ternary codebook implicitly represents the original magnitudes as $W \approx \gamma\widetilde W$. The paper does not write the word-for-word runtime prescription, but everything the paper says (the definition of $\gamma$, the math identity $A W \approx \gamma (A\widetilde W)$, and their claims about “integer-add only” GEMMs and using optimized 2‑bit kernels) points to the scale being preserved and applied as a single per-matrix scalar factor (or fused away), not being discarded.

Why this is the only sensible reading: the quantizer is
$$
\widetilde W=\mathrm{RoundClip}\!\big(\tfrac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},-1,1\big)
\quad\text{with}\quad
\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|.
$$
That definition makes $\gamma$ the per-weight-matrix magnitude reference, so mathematically
$$
A W \approx A(\gamma\widetilde W)=\gamma\,(A\widetilde W).
$$
If you dropped $\gamma$ entirely you would lose amplitude information and could not match FP16 behavior. In practice quantized inference therefore computes the integer/ternary accumulation $S=A_{\text{q}}\widetilde W$ and then multiplies by the scalar dequant factor(s) (for example $\gamma$ and any activation scale $\alpha$): $Y\approx\gamma\alpha S$.

Where that multiply happens and whether it was “counted” in the paper’s cost numbers: the paper reports latency/energy using FasterTransformer with a 2‑bit kernel (Ladder) integrated, and repeatedly emphasizes that the heavy inner GEMM work is integer-addition dominated. That implies the implementation uses an optimized integer kernel that does the inner accumulations with cheap adds/subtracts (ternary multiplications are sign flips) and then either (a) applies the per-matrix scalar dequant multiply once per output vector, or (b) fuses/absorbs the scalar into neighbouring operations (activation scaling per token, or folded into following layer normalization / next-layer weights) so it is not an extra full-precision multiply per MAC. In short, any dequant multiply is a single per-matrix/per-vector scalar scaling or fused operation (cheap compared to replacing all FP MACs), and the paper’s measured latency/energy already reflect the behavior of their optimized kernels (i.e., they did not pretend the scalar is free, but it is not the dominant cost).

Practical implementation note: because $\gamma$ is defined per-matrix (not per-channel), the runtime cost is small: one scalar multiply per output block (or a fused scale) rather than a multiply per MAC. If you want to reproduce exactly, inspect the BitLinear / Ladder 2‑bit kernel in their code or FasterTransformer integration — that kernel will show whether the scalar is applied after accumulation or fused into activation quantization. The upshot is: magnitude is recovered via $\gamma$; it isn’t “dropped”, and the paper’s performance/energy claims are for an implementation that keeps that scale but implements it cheaply (hence the “integer-add dominated” accounting).
