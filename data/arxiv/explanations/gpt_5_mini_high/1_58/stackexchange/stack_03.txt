\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Over which axis is the absmean scale $\gamma$ computed, and why not per-channel or groupwise scaling?
Question:
You define $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|$, i.e., an abs-mean over the entire matrix. Many quantization methods use per-output-channel (row-wise or column-wise) or groupwise scales to improve fidelity. Why choose a per-matrix abs-mean here? Did you try per-channel $\gamma_c = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i |W_{ic}|$ or groupwise variants, and what were the accuracy/speed trade-offs? Does per-channel scaling add too many post-matmul multiplies that hurt the "almost no multiplication" claim?
Answer:
Short answer: in the paper $\gamma$ is computed over the entire weight matrix (all $i,j$): $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|$. The manuscript does not report per-channel or groupwise $\gamma$ experiments; the per-matrix abs-mean was chosen for simplicity, hardware/kernelling friendliness, and because BitNet is trained from scratch so the network can adapt to a single global scale.

Why that choice makes sense (intuitive + math): the quantizer produces ternary weights
$\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\big(W/(\gamma+\epsilon), -1, 1\big)$,
so during inference you can treat $W \approx \gamma \widetilde{W}$. For a linear layer $Y = X W$ we get
$Y \approx \gamma (X \widetilde{W})$. The huge win of BitNet is that the expensive inner products $X \widetilde{W}$ can be implemented with integer sign flips and additions (no FP multiplies). All that remains is the cheap rescaling by $\gamma$ on the layer outputs.

Cost/complexity trade-offs vs per‑channel/groupwise scaling: switching to per‑channel scales $\gamma_j$ (or groupwise scales) does reduce per‑column quantization error in general — that is why many post‑training quant methods use them — but it changes the system trade-offs. Crucially, both per‑matrix and per‑channel approaches require one scalar multiply per output element to apply the scale (you still must do $m$ multiplies to scale an $m$‑dimensional output). What differs is metadata, memory traffic and kernel complexity: per‑channel scaling forces you to store/load a vector of $m$ scales, prevents easy broadcast/fusion of a single scalar, and makes handcrafted integer‑only kernels and hardware less efficient. In practice that extra memory loads and loss of simple broadcastability can hurt latency and energy even if the raw multiply count is the same order. For BitNet (trained from scratch) the authors pick the simpler per‑matrix absmean so the model adapts to the global scale and the inference kernel remains extremely simple and hardware‑friendly.

About accuracy experiments: the paper does not present per‑channel/groupwise ablations, so it does not claim whether per‑channel would materially improve final perplexity/zero‑shot accuracy in the BitNet training regime. A likely reason to omit them is that BitNet is quantization‑aware during training (not PTQ), so the model can learn to live with a global scale; the marginal accuracy gain from per‑channel scales may be small compared to the extra engineering and runtime cost. If you care about absolute fidelity in a PTQ setting, per‑channel is often helpful; if you train quantized from scratch and aim for minimal kernel complexity, per‑matrix is a pragmatic choice.

If you want to explore this yourself, run a small ablation: train identical small models with per‑matrix, groupwise (blocks of channels), and per‑channel $\gamma$; measure validation loss and profile inference latency and memory traffic. Also try fusing the per‑channel scale into a following fused kernel (or quantize scales into power‑of‑two) to reduce the practical cost of per‑channel scaling.
