\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### What drives the larger speedups at bigger model sizes—compute-bound vs memory-bound effects?
Question:
You report that speedup increases with model size (e.g., 4.1x at 70B). Is this mainly because a larger fraction of time is spent in BitLinear GEMMs as depth/width grow (shifting to compute-bound where int8 adds excel), or because bandwidth pressure is reduced by smaller weights? Do you have a roofline-style analysis contrasting FP16 vs ternary across sizes to illustrate the regime shift?
Answer:
Short answer: both effects matter, but the dominant reason the reported speedup grows with model size is that larger models spend a bigger fraction of their runtime in large nn.Linear GEMMs (so you get a bigger payoff from accelerating those ops). Quantization (1.58-bit weights, 8-bit activations / 2-bit kernels used in the measurements) amplifies that payoff in two ways: it (1) reduces bytes transferred from DRAM (raising arithmetic intensity and shifting some kernels out of the memory‑bound regime) and (2) replaces costly FP16 multiply–add work with much cheaper integer additions on the optimized kernel side (raising the effective compute ceiling). The paper does not include a formal roofline plot, but the text and measurements (e.g., $\sim 2.7\times$ at small sizes up to $4.1\times$ at 70B, large throughput increases because batch sizes fit in memory) are consistent with the two mechanisms combining and reinforcing each other as model size grows.

Why that combination matters (intuitively and technically). A dense GEMM of shapes $(m,n,k)$ does about $F=2mnk$ floating‑point MACs and must move roughly $B\approx m k\cdot s_A + k n\cdot s_B + m n\cdot s_C$ bytes (where $s_\cdot$ are bytes per element for the operands/outputs). The arithmetic intensity is $AI=\dfrac{F}{B}$. In a roofline model you compare $AI$ to the machine threshold $\dfrac{P_{\text{peak}}}{\text{BW}}$ (peak compute divided by peak bandwidth). If $AI$ is below that threshold your kernel is memory‑bound; above it you are compute‑bound. Quantizing weights from 16 bits to roughly 1.58 bits (and activations to 8 bits) reduces the bytes term $B$, which increases $AI$ and therefore tends to move GEMMs to the right on a roofline plot. At the same time, the optimized 2‑bit/INT8 kernels used here have a much higher practical throughput per byte or per clock for the operations they perform than the FP16 baseline, so the effective compute roof for the quantized path is higher (or at least more favourable for the particular integer-add workload BitNet uses). Put succinctly, quantization both shifts the point right and raises the attainable roof.

How this produces larger gains for bigger models. In small models a nontrivial fraction of runtime and memory is taken by embeddings, attention bookkeeping, kernels with small GEMMs, or framework overhead; compressing weights helps, but there is less nn.Linear time to accelerate, so the end‑to‑end speedup is moderate. As the model grows the share of time spent in large GEMMs increases (the paper explicitly notes "the time cost for nn.Linear grows with the model size" and shows the speedup trend). When those GEMMs dominate, accelerating them — and simultaneously increasing their arithmetic intensity by reducing weight bytes — yields a multiplicative effect: the kernels that used to be memory-bound become compute‑bound (so you can exploit the faster integer kernel) and a larger portion of total runtime benefits. That is why the paper observes increasing speedups (e.g., the $4.1\times$ figure at 70B) and why throughput gains are especially large (the 70B BitNet run could support $\sim 11\times$ the batch size and produced $\sim 8.9\times$ higher throughput on two 80GB A100s).

A compact roofline-style algebraic view you can use to reason or to measure: let the FP baseline time for a GEMM be $t_{\text{fp}} \approx \max\!\left(\frac{F}{P_{\text{fp}}},\frac{B_{\text{fp}}}{\text{BW}}\right)$ and the quantized time $t_{\text{q}} \approx \max\!\left(\frac{F'}{P_{\text{q}}},\frac{B_{\text{q}}}{\text{BW}}\right)$, where $P$ are the relevant peak throughputs, $BW$ is memory bandwidth, $F$ vs $F'$ reflect the differing operation counts/definitions (if you count ternary additions differently), and $B$ are bytes moved. The speedup is $S=t_{\text{fp}}/t_{\text{q}}$. From this expression you can see three limiting cases: if both versions are memory‑bound then $S\approx B_{\text{fp}}/B_{\text{q}}$ (pure bandwidth win); if both are compute‑bound then $S\approx P_{\text{q}}/P_{\text{fp}}$ (pure compute win); and if fp is memory‑bound while quantized is compute‑bound you get a compound effect (bandwidth reduction moves you past the ridge point so you also gain from higher integer throughput).

What the paper provides and what it does not. The paper reports end‑to‑end latency, memory, energy and throughput numbers measured with FasterTransformer (and a 2‑bit Ladder kernel), and it explicitly points to the two mechanisms I described: nn.Linear cost fraction increases with model size, and the quantized model has a much smaller memory footprint. It does not, however, include per‑kernel roofline plots or a microprofiling decomposition (time spent in DRAM traffic vs compute for each GEMM) that would let you cleanly quantify the relative contribution of "compute‑bound gain" vs "bandwidth reduction" to the reported overall speedups.

How you (or we) can quantify the split with a roofline experiment. Microbenchmark single GEMMs with the shapes used in the model (layer dims), record the measured runtime, measure bytes read/written (profilers or instrumented kernels), compute $AI=\dfrac{F}{B}$ and plot attained performance ($F/\text{time}$) versus $AI$. Do this for FP16 (cuBLAS/cutlass) and for the BitLinear / 2‑bit kernel. That plot will show whether FP16 points sit left of the ridge (memory‑bound) and whether the quantized points move right of the ridge and/or to a higher roof. Finally, run full‑model profiling (time per operator) to get the fraction of wall time spent in the accelerated GEMMs; multiply that by the per-GEMM speedups to estimate the end‑to‑end contribution. The algebra above (the $\max$ expressions) gives you the mapping from per‑kernel behaviour to overall speedup.
