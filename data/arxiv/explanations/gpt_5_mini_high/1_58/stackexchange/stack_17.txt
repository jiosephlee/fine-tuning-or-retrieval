\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### What accumulator precision is used for the int8-by-ternary dot products, and how do you prevent overflow?
Question:
With int8 activations in $[-127,127]$ and weights in $\{-1,0,1\}$, the worst-case dot product magnitude is $127d$ for length $d$. For typical widths (e.g., $d=8192$), this fits in int32 but not int16. Do your kernels accumulate in int32 and then rescale, and are there any intermediate downscales or blocking strategies to guard against overflow in very wide layers?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not explicitly state the accumulator width at the kernel level, but everything in the paper (and the facts you listed) strongly indicates the implementation uses 32-bit integer accumulation and then rescales to floating point. Int32 accumulation is the standard choice for int8$\times\{-1,0,1\}$ GEMMs (and is what FasterTransformer / typical GPU int8 paths do), and it is sufficient for the LLM inner dimensions used in the paper without any extra intermediate downscaling.

Why that is safe and how it is done (intuitively + a few formulas). With 8‑bit activations quantized to integers in $[-127,127]$ and ternary weights in $\{-1,0,1\}$, the worst‑case absolute value of a dot product of length $d$ is
$$
\max |\,\text{dot}\,| = 127\cdot d.
$$
For a typical hidden dimension $d=8192$ this is $127\cdot8192=1\,040\,384$, which is tiny compared to the signed 32‑bit range. In fact the largest $d$ you can handle before a signed 32‑bit accumulator overflows is
$$
d_{\max}=\left\lfloor\frac{2^{31}-1}{127}\right\rfloor\approx 16{,}909{,}320,
$$
so all usual Transformer widths (4k, 8k, etc.) are well inside the safe region. By contrast a 16‑bit accumulator (max 32767) only supports $d\le\lfloor32767/127\rfloor\approx 258$, so int16 would not work for typical LLM dims.

How the quantized pipeline maps to accumulation and scaling (matches the paper’s quantization math). The paper quantizes weights as
$$
\widetilde W=\operatorname{RoundClip}\!\bigl(W/(\gamma+\epsilon),-1,1\bigr)
$$
so the real (FP) weight is recovered approximately as $(\gamma+\epsilon)\widetilde W$. Activations are quantized per token to integer values $q_a\in[-Q_b,Q_b]$ (with $Q_b=127$ for 8‑bit). A kernel therefore computes an integer accumulator
$$
S=\sum_i q_{a,i}\,q_{w,i}\quad (q_{w,i}\in\{-1,0,1\}),
$$
and the floating output is formed by a single rescale
$$
\text{output} = S\cdot s_a\cdot s_w \;+\;\text{(bias)}\!,
$$
where $s_a$ and $s_w$ are the activation and weight scales (the paper’s per‑token activation scale and the $\gamma+\epsilon$ weight scale). So you accumulate purely in integers (avoiding FP multiplies inside the inner loop) and do one fused multiply by the two scales after accumulation.

Overflow mitigation and practical alternatives. For the layer sizes used in this work, no special intermediate downscaling is required because an int32 accumulator is large enough. If you ever need to support extremely wide dot products (approaching millions of terms) or a different activation range, common practical strategies are: (1) chunk (tile) the input dimension and accumulate partial int32 sums per chunk, converting/rescaling them to float and summing the floats across chunks; (2) use pairwise or Kahan‑style summation only to control numerical error (not overflow); (3) fall back to int64 accumulation if you truly need a larger integer range (but that is slower); or (4) choose a smaller quantization range (reduce $Q_b$) or apply deliberate block scaling so each block’s partial sum fits comfortably in int32. Any of these are standard engineering tradeoffs; the paper does not report using special blocking because it is unnecessary for their chosen dimensions.

One implementation note tied to the paper: they measured latency with the FasterTransformer codebase and integrated a 2‑bit kernel from Ladder. Both of those GPU quantized paths follow the usual convention of int8 inputs producing int32 accumulators and then converting/rescaling, so that is the most likely kernel behavior behind the paper’s claims.

If you are implementing this yourself, the simplest correct approach that matches the paper is: accumulate into signed int32, then apply the two scale factors (weight scale and per‑token activation scale) in FP (or fused FP16/BF16 if you prefer) to produce the output. No intermediate downscales are needed for typical LLM widths; only consider chunking/int64 if you have an unusually huge inner dimension.
