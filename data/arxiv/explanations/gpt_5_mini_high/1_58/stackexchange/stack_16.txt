\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Is $\gamma$ recomputed each forward pass per matrix, and how sensitive is training to $\epsilon$ or to using a moving average?
Question:
In $\tilde{W}=\mathrm{RoundClip}(W/(\gamma+\epsilon),-1,1)$ with $\gamma=\text{absmean}(W)$, do you recompute $\gamma$ from the current real-valued $W$ each step, or maintain an EMA across steps to stabilize scaling? What value do you use for $\epsilon$, and how sensitive are results to it? Did you try median/percentile-based scales to reduce outlier effects?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper defines $\gamma$ as the per-matrix average absolute value $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|$ and uses it in the quantizer $\widetilde W=\mathrm{RoundClip}\big(W/(\gamma+\epsilon),-1,1\big)$. That formulation implies $\gamma$ is taken from the current real-valued $W$ when you quantize (i.e. recomputed from the current weights per matrix at quantization time). The paper does not report using an EMA for $\gamma$, does not give a numeric value for $\epsilon$, and does not report experiments with median/percentile-based scales or an ablation on $\epsilon$.

Why this matters and what to watch for: recomputing $\gamma$ every forward makes the quantizer adapt immediately to the current weight distribution. The rounding-to-zero condition is transparent from the formula: a weight becomes zero when
$$
\left|\frac{W_{ij}}{\gamma+\epsilon}\right|<0.5
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
|W_{ij}|<0.5(\gamma+\epsilon).
$$
Because $\gamma$ is the mean absolute value, a few large outliers will inflate $\gamma$, raise that threshold, and therefore cause more (moderate) weights to quantize to zero. That is the principal robustness trade-off: mean-based scaling is simple and cheap, but sensitive to outliers.

About $\epsilon$: the paper includes $\epsilon$ only as a numeric stabilizer in the formula and does not state its value or report sensitivity studies. In practice $\epsilon$ only matters when $\gamma$ is comparable to or smaller than $\epsilon$; if $\epsilon \ll \gamma$ it has negligible effect. Typical engineering choices (outside this paper) are tiny constants like $10^{-6}$–$10^{-8}$ or using a relative floor (e.g. $\epsilon = \alpha\cdot\mathrm{max}(\gamma, \mathrm{something})$). The authors do not report that their results are sensitive to $\epsilon$, but they also did not publish an explicit sweep.

About using an EMA for $\gamma$: the paper does not mention maintaining a moving average. An EMA would smooth step-to-step fluctuations in scale (possibly stabilizing early training or reducing jitter in per-step sparsity), but it also introduces lag (the scale can be slow to follow genuine changes in weight magnitude). Recomputing $\gamma$ each forward is simplest and ensures the quantization threshold tracks the current parameter distribution; using an EMA is a reasonable implementation variant if you observe instability, but it is not what the paper reports.

About median/percentile-based scales and other robust alternatives: the paper does not report trying median or percentile scaling. Conceptually, replacing $\gamma=\mathrm{absmean}(W)$ with a robust statistic such as $\mathrm{median}(|W|)$ or a trimmed mean/percentile can reduce the influence of outliers and therefore avoid the inflated-zero problem described above. The trade-offs are that robust statistics may change the overall sparsity pattern and dynamic range used by the ternary mapping, and they may be slightly more expensive to compute (percentile/median is costlier than a mean unless approximated). If you see too much sparsity caused by occasional large weights, a median or trimmed-mean scale is a natural experiment to run.

A practical implementation checklist (recommended experiments, not claimed results from the paper): recompute $\gamma$ per-matrix per-quantize as in the paper to match their setup; if training is noisy try either detaching the computed $\gamma$ from the gradient graph (so scale is not backpropagated through) or maintain an EMA of $\gamma$ with a high decay ($0.99$–$0.999$) and compare; pick a very small $\epsilon$ (e.g. $10^{-6}$) as a default and test larger values only if $\gamma$ sometimes goes to zero; if outliers are creating undesired sparsity, try median or a percentile-based scale or a clipped mean.
