\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How are gradients passed through the non-differentiable RoundClip—do you use an STE, and with what details?
Question:
During training, $\tilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(\cdot)$ is piecewise constant. Do you use a straight-through estimator with $\partial \tilde{W}/\partial W = 1$ in some range and $0$ outside, or another surrogate? How are gradients handled at the clip boundaries $\pm 1$? Is there any gradient scaling/annealing on $W/(\gamma+\epsilon)$ early in training to control quantization noise?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not explicitly state the backward-through-RoundClip trick it used. However, given that BitNet-style quantization and standard quantization-aware training practices are referenced, the most plausible and commonly used approach is a straight-through estimator (STE): round is treated as identity in the backward pass, clipping contributes the usual indicator-mask derivative (1 inside the unclipped interval, 0 where saturated), and the scalar scale $\gamma$ is normally detached so gradients do not backpropagate through the global scale. The paper does not report any special annealing schedule on $W/(\gamma+\epsilon)$.

Why that is the natural interpretation and what it means mathematically. Define $s=\gamma+\epsilon$ and

$$
x = \frac{W}{s},\qquad \widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(x,-1,1).
$$

In practice one usually reconstructs a forward weight (for matmuls) as $W_q = s\cdot\widetilde{W}$. Because $\mathrm{round}(\cdot)$ and clipping are piecewise-constant / non-differentiable, STE replaces their backward Jacobian by a simple surrogate. The typical surrogate used in this family of papers is to let rounding be identity in the backward pass and let clipping pass gradient only where $|x|<1$. If $s$ is detached (no gradient through $\gamma$) this gives the simple local backward rule

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W} \;=\; \frac{1}{s}\,\mathbf{1}_{|x|<1}\odot\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_q},
$$

i.e. gradients to each weight are the upstream gradient divided by $s$ and masked off for saturated (clipped) inputs. If you instead treat $\widetilde W$ itself as the parameter used in the rest of the graph (no de-scaling), replace $\partial L/\partial W_q$ with $\partial L/\partial\widetilde W$ and the same mask/divide-by-$s$ factor appears from the chain rule.

What happens at the boundaries $\pm 1$. The derivative is technically undefined at exactly $x=\pm1$, but those exact values occur on a measure-zero set in continuous weights and are not an issue in practice. Implementations typically use the clamp derivative (1 when $|x|<1$, 0 when $|x|>1$); the value at the exact boundary can be chosen arbitrarily (frameworks usually return 0 or one-sided derivative). A common and robust implementation trick that implements STE in frameworks is

$$
\widetilde x_{\text{STE}} = (\mathrm{round}(\mathrm{clamp}(x,-1,1)) - \mathrm{clamp}(x,-1,1)).\text{detach}() \;+\; \mathrm{clamp}(x,-1,1),
$$

so forward uses the rounded/clamped integer but the backward gradient is exactly that of the unclamped $\mathrm{clamp}(x,-1,1)$ (i.e. the mask behavior above). That makes boundary handling pragmatic and stable.

Why people detach $\gamma$. If you do not detach $s=\gamma+\epsilon$ then gradients pick up a global, cross-weight coupling (because $\gamma=\tfrac{1}{nm}\sum |W_{ij}|$ depends on all weights). That produces extra terms of the form proportional to $\partial s/\partial W$ (i.e. $\operatorname{sign}(W)/nm$) and tends to introduce instability and unexpected global interactions. The usual, stable choice is to compute $\gamma$ from the current weights but stop gradients through it (i.e. use $\gamma.\text{detach}()$). The paper defines $\gamma$ as the abs-mean and does not say it backpropagates through it, so detaching is the conservative, standard assumption.

About annealing / gradient scaling. The paper does not report a specific annealing schedule on $W/s$ or a special gradient scaling to gradually introduce quantization. They state models are trained from scratch with the 1.58-bit weights (and 8‑bit activations), so the stable training recipe is probably obtained by the combination of the STE surrogate, detaching the scale, standard optimizer schedules (LR warmup/decay), and architecture choices (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, no biases). If you observe excessive quantization noise in practice, common remedies are (not stated in the paper): (i) soft surrogates / softround that interpolate between identity and hard round and anneal a temperature, (ii) add small uniform noise to emulate quantization during early training, or (iii) progressive quantization (start in higher precision and move to ternary). The paper does not report using any of these.

Concrete PyTorch-style idiom (what people typically implement). Compute the scale, detach it, normalize, clip/round in forward, and use the STE trick so the backward gives the unclipped gradient:
s = W.abs().mean()
s_det = s.detach()
x = W / (s_det + eps)
x_clamped = x.clamp(-1,1)
x_rounded = x_clamped.round()
x_q_ste = (x_rounded - x_clamped).detach() + x_clamped   # STE: forward uses rounded, backward uses unclamped x
W_q = x_q_ste * (s_det + eps)                            # use W_q for forward matmuls

This yields exactly the masked $1/s$ factor in the backward pass and avoids gradient flow through $\gamma$.

If you need a definitive answer for this paper, check the authors' implementation/repo or ask the authors; the manuscript itself gives the quantization formula but does not list the exact backward/training surrogate or claim any special annealing.
