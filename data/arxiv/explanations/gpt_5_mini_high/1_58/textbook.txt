\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

The Era of 1-bit LLMs: Motivation and Overview

# Why LLM cost and scalability matter (latency, memory, energy, economics, environment)

Large Language Models (LLMs) have grown rapidly in parameter count and capability. That growth comes with practical costs: latency (how fast a model responds), memory (how much RAM and on-chip storage it needs), energy (the electricity burned during training and inference), and, ultimately, economic and environmental impacts. The paper motivates 1-bit LLMs by pointing out that these costs are not small details: as models grow, deployment becomes harder, inference becomes more expensive, and the energy footprint becomes a serious concern.

Two technical facts underlie most of these costs. First, the bulk of LLM computation is matrix multiplication: the dense linear layers that implement attention and the feed‑forward blocks represent most of the floating‑point work. Second, floating‑point arithmetic (multiplication and addition) and moving large weight matrices from DRAM into on‑chip memory (e.g., SRAM) are both expensive in time and energy. The paper emphasizes that increasing on‑chip SRAM to avoid repeated DRAM transfers is itself costly. Because power (energy per time) is often the limiting resource on modern chips, reducing arithmetic complexity and memory traffic translates directly into lower latency and energy and, therefore, lower operating cost.

Finally, memory constraints also limit model features such as batch size and context length: KV caches for long‑context inference are memory intensive, and activation storage grows with sequence length. Reducing the bits used for weights and activations can therefore make larger contexts and higher throughput feasible on the same hardware footprint. These are the deployment and scaling pressures that motivate moving to ultra‑low‑bit LLMs.

# From FP16/BF16 to 1–4-bit models: the emerging trend

Historically, many high‑performance LLMs have used 16‑bit floating point formats (FP16 or BF16) as a practical compromise between numerical precision and resource use. Industry practice has increasingly explored lower bit widths for inference: post‑training quantization (PTQ) methods convert a trained high‑precision model to a lower‑bit representation, often enabling 4‑bit inference kernels that save memory and computation. The paper notes that PTQ has been widely used to reduce inference cost, but is generally considered sub‑optimal compared to designing models and training procedures that natively use low‑bit representations.

A different, and potentially more powerful, approach is to design architectures and training methods that operate with low‑bit parameters from the start. The BitNet family is an example of such an architecture: by replacing standard linear layers with BitLinear and training with quantized weights, BitNet enables a “new computation paradigm” in which many multiplications become simple integer additions. The paper positions BitNet b1.58 as a successor in this line: rather than starting from a full‑precision model and compressing it after the fact, BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with ternary (three‑state) weights and low‑bit activations. This tight co‑design of model, quantization, and training can close the performance gap that PTQ encounters and unlock larger system‑level savings.

# What 1.58 bits means: ternary weights \{-1, 0, +1\} and $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$

Saying a model is “1.58‑bit” refers to the information content per weight when weights take three possible values. A single ternary weight can be one of three states: $-1$, $0$, or $+1$. The number of bits of information per weight is the base‑2 logarithm of the number of states:
\[
\text{bits per weight} = \log_2(3) \approx 1.58.
\]
So “1.58 bits” is another way to say “ternary weights.”

There is an important modeling rationale for allowing a zero state in addition to $\pm 1$. With only $\{-1,+1\}$ weights, every weight always contributes (possibly with a sign flip) to the output. Including $0$ gives the network an explicit option to silence a connection — a kind of feature filtering — which can increase modeling flexibility. The paper highlights this as a reason why BitNet b1.58 has stronger modeling capability than a purely binary (two‑state) 1‑bit model.

On the computation side, ternary weights change the cost profile of matrix multiplication. Multiplication by $+1$ or $-1$ reduces to a pass‑through or sign flip; multiplication by $0$ eliminates the term entirely. In implementations this means many multiply operations can be replaced by conditional additions/subtractions or skipped altogether, so the dominant arithmetic becomes integer additions rather than floating‑point multiply‑accumulate. The paper emphasizes that this shift yields large energy savings because integer addition is far cheaper than FP16 multiply‑add on modern chips.

# BitNet b1.58 at a glance: training from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations

BitNet b1.58 is a concrete architecture and training recipe that instantiates the ideas above. Architecturally, it follows the BitNet design in replacing standard dense layers (e.g., PyTorch’s nn.Linear) with a quantized BitLinear implementation. The model is trained from scratch with ternary weights (the 1.58‑bit representation) and 8‑bit activations; embeddings are kept in full precision. To ease adoption, the design adopts the common LLaMA style choices: RMSNorm for normalization, SwiGLU activations in the feed‑forward block, rotary embeddings for attention, and the removal of biases. These choices make the model easier to integrate into popular open‑source stacks.

The quantization used for weights is an “absmean” scaling followed by rounding and clipping. Given a weight matrix $W$ of shape $n\times m$, the procedure defines a per‑matrix scale $\gamma$ as the mean absolute value of the entries:
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
\]
Weights are scaled by this $\gamma$ (with a small $\epsilon$ added for numerical safety) and then rounded and clipped to the ternary set:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\; -1,\; 1\right),
\]
where
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max\bigl(a,\; \min\bigl(b,\; \mathrm{round}(x)\bigr)\bigr).
\]
This produces $\widetilde{W}$ with entries in $\{-1,0,1\}$ after scaling and rounding.

For activations, BitNet b1.58 follows the BitNet implementation but modifies the pre‑nonlinearity scaling. Instead of scaling activations to $[0,Q_b]$ (which would require a zero point in integer quantization), activations are scaled per token to the symmetric range $[-Q_b,Q_b]$. This removes the need for a zero‑point offset in activation quantization, simplifying implementation and system‑level optimization while causing only negligible performance differences in the experiments reported.

Because embeddings remain in full precision, a portion of the memory budget is still FP16/BF16; however the paper points out that the embedding proportion of total memory becomes smaller as model size grows, so the relative system benefit of the 1.58‑bit representation increases with scale. The implementation work for inference in the paper used a well‑optimized kernel stack (FasterTransformer) and integrated a 2‑bit kernel from Ladder to measure latency and throughput.

# Key claims: FP16 parity from 3B parameters, large cost reductions, new scaling law

The central experimental claim of the work is that BitNet b1.58 can match a full‑precision (FP16/BF16) Transformer of the same size and training budget starting from the 3B parameter scale, while substantially reducing inference cost. Concretely, the models were trained from scratch on the RedPajama corpus for 100 billion tokens and evaluated on perplexity (e.g., WikiText2, C4) and a suite of zero‑shot benchmarks (ARC‑Easy, ARC‑Challenge, HellaSwag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenBookQA, BoolQ). Runtime memory and latency were measured using an optimized inference stack, with time per output token as the primary runtime metric.

The reported cost and performance comparisons include the following highlights. At the 3B model size, BitNet b1.58 matches the FP16 LLaMA baseline in perplexity and downstream tasks while being significantly cheaper to run: the 3B BitNet b1.58 configuration is reported as 2.71 times faster in latency and consumes 3.55 times less GPU memory than the FP16 baseline. A slightly larger BitNet b1.58 model (3.9B) is still 2.4 times faster and uses 3.32 times less memory while performing noticeably better than the 3B FP16 LLaMA model.

As models scale, the performance advantage in cost becomes larger. The paper reports that the speed‑up factor increases with model size, culminating in a 70B BitNet b1.58 that is 4.1 times faster than the corresponding FP16 baseline under the measured setup. Memory trends are similar: because the embedding remains full precision but constitutes a smaller fraction of total memory as size grows, the overall footprint advantage of ternary weights grows with scale.

Energy and throughput claims are also highlighted. Focusing on arithmetic energy for matrix multiplication (the dominant contributor), BitNet b1.58’s arithmetic profile is primarily integer additions (INT8 additions), whereas the FP16 baseline relies on FP16 adds and multiplies. Using the paper’s energy accounting, BitNet b1.58 saves a large factor in arithmetic energy for matrix multiplication: the paper reports a 71.4× reduction for that component on a 7 nm energy model. For end‑to‑end throughput, the paper evaluated 70B models on two 80GB A100 GPUs using pipeline parallelism: BitNet b1.58 supported up to 11× the batch size of the FP16 baseline on those devices and achieved an 8.9× higher throughput in that setting.

Beyond these system metrics, the paper also addresses training‑time generalization. A BitNet b1.58 model trained with 2 trillion tokens following the StableLM‑3B data recipe was evaluated on a small cross‑task benchmark (Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, ARC‑easy) and reported superior zero‑shot performance compared to the StableLM 3B numbers cited from that work. Taken together, these results lead the authors to characterize BitNet b1.58 as a Pareto improvement: at equal or better task performance, it reduces latency, memory, and energy cost.

The paper distills a practical “scaling law” from the measured tradeoffs: certain 1.58‑bit model sizes dominate smaller FP16 models in cost. In the reported equivalences, a 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient (latency, memory, energy) than a 3B FP16 LLM; a 30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 7B FP16 LLM; and a 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 13B FP16 LLM. These equivalences provide a way to reason about the effective compute‑and‑cost capacity of ternary models versus traditional FP16 models.

Transformers and Their Cost Drivers

# Transformer blocks: attention, MLP, layer norms, embeddings

Modern large language models are built by stacking Transformer blocks. In the work summarized in the source paper, the base model family is a standard Transformer design (a LLaMA-like backbone) but with a key change in the linear layers: every standard dense layer (implemented in common frameworks as an operation like nn.Linear) is replaced by a BitLinear variant. The BitNet family described in the paper preserves the familiar LLaMA-alike ingredients so it integrates easily with the open-source ecosystem: it uses RMSNorm for normalization, the SwiGLU activation in the feed-forward (MLP) block, rotary positional embeddings, and removes explicit biases. These implementation choices make the BitNet architecture a close functional match to the widely used LLaMA implementations while changing how the bulk compute (the dense linear transforms) is represented and executed.

Although the paper does not re-derive Transformer math, it emphasizes where cost concentrates in a block: attention, MLP, layer norms and embeddings are the structural pieces, but the expensive operations in both attention and MLP arise from the many linear transforms (matrix–vector and matrix–matrix products) implemented by nn.Linear. The embedding table that turns tokens into vectors in practice is kept at full precision in BitNet b1.58; the paper explicitly notes that the embedding stays full precision and that its relative share of memory becomes smaller as models scale. By contrast, the linear transforms inside attention and the MLP are the primary targets for quantization and alternative implementations because they account for most of the arithmetic and memory traffic.

BitNet b1.58 constrains weights to the ternary set $\{-1,0,1\}$ and uses 8-bit activations. The paper describes an "absmean" quantizer that scales a floating-point weight matrix by its average absolute value and then rounds and clips each entry to $\{-1,0,1\}$. The equations the paper uses are
\[
\widetilde{W}=\mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},-1,1\right),
\]
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max\!\big(a,\min\!\big(b,\mathrm{round}(x)\big)\big),
\]
\[
\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|.
\]
This formulation makes the quantizer operate relative to the scale of the weight matrix (via $\gamma$) and forces the final stored weight values into three states. Adding the explicit zero state gives BitNet b1.58 an extra modeling degree compared to pure $\{\pm 1\}$ weights: the zero value enables explicit feature filtering (weights can effectively turn off a connection), which the paper highlights as a reason the ternary variant has stronger modeling capability than the original 1-bit variant.

Finally, activations in BitNet b1.58 are quantized differently than weights. The paper notes that activations are scaled per token into the symmetric range $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ (rather than using a non-negative range with a zero point). This symmetric per-token scaling removes zero-point complications in the activation quantization and is chosen because it is simpler to implement and has negligible impact on empirical performance in the experiments reported.

# Why matrix multiplications dominate runtime

A central empirical observation in the paper is that "the bulk of any LLM is matrix multiplication." That statement is the organizing principle for the efficiency claims: when most of the model’s work is matrix multiply (dense linear) operations, changing the representation and compute pattern of those matrix multiplies has outsized effect on latency, memory, throughput and energy.

Matrix multiplications show up everywhere in a Transformer: the projection matrices inside attention (queries, keys, values, and output), the linear projections that implement the MLP (feed-forward) layers, and the final output projection. Each of those is implemented with dense linear algebra primitives that perform many floating-point multiplications and additions when stored in FP16/BF16. Because the amount of work in these operations grows quickly with hidden dimension and number of heads, the relative time spent in these dense multiplies increases as model size increases. The paper documents this empirically: the runtime cost of nn.Linear (i.e., dense matrix multiplication) grows with model size, and as a consequence the relative speed-up that a cheaper representation can provide also grows as models scale.

The BitNet approach targets exactly this kernel: by representing weights in a ternary form and using integer-dominated computation for the core multiplies, the model reduces the dominant compute and memory traffic. Practically, this is why the latency and memory advantages of BitNet b1.58 become larger for bigger models (the paper reports larger speed-ups for 70B than for 3B): removing expensive floating-point multiplication where it matters most has a multiplying effect on end-to-end inference cost.

# FP16/BF16 arithmetic: multiply-accumulate and its energy profile

Vanilla Transformer LLMs in common practice are trained and deployed using 16-bit floating formats such as FP16 or BF16. Those floating-point matrix multiplications are implemented as sequences of multiply–accumulate operations: a floating-point multiplication followed by a floating-point addition (or fused multiply–add). From an energy and hardware standpoint these floating-point arithmetic operations are comparatively costly, both because of the logic complexity and because their use tends to dominate power draw when matrix multiplies are the hot loop.

The BitNet b1.58 design replaces expensive FP16 matrix multiplies with computation that is dominated by integer additions. Concretely, the paper emphasizes that the matrix multiplication in BitNet can be implemented without the floating-point multiplications that appear in FP16 matrix multiply kernels. In their energy accounting, using the energy model cited in the paper (for a representative 7 nm process), BitNet b1.58 saves a large factor in arithmetic energy for matrix multiplication: the paper reports a reduction by a factor of $71.4$ in arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication when comparing BitNet b1.58 to an FP16 LLaMA baseline on 7 nm chips. The paper also breaks down the operation mix: the majority of BitNet b1.58 arithmetic is INT8 addition, whereas the FP16 baseline consists of both FP16 addition and FP16 multiplication primitives.

Because many modern accelerators are power-limited, arithmetic energy translates directly into practical resource limits: if each multiply–accumulate draws more energy, the chip must either reduce clocking or limit parallelism; reducing per-operation energy therefore opens the door to higher throughput and lower latency. The paper uses this reasoning to explain how the arithmetic savings from ternary weights and integer arithmetic can produce order-of-magnitude reductions in energy for the matrix-multiply dominated parts of inference.

# Inference pipeline and time-per-output-token as the dominant cost

When measuring inference for language models the paper focuses on time per output token as the most relevant real-world metric: the experiments report latency (time per token) using the FasterTransformer codebase, which is a highly optimized inference stack for GPU devices. For BitNet b1.58, the paper integrates a 2-bit kernel from Ladder to serve the quantized compute path and reports runtime GPU memory and per-token latency under the same conditions used for the FP16 LLaMA baselines.

The experimental results reported in the paper show concrete end-to-end benefits in the per-token metric. For example, when pre-trained on the same RedPajama data for $100$ billion tokens, BitNet b1.58 matches the full-precision LLaMA baseline in perplexity starting at model size $3\,$B, while operating faster and using less memory. Specifically, at $3\,$B BitNet b1.58 is reported to be $2.71\times$ faster and use $3.55\times$ less GPU memory than the FP16 LLaMA reproduction. A BitNet b1.58 model of size $3.9\,$B was $2.4\times$ faster, used $3.32\times$ less memory, and performed better than a $3\,$B FP16 LLaMA. At very large scale the trend continues: BitNet b1.58 at $70\,$B is reported as $4.1\times$ faster than the corresponding FP16 baseline.

Throughput measurements of large models further illustrate the practical effect on serving: for a $70\,$B model spread across two $80\,$GB A100 cards (using pipeline parallelism so that the FP16 baseline could run), BitNet b1.58 supported up to $11\times$ the batch size of the FP16 LLaMA model and achieved $8.9\times$ higher throughput before hitting GPU memory limits. These numbers show how changing the representation and arithmetic of the dominant kernel directly translates into more examples per second and lower latency per generated token in real inference stacks.

# Memory movement and bandwidth bottlenecks (DRAM to on-chip SRAM)

A frequent pain point for deployment of very large models is the cost of getting model parameters and activations onto the on-chip compute fabric. The paper highlights that moving parameters from DRAM into the on-chip memory of accelerators (for example into SRAM on an inference chip) is expensive in both time and energy. Increasing on-chip SRAM capacity to avoid repeated DRAM transfers is possible but significantly costlier in silicon area and dollars than relying on DRAM. Consequently, memory capacity and bandwidth are recurring bottlenecks for inference.

Because BitNet b1.58 represents most parameters with ternary values and uses lower-bit activations, it shrinks both the capacity required to hold weights and the bandwidth required to stream them from off-chip memory. The paper emphasizes that the reduction is meaningful from both a capacity and a bandwidth standpoint: smaller stored weights mean fewer bits to read from DRAM at each layer, and smaller activation representations reduce the size of intermediate data that must be moved through caches and between chips. Those two effects together reduce the time cost of loading weights and activations from DRAM and thus lower the end-to-end inference latency.

Empirically, the memory savings are large enough to increase the effective batch size and throughput the hardware can sustain. As reported above, BitNet b1.58 uses several times less GPU memory than the corresponding FP16 LLaMA models in the tested sizes; this directly enables larger batch sizes (as observed in the $70\,$B throughput experiment) and lower pressure on inter-chip communication for distributed deployments. The paper also notes that embedding tables remain full precision, which is why they retain a non-zero memory footprint, but as model size increases the embedding’s relative share of overall memory drops, so the benefits of quantizing the many dense projection matrices grow with scale.

# KV cache growth with context length and its implications

One specific memory challenge for long-context inference is the growth of the key/value (KV) caches that attention mechanisms store for autoregressive generation. The paper calls out KV cache memory consumption as "one major challenge for long sequence inference." Because KV caches store intermediate activations per past token, their memory footprint grows linearly with the context length; in full-precision systems those stored activations are usually 16-bit, so long contexts imply large memory and bandwidth costs.

BitNet b1.58 impacts this story by reducing activation precision: activations are represented as 8-bit in the reported design. The paper points out that this immediately allows roughly doubling the context length for the same memory budget (16-bit → 8-bit halves the memory needed for the caches). The authors further suggest that the activation representation for 1.58-bit LLMs could be losslessly compressed to 4 bits or even lower in future work, which would provide additional headroom for longer contexts without increasing hardware costs. Reducing the KV cache footprint not only enables native support for longer sequences on existing accelerators but also reduces inter-chip communication and device count in distributed setups.

Reducing activation and weight precision has practical implications for deployment beyond raw latency: it makes it more feasible to run larger-context workloads on a single device or with fewer devices, lowers the energy and memory cost of KV cache movement, and therefore directly addresses one of the tightest constraints in long-sequence LLM inference.

# Concluding perspective

Across the Transformer components, the dominant cost drivers are the dense linear algebra kernels (matrix multiplications) and the movement of those large parameter and activation tensors between off-chip and on-chip memories. The BitNet b1.58 approach in the source paper tackles both of these pain points by turning most weight storage into a ternary format and by using lower-precision activations. That design shifts the compute mix away from expensive FP16 multiply–accumulate operations toward integer additions, reduces DRAM bandwidth pressure, shrinks KV caches, and yields empirical gains in per-token latency, GPU memory, throughput and arithmetic energy. The result is a set of practical trade-offs and scaling observations: quantizing the dominant kernels changes the primary bottlenecks of Transformer inference, enabling new operating points (lower latency, lower energy, larger context lengths) while, in the reported results, matching full-precision perplexity and end-task performance starting at moderate model sizes.

Quantization for LLMs: Concepts, Methods, and Limits

# Quantization basics: uniform vs non-uniform, symmetric vs asymmetric, zero-point

Quantization is the process of replacing continuous numerical representations (floating-point weights and activations) with a small discrete set of values so that a model requires less memory and cheaper arithmetic during inference. The motivating observations in the paper are straightforward: modern Transformer LLMs are dominated by matrix multiplications carried out in 16-bit floating point (FP16 or BF16). Reducing the precision of weights and activations lowers both the memory footprint and the cost of the arithmetic for those matrix multiplications, which in turn reduces latency, memory-bandwidth pressure, and energy consumption.

BitNet b1.58 follows a particularly simple and explicit quantization design: every weight is constrained to take one of the three integer values $\{-1,0,1\}$. Written compactly, the weight quantization used in BitNet b1.58 is
$$
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
$$
with the clipping/rounding helper
$$
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max\big(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x))\big),
$$
and the scalar scale
$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
$$
This "absmean" quantization divides a weight matrix by its mean absolute value $\gamma$, then rounds and clips each entry to $\{-1,0,1\}$. Because the discrete set is centered on zero, this design is symmetric (levels are balanced around zero) and does not require an explicit additive zero-point offset for weights.

A helpful way to measure the storage advantage is to count the number of representable levels: ternary weights have $3$ levels, so the idealized information content per weight is
$$
\log_2(3) \approx 1.585\ \text{bits}.
$$
This is where the paper's name "b1.58" comes from: the ternary set corresponds to about $1.58$ bits of information per parameter under a simple information-theoretic view. In practice, additional metadata (for example the per-matrix scale $\gamma$) must also be stored, so the real-storage per parameter is slightly higher than the idealized $\log_2(3)$, but the large reduction relative to 16-bit floats remains.

Two common axes used to classify quantizers are "uniform vs non-uniform" and "symmetric vs asymmetric (zero-point)". Uniform quantization places its discrete levels at equal intervals (after some global scale and shift); BitNet's integer rounding to $\{-1,0,1\}$ after dividing by $\gamma$ behaves like a simple uniform, symmetric quantizer on that scaled range. An asymmetric quantizer introduces a non-zero zero-point so that zero in floating point maps to some non-zero codeword; the paper explicitly avoids such a zero-point for activations by operating on symmetric ranges (see the activations section below). This symmetry simplifies integer kernels and (as the paper highlights) removes the need for a zero-point offset in the activation path.

Finally, the practical payoff of symmetric ternary weights is not only smaller storage: because each ternary weight multiplies an activation by either $+1$, $-1$, or $0$, the core inner-product operation reduces to selecting, adding, subtracting, or skipping accumulation of activation elements rather than performing general floating-point multiplication. This is the core of the "integer-addition" computation paradigm emphasized in the paper.

# Post-training quantization (PTQ) vs quantization-aware training (QAT)

The paper frames two broad approaches to push LLMs to low-bit inference representations. One is post-training quantization (PTQ): take a trained FP16/BF16 model and map its weights and (sometimes) activations down to low-bit representations for inference. PTQ approaches are popular in industry because they avoid retraining and can be applied as a conversion step. The paper cites examples of recent PTQ-style work in the LLM space (references include SmoothQuant, GPTQ, QuIP / QuIP-Sharp, and AWQ) and notes that the community trend has been to move from 16-bit down to 4-bit variants for inference savings.

The other approach is to make quantization a first-class constraint during model development: either quantization-aware training (QAT) where quantization operators are simulated during the training process, or training-from-scratch in a quantized parameterization so that the model's weights are learned under the low-bit constraint. BitNet b1.58 follows this latter path: it is trained from scratch with 1.58-bit (ternary) weights and 8-bit activations. This design choice is central to the paper's empirical claim: BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision FP16 LLaMA baselines (in perplexity and downstream tasks) at and above a 3B model size while delivering large improvements in latency, memory usage, and energy.

The trade between PTQ and QAT is therefore evident in the paper. PTQ is convenient and widely used, but, as the authors state, "post-training quantization is sub-optimal, even though it is widely used in industry LLMs" when pushing to extreme low-bit regimes. QAT or training-from-scratch with quantized weights, as in BitNet b1.58, lets the optimization process find parameters that are intrinsically compatible with coarse discrete representations, producing models that can achieve parity with FP16 baselines at practical scales.

# Representative LLM PTQ/QAT methods: SmoothQuant, GPTQ, AWQ, QuIP/QuIP-Sharp

The paper lists several representative methods by name to situate BitNet in the existing literature. SmoothQuant, GPTQ, AWQ, and QuIP / QuIP-Sharp are cited as recent advances in post-training quantization for LLM inference. The paper uses these works to motivate the trajectory of industry practice (moving from 16-bit to 4-bit quantization) and then to point out the limitation: that PTQ has not, by itself, produced an LLM operating efficiently and accurately at the extreme 1–2-bit end.

BitNet b1.58 deliberately takes a different stance: rather than using PTQ to push a pre-trained FP16 model down to ternary weights, the model is designed and trained with low-bit weights built into the architecture (replaceable nn.Linear → BitLinear) and an explicit quantization function (the absmean quantizer described earlier). The paper does not provide internal algorithmic descriptions of SmoothQuant, GPTQ, AWQ, or QuIP; it references them as context and contrasts them with the training-from-scratch ternary approach.

# Activation quantization: range calibration and clipping

Activations are the other half of the quantization story. The dynamic range of activations is often larger and more input-dependent than weight ranges, and activation quantization requires careful range calibration and sometimes clipping to avoid catastrophic precision loss. In BitNet b1.58, activations are quantized to 8 bits, and the paper highlights a design choice that simplifies implementation: activations are scaled to the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ on a per-token basis, rather than being scaled into $[0,Q_b]$ before non-linear functions (as in the original BitNet implementation).

This per-token symmetric scaling explicitly "gets rid of the zero-point quantization" for activations. In other words, by mapping the activation range to a symmetric interval around zero, you avoid the offset that would otherwise be necessary if the representation were inherently non-symmetric. The paper emphasizes that this symmetric per-token scaling is more convenient and simpler for implementation and system-level optimization, and that it had negligible effect on empirical performance in their experiments.

Practical benefits tied to activation quantization are also highlighted in the paper's systems observations. For example, reducing activations from 16-bit to 8-bit halves the memory required for KV caches, which directly supports longer context windows in inference. The paper further notes that these 8-bit activation representations could be compressible even further (to 4 bits or lower) in future work, which would amplify the benefits for long-sequence inference.

# Why PTQ becomes sub-optimal at extreme low bit-widths

The paper is explicit but concise about this point: while PTQ approaches are widely used and effective at modest reductions in precision (for example, down to 4 bits), they are not sufficient to reach the extreme 1–2-bit regime while preserving model quality. Rather than presenting an exhaustive technical diagnosis, the authors make the empirical case that designing and training the model with tight quantization constraints produces substantially better outcomes at ternary precision. BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with ternary weights and matches FP16 baselines in perplexity and downstream zero-shot performance at model sizes starting from 3B.

The implication supported by the paper's experiments is that moving to extreme low-bit quantization often requires changes beyond a post-hoc mapping of weights. These changes include (a) rethinking the parameter parameterization (here: BitLinear which uses ternary weights), (b) choosing an appropriate quantization function and scale (here: absmean scaling by $\gamma$), and (c) adopting compatible activation quantization and normalization choices (e.g., RMSNorm, SwiGLU, symmetric activation scaling). In other words, extreme quantization can be fundamentally a modeling and optimization problem that PTQ alone cannot resolve reliably; training-time integration of quantization constraints is a more robust pathway, as the BitNet results demonstrate.

# Trade-offs: accuracy vs compression vs implementation complexity

Any quantization strategy balances three intertwined axes: accuracy, compression (memory, bandwidth, arithmetic complexity), and implementation complexity (software kernels, retraining, and hardware support). The paper presents concrete empirical evidence that the ternary-training path can achieve an attractive point in this trade-off space: BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 baselines at and above 3B model size (accuracy), while delivering substantial compression and runtime benefits.

Key empirical trade-offs and concrete numbers from the paper include:

- At 3B model size, BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 LLaMA in perplexity while running 2.71× faster and using 3.55× less GPU memory. A 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model is 2.4× faster, uses 3.32× less memory, and performs better than the FP16 3B baseline.

- Latency and memory speed-ups grow with model size because the proportion of compute and memory spent in matrix multiplications (the \emph{nn.Linear} layers) increases. For a 70B model, BitNet b1.58 is reported to be 4.1× faster than the FP16 baseline; in a throughput experiment with two 80GB A100 GPUs, 70B BitNet b1.58 supports up to 11× the batch size of the 70B FP16 LLaMA and delivers 8.9× higher throughput.

- On the energy front, focusing on the matrix-multiplication arithmetic, the paper reports that BitNet b1.58 saves approximately 71.4× in arithmetic operation energy for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips compared to FP16 matrix multiplication. The bulk of BitNet's arithmetic is INT8 additions (or addition/subtraction), whereas FP16 LLaMA requires both FP16 multiplications and additions; this change in the arithmetic mix drives the large energy savings.

These advantages come with implementation and engineering costs. BitNet b1.58 requires rearchitecting linear layers (BitLinear), incorporating a quantization-aware training procedure (training from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations), and integrating specialized inference kernels (the paper integrates a 2-bit kernel from Ladder and uses the FasterTransformer codebase). There is also the additional bookkeeping of per-matrix scales like $\gamma$. Finally, some parts of the network (for example token embeddings) remain full precision in the reported experiments, which affects the absolute compression ratio; however, as models scale, these fixed-cost high-precision components occupy a smaller fraction of total memory.

Beyond the software and training-level trade-offs, the paper highlights the exciting systems and hardware implications: the integer-addition computation paradigm enabled by ternary weights invites custom hardware and accelerator designs optimized for low-bit addition rather than floating-point multiplication, and it lowers the barrier to deploying large models on resource-constrained devices (edge, mobile, and CPU-only environments). The paper also suggests that lower-precision representations simplify working with Mixture-of-Experts architectures (reducing inter-chip communication) and make native long-sequence inference more tractable by shrinking KV-cache footprints.

# Closing summary

The central lesson of the paper is that extreme low-bit operation for LLMs—down to ternary weights corresponding to roughly $1.58$ bits per parameter—can be achieved without sacrificing model quality, provided the quantization is treated as part of the model design and training process rather than as a purely post hoc conversion. BitNet b1.58 operationalizes this lesson through a simple, symmetric ternary quantizer (absmean scaling plus rounding/clipping), 8-bit activations with symmetric per-token scaling, and a training-from-scratch pipeline. The empirical payoffs include large reductions in inference latency, memory, energy, and improved throughput, with parity or improvements in perplexity and downstream zero-shot tasks at practical model scales. At the same time, these gains require additional implementation effort and a willingness to co-design models, software kernels, and eventually hardware to realize the full potential of the 1-bit (ternary) paradigm.

From Binary to Ternary Networks: Capacity and Computation

# Historical context: binary and ternary neural networks

Over the past several years the deep learning community has pursued ever denser and more capable Large Language Models (LLMs). That progress has come at a growing cost in compute, memory, and energy. One practical response has been to quantize models—reduce the numeric precision of weights and activations—to lower the cost of inference. The paper that motivates this chapter places BitNet and its successor BitNet b1.58 squarely in that line of work: moving from 16-bit floating point (FP16/BF16) down into very low-bit representations for the parameters and activations. The paper cites prior post-training quantization approaches that have sought to make FP16 models cheaper for inference, and it highlights a different, more aggressive route: designing models whose parameters are themselves discrete from the start (trained from scratch) so that the dominant compute (large matrix multiplications) can be implemented in a radically cheaper way.

BitNet was a first step in this direction by building a Transformer in which the linear layers use extremely low-bit weights. BitNet b1.58 is a concrete variant of that idea in which every weight is ternary, taking values in the set \{-1, 0, +1\}. Compared to full-precision Transformer backbones (for example, LLaMA-style models), this design changes both the representational substrate (what a single parameter can encode) and the computational substrate (what inner-loop arithmetic the hardware must perform). The paper documents empirical evidence—pretraining on RedPajama for 100B tokens and evaluation on standard zero-shot tasks—showing that the ternary models can match FP16 baselines at sufficient scale while bringing large improvements in latency, memory, throughput, and arithmetic energy.

This historical arc—moving from floating point to quantized post-training, and from there to models trained with discrete weights—creates a new trade space. On one side is the classical, high-precision model that is easy to optimize and expressive per-parameter; on the other are extreme-quantization designs that reduce operational cost dramatically. BitNet b1.58 is an example showing that the latter can, at realistic model sizes, reach parity with the former on perplexity and downstream tasks.

# Representational capacity of \{-1, 0, +1\} and information content

When we reason about what a neural network can represent, a useful first approximation is to consider how many distinct parameter configurations are possible. If a model has \(N\) scalar parameters and each parameter can take \(m\) distinct values, then the total number of distinct weight configurations is \(m^{N}\). In information-theoretic terms that corresponds to \(N\log_{2} m\) bits of information stored in the parameter vector.

For ternary weights that take values in the set \(\{-1,0,1\}\), we have \(m=3\). Thus the information content per weight, in bits, is
\[
\log_{2} 3 \approx 1.58496\ldots,
\]
which the paper abbreviates as “1.58 bits.” For \(N\) parameters the total combinatorial capacity is therefore
\[
N \log_{2} 3.
\]
Compare this to binary weights that take only two values (for example \(\{-1,1\}\)), where the per-parameter bit content is exactly \(\log_{2} 2 = 1\) bit. Compared to an FP16 representation (16 bits per parameter), ternary weights store far less raw information per parameter, but the representational story for neural networks is more subtle than raw bit counts alone.

There are two important caveats to bear in mind when translating “bits of storage” into “ability to learn functions.” First, a network’s capacity to approximate functions depends strongly on architecture, depth, and the interaction of weights with activations and non-linearities, not just the per-weight precision. Second, quantized networks typically include additional continuous degrees of freedom in the form of learned or computed scaling factors (per-matrix or per-channel), and those scales multiply the quantized integers when reconstructing approximate real-valued weights. In BitNet b1.58 the paper explicitly computes a per-weight-matrix scale \(\gamma\) (the average absolute value of the underlying full-precision weights) and uses it in the quantization function. The quantization step is written as
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\biggl(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\,-1,\,1\biggr),
\]
with
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max\bigl(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x))\bigr),
\qquad
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
\]
This scale \(\gamma\) is important: it places the ternary values \(-1,0,1\) on an absolute magnitude scale that enables the discrete weights to carry meaningful amplitude information beyond their sign. From an information viewpoint, adding such a scale effectively mixes a small number of continuous (or high-precision) auxiliary variables with a large number of coarse integer variables; together they can represent functions far richer than the integer values alone.

Empirically the paper shows that this combination—ternary weights plus per-matrix scale plus 8-bit activations—can approach the task performance of FP16 models for sufficiently large model sizes. That demonstrates a central point: while per-parameter precision constrains individual degrees of freedom, the overall function class the network can learn depends on the full system of discrete parameters, scales, activations, and architecture.

# Feature filtering enabled by explicit zeros in weights

A single-bit weight scheme that allows only \{-1, +1\} forces every incoming feature to either be passed (with sign) or inverted; there is no option to silence a connection. Introducing a third value, \(0\), gives the network the explicit ability to drop an input's contribution to a neuron. This is the core of what the paper calls “feature filtering.”

To see the effect concretely, consider a single output scalar computed by a linear layer:
\[
y = \sum_{i} \widetilde{W}_{i} \, a_{i},
\]
where \(\widetilde{W}_{i} \in \{-1,0,1\}\) are ternary weights and \(a_{i}\) are the (quantized) activations. If \(\widetilde{W}_{i}=0\), the product \(\widetilde{W}_{i}a_{i}\) is zero and the corresponding feature \(a_{i}\) makes no contribution to \(y\). Thus zeros implement a hard gating mechanism at the weight level.

There are multiple ways this mechanism helps modeling:

1. Sparse routing of information. Zeros allow each neuron to pick a subset of inputs to attend to; this can implement a form of learned feature selection or gating without the extra overhead of separate gating networks.

2. Noise suppression. Small-magnitude weights in a full-precision model that would otherwise contribute noisy signals can be replaced by zeros; that both reduces noise in the forward pass and reduces the number of nonzero contributions the hardware must sum.

3. Better expressivity per parameter. With \{-1,1\} weights the only choice is direction (sign). Allowing 0 adds a qualitatively different choice—omission—that cannot be mimicked by any scale of \{-1,1\} without altering other weights or introducing additional structure.

The paper explicitly notes that adding the value 0 strengthens modeling capability because of this explicit support for feature filtering, and it documents that BitNet b1.58 (ternary) can match FP16 baselines starting from model sizes of 3B parameters when trained under the same conditions. That empirical fact supports the intuition that zeros are not only computationally convenient but also beneficial for learning.

# Integer-add-only matrix multiplication and multiplier elimination

One of the most striking computational consequences of ternary weights is that the elementwise multiply in the inner loop of a matrix multiplication becomes trivial: when a weight is \(+1\) the multiply is an identity, when it is \(-1\) the multiply is a sign flip, and when it is \(0\) the multiply is a skip. Concretely, for a single output coordinate
\[
\widetilde{W}_{i} a_{i} =
\begin{cases}
a_{i}, & \widetilde{W}_{i}=+1,\\[4pt]
0, & \widetilde{W}_{i}=0,\\[4pt]
- a_{i}, & \widetilde{W}_{i}=-1.
\end{cases}
\]
Thus the high-volume work of the multiply–accumulate becomes a sequence of integer additions and subtractions (and skipping zeros), rather than a stream of general-purpose multiplications followed by additions.

The implications are twofold. First, hardware multipliers are expensive in area, latency, and energy compared to adders; replacing a majority of multiplications by sign operations and additions substantially reduces arithmetic energy per operation. The paper quantifies this benefit: focusing on matrix multiplication (the dominant cost in Transformer LLMs), BitNet b1.58 reduces the arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication by a large factor—the paper reports a savings of about 71.4 times on a 7 nm process using the cited energy model. Second, because the inner loop is simpler and uses lower-bit operands (activations are 8-bit in BitNet b1.58), the memory bandwidth and on-chip storage requirements fall: more weights and activations can be kept on-chip or transferred with lower bandwidth, reducing DRAM/SRAM transfer costs and thus latency.

There is one important implementation detail tied to the scaling factor \(\gamma\). The quantization function in the paper computes
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\biggl(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\,-1,\,1\biggr),
\qquad
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
\]
Because \(\widetilde{W}\) is obtained by dividing by \(\gamma+\epsilon\) before rounding, the original real-valued weight is approximated by \((\gamma+\epsilon)\widetilde{W}\). Practically, this means the high-volume inner loop uses integer-add-only arithmetic to form the sum \(\sum_{i} \widetilde{W}_{i} a_{i}\), and then a relatively cheap (low-volume) scaling by \(\gamma+\epsilon\) is applied to the accumulated output. The bulk of the heavy work—per-element operations across huge matrices—is add/sub/skip; the few scalar multiplies by \(\gamma\) do not dominate runtime or energy.

Empirical runtime and memory measurements in the paper corroborate this reasoning. For the same training token budget and model configuration, BitNet b1.58 reaches parity with FP16 LLaMA at 3B and offers orders-of-magnitude improvements in latency and memory as model size increases (for example, 2.71× faster and 3.55× less GPU memory at 3B; 4.1× speedup at 70B). These trends align with the intuition that eliminating the per-element multiply yields larger payoffs as the fraction of time spent in large linear layers grows with model size.

# Training challenges and the role of straight-through estimators (STE)

Designing and optimizing networks with discrete weights introduces training challenges that are not present in ordinary full-precision training. The BitNet b1.58 quantization function contains non-differentiable operations: rounding and clipping. Specifically,
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\biggl(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\,-1,\,1\biggr)
\]
uses \(\mathrm{round}(\cdot)\) and \(\max/\min\) functions that are not differentiable in the standard sense. When training from scratch with such a quantization map, naive backpropagation through the rounding operation will yield zero gradients almost everywhere, preventing learning.

A common approach in the quantization literature to overcome this is to use a surrogate gradient during backpropagation. One widely used surrogate is the straight-through estimator (STE): in the forward pass the discrete operation (for example rounding) is used exactly, but in the backward pass the gradient is propagated as if the rounding were the identity function (or some other smooth approximation). In other words, if the forward quantization is \(q(x) = \mathrm{round}(x)\), STE replaces \(\partial q / \partial x\) with \(\partial \tilde{q} / \partial x\) where \(\tilde{q}(x)\) is the identity or another differentiable surrogate. The result is a lightweight approximation that provides nonzero gradients and allows training to proceed.

The paper does not provide an exhaustive algorithmic description of the gradient estimator used, but it does emphasize that BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations and that the activation quantization is arranged to simplify implementation: activations are scaled to the symmetric range \([-Q_{b}, Q_{b}]\) per token, avoiding a nonzero “zero-point” offset. This per-token symmetric scaling reduces the complications of asymmetric quantization and makes the forward and backward behavior more regular. The combination of symmetric activation quantization and the per-matrix scale \(\gamma\) for weights forms a training-friendly scaffolding for learning with discrete weights.

Beyond surrogate gradients, training such networks reliably typically requires attention to initialization, learning rates, and the balance between the continuous auxiliary variables (such as \(\gamma\)) and the discrete cores. The paper’s empirical success—matching FP16 performance at scale—indicates these engineering details can be managed effectively, even if the precise recipe (for example, whether STE or a different estimator is used) is not spelled out in the excerpt.

# Error sources: quantization error vs model capacity

When replacing real-valued weights \(W\) by quantized counterparts \(\widetilde{W}\) one introduces approximation errors. From the quantization definition in the paper we can write a simple dequantization approximation
\[
\widehat{W} \approx (\gamma + \epsilon)\widetilde{W},
\]
so the per-weight quantization error is
\[
e_{ij} = W_{ij} - (\gamma + \epsilon)\widetilde{W}_{ij}.
\]
These per-parameter errors propagate through linear layers: for an output coordinate \(j\) with activations \(a\), the difference between the true pre-activation
\[
y_{j} = \sum_{i} W_{ji} a_{i}
\]
and the quantized pre-activation
\[
\widehat{y}_{j} = (\gamma + \epsilon)\sum_{i} \widetilde{W}_{ji} a_{i}
\]
is
\[
y_{j} - \widehat{y}_{j} = \sum_{i} e_{ji} a_{i}.
\]
Thus quantization error acts like structured noise on the linear map and can perturb downstream activations.

Two broad mechanisms determine how harmful this noise is to final task performance:

1. The magnitude and correlation structure of the quantization error. If the quantization scheme (scales, clipping, rounding rules) keeps the expected magnitude of \(e_{ij}\) small and distributes the error benignly across neurons, the network can tolerate the approximation.

2. The model’s capacity and redundancy. Larger networks have more degrees of freedom to represent the same function even with coarse weights; they can learn representations that are robust to (or even exploit) the discrete weight structure. Empirically the paper demonstrates precisely this second effect: BitNet b1.58 models match FP16 performance starting at 3B parameters and improve parity as size grows. This suggests that above a certain scale, capacity and training data can compensate for the reduced per-parameter precision.

There is also a trade in the design choices that affects these error sources. Allowing the zero value in weights reduces quantization error in scenarios where the best small-magnitude real weight would be closer to zero than to \(\pm 1\); mapping such a weight to \(0\) is often less damaging than forcing it to \(\pm 1\). Similarly, the choice to quantize activations to symmetric ranges \([-Q_{b}, Q_{b}]\) per token avoids introducing nonzero zero-points that could create systematic biases in the propagated errors.

Finally, other axes like number of training tokens and model architecture interact with quantization effects. The paper reports that training a BitNet b1.58 model on 2T tokens following a particular data recipe yields superior zero-shot results compared to a strong open-source 3B baseline, showing that token budget and data processing also matter for overcoming or leveraging quantization-induced noise.

# Closing remarks

Ternary networks like BitNet b1.58 sit at a compelling point in the design space: they combine extremely low per-weight storage (about \(\log_{2}3\) bits per parameter) with per-matrix scaling and quantized activations to preserve modeling power. The explicit inclusion of the zero weight adds the important capability of feature filtering, enabling sparsity and gating behavior that pure \(\pm 1\) schemes cannot express. On the computational side, reducing the inner product to integer adds, sign flips, and skips eliminates the need for most elementwise multipliers, yielding large practical reductions in latency, memory bandwidth, and arithmetic energy; the paper reports substantial empirical speedups, memory reductions, and energy savings across model sizes.

From a learning-theoretic perspective, quantization introduces approximation error that must be managed. The two levers for doing so are (a) algorithmic: training strategies that cope with non-differentiable quantization (for which surrogate gradients like STE are commonly applied in the literature) and careful activation/scale design, and (b) scale: larger models and more training data can compensate for the coarse per-parameter precision. BitNet b1.58’s experimental results—matching FP16 baselines at 3B and becoming increasingly favorable at larger scales—illustrate how these levers can be combined in practice.

Understanding these mechanisms—how discrete parameter alphabets trade off information content and hardware cost, how zeros enable feature filtering, how inner-loop arithmetic simplifies to integer-adds, and how training and scale interact with quantization error—prepares you to read and evaluate the growing literature on extreme quantization and its implications for model design, systems, and hardware.

The BitNet Family and Its Computation Paradigm

# BitNet overview: replacing nn.Linear with BitLinear

BitNet is a family of Transformer designs that makes a single, radical change to the usual Transformer building block: every standard dense linear layer (the common software primitive often exposed as `nn.Linear`) is replaced by a quantized variant called BitLinear. In conventional Transformers the dominant cost—both in compute and memory—comes from those dense matrix multiplications performed in floating point (FP16 / BF16 in modern models). BitLinear rewrites that cost profile by storing weights in a low-bit, sign-oriented format and by arranging the computation so that the heavy inner products are computed with integer additions rather than floating-point multiplications.

The concrete quantization rule used in the BitNet family (and in the BitNet b1.58 variant) is simple and systematic. For a weight matrix $W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ BitNet computes a per-matrix scale
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|
\]
and then quantizes each entry by first dividing by this average absolute value and then rounding and clipping into the ternary set $\{-1,0,1\}$:
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\Big(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},-1,1\Big),
\qquad
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x))).
\]
The small constant $\epsilon$ is used for numerical stability. Because $\widetilde{W}$ takes values only in $\{-1,0,1\}$, a multiply by a weight turns into either adding the activation, subtracting it, or skipping it entirely. The original BitNet used a two-valued weight set (effectively $\{+1,-1\}$); BitNet b1.58 inserts a third value $0$ (hence “b1.58” for a ternary representation) which explicitly enables feature filtering by allowing exact zeros in the weight matrix.

Activations are quantized in a compatible way: BitNet b1.58 uses 8-bit activations, but importantly the activations are scaled per token into a symmetric integer range $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ (this removes the need for zero-point offsets). The combination of ternary weights and per-token symmetric 8-bit activations is the key enabler of BitLinear: the dense linear transform can be implemented with integer-add-centric kernels rather than dense floating-point multiply–accumulate operations.

# Dataflow for add-centric matrix multiplication

To understand the run-time dataflow of a BitLinear layer, consider the usual dense product $y = W x$. After quantization we approximate $W$ by $(\gamma+\epsilon)\widetilde{W}$, so the product is
\[
y \approx (\gamma+\epsilon)\,\big(\widetilde{W}\,x\big).
\]
If the input activation vector $x$ has been quantized (and scaled per token) to an integer vector $\tilde{x}$, then the core integer computation is
\[
s = \widetilde{W}\,\tilde{x},\qquad s_i = \sum_j \widetilde{W}_{ij}\,\tilde{x}_j.
\]
Because each $\widetilde{W}_{ij}\in\{-1,0,1\}$, each inner product reduces algebraically to a sum and a subtraction:
\[
s_i = \sum_{j:\,\widetilde{W}_{ij}=1}\tilde{x}_j - \sum_{j:\,\widetilde{W}_{ij}=-1}\tilde{x}_j.
\]
This expression is purely integer addition and subtraction plus index selection; no per-element multiplications by arbitrary values are required. The final floating-point scaling by $(\gamma+\epsilon)$ (and any activation-scale used during quantization) is applied after the integer accumulation, which means that the large majority of elementwise work becomes integer accumulation and the expensive per-element FP multiply is essentially eliminated at the inner-product level.

From an implementation point of view this lets the runtime perform two classes of operations cheaply and in parallel: gather-and-add over positions where the weight is $+1$, gather-and-subtract over positions where the weight is $-1$, and completely skip positions where the weight is $0$. Practically this can be implemented as two integer-accumulation streams (one for positive positions, one for negative positions) and a final subtraction, or by bitmask-based kernels that exploit the ternary structure. Because the activations are symmetrically quantized per token, there is no additional zero-point correction to be applied to the integer accumulators, which simplifies the dataflow and the kernel logic.

The paper integrates an optimized low-bit kernel (a 2-bit kernel from an external codebase) into a high-performance inference stack (FasterTransformer) to realize these integer-add-centric matrix multiplications. The upshot is that the expensive multiply–accumulate loops that used to dominate both latency and energy are replaced by much cheaper integer additions, with only one coarser-grained scaling step remaining per output.

# Memory footprint and bandwidth savings with low-bit weights

One of the strongest system-level consequences of moving the bulk of weight storage to a low-bit representation is immediate reduction of memory footprint and the bandwidth required to stream weights from off-chip memory. BitNet b1.58 stores weights in a ternary form instead of 16-bit floating point, and activations in 8-bit rather than 16-bit. The paper reports substantial end-to-end resource improvements that follow from this compression.

Concretely, when both BitNet b1.58 and a reproduced FP16 LLaMA model are trained under the same conditions (same model sizes and same 100B-token pretraining on RedPajama), the BitNet variant achieves large runtime improvements and memory reductions. For example, BitNet b1.58 at 3B parameters is reported as 2.71× faster at inference and uses 3.55× less GPU memory than the FP16 baseline; a 3.9B BitNet model is 2.4× faster and 3.32× lower-memory while outperforming the 3B FP16 model. As models grow, the benefits increase: the paper reports a 70B-parameter BitNet b1.58 being 4.1× faster than the FP16 LLaMA 70B baseline. Throughput results shown for two 80 GB A100 GPUs indicate that BitNet b1.58 70B can support up to 11× the batch size of the FP16 baseline in the same memory footprint, yielding an 8.9× higher throughput under the evaluated conditions.

Two reasons drive these improvements. First, storing weights and activations with fewer bits reduces the DRAM bandwidth needed to load them, which is a major bottleneck at inference time. Second, the compute kernels themselves are simpler (integer additions instead of FP multiply-accumulates), which reduces latency and energy per operation. The paper also notes that the embedding table is kept full precision in BitNet b1.58; because embedding memory is a smaller fraction of total memory as models get larger, the relative memory savings of low-bit weights and activations become larger at scale. Finally, reducing activation precision (for example, KV caches in attention) from 16 bits to 8 bits directly reduces cache memory and enables longer native context lengths for a given hardware budget; the authors point out that such activations could be further compressed in future work.

# Comparing original 1-bit BitNet and the b1.58 variant

The original BitNet architecture was built around weights that effectively take two values (sign-only, e.g., $\{\!+1,-1\!\}$) and activations quantized to low precision, turning dense matrix multiplications into sequences of additions and subtractions. BitNet b1.58 extends that idea by inserting a third weight value, $0$, producing ternary weights $\{-1,0,1\}$. The inclusion of exact zeros is not merely a storage trick: it enables explicit feature filtering, allowing a weight to suppress a contribution entirely rather than forcing a sign flip. According to the paper, that explicit zero value strengthens modeling ability and helps BitNet b1.58 match full-precision baselines at practical model scales.

BitNet b1.58 retains the core system advantages of the original BitNet: most of the matrix multiplication work reduces to integer additions (the same compute paradigm), the arithmetic energy profile is overwhelmingly dominated by cheap integer additions, and memory and latency costs are significantly reduced relative to FP16 baselines. The paper reports that BitNet b1.58 "matches the full-precision Transformer LLM with the same model size and training tokens in terms of both perplexity and end-task performance" when models are large enough (matching begins at about 3B parameters in their experiments) while enjoying the runtime and energy benefits. In other words, by adding a zero state the designers improved expressivity without surrendering the primary system advantages.

On the architectural side BitNet b1.58 uses LLaMA-compatible components—RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings—and removes biases so that the model can be integrated with popular open-source tooling with minimal changes. Training is performed from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations; the quantization function applied to weights is the absmean-based rounding described above, and activations are quantized symmetrically per token into $[-Q_b,Q_b]$.

# Where multiplications still remain (if any) and system implications

Although BitLinear moves the vast majority of expensive multiplications out of the inner loops of matrix multiplication, a small number of multiplications remain in the end-to-end computation graph and in system-level bookkeeping. The most important such multiply is the coarse-grained reconstruction scaling that converts the integer accumulator into a floating-point approximation of the original dense product. Using the notation above, after computing the integer accumulation $s=\widetilde{W}\,\tilde{x}$ the layer typically applies the shared per-matrix scale $(\gamma+\epsilon)$ (and any per-token activation scale) to convert to the floating-point domain:
\[
y \approx (\gamma+\epsilon)\,s.
\]
That multiplication is applied at the granularity of outputs (for example, one multiply per output feature or per block) instead of per weight, so the number of multiplies is drastically smaller than in a conventional dense multiply–accumulate implementation.

Other multiplications remain in non-linear or auxiliary components of the Transformer block that are not replaced by BitLinear. BitNet b1.58 adopts LLaMA-alike components such as RMSNorm and SwiGLU, and these layers involve elementwise multiplies, scaling, and normalization operations. Attention softmax, rotary embeddings, and embedding matrix lookups also require floating-point math; in the reported implementation, the embedding table is left in full precision. The paper summarizes the net effect succinctly: the majority of BitNet b1.58’s arithmetic is INT8 additions, whereas the FP16 baseline contains both FP16 additions and FP16 multiplications. Because matrix multiplication dominates FLOPs as model size grows, reducing per-multiply cost yields increasingly large relative benefits for larger models.

The system implications of this shift are substantial. Replacing large numbers of FP multiplies with integer adds lowers arithmetic energy consumption for the heaviest kernels—an energy-model-based estimate presented in the paper reports a 71.4× reduction in arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7 nm chips. Reduced memory footprint and bandwidth means fewer DRAM transfers, smaller KV cache usage for long-context inference, and the possibility of fitting larger models (or larger parts of a model) entirely on-chip—improving throughput and lowering latency. These savings also change software and hardware trade-offs: fewer devices are required to host a model (important for techniques such as Mixture-of-Experts), inter-chip communication can be reduced, and specialized hardware that targets integer-add-centric LLM computation becomes an attractive engineering path. The paper explicitly calls out the potential for new hardware optimizations and notes that the 2-bit kernel from Ladder and the FasterTransformer integration are concrete system-level steps toward realizing the add-centric dataflow at scale.

In summary, BitLinear replaces the per-element FP multiply–accumulate machinery of dense layers with integer gather-and-add patterns plus a small number of coarse-grained scalings. That reorganization preserves model expressivity (especially after adding the zero state in b1.58), reduces memory and bandwidth demands, improves latency and throughput in practice, and shifts the system design point toward integer-add-centric kernels and accelerators optimized for the new computation paradigm.

BitNet b1.58 Quantization Mechanics

This chapter explains the quantization functions used in BitNet b1.58 and the practical reasons they work well in large Transformer LLMs. BitNet b1.58 trains from scratch with ternary weights taking values in $\{-1,0,+1\}$ (hence "1.58-bit" since $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$) and 8-bit activations. The design choices—an absolute-mean scaling for weights, a simple RoundClip operator, a small stability term, and per-token symmetric activation scaling—are chosen to be both implementable and effective in practice. Below we unpack each component, give the exact formulas used in the model, and develop the intuition for why these choices preserve model capacity while making inference and training much cheaper.

# Absmean weight quantization: scaling by average absolute value $\gamma$

The core idea for weight quantization in BitNet b1.58 is to normalize the raw floating-point weight matrix by a robust measure of its scale before discretizing. Concretely, for a weight matrix $W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ BitNet computes the scalar
$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|.
$$
This is the mean absolute value (the per-element $L_1$ mean) of the matrix. The quantized weight matrix $\widetilde{W}$ is then obtained by dividing $W$ by $\gamma$ (plus a small $\epsilon$; see the next section) and applying the ternary rounding/clipping operator:
$$
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\Big(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\,-1,\,1\Big).
$$

Why scale by the mean absolute value? Dividing by $\gamma$ centers the matrix around a predictable scale so that the subsequent rounding to $\{-1,0,+1\}$ produces meaningful relative choices rather than being dominated by arbitrary global magnitudes. Using the average absolute value (the L1 mean) is a simple, single-number normalization that is robust to the sign structure of the matrix and less sensitive to a few extreme outliers than, for example, a simple max-based scale. Because $\gamma$ is a scalar per weight matrix, this approach is easy to implement and cheap to compute during training and inference, and it keeps the quantization decision local to each matrix rather than requiring costly per-channel bookkeeping.

Intuitively, after dividing by $\gamma$ many weights will fall in a typical range around zero: those with magnitude much smaller than $\gamma$ will be rounded to $0$, those of order $\gamma$ will be rounded to $\pm 1$. Thus $\gamma$ effectively sets the "threshold" between keeping a connection (mapped to $\pm 1$) and filtering it out (mapped to $0$). This behavior underlies the claimed benefit that ternary weights explicitly support feature filtering.

# RoundClip operator and clipping to \{-1, 0, +1\}

BitNet uses a simple, deterministic operator to turn a scaled continuous value into a discrete ternary value. The operator is written in the paper as
$$
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max\!\big(a,\;\min\!\big(b,\;\mathrm{round}(x)\big)\big),
$$
so that the full weight quantization is
$$
\widetilde{W}_{ij} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\Big(\frac{W_{ij}}{\gamma + \epsilon},\,-1,\,1\Big).
$$

This operator first rounds to the nearest integer and then clips to the allowed interval $[a,b]$. For BitNet b1.58 the allowed integers are $-1,0,1$. The combination of rounding and clipping is intentionally simple: rounding maps to the nearest discrete level, and clipping ensures saturating bounds so that large scaled values are still representable in the target ternary set. Because the scheme is matrix-wise, it imposes the same relative decision boundary across all entries of a given matrix.

Two important properties follow. First, the resulting weight entries are exactly ternary, which means they can be stored and processed in highly compact and efficient integer form; each weight’s entropy is $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ bits, which motivates the "1.58 bits" terminology. Second, the presence of the $0$ level (unlike a pure $\pm 1$ binary) enables explicit feature filtering: a zero weight exactly removes a connection, which both reduces computation at inference when implemented with sparse-aware kernels and increases representational flexibility during training because the model can choose to drop weak signals rather than always flip sign.

# Role of $\epsilon$ in $\gamma + \epsilon$ for numerical stability

The denominator $\gamma + \epsilon$ appears in the scaling step; $\epsilon$ is a small positive constant added to protect the division. Two practical issues motivate this addition:

1. If a weight matrix has very small mean absolute value (for a pathological initialization or transient state), dividing by $\gamma$ alone could produce very large scaled values, causing almost all entries to be clipped to $\pm 1$ and destabilizing training. The $\epsilon$ term prevents such uncontrolled amplification.

2. If $\gamma$ were exactly zero (which can only happen if every weight is exactly zero), division would be undefined; $\epsilon$ makes the formula well-defined for all inputs.

In short, $\epsilon$ acts as a floor on the effective scale. It is chosen small so that under typical magnitudes the scale is essentially $\gamma$, but it prevents extreme or undefined behavior in degenerate cases. The paper does not prescribe a specific numeric $\epsilon$ here; implementers normally use a tiny constant (machine-dependent) so that the floor only activates when $\gamma$ is tiny compared to normal weight magnitudes.

# Activation quantization: per-token scaling to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$

For activations, BitNet b1.58 departs from some common per-tensor or per-channel schemes and instead scales activations "per token" into a symmetric integer range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, where $Q_b$ denotes the integer bound associated with the chosen activation bitwidth (BitNet uses 8-bit activations). The paper states that, unlike the original BitNet implementation that scaled activations to $[0,Q_b]$ before nonlinearities, BitNet b1.58 scales them to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ on a per-token basis.

Per-token scaling means that for each token position (across the model’s feature dimension) a local scale is computed and then applied to the activations at that token so that the quantized representation fits the symmetric integer range. Conceptually, if $a_{t}$ is the vector of activations for token $t$, a per-token quantization can be written generically as
$$
\widetilde{a}_{t} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\Big(\frac{a_t}{s_t},\,-Q_b,\,Q_b\Big),
$$
with a token-specific scale $s_t>0$ chosen so that the mapped integers lie within $[-Q_b,Q_b]$. The exact form for $s_t$ is an implementation detail, but the core idea is to adapt the dynamic range to each token independently.

Per-token scaling reduces quantization error that would arise if a single global scale had to accommodate tokens with very different magnitudes. Because transformer activations vary substantially across tokens and across sequence positions, adapting the scale per token keeps the quantized integers informative and reduces clipping-induced distortion. This localized scaling is simple to compute at inference for streaming generation and introduces minimal bookkeeping: a single scale value per token.

# Eliminating zero-point quantization for activations

One operation commonly used in asymmetric quantization is the zero-point: an integer offset that maps the real value $0$ to some integer code in the quantized domain. BitNet b1.58 deliberately avoids zero-point quantization for activations by choosing a symmetric integer range $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ so that real zero maps naturally to integer zero. The paper emphasizes that this choice "gets rid of the zero-point quantization" and that doing so is "more convenient and simple for both implementation and system-level optimization," while producing only negligible performance differences in their experiments.

Eliminating the zero-point simplifies arithmetic: integer representations become signed symmetric and there is no need to add or subtract a per-tensor (or per-channel) offset when performing integer matrix multiply–accumulate operations. This simplification reduces implementation complexity, reduces the bookkeeping (no per-tensor zero-point to store and apply), and makes it easier to design efficient system-level kernels because integer arithmetic can be performed in symmetric signed ranges without additional correction terms. In practice the BitNet b1.58 results show that this simplification does not meaningfully harm model quality for the tested models and sizes.

# Implications for gradient flow and training stability

BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with these quantization rules (ternary weights and 8-bit activations) and, according to the reported experiments, matches full-precision baselines starting from the 3B size. This empirical result is the strongest evidence that the chosen quantization mechanics are compatible with stable training.

From the design point of view, several aspects of the quantization scheme support stable gradients during training:

- Matrix-wise absmean scaling ($\gamma$) normalizes each weight matrix to a stable scale before rounding. By keeping the pre-quantization magnitudes in a predictable range, the quantizer reduces the chance that small changes in floating-point weights produce wildly disproportionate discrete changes after rounding. In other words, the scaling aligns the continuous optimization space with the discrete codebook $\{-1,0,1\}$ so that gradient steps are less likely to be catastrophic.

- The inclusion of a $0$ level in the ternary alphabet provides an explicit, sparse option for weak connections. This can act as an implicit regularizer: rather than being forced to choose a sign for every weight, the optimizer can zero out connections that are not helpful. That sparsity tendency can reduce variance in activations and gradients and help with optimization, particularly in large networks with many weak connections.

- Per-token symmetric activation scaling reduces quantization-induced noise in the forward pass by adapting the integer dynamic range to local activation magnitudes. Lower forward-pass noise translates to more meaningful gradient signals during backpropagation, improving convergence behavior compared to a coarse global activation scale.

It is important to note that the rounding operation is not differentiable in the strict mathematical sense, and the paper does not supply the implementation detail of how gradients are routed through the discrete quantization steps. The empirical claim—successful from-scratch training and parity with FP16 at sufficient scale—shows that the overall recipe is trainable in practice, but implementers should be aware that a gradient-handling strategy (for example, a surrogate gradient or other practical technique) is necessary for backpropagation across quantized operators. The paper focuses on the high-level quantization design and empirical outcomes rather than the low-level gradient estimator implementation.

# Why 8-bit activations strike a good balance for LLMs

BitNet b1.58 uses 8-bit activations together with 1.58-bit (ternary) weights. The paper reports that this configuration is a pragmatic compromise: it cuts activation memory in half relative to 16-bit activations while preserving model quality across a wide range of model sizes and tasks. A few practical advantages are highlighted in the paper:

- Memory and context-length trade-off. Reducing activations from 16 bits to 8 bits frees up activation memory and bandwidth, which is especially valuable for transformers where KV caches grow with sequence length. The paper notes that moving from 16-bit to 8-bit activations "allows the context length to be doubled given the same resources," an important systems-level benefit for long-context use cases.

- Maintain performance while enabling efficient integer kernels. Empirically, BitNet b1.58 finds that 8-bit activations together with ternary weights produce negligible performance degradation compared to FP16 models at sufficient model scale. The 8-bit activations fit naturally with integer-dominated computation if the weight side is ternary (mostly sign flips and add operations), enabling highly efficient integer kernels that reduce latency, memory bandwidth and energy.

- Simpler activation quantization and implementation. With the per-token symmetric scaling and the removal of zero-point offsets, 8-bit activations are straightforward to encode, transfer, and use in accelerator-friendly integer arithmetic without extra offset-correction in the inner loops.

In short, 8-bit activations provide a favorable point on the trade-off curve: substantial system and cost advantages with little measurable harm to model quality in the reported experiments.

Conclusion

BitNet b1.58’s quantization mechanics are deliberately simple: normalize each weight matrix by its mean absolute value, round and clip to $\{-1,0,1\}$ (with a small stability floor), and quantize activations per token into a symmetric integer range with 8-bit precision. Each component is chosen for implementability (few extra bookkeeping variables, symmetric integer ranges avoid zero-point offsets), numerical robustness (the $\epsilon$ floor and matrix-wise normalization), and empirical effectiveness (training-from-scratch parity with FP16 for sufficiently large models). Together these rules make a compact, hardware-friendly representation that preserves modeling power (the extra $0$ level supports feature filtering) while enabling large practical savings in memory, latency, and energy.

Architectural Choices: LLaMA-alike Components

BitNet b1.58 intentionally adopts the "LLaMA-alike" set of architectural primitives (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and bias-free layers) so that the ultra-low-bit quantization recipe (ternary weights $\{-1,0,1\}$, 8-bit activations, per-token activation scaling, and the absmean quantizer) can be deployed with minimal engineering friction in the open-source ecosystem. This chapter explains each of those LLaMA-like components, shows how they are used in BitNet b1.58, and describes how they interact with the paper's quantization choices (in particular the absmean quantizer and the per-token activation scaling to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$). Wherever the paper provides explicit equations we reproduce them; for standard component definitions we give the common mathematical forms so you can see how these pieces compose in a quantized Transformer.

The paper's weight quantization (the "absmean" quantizer) is central to how the LLaMA-alike components are used. The quantizer for a weight matrix $W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ is written in the paper as
$
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\big(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\big),
$
with
$
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x)))
$
and the scale
$
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
$
Activations are quantized to 8 bits with a per-token scaling to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ (the paper emphasizes this per-token symmetric scaling to remove zero-point handling). BitLinear replaces standard $\texttt{nn.Linear}$ so that core matrix multiplications use the ternary weights; other components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE) are chosen to match LLaMA design and to work alongside these quantization choices.

# RMSNorm and its stability benefits

BitNet b1.58 adopts RMSNorm as one of its normalization layers, following the LLaMA-alike design choices. In form, a standard RMSNorm acting on a hidden vector $x\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is commonly written as
$
\mathrm{RMSNorm}(x) = \frac{x}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^d x_i^2} + \epsilon}\odot g,
$
where $g\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is a learned per-dimension scale and $\epsilon$ is a small constant to stabilize the denominator. (The paper does not reproduce this formula but explicitly states that RMSNorm is used as a LLaMA-alike component.)

Two points from the paper connect RMSNorm to the quantized design. First, RMSNorm is a simple, bias-free normalization that does not rely on per-batch running statistics; that simplicity matches the paper's goal of keeping the architecture compact and aligned with open-source LLaMA backbones. Second, because activations in BitNet b1.58 are deliberately scaled per token into a symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ before non-linearities (to avoid zero-point quantization), a normalization that focuses on controlling the root-mean-square of activations (rather than separately tracking mean and variance) fits cleanly into that pipeline: the normalized outputs have predictable magnitudes which simplify the subsequent fixed-range quantization and the behavior of BitLinear layers. The paper notes that these LLaMA-alike choices (including RMSNorm) are part of why BitNet b1.58 can be integrated into existing open-source stacks with minimal effort.

# SwiGLU in the MLP and compatibility with quantization

BitNet b1.58 keeps the MLP nonlinearity used in LLaMA-style models, namely the SwiGLU variant. The SwiGLU block is usually implemented by splitting a linear projection into two halves and applying a gated nonlinearity; in conventional notation, given input $x$ and linear projections $W_1,W_2$:
$
\mathrm{SwiGLU}(x) = (W_1 x) \odot \mathrm{SiLU}(W_2 x),
$
and the MLP then projects this gated activation back with an output projection $W_o$. SwiGLU replaces older single-branch activations with a multiplicative gating that tends to improve model expressivity per parameter.

In BitNet b1.58 this gated MLP structure composes with ternary BitLinear layers (the paper replaces $\texttt{nn.Linear}$ with BitLinear everywhere weights are quantized to $\{-1,0,1\}$) and 8-bit activations. Importantly, the paper highlights that adding the zero value into the ternary weight set gives "explicit support for feature filtering" — that is, weight zeros allow certain features to be turned off. A gated nonlinearity such as SwiGLU is naturally compatible with this filtering: one branch of the gate can be attenuated or zeroed by ternary weights, which interacts usefully with the other branch's SiLU nonlinearity to provide fine-grained selective passing of features even when weight precision is extremely low. The paper trains these blocks from scratch with the absmean quantizer and reports that this configuration (SwiGLU with ternary BitLinear and 8-bit activations, plus per-token symmetric scaling) matches full-precision baselines at sufficient model scale.

From an implementation viewpoint the paper's choice to scale activations to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token before nonlinearities means the SwiGLU gating and SiLU evaluation operate on bounded, symmetric inputs; that simplifies fixed-point or low-bit implementations of the nonlinearity (and avoids asymmetric zero-point complications), which the authors found convenient for both implementation and system-level optimization.

# Rotary positional embeddings (RoPE)

BitNet b1.58 follows the LLaMA-alike choice of rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) for encoding token positions. RoPE applies a fixed, position-dependent rotation to pairs of dimensions in query/key representations so that relative position information is encoded multiplicatively into the attention dot-products. In compact mathematical form, for a position $p$ and a pairwise chunk of the vector $(u_{2k},u_{2k+1})$, the rotation by angle $\phi_{k} p$ is:
$
\begin{pmatrix} u'_{2k} \\ u'_{2k+1}\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix} \cos(\phi_k p) & -\sin(\phi_k p) \\ \sin(\phi_k p) & \cos(\phi_k p) \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} u_{2k} \\ u_{2k+1}\end{pmatrix}.
$
(The paper does not reproduce this formula but lists rotary embedding as one of the LLaMA components adopted.)

RoPE operates on the continuous query/key vectors that are produced by linear projections; in BitNet b1.58 those projections are implemented via BitLinear for the weight matrices when weights are ternary, while the activations themselves are represented in 8-bit with per-token symmetric scaling. Because RoPE is a norm-preserving orthogonal rotation, applying it to a bounded, symmetric fixed-point representation preserves the norm structure of the vectors (up to quantization error). The paper explicitly moved to symmetric per-token scaling $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ for activations "to get rid of the zero-point quantization," and that decision harmonizes with RoPE: the rotation does not introduce asymmetric offsets that would interact poorly with a symmetric quantization range. In short, using RoPE together with per-token symmetric activation scaling and ternary weights lets BitNet b1.58 keep the positional encoding machinery of LLaMA while staying within the ultra-low-bit computation paradigm the paper advocates.

# Bias-free layers to simplify quantized compute

One explicit LLaMA-alike choice in BitNet b1.58 is to remove all biases in linear and projection layers: the paper states that the model "uses RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding, and removes all biases." This bias-free design is part of the LLaMA architecture family and is carried into the ternary-weight BitLinear implementation.

Removing biases has a few practical consequences emphasized by the paper's design goals. Bias vectors are additional parameter tensors that would need to be represented, loaded, and (if quantized) handled with their own scale and zero-point considerations; by dropping biases entirely the implementation and the quantized inference kernels are simpler. The paper explicitly frames these LLaMA-like choices (including being bias-free) as enabling "minimal efforts" to integrate BitNet b1.58 into popular open-source software. In the context of the paper's absmean quantization and symmetric per-token activation scaling, a bias-free pipeline reduces the number of asymmetric offsets and per-parameter exceptions the system needs to handle, simplifying both software and optimized kernels (for example, the paper integrates a 2-bit kernel from Ladder into FasterTransformer for inference measurements).

# Embedding precision and its share of memory footprint

The paper notes that the token embedding stays full precision (FP16/BF16) in BitNet b1.58, and that its share of the model memory decreases as model size increases: "the embedding remains full precision and its memory proportion is smaller for larger models." Keeping embeddings in full precision is a pragmatic design choice visible in the paper's memory/latency analysis: the large memory and latency gains from ternary weights and 8-bit activations accrue mostly from the many large linear layers (which are replaced by BitLinear), while the relatively small embedding matrix remains a full-precision part of the footprint.

Two practical implications reported or discussed in the paper follow. First, because embeddings are still in higher precision, the absolute memory savings are less dramatic at very small model sizes where the embedding can be a non-negligible fraction of parameters, but the savings grow as the Transformer body (the many projection matrices) dominates the parameter count. This is consistent with the paper's empirical finding that BitNet b1.58's speed-up and memory reduction increase with model size (e.g., the 70B model shows higher relative gains than smaller models). Second, in the inference context the paper highlights KV cache memory as a bottleneck for long-context inference; BitNet b1.58 reduces activation precision (16-bit $\rightarrow$ 8-bit) which directly affects KV cache size and allows larger context lengths for a given memory budget. The paper mentions that the 8-bit activations "allow the context length to be doubled given the same resources" and that further compression remains possible as future work.

# Software compatibility: Hugging Face, vLLM, llama.cpp integration considerations

A practical objective of BitNet b1.58 is to be usable within the existing open-source LLaMA ecosystem. The paper explicitly states that by adopting LLaMA-alike components — RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE, and being bias-free — the model "can be integrated into the popular open-source software (e.g., Huggingface, vLLM, and llama.cpp) with minimal efforts." There are a few concrete engineering touch points implied by the paper that a practitioner should expect when bringing BitNet b1.58 into these frameworks.

First, the core linear operators must be replaced with the BitLinear implementation that encodes ternary weights using the paper's absmean quantizer. This requires support for loading ternary weight tensors (often stored compactly) and for invoking specialized integer-addition-dominated kernels at inference. The paper reports that for latency measurements it used NVIDIA's FasterTransformer codebase and integrated a 2-bit kernel from Ladder to run BitNet b1.58 efficiently; this demonstrates that end-to-end speedups rely on specialized low-bit kernels and not just on parameter-format changes.

Second, activations are quantized to 8 bits with per-token symmetric scaling to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ before non-linearities. Practically, that means integration code must implement that per-token scaling step consistently around the non-linear layers (SwiGLU, attention softmax inputs after RoPE, etc.). The paper emphasizes that this design choice "is more convenient and simple for both implementation and system-level optimization, while introduces negligible effects to the performance in our experiments," which is an important operational guideline for implementers.

Finally, because the model adheres to LLaMA conventions (same normalization, activation shape, positional embedding flavor, and bias-free layers), most of the model-loading and tooling that expects LLaMA-style architectures can be reused; what changes are the linear kernels (BitLinear vs. $\texttt{nn.Linear}$), the weight formats (ternary with absmean scaling), and the activation quantization hooks. The paper's integration into FasterTransformer with Ladder kernels and its claim of minimal effort integration into Hugging Face, vLLM, and llama.cpp reflect that these are the main engineering tasks rather than wholesale API redesign.

Conclusion

BitNet b1.58 combines a ternary-weight, 8-bit-activation quantization recipe (the absmean quantizer and per-token symmetric activation scaling) with LLaMA-alike architectural primitives (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE, and bias-free layers) so that the low-bit computation paradigm can be realized without abandoning the widely used LLaMA backbone. The LLaMA-like choices make the model fit into existing software ecosystems, and—according to the paper—work harmoniously with the quantization design: RMSNorm and symmetric per-token scaling provide predictable activation magnitudes for quantization, SwiGLU's gated structure pairs well with ternary weights (including zero for feature filtering), RoPE remains usable with bounded activations, and removing biases simplifies quantized kernel design. Together these choices enable the practical performance and system advantages reported in the paper (matching FP16 baselines at scale while substantially reducing memory, latency, and energy cost).

Training BitNet b1.58 from Scratch

# Training with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations from initialization

BitNet b1.58 is a quantized Transformer that is trained from scratch with ternary weights and low-bit activations: every parameter (weight) is constrained to the set \{-1, 0, +1\} (the ternary alphabet), while activations are represented in 8 bits. Concretely, the architecture is a LLaMA-like Transformer where every standard linear layer (i.e., every nn.Linear) is replaced by a BitLinear implementation that applies the ternary weight constraint. Training from scratch (rather than applying post-training quantization) is a core choice in the paper: post-training quantization is labeled as sub-optimal and the reported parity with FP16 baselines is achieved by baking quantization into the model during training.

The quantization that enforces ternary weights in BitNet b1.58 is expressed in the paper as an "absmean" quantizer. For a weight matrix \(W\) of size \(n\times m\) the forward quantization is:

\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\,-1,\,1\right),
\]

\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))),
\]

\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|.
\]

This is a compact, per-matrix scheme: the scalar \(\gamma\) is the mean absolute value of the entries in \(W\), used to normalize the matrix so that typical magnitudes lie near the ternary thresholds. The small \(\epsilon\) avoids division-by-zero. After scaling by \(\gamma+\epsilon\), each entry is rounded to the nearest integer and clipped to \(-1\) or \(+1\) (or becomes \(0\) if it rounds to zero). During training this mapping constrains the representable weight values to the ternary set; the paper emphasizes that including \(0\) (the ternary extension of the original 1-bit design) improves modeling capability by explicitly supporting feature filtering (weights can completely suppress contributions by being zero).

Activations follow the BitNet implementation except for one important change in BitNet b1.58: instead of scaling activations before nonlinearities into \([0, Q_b]\) (which introduces a zero-point), activations are scaled per token to a symmetric range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\). This removes the zero-point quantization, is simpler for implementation and system-level optimization, and the authors report negligible performance impact from this change. Practically, this means activations keep symmetric ranges that are straightforward to map to signed 8-bit integers per token.

Architectural details adopted from the LLaMA family are also part of the training setup: BitNet b1.58 uses RMSNorm, the SwiGLU feed-forward nonlinearity, rotary position embeddings (RoPE), and removes all biases. These LLaMA-alike components make the model compatible with common open-source tooling (Huggingface, vLLM, llama.cpp) and are the backbone for the experiments reported in the paper.

# Data and token budgets: RedPajama 100B tokens; scaling to 2T tokens

The paper’s baseline training experiments were run on RedPajama data with a 100 billion token training budget; this was the comparison point against a reproduced FP16 LLaMA baseline trained under the same tokens and model size. Using the same token budget and pretraining corpus yields a fair apples-to-apples measure of how the 1.58-bit parameterization affects modeling quality (perplexity) and downstream zero-shot performance.

To probe scalability with respect to token budget, the authors also trained a BitNet b1.58 variant using a 2 trillion token budget following the StableLM-3B data recipe. That longer training run was evaluated on a compact end-task benchmark (Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, ARC-easy), and the paper reports that BitNet b1.58 at 2T tokens achieved superior end-task accuracy compared to the StableLM 3B baseline at 2T tokens. The experiments therefore show that BitNet b1.58 is compatible with large token budgets and benefits from extended training in the same way as full-precision models.

Two practical implications from the paper’s experiments are worth stressing. First, token budget matters: training longer (to 2T tokens in the paper’s experiment) improves generalization for these low-bit models. Second, parity with full-precision baselines emerges starting at moderate model size: BitNet b1.58 is reported to match FP16 LLaMA performance beginning at the 3B parameter size when trained under the same configuration and token budget.

# Initialization and calibration concerns for quantized models

Quantization introduces calibration questions that do not appear in full-precision training. The paper’s design decisions address these concerns explicitly:

- Per-matrix scaling via the absmean factor \(\gamma\) is the primary calibration mechanism for weights. By dividing a weight matrix by its average absolute magnitude, the quantizer normalizes matrices that may have different dynamic ranges so that the ternary thresholds make sense for each matrix independently. This avoids having a single global scale that would under- or over-saturate many layers.

- The small \(\epsilon\) in \(\gamma+\epsilon\) is an implementation detail that prevents division-by-zero and keeps the normalized values stable when a matrix contains extremely small magnitudes.

- For activations, the switch to symmetric per-token scaling to \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) avoids zero-point quantization. Zero-point quantization (mapping to \([0,Q_b]\)) introduces offsets which complicate integer arithmetic and often adds implementation complexity; by keeping activations symmetric and scaling per token, the authors simplify the quantization pipeline and keep activation statistics well-centered for signed integer representations.

- Embeddings are treated as full precision in the paper’s memory accounting and implementations (the paper notes that the embedding remains full precision and its memory proportion shrinks for larger models). Keeping embeddings full precision is a conservative choice: embeddings are sensitive to small numeric shifts and keeping them in higher precision helps preserve representational fidelity, especially for smaller models or early in training.

When implementing these calibration steps, the two practical checks emphasized by the paper are: (1) compute and verify \(\gamma\) per weight matrix and inspect the pre-rounded distribution \(W/(\gamma+\epsilon)\) to ensure it occupies the range where meaningful ternary rounding occurs (i.e., you get a mix of \(-1,0,1\) rather than everything collapsing to the same integer), and (2) confirm that activations are quantized with symmetric per-token scaling so that zero-point artifacts are eliminated.

# Stability tactics: gradient management, clipping, and regularization

The BitNet b1.58 paper does not present a new, explicit recipe of gradient clipping thresholds, optimizer hyperparameters, or specialized regularizers; instead, the reported approach to training stability is dominated by architectural and quantization design choices and by the decision to train with quantized weights from the start (quantization-aware training) rather than applying a post hoc quantizer.

Concretely, the stability-related design elements described in the paper are:

- Bake quantization into training. The paper stresses that post-training quantization (PTQ) is sub-optimal; stability and final performance require that the model learn with the quantized representation active during training.

- Use the absmean quantizer that normalizes by \(\gamma\) (the mean absolute value) so that each weight matrix self-calibrates its quantization thresholds. This reduces the chance that layers either saturate to \(\pm 1\) or collapse to \(0\).

- Include \(0\) in the weight alphabet. Allowing weights to be exactly zero is reported to strengthen modeling capability by enabling explicit filtering: parameters can learn to suppress entire input features rather than merely flipping their sign.

- Adopt LLaMA-like building blocks (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE) and remove biases. The paper frames these choices as part of the overall architecture; they also help make BitNet b1.58 compatible with widely-used open-source toolchains.

Because the paper focuses on these design-level stability tactics rather than on low-level optimizer recipes, practitioners aiming to reproduce the results should prioritize implementing the quantizer and per-token activation scaling correctly, use the LLaMA-like architecture choices in the paper, and train with the quantized weights active. The paper demonstrates that these choices together suffice for stable training across the model sizes reported (3B up to 70B) and for long-token budgets (up to the 2T experiment).

# Checkpoints, reproducibility, and evaluation cadence

The paper’s experimental setup contains several reproducibility-friendly choices and concrete evaluation protocols that you should emulate if you want to reproduce the results:

- Dataset and token budget: the main comparison is trained on the RedPajama dataset for 100B tokens. The 2T-token experiment followed the StableLM-3B data recipe. Fixing the dataset and token budget is central to fair comparisons.

- Architecture and components: BitNet b1.58 adopts LLaMA-like components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings) and removes biases. Using the same architectural primitives ensures behavioral parity and easier integration with existing toolchains.

- Quantization implementation: use the absmean quantization for weights (the \(\gamma\) formula and RoundClip) and symmetric per-token activation quantization to \([-Q_b,Q_b]\) as described. These are the key implementation details that define BitNet b1.58.

- Evaluation metrics and tooling: validation perplexity was reported on WikiText2 and C4; zero-shot evaluation used standard benchmarks (ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, BoolQ) and the authors used the lm-evaluation-harness pipeline. For runtime and system measurements, the authors used the FasterTransformer codebase (integrated with a Ladder 2-bit kernel for BitNet b1.58) and reported "time per output token" as the primary latency figure. Throughput experiments used pipeline parallelism on pairs of 80GB A100 GPUs for the 70B models.

If your goal is reproducibility, follow the same chain: implement the quantizer exactly, use the same datasets and token counts, adopt the LLaMA-like components, and use the same evaluation harnesses and system kernels (FasterTransformer + Ladder 2-bit kernel) to measure latency and memory as in the paper. The paper’s choice to conform to open-source conventions (Huggingface, vLLM, llama.cpp compatibility) further assists reproducibility.

# Common pitfalls when training ultra-low-bit LLMs and how to debug

The paper exposes several pitfalls that are important when adopting an ultra-low-bit training regime and offers the corresponding mitigations that the authors used.

1. Post-training quantization (PTQ) performance drop. The paper explicitly points out that PTQ is sub-optimal. If you quantize a fully-trained full-precision model post hoc, expect degraded perplexity or downstream performance. The remedy in the paper is to perform quantization-aware training (i.e., train with the quantizer active), as BitNet b1.58 does.

2. Incorrect weight scaling causes collapse. If your per-matrix scale \(\gamma\) is computed incorrectly or omitted, the pre-rounded values \(W/(\gamma+\epsilon)\) may lie far outside the intended range, causing most values to map to the same ternary value (e.g., all zeros or all \(\pm 1\)). Debugging step: inspect histograms of \(W/(\gamma+\epsilon)\) for representative layers during training; a healthy histogram should show spread around \(-1,0,1\), not a single spike.

3. Zero-point activation artifacts. Using asymmetric activation quantization with a zero point (i.e., mapping into \([0,Q_b]\)) introduces offsets that complicate integer arithmetic and system integration. The paper avoids this by scaling activations per token to the symmetric range \([-Q_b,Q_b]\). If you see implementation- or performance-related surprises, confirm that activation scaling is symmetric per token as described.

4. Embeddings and small-model sensitivity. The paper treats embeddings as full precision and notes that their memory fraction is smaller for larger models. If you quantize embeddings aggressively, small models (e.g., below 3B) are more likely to suffer. Debugging step: keep embeddings in full precision and validate whether performance changes.

5. Implementation and kernel mismatch for inference. The reported runtime, memory, and throughput gains depend on using an optimized 2-bit kernel (the Ladder kernel) and FasterTransformer. If your inference numbers are far off the paper’s, ensure you are using comparable kernels and that BitLinear/bit kernels integrate correctly with the runtime. Reproduce the pipeline-parallel setup (if applicable) when measuring large-model throughput.

6. Expect scale-dependent behavior. In the paper, BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 baselines starting at 3B; below that scale you may see gaps. When trying new model sizes, be prepared to validate whether the same parity holds.

7. KV cache and long-context memory. The paper notes that reducing activation bits (from 16 to 8 in their implementation) directly reduces KV cache memory for decoding. If you plan long-context decoding, confirm that activation quantization is applied to the KV cache as intended; this reduces context memory and allows longer native sequence lengths.

Concrete debugging checklist (grounded in what the paper reports):

- During training, log and inspect distributions of \(W\), \(W/(\gamma+\epsilon)\), and rounded \(\widetilde{W}\) for several layers to ensure a healthy ternary distribution.

- Verify per-token activation scaling to \([-Q_b,Q_b]\) is implemented and that activations do not carry an unintended offset.

- Keep embeddings in full precision and confirm memory accounting (paper uses full-precision embeddings).

- Evaluate validation perplexity (WikiText2, C4) during training and final zero-shot performance (ARC, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, BoolQ) using the lm-evaluation-harness to compare against the numbers reported in the paper.

- For inference benchmarking, use FasterTransformer and the Ladder 2-bit kernel as in the paper and measure "time per output token" and peak GPU memory to replicate latency and memory claims.

# Summary and practical checklist (as implemented in the paper)

The paper’s training recipe for BitNet b1.58 can be summarized into a concise checklist for reproduction and practical engineering:

- Model design: Replace nn.Linear with BitLinear using ternary weights \(\{-1,0,+1\}\). Use LLaMA-like components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE) and remove biases.

- Weight quantization: Implement the absmean quantizer with
  \(\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|\) and
  \(\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},-1,1\right)\).

- Activation quantization: Scale activations per token to \([-Q_b,Q_b]\) and quantize to 8-bit signed integers (no zero point).

- Embeddings: Keep embeddings full precision (as in the paper’s memory accounting).

- Training regime: Train from scratch with the quantizer active (quantization-aware training); use RedPajama 100B tokens for the principal experiments and adopt larger budgets (e.g., 2T tokens using the StableLM recipe) to probe long-token benefits.

- Evaluation: Track validation perplexity on WikiText2 and C4; run zero-shot evaluations using lm-evaluation-harness on the standard benchmarks listed in the paper. For system metrics, measure time per output token and peak GPU memory using FasterTransformer and the Ladder 2-bit kernel.

- Debugging: Inspect pre-rounded weight distributions and activation distributions, confirm symmetric per-token activation scaling, and ensure kernel-level integration for inference.

Following the steps and checks above (all drawn from the BitNet b1.58 paper) yields the recipe used by the authors to obtain parity with FP16 LLaMA baselines starting at the 3B scale while gaining large improvements in runtime, memory, throughput, and energy. The paper’s experiments also show that the approach scales to much larger token budgets (2T) and larger parameter counts (up to 70B), making BitNet b1.58 a practical and reproducible model family for training ultra-low-bit LLMs.

Evaluation Methodology and Benchmarks

This chapter explains the evaluation suite and measurement protocols the paper uses to compare BitNet b1.58 against reproduced FP16 LLaMA baselines. The authors designed a set of model-level and system-level measurements to judge both modeling quality (perplexity and zero-shot task accuracy) and deployment cost (runtime memory, latency, throughput, and energy). For fairness, the primary comparison models were pretrained on the same corpus (RedPajama) for 100 billion tokens; evaluations then ran across multiple model sizes (examples in the paper include 3B, 3.9B, 7B, 13B and 70B). The experiments combine standard NLP evaluation tooling (lm-evaluation-harness), an optimized GPU inference stack (FasterTransformer), and a 2-bit kernel integration (Ladder) for BitNet b1.58 inference kernels. The energy analysis focuses on arithmetic-operation energy of matrix multiplications using a 7nm energy model from the cited references.

# Perplexity on WikiText-2 and C4

Perplexity on held-out corpora is the paper's primary modeling-quality scalar. The authors report validation perplexity on WikiText-2 and on C4, using the models that were pretrained for 100B tokens on RedPajama. These two validation sets are used to capture next-token predictive performance at different scales of text data (the paper reports both datasets' perplexities as the standard held-out measurements).

BitNet b1.58 uses ternary weights and 8-bit activations (details below), and the reported perplexities demonstrate its ability to match FP16 LLaMA models at sufficient scale. Concretely, the paper emphasizes that BitNet b1.58 begins to match the full-precision LLaMA baseline in perplexity at the 3B model size, and that a 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model not only matches but outperforms a 3B FP16 LLaMA baseline in perplexity while achieving major system-efficiency gains. These perplexity measurements serve as a check that the drastic quantization does not irreparably damage the model's base language modeling capability when trained under the reported recipe.

# Zero-shot tasks and lm-evaluation-harness pipeline

To evaluate downstream generalization, the paper uses zero-shot evaluation on a set of established NLP tasks. All zero-shot evaluations follow the lm-evaluation-harness pipeline to ensure consistency and reproducibility: the authors "followed the pipeline from lm-evaluation-harness" to run the benchmark suite. Using a common evaluation harness reduces variability introduced by prompt formatting, scoring conventions, and result aggregation, so differences are attributable to model behavior and not tooling mismatches.

The zero-shot results reported in the paper show that the performance gap between BitNet b1.58 and the FP16 LLaMA baseline narrows as model size increases. The authors note that BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision end-task performance starting at the 3B size, mirroring the perplexity observation. These zero-shot evaluations complement perplexity: while perplexity measures next-token modeling, zero-shot task accuracy assesses how that modeling ability transfers to concrete reasoning and QA-style tasks without task-specific fine-tuning.

# Task suite: ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenBookQA, BoolQ

The core zero-shot task suite used for comparisons includes ARC-Easy and ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenBookQA, and BoolQ. The paper reports zero-shot accuracy on these benchmarks as representative end-task measures; the evaluation run is the one mentioned above via lm-evaluation-harness, so the same harness and scoring code are used across both BitNet b1.58 and FP16 LLaMA models. Using a multi-task suite provides a broader picture of generalization beyond a single benchmark, ensuring that reported parity in one metric (e.g., perplexity) aligns with real-task performance.

In addition to the main 100B-token experiments, the authors also trained a BitNet b1.58 model with a larger 2T-token recipe (following the StableLM-3B data recipe) and evaluated it on a slightly different set of end tasks (Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, and ARC-easy). For this 2T-token experiment the paper reports that BitNet b1.58 achieved superior zero-shot performance relative to the StableLM 3B baseline trained on the same 2T tokens, indicating that the ternary-weight model continues to generalize strongly even with much more training data.

# Runtime memory and latency measurement with FasterTransformer

System-level runtime cost was measured using the FasterTransformer codebase. FasterTransformer is an inference-optimized library for transformer models on GPUs; the authors used it to measure both runtime GPU memory consumption and latency. Latency is reported primarily as time per output token (see the dedicated section below), and GPU memory measurements are reported alongside to capture the inference memory footprint.

These FasterTransformer-based measurements are the basis for the paper's principal system claims. For example, the paper reports that at the 3B size BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 LLaMA in perplexity while being 2.71 times faster and using 3.55 times less GPU memory. A 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model is reported as 2.4 times faster and consuming 3.32 times less GPU memory than LLaMA 3B, while achieving better modeling performance. As model size increases, the paper reports larger speedups: when scaled up to 70B, BitNet b1.58 is reported as 4.1 times faster than the FP16 LLaMA baseline. The authors attribute these gains to the fact that the dominant compute kernel in Transformers is the dense linear (nn.Linear) matrix multiplication, whose time grows with model size; by changing the compute paradigm used for those linear layers, BitNet b1.58 reduces that dominating cost.

The FasterTransformer measurements reported in the paper were made with an integrated 2-bit kernel (from Ladder) for BitNet b1.58, which the paper treats as part of the optimized inference stack. Because the experiments used this specialized kernel, the authors note there is further room for optimization at the kernel and system level.

# Using the Ladder 2-bit kernel for BitNet b1.58

To enable efficient inference for the ternary-weight model, the BitNet b1.58 implementation used a 2-bit kernel from Ladder integrated into the FasterTransformer measurement pipeline. The Ladder 2-bit kernel replaces the standard dense kernels with an implementation that better matches the low-bit representation and arithmetic of BitNet b1.58; this kernel is therefore central to achieving the reported runtime and memory numbers.

By integrating the Ladder kernel for the latency and memory measurements, the paper reports realistic performance figures for a production-grade inference stack rather than unoptimized or purely academic kernels. The authors emphasize that measurements using the 2-bit kernel show substantial gains already, and that additional kernel-level and hardware-level optimizations remain possible to further lower latency and memory beyond what is reported.

# Time per output token as the primary latency metric

The authors choose "time per output token" as the primary latency metric for inference. They explicitly state that time per output token is "the major cost for inference" in autoregressive generation workloads, and thus it is the appropriate focal metric when comparing runtime efficiency. This metric measures how long it takes the system to produce a single generated token at inference time under the chosen inference pipeline and kernel configuration.

Using per-token generation time emphasizes the dominant cost in practical interactive and streaming scenarios where models are used to generate sequences step-by-step. The paper links this metric to model scale: because the computation cost of nn.Linear (dense matrix multiplication) grows with model size, per-token time is particularly sensitive to how linear layers are implemented. BitNet b1.58's alternative compute paradigm reduces the per-token cost increasingly as model size grows, which explains the observed larger relative speedups at 13B and 70B compared to smaller sizes.

# Throughput setup: two 80GB A100 GPUs, pipeline parallelism, sequence length 512

For throughput measurements on the largest scale, the paper evaluates 70B-parameter models on two 80GB A100 GPUs. Pipeline parallelism is used so that the 70B FP16 LLaMA baseline can be executed on the pair of devices; throughput experiments then push both models (BitNet b1.58 70B and FP16 LLaMA 70B) until GPU memory limits are reached. The authors fix the sequence length at 512 and increase the batch size incrementally until the available GPU memory is exhausted; throughput is then compared at those maxima.

Under this setup, the paper reports that BitNet b1.58 70B can support up to 11 times the batch size of the FP16 LLaMA 70B baseline, leading to an 8.9× higher throughput under the measured conditions. The throughput experiment therefore demonstrates how the reduced memory footprint of BitNet b1.58 not only lowers latency for single-stream generation but also directly translates into higher batch throughput when memory capacity is the limiting resource.

# Energy estimation approach and 7nm energy model references

Energy estimation in the paper focuses on the arithmetic-operation energy consumption of matrix multiplications because matrix multiply operations account for the dominant arithmetic cost in transformer inference. The authors compute an energy composition for the core arithmetic: for BitNet b1.58 the majority of matrix-multiplication arithmetic becomes INT8 addition, whereas for FP16 LLaMA the arithmetic mix includes both FP16 additions and FP16 multiplications. The energy accounting uses the cited energy models from the references (denoted in the paper as the energy model in [energycost, pokebnn]) and targets a 7nm process technology for per-operation energy estimates.

Using that energy model, the paper reports a dramatic reduction in arithmetic-operation energy for matrix multiplication: BitNet b1.58 saves 71.4 times the arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips compared with the FP16 baseline. The authors also provide end-to-end energy cost numbers for models processing 512 tokens (reported alongside latency and memory results), and they observe that BitNet b1.58 becomes relatively more energy-efficient as model size increases. The rationale is the same as for latency: the proportion of compute and energy consumed by nn.Linear grows with model size, so replacing the expensive FP16 multiply-adds with lower-bit additions yields larger fractional savings at larger scales.

The paper explicitly states that the energy analysis focuses mainly on arithmetic operations for matrix multiplication because of its dominant contribution to cost; the energy model citations are used to convert operation counts and types into energy estimates appropriate for a 7nm silicon process.

# Putting the pieces together: what the evaluation protocol guarantees

Taken together, the evaluation methodology in the paper mixes modeling and systems measurements with careful controls to make the comparisons meaningful. Models were pretrained on the same data for the same token budget (100B tokens) when comparing modeling metrics; zero-shot evaluations ran through a shared, community-standard harness (lm-evaluation-harness); runtime and memory use were measured with a production-oriented GPU inference stack (FasterTransformer) with a Ladder 2-bit kernel integrated for BitNet b1.58; throughput used a hardware-conscious setup (two 80GB A100s, pipeline parallelism, sequence length 512) and increased batch size until memory limits; and energy estimates use a referenced 7nm energy model focused on the dominating matrix-multiplication arithmetic.

These choices together ensure that the paper's claims about matching FP16 modeling performance (from about 3B up) and Pareto improvements in latency, memory, throughput, and arithmetic energy consumption are grounded in comparable model training and fair, realistic system measurements.

Results and Analysis: Accuracy, Cost, and Scaling

# Parity with FP16 from 3B parameters on perplexity and end tasks

One of the central empirical findings reported in the paper is that BitNet b1.58 — a Transformer trained from scratch with ternary weights \{-1,0,1\} (i.e., "$1.58$"-bit weights) and 8-bit activations — reaches parity with a reproduced FP16 LLaMA-style baseline when both models are trained with the same configuration and the same number of tokens. Concretely, when both models are pre-trained on the RedPajama corpus for $100$ billion tokens, BitNet b1.58 matches the FP16 baseline in validation perplexity (measured on held-out text such as WikiText2 and C4) and in zero-shot accuracy on a broad set of language tasks, starting at model sizes of about $3\mathrm{B}$ parameters.

This parity is important for two reasons. First, it shows that the extreme reduction in weight precision (from FP16 to ternary) does not, by itself, condemn model quality: with the right architecture choices and training-from-scratch recipe (including the absmean quantization, per-token activation scaling, and LLaMA-like components such as RMSNorm and SwiGLU), the model can learn to use ternary weights without losing predictive performance. Second, it establishes a practical threshold: the paper’s experiments indicate that at around $3\mathrm{B}$ parameters and above, BitNet b1.58 can be used as a drop-in, lower-cost alternative to a conventional FP16 LLaMA-style model while maintaining comparable perplexity and downstream zero-shot behavior.

Intuitively, the combination of (a) training from scratch with quantized weights rather than post-training quantization, (b) an extra weight value of $0$ (so weights are ternary, enabling explicit feature filtering), and (c) 8-bit activations gives enough expressive and optimization flexibility for larger models to close the gap with FP16. The empirical result—matching FP16 on both intrinsic and extrinsic metrics—validates that the design choices are sufficient to preserve model quality at practical sizes.

# 3.9B BitNet b1.58 vs 3B FP16: better accuracy with lower latency and memory

Beyond parity at equal sizes, the experiments show a stronger result: a BitNet b1.58 model with $3.9\mathrm{B}$ parameters outperforms a FP16 LLaMA-style model of $3\mathrm{B}$ parameters on downstream tasks, while also consuming fewer resources. The paper reports that the $3.9\mathrm{B}$ BitNet b1.58 model is $2.4\times$ faster and uses $3.32\times$ less GPU memory than the FP16 LLaMA $3\mathrm{B}$ baseline, and yet achieves significantly better end-task performance.

This comparison illustrates a practical trade unlocked by the quantization: because each parameter in BitNet b1.58 is stored and processed at effectively $1.58$ bits, you can increase the parameter count (here from $3\mathrm{B}$ to $3.9\mathrm{B}$) while still lowering wall-clock inference cost and memory use relative to a smaller FP16 model. The extra parameter budget buys modeling capacity (hence better accuracy), while the low-bit representation reduces the per-parameter storage and compute overhead. In other words, BitNet b1.58 moves the practitioner’s design point: for a given resource envelope, one can afford a larger, more accurate model, or for a fixed accuracy target one can substantially lower latency and memory.

Mechanistically, the speed and memory advantages come from two sources emphasized in the paper. First, arithmetic during the dominant matrix multiplications shifts from FP16 multiplications and additions to mostly integer additions (and highly optimized low-bit kernels), which are both cheaper and faster on modern hardware. Second, the bandwidth and capacity demands for weight loading and caching are much smaller, reducing the DRAM-to-SRAM traffic that is often a bottleneck for inference.

# Latency gains: e.g., ~2.71× at 3B; up to ~4.1× at 70B

Latency improvements are among the clearest operational benefits reported. When measuring inference time per output token using an optimized inference stack (FasterTransformer with an integrated 2-bit kernel from Ladder), BitNet b1.58 shows substantial speedups compared to FP16 LLaMA across model scales. For a model around $3\mathrm{B}$ parameters the paper reports a latency reduction of about $2.71\times$. As model size grows, the speed-up increases: by the time models reach $70\mathrm{B}$ parameters, BitNet b1.58 is reported to be approximately $4.1\times$ faster than the FP16 baseline.

Two points explain this scaling of speed-up. First, the time spent in dense linear layers (\texttt{nn.Linear}) grows with model size; these layers are precisely where the BitNet computation paradigm replaces multiplications with cheaper integer operations, so the larger the model, the larger the fraction of work that benefits from the cheaper arithmetic. Second, reduced memory traffic (smaller weights and lower-precision activations) reduces stalls due to bandwidth constraints, which are more pronounced for large models. The measurements in the paper were conservative in the sense that they were obtained using a 2-bit kernel; the authors note there is still room for kernel-level optimization, so real-world speedups could improve further.

# Memory reductions: e.g., ~3.55× at 3B; ~3.32× at 3.9B

Memory usage is a key bottleneck for deployment, and BitNet b1.58 delivers large reductions. For the $3\mathrm{B}$ comparison the paper reports about $3.55\times$ less GPU memory used; for the $3.9\mathrm{B}$ BitNet vs $3\mathrm{B}$ FP16 case the reported memory reduction is about $3.32\times$. These reductions reflect both the compressed storage of model weights (ternary instead of 16-bit floats) and smaller activation/KV cache footprints from using 8-bit activations.

It is useful to separate two rough effects. The first is the per-parameter storage change: FP16 uses $16$ bits per weight while ternary weights require effectively $1.58$ bits on average, so the naive bit-level ratio is about $16 / 1.58 \approx 10.1\times$. In practice the observed GPU-level reductions are smaller ($\sim 3.3$–$3.6\times$) because a real deployed model includes other FP16 components that remain unchanged (for example, embeddings in these experiments were kept in full precision), and because runtime overheads, buffering, optimizer state (during training), and per-layer implementation details consume memory. The second effect is activation size: the KV cache and intermediate activations are halved when moving from $16$-bit to $8$-bit, allowing larger sequence lengths or larger batches within the same memory budget.

Because the embedding layer remains full precision, its relative cost decreases as model size grows; this partly explains why the memory savings tend to increase with model scale. The net result is a large practical reduction in GPU memory pressure, enabling larger batch sizes, longer context lengths, or deployment on smaller devices.

# Throughput gains at 70B: up to ~8.9× with ~11× larger batch

The paper reports a throughput experiment for $70\mathrm{B}$-parameter models on two 80GB A100 GPUs using pipeline parallelism. The procedure increased batch size until the devices hit memory limits (sequence length fixed at 512), and compared the maximum achievable throughput. BitNet b1.58 at $70\mathrm{B}$ could accommodate up to $11\times$ the batch size that the FP16 LLaMA $70\mathrm{B}$ setup could fit, and this translated into approximately an $8.9\times$ higher throughput (tokens processed per second).

This is a practical demonstration of how reduced memory footprint directly converts into higher device utilization. With smaller per-example memory, more examples can be processed in parallel, and pipeline stages can be balanced more effectively. For applications that are batch-friendly (e.g., high-throughput inference services), this kind of throughput advantage can translate into substantial cost savings per token served. The experiment was done under a realistic constraint (two A100s, pipeline parallelism) to show how BitNet b1.58 operates under production-like settings.

# Arithmetic energy savings: ~71.4× for matrix multiplications on 7nm

A striking quantitative claim in the paper concerns arithmetic energy. Using an energy cost model from prior literature, the authors break down the arithmetic energy composition of matrix multiplications and report that, on a $7\,$nm process, BitNet b1.58 can save approximately $71.4\times$ in arithmetic energy for the matrix multiplication component compared to FP16-based LLaMA. The reason is straightforward: FP16 matrix multiplies involve costly floating-point multiplications and additions, whereas the BitNet computation paradigm reduces the dominant operations to integer additions (and works with low-bit representations), which require much less energy per operation on modern silicon.

Two clarifying points: (1) the $71.4\times$ figure refers to arithmetic operation energy for matrix multiplications, which are the dominant FLOP-type in transformers; (2) end-to-end energy savings for a full inference pass (e.g., $512$ tokens) are smaller than $71.4\times$ because there are other components (embedding lookups, attention softmax, miscellaneous overheads) that do not benefit as much from ternary weights. Nevertheless, the paper shows that end-to-end energy efficiency improves with model size, because matrix-multiply-dominated costs take a larger share of total work for larger models. The energy savings therefore compound the latency and memory benefits, yielding a more energy-efficient inference regime overall.

# New scaling law: efficiency equivalences across model sizes and precisions

One of the most consequential conceptual claims in the paper is the articulation of a new scaling relationship between model size, precision, and inference cost. The empirical data lead to equivalence statements of the following form: a larger BitNet b1.58 model is simultaneously faster, smaller in memory, and more energy-efficient than a much smaller FP16 model. The paper summarizes three practical equivalences:

- $13\mathrm{B}$ BitNet b1.58 is more efficient (latency, memory, energy) than $3\mathrm{B}$ FP16.
- $30\mathrm{B}$ BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than $7\mathrm{B}$ FP16.
- $70\mathrm{B}$ BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than $13\mathrm{B}$ FP16.

These equivalences are read as operational rules-of-thumb: by switching to a $1.58$-bit weight representation, you can move to a model that is an order of magnitude larger in parameter count while still reducing (or at least not increasing) inference cost. The practical implication is that the familiar storage/compute budgets used to size models and deployments must be revised: bit-precision becomes a first-class axis of the scaling law, not just parameter count and the number of training tokens.

Put differently, the Pareto frontier of achievable (accuracy, cost) pairs shifts outward when low-bit architectures of this form are used. Instead of deciding between "smaller FP16" or "larger FP16", practitioners can choose "larger b1.58" models that dominate the smaller FP16 models across latency, memory, and energy simultaneously.

# Generalization with 2T tokens: outperforming StableLM-3B on multiple tasks

To test whether these benefits persist when models are trained on much larger token budgets, the authors trained a BitNet b1.58 model following the StableLM-3B data recipe up to $2$ trillion tokens. They then evaluated zero-shot performance on a small benchmark consisting of Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, and ARC-easy. Against the reported results of StableLM-3B trained at $2$T tokens, BitNet b1.58 achieves superior performance on all these end tasks (for tasks with both an accuracy and a normalized score, the reported metric averaged the two).

This experiment underscores that BitNet b1.58 does not merely preserve quality at moderate token budgets; it can also scale in the same way conventional FP16 models scale with extra training data. In other words, the quantized training-from-scratch recipe generalizes to large token regimes: when given large-scale training signals, BitNet b1.58 continues to improve and can outperform an established FP16 model trained under the same token budget. That supports the claim that $1.58$-bit LLMs are a viable path for future large-scale model development rather than a niche technique limited to small experiments.

# Pareto frontier view: simultaneous accuracy and efficiency improvements

Taken together, the results support a simple but powerful claim: BitNet b1.58 produces a Pareto improvement over the FP16 LLaMA-style baselines for model sizes at and above the $3\mathrm{B}$ threshold reported in the paper. In practical terms, points representing BitNet b1.58 in the (accuracy, latency/memory/energy) design space dominate many of the comparable FP16 points—i.e., they achieve the same or higher accuracy while using less time, memory, and energy. The $3.9\mathrm{B}$ vs $3\mathrm{B}$ example is the clearest demonstration: higher accuracy at strictly lower cost.

Viewing results through a Pareto lens is useful when making deployment decisions. Rather than treat precision as a secondary implementation detail, these experiments suggest that precision choices can be used strategically to move to strictly better points on the accuracy-cost frontier. The practical corollaries are numerous: one can allocate budget to more parameters (to raise quality) while saving cost, or maintain model size but lower operational cost, or trade along other axes such as context length and batch size. The paper’s experiments provide concrete data points to guide those choices.

# Closing summary: what these results mean in practice

The empirical story is consistent and actionable:

- At around $3\mathrm{B}$ parameters and above, BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 baselines in perplexity and zero-shot end-task performance.
- At modestly larger parameter counts (for example, $3.9\mathrm{B}$ versus $3\mathrm{B}$), BitNet b1.58 can provide strictly better accuracy while reducing inference latency ($\sim 2.4\times$ faster) and GPU memory ($\sim 3.32\times$ less).
- As models scale, the relative computational and memory advantages of BitNet b1.58 grow: latency speedups increase (up to $\sim 4.1\times$ at $70\mathrm{B}$), throughput capacity increases dramatically (up to $\sim 8.9\times$ throughput and $\sim 11\times$ batch size in a $70\mathrm{B}$ two-GPU experiment), and estimated arithmetic energy for matrix multiplications can fall by a large factor (about $\sim 71.4\times$ on $7\,$nm according to the employed energy model).
- These gains yield a new, empirically grounded scaling perspective: a $13\mathrm{B}$ BitNet b1.58 model is operationally cheaper than a $3\mathrm{B}$ FP16 model (and analogous equivalences hold at larger scales), and BitNet b1.58 continues to scale with large token budgets (e.g., $2$T tokens) showing superior generalization compared to an FP16 baseline trained under the same recipe.

Taken together, these results argue that $1.58$-bit LLMs are not merely a compression trick for inference but constitute a different point in the design space for large language models—one that enables larger models, lower operating costs, and competitive or superior accuracy.

Systems Perspective: Kernels, Memory, and Inference

# DRAM-to-SRAM transfers and bandwidth savings with ternary weights

A dominant cost in inference for large Transformer models is moving parameters from off‑chip DRAM into the on‑chip memory of accelerators (SRAM, caches) and then into compute units. The paper emphasizes that this DRAM→SRAM transfer is “expensive during inference,” both in time (latency) and energy; reducing the amount of data moved therefore directly improves latency and power.

BitNet b1.58 attacks this cost by constraining every parameter to the ternary set \{-1, 0, +1\}. The weight matrix is computed as a scaled, rounded version of the FP weights via the absmean quantization function used in the paper:
$$
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
$$
$$
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\big(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))\big),
$$
$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
$$
Because each stored parameter now takes one of three values, the model’s weight storage and the per‑inference bandwidth needed to load weights are dramatically reduced compared to full‑precision (FP16/BF16) parameters. The paper demonstrates the system implications empirically: at the 3B scale BitNet b1.58 is 2.71× faster and uses 3.55× less GPU memory than the FP16 LLaMA baseline, and at larger scales the gains grow (e.g., BitNet b1.58 70B is 4.1× faster). These measured improvements are exactly the kind of wins one gets when DRAM bandwidth and memory footprint are substantially lowered.

Two additional system consequences follow directly. First, because the cost of weight transfers grows with model size (the time spent in the bulk linear layers increases), the relative benefit of smaller weights increases for larger models; this is why the paper sees larger speedups at larger scales. Second, smaller weight representations relax on‑chip memory design constraints: less SRAM is required to hold working sets, and the time and energy overhead of loading weights from DRAM is reduced, enabling both lower latency and lower inference energy in practice (the paper reports large arithmetic energy savings from this shift). In short, cutting the bits that must transit DRAM into computation is a primary, measurable lever for improving inference at scale in BitNet b1.58.

# INT8 addition–centric kernels and opportunities for operator fusion

A conceptual shift at the kernel level is central to BitNet b1.58. Traditional FP16 Transformer matmuls rely on floating‑point multiply–accumulate operations; BitNet b1.58’s ternary weights and 8‑bit activations let the dominant arithmetic be integer additions rather than FP multiplies. The paper puts this plainly: “the matrix multiplication of BitNet only involves integer addition,” and “the majority of BitNet b1.58 is INT8 addition calculation.” Changing the arithmetic mix from FP multiplies to integer additions has immediate system effects: integer additions are cheaper in energy per operation and can be implemented with simpler datapaths on specialized kernels or hardware.

On an implementation level the paper integrates a low‑bit kernel (the Ladder 2‑bit kernel) into FasterTransformer to bring these savings to practice. That kernel shifts the hot path of nn.Linear away from FP mixed‑precision matmuls and toward low‑precision integer computation, producing the measured latency and memory benefits.

Because the arithmetic and data formats are simpler, there are natural opportunities for operator fusion and kernel consolidation that reduce memory traffic and intermediate conversions. For example, BitNet b1.58 uses per‑token activation scaling to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ (instead of a zero‑point quantization to $[0,Q_b]$), which the paper describes as “more convenient and simple for both implementation and system‑level optimization.” That single design choice reduces the bookkeeping and conversion overhead between quantization and the core matmul. More generally, when kernels operate on small, fixed integer alphabets (here ternary weights and 8‑bit activations), implementers can place scaling, clamping, and accumulation inside the same low‑bit kernel so that fewer full‑precision buffers are read/written. The paper treats these simplifications as part of a “new computation paradigm” enabled by BitNet, and the observed throughput and energy wins are evidence that moving toward addition‑centric, fused integer kernels is a highly practical systems lever.

# Role and limits of the current 2‑bit kernel; avenues for further speedups

In the experiments the authors used the Ladder 2‑bit kernel integrated into FasterTransformer as the production‑level low‑bit matmul implementation for BitNet b1.58. That choice demonstrates the practicality of deploying low‑bit kernels and delivers the quantitative wins reported (reduced latency, memory, energy). The paper also emphasizes that these measurements were made “with a 2‑bit kernel, so there is still room for optimization to further reduce the cost.” That sentence captures two important points for system designers:

1. The current 2‑bit kernel is a pragmatic, high‑quality building block, but it is not necessarily the final kernel design for ternary models. Ladder’s 2‑bit kernel is effective for low‑bit GEMMs in general, yet ternary weights and the per‑token activation scaling used in BitNet b1.58 imply a somewhat different target representation and dataflow that bespoke kernels could exploit further.

2. Some parts of an LLM still remain in higher precision (for example, embeddings are kept full precision in the paper’s implementation), and there are other fixed costs (norms, activation functions, I/O, and control logic) that will limit end‑to‑end speedups until they too are optimized. Thus kernel improvements provide substantial gains but are not the only path to better end‑to‑end performance.

The paper points to natural avenues for additional speedups consistent with its findings: tighter kernels designed specifically for ternary weights and 8‑bit activations; better fusion of scaling/quantization into the matmul path; and system/hardware support specialized for addition‑centric operators. The authors explicitly call for new hardware designs tailored to 1‑bit/ternary LLMs. Practically speaking, therefore, the Ladder 2‑bit kernel is a strong intermediate step that validates the approach, but both software kernel engineering and hardware specialization remain available levers to extract yet more speed and energy efficiency.

# Embedding precision and its diminishing memory share as models scale

One noticeable implementation detail in the BitNet b1.58 design is that embeddings are kept in full precision (FP16/BF16), even while most linear weights are ternary. The paper points out that the embedding “remains full precision and its memory proportion is smaller for larger models.” This is an important system observation.

The vocabulary embedding matrix is often a sizable, but fixed, chunk of model parameters: its size is proportional to vocabulary size × embedding dimension. For smaller models that embedding can be a significant share of memory; for large models, however, the parameter budget is dominated by many large linear layers and attention projections, so the embedding’s fraction of total memory shrinks. The practical upshot in the experiments is that moving all of the linear layers to ternary representations generated large total memory and bandwidth wins even with the embedding unchanged. That makes the tradeoff attractive: keep embeddings full precision to preserve compatibility and a small accuracy cushion, while quantizing the large, repetitive linear layers that dominate compute and transfer costs.

The paper’s measurements show that this choice is sufficient to unlock most of the system gains: latency and memory reduce substantially as model size scales, because the relative weight of full‑precision embeddings becomes negligible compared to the many quantized linear layers.

# KV cache memory and benefits from 8‑bit activations

Autoregressive decoding relies on key/value (KV) caches that store per‑token projections so that attention over the context does not recompute earlier activations. These KV caches are a primary activation memory consumer during inference: their size grows linearly with context length and with the model’s hidden dimension. The BitNet b1.58 design reduces activation precision from 16 bits to 8 bits, and the paper highlights the direct system consequence: halving the number of bits used for the cached activations roughly halves the KV cache memory footprint.

Concretely, the paper states that reducing activations from 16 bits to 8 bits “allows the context length to be doubled given the same resources.” That is a simple, practical trade: with the same memory budget for KV caches, an 8‑bit representation supports roughly twice as many cached tokens as a 16‑bit representation, enabling longer contexts for a given device. The authors also note future potential: these 8‑bit activations could be further compressed (the paper mentions the possibility of lossless compression to 4 bits or lower as future work), which would further expand effective context length or reduce memory pressure.

From a systems perspective, this relationship between activation bitwidth and KV cache size is a high‑leverage knob: lowering activation precision buys either more context length or more batch capacity for the same device memory. BitNet b1.58 therefore directly improves the feasibility of “native” long‑context inference on current hardware, and it creates a favorable path for further compression research focused on KV caches.

# Profiling bottlenecks and practical optimization playbook

The experimental work in the paper supplies a clear, empirically grounded playbook that a systems engineer should follow when porting or optimizing BitNet b1.58 (or similar low‑bit LLMs). Derived from the paper’s methods and observations, the practical sequence is:

1. Use an optimized inference framework and measure time per output token. The paper uses FasterTransformer and reports time per output token as the canonical latency metric; starting from a high‑quality baseline is essential to make fair comparisons.

2. Integrate low‑bit matmul kernels early. The authors integrated Ladder’s 2‑bit kernel into FasterTransformer to implement the low‑precision nn.Linear paths. That integration is a must‑have to move the hot path into integer additions and to realize memory/bandwidth savings.

3. Profile to identify the dominant hotspots. In the experiments nn.Linear grows to dominate runtime as model size increases; quantized matmuls are therefore the first optimization target. The paper’s measured speedups are explained largely by accelerating these linear layers.

4. Reduce memory movement via quantization and per‑token activation scaling. The paper implements an absmean weight quantization and scales activations per token to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ to “get rid of the zero‑point quantization,” which simplifies kernels and reduces extra memory conversions. Implementations should fold scaling/clamping into the low‑bit kernels where possible to avoid writing intermediate full‑precision buffers.

5. Keep a pragmatic stance on full‑precision pieces. Preserve full precision where it matters for compatibility or where the memory share is small (the paper keeps embeddings full precision). As models grow, this conservative choice becomes less costly in memory proportion.

6. Use parallelism to increase throughput while respecting memory. For very large models the authors use pipeline parallelism to place parts of the model across devices and then increase batch size until memory limits are reached. Testing device batching limits produced the throughput numbers in the paper (e.g., BitNet b1.58 70B achieving 11× batch size and 8.9× throughput over FP16 LLaMA on two 80GB A100s).

7. Measure energy via arithmetic models. To reason about energy, the paper applies existing arithmetic energy models and finds very large savings for matrix multiplication (a 71.4× arithmetic energy reduction on 7 nm chips for matmul). Systems teams should similarly include arithmetic energy accounting in their evaluations.

8. Leave room for kernel and hardware co‑design. The paper’s conclusion is explicit: BitNet b1.58 “enables a new computation paradigm and opens the door for designing specific hardware optimized for 1‑bit LLMs.” That means software teams should build on modular kernel interfaces so that future, more specialized kernels or hardware accelerators can be dropped in with minimal system changes.

Following these steps reproduces the paper’s observed wins and points toward further engineering investments that are likely to pay off: better low‑bit kernels, fused operators that reduce memory passes, KV cache compression, and ultimately hardware tailored to addition‑centric low‑bit computation.

# Closing perspective

BitNet b1.58 is a systems‑level story as much as it is an algorithmic one. Quantizing the weight alphabet to \{-1,0,+1\} and using 8‑bit activations shifts the computational and memory profile of Transformers: heavy, expensive FP multiplies become cheaper integer additions; DRAM bandwidth and KV cache pressure are reduced; and latency, memory footprint, throughput, and energy all improve in measured deployments. The paper’s experiments show how these shifts manifest at scale (speedups and memory reductions at 3B and larger, major energy savings for matmul, and much higher throughput for large models). For system designers, the central lessons are clear and actionable: prioritize low‑bit kernels for the matmul hot path, fold quantization and scaling into those kernels to cut memory traffic, treat embeddings pragmatically, and exploit reduced activation precision to extend context or batch sizes. The work also flags remaining system opportunities—better kernels, KV compression, and new hardware—which align naturally with the new computation paradigm BitNet b1.58 introduces.

Extensions and Deployment: MoE, Long Contexts, Edge/Mobile, and Hardware

This chapter explores how the BitNet b1.58 design — i.e., a Transformer with ternary weights $\{-1,0,1\}$ (approximately $1.58$ bits per parameter) and low-bit activations — enables new model architectures and deployment paradigms. The improvements reported for BitNet b1.58 in the paper (matching FP16 LLaMA baselines in perplexity and end-task performance from the 3B scale while reducing latency, memory, throughput, and energy) are not just pointwise gains: they change where the bottlenecks lie for large models and therefore open opportunities in model design (Mixture-of-Experts), support for long contexts, on-device and edge/mobile deployment, and co-designed hardware and software stacks. Each section below elaborates on a specific extension or deployment implication emphasized in the paper, explaining both the technical root causes and the practical consequences.

# 1-bit/1.58-bit Mixture-of-Experts: reducing memory and inter-chip communication

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures trade parameter storage for conditional computation: at inference, only a subset of experts are active per token, which can dramatically reduce FLOPs per token compared to a dense model of the same total parameter count. That efficiency in compute, however, comes with costs that limit practical deployment: the full set of experts must be stored somewhere, and when a model is too large to fit on a single device the routing of activations to the remote experts incurs inter-chip communication overhead and higher memory pressure across the cluster.

BitNet b1.58 directly addresses the two main deployment pain points for MoE that the paper calls out. First, the ternary weight representation substantially reduces the memory footprint of stored experts. With each parameter encoded in the $\{-1,0,1\}$ ternary alphabet (the paper refers to this as a $1.58$-bit representation), fewer devices are required to hold the same total parameter set. Second, when fewer devices hold the model, the volume of activation traffic that must be transferred over the network between chips to route tokens to experts is reduced. The paper explicitly notes that the reduced memory footprint “reduces the number of devices required to deploy MoE models” and “significantly reduces the overhead of transferring activations across networks.” In the limiting case where the entire model fits on a single chip, the inter-chip activation transfer overhead would disappear entirely.

Beyond these qualitative statements, the paper’s measurements show that BitNet b1.58’s benefits grow with model size: the time and memory cost of dense linear layers ($\texttt{nn.Linear}$) become a larger fraction of inference as models scale, so compressing those layers yields larger absolute savings for large models. That same trend applies to MoE: as expert counts and total parameters grow, the memory and bandwidth savings from ternary weights amplify the practical value of MoE by lowering the device-count and communication cost required to achieve its compute efficiency.

# Native support for long sequences via smaller activation and KV caches

One of the main practical constraints on increasing context length for autoregressive LLM inference is the memory used by the key/value (KV) caches. For each decoding step, past token representations (keys and values) must be stored and revisited; the memory cost of these caches grows linearly with context length and is proportional to the precision (bits) used for activations and cached tensors.

BitNet b1.58 reduces activation precision from the common FP16/BF16 to 8-bit activations in its baseline configuration. The paper emphasizes the direct payoff of that reduction: halving activation bit-width from 16 bits to 8 bits allows, in principle, doubling of the context length for a fixed KV cache memory budget. Put another way, because cache memory is proportional to activation bit-width times sequence length, lowering the bit-width provides an immediate multiplier on usable context length. The paper explicitly states that BitNet b1.58’s 8-bit activations “allow the context length to be doubled given the same resources.”

The BitNet design also adopts a per-token activation scaling strategy that simplifies quantization: instead of scaling activations into $[0,Q_b]$ with a zero point, activations are scaled to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ per token to eliminate zero-point quantization and simplify implementation and system-level optimization. This choice is part of the practical engineering that makes lower-bit activations usable in real inference stacks.

Finally, the paper points toward future compression headroom: it notes that the reduced activations can be “further losslessly compressed to 4 bits or even lower for 1.58-bit LLMs,” which would further extend context length capabilities once those compression methods are matured. In short, by shrinking the per-token memory footprint of activations, BitNet b1.58 makes longer contexts much more practical without requiring changes to the core model algorithm.

# Edge and mobile deployment: memory/energy fit and CPU friendliness

Edge and mobile environments are constrained by memory capacity, memory bandwidth, energy, and the predominance of CPUs (rather than large GPU accelerators). The paper argues that BitNet b1.58 can substantially expand the set of feasible LLM deployments on such devices by lowering both memory and energy requirements.

From the energy perspective, a key architectural difference is the change in the dominant arithmetic. The paper highlights that large parts of BitNet’s matrix multiplication can be implemented as integer additions rather than FP16 multiply-adds, and that the majority of BitNet b1.58 compute for matrix multiplication is INT8 additions. Using an energy model the paper cites, BitNet b1.58 achieves a $71.4\times$ savings in arithmetic operations energy for matrix multiplication on $7\,$nm process chips relative to a FP16 baseline. That massive per-operation energy reduction is directly relevant to battery- and thermally-constrained devices.

From the memory and latency side, the paper reports concrete measured inference improvements relative to FP16 LLaMA baselines: for example, BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 perplexity and end-task performance from the $3\,$B scale while being about $2.71\times$ faster and using $3.55\times$ less GPU memory (measured in their FasterTransformer-based evaluation). Larger models show larger speedups: the $70\,$B BitNet b1.58 is reported as $4.1\times$ faster than the $70\,$B FP16 baseline, and throughput experiments show that BitNet b1.58 can support up to $11\times$ the batch size on two 80GB A100 cards, yielding an $8.9\times$ higher throughput in that setup. These numbers are provided in the paper to demonstrate that low-bit model and kernel engineering can translate to tangible deployment gains.

The combination of lower memory use and lower arithmetic energy makes BitNet b1.58 “more friendly to CPU devices,” as stated in the paper. Integer-add-centric computation maps well to general-purpose CPUs because it reduces dependence on high-throughput FP multiply units and on costly memory transfers; combined with the smaller weight and activation representations, this improves the feasibility of running sizable LLMs on constrained devices. The paper also notes that embeddings remain full precision in their implementation, which leaves some residual memory cost; however, embeddings represent a smaller fraction of total memory as model size increases, so the net effect favors large models for which ternary compression yields the biggest gains.

# Prospects for specialized hardware for 1-bit LLMs (e.g., add-centric LPUs)

BitNet b1.58 represents a shift in the dominant low-level operation of the model: from FP multiply–accumulate (the usual target of many existing accelerators) toward integer additions and ternary-weight-aware operations. The paper explicitly frames this as a “new computation paradigm” and calls for hardware and system design that takes advantage of it.

There are two complementary hardware-level opportunities highlighted or implied by the paper. First, hardware that is optimized for add-centric kernels and extremely low-bitweight storage (for example, local processing units or LPUs that favor integer-add throughput and low-bit memory) can amplify the energy and latency advantages that BitNet demonstrates in software. The paper cites Groq as an example of recent hardware work and advocates for similar co-design efforts aimed at $1$-bit and ternary models.

Second, because BitNet shifts the balance of costs, the critical hardware resources migrate: memory bandwidth and on-chip SRAM residency become relatively more important than raw FP multiply throughput. Specialized hardware for 1.58-bit LLMs should therefore emphasize very-low-latency on-chip storage for ternary weights, efficient integer-add datapaths, and tight coupling between quantized memory formats and compute kernels. The paper’s $71.4\times$ arithmetic energy estimate for matrix multiply on $7$nm chips underscores the potential payoffs for hardware optimized for this arithmetic.

The paper also demonstrates that software ecosystems (e.g., FasterTransformer) can already integrate specialized low-bit kernels such as the Ladder 2-bit kernel for inference. This shows a path in which software kernel development and hardware design proceed in parallel: as specialized integer-add kernels mature in software, hardware vendors can target those kernels to achieve new levels of deployment efficiency for large models.

# SRAM vs DRAM trade-offs and on-chip model residency

A recurring systems-level theme in the paper is the cost of moving weights from off-chip memory (DRAM) into on-chip memory (SRAM or an accelerator’s local memory) for inference. DRAM provides higher capacity at lower cost-per-bit, but accessing DRAM costs both time (latency) and energy. Conversely, SRAM and other on-chip memories provide much lower-latency, lower-energy access but are substantially more expensive to provision at large capacities.

The paper observes that enlarging SRAM to reduce DRAM traffic is often prohibitively costly; therefore a practical alternative is to reduce the model’s memory footprint so that a larger fraction (or all) of the model can be held on-chip. BitNet b1.58’s ternary-weight and low-bit activation representation reduces both capacity and bandwidth demands: fewer bits must be transferred from DRAM to on-chip SRAM and fewer bytes must be read repeatedly during inference. In practical terms, the paper shows memory reductions such as $3.55\times$ less GPU memory for a 3B model and $3.32\times$ less memory for a 3.9B configuration (relative to their FP16 LLaMA baselines), demonstrating that the reduced footprint is large enough to change how inference can be provisioned.

When more of a model can be resident in on-chip memory, two important system benefits follow. First, latency per token drops because expensive DRAM transfers are avoided or reduced. Second, energy per token is lowered because on-chip accesses are much cheaper than off-chip accesses. The paper emphasizes both effects as part of the broader argument that 1.58-bit LLMs enable a different deployment frontier — for example, enabling larger models to be run with lower latency and lower power on a single accelerator, or enabling MoE deployments with fewer devices.

It is also important to recall the caveat the paper gives: embedding tables in their implementation remain full precision, which leaves a persistent DRAM/SRAM demand. Nonetheless, because embedding proportion shrinks as the rest of the model scales, the overall benefit from ternary compression becomes stronger for larger models where matrix multiplications dominate memory and compute.

# Ecosystem implications and standardization opportunities

BitNet b1.58 was intentionally engineered to fit into the existing open-source LLM ecosystem and to minimize integration friction. The model uses LLaMA-alike components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and removal of biases) so that it can be integrated with toolchains and runtimes such as HuggingFace, vLLM, and llama.cpp with minimal changes. On the inference side, the paper demonstrates integration with FasterTransformer and includes a Ladder 2-bit kernel. Those choices illustrate an important point: to move from research prototype to widespread adoption, quantized-model designs must align with existing software stacks and kernel ecosystems.

There are several concrete standardization opportunities implied by the paper’s design and engineering decisions. Quantization semantics are one clear target: the paper describes an `absmean` quantization function for ternary weights (scale by the mean absolute weight and then round-and-clip into $\{-1,0,1\}$), which the paper writes as
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\Big(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\Big),
\qquad
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x))),
\qquad
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
\]
Activation quantization choices are another area for common interfaces: the paper departs from a zero-point $[0,Q_b]$ scaling and instead scales activations to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ per token to eliminate zero-point quantization and simplify implementation. Agreeing on canonical scaling conventions, rounding rules, and small constants (e.g., $\epsilon$ in the weight scale) would smooth interoperability across frameworks and hardware.

Kernel and runtime APIs are a third area. The paper’s use of FasterTransformer and a 2-bit Ladder kernel shows that specialized kernels are needed to realize the runtime benefits. Standardized interfaces for low-bit kernels and consistent tensor layouts for ternary weights and low-bit activations would allow runtimes and hardware vendors to plug in optimized primitives without bespoke glue code for each model format.

Finally, the paper’s choice to adopt LLaMA-alike architecture components to ease community integration is itself an ecosystem design decision: standardizing around a common set of architectural conventions (normalization, activation function, positional embeddings, bias handling) makes it easier for quantized models to be dropped into existing evaluation and deployment pipelines. Taken together, these software and format standardization opportunities — quantization semantics, activation scaling, kernel APIs, and architectural conventions — will accelerate adoption and hardware-software co-design for $1$-bit / $1.58$-bit LLMs.

Closing remarks

BitNet b1.58 shows that moving to ternary weights and low-bit activations is not merely an exercise in model compression; it shifts the dominant costs and opens new design and deployment frontiers. The paper documents empirically that from the $3\,$B scale upward BitNet b1.58 can match FP16 LLaMA performance while reducing latency, memory, throughput, and energy; it also lays out promising directions where those gains matter most in practice: enabling more compact MoE deployments, supporting longer native contexts by shrinking KV caches, making LLMs feasible on edge and mobile devices, and motivating hardware specially tuned for add-centric, low-bit computation. Taken together, these points argue for a coordinated software and hardware ecosystem effort — standardized quantization rules, kernel primitives, and hardware support — to realize the full practical potential of $1$-bit and $1.58$-bit LLMs described in the paper.

Practical Implementation and Projects

# Implementing BitLinear and the absmean quantizer in PyTorch

The core change that makes BitNet b1.58 different from a standard Transformer is that every parameter (weight) in its linear layers is constrained to the ternary set \{-1, 0, +1\}. The paper describes a simple, explicit quantizer called the absmean quantizer that converts a full-precision weight matrix \(W\) into these ternary values by first normalizing by the average absolute magnitude and then rounding and clipping. The quantizer is written in the paper as

\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]

\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\!\big(a,\,\min\!\big(b,\,\mathrm{round}(x)\big)\big),
\]

\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
\]

These formulas give you everything you need to implement the weight quantization logic: compute the average absolute value \(\gamma\) for a weight matrix, divide the matrix by \(\gamma\) (with a small \(\epsilon\) to avoid division by zero), round each entry to the nearest integer, and clip to the allowed range \([-1,1]\). The result \(\widetilde{W}\) is a matrix with entries in \{-1,0,1\}.

When you implement a BitLinear module (the BitNet analogue of \texttt{nn.Linear}), you will want to keep two pieces of state per weight matrix: (1) the ternary matrix \(\widetilde{W}\) (stored in an integer-friendly dtype) and (2) the scale \(\gamma\) (a small floating-point scalar per weight matrix). The design in the paper implies this two-part representation: the ternary matrix encodes direction/sign/sparsity while \(\gamma\) preserves magnitude information across the layer. At inference time you can perform integer accumulation with \(\widetilde{W}\) and then apply \(\gamma\) as a single scale to the accumulated outputs; this is the mechanism that reduces the number of floating-point multiplies inside the inner loop. During training the paper trains "from scratch" with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations, so the forward pass uses the quantized weights; the weight-update strategy is a detail not spelled out in the text, but the paper is explicit that final models are trained under these quantization constraints.

From an implementation perspective in PyTorch, the steps are conceptually straightforward and directly follow the equations above: compute \(\gamma\) over the weight tensor, compute \(\widetilde{W}\) via the RoundClip formula, store \(\widetilde{W}\) (as integers or packed ternary storage) and \(\gamma\), and implement the forward product in a way that exploits integer accumulation of \(\widetilde{W}\). Unit tests you should add: verify that \(\widetilde{W}\in\{-1,0,1\}\), verify \(\gamma\) is the mean absolute value, and verify the reconstruction behavior (apply \(\gamma\) to the integer accumulation and check reasonable agreement with full-precision linear outputs). The paper also notes that activations are handled at 8 bits (see the next section), so your BitLinear should accept quantized activations or provide the pathway to apply activation quantization before the matrix multiply.

# Per-token activation scaling to [-Q_b, Q_b] without zero-point

The paper emphasizes a simple activation quantization choice: instead of applying a zero-point offset and scaling to \([0,Q_b]\) before nonlinearities (a scheme used in some quantization pipelines), BitNet b1.58 scales activations per token into a symmetric range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\). The key motivation reported is simplicity of implementation and system-level optimization: symmetric ranges remove the need for a zero-point and keep quantized activations centered around zero, which is convenient for signed ternary weights and for integer accumulation.

The paper states this per-token scheme succinctly: activations are "scaled to \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) per token to get rid of the zero-point quantization." Put intuitively, you treat the activations corresponding to a single token (that is, the feature vector at one token position) as the unit for computing a scale factor. That scale factor normalizes the token's features so they fit the discrete signed range that your activation quantizer uses (the paper uses 8-bit activations). Implementationally, you will associate a small per-token scale with each token activation that is applied before quantizing and stored alongside the quantized activation if you need to recover floating-point values later (for example in cached KV stores).

Working at a token granularity keeps the mapping symmetric and avoids storing or applying a zero-point offset. The paper notes that this choice is "more convenient and simple for both implementation and system-level optimization" and that it "introduces negligible effects to the performance in our experiments." When you implement this, follow the same per-token scaling philosophy the paper describes: compute a scale for each token's activation vector so the quantized values lie in the allowed symmetric range, quantize the token's activation vector, and carry the scale for later dequantization or downstream arithmetic as needed.

A useful implementation detail to keep in mind from the paper is that KV caches and other activation stores become much smaller if activations are 8-bit rather than 16-bit; the paper highlights that this reduction in activation bitwidth materially helps support longer sequences for the same memory budget.

# Integration tips with LLaMA-like stacks (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE, no biases)

BitNet b1.58 deliberately adopts LLaMA-like architectural choices so that integration into popular open-source stacks (HuggingFace, vLLM, llama.cpp) is straightforward. Concretely, the paper specifies four architecture components that you should match to follow their recipe: RMSNorm (instead of LayerNorm), SwiGLU as the feed-forward nonlinearity, rotary positional embeddings (RoPE), and the removal of all biases.

When you refactor an existing LLaMA-like implementation to BitNet b1.58, the primary changes are:

- Replace \texttt{nn.Linear} modules with the BitLinear module described above. This covers attention projections, output projections, and MLP linear layers.
- Keep embeddings in full precision. The paper explicitly points out that embeddings remain in full precision and that, in large models, their memory share is a smaller fraction of the total, so this choice is consistent with the paper's experiments.
- Use RMSNorm where the original LLaMA used LayerNorm; this preserves the LLaMA-alike normalization behavior the paper relies on.
- Use SwiGLU in the MLP blocks and implement rotary embeddings in the attention mechanism as in LLaMA.
- Remove layer biases throughout the model, matching the paper's bias-free design.

Because these choices mirror LLaMA's design, the paper reports that BitNet b1.58 "can be integrated into the popular open-source software (e.g., Huggingface, vLLM, and llama.cpp) with minimal efforts." In practice, that means the scaffolding around tokenization, positional embedding application, and inference pipelines in those ecosystems can remain unchanged; you primarily plug in the BitLinear layer and the per-token activation quantization.

# Running lm-evaluation-harness for zero-shot tasks

The paper evaluates BitNet b1.58 in a standard zero-shot evaluation pipeline built upon the lm-evaluation-harness. They pre-trained models on RedPajama for 100 billion tokens and then measured zero-shot performance on a suite of tasks: ARC-Easy and ARC-Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, and BoolQ. Perplexity was reported on WikiText2 and C4.

To reproduce that evaluation flow, follow the same high-level steps the paper used:

- Ensure your trained BitNet b1.58 model is available in a format that the evaluation harness can call (the paper emphasizes compatibility with common open-source runtimes).
- Use the lm-evaluation-harness pipeline to execute zero-shot evaluations on the listed datasets. The paper followed the harness pipeline exactly and used its standard metrics (accuracy for classification-style tasks and perplexity for language modeling).
- For the experiments reported with larger token budgets (2T tokens), the paper follows the StableLM-3B data recipe and reports averages appropriately (for tasks measured with both accuracy and normalized accuracy they averaged the two).

The paper's results highlight a consistent trend: the performance gap between BitNet b1.58 and full-precision LLaMA narrows as model size increases, and starting at 3B parameters BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision LLaMA in both perplexity and end-task zero-shot performance under the same training-token and model-size conditions.

# Measuring latency and memory with FasterTransformer

Latency and memory were measured in the paper using NVIDIA's FasterTransformer codebase, integrating a 2-bit kernel from Ladder for BitNet b1.58. The main inference metric reported is time per output token, which the authors call "the major cost for inference." Memory is reported as runtime GPU memory consumption during inference.

If you want to reproduce or extend these measurements, follow the measurement choices the paper uses: deploy your inference implementation through FasterTransformer (with the 2-bit Ladder kernel integrated for BitNet b1.58) and report the time-per-output-token and peak GPU memory used for inference. The paper reports concrete comparative outcomes to aim for: at 3B, BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 LLaMA in perplexity while being 2.71× faster and using 3.55× less GPU memory; the 3.9B BitNet b1.58 is 2.4× faster, 3.32× less memory, and performs better than LLaMA 3B. As model size grows, the speedups increase—the paper reports that BitNet b1.58 70B is 4.1× faster than the FP16 LLaMA baseline.

The paper also notes that these measurements were taken with a 2-bit kernel, and that there is room for further system-level optimization. When measuring, it is useful to match the experimental conditions in the paper (same sequence lengths and batching) so comparisons are fair.

# Throughput experiments with pipeline parallelism on A100 GPUs

To measure throughput at large model sizes the paper runs a pipeline-parallel setup across two 80GB A100 GPUs so that FP16 LLaMA 70B can be run for comparison. They used pipeline parallelism (GPipe-style) and increased the batch size until the GPUs reached their memory limit, with sequence length fixed at 512. The paper reports that BitNet b1.58 70B supported up to 11× the batch size that the FP16 LLaMA 70B could, resulting in an 8.9× higher throughput under the same hardware constraints.

If you plan the same experiment, match the paper's choices: use two 80GB A100s, apply pipeline parallelism to distribute the model, fix sequence length to 512, and ramp batch size until memory limits are hit. The key outcome to capture is how many examples the model can process in parallel and the resulting throughput (tokens/sec or sequences/sec). The paper's result shows a large practical throughput advantage for BitNet b1.58 at the 70B scale under these constraints.

# Designing a mini scaling-law study (accuracy vs cost vs batch size)

The paper positions BitNet b1.58 as enabling a new practical scaling law where model performance and inference cost tradeoffs shift in favor of quantized models. To design a mini scaling-law experiment in the spirit of the paper, choose a small grid of model sizes and compare BitNet b1.58 vs FP16 LLaMA along three axes: validation/perplexity and zero-shot accuracy (performance), runtime latency and GPU memory (cost), and throughput as a function of batch size (capacity to service requests).

A minimal experiment that follows the paper would:

- Pick model sizes that the paper studies (examples: 3B, 3.9B, 7B, 13B, 30B, 70B) for both BitNet b1.58 and FP16 LLaMA variants.
- Pre-train (or use pretrained checkpoints) with the same data and number of tokens. The paper uses RedPajama for 100B tokens as the primary training corpus; for a token-scaling experiment the paper also trained a 3B model with 2T tokens following the StableLM-3B recipe and reported improved generalization.
- Measure validation perplexity (WikiText2 and C4 as in the paper) and zero-shot accuracy on the task suite the authors used.
- Measure time per output token and peak GPU memory during inference using FasterTransformer (2-bit kernel for BitNet b1.58).
- Optionally estimate arithmetic energy costs for matrix multiplication using the energy-model references the paper cites; the authors report that BitNet b1.58 saves 71.4× arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips (this is the matrix-multiplication arithmetic-op energy comparison reported in the paper).

Plot performance versus cost (for example, perplexity or accuracy on one axis and latency or energy on the other) to visualize Pareto fronts. The paper gives examples of equivalences observed in their experiments that your mini study can try to reproduce: 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient (latency, memory, energy) than 3B FP16; 30B b1.58 is more efficient than 7B FP16; and 70B b1.58 is more efficient than 13B FP16. These equivalences capture the new practical scaling law the paper emphasizes and are a clear objective to reproduce in a focused experiment.

# Reflecting on environmental and societal impacts of energy-efficient LLMs

A major motivation and recurring theme in the paper is the environmental and economic cost of ever-larger, full-precision LLMs, and how a 1.58-bit design can materially reduce those costs. The paper emphasizes three concrete impacts.

First, BitNet b1.58 substantially reduces arithmetic energy consumption for the dominant matrix-multiplication work. The paper reports that the arithmetic-op energy composition of BitNet b1.58 is dominated by INT8 addition, whereas FP16 LLaMA contains both FP16 additions and multiplications; using the energy model cited in the paper, BitNet b1.58 saves 71.4× arithmetic operations energy for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips. The authors also show that as model size increases—where \texttt{nn.Linear} contributes an increasing share of runtime—BitNet b1.58's energy advantage grows.

Second, memory- and bandwidth-related costs are reduced. The model size and activation quantization reduce the DRAM-to-accelerator transfer overhead, and smaller activation bitwidths mean KV caches can be much smaller. The paper points out that reducing activation precision from 16-bit to 8-bit gives room to double context length for the same memory footprint, and further lossless compression is left as future work in the paper. The reduced memory footprint also enables higher throughput (more batch capacity on the same hardware) and makes large models more feasible on fewer devices.

Third, these efficiency gains enable new deployment regimes and system designs that have societal consequences. The paper highlights opportunities for larger MoE models with smaller memory footprints and reduced communication overhead between devices, better native long-context support, and the feasibility of deploying capable LLMs on edge and mobile devices (with CPUs) due to smaller memory and energy requirements. The paper also calls for designing new hardware optimized for 1-bit LLMs, noting that the new computation paradigm BitNet creates—using integer-dominant matrix multiplication—opens the door to specialized accelerators.

Taken together, the energy, memory, and throughput improvements reported in the paper point to practical ways to reduce the environmental footprint and lower the marginal cost of running LLMs, while also broadening access to inference-capable LLMs on heterogeneous hardware. These are the concrete, paper-grounded impacts that a practitioner should keep in mind when designing experiments and deployments for BitNet b1.58.
