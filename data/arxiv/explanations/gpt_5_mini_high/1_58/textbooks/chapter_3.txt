\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 3: Quantization for LLMs: Concepts, Methods, and Limits

# Quantization basics: uniform vs non-uniform, symmetric vs asymmetric, zero-point

Quantization is the process of replacing continuous numerical representations (floating-point weights and activations) with a small discrete set of values so that a model requires less memory and cheaper arithmetic during inference. The motivating observations in the paper are straightforward: modern Transformer LLMs are dominated by matrix multiplications carried out in 16-bit floating point (FP16 or BF16). Reducing the precision of weights and activations lowers both the memory footprint and the cost of the arithmetic for those matrix multiplications, which in turn reduces latency, memory-bandwidth pressure, and energy consumption.

BitNet b1.58 follows a particularly simple and explicit quantization design: every weight is constrained to take one of the three integer values $\{-1,0,1\}$. Written compactly, the weight quantization used in BitNet b1.58 is
$$
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
$$
with the clipping/rounding helper
$$
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max\big(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x))\big),
$$
and the scalar scale
$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
$$
This "absmean" quantization divides a weight matrix by its mean absolute value $\gamma$, then rounds and clips each entry to $\{-1,0,1\}$. Because the discrete set is centered on zero, this design is symmetric (levels are balanced around zero) and does not require an explicit additive zero-point offset for weights.

A helpful way to measure the storage advantage is to count the number of representable levels: ternary weights have $3$ levels, so the idealized information content per weight is
$$
\log_2(3) \approx 1.585\ \text{bits}.
$$
This is where the paper's name "b1.58" comes from: the ternary set corresponds to about $1.58$ bits of information per parameter under a simple information-theoretic view. In practice, additional metadata (for example the per-matrix scale $\gamma$) must also be stored, so the real-storage per parameter is slightly higher than the idealized $\log_2(3)$, but the large reduction relative to 16-bit floats remains.

Two common axes used to classify quantizers are "uniform vs non-uniform" and "symmetric vs asymmetric (zero-point)". Uniform quantization places its discrete levels at equal intervals (after some global scale and shift); BitNet's integer rounding to $\{-1,0,1\}$ after dividing by $\gamma$ behaves like a simple uniform, symmetric quantizer on that scaled range. An asymmetric quantizer introduces a non-zero zero-point so that zero in floating point maps to some non-zero codeword; the paper explicitly avoids such a zero-point for activations by operating on symmetric ranges (see the activations section below). This symmetry simplifies integer kernels and (as the paper highlights) removes the need for a zero-point offset in the activation path.

Finally, the practical payoff of symmetric ternary weights is not only smaller storage: because each ternary weight multiplies an activation by either $+1$, $-1$, or $0$, the core inner-product operation reduces to selecting, adding, subtracting, or skipping accumulation of activation elements rather than performing general floating-point multiplication. This is the core of the "integer-addition" computation paradigm emphasized in the paper.

# Post-training quantization (PTQ) vs quantization-aware training (QAT)

The paper frames two broad approaches to push LLMs to low-bit inference representations. One is post-training quantization (PTQ): take a trained FP16/BF16 model and map its weights and (sometimes) activations down to low-bit representations for inference. PTQ approaches are popular in industry because they avoid retraining and can be applied as a conversion step. The paper cites examples of recent PTQ-style work in the LLM space (references include SmoothQuant, GPTQ, QuIP / QuIP-Sharp, and AWQ) and notes that the community trend has been to move from 16-bit down to 4-bit variants for inference savings.

The other approach is to make quantization a first-class constraint during model development: either quantization-aware training (QAT) where quantization operators are simulated during the training process, or training-from-scratch in a quantized parameterization so that the model's weights are learned under the low-bit constraint. BitNet b1.58 follows this latter path: it is trained from scratch with 1.58-bit (ternary) weights and 8-bit activations. This design choice is central to the paper's empirical claim: BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision FP16 LLaMA baselines (in perplexity and downstream tasks) at and above a 3B model size while delivering large improvements in latency, memory usage, and energy.

The trade between PTQ and QAT is therefore evident in the paper. PTQ is convenient and widely used, but, as the authors state, "post-training quantization is sub-optimal, even though it is widely used in industry LLMs" when pushing to extreme low-bit regimes. QAT or training-from-scratch with quantized weights, as in BitNet b1.58, lets the optimization process find parameters that are intrinsically compatible with coarse discrete representations, producing models that can achieve parity with FP16 baselines at practical scales.

# Representative LLM PTQ/QAT methods: SmoothQuant, GPTQ, AWQ, QuIP/QuIP-Sharp

The paper lists several representative methods by name to situate BitNet in the existing literature. SmoothQuant, GPTQ, AWQ, and QuIP / QuIP-Sharp are cited as recent advances in post-training quantization for LLM inference. The paper uses these works to motivate the trajectory of industry practice (moving from 16-bit to 4-bit quantization) and then to point out the limitation: that PTQ has not, by itself, produced an LLM operating efficiently and accurately at the extreme 1–2-bit end.

BitNet b1.58 deliberately takes a different stance: rather than using PTQ to push a pre-trained FP16 model down to ternary weights, the model is designed and trained with low-bit weights built into the architecture (replaceable nn.Linear → BitLinear) and an explicit quantization function (the absmean quantizer described earlier). The paper does not provide internal algorithmic descriptions of SmoothQuant, GPTQ, AWQ, or QuIP; it references them as context and contrasts them with the training-from-scratch ternary approach.

# Activation quantization: range calibration and clipping

Activations are the other half of the quantization story. The dynamic range of activations is often larger and more input-dependent than weight ranges, and activation quantization requires careful range calibration and sometimes clipping to avoid catastrophic precision loss. In BitNet b1.58, activations are quantized to 8 bits, and the paper highlights a design choice that simplifies implementation: activations are scaled to the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ on a per-token basis, rather than being scaled into $[0,Q_b]$ before non-linear functions (as in the original BitNet implementation).

This per-token symmetric scaling explicitly "gets rid of the zero-point quantization" for activations. In other words, by mapping the activation range to a symmetric interval around zero, you avoid the offset that would otherwise be necessary if the representation were inherently non-symmetric. The paper emphasizes that this symmetric per-token scaling is more convenient and simpler for implementation and system-level optimization, and that it had negligible effect on empirical performance in their experiments.

Practical benefits tied to activation quantization are also highlighted in the paper's systems observations. For example, reducing activations from 16-bit to 8-bit halves the memory required for KV caches, which directly supports longer context windows in inference. The paper further notes that these 8-bit activation representations could be compressible even further (to 4 bits or lower) in future work, which would amplify the benefits for long-sequence inference.

# Why PTQ becomes sub-optimal at extreme low bit-widths

The paper is explicit but concise about this point: while PTQ approaches are widely used and effective at modest reductions in precision (for example, down to 4 bits), they are not sufficient to reach the extreme 1–2-bit regime while preserving model quality. Rather than presenting an exhaustive technical diagnosis, the authors make the empirical case that designing and training the model with tight quantization constraints produces substantially better outcomes at ternary precision. BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with ternary weights and matches FP16 baselines in perplexity and downstream zero-shot performance at model sizes starting from 3B.

The implication supported by the paper's experiments is that moving to extreme low-bit quantization often requires changes beyond a post-hoc mapping of weights. These changes include (a) rethinking the parameter parameterization (here: BitLinear which uses ternary weights), (b) choosing an appropriate quantization function and scale (here: absmean scaling by $\gamma$), and (c) adopting compatible activation quantization and normalization choices (e.g., RMSNorm, SwiGLU, symmetric activation scaling). In other words, extreme quantization can be fundamentally a modeling and optimization problem that PTQ alone cannot resolve reliably; training-time integration of quantization constraints is a more robust pathway, as the BitNet results demonstrate.

# Trade-offs: accuracy vs compression vs implementation complexity

Any quantization strategy balances three intertwined axes: accuracy, compression (memory, bandwidth, arithmetic complexity), and implementation complexity (software kernels, retraining, and hardware support). The paper presents concrete empirical evidence that the ternary-training path can achieve an attractive point in this trade-off space: BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 baselines at and above 3B model size (accuracy), while delivering substantial compression and runtime benefits.

Key empirical trade-offs and concrete numbers from the paper include:

- At 3B model size, BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 LLaMA in perplexity while running 2.71× faster and using 3.55× less GPU memory. A 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model is 2.4× faster, uses 3.32× less memory, and performs better than the FP16 3B baseline.

- Latency and memory speed-ups grow with model size because the proportion of compute and memory spent in matrix multiplications (the \emph{nn.Linear} layers) increases. For a 70B model, BitNet b1.58 is reported to be 4.1× faster than the FP16 baseline; in a throughput experiment with two 80GB A100 GPUs, 70B BitNet b1.58 supports up to 11× the batch size of the 70B FP16 LLaMA and delivers 8.9× higher throughput.

- On the energy front, focusing on the matrix-multiplication arithmetic, the paper reports that BitNet b1.58 saves approximately 71.4× in arithmetic operation energy for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips compared to FP16 matrix multiplication. The bulk of BitNet's arithmetic is INT8 additions (or addition/subtraction), whereas FP16 LLaMA requires both FP16 multiplications and additions; this change in the arithmetic mix drives the large energy savings.

These advantages come with implementation and engineering costs. BitNet b1.58 requires rearchitecting linear layers (BitLinear), incorporating a quantization-aware training procedure (training from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations), and integrating specialized inference kernels (the paper integrates a 2-bit kernel from Ladder and uses the FasterTransformer codebase). There is also the additional bookkeeping of per-matrix scales like $\gamma$. Finally, some parts of the network (for example token embeddings) remain full precision in the reported experiments, which affects the absolute compression ratio; however, as models scale, these fixed-cost high-precision components occupy a smaller fraction of total memory.

Beyond the software and training-level trade-offs, the paper highlights the exciting systems and hardware implications: the integer-addition computation paradigm enabled by ternary weights invites custom hardware and accelerator designs optimized for low-bit addition rather than floating-point multiplication, and it lowers the barrier to deploying large models on resource-constrained devices (edge, mobile, and CPU-only environments). The paper also suggests that lower-precision representations simplify working with Mixture-of-Experts architectures (reducing inter-chip communication) and make native long-sequence inference more tractable by shrinking KV-cache footprints.

# Closing summary

The central lesson of the paper is that extreme low-bit operation for LLMs—down to ternary weights corresponding to roughly $1.58$ bits per parameter—can be achieved without sacrificing model quality, provided the quantization is treated as part of the model design and training process rather than as a purely post hoc conversion. BitNet b1.58 operationalizes this lesson through a simple, symmetric ternary quantizer (absmean scaling plus rounding/clipping), 8-bit activations with symmetric per-token scaling, and a training-from-scratch pipeline. The empirical payoffs include large reductions in inference latency, memory, energy, and improved throughput, with parity or improvements in perplexity and downstream zero-shot tasks at practical model scales. At the same time, these gains require additional implementation effort and a willingness to co-design models, software kernels, and eventually hardware to realize the full potential of the 1-bit (ternary) paradigm.
