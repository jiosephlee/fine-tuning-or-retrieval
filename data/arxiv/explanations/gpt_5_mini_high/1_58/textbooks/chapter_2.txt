\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 2: Transformers and Their Cost Drivers

# Transformer blocks: attention, MLP, layer norms, embeddings

Modern large language models are built by stacking Transformer blocks. In the work summarized in the source paper, the base model family is a standard Transformer design (a LLaMA-like backbone) but with a key change in the linear layers: every standard dense layer (implemented in common frameworks as an operation like nn.Linear) is replaced by a BitLinear variant. The BitNet family described in the paper preserves the familiar LLaMA-alike ingredients so it integrates easily with the open-source ecosystem: it uses RMSNorm for normalization, the SwiGLU activation in the feed-forward (MLP) block, rotary positional embeddings, and removes explicit biases. These implementation choices make the BitNet architecture a close functional match to the widely used LLaMA implementations while changing how the bulk compute (the dense linear transforms) is represented and executed.

Although the paper does not re-derive Transformer math, it emphasizes where cost concentrates in a block: attention, MLP, layer norms and embeddings are the structural pieces, but the expensive operations in both attention and MLP arise from the many linear transforms (matrix–vector and matrix–matrix products) implemented by nn.Linear. The embedding table that turns tokens into vectors in practice is kept at full precision in BitNet b1.58; the paper explicitly notes that the embedding stays full precision and that its relative share of memory becomes smaller as models scale. By contrast, the linear transforms inside attention and the MLP are the primary targets for quantization and alternative implementations because they account for most of the arithmetic and memory traffic.

BitNet b1.58 constrains weights to the ternary set $\{-1,0,1\}$ and uses 8-bit activations. The paper describes an "absmean" quantizer that scales a floating-point weight matrix by its average absolute value and then rounds and clips each entry to $\{-1,0,1\}$. The equations the paper uses are
\[
\widetilde{W}=\mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},-1,1\right),
\]
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max\!\big(a,\min\!\big(b,\mathrm{round}(x)\big)\big),
\]
\[
\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|.
\]
This formulation makes the quantizer operate relative to the scale of the weight matrix (via $\gamma$) and forces the final stored weight values into three states. Adding the explicit zero state gives BitNet b1.58 an extra modeling degree compared to pure $\{\pm 1\}$ weights: the zero value enables explicit feature filtering (weights can effectively turn off a connection), which the paper highlights as a reason the ternary variant has stronger modeling capability than the original 1-bit variant.

Finally, activations in BitNet b1.58 are quantized differently than weights. The paper notes that activations are scaled per token into the symmetric range $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ (rather than using a non-negative range with a zero point). This symmetric per-token scaling removes zero-point complications in the activation quantization and is chosen because it is simpler to implement and has negligible impact on empirical performance in the experiments reported.

# Why matrix multiplications dominate runtime

A central empirical observation in the paper is that "the bulk of any LLM is matrix multiplication." That statement is the organizing principle for the efficiency claims: when most of the model’s work is matrix multiply (dense linear) operations, changing the representation and compute pattern of those matrix multiplies has outsized effect on latency, memory, throughput and energy.

Matrix multiplications show up everywhere in a Transformer: the projection matrices inside attention (queries, keys, values, and output), the linear projections that implement the MLP (feed-forward) layers, and the final output projection. Each of those is implemented with dense linear algebra primitives that perform many floating-point multiplications and additions when stored in FP16/BF16. Because the amount of work in these operations grows quickly with hidden dimension and number of heads, the relative time spent in these dense multiplies increases as model size increases. The paper documents this empirically: the runtime cost of nn.Linear (i.e., dense matrix multiplication) grows with model size, and as a consequence the relative speed-up that a cheaper representation can provide also grows as models scale.

The BitNet approach targets exactly this kernel: by representing weights in a ternary form and using integer-dominated computation for the core multiplies, the model reduces the dominant compute and memory traffic. Practically, this is why the latency and memory advantages of BitNet b1.58 become larger for bigger models (the paper reports larger speed-ups for 70B than for 3B): removing expensive floating-point multiplication where it matters most has a multiplying effect on end-to-end inference cost.

# FP16/BF16 arithmetic: multiply-accumulate and its energy profile

Vanilla Transformer LLMs in common practice are trained and deployed using 16-bit floating formats such as FP16 or BF16. Those floating-point matrix multiplications are implemented as sequences of multiply–accumulate operations: a floating-point multiplication followed by a floating-point addition (or fused multiply–add). From an energy and hardware standpoint these floating-point arithmetic operations are comparatively costly, both because of the logic complexity and because their use tends to dominate power draw when matrix multiplies are the hot loop.

The BitNet b1.58 design replaces expensive FP16 matrix multiplies with computation that is dominated by integer additions. Concretely, the paper emphasizes that the matrix multiplication in BitNet can be implemented without the floating-point multiplications that appear in FP16 matrix multiply kernels. In their energy accounting, using the energy model cited in the paper (for a representative 7 nm process), BitNet b1.58 saves a large factor in arithmetic energy for matrix multiplication: the paper reports a reduction by a factor of $71.4$ in arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication when comparing BitNet b1.58 to an FP16 LLaMA baseline on 7 nm chips. The paper also breaks down the operation mix: the majority of BitNet b1.58 arithmetic is INT8 addition, whereas the FP16 baseline consists of both FP16 addition and FP16 multiplication primitives.

Because many modern accelerators are power-limited, arithmetic energy translates directly into practical resource limits: if each multiply–accumulate draws more energy, the chip must either reduce clocking or limit parallelism; reducing per-operation energy therefore opens the door to higher throughput and lower latency. The paper uses this reasoning to explain how the arithmetic savings from ternary weights and integer arithmetic can produce order-of-magnitude reductions in energy for the matrix-multiply dominated parts of inference.

# Inference pipeline and time-per-output-token as the dominant cost

When measuring inference for language models the paper focuses on time per output token as the most relevant real-world metric: the experiments report latency (time per token) using the FasterTransformer codebase, which is a highly optimized inference stack for GPU devices. For BitNet b1.58, the paper integrates a 2-bit kernel from Ladder to serve the quantized compute path and reports runtime GPU memory and per-token latency under the same conditions used for the FP16 LLaMA baselines.

The experimental results reported in the paper show concrete end-to-end benefits in the per-token metric. For example, when pre-trained on the same RedPajama data for $100$ billion tokens, BitNet b1.58 matches the full-precision LLaMA baseline in perplexity starting at model size $3\,$B, while operating faster and using less memory. Specifically, at $3\,$B BitNet b1.58 is reported to be $2.71\times$ faster and use $3.55\times$ less GPU memory than the FP16 LLaMA reproduction. A BitNet b1.58 model of size $3.9\,$B was $2.4\times$ faster, used $3.32\times$ less memory, and performed better than a $3\,$B FP16 LLaMA. At very large scale the trend continues: BitNet b1.58 at $70\,$B is reported as $4.1\times$ faster than the corresponding FP16 baseline.

Throughput measurements of large models further illustrate the practical effect on serving: for a $70\,$B model spread across two $80\,$GB A100 cards (using pipeline parallelism so that the FP16 baseline could run), BitNet b1.58 supported up to $11\times$ the batch size of the FP16 LLaMA model and achieved $8.9\times$ higher throughput before hitting GPU memory limits. These numbers show how changing the representation and arithmetic of the dominant kernel directly translates into more examples per second and lower latency per generated token in real inference stacks.

# Memory movement and bandwidth bottlenecks (DRAM to on-chip SRAM)

A frequent pain point for deployment of very large models is the cost of getting model parameters and activations onto the on-chip compute fabric. The paper highlights that moving parameters from DRAM into the on-chip memory of accelerators (for example into SRAM on an inference chip) is expensive in both time and energy. Increasing on-chip SRAM capacity to avoid repeated DRAM transfers is possible but significantly costlier in silicon area and dollars than relying on DRAM. Consequently, memory capacity and bandwidth are recurring bottlenecks for inference.

Because BitNet b1.58 represents most parameters with ternary values and uses lower-bit activations, it shrinks both the capacity required to hold weights and the bandwidth required to stream them from off-chip memory. The paper emphasizes that the reduction is meaningful from both a capacity and a bandwidth standpoint: smaller stored weights mean fewer bits to read from DRAM at each layer, and smaller activation representations reduce the size of intermediate data that must be moved through caches and between chips. Those two effects together reduce the time cost of loading weights and activations from DRAM and thus lower the end-to-end inference latency.

Empirically, the memory savings are large enough to increase the effective batch size and throughput the hardware can sustain. As reported above, BitNet b1.58 uses several times less GPU memory than the corresponding FP16 LLaMA models in the tested sizes; this directly enables larger batch sizes (as observed in the $70\,$B throughput experiment) and lower pressure on inter-chip communication for distributed deployments. The paper also notes that embedding tables remain full precision, which is why they retain a non-zero memory footprint, but as model size increases the embedding’s relative share of overall memory drops, so the benefits of quantizing the many dense projection matrices grow with scale.

# KV cache growth with context length and its implications

One specific memory challenge for long-context inference is the growth of the key/value (KV) caches that attention mechanisms store for autoregressive generation. The paper calls out KV cache memory consumption as "one major challenge for long sequence inference." Because KV caches store intermediate activations per past token, their memory footprint grows linearly with the context length; in full-precision systems those stored activations are usually 16-bit, so long contexts imply large memory and bandwidth costs.

BitNet b1.58 impacts this story by reducing activation precision: activations are represented as 8-bit in the reported design. The paper points out that this immediately allows roughly doubling the context length for the same memory budget (16-bit → 8-bit halves the memory needed for the caches). The authors further suggest that the activation representation for 1.58-bit LLMs could be losslessly compressed to 4 bits or even lower in future work, which would provide additional headroom for longer contexts without increasing hardware costs. Reducing the KV cache footprint not only enables native support for longer sequences on existing accelerators but also reduces inter-chip communication and device count in distributed setups.

Reducing activation and weight precision has practical implications for deployment beyond raw latency: it makes it more feasible to run larger-context workloads on a single device or with fewer devices, lowers the energy and memory cost of KV cache movement, and therefore directly addresses one of the tightest constraints in long-sequence LLM inference.

# Concluding perspective

Across the Transformer components, the dominant cost drivers are the dense linear algebra kernels (matrix multiplications) and the movement of those large parameter and activation tensors between off-chip and on-chip memories. The BitNet b1.58 approach in the source paper tackles both of these pain points by turning most weight storage into a ternary format and by using lower-precision activations. That design shifts the compute mix away from expensive FP16 multiply–accumulate operations toward integer additions, reduces DRAM bandwidth pressure, shrinks KV caches, and yields empirical gains in per-token latency, GPU memory, throughput and arithmetic energy. The result is a set of practical trade-offs and scaling observations: quantizing the dominant kernels changes the primary bottlenecks of Transformer inference, enabling new operating points (lower latency, lower energy, larger context lengths) while, in the reported results, matching full-precision perplexity and end-task performance starting at moderate model sizes.
