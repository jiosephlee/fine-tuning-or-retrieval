\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 11: Systems Perspective: Kernels, Memory, and Inference

# DRAM-to-SRAM transfers and bandwidth savings with ternary weights

A dominant cost in inference for large Transformer models is moving parameters from off‑chip DRAM into the on‑chip memory of accelerators (SRAM, caches) and then into compute units. The paper emphasizes that this DRAM→SRAM transfer is “expensive during inference,” both in time (latency) and energy; reducing the amount of data moved therefore directly improves latency and power.

BitNet b1.58 attacks this cost by constraining every parameter to the ternary set \{-1, 0, +1\}. The weight matrix is computed as a scaled, rounded version of the FP weights via the absmean quantization function used in the paper:
$$
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
$$
$$
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max\big(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))\big),
$$
$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
$$
Because each stored parameter now takes one of three values, the model’s weight storage and the per‑inference bandwidth needed to load weights are dramatically reduced compared to full‑precision (FP16/BF16) parameters. The paper demonstrates the system implications empirically: at the 3B scale BitNet b1.58 is 2.71× faster and uses 3.55× less GPU memory than the FP16 LLaMA baseline, and at larger scales the gains grow (e.g., BitNet b1.58 70B is 4.1× faster). These measured improvements are exactly the kind of wins one gets when DRAM bandwidth and memory footprint are substantially lowered.

Two additional system consequences follow directly. First, because the cost of weight transfers grows with model size (the time spent in the bulk linear layers increases), the relative benefit of smaller weights increases for larger models; this is why the paper sees larger speedups at larger scales. Second, smaller weight representations relax on‑chip memory design constraints: less SRAM is required to hold working sets, and the time and energy overhead of loading weights from DRAM is reduced, enabling both lower latency and lower inference energy in practice (the paper reports large arithmetic energy savings from this shift). In short, cutting the bits that must transit DRAM into computation is a primary, measurable lever for improving inference at scale in BitNet b1.58.

# INT8 addition–centric kernels and opportunities for operator fusion

A conceptual shift at the kernel level is central to BitNet b1.58. Traditional FP16 Transformer matmuls rely on floating‑point multiply–accumulate operations; BitNet b1.58’s ternary weights and 8‑bit activations let the dominant arithmetic be integer additions rather than FP multiplies. The paper puts this plainly: “the matrix multiplication of BitNet only involves integer addition,” and “the majority of BitNet b1.58 is INT8 addition calculation.” Changing the arithmetic mix from FP multiplies to integer additions has immediate system effects: integer additions are cheaper in energy per operation and can be implemented with simpler datapaths on specialized kernels or hardware.

On an implementation level the paper integrates a low‑bit kernel (the Ladder 2‑bit kernel) into FasterTransformer to bring these savings to practice. That kernel shifts the hot path of nn.Linear away from FP mixed‑precision matmuls and toward low‑precision integer computation, producing the measured latency and memory benefits.

Because the arithmetic and data formats are simpler, there are natural opportunities for operator fusion and kernel consolidation that reduce memory traffic and intermediate conversions. For example, BitNet b1.58 uses per‑token activation scaling to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ (instead of a zero‑point quantization to $[0,Q_b]$), which the paper describes as “more convenient and simple for both implementation and system‑level optimization.” That single design choice reduces the bookkeeping and conversion overhead between quantization and the core matmul. More generally, when kernels operate on small, fixed integer alphabets (here ternary weights and 8‑bit activations), implementers can place scaling, clamping, and accumulation inside the same low‑bit kernel so that fewer full‑precision buffers are read/written. The paper treats these simplifications as part of a “new computation paradigm” enabled by BitNet, and the observed throughput and energy wins are evidence that moving toward addition‑centric, fused integer kernels is a highly practical systems lever.

# Role and limits of the current 2‑bit kernel; avenues for further speedups

In the experiments the authors used the Ladder 2‑bit kernel integrated into FasterTransformer as the production‑level low‑bit matmul implementation for BitNet b1.58. That choice demonstrates the practicality of deploying low‑bit kernels and delivers the quantitative wins reported (reduced latency, memory, energy). The paper also emphasizes that these measurements were made “with a 2‑bit kernel, so there is still room for optimization to further reduce the cost.” That sentence captures two important points for system designers:

1. The current 2‑bit kernel is a pragmatic, high‑quality building block, but it is not necessarily the final kernel design for ternary models. Ladder’s 2‑bit kernel is effective for low‑bit GEMMs in general, yet ternary weights and the per‑token activation scaling used in BitNet b1.58 imply a somewhat different target representation and dataflow that bespoke kernels could exploit further.

2. Some parts of an LLM still remain in higher precision (for example, embeddings are kept full precision in the paper’s implementation), and there are other fixed costs (norms, activation functions, I/O, and control logic) that will limit end‑to‑end speedups until they too are optimized. Thus kernel improvements provide substantial gains but are not the only path to better end‑to‑end performance.

The paper points to natural avenues for additional speedups consistent with its findings: tighter kernels designed specifically for ternary weights and 8‑bit activations; better fusion of scaling/quantization into the matmul path; and system/hardware support specialized for addition‑centric operators. The authors explicitly call for new hardware designs tailored to 1‑bit/ternary LLMs. Practically speaking, therefore, the Ladder 2‑bit kernel is a strong intermediate step that validates the approach, but both software kernel engineering and hardware specialization remain available levers to extract yet more speed and energy efficiency.

# Embedding precision and its diminishing memory share as models scale

One noticeable implementation detail in the BitNet b1.58 design is that embeddings are kept in full precision (FP16/BF16), even while most linear weights are ternary. The paper points out that the embedding “remains full precision and its memory proportion is smaller for larger models.” This is an important system observation.

The vocabulary embedding matrix is often a sizable, but fixed, chunk of model parameters: its size is proportional to vocabulary size × embedding dimension. For smaller models that embedding can be a significant share of memory; for large models, however, the parameter budget is dominated by many large linear layers and attention projections, so the embedding’s fraction of total memory shrinks. The practical upshot in the experiments is that moving all of the linear layers to ternary representations generated large total memory and bandwidth wins even with the embedding unchanged. That makes the tradeoff attractive: keep embeddings full precision to preserve compatibility and a small accuracy cushion, while quantizing the large, repetitive linear layers that dominate compute and transfer costs.

The paper’s measurements show that this choice is sufficient to unlock most of the system gains: latency and memory reduce substantially as model size scales, because the relative weight of full‑precision embeddings becomes negligible compared to the many quantized linear layers.

# KV cache memory and benefits from 8‑bit activations

Autoregressive decoding relies on key/value (KV) caches that store per‑token projections so that attention over the context does not recompute earlier activations. These KV caches are a primary activation memory consumer during inference: their size grows linearly with context length and with the model’s hidden dimension. The BitNet b1.58 design reduces activation precision from 16 bits to 8 bits, and the paper highlights the direct system consequence: halving the number of bits used for the cached activations roughly halves the KV cache memory footprint.

Concretely, the paper states that reducing activations from 16 bits to 8 bits “allows the context length to be doubled given the same resources.” That is a simple, practical trade: with the same memory budget for KV caches, an 8‑bit representation supports roughly twice as many cached tokens as a 16‑bit representation, enabling longer contexts for a given device. The authors also note future potential: these 8‑bit activations could be further compressed (the paper mentions the possibility of lossless compression to 4 bits or lower as future work), which would further expand effective context length or reduce memory pressure.

From a systems perspective, this relationship between activation bitwidth and KV cache size is a high‑leverage knob: lowering activation precision buys either more context length or more batch capacity for the same device memory. BitNet b1.58 therefore directly improves the feasibility of “native” long‑context inference on current hardware, and it creates a favorable path for further compression research focused on KV caches.

# Profiling bottlenecks and practical optimization playbook

The experimental work in the paper supplies a clear, empirically grounded playbook that a systems engineer should follow when porting or optimizing BitNet b1.58 (or similar low‑bit LLMs). Derived from the paper’s methods and observations, the practical sequence is:

1. Use an optimized inference framework and measure time per output token. The paper uses FasterTransformer and reports time per output token as the canonical latency metric; starting from a high‑quality baseline is essential to make fair comparisons.

2. Integrate low‑bit matmul kernels early. The authors integrated Ladder’s 2‑bit kernel into FasterTransformer to implement the low‑precision nn.Linear paths. That integration is a must‑have to move the hot path into integer additions and to realize memory/bandwidth savings.

3. Profile to identify the dominant hotspots. In the experiments nn.Linear grows to dominate runtime as model size increases; quantized matmuls are therefore the first optimization target. The paper’s measured speedups are explained largely by accelerating these linear layers.

4. Reduce memory movement via quantization and per‑token activation scaling. The paper implements an absmean weight quantization and scales activations per token to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ to “get rid of the zero‑point quantization,” which simplifies kernels and reduces extra memory conversions. Implementations should fold scaling/clamping into the low‑bit kernels where possible to avoid writing intermediate full‑precision buffers.

5. Keep a pragmatic stance on full‑precision pieces. Preserve full precision where it matters for compatibility or where the memory share is small (the paper keeps embeddings full precision). As models grow, this conservative choice becomes less costly in memory proportion.

6. Use parallelism to increase throughput while respecting memory. For very large models the authors use pipeline parallelism to place parts of the model across devices and then increase batch size until memory limits are reached. Testing device batching limits produced the throughput numbers in the paper (e.g., BitNet b1.58 70B achieving 11× batch size and 8.9× throughput over FP16 LLaMA on two 80GB A100s).

7. Measure energy via arithmetic models. To reason about energy, the paper applies existing arithmetic energy models and finds very large savings for matrix multiplication (a 71.4× arithmetic energy reduction on 7 nm chips for matmul). Systems teams should similarly include arithmetic energy accounting in their evaluations.

8. Leave room for kernel and hardware co‑design. The paper’s conclusion is explicit: BitNet b1.58 “enables a new computation paradigm and opens the door for designing specific hardware optimized for 1‑bit LLMs.” That means software teams should build on modular kernel interfaces so that future, more specialized kernels or hardware accelerators can be dropped in with minimal system changes.

Following these steps reproduces the paper’s observed wins and points toward further engineering investments that are likely to pay off: better low‑bit kernels, fused operators that reduce memory passes, KV cache compression, and ultimately hardware tailored to addition‑centric low‑bit computation.

# Closing perspective

BitNet b1.58 is a systems‑level story as much as it is an algorithmic one. Quantizing the weight alphabet to \{-1,0,+1\} and using 8‑bit activations shifts the computational and memory profile of Transformers: heavy, expensive FP multiplies become cheaper integer additions; DRAM bandwidth and KV cache pressure are reduced; and latency, memory footprint, throughput, and energy all improve in measured deployments. The paper’s experiments show how these shifts manifest at scale (speedups and memory reductions at 3B and larger, major energy savings for matmul, and much higher throughput for large models). For system designers, the central lessons are clear and actionable: prioritize low‑bit kernels for the matmul hot path, fold quantization and scaling into those kernels to cut memory traffic, treat embeddings pragmatically, and exploit reduced activation precision to extend context or batch sizes. The work also flags remaining system opportunities—better kernels, KV compression, and new hardware—which align naturally with the new computation paradigm BitNet b1.58 introduces.
