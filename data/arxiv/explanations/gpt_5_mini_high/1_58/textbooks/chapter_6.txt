\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 6: BitNet b1.58 Quantization Mechanics

This chapter explains the quantization functions used in BitNet b1.58 and the practical reasons they work well in large Transformer LLMs. BitNet b1.58 trains from scratch with ternary weights taking values in $\{-1,0,+1\}$ (hence "1.58-bit" since $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$) and 8-bit activations. The design choices—an absolute-mean scaling for weights, a simple RoundClip operator, a small stability term, and per-token symmetric activation scaling—are chosen to be both implementable and effective in practice. Below we unpack each component, give the exact formulas used in the model, and develop the intuition for why these choices preserve model capacity while making inference and training much cheaper.

# Absmean weight quantization: scaling by average absolute value $\gamma$

The core idea for weight quantization in BitNet b1.58 is to normalize the raw floating-point weight matrix by a robust measure of its scale before discretizing. Concretely, for a weight matrix $W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ BitNet computes the scalar
$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|.
$$
This is the mean absolute value (the per-element $L_1$ mean) of the matrix. The quantized weight matrix $\widetilde{W}$ is then obtained by dividing $W$ by $\gamma$ (plus a small $\epsilon$; see the next section) and applying the ternary rounding/clipping operator:
$$
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\Big(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\,-1,\,1\Big).
$$

Why scale by the mean absolute value? Dividing by $\gamma$ centers the matrix around a predictable scale so that the subsequent rounding to $\{-1,0,+1\}$ produces meaningful relative choices rather than being dominated by arbitrary global magnitudes. Using the average absolute value (the L1 mean) is a simple, single-number normalization that is robust to the sign structure of the matrix and less sensitive to a few extreme outliers than, for example, a simple max-based scale. Because $\gamma$ is a scalar per weight matrix, this approach is easy to implement and cheap to compute during training and inference, and it keeps the quantization decision local to each matrix rather than requiring costly per-channel bookkeeping.

Intuitively, after dividing by $\gamma$ many weights will fall in a typical range around zero: those with magnitude much smaller than $\gamma$ will be rounded to $0$, those of order $\gamma$ will be rounded to $\pm 1$. Thus $\gamma$ effectively sets the "threshold" between keeping a connection (mapped to $\pm 1$) and filtering it out (mapped to $0$). This behavior underlies the claimed benefit that ternary weights explicitly support feature filtering.

# RoundClip operator and clipping to \{-1, 0, +1\}

BitNet uses a simple, deterministic operator to turn a scaled continuous value into a discrete ternary value. The operator is written in the paper as
$$
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max\!\big(a,\;\min\!\big(b,\;\mathrm{round}(x)\big)\big),
$$
so that the full weight quantization is
$$
\widetilde{W}_{ij} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\Big(\frac{W_{ij}}{\gamma + \epsilon},\,-1,\,1\Big).
$$

This operator first rounds to the nearest integer and then clips to the allowed interval $[a,b]$. For BitNet b1.58 the allowed integers are $-1,0,1$. The combination of rounding and clipping is intentionally simple: rounding maps to the nearest discrete level, and clipping ensures saturating bounds so that large scaled values are still representable in the target ternary set. Because the scheme is matrix-wise, it imposes the same relative decision boundary across all entries of a given matrix.

Two important properties follow. First, the resulting weight entries are exactly ternary, which means they can be stored and processed in highly compact and efficient integer form; each weight’s entropy is $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ bits, which motivates the "1.58 bits" terminology. Second, the presence of the $0$ level (unlike a pure $\pm 1$ binary) enables explicit feature filtering: a zero weight exactly removes a connection, which both reduces computation at inference when implemented with sparse-aware kernels and increases representational flexibility during training because the model can choose to drop weak signals rather than always flip sign.

# Role of $\epsilon$ in $\gamma + \epsilon$ for numerical stability

The denominator $\gamma + \epsilon$ appears in the scaling step; $\epsilon$ is a small positive constant added to protect the division. Two practical issues motivate this addition:

1. If a weight matrix has very small mean absolute value (for a pathological initialization or transient state), dividing by $\gamma$ alone could produce very large scaled values, causing almost all entries to be clipped to $\pm 1$ and destabilizing training. The $\epsilon$ term prevents such uncontrolled amplification.

2. If $\gamma$ were exactly zero (which can only happen if every weight is exactly zero), division would be undefined; $\epsilon$ makes the formula well-defined for all inputs.

In short, $\epsilon$ acts as a floor on the effective scale. It is chosen small so that under typical magnitudes the scale is essentially $\gamma$, but it prevents extreme or undefined behavior in degenerate cases. The paper does not prescribe a specific numeric $\epsilon$ here; implementers normally use a tiny constant (machine-dependent) so that the floor only activates when $\gamma$ is tiny compared to normal weight magnitudes.

# Activation quantization: per-token scaling to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$

For activations, BitNet b1.58 departs from some common per-tensor or per-channel schemes and instead scales activations "per token" into a symmetric integer range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, where $Q_b$ denotes the integer bound associated with the chosen activation bitwidth (BitNet uses 8-bit activations). The paper states that, unlike the original BitNet implementation that scaled activations to $[0,Q_b]$ before nonlinearities, BitNet b1.58 scales them to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ on a per-token basis.

Per-token scaling means that for each token position (across the model’s feature dimension) a local scale is computed and then applied to the activations at that token so that the quantized representation fits the symmetric integer range. Conceptually, if $a_{t}$ is the vector of activations for token $t$, a per-token quantization can be written generically as
$$
\widetilde{a}_{t} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\Big(\frac{a_t}{s_t},\,-Q_b,\,Q_b\Big),
$$
with a token-specific scale $s_t>0$ chosen so that the mapped integers lie within $[-Q_b,Q_b]$. The exact form for $s_t$ is an implementation detail, but the core idea is to adapt the dynamic range to each token independently.

Per-token scaling reduces quantization error that would arise if a single global scale had to accommodate tokens with very different magnitudes. Because transformer activations vary substantially across tokens and across sequence positions, adapting the scale per token keeps the quantized integers informative and reduces clipping-induced distortion. This localized scaling is simple to compute at inference for streaming generation and introduces minimal bookkeeping: a single scale value per token.

# Eliminating zero-point quantization for activations

One operation commonly used in asymmetric quantization is the zero-point: an integer offset that maps the real value $0$ to some integer code in the quantized domain. BitNet b1.58 deliberately avoids zero-point quantization for activations by choosing a symmetric integer range $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ so that real zero maps naturally to integer zero. The paper emphasizes that this choice "gets rid of the zero-point quantization" and that doing so is "more convenient and simple for both implementation and system-level optimization," while producing only negligible performance differences in their experiments.

Eliminating the zero-point simplifies arithmetic: integer representations become signed symmetric and there is no need to add or subtract a per-tensor (or per-channel) offset when performing integer matrix multiply–accumulate operations. This simplification reduces implementation complexity, reduces the bookkeeping (no per-tensor zero-point to store and apply), and makes it easier to design efficient system-level kernels because integer arithmetic can be performed in symmetric signed ranges without additional correction terms. In practice the BitNet b1.58 results show that this simplification does not meaningfully harm model quality for the tested models and sizes.

# Implications for gradient flow and training stability

BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with these quantization rules (ternary weights and 8-bit activations) and, according to the reported experiments, matches full-precision baselines starting from the 3B size. This empirical result is the strongest evidence that the chosen quantization mechanics are compatible with stable training.

From the design point of view, several aspects of the quantization scheme support stable gradients during training:

- Matrix-wise absmean scaling ($\gamma$) normalizes each weight matrix to a stable scale before rounding. By keeping the pre-quantization magnitudes in a predictable range, the quantizer reduces the chance that small changes in floating-point weights produce wildly disproportionate discrete changes after rounding. In other words, the scaling aligns the continuous optimization space with the discrete codebook $\{-1,0,1\}$ so that gradient steps are less likely to be catastrophic.

- The inclusion of a $0$ level in the ternary alphabet provides an explicit, sparse option for weak connections. This can act as an implicit regularizer: rather than being forced to choose a sign for every weight, the optimizer can zero out connections that are not helpful. That sparsity tendency can reduce variance in activations and gradients and help with optimization, particularly in large networks with many weak connections.

- Per-token symmetric activation scaling reduces quantization-induced noise in the forward pass by adapting the integer dynamic range to local activation magnitudes. Lower forward-pass noise translates to more meaningful gradient signals during backpropagation, improving convergence behavior compared to a coarse global activation scale.

It is important to note that the rounding operation is not differentiable in the strict mathematical sense, and the paper does not supply the implementation detail of how gradients are routed through the discrete quantization steps. The empirical claim—successful from-scratch training and parity with FP16 at sufficient scale—shows that the overall recipe is trainable in practice, but implementers should be aware that a gradient-handling strategy (for example, a surrogate gradient or other practical technique) is necessary for backpropagation across quantized operators. The paper focuses on the high-level quantization design and empirical outcomes rather than the low-level gradient estimator implementation.

# Why 8-bit activations strike a good balance for LLMs

BitNet b1.58 uses 8-bit activations together with 1.58-bit (ternary) weights. The paper reports that this configuration is a pragmatic compromise: it cuts activation memory in half relative to 16-bit activations while preserving model quality across a wide range of model sizes and tasks. A few practical advantages are highlighted in the paper:

- Memory and context-length trade-off. Reducing activations from 16 bits to 8 bits frees up activation memory and bandwidth, which is especially valuable for transformers where KV caches grow with sequence length. The paper notes that moving from 16-bit to 8-bit activations "allows the context length to be doubled given the same resources," an important systems-level benefit for long-context use cases.

- Maintain performance while enabling efficient integer kernels. Empirically, BitNet b1.58 finds that 8-bit activations together with ternary weights produce negligible performance degradation compared to FP16 models at sufficient model scale. The 8-bit activations fit naturally with integer-dominated computation if the weight side is ternary (mostly sign flips and add operations), enabling highly efficient integer kernels that reduce latency, memory bandwidth and energy.

- Simpler activation quantization and implementation. With the per-token symmetric scaling and the removal of zero-point offsets, 8-bit activations are straightforward to encode, transfer, and use in accelerator-friendly integer arithmetic without extra offset-correction in the inner loops.

In short, 8-bit activations provide a favorable point on the trade-off curve: substantial system and cost advantages with little measurable harm to model quality in the reported experiments.

Conclusion

BitNet b1.58’s quantization mechanics are deliberately simple: normalize each weight matrix by its mean absolute value, round and clip to $\{-1,0,1\}$ (with a small stability floor), and quantize activations per token into a symmetric integer range with 8-bit precision. Each component is chosen for implementability (few extra bookkeeping variables, symmetric integer ranges avoid zero-point offsets), numerical robustness (the $\epsilon$ floor and matrix-wise normalization), and empirical effectiveness (training-from-scratch parity with FP16 for sufficiently large models). Together these rules make a compact, hardware-friendly representation that preserves modeling power (the extra $0$ level supports feature filtering) while enabling large practical savings in memory, latency, and energy.
