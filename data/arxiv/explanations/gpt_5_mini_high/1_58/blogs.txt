# The 1.58‑Bit Breakthrough: How Ternary Weights Make LLMs Smaller, Faster, and Just as Smart

## Introduction

Large language models keep getting bigger and better, but that progress comes at a steep cost: memory, latency, and energy. BitNet b1.58 is a striking counterexample: by training a Transformer with ternary weights that take values in \{-1, 0, +1\} (which is $\log_2 3 \approx 1.58$ bits of information per weight), the authors show you can match FP16/BF16 model quality while drastically cutting runtime cost.

This post gives a plain‑English tour of what "1.58‑bit" means, why adding the zero state matters, how the model is built, and the concrete performance and efficiency wins the paper reports.

## What does "1.58‑bit" mean?

When a parameter can take three distinct values, the information content per parameter is
$\log_2 3 \approx 1.58$ bits. Practically speaking, BitNet b1.58 constrains every weight to one of three choices: -1, 0, or +1. Compared with a strict 1‑bit model (two values: -1 and +1), the ternary choice adds a qualitatively different option: "turn this feature off."

That extra state is small numerically, but it unlocks both modeling and systems benefits (explained below).

## How BitNet b1.58 works (high level)

BitNet b1.58 is a BitNet variant built on a LLaMA‑style Transformer backbone and trained from scratch with ternary weights and 8‑bit activations. The design choices that matter:

- Weights are ternary (\{-1, 0, +1\}); activations are quantized to 8 bits.
- The model adopts LLaMA‑like components for easy integration with open‑source tooling: RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, and no biases.
- The weight quantization uses an "absmean" scaling then a round+clip to the ternary set. Concretely,
  $$
  \widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\,-1,\,1\right),
  $$
  with
  $$
  \mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x)))
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
  $$
  Scaling by the average absolute weight (the $\gamma$ term) aligns each weight matrix to the ternary levels before rounding.
- Activations are quantized per token to a symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, avoiding zero‑point shifts; this is simpler to implement and had negligible effect on quality in the experiments.
- The network replaces standard linear layers with BitLinear (the BitNet primitive), so inference kernels can exploit the ternary structure.

A useful systems observation: when weights are only -1, 0, or +1, multiplying an activation by a weight becomes one of three simple operations — copy, negate, or skip — which can be implemented with additions and sign flips rather than general FP multiply‑accumulate. That dramatically reduces arithmetic cost and energy for the matrix multiplications that dominate LLM computation.

## Why adding the zero state matters

Going from binary \{-1,+1\} to ternary \{-1,0,+1\} may look like a small change, but it gives the model explicit "feature filtering" capacity: a weight of 0 simply suppresses a contribution. Intuitively:

- With only +1/−1 you can flip sign or preserve a feature, but you can't silence it.
- Zero lets the model shut off irrelevant or noisy signals, increasing expressiveness and reducing interference.

The paper reports that this extra expressiveness helps BitNet b1.58 match full‑precision (FP16/BF16) model quality starting at the 3B scale, while preserving the efficiency benefits of the 1‑bit computation paradigm.

## Real results — accuracy plus big cost wins

The authors trained BitNet b1.58 models from scratch on RedPajama for 100B tokens and evaluated zero‑shot performance on a range of benchmarks (ARC‑Easy, ARC‑Challenge, HellaSwag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenBookQA, BoolQ) and perplexity on WikiText2 and C4. The main empirical takeaways reported:

- Model quality: BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 LLaMA‑style models in perplexity and end‑task zero‑shot performance starting at the 3B scale, and the performance gap narrows as models grow.
- Inference speed and memory (selected highlights):
  - At the 3B point, BitNet b1.58 is 2.71× faster and uses 3.55× less GPU memory than an FP16 LLaMA baseline.
  - A 3.9B BitNet b1.58 is 2.4× faster and uses 3.32× less memory than LLaMA 3B, while performing significantly better.
  - The speedup grows with model size: BitNet b1.58 70B is reported as 4.1× faster than the FP16 baseline.
- Energy: focusing on matrix‑multiply arithmetic, the paper estimates that BitNet b1.58 reduces arithmetic energy for matmul by about 71.4× on a 7nm process, according to the energy model used in the study. End‑to‑end energy savings for full inference grow with model size because matrix multiplies dominate larger models.
- Throughput (multi‑GPU): on two 80GB A100s (70B models, pipeline parallelism), BitNet b1.58 can support up to 11× the batch size of the FP16 LLaMA run and delivers about 8.9× higher throughput.
- Token scaling: a 1.58‑bit model trained with 2T tokens (StableLM‑3B recipe) outperformed the published StableLM 3B results on the evaluated tasks (Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, ARC‑easy), indicating good generalization when trained with more data.

Those numbers were measured with an optimized inference stack (FasterTransformer) and a 2‑bit kernel integrated from Ladder; the authors note there is further room for kernel and system optimizations.

A useful way they summarize the new "cost vs accuracy" tradeoffs: larger BitNet b1.58 models can replace smaller FP16 models while being more efficient. Examples from the paper:

- 13B b1.58 is more efficient (latency, memory, energy) than a 3B FP16 LLM.
- 30B b1.58 is more efficient than a 7B FP16 LLM.
- 70B b1.58 is more efficient than a 13B FP16 LLM.

That suggests a new scaling law for practical deployments: invest parameters in a low‑bit model and get FP16‑level performance at much lower runtime cost.

## Practical implications

The paper highlights several immediate and near‑term implications:

- Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE): MoE reduces FLOPs but increases memory and communication overhead. Ternary weights shrink model memory and activation footprints, which reduces device count and inter‑chip bandwidth needs for MoE, potentially making large MoE runs much cheaper to host.
- Long contexts: halving activation precision from 16 bits to 8 bits directly halves KV cache memory, which can allow doubling context length for the same hardware budget. The authors suggest further lossless compression to 4 bits or lower is possible for 1.58‑bit LLMs (left for future work).
- Edge and mobile: lower memory and energy makes larger LLMs more plausible on CPU and mobile hardware, widening deployment options.
- New hardware: because the core compute becomes primarily integer additions and sign operations rather than FP multiplies, there is an opportunity to design accelerators tailored for this computation pattern (the paper explicitly calls for hardware optimized for 1‑bit/ternary LLMs).

## Caveats and open points

A few important caveats to keep in mind:

- Embeddings remain full precision in the reported experiments, so the relative memory benefit grows with model size (embedding share is smaller in bigger models).
- Measurements used existing optimized kernels (FasterTransformer + Ladder 2‑bit kernel); the authors note there is further room to optimize kernels and system stacks to squeeze additional improvements.
- The paper reports parity with FP16 starting at 3B; below that scale the parity was not claimed.

## Takeaway

BitNet b1.58 shows that constraining weights to three states (-1, 0, +1) and using 8‑bit activations can deliver FP16‑level language modeling quality from the 3B scale onward while producing large, practical wins in latency, memory, throughput, and arithmetic energy. The ternary choice adds a small amount of information per parameter ($\log_2 3 \approx 1.58$ bits) but yields outsized benefits: explicit feature filtering, cheaper matrix multiplies (additions and sign operations), and a different—and more efficient—hardware/software design point for LLMs.

If you care about deploying capable LLMs at much lower cost (cloud or edge), the 1.58‑bit idea is worth paying attention to. The next steps are system and kernel engineering, activation compression, and hardware that natively exploits this ternary computation pattern.



# Inside BitNet b1.58: The Simple Math Trick That Ditches Most Multiplications

Large language models are getting smarter — and hungrier for compute. BitNet b1.58 is an elegant, pragmatic twist on the standard Transformer that swaps the heavy-weight floating-point multiply-accumulate workhorse for mostly cheap integer additions. The trick is simple: force weights to be ternary $\{-1,0,1\}$ and quantize activations to compact signed 8-bit integers with symmetric scaling. The result: most per-element multiplications disappear, leaving fast, low-energy integer accumulation and a tiny number of scale multiplications at the end.

Below I unpack the core math (the "absmean" quantizer), show exactly how matrix multiplies become sign-based adds/subtracts, and explain the implementation choices that make BitNet b1.58 a drop-in‑friendly option for LLaMA-style stacks.

## The core idea in one sentence
Replace each dense weight matrix $W$ with a tiny ternary matrix $\widetilde{W}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{n\times m}$ (about $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ bits per weight), quantize activations to signed 8‑bit integers, compute integer accumulations of $\widetilde{W}$ times the int8 activations (add/subtract/skip), and apply a small floating-point scale afterwards. That removes almost all FP multiplies from the dominant matrix multiplications.

## Ternary weights and “1.58 bits”
BitNet b1.58 stores every weight as one of three values $\{-1,0,1\}$. Storing three discrete states costs only $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ bits per parameter, hence the name “1.58‑bit” model. Compared to 16-bit floating point, this is a huge reduction in raw weight representation and — more importantly — it enables very different, much cheaper arithmetic during inference.

## The absmean quantizer — math and intuition
Weights are quantized per matrix using a simple scale based on the mean absolute weight (the “absmean”). Concretely:

- Compute the per-matrix scale
  $$
  \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|.
  $$
- Quantize each weight to $\{-1,0,1\}$ by scaling by $\gamma$ (plus a tiny $\epsilon$ for stability) and rounding/clipping:
  $$
  \widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\,-1,\,1\right),
  $$
  where
  $$
  \mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max\!\big(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x))\big).
  $$

Intuition: dividing by the average absolute weight highlights entries that are meaningfully larger or smaller than the typical weight. After rounding and clipping, large positive weights become $+1$, large negative weights become $-1$, and small weights (near zero relative to the mean) become $0$. The scale $\gamma$ is kept so the original full-precision matrix can be approximated as $W \approx \gamma \widetilde{W}$.

## How matrix multiplies turn into integer additions (step by step)
This is the central arithmetic trick.

1. Express the original matmul with the quantized weights and a (signed) integer-quantized activation:
   - Let $A$ be a (float) activation vector or matrix. Quantize it per token to an int8 representation $Q_A$ with scale $s_a$ so that $A \approx s_a Q_A$ and $Q_A\in[-Q_b,Q_b]\subset\mathbb{Z}$.
   - Using $W \approx \gamma\widetilde{W}$, the product is
     $$
     W A \approx (\gamma s_a)\,(\widetilde{W} Q_A).
     $$

2. Compute the inner integer product $\widetilde{W} Q_A$ directly in integer arithmetic. Because each element $\widetilde{W}_{ij}\in\{-1,0,1\}$, each scalar multiplication reduces to:
   - Multiply by $+1$: add $Q_A_j$ into the accumulator.
   - Multiply by $-1$: subtract $Q_A_j$ from the accumulator.
   - Multiply by $0$: skip (no contribution).

   In other words, the entire dense matrix multiply becomes many conditional integer additions/subtractions and skips. With int8 inputs, the accumulators are integer (e.g., int32), not FP16 multiplies.

3. After the integer accumulation is done, convert the accumulated integer result back to floating point by applying the (small) scalar factor $(\gamma s_a)$ per row/column as needed.

Two practical observations:
- The vast majority of compute in a dense matmul is eliminated as FP multiplies; it's replaced by cheap integer adds/subtracts and sparse skips.
- The remaining floating-point work is a small number of scalar multiplies (the per-matrix or per-block scales), which is negligible compared to the removed MACs.

The paper summarizes this as requiring "almost no multiplication operations for matrix multiplication" and reports that most of the computation becomes INT8 additions.

## Activations: 8-bit symmetric scaling and zero-point-free math
BitNet b1.58 keeps activations at 8 bits. A key implementation choice is symmetric per-token scaling: activations are scaled to the signed integer range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ (rather than to $[0,Q_b]$). This eliminates a zero-point offset in quantized activations, which simplifies the integer arithmetic (no offset compensation) and is friendlier to system-level kernels.

The approach in the paper is:
- Do not rescale activations before nonlinearities to $[0,Q_b]$.
- Instead, scale activations per token to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ so that the integer representation is symmetric around zero.
This symmetry makes the $\{-1,0,1\}$ weight multiplications behave exactly as add/subtract/skip on the signed int8 values, without extra bias terms.

## Implementation and LLaMA‑style compatibility
BitNet b1.58 is a drop-in variant of the BitNet architecture: it replaces every usual dense layer (e.g., PyTorch's `nn.Linear`) with `BitLinear` that implements the ternary-weight, int8-activation computation described above. To maximize adoption and interoperability with popular open-source LLM stacks, the model uses LLaMA-like choices for the rest of the architecture: RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary position embeddings (RoPE), and removal of all biases. These choices make it straightforward to plug BitNet b1.58 into toolkits such as Hugging Face, vLLM, and llama.cpp with minimal modification.

For inference latency measurements the authors integrated a 2-bit kernel from Ladder into NVIDIA’s FasterTransformer, showing real-world speed and memory wins (details below).

## Practical results reported in the paper
All models in the comparison were trained from scratch on the RedPajama dataset for 100B tokens. Highlights from the reported empirical results:

- Perplexity and task performance: BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision (FP16) LLaMA baselines starting at the 3B model size when trained with the same setup (model size, training tokens, etc.).
- Latency and memory (examples from the paper):
  - At 3B, BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 perplexity while being about 2.71× faster and using 3.55× less GPU memory.
  - A 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model is 2.4× faster and uses 3.32× less memory while outperforming a FP16 LLaMA 3B model.
  - At larger scales, speedups increase; BitNet b1.58 70B is reported as 4.1× faster than the FP16 LLaMA 70B baseline (measured with a 2-bit kernel in FasterTransformer).
- Energy: Focusing on matrix-multiply arithmetic energy (the dominant cost), BitNet b1.58 is estimated to save about 71.4× arithmetic energy for matrix multiplication on a 7nm process compared to FP16 multiply-accumulate.
- Throughput: On two 80GB A100 GPUs using pipeline parallelism, BitNet b1.58 70B supports up to 11× the batch size of the FP16 LLaMA 70B implementation in the experiment, yielding an 8.9× higher throughput.
- Scaling equivalences reported:
  - 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient (latency, memory, energy) than a 3B FP16 LLM.
  - 30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 7B FP16.
  - 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 13B FP16.

The paper also trained a 3B BitNet b1.58 model for 2T tokens using the StableLM-3B data recipe and found it to outperform the StableLM 3B baseline across a handful of downstream tasks.

## Why the zero ($0$) in $\{-1,0,1\}$ matters
Binary $\{-1,+1\}$ weights can represent sign but cannot explicitly drop a connection. Adding $0$ introduces a simple, effective form of feature filtering or gating: small-magnitude weights become zero and cease to contribute. The paper notes that this explicit support for filtering increases modeling capability and helps BitNet b1.58 match FP16 performance at practical model sizes.

## A concise inference pipeline
Putting the pieces together, a single inference matmul looks like:

1. For a stored full-precision $W$, precompute $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum |W_{ij}|$ and store $\widetilde{W}=\mathrm{RoundClip}(W/(\gamma+\epsilon),-1,1)$ (this is static after quantization).
2. For each token, quantize activations: $A \approx s_a Q_A$ with $Q_A\in\mathbb{Z}_8$ in $[-Q_b,Q_b]$.
3. Compute integer accumulation $S = \widetilde{W} Q_A$ using add/subtract/skip only.
4. Convert to float output by multiplying $S$ by the scalar $(\gamma s_a)$.

Step 3 is where the multiply-heavy work disappears; step 4 is a small number of scale multiplications.

## Caveats and future directions
The paper highlights several interesting directions enabled by this approach: 1-bit Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) designs with smaller device counts and less communication overhead, better native support for long contexts thanks to reduced KV cache size, improved feasibility of running large models on edge and mobile hardware, and the opportunity to design hardware (LPUs or other accelerators) that are native to 1-bit/1.58-bit compute patterns.

One practical note: the experiments measured latency and memory using a 2-bit kernel (Ladder) integrated into FasterTransformer; the paper suggests there is still room for further kernel and hardware optimization.

## Takeaway
BitNet b1.58 is a clear, implementable example of how aggressive, structured quantization (ternary weights + signed 8-bit activations) changes the arithmetic story for LLMs. By turning the innermost multiplications into conditional integer adds/subtracts and deferring scaling to a small number of post-accumulation multiplies, it achieves large reductions in latency, memory footprint, and energy while matching FP16 performance from practical model sizes upward. If you care about inference cost at scale, the math is simple and the implications are big.



# Can 1.58-Bit Models Match Full Precision? Benchmarks Explained

## Quick summary
BitNet b1.58 is a ternary-weight Transformer where every parameter takes values in $\{-1,0,1\}$ (hence “1.58 bits” on average). The paper reports that, when trained from scratch on the same data and token budget as FP16 LLaMA-style models, BitNet b1.58 reaches parity with full-precision (FP16/BF16) models starting at about 3B parameters. A 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model not only surpasses a 3B FP16 baseline in perplexity and downstream zero-shot accuracy, it does so while being substantially cheaper to run: lower latency, less GPU memory, and much lower arithmetic energy for matrix multiplications.

Below I walk through the core benchmark claims, the intuition behind them, and what they mean for practitioners.

## What “1.58 bits” means (short primer)
BitNet b1.58 forces each weight to be ternary, $\{-1,0,1\}$, rather than 16-bit floats. The quantization used in the paper is an “absmean” style clip-and-round:

$ \widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right) $

$ \mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))) $

$ \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}| $

(Here $\epsilon$ is included in the paper’s formula.) Activations are quantized to 8 bits and — unlike the original BitNet implementation — are scaled per token to the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ to avoid a zero-point, which simplifies implementation. Architecturally, BitNet b1.58 keeps LLaMA-like choices (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings) and replaces standard dense layers (nn.Linear) with BitLinear layers that operate on these quantized weights.

Crucially, BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with these quantized weights (not just post-training quantized), and the addition of the $0$ weight gives the model explicit feature-filtering capacity that the strict $\{-1,1\}$ 1-bit variant lacks.

## Experimental setup (what was compared)
- Both BitNet b1.58 and the FP16 LLaMA-style baselines were trained from scratch on the RedPajama corpus for 100B tokens (same token budget).
- Zero-shot evaluations used the standard lm-evaluation-harness pipeline across a set of common tasks: ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenBookQA, BoolQ.
- Perplexity was reported on WikiText2 and C4 validation sets.
- Runtime/latency measurements were taken with NVIDIA’s FasterTransformer; the authors also integrated a 2-bit kernel (Ladder) for the BitNet runtime kernels. Throughput experiments for the largest models used two 80GB A100 GPUs with pipeline parallelism.

## Perplexity: parity begins around 3B
Perplexity is the basic next-token prediction metric: lower perplexity means the model assigns higher probability to held-out text. The paper reports that BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 baselines in perplexity beginning at the 3B parameter scale when both models are trained with the same recipe and token budget.

In addition to matching perplexity, BitNet b1.58 is materially cheaper to run at that scale: at the 3B point the paper reports BitNet b1.58 being 2.71× faster and using 3.55× less GPU memory than the reproduced FP16 LLaMA baseline.

The paper also shows that as model size increases the performance gap between ternary and full-precision models shrinks — a common empirical pattern where larger models are more robust to low-precision parametrizations.

## Zero-shot tasks: 3.9B b1.58 beats 3B FP16 (while being smaller/faster)
For downstream zero-shot evaluations, the authors report the same general trend: the accuracy gap narrows with scale, and BitNet b1.58 reaches parity with FP16 starting at 3B. More strikingly, a 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model is reported to outperform a 3B FP16 LLaMA-style model on the evaluated tasks, while also being more efficient:

- 3.9B BitNet b1.58 is 2.4× faster than the 3B FP16 LLaMA baseline (reported latency).
- It uses 3.32× less GPU memory than the 3B FP16 baseline.
- Its zero-shot task performance and perplexity are reported as better than the 3B FP16 model (on the same training data and token budget).

So at practical model sizes, the ternary model can give you both improved quality (versus a smaller FP16 model) and substantially lower inference cost.

## Training at scale: the 2T-token experiment
To test generalization with larger token budgets, the paper trained a BitNet b1.58 model with 2 trillion tokens following the StableLM-3B data recipe. Evaluated on a benchmark averaging Winogrande, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, and ARC-easy, BitNet b1.58 at 2T tokens achieved superior performance on all these end tasks compared to the StableLM 3B baseline trained with the same 2T tokens (results taken from StableLM’s technical report). This suggests that low-precision, ternary-weight models can still benefit from large token budgets and generalize strongly when trained appropriately.

## Cost and scaling advantages reported
Beyond model quality, the paper highlights several run-time and energy advantages:

- The speed-up of BitNet b1.58 increases with model size; for example, a 70B BitNet b1.58 is reported as 4.1× faster than a 70B FP16 LLaMA baseline.
- Arithmetic energy for matrix multiplication — the dominant compute for transformers — is estimated to be dramatically lower: the paper reports a 71.4× reduction in arithmetic operation energy for matrix multiplication on 7nm technology nodes (BitNet b1.58’s matmuls use mostly INT8 additions vs FP16 multiplies/adds).
- Throughput: on two 80GB A100 GPUs using pipeline parallelism, the 70B BitNet b1.58 model could support up to 11× the batch size that the FP16 70B model could, yielding an 8.9× higher throughput in that setup.
- The authors present a rough “equivalence” mapping where, in latency/memory/energy terms, larger BitNet b1.58 models dominate smaller FP16 models (e.g., 13B b1.58 is more efficient than 3B FP16; 30B b1.58 beats 7B FP16; 70B b1.58 beats 13B FP16 according to their measurements).

These gains are driven by using integer addition-dominant compute for the innermost matmuls, a smaller memory footprint (weights and activations), and reduced DRAM bandwidth needs for weight streaming.

## Why ternary weights can work (intuitions from the paper)
A few points the paper makes that help explain the results:

- Training from scratch with quantized weights (rather than post-training quantization) lets the optimizer adapt to the discrete weight space during learning.
- Allowing a zero weight ($\{-1,0,1\}$ instead of $\{-1,1\}$) gives explicit feature-filtering capacity that increases modeling flexibility for a 1-bit-like parametrization.
- Activations are kept at 8 bits, so the network still has reasonably fine-grained internal representations even though the weights are coarse.
- Empirically, larger models are more robust to aggressive quantization — hence parity emerges at larger scales (around 3B and up in these experiments).

## Practical takeaways and caveats
- If you are deploying models at or above ~3B parameters, BitNet b1.58 — trained from scratch — appears capable of matching FP16 model quality while lowering latency, memory, and arithmetic energy for matmuls. The 3.9B point in the paper is a useful “sweet spot”: better quality than 3B FP16 plus substantially lower inference cost.
- These results come from models trained with the same token budget and evaluated with specific software kernels (FasterTransformer and a Ladder 2-bit kernel). Real-world gains will depend on available runtime kernels and hardware support for low-bit integer operations and efficient memory formats.
- The paper’s positive results are reported for models trained from scratch in the BitNet way. Post-training quantization of existing FP16 checkpoints is a different problem and is known to be more challenging.
- The paper focuses on specific evaluation suites and a particular pretraining dataset (RedPajama). As always, behavior may vary for other datasets, tasks, or fine-tuning regimes.

## Closing thought
BitNet b1.58 shows that aggressive, structured quantization (ternary weights + 8-bit activations), combined with training-from-scratch and a few architectural pragmatisms, can reach parity with standard FP16 models at surprisingly modest scales (around 3B). The combination of matched or better model quality and large inference/energy savings makes this a compelling direction for both software runtimes and specialized hardware. If you care about inference cost or deploying large-context models on constrained hardware, the 1.58‑bit family from this paper is one to watch.



# Faster, Leaner, Greener: What 1.58-Bit Means for Latency, Memory, Throughput, and Energy

The headline is simple: when you shrink the numeric representation that a large language model (LLM) uses for its weights from 16-bit floats to ternary values $\{-1,0,1\}$ (about $1.58$ bits of information per weight), the system-level wins are dramatic. BitNet b1.58—an LLM trained from scratch with ternary weights and 8-bit activations—matches full-precision baselines (FP16) in perplexity and end-task performance starting around the 3B parameter scale, while delivering much lower latency, much smaller runtime memory, much higher throughput, and far lower arithmetic energy for the dominant matrix multiplies.

Below I unpack the systems story: why these models feel snappy, why the speedup grows with model size, and what the measured throughput and energy wins look like in practice.

## How 1.58-bit changes the compute story

Transformers are dominated by matrix multiplies (the many instances of `nn.Linear`). In FP16/BF16 models those multiplies are floating-point multiply–adds; both the compute energy and the memory bandwidth to stream weights become the dominant costs. BitNet b1.58 changes two things simultaneously:

- Every weight is constrained to the ternary set $\{-1,0,1\}$ (roughly $\log_2 3 \approx 1.58$ bits per weight). That reduces both stored weight size and the arithmetic type used during multiply-accumulate.
- Activations are quantized to 8 bits, shrinking the activation footprint (important for KV caches and per-token state).

The ternary quantization used in BitNet b1.58 is simple and effective: each weight matrix $W$ is scaled by its mean absolute value $\gamma$ and then rounded and clipped to the ternary set:

- $\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\dfrac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$
- $\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x)))$
- $\gamma = \dfrac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$

(Activations follow the BitNet approach but are scaled per token to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ to avoid zero-point complications.)

Two system-level consequences follow: (1) most of the heavy linear algebra becomes integer additions or very small-integer operations rather than FP16 multiply–adds, and (2) the amount of data that must move from DRAM to on-chip memory is much smaller in both capacity and bandwidth. Less arithmetic intensity per op, fewer multiplies, and less memory movement are the trifecta for latency, throughput and energy.

## Latency and memory: why gains grow with model size

Because the cost of `nn.Linear` layers grows with model width and depth, the relative advantage of replacing FP16 multiply–adds with low-bit integer additions increases for larger models. The BitNet b1.58 paper reports these measured system wins (all evaluated with a well-optimized inference stack—FasterTransformer—and with a 2-bit kernel integrated for the ternary kernels):

- At the 3B scale, BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 LLaMA in perplexity and end-task performance while running 2.71× faster and using 3.55× less GPU memory.
- A 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model is 2.4× faster, uses 3.32× less memory, and outperforms LLaMA 3B in tasks—so you can pick a larger model for better quality with lower cost.
- At the 70B scale, BitNet b1.58 runs 4.1× faster than the FP16 LLaMA baseline.

A small practical detail also helps: embeddings remain full precision in this setup, but their share of total memory decreases as model size grows, so the ternary weight advantage dominates for larger models. The latency and memory numbers quoted above were measured as time-per-output-token; the authors note that the current results already use a 2-bit kernel, so there is likely further headroom for optimization.

## Throughput: fit more work in the same device budget

Lower per-model memory lets you increase batch sizes (or sequence parallelism) before hitting GPU memory limits. In a throughput test with 70B models across two 80GB A100 cards (pipeline parallelism used so the FP16 baseline could run), the BitNet b1.58 70B model was able to support up to 11× the batch size of the FP16 LLaMA 70B run, producing an 8.9× higher throughput in that setup (sequence length 512). The mechanism is straightforward: smaller weight and activation footprints let GPUs accommodate more concurrent work and amortize fixed costs across more tokens.

## Energy: cheap integer adds beat FP multiplies

From an arithmetic energy standpoint the switch is stark. The majority of BitNet b1.58’s matrix-multiply work is INT8 additions or similarly inexpensive integer operations, while the FP16 baseline performs many FP16 multiplies and adds. Using an energy model referenced in the paper (targeting 7nm chips), the authors estimate a 71.4× reduction in arithmetic energy for matrix multiplication alone when comparing BitNet b1.58 to FP16 LLaMA. Measured end-to-end energy per 512-token inference also shows growing savings as model size increases, because `nn.Linear` becomes a larger fraction of total cost in larger models.

The takeaway: replacing costly FP multiplies with integer additions produces disproportionately large energy wins on modern silicon.

## A new inference scaling law (practical equivalences)

Because BitNet b1.58 reduces latency, memory and energy significantly, you can often pick a much larger 1.58-bit model while staying within the same system budget as a smaller FP16 model. The paper frames a few practical equivalences from their measurements:

- 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient (latency, memory, energy) than a 3B FP16 LLM.
- 30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 7B FP16 LLM.
- 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than a 13B FP16 LLM.

That’s a deployer’s superpower: trade cost for quality more aggressively because the cost baseline has shifted downward.

## Where this matters most (and what it unlocks)

A few concrete deployment scenarios gain immediately:

- Long-context inference: activations are 8-bit instead of 16-bit, so KV cache memory is smaller and—given the same resources—context windows can be doubled, or KV caches can be further compressed in future work.
- Mixture-of-Experts (MoE): MoE benefits from lower memory per parameter and smaller activation transfers, reducing device counts and cross-device communication costs—potentially removing the biggest practical hurdles for scaling MoE inference.
- Edge and mobile: the smaller memory and integer-friendly compute profile make BitNet b1.58 more friendly to CPU and embedded devices.
- New hardware: the computation paradigm (few multiplies, many cheap integer adds and tiny weight representations) calls for accelerators and kernels tuned to 1-bit/ternary LLMs.

## How the comparisons were done (short methods)

For fair comparisons the BitNet b1.58 family and the FP16 LLaMA reproductions were pre-trained on the RedPajama dataset for 100B tokens. Inference latency and per-token time were measured with FasterTransformer; ternary kernels used a 2-bit implementation (Ladder). Throughput tests used pipeline parallelism on two 80GB A100 GPUs and a sequence length of 512. The paper also reports a 2T-token training experiment (following the StableLM-3B recipe) where BitNet b1.58 showed superior end-task performance to StableLM 3B, demonstrating good scaling with tokens.

## Bottom line

BitNet b1.58 shows that you do not need FP16 everywhere to get modern LLM quality. With ternary weights $\{-1,0,1\}$ and 8-bit activations, you get:

- Substantially lower latency that grows into larger wins at bigger model sizes (up to $\sim$4.1× at 70B),
- Much smaller runtime memory usage (e.g., 3.55× less at 3B),
- Much higher throughput by being able to run larger batches (up to 8.9× measured at 70B),
- Dramatic arithmetic energy savings for matrix multiplies (estimated $\sim$71.4× on 7nm).

If your primary constraints are latency, memory, throughput or energy, the 1.58-bit operating point is worth exploring: it shifts the Pareto frontier, allowing either cheaper inference at the same quality or better models within the same cost envelope. The remaining engineering work is now at the intersection of kernels, deployment stacks, and hardware specialized for this new, very efficient numeric regime.



# A New Scaling Law for LLMs: Picking the Right Size Under a Fixed Budget

## TL;DR
BitNet b1.58 (a ternary-weight Transformer with 8-bit activations) changes the practical trade-offs between model size and deployment cost. In the paper's measurements, a 1.58‑bit BitNet can be significantly larger than an FP16 LLaMA-style model while using less latency, memory, and energy. Concretely, the authors report that, under their setup, 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 3B FP16; 30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 7B FP16; and 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 13B FP16. For teams working to maximize capability under a fixed latency/memory/energy budget, the simple rule is: pick the largest BitNet b1.58 you can fit in that budget (not the largest FP16 model).

Below I summarize what BitNet b1.58 is, the empirical “equivalence” results from the paper, and a short, practical decision recipe for choosing model size under cost constraints.

## Why the bit budget matters in practice
Most of a modern Transformer LLM’s runtime cost comes from matrix multiplications (the various linear layers). In FP16/BF16 models these involve floating-point adds and multiplies; in BitNet b1.58 the weights are ternary (\{-1, 0, +1\}) and matrix multiply reduces to much cheaper integer additions and sign operations. That shift reduces:

- arithmetic energy per multiply-accumulate,
- DRAM → on-chip memory bandwidth pressure (weights occupy far less capacity and bandwidth),
- and overall inference latency and throughput for the same hardware.

So, instead of squeezing models into lower-precision FP formats post‑hoc, BitNet trains a model architecture whose weights are inherently ternary and whose runtime arithmetic is dramatically cheaper.

## What BitNet b1.58 actually is (short, technical)
BitNet b1.58 constrains every parameter (weight) to the set \{-1, 0, +1\} (ternary). The authors train Transformers from scratch with these weights and 8‑bit activations, and they adopt LLaMA-like components (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, no biases) so the model integrates with common open-source tooling.

The weight quantization used is an “absmean” scale + rounding to \{-1,0,1\}. In the paper the quantized weights \(\widetilde{W}\) are computed as:

\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|,
\]
\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
\]
\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x))).
\]

Activations are quantized to 8 bits and (for implementation simplicity) are scaled per token to the symmetric range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\), avoiding zero‑point complications.

Important operational details for the reported results: models were trained on RedPajama for 100B tokens (except where explicitly noted), and inference measurements used NVIDIA’s FasterTransformer with a 2‑bit kernel from Ladder integrated for the BitNet runtime.

## The practical scaling law — measured equivalences
The paper frames a new, pragmatic scaling law: given a fixed inference budget (latency, GPU memory, energy), you can afford a much larger BitNet b1.58 model than an FP16 model and often improve quality as a result. Key measured points from the paper (same training tokens / setups where noted):

- BitNet b1.58 begins to match full‑precision (FP16) LLaMA in perplexity and end‑task performance at the 3B model size (trained 100B tokens). At that scale BitNet is also cheaper: the paper reports BitNet 3B is 2.71× faster and uses 3.55× less GPU memory than the FP16 LLaMA 3B baseline.
- A 3.9B BitNet model in the paper runs 2.4× faster and uses 3.32× less memory than LLaMA 3B while performing significantly better than LLaMA 3B (same training tokens/config).
- As model size grows, the BitNet advantage grows: BitNet 70B is reported to be 4.1× faster than the FP16 LLaMA baseline of the same size.
- Throughput example: on two 80GB A100 GPUs (pipeline parallelism, sequence length 512), BitNet 70B supported up to 11× the batch size of FP16 LLaMA 70B and achieved 8.9× higher throughput in the authors’ measurement.
- Energy: using an arithmetic energy model for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips, BitNet saves 71.4× in arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication (INT8 additions vs FP16 adds/mults). End‑to‑end energy (512 tokens) also trends strongly in BitNet’s favor as size increases.
- The authors summarize three convenient equivalences (in latency/memory/energy terms): 13B BitNet b1.58 ≺ 3B FP16; 30B BitNet b1.58 ≺ 7B FP16; 70B BitNet b1.58 ≺ 13B FP16 (where “≺” denotes “is more efficient than” under their measured costs).

Additionally, when trained on a large token budget (the paper reports a 3B BitNet trained on 2T tokens following the StableLM recipe), BitNet achieved better zero‑shot accuracy than the corresponding StableLM 3B baseline across the evaluated tasks.

## How to pick the right model for your budget — a short recipe
If you care about getting the most capability for a fixed latency/memory/energy budget, the paper suggests the following practical process.

1. Identify your current deployment baseline in FP16 (or the FP16 model that fits your production budget). Record the three constraints that matter most: per‑token latency, peak GPU memory, and per‑inference energy (or throughput requirement).

2. Map that FP16 baseline to a BitNet target using the paper’s equivalences. Use the reported rules of thumb:
   - FP16 3B budget → consider BitNet b1.58 13B (or at least bump to 3.9B in the short term — the paper shows 3.9B already outperforms FP16 3B).
   - FP16 7B budget → consider BitNet b1.58 30B.
   - FP16 13B budget → consider BitNet b1.58 70B.

3. Validate in your stack. The paper’s measurements used FasterTransformer + Ladder kernels and specific hardware; you should re‑measure latency/peak memory/throughput on your exact hardware and software. Expect the BitNet advantage to be most pronounced as the model size grows because linear layers dominate runtime cost.

4. Check training logistics. BitNet b1.58 models in the paper are trained from scratch (not post‑training quantization). If you plan to train, replicate the data/training recipe you need (the paper’s main experiments use 100B tokens on RedPajama; an additional experiment used a 2T token recipe).

5. Perform quality checks at your target size. The paper reports BitNet matches FP16 quality starting at 3B and scales well; still, measure perplexity and the end tasks you care about at the target size.

## Concrete scenario examples (from the paper)
- If your production node currently runs FP16 LLaMA 3B and you are constrained by latency and GPU memory, switching to a BitNet b1.58 model lets you run a much larger model within the same budget. In the authors’ setup, BitNet 3B ran 2.71× faster and used 3.55× less GPU memory than FP16 3B; 3.9B BitNet ran 2.4× faster, used 3.32× less memory, and yielded higher task accuracy than LLaMA 3B.

## Caveats and practical considerations
- Training from scratch vs post‑training quantization: BitNet b1.58 experiments in the paper are trained from scratch. These results are not a claim that you can simply post‑quantize an FP16 checkpoint to ternary and get identical behavior.
- Activation and embedding precisions: weights are ternary, activations are 8‑bit, and embeddings remain full precision (FP16/BF16). The embedding footprint thus still affects peak memory, though proportionally less at large sizes.
- Measured stack: latency and throughput numbers come from FasterTransformer with Ladder’s 2‑bit kernel; your software/hardware stack will influence absolute numbers and speedups.
- Energy estimate: the 71.4× figure is for arithmetic operations energy in matrix multiplication on 7nm chips (per the cited energy model). End‑to‑end system energy also improved in the paper’s measurements, but arithmetic‑only numbers are not the full system power story.
- Matching quality: the paper reports that BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 perplexity and end‑task performance starting at 3B in their training setup. If you target smaller models (<3B) or use significantly different training data / tokens, validate quality thoroughly.

## What this changes for teams
If your primary constraint is deployment cost (latency, memory, battery/energy), the conventional “pick the biggest FP16 model you can afford” rule is no longer the only choice. BitNet b1.58 lets you shift budget from compute/precision into model size. Practically, that means:

- You can run noticeably larger models for the same inference cost and often improve quality.
- For production throughput, BitNet enables much higher batch sizes and total requests/sec on the same hardware.
- For energy‑constrained deployments (edge, mobile, or large scale inference fleets), BitNet arithmetic drastically reduces matrix‑multiply energy, which compounds across many inferences.

The paper also highlights downstream opportunities: cheaper MoE, longer context windows by reducing KV cache bitwidth, and the case for new hardware optimized for ternary/1‑bit weights.

## Bottom line
Under the measurement and training setups in the paper, BitNet b1.58 rewrites the practical size-vs-cost trade-off: for a given latency/memory/energy budget you can often run a much larger model in 1.58 bits than in FP16 and get better performance. The quick heuristic from the paper is useful: if you could afford FP16 3B, you can likely run 13B BitNet b1.58 (or at minimum a 3.9B BitNet) and improve capability while reducing cost. Validate on your stack, but if cost matters, choosing the largest BitNet model that fits your budget is a strong place to start.



# Longer Context on the Same Hardware: 1.58-Bit KV Caches and What They Unlock

Context length is one of the practical bottlenecks for deploying large language models. The reason is simple: autoregressive decoding needs to keep every token's keys and values (the "KV cache") in memory, and that cache grows linearly with sequence length. The BitNet b1.58 design presented in "The Era of 1-bit LLMs" attacks this bottleneck by cutting activation precision from 16 bits to 8 bits. That halves the per‑token KV memory footprint and, on the same hardware, literally lets you store roughly twice as many tokens in the cache. Below I unpack what that means, why it matters, and what new use cases it unlocks.

## Why the KV cache is the context wall

During decoding, every layer of a Transformer produces key and value vectors for each consumed token. The model stores those K/V vectors and re-uses them for future attention computations, so the memory consumed by the KV cache grows with sequence length. A convenient formula for the cache memory per token (in bytes) is:

$${\text{bytes per token}} = 2 \cdot L \cdot d_{\text{model}} \cdot \frac{\text{bits per activation}}{8},$$

where $L$ is the number of layers and $d_{\text{model}}$ is the hidden dimension (keys and values combined across heads). The factor 2 comes from storing both K and V.

Because this term scales linearly with sequence length, KV cache memory is often the dominant consumer of GPU memory in long‑context scenarios. When you run out of device memory you either have to shorten the context, shard the cache across devices (with networking overhead), or offload the cache to CPU (with latency penalties). These are the practical limits that stop many systems from going beyond a few thousand tokens.

## How BitNet b1.58 halves the KV cache cost

BitNet b1.58 changes two linked things that matter for the cache:

- Model weights are ternary $\{-1,0,1\}$ (1.58 bits of information per weight).
- Activations (including the vectors stored in the KV cache) are quantized to 8 bits. Activations are scaled per token to the range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, removing the need for zero‑point offsets and simplifying system integration.

Because the KV cache stores activations, reducing activation precision from 16 bits (FP16/BF16) to 8 bits directly halves the bytes per cached token in the formula above. If FP16 supports $N$ tokens in cache on a given GPU, BitNet b1.58’s 8‑bit activations let you store approximately $2N$ tokens on the same device (ignoring small overheads such as full‑precision embeddings, which the paper notes remain in FP16).

Importantly, the paper reports that this 8‑bit activation design introduces negligible performance degradation in their experiments: BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 baselines on perplexity and downstream tasks starting from the 3B scale while delivering substantial memory and latency gains.

## What doubling context gets you (practical wins)

Having double the context on the same GPU is not just a neat number — it changes what is practical in real systems:

- Long‑document QA and summarization: You can keep more of a long document directly in the cache, reducing the need for pre‑chunking, retrieval hacks, or multi‑pass strategies that lose global coherence.
- Code understanding and program synthesis: Code contexts are token‑heavy (identifiers, whitespace, diffs). More tokens in the cache means the model can reason about larger codebases or entire files without truncation.
- Retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG): Retrieval chains typically append dozens or hundreds of retrieved passages. Doubling cache capacity lets you inject more retrieved evidence into a single forward pass, simplifying system design and reducing the chance of missing relevant context.
- Multi‑turn, stateful assistants: Longer maintained histories let assistants use more dialog turns as context without offloading or aggressive summarization.

Two caveats: (1) doubling the cache capacity does not remove the compute cost of processing longer contexts — generation time per token increases with the amount of attention to earlier tokens. (2) small models or deployments where the embedding (kept in full precision) dominates memory will see less than a 2× gain; the paper notes the embedding remains full precision and is proportionally less important for larger models.

## Beyond 8 bits: toward 4 bits and even tighter caches

The BitNet b1.58 paper also points out a promising next step: the 8‑bit activations can be further compressed. The authors claim that for 1.58‑bit LLMs the activation (KV) representations "can be further losslessly compressed to 4 bits or even lower," and they frame this as future work. If achievable in practice, moving from 8 bits to 4 bits would again halve the KV cache footprint — so a model running with 16‑bit activations could, in principle, support up to 4× the context on the same hardware.

That path is attractive because BitNet already replaces multiplication‑heavy matrix multiplies with integer additions (a major compute and energy win) and integrates low‑bit kernels (the evaluation used a 2‑bit kernel in parts of the stack). A straightforward pipeline could be: train a model with ternary weights and 8‑bit activations, then investigate lossless or near‑lossless compressor/packer approaches for the cached K/V tensors to push the cache even smaller.

## System implications and measured evidence

The paper supplies several system‑level measurements that illustrate the practical impact of the design:

- Starting at 3B parameters BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 LLaMA in perplexity and end‑task performance while using substantially less memory and latency. For instance, in the reported experiments BitNet b1.58 at 3B was faster and used less GPU memory than the FP16 baseline.
- Memory and latency advantages grow with model size: BitNet b1.58 70B was measured to be 4.1× faster than the FP16 LLaMA baseline in the paper’s tests, reflecting that the proportion of cost attributable to matrix multiplies and activations grows with model size.
- In a throughput benchmark (70B models on two 80GB A100s), BitNet b1.58 supported up to 11× the batch size and yielded an 8.9× higher throughput under memory‑limited settings.

Those results show that the memory savings are not just theoretical: halving activation bits translates to practical headroom for longer contexts, larger batches, or fewer devices per model.

## Practical notes for adopters

A few practical points to keep in mind if you want to take advantage of this approach:

- BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with ternary weights and 8‑bit activations (the paper emphasizes that post‑training quantization is commonly sub‑optimal). Adopting the same benefits for an existing FP16 model will likely require retraining or a careful fine‑tuning pipeline.
- The activation quantization is implemented with per‑token scaling to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, which removes zero‑point complexity and simplifies system implementation.
- The authors integrated their measurements using FasterTransformer and low‑bit kernels (they used a 2‑bit kernel component from Ladder), so productionizing these gains will depend on kernel and runtime support in your stack.
- For small models the embedding (kept full precision) can be a larger fraction of memory; the benefits scale better for larger models where embeddings are relatively smaller.

## Takeaway

The KV cache is the practical memory wall for long‑context inference. BitNet b1.58 tackles that wall by halving activation precision from 16 bits to 8 bits while keeping model quality competitive, which means you can, on the same GPU, hold approximately twice as many tokens in the cache. That straightforward reduction unlocks more coherent long‑document QA, larger code contexts, richer retrieval inputs, and generally simpler deployment architectures (fewer offloads and cross‑device sharding). The paper also points to an exciting next frontier — lossless 4‑bit KV caches and specialized hardware — which would push the context wall even further back.

If your application is limited by context length rather than raw FLOPs, BitNet b1.58-style activation quantization is worth investigating: it directly increases the tokens you can keep in device memory, and the paper shows that, at scale, those gains translate into real latency, memory, and throughput improvements.



# On‑Device AI Without the Battery Anxiety: Why 1.58-Bit LLMs Fit Phones and Edge Boxes

Edge devices—phones, laptops, and gateway boxes—want smarter local assistants, longer on-device context, and privacy without paying the cloud tax on latency or data exposure. The main blocker has been the cost: modern LLMs are heavy on memory, bandwidth, and floating‑point compute, which chews battery and makes offline deployment impractical.

A recent line of work (BitNet and its 1.58‑bit variant) shows a realistic path out of that tradeoff. BitNet b1.58 replaces dense FP16 weights with ternary weights \{-1, 0, 1\} (about 1.58 bits per parameter) and uses 8‑bit activations. The result: a model family that, in the authors’ experiments, matches FP16 LLaMA-like baselines on perplexity and zero‑shot tasks starting around 3B parameters, while delivering large savings in memory, latency, throughput, and arithmetic energy. Below I unpack the key ideas and why they matter for on‑device AI.

## What “1.58‑bit” means (in plain terms)

BitNet b1.58 constrains every weight to one of three values: -1, 0, or +1. That ternary constraint makes linear layers extremely cheap to evaluate: multiplying by -1, 0, or 1 reduces to a sign flip, a zero, or identity. In other words, for a single weight $w$ and activation $x$,

$w \in \{-1,0,1\} \quad\Rightarrow\quad w\cdot x \in \{-x,\,0,\,x\}.$

Weights are produced by a simple quantization scheme that scales by the per‑matrix average absolute value and then rounds/clips to the ternary set. Concretely (notation as in the paper):

$\displaystyle \widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\Big(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\Big),$

$\displaystyle \mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))),$

$\displaystyle \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.$

Activations are quantized to 8 bits and scaled per token to the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ (the paper deliberately avoids zero‑point quantization to keep kernels simple).

BitNet b1.58 otherwise keeps a LLaMA‑style Transformer stack (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, biases removed), which makes it straightforward to plug into existing toolchains.

## Why this is a practical win for phones and edge boxes

The on‑device deployment constraints boil down to three resources: memory capacity, memory bandwidth, and energy (plus the type of compute available on a device—CPUs on phones are optimized for integer arithmetic more than FP16 matrix multiply). BitNet b1.58 addresses all three.

Memory and bandwidth. Ternary weights plus 8‑bit activations cut weight storage and transfer costs dramatically. That reduces DRAM-to-SRAM traffic during inference, and makes it much easier to fit useful model sizes into the limited memory budget of edge devices. The paper reports that, at 3B model size, BitNet b1.58 uses about 3.55× less GPU memory than an FP16 LLaMA baseline; for larger models the relative saving grows because the proportion of time spent in large linear layers increases.

Compute and energy. Because weight multiplications collapse to sign/zero/identity operations, the heavy FP16 multiply–accumulate work is replaced by integer additions and sign handling. Using an energy model cited in the paper, matrix multiplications in BitNet b1.58 consume about 71.4× less arithmetic energy (on a 7nm process) than FP16 matrix multiplications. That is a dramatic per‑op saving and directly translates into lower battery draw for inference work that is dominated by dense layers.

Latency and throughput. On GPU measurements (FasterTransformer with a 2‑bit kernel), BitNet b1.58 runs substantially faster. Examples reported in the paper:

- At 3B parameters, BitNet b1.58 is about 2.71× faster and uses 3.55× less GPU memory compared to an FP16 LLaMA-like model trained with the same data.
- A 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model is 2.4× faster and uses 3.32× less memory while outperforming a 3B FP16 LLaMA.
- At large scale, a 70B BitNet b1.58 model runs about 4.1× faster than its FP16 counterpart.
- In a throughput test on two 80GB A100 GPUs (pipeline parallelism, sequence length 512), a 70B BitNet b1.58 model supported up to 11× the batch size and achieved about 8.9× higher throughput than the FP16 baseline.

These numbers show that the advantage is not just theoretical—on practical inference stacks it translates into multiple× speedups and more concurrent work per device.

Longer on‑device context (KV cache). KV cache size grows with sequence length and is typically stored in 16‑bit activations on common stacks; the paper points out that moving activations to 8 bits already doubles the feasible context length for the same memory budget. This directly helps real‑world on‑device assistants that want longer conversational histories without running out of RAM. The authors also note that further lossless compression to 4 bits (or lower) is possible research direction.

CPU friendliness and system integration. Integer additions and low‑bit kernels are a much better match for mobile CPUs than FP16 GEMMs. The architecture keeps LLaMA‑compatible components so integration into common runtimes (Hugging Face, vLLM, llama.cpp) is straightforward—meaning developers can reuse software stacks and focus on device ports and kernel optimizations.

## What this enables for on‑device assistants

Putting the above together, BitNet b1.58 makes several concrete capabilities far more realistic on phones and edge boxes:

- Private, offline assistants: smaller models that still match FP16 performance (starting around 3B in the authors’ experiments) can run locally without cloud calls, reducing latency and removing privacy leakages.
- Night‑long or always‑on assistants: substantially lower arithmetic energy for matrix multiply reduces battery impact for repeated or sustained inference.
- Longer context windows on-device: 8‑bit activations (and future activation compression) push practical context length upward within the same RAM footprint.
- Higher concurrency on local gateways: better throughput and smaller memory per model mean more concurrent sessions/streams can be served from a single edge box.

The paper also highlights that these gains scale: a 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient (in latency, memory, and energy) than a 3B FP16 model, and similar pairings hold as models grow (30B b1.58 beats 7B FP16, 70B b1.58 beats 13B FP16 on efficiency).

## Practical caveats and where more work is needed

BitNet b1.58 is not just a drop‑in quantization of an FP16 model—these models are trained from scratch with ternary weights and 8‑bit activations. The paper’s experiments pretrain models on RedPajama (100B tokens) and also report a 2T‑token 3B experiment where BitNet b1.58 outperforms StableLM‑3B on a small end‑task suite. So expect that, today, the best route is training or finetuning with the low‑bit recipe rather than naive post‑hoc quantization.

Other practical points:

- Some components (for example, token embeddings) may remain stored in higher precision in current implementations; embeddings’ relative cost shrinks as models grow, but for very small models embedding footprint matters.
- The measured kernels used FasterTransformer and an integration of a 2‑bit kernel from Ladder—there is still room to optimize device‑specific kernels (mobile SIMD, NPUs, or specialized hardware).
- Hardware and system co‑design matters: the greatest wins happen when memory system, quantized kernels, and model recipes are optimized together. The authors suggest new hardware units optimized for 1‑bit/ternary compute would amplify the benefits.

## Final takeaway

BitNet b1.58 demonstrates that you do not have to compromise model quality to get orders‑of‑magnitude improvements in the resources that matter for on‑device AI: memory, bandwidth, latency, and energy. By converting most heavy computation into low‑bit integer additions and pruning the weight alphabet to \{-1,0,1\}, these models make private, offline, and longer‑context assistants much more realistic on phones and edge boxes—all without the “battery anxiety” that typically kills local LLM use cases.

If you care about on‑device assistants, the practical next steps are clear: explore low‑bit training recipes for your target model sizes, benchmark device‑specific kernels for ternary weights + 8‑bit activations, and consider system/hardware co‑design to push the remaining overheads out of the way. The paper shows the algorithms are there; the rest is engineering.



# Beyond the Model: 1‑Bit MoE and the Case for New LLM Hardware

Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures give us a cheap way to scale capacity: route each input token to a small subset of "experts" and avoid computing dense feed‑forward layers for every token. But MoE's arithmetic savings often run headlong into two hard limits: memory (storing many expert parameters) and network/interconnect (moving activations and routing across devices). BitNet b1.58 — a ternary, 1.58‑bit LLM variant — materially changes that calculus. By shrinking weights to $\{-1,0,1\}$ and using 8‑bit activations, it reduces storage and bandwidth needs and converts much of the compute to add‑heavy integer operations. That combination both makes MoE deployments more practical and motivates a rethink of accelerator design around low‑precision, add‑centric primitives.

Below I summarize why that matters, what BitNet b1.58 does under the hood, the concrete implications for MoE, and what hardware co‑design should prioritize.

## MoE at scale: big capacity, hidden costs

The attraction of MoE is simple: if each token only touches a few experts, total FLOPs per token drop while model capacity (total parameter count) balloons. The practical price is elsewhere:

- Memory: every expert contributes parameters that must be stored (often across many devices). High capacity means lots of memory, even if compute per token is low.
- Interconnect/communication: routing tokens to experts frequently requires moving activations between chips or nodes, which becomes a throughput and latency bottleneck.
- KV / activation caches: long context inference and MoE routing both inflate activation storage and bandwidth requirements.

In short, MoE trades arithmetic cost for memory and network cost. That trade is often the dominant limiter for deployment.

## What BitNet b1.58 changes

BitNet b1.58 is a re‑engineered Transformer that replaces dense linear layers with a BitLinear operator and constrains weights to ternary values $\{-1,0,1\}$. Key design points from the paper:

- Weights are ternary and encoded effectively as 1.58 bits per parameter (the information content of three states is $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ bits).
- Activations are quantized to 8 bits and, importantly, scaled per token to the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ to avoid expensive zero‑point quantization.
- Quantization for weights uses an "absmean" scale and clipping:
  \[
    \widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
  \]
  \[
    \mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))),
  \]
  \[
    \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
  \]
- The model keeps LLaMA‑style design choices (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings) and is trained from scratch with 1.58‑bit weights and 8‑bit activations (not just post‑training quantized).
- Because weights contain explicit zeros, ternary weights provide an extra degree of sparsity/feature filtering compared to pure binary 1‑bit schemes.

Two implementation consequences are critical for system designers: matrix multiplies become largely integer addition work (ternary weights mean many multiplies reduce to sign flips or skips), and the overall storage footprint of model weights drops sharply.

## Concrete gains reported in the paper

The BitNet b1.58 experiments were trained from scratch on the RedPajama corpus and compared to FP16 LLaMA baselines. Highlights:

- Starting at the 3B scale, BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 LLaMA on perplexity and zero‑shot downstream tasks. A 3.9B b1.58 model outperforms LLaMA 3B while using less memory and running faster.
- Inference cost: at 3B, b1.58 is 2.71× faster and uses 3.55× less GPU memory than FP16 LLaMA (measured with NVIDIA FasterTransformer and a 2‑bit kernel from Ladder). The speedup increases with model scale; a 70B b1.58 runs 4.1× faster than the FP16 70B baseline.
- Throughput: on two 80GB A100 GPUs with pipeline parallelism, b1.58 70B supports up to 11× the batch size of FP16 LLaMA 70B, yielding an 8.9× higher throughput.
- Energy: focusing on matrix multiplication (the dominant cost), the paper estimates a 71.4× reduction in arithmetic operations energy for matmuls on 7nm chips versus FP16 multiply‑adds. This energy advantage grows with model size because linear layers dominate runtime for larger models.
- New scaling equivalences the paper highlights:
  - 13B b1.58 is more efficient (latency, memory, energy) than 3B FP16.
  - 30B b1.58 is more efficient than 7B FP16.
  - 70B b1.58 is more efficient than 13B FP16.

Taken together, these results show you can get FP16‑class accuracy with a much smaller execution and energy budget once you move to ternary weights + 8‑bit activations.

## How this relaxes MoE bottlenecks

MoE deployments are constrained because the full expert inventory (parameters + associated activations) must be stored and often distributed across devices. BitNet b1.58 shifts the balance in several ways:

- Smaller weights: ternary weights drastically reduce the stored parameter footprint (the paper reports multi‑fold GPU memory reductions in end‑to‑end measurements). Less weight per expert means more experts can fit on the same device or rack.
- Less bandwidth per activation: 8‑bit activations and per‑token scaling reduce the amount of data that must be moved during routing and for KV caches. With lower activation precision, the effective size of caches and routed payloads is smaller.
- Add‑centric compute: since much of the expert work becomes integer additions and conditional sign operations (zero weights imply skips), the arithmetic units and memory subsystem can be optimized to favor high‑throughput, energy‑cheap integer add streaming rather than FP multiply‑accumulate pipelines.
- Increased on‑chip capacity: with smaller parameters, SRAM can hold a larger fraction of the model and expert parameters, reducing DRAM transfers and cross‑device network traffic. The paper explicitly notes that reduced memory footprint lowers the number of devices required to deploy MoE and cuts activation transfer overhead.

In practice, these effects mean you can place more experts per device, reduce cross‑device routing, and in some cases (depending on device memory and model size) eliminate a lot of the interconnect traffic that made MoE expensive to run.

## What hardware co‑design should prioritize

BitNet b1.58 doesn't just suggest a new software recipe — it opens the door to a different accelerator and system design point. If MoE + ternary LLMs are going to be competitive in production, hardware teams should explore the following directions (all motivated by the paper's observations):

- Add‑centric ALUs. Optimize integer add and conditional sign/gate operations rather than FP multiply‑accumulate as the primary fast path. Ternary weight multiplies can be implemented as sign flips or conditional zeroing; dedicated units for these patterns will be cheaper and more energy efficient than wide FP MAC arrays.
- Native support for ternary / 2‑bit kernels. The paper integrates a 2‑bit kernel (Ladder) for inference. Exposing hardware primitives to accelerate 2‑bit/ternary kernels (efficient encodings, wide vector adds, fast decompression) will pay off.
- Larger low‑latency SRAM and smarter caching. With weights 1.58 bits each, a greater share of model parameters can be kept on‑chip. Hardware should optimize SRAM capacity and bandwidth to hold multiple experts and their routing state, minimizing DRAM and interconnect usage.
- Compact on‑chip formats and DMA paths. Efficient compressed storage formats for ternary weights and token‑wise activation scaling (the paper uses per‑token scaling to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$) reduce DMA pressure. Hardware DMA engines that understand these formats can stream experts into compute units with minimal CPU coordination.
- Network‑on‑chip and interconnect tuned for low‑precision activation traffic. If routing payloads shrink from 16 bits to 8 (or lower), network topologies and routers can be re‑balanced toward lower‑latency, lower‑power operation with higher effective throughput for MoE bursts.
- Support for large, low‑precision KV caches. The paper points out that halving activation precision (16→8 bit) effectively doubles context length for the same memory budget; hardware that natively manages low‑precision KV caches and fast packing/unpacking will enable longer contexts and reduce off‑chip traffic.
- Software/kernel co‑design. Finally, hardware needs to be accompanied by optimized kernels (the paper uses FasterTransformer and Ladder) that map ternary/2‑bit operators efficiently onto the new primitives.

Groq is cited in the paper as an example of specialized LLM accelerators; BitNet b1.58 suggests the next generation of accelerators should go further and be explicitly tuned for ternary weights + add‑heavy execution.

## What this could unlock

Put together, these changes reshape practical MoE deployments:

- Denser expert placement: more experts per chip means less cross‑device routing and lower latency.
- Cheaper, higher‑throughput inference: the paper shows multi‑× throughput and latency improvements as model size grows (e.g., 4.1× faster at 70B, 8.9× throughput on two A100s).
- Energy savings at scale: dramatic arithmetic energy reductions for matmuls in the paper (71.4× for matmuls on 7nm) translate to lower operating costs and lower thermal limits.
- More practical long‑context and edge deployments: smaller KV caches and lighter compute allow longer contexts on the same hardware and better suitability for CPU/edge environments.

All of these outcomes lower the operational friction that has held MoE back from broader deployment.

## Takeaway

BitNet b1.58 demonstrates that you can recover FP16‑class modeling performance while changing the entire system trade space: ternary weights + 8‑bit activations shrink memory and bandwidth demands and turn heavy FP MAC workloads into add‑dominant integer workloads. For MoE in particular, that reduces the two biggest frictions — memory and interconnect — making large, high‑capacity expert models far easier to place and run.

If MoE is going to be a mainstream scaling strategy, we should stop treating model and hardware as separate problems. The BitNet b1.58 results make a clear case for co‑design: new accelerators that prioritize add‑heavy integer kernels, on‑chip capacity for compressed experts, and interconnects tuned for low‑precision activation streaming can unlock the next wave of scalable, efficient LLM deployments. The paper ends with a call to action for such hardware — given these system‑level wins, it seems worth answering.
