# Inside BitNet b1.58: The Simple Math Trick That Ditches Most Multiplications

Large language models are getting smarter — and hungrier for compute. BitNet b1.58 is an elegant, pragmatic twist on the standard Transformer that swaps the heavy-weight floating-point multiply-accumulate workhorse for mostly cheap integer additions. The trick is simple: force weights to be ternary $\{-1,0,1\}$ and quantize activations to compact signed 8-bit integers with symmetric scaling. The result: most per-element multiplications disappear, leaving fast, low-energy integer accumulation and a tiny number of scale multiplications at the end.

Below I unpack the core math (the "absmean" quantizer), show exactly how matrix multiplies become sign-based adds/subtracts, and explain the implementation choices that make BitNet b1.58 a drop-in‑friendly option for LLaMA-style stacks.

## The core idea in one sentence
Replace each dense weight matrix $W$ with a tiny ternary matrix $\widetilde{W}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{n\times m}$ (about $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ bits per weight), quantize activations to signed 8‑bit integers, compute integer accumulations of $\widetilde{W}$ times the int8 activations (add/subtract/skip), and apply a small floating-point scale afterwards. That removes almost all FP multiplies from the dominant matrix multiplications.

## Ternary weights and “1.58 bits”
BitNet b1.58 stores every weight as one of three values $\{-1,0,1\}$. Storing three discrete states costs only $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ bits per parameter, hence the name “1.58‑bit” model. Compared to 16-bit floating point, this is a huge reduction in raw weight representation and — more importantly — it enables very different, much cheaper arithmetic during inference.

## The absmean quantizer — math and intuition
Weights are quantized per matrix using a simple scale based on the mean absolute weight (the “absmean”). Concretely:

- Compute the per-matrix scale
  $$
  \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|.
  $$
- Quantize each weight to $\{-1,0,1\}$ by scaling by $\gamma$ (plus a tiny $\epsilon$ for stability) and rounding/clipping:
  $$
  \widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\,-1,\,1\right),
  $$
  where
  $$
  \mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max\!\big(a,\min(b,\mathrm{round}(x))\big).
  $$

Intuition: dividing by the average absolute weight highlights entries that are meaningfully larger or smaller than the typical weight. After rounding and clipping, large positive weights become $+1$, large negative weights become $-1$, and small weights (near zero relative to the mean) become $0$. The scale $\gamma$ is kept so the original full-precision matrix can be approximated as $W \approx \gamma \widetilde{W}$.

## How matrix multiplies turn into integer additions (step by step)
This is the central arithmetic trick.

1. Express the original matmul with the quantized weights and a (signed) integer-quantized activation:
   - Let $A$ be a (float) activation vector or matrix. Quantize it per token to an int8 representation $Q_A$ with scale $s_a$ so that $A \approx s_a Q_A$ and $Q_A\in[-Q_b,Q_b]\subset\mathbb{Z}$.
   - Using $W \approx \gamma\widetilde{W}$, the product is
     $$
     W A \approx (\gamma s_a)\,(\widetilde{W} Q_A).
     $$

2. Compute the inner integer product $\widetilde{W} Q_A$ directly in integer arithmetic. Because each element $\widetilde{W}_{ij}\in\{-1,0,1\}$, each scalar multiplication reduces to:
   - Multiply by $+1$: add $Q_A_j$ into the accumulator.
   - Multiply by $-1$: subtract $Q_A_j$ from the accumulator.
   - Multiply by $0$: skip (no contribution).

   In other words, the entire dense matrix multiply becomes many conditional integer additions/subtractions and skips. With int8 inputs, the accumulators are integer (e.g., int32), not FP16 multiplies.

3. After the integer accumulation is done, convert the accumulated integer result back to floating point by applying the (small) scalar factor $(\gamma s_a)$ per row/column as needed.

Two practical observations:
- The vast majority of compute in a dense matmul is eliminated as FP multiplies; it's replaced by cheap integer adds/subtracts and sparse skips.
- The remaining floating-point work is a small number of scalar multiplies (the per-matrix or per-block scales), which is negligible compared to the removed MACs.

The paper summarizes this as requiring "almost no multiplication operations for matrix multiplication" and reports that most of the computation becomes INT8 additions.

## Activations: 8-bit symmetric scaling and zero-point-free math
BitNet b1.58 keeps activations at 8 bits. A key implementation choice is symmetric per-token scaling: activations are scaled to the signed integer range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ (rather than to $[0,Q_b]$). This eliminates a zero-point offset in quantized activations, which simplifies the integer arithmetic (no offset compensation) and is friendlier to system-level kernels.

The approach in the paper is:
- Do not rescale activations before nonlinearities to $[0,Q_b]$.
- Instead, scale activations per token to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ so that the integer representation is symmetric around zero.
This symmetry makes the $\{-1,0,1\}$ weight multiplications behave exactly as add/subtract/skip on the signed int8 values, without extra bias terms.

## Implementation and LLaMA‑style compatibility
BitNet b1.58 is a drop-in variant of the BitNet architecture: it replaces every usual dense layer (e.g., PyTorch's `nn.Linear`) with `BitLinear` that implements the ternary-weight, int8-activation computation described above. To maximize adoption and interoperability with popular open-source LLM stacks, the model uses LLaMA-like choices for the rest of the architecture: RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary position embeddings (RoPE), and removal of all biases. These choices make it straightforward to plug BitNet b1.58 into toolkits such as Hugging Face, vLLM, and llama.cpp with minimal modification.

For inference latency measurements the authors integrated a 2-bit kernel from Ladder into NVIDIA’s FasterTransformer, showing real-world speed and memory wins (details below).

## Practical results reported in the paper
All models in the comparison were trained from scratch on the RedPajama dataset for 100B tokens. Highlights from the reported empirical results:

- Perplexity and task performance: BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision (FP16) LLaMA baselines starting at the 3B model size when trained with the same setup (model size, training tokens, etc.).
- Latency and memory (examples from the paper):
  - At 3B, BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 perplexity while being about 2.71× faster and using 3.55× less GPU memory.
  - A 3.9B BitNet b1.58 model is 2.4× faster and uses 3.32× less memory while outperforming a FP16 LLaMA 3B model.
  - At larger scales, speedups increase; BitNet b1.58 70B is reported as 4.1× faster than the FP16 LLaMA 70B baseline (measured with a 2-bit kernel in FasterTransformer).
- Energy: Focusing on matrix-multiply arithmetic energy (the dominant cost), BitNet b1.58 is estimated to save about 71.4× arithmetic energy for matrix multiplication on a 7nm process compared to FP16 multiply-accumulate.
- Throughput: On two 80GB A100 GPUs using pipeline parallelism, BitNet b1.58 70B supports up to 11× the batch size of the FP16 LLaMA 70B implementation in the experiment, yielding an 8.9× higher throughput.
- Scaling equivalences reported:
  - 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient (latency, memory, energy) than a 3B FP16 LLM.
  - 30B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 7B FP16.
  - 70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 13B FP16.

The paper also trained a 3B BitNet b1.58 model for 2T tokens using the StableLM-3B data recipe and found it to outperform the StableLM 3B baseline across a handful of downstream tasks.

## Why the zero ($0$) in $\{-1,0,1\}$ matters
Binary $\{-1,+1\}$ weights can represent sign but cannot explicitly drop a connection. Adding $0$ introduces a simple, effective form of feature filtering or gating: small-magnitude weights become zero and cease to contribute. The paper notes that this explicit support for filtering increases modeling capability and helps BitNet b1.58 match FP16 performance at practical model sizes.

## A concise inference pipeline
Putting the pieces together, a single inference matmul looks like:

1. For a stored full-precision $W$, precompute $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum |W_{ij}|$ and store $\widetilde{W}=\mathrm{RoundClip}(W/(\gamma+\epsilon),-1,1)$ (this is static after quantization).
2. For each token, quantize activations: $A \approx s_a Q_A$ with $Q_A\in\mathbb{Z}_8$ in $[-Q_b,Q_b]$.
3. Compute integer accumulation $S = \widetilde{W} Q_A$ using add/subtract/skip only.
4. Convert to float output by multiplying $S$ by the scalar $(\gamma s_a)$.

Step 3 is where the multiply-heavy work disappears; step 4 is a small number of scale multiplications.

## Caveats and future directions
The paper highlights several interesting directions enabled by this approach: 1-bit Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) designs with smaller device counts and less communication overhead, better native support for long contexts thanks to reduced KV cache size, improved feasibility of running large models on edge and mobile hardware, and the opportunity to design hardware (LPUs or other accelerators) that are native to 1-bit/1.58-bit compute patterns.

One practical note: the experiments measured latency and memory using a 2-bit kernel (Ladder) integrated into FasterTransformer; the paper suggests there is still room for further kernel and hardware optimization.

## Takeaway
BitNet b1.58 is a clear, implementable example of how aggressive, structured quantization (ternary weights + signed 8-bit activations) changes the arithmetic story for LLMs. By turning the innermost multiplications into conditional integer adds/subtracts and deferring scaling to a small number of post-accumulation multiplies, it achieves large reductions in latency, memory footprint, and energy while matching FP16 performance from practical model sizes upward. If you care about inference cost at scale, the math is simple and the implications are big.
