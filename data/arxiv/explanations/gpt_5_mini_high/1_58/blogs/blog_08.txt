\title{Blogs about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

# Beyond the Model: 1‑Bit MoE and the Case for New LLM Hardware

Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures give us a cheap way to scale capacity: route each input token to a small subset of "experts" and avoid computing dense feed‑forward layers for every token. But MoE's arithmetic savings often run headlong into two hard limits: memory (storing many expert parameters) and network/interconnect (moving activations and routing across devices). BitNet b1.58 — a ternary, 1.58‑bit LLM variant — materially changes that calculus. By shrinking weights to $\{-1,0,1\}$ and using 8‑bit activations, it reduces storage and bandwidth needs and converts much of the compute to add‑heavy integer operations. That combination both makes MoE deployments more practical and motivates a rethink of accelerator design around low‑precision, add‑centric primitives.

Below I summarize why that matters, what BitNet b1.58 does under the hood, the concrete implications for MoE, and what hardware co‑design should prioritize.

## MoE at scale: big capacity, hidden costs

The attraction of MoE is simple: if each token only touches a few experts, total FLOPs per token drop while model capacity (total parameter count) balloons. The practical price is elsewhere:

- Memory: every expert contributes parameters that must be stored (often across many devices). High capacity means lots of memory, even if compute per token is low.
- Interconnect/communication: routing tokens to experts frequently requires moving activations between chips or nodes, which becomes a throughput and latency bottleneck.
- KV / activation caches: long context inference and MoE routing both inflate activation storage and bandwidth requirements.

In short, MoE trades arithmetic cost for memory and network cost. That trade is often the dominant limiter for deployment.

## What BitNet b1.58 changes

BitNet b1.58 is a re‑engineered Transformer that replaces dense linear layers with a BitLinear operator and constrains weights to ternary values $\{-1,0,1\}$. Key design points from the paper:

- Weights are ternary and encoded effectively as 1.58 bits per parameter (the information content of three states is $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ bits).
- Activations are quantized to 8 bits and, importantly, scaled per token to the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ to avoid expensive zero‑point quantization.
- Quantization for weights uses an "absmean" scale and clipping:
  \[
    \widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right),
  \]
  \[
    \mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x))),
  \]
  \[
    \gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|.
  \]
- The model keeps LLaMA‑style design choices (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings) and is trained from scratch with 1.58‑bit weights and 8‑bit activations (not just post‑training quantized).
- Because weights contain explicit zeros, ternary weights provide an extra degree of sparsity/feature filtering compared to pure binary 1‑bit schemes.

Two implementation consequences are critical for system designers: matrix multiplies become largely integer addition work (ternary weights mean many multiplies reduce to sign flips or skips), and the overall storage footprint of model weights drops sharply.

## Concrete gains reported in the paper

The BitNet b1.58 experiments were trained from scratch on the RedPajama corpus and compared to FP16 LLaMA baselines. Highlights:

- Starting at the 3B scale, BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 LLaMA on perplexity and zero‑shot downstream tasks. A 3.9B b1.58 model outperforms LLaMA 3B while using less memory and running faster.
- Inference cost: at 3B, b1.58 is 2.71× faster and uses 3.55× less GPU memory than FP16 LLaMA (measured with NVIDIA FasterTransformer and a 2‑bit kernel from Ladder). The speedup increases with model scale; a 70B b1.58 runs 4.1× faster than the FP16 70B baseline.
- Throughput: on two 80GB A100 GPUs with pipeline parallelism, b1.58 70B supports up to 11× the batch size of FP16 LLaMA 70B, yielding an 8.9× higher throughput.
- Energy: focusing on matrix multiplication (the dominant cost), the paper estimates a 71.4× reduction in arithmetic operations energy for matmuls on 7nm chips versus FP16 multiply‑adds. This energy advantage grows with model size because linear layers dominate runtime for larger models.
- New scaling equivalences the paper highlights:
  - 13B b1.58 is more efficient (latency, memory, energy) than 3B FP16.
  - 30B b1.58 is more efficient than 7B FP16.
  - 70B b1.58 is more efficient than 13B FP16.

Taken together, these results show you can get FP16‑class accuracy with a much smaller execution and energy budget once you move to ternary weights + 8‑bit activations.

## How this relaxes MoE bottlenecks

MoE deployments are constrained because the full expert inventory (parameters + associated activations) must be stored and often distributed across devices. BitNet b1.58 shifts the balance in several ways:

- Smaller weights: ternary weights drastically reduce the stored parameter footprint (the paper reports multi‑fold GPU memory reductions in end‑to‑end measurements). Less weight per expert means more experts can fit on the same device or rack.
- Less bandwidth per activation: 8‑bit activations and per‑token scaling reduce the amount of data that must be moved during routing and for KV caches. With lower activation precision, the effective size of caches and routed payloads is smaller.
- Add‑centric compute: since much of the expert work becomes integer additions and conditional sign operations (zero weights imply skips), the arithmetic units and memory subsystem can be optimized to favor high‑throughput, energy‑cheap integer add streaming rather than FP multiply‑accumulate pipelines.
- Increased on‑chip capacity: with smaller parameters, SRAM can hold a larger fraction of the model and expert parameters, reducing DRAM transfers and cross‑device network traffic. The paper explicitly notes that reduced memory footprint lowers the number of devices required to deploy MoE and cuts activation transfer overhead.

In practice, these effects mean you can place more experts per device, reduce cross‑device routing, and in some cases (depending on device memory and model size) eliminate a lot of the interconnect traffic that made MoE expensive to run.

## What hardware co‑design should prioritize

BitNet b1.58 doesn't just suggest a new software recipe — it opens the door to a different accelerator and system design point. If MoE + ternary LLMs are going to be competitive in production, hardware teams should explore the following directions (all motivated by the paper's observations):

- Add‑centric ALUs. Optimize integer add and conditional sign/gate operations rather than FP multiply‑accumulate as the primary fast path. Ternary weight multiplies can be implemented as sign flips or conditional zeroing; dedicated units for these patterns will be cheaper and more energy efficient than wide FP MAC arrays.
- Native support for ternary / 2‑bit kernels. The paper integrates a 2‑bit kernel (Ladder) for inference. Exposing hardware primitives to accelerate 2‑bit/ternary kernels (efficient encodings, wide vector adds, fast decompression) will pay off.
- Larger low‑latency SRAM and smarter caching. With weights 1.58 bits each, a greater share of model parameters can be kept on‑chip. Hardware should optimize SRAM capacity and bandwidth to hold multiple experts and their routing state, minimizing DRAM and interconnect usage.
- Compact on‑chip formats and DMA paths. Efficient compressed storage formats for ternary weights and token‑wise activation scaling (the paper uses per‑token scaling to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$) reduce DMA pressure. Hardware DMA engines that understand these formats can stream experts into compute units with minimal CPU coordination.
- Network‑on‑chip and interconnect tuned for low‑precision activation traffic. If routing payloads shrink from 16 bits to 8 (or lower), network topologies and routers can be re‑balanced toward lower‑latency, lower‑power operation with higher effective throughput for MoE bursts.
- Support for large, low‑precision KV caches. The paper points out that halving activation precision (16→8 bit) effectively doubles context length for the same memory budget; hardware that natively manages low‑precision KV caches and fast packing/unpacking will enable longer contexts and reduce off‑chip traffic.
- Software/kernel co‑design. Finally, hardware needs to be accompanied by optimized kernels (the paper uses FasterTransformer and Ladder) that map ternary/2‑bit operators efficiently onto the new primitives.

Groq is cited in the paper as an example of specialized LLM accelerators; BitNet b1.58 suggests the next generation of accelerators should go further and be explicitly tuned for ternary weights + add‑heavy execution.

## What this could unlock

Put together, these changes reshape practical MoE deployments:

- Denser expert placement: more experts per chip means less cross‑device routing and lower latency.
- Cheaper, higher‑throughput inference: the paper shows multi‑× throughput and latency improvements as model size grows (e.g., 4.1× faster at 70B, 8.9× throughput on two A100s).
- Energy savings at scale: dramatic arithmetic energy reductions for matmuls in the paper (71.4× for matmuls on 7nm) translate to lower operating costs and lower thermal limits.
- More practical long‑context and edge deployments: smaller KV caches and lighter compute allow longer contexts on the same hardware and better suitability for CPU/edge environments.

All of these outcomes lower the operational friction that has held MoE back from broader deployment.

## Takeaway

BitNet b1.58 demonstrates that you can recover FP16‑class modeling performance while changing the entire system trade space: ternary weights + 8‑bit activations shrink memory and bandwidth demands and turn heavy FP MAC workloads into add‑dominant integer workloads. For MoE in particular, that reduces the two biggest frictions — memory and interconnect — making large, high‑capacity expert models far easier to place and run.

If MoE is going to be a mainstream scaling strategy, we should stop treating model and hardware as separate problems. The BitNet b1.58 results make a clear case for co‑design: new accelerators that prioritize add‑heavy integer kernels, on‑chip capacity for compressed experts, and interconnects tuned for low‑precision activation streaming can unlock the next wave of scalable, efficient LLM deployments. The paper ends with a call to action for such hardware — given these system‑level wins, it seems worth answering.
