\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Introduction: Why Patches Scale Better Than Tokens

# Limitations of fixed-vocabulary tokenization
Modern large language models rely on a short preprocessing step called tokenization: a fixed, finite vocabulary of multi-byte tokens is learned or chosen ahead of training, and every input string is deterministically mapped to a sequence of those tokens. This design gives practical advantages (compact sequences for many languages, small embedding tables, and a fixed discrete output space), but it also imposes several structural limitations that affect performance and robustness.

First, tokenization encodes a particular compression of byte strings that is independent of the model’s prediction problem. That compression is induced by heuristics or algorithms that optimize for frequency/compression, not for how hard it is to predict the next byte under the learned model. As a result, the token boundaries and sizes chosen by a tokenizer are often misaligned with where a model should expend its compute. Tokens therefore introduce domain and modality sensitivity: a static tokenizer that is tuned on one mixture of languages or content types can under-represent rare domains or scripts, producing suboptimal compressions and hurting downstream performance in those modalities.

Second, tokenizers can be brittle to small, character-level perturbations. A tiny edit (an inserted character, casing change, or accidental symbol) can change the tokenization pattern drastically, producing a very different discrete sequence and therefore a very different pattern of compute and learned behaviour. This noise fragility means token-based models can lose performance on noisy inputs unless the tokenizer and training mixture incidentally covered those perturbations.

Third, because token-based models operate on opaque token identifiers, they do not have direct access to the underlying byte-level constituents of their inputs. This makes certain kinds of orthographic reasoning and character-manipulation tasks (spelling, grapheme-to-phoneme mapping, fine-grained string edits) harder to learn: the model must implicitly reconstruct character structure from token ids instead of observing bytes directly.

Finally, these issues combine into multilingual inequity. A fixed token vocabulary gives different effective granularities for different languages and scripts (for example, frequent substrings in high-resource languages are compressed into few tokens, while low-resource scripts may require many tokens per word). Because a model allocates the same compute per token, this uneven compression leads to unequal compute per meaningful linguistic unit across languages.

In short, tokenization fixes where and how much compute is applied, and the tokenization heuristic is not tailored to the actual predictive difficulty of different byte positions in context.

# Why historical byte-level LMs were costly
Training and running models directly on raw bytes avoids many of the above issues, but naive byte-level models face a fundamental cost problem: bytes lengthen sequences. If a word compresses to one token under a tokenizer but to many bytes, the model must process many more positions. Transformer architectures expend non-trivial work per input position, and at large scale the dominant per-position cost comes from the feed-forward (FFN) layers: these large MLPs are applied at every position and quickly dominate total FLOPs as model size grows.

Prior work reduced the attention cost of long byte sequences (for instance by using sparse or specialized attention mechanisms), which helps when attention is the bottleneck. However, when models scale and FFN layers dominate compute, attention-speedups alone are not enough: the expensive FFNs still run once per byte. This is the key computational obstacle to making byte-level models competitive at large scale.

What is needed is a way to reduce the number of positions at which the heavy global computation (the big FFNs inside the main Transformer) must run, while still retaining the ability to read and produce raw bytes. That is the motivation for grouping bytes into larger computation units when the data is predictable and keeping fine-grained steps where the data is complex.

# Core idea: dynamically allocate compute via entropy-aware patches
The central idea of the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) is to make the grouping of bytes into computation units dynamic and data-driven. Formally, a patching function $f_p$ segments a sequence of bytes $\mathbf{x} = \{x_i\}_{i=1}^n$ into a shorter sequence of patches $\mathbf{p} = \{p_j\}_{j=1}^m$ by marking certain byte positions as starts of new patches. Concretely, $f_p$ maps each byte index $i$ to $\{0,1\}$ where a value of $1$ indicates a new patch start, producing $m < n$ patches in practice. The effective computational load of the main Transformer is determined primarily by the number of such patches: fewer patches means fewer heavy Transformer steps.

BLT proposes entropy-aware patching: use a small byte-level next-byte language model to estimate uncertainty at each byte and use those entropy estimates to decide patch boundaries. If $p_e(\cdot\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{<i})$ denotes the entropy model’s predictive distribution over the byte vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ at position $i$, the next-byte uncertainty is computed as

\[
H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_e(x_i=v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_e(x_i=v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}).
\]

(Practically, BLT experiments with two simple decision rules built on these entropies: a global threshold rule $H(x_t) > \theta_g$, and an approximate-monotonicity rule $H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r$ that marks positions where entropy increases relative to the immediate past.)

The intuition is simple and powerful. Many byte predictions in natural data are easy and low-entropy (for example, the trailing letters of a common word once its initial character(s) are known). Predictive complexity is concentrated at a few “hard” bytes (e.g., the start of a new word or the first character of a new token), and those are the positions where we want the large global Transformer to apply more capacity. By starting a new patch at high-entropy positions, BLT allocates more compute to difficult decisions and groups predictable runs of bytes into longer patches that are processed as a single step by the heavy global model.

There are simpler patching alternatives. Fixed-stride patching (group every $k$ bytes) is trivial to implement but cannot adapt to context. Space-based patching (start a new patch after space-like bytes) aligns computation to word boundaries, which is often helpful, but it is a rule-based heuristic that cannot vary patch sizes by uncertainty. Entropy-aware patching is the data-driven mechanism that adapts patch size to local predictability while preserving an important practical constraint: patching must be incremental during generation. BLT requires that $f_p$ be incremental in the sense that

\[
f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i},
\]

so that patch boundaries for a prefix do not depend on unseen future bytes. Standard subword tokenization (BPE) is not an incremental patching scheme: the same prefix can be tokenized differently depending on the continuation, which makes it unsuitable for on-the-fly patch decisions in autoregressive generation without additional markers.

Entropy patching is computed as a lightweight preprocessing step during dataloading, so it is inexpensive to apply during training. Because the entropy model is small and its scope is limited, BLT can afford to compute these entropies on the training data and use them to derive context-sensitive patches without changing the main model’s parameterization.

# BLT at a glance: small local byte models plus a large global latent transformer
BLT is organized around three interacting modules that together let the model operate on bytes while running the heavy global Transformer at a much lower temporal resolution.

At the bottom are two lightweight byte-level models: a local encoder $\mathcal{E}$ and a local decoder $\mathcal{D}$. The encoder maps raw byte embeddings into a sequence of latent patch representations, one per patch. The decoder converts latent patch outputs back into byte-level predictions. Sitting between them is the latent global Transformer $\mathcal{G}$, a large autoregressive Transformer which consumes the sequence of patch representations and models long-range dependencies across patches. The global model uses a block-causal attention mask so that attention is restricted to the current and earlier patches within the document; it is this model that contains the bulk of BLT’s parameters and the majority of the FLOPs during pretraining and inference.

The local encoder is a small Transformer stack ($l_{\mathcal{E}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$) whose byte inputs are embedded with a $256 \times h_{\mathcal{E}}$ embedding matrix to get per-byte vectors $x_i$. To enrich these byte embeddings with contextual string information, BLT augments them with hashed $n$-gram embeddings. For each byte position $i$ and for $n\in\{3,4,5,6,7,8\}$, form the byte-gram

\[
g_{i,n} = \{b_{i-n+1},\ldots,b_i\},
\]

and add a learned hash embedding $E_n^{hash}(\mathrm{Hash}(g_{i,n}))$ to the byte embedding. The augmented embedding is

\[
e_i = x_i + \sum_{n=3}^{8} E_n^{hash}(\mathrm{Hash}(g_{i,n})),
\]

normalized by the number of n-gram sizes plus one. These hash $n$-gram embeddings provide a compact signal about recent byte sequences without requiring a huge discrete vocabulary.

The encoder then converts bytes into patch vectors via a sequence of alternating Transformer layers and a Perceiver-style cross-attention block. Each patch $p_j$ initializes a query by pooling the embeddings of the bytes that belong to that patch and then cross-attends only to those bytes; this produces the latent patch representation used by the global Transformer. The cross-attention update at layer $l$ can be written in the paper’s notation as

\[
P_l = P_{l-1} + W_o\left(\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V\right),
\]

with $Q_j = W_q(P_{l-1,j})$, $K_i = W_k(h_{l-1,i})$, and $V_i = W_v(h_{l-1,i})$, and where the attention mask restricts each query $Q_j$ to attend only to the bytes inside patch $p_j$.

After the local encoder produces patch representations, the global Transformer $\mathcal{G}$ processes the sequence of patches autoregressively. Because patches are typically much fewer than bytes, the global Transformer is invoked far less often than a per-byte Transformer would be, and its heavy FFN computation is amortized over more bytes. Finally, a lightweight local decoder $\mathcal{D}$ uses cross-attention (now with byte positions as queries and patch outputs as keys/values) to reconstruct the byte sequence and produce next-byte probabilities. The decoder layers follow the reverse pattern: cross-attention into patch outputs followed by byte-level Transformer layers that model short-range dependencies in the reconstructed byte sequence.

To understand the FLOP tradeoffs precisely, BLT computes per-byte FLOPs by summing the costs of the encoder Transformer, the global latent Transformer run at the patch rate, the decoder Transformer, and the cross-attention operations that map between bytes and patches. In the paper this is expressed compactly as

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}} &= \frac{\text{Transf.FL}\!\big(h_{\mathcal{G}},\, l_{\mathcal{G}},\, m=n_{\text{ctx}}/n_p,\, V=0\big)}{n_p} \\
&\quad + \text{Transf.FL}\!\big(h_{\mathcal{E}},\, l_{\mathcal{E}},\, m=w_{\mathcal{E}},\, V=0\big) \\
&\quad + \text{Transf.FL}\!\big(h_{\mathcal{D}},\, l_{\mathcal{D}},\, m=w_{\mathcal{D}},\, V=256\big) \\
&\quad + \text{CrossAttn.FL}\!\big(h_{\mathcal{E}},\, l_{\mathcal{E}},\, m=n_p,\, r=n_p/k\big)\times\frac{k}{n_p} \\
&\quad + \text{CrossAttn.FL}\!\big(h_{\mathcal{D}},\, l_{\mathcal{D}},\, m=k,\, r=k/n_p\big),
\end{aligned}
\]

where $n_{\text{ctx}}$ is the byte context length, $n_p$ is average patch size, $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ and $w_{\mathcal{D}}$ are local attention windows, $k$ is a ratio describing how local and global dimensions split/concatenate, and $V$ is the output vocabulary size (only relevant in the decoder). The important qualitative point is that the dominant, high-parameter global Transformer cost is amortized over $n_p$ bytes per step; increasing $n_p$ or selecting longer patches where the text is predictable reduces the heavy per-byte cost.

# Contributions and key results: compute-optimal parity, inference FLOP savings, robustness, and a new scaling axis
BLT makes four interlocking contributions.

First, BLT provides a tokenizer-free LLM architecture that trains end-to-end from raw bytes while matching the compute-optimal scaling trends of token-based models at large scale. The paper reports the first flop-controlled scaling study of byte-level models up to 8B parameters and 4T training bytes, showing that the BLT architecture can achieve parity in training performance with strong tokenizer-based baselines (such as Llama 3) when measured under matched training FLOPs.

Second, BLT improves inference efficiency by dynamically selecting longer patches in predictable contexts. Because the global Transformer runs less often when patches are longer on average, BLT can reduce inference FLOPs substantially: the paper reports up to roughly 50% fewer inference FLOPs for some configurations (for example, average patch sizes of about 8 bytes can lead to nearly 50% inference FLOP savings). Importantly, reducing the number of global steps can be traded for larger global model size while keeping the same inference budget, opening a new scaling axis.

Third, BLT improves robustness and long-tail generalization by directly modeling raw bytes. Empirically, BLT models are more robust to noisy inputs, show stronger character-level understanding (orthographic manipulation, grapheme-to-phoneme tasks), and better generalize on lower-resource and rare byte sequences (improvements on long-tail multilingual translation pairs). These improvements follow from the model’s direct access to byte features plus the encoder’s $n$-gram hash embeddings and cross-attention pooling, which help it learn sub-token structure that tokenizers hide.

Fourth, BLT unlocks a new practical scaling lever: patch size. Because BLT has no fixed discrete vocabulary, it can vary average patch size without the embedding-table tradeoff faced by tokenizers (in token models, larger tokens reduce sequence length but force larger embeddings and output projections). In BLT, longer average patches reduce the number of costly global steps; the saved FLOPs can be reallocated to increase the capacity of the global Transformer, yielding better models for a fixed inference FLOP budget. The paper demonstrates this by training flop-equivalent models with different patch sizes and model sizes and observing that BLT’s scaling curves (with larger patch sizes and larger global Transformers) outperform tokenization-based baselines in fixed-inference experiments.

Beyond these headline claims, BLT is designed with practical considerations that make it usable: entropy patching is computed in a lightweight dataloading step (the entropy model itself is small and can even be encoded as a lookup when its receptive field is tiny), BLT’s encoder and decoder are deliberately kept lightweight so that the dominant cost remains the global Transformer, and the architecture preserves incrementality required for autoregressive generation. The paper also provides empirical ablations tying improvements to specific ingredients (entropy patching, hash $n$-grams, cross-attention design) and releases training and inference code to reproduce the results.

In sum, replacing fixed tokenization with dynamic, entropy-aware patches lets the model allocate its most expensive computations where they are most needed. This reallocation reduces wasted computation on predictable byte runs, increases robustness by preserving byte-level information, and creates a new degree of freedom (patch size) that can be exploited to produce larger, better models under fixed inference budgets. These properties explain why, in the experiments reported, "patches scale better than tokens."

Background: From Tokens and Bytes to Information and Compute

# Tokens versus bytes: vocabularies, subword units, and out-of-vocabulary handling

Modern language models normally begin by converting raw text into discrete units that the model will process. Those units are most often tokens derived by a tokenizer: a deterministic pre-processing step that maps sequences of bytes into members of a fixed, finite vocabulary. Tokenizers based on subword algorithms compress common byte patterns (words, prefixes, suffixes) into single entries in that vocabulary. The effect is a shorter sequence for the Transformer to process, which reduces the number of autoregressive steps and therefore the nominal compute needed during both training and inference. This is why subword tokenization has been central to scaling up large language models.

However, that very compression also introduces inductive biases. Because tokens are chosen ahead of model training, their boundaries and sizes reflect the heuristic or statistical criteria used to build the tokenizer rather than the downstream model’s notion of where hard predictions occur. The paper documents several practical consequences of this bias: tokenization can be domain- or modality-sensitive, it can make models brittle to small input perturbations, and it can obscure orthographic and character-level structure that is important for some tasks (for example, certain multilingual or low-resource scenarios). Tokenizers also introduce a hard limit on the vocabulary: anything not covered by the trained vocabulary is represented by combinations of tokens rather than by truly atomic raw bytes.

Bytes offer an alternative atomic unit. Working at the byte level removes the need for a precomputed vocabulary: every input is a sequence over the byte alphabet and there is no out‑of‑vocabulary in the usual sense. In the BLT architecture the local encoder begins with an embedding matrix for raw bytes (the paper uses an embedding matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{256 \times h_{\mathcal{E}}}$), which is enough to represent any byte sequence. The cost of byte-level modeling is that sequences become longer than with subword tokenization; every byte must be considered unless additional compression is introduced by the model.

Patches are a middle ground introduced by BLT. A patch is a dynamically-sized group of bytes that the model treats as a single higher-level unit of computation. Unlike tokens, patches are not drawn from a finite fixed vocabulary decided before training. Instead, arbitrary groups of bytes are mapped to latent patch representations by light-weight learned encoder and decoder modules. This design keeps the coverage and robustness advantages of bytes (no fixed vocabulary, direct access to orthographic information) while recovering much of the computational benefit of having fewer high-level steps for the large, expensive parts of the model.

There is an important systems-level tradeoff tied to vocabulary size for token models: increasing the size of the tokenizer vocabulary tends to increase the average number of bytes per token, which decreases the number of Transformer steps needed, but it also increases the embedding and final output projection sizes. The paper gives a concrete illustration: moving from one tokenizer to another raised the average token size from 3.7 to 4.4 bytes while increasing the size of the embedding table by around $4\times$. Patch-based designs like BLT break this coupling: patch size can be varied independently of a fixed output vocabulary because the encoder/decoder map bytes to latent representations rather than indexing into a giant embedding table.

# BPE tokenization and its non-incremental nature

Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) and related subword algorithms are widely used to construct tokenizers. The characteristic feature of BPE-style tokenizers is that they produce a fixed, finite vocabulary before model training. Once that vocabulary is fixed, each byte sequence maps deterministically to a sequence of tokens (given full context). But there is a subtle operational property that matters once we consider autoregressive generation: tokenization is not necessarily prefix-consistent.

To make this precise, the paper introduces a useful formal notion. Let $f_p$ be a segmentation function that partitions a byte sequence $\mathbf{x} = \{x_i \mid i=1,\ldots,n\}$ into patches. We say a segmentation function is incremental (or prefix-consistent) if for every position $i$ the segmentation of the prefix is the prefix of the segmentation of the whole sequence:

\[
f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i}.
\]

In words: if you only have the bytes up to position $i-1$, applying the segmentation function to that prefix yields exactly the same patch boundaries as if you had applied the segmentation function to the entire (unknown) continuation and then truncated to the prefix.

BPE tokenization generally does not satisfy this incremental property. The reason is that the way a prefix is split into subwords can depend on the continuation; the same byte prefix can be partitioned differently depending on what follows. This non-incrementality is acceptable for many offline evaluation setups, but it becomes problematic in autoregressive generation where the model must decide on patch boundaries without seeing the future tokens that would influence the tokenizer’s segmentation. (The paper notes that one can force BPE to be incremental by inserting a special delimiter to mark boundaries, but that increases sequence length and is not necessarily desirable.)

Because of this mismatch, BLT treats tokens and patches differently: it explicitly works with segmentation schemes designed to be incremental (strided patches, whitespace-based patches, or entropy-based patches computed from a next-byte predictor), or it handles the boundary decision in a way that does not rely on future bytes.

# Incremental segmentation: why generation requires prefix-consistent boundaries

Autoregressive generation proceeds left-to-right: at each generation step the model has only the prefix that it has already produced. Any decision that controls whether additional, more expensive computation is invoked (for example, whether to emit a new patch and run the global latent transformer) must therefore be made based only on that prefix. If segmentation were allowed to look at future bytes, generation would require peeking at data that does not yet exist.

Formally, the incremental property $f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i}$ captures this constraint. When a patching scheme satisfies this property it means that the segmentation the model would adopt after observing $i-1$ bytes is exactly the same segmentation you would obtain if you had access to the entire sequence and then truncated it. A segmentation scheme that violates this property cannot be used directly in online generation, unless the generation procedure alters the segmentation logic (for example, by inserting explicit boundary tokens or by maintaining additional state).

BLT’s practical patching options are chosen with incrementality in mind. Fixed-stride patches (take every $k$ bytes) are trivially incremental because the boundary rule depends only on absolute position. Space-based patching (start a new patch at spaces or space-like bytes) is also incremental because the boundary decision depends only on bytes already observed. Entropy-based patching uses next-byte entropies from a small byte-level predictor; to be usable at generation time those entropies must be estimable from just the current prefix (for example, by running the small predictor incrementally or by relying on a sliding window of past bytes). The paper implements entropy patching in a preprocessing step during data loading for training, and for inference there are implementation choices (for instance, a small entropy predictor with sliding window attention) that allow boundary decisions to be made on the fly.

Understanding incrementality is crucial because any architecture that invokes different computational paths depending on segmentation must make those branching decisions only from the prefix. Otherwise, the model could not be used for autoregressive generation without modifying the tokenization protocol.

# Information-theoretic preliminaries: entropy and information density

A central idea behind BLT’s dynamic segmentation is that not all bytes carry the same amount of information. If two different pieces of text require very different effort to predict the next byte, it is wasteful to treat them identically in terms of how much of the model is run for each step. Information theory gives us the right formal quantity to measure this intuition: entropy.

If $p_e(\cdot \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i})$ is the probability distribution assigned by a small, trained byte-level language model (the entropy model) to the next byte $x_i$ given the prefix $\mathbf{x}_{<i}$, then the next-byte entropy is

\[
H(x_i) \;=\; -\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_e(x_i = v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_e(x_i = v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}).
\]

This quantity measures the expected information (uncertainty) in bits (if the logarithm is base 2) about the next byte. High entropy means the next byte is hard to predict (high information content); low entropy means it is predictable (low information content).

BLT uses these entropy estimates to segment byte sequences into patches in order to produce groups of bytes that have more uniform information density. The intuition is simple: within many natural linguistic units the uncertainty about each subsequent byte tends to fall as the unit is revealed. For example, once the first character of a word is known the remaining characters of that word are, on average, more predictable. A segmentation that keeps together predictable regions into longer patches and isolates high-uncertainty boundaries as patch starts will reduce the number of times the expensive global transformer must be invoked while concentrating computation on genuinely hard predictions.

The paper experiments with two concrete, local rules to decide where to cut patches using entropies computed by the small predictor. One is a global threshold:

\[
H(x_t) > \theta_g,
\]

which creates a patch boundary whenever the entropy exceeds some fixed threshold $\theta_g$. The second is an approximate monotonicity rule,

\[
H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r,
\]

which triggers a boundary if the entropy rises substantially relative to the previous byte, thereby identifying points that break an approximately decreasing entropy profile inside a patch. Both rules are local and can be implemented by a small predictor that only uses prefix information, making them compatible with the incremental requirement for generation. Practically, the entropy predictor used in the paper is a lightweight byte-level Transformer whose entropies are computed and then used as a preprocessing step during data loading. The entropy model’s size and context window affect the quality of patching: larger entropy models and larger receptive fields reduce noisy segmentation errors, but with diminishing returns beyond a certain size.

# Metrics: perplexity versus bits-per-byte (BPB) for tokenizer-independent evaluation

Perplexity is the standard measure of a model’s uncertainty on a tokenized sequence: it is a monotone transformation of the average cross-entropy per token under the model. But perplexity is defined relative to a particular tokenizer or token vocabulary: changing the tokenizer changes what is considered a token and therefore changes perplexity in ways that are not comparable across tokenizers.

When we want a tokenizer-independent metric that allows direct comparison between byte-level models and token-based models, the paper uses Bits-Per-Byte (BPB). BPB is simply the average cross-entropy per byte, converted to bits. If $\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\mathbf{x})$ denotes the sum of cross-entropy losses of a model on the byte sequence $\mathbf{x}$, and $n_{\text{bytes}}$ denotes the number of bytes in $\mathbf{x}$, then

\[
\text{BPB}(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\mathbf{x})}{\ln(2)\cdot n_{\text{bytes}}}.
\]

The division by $\ln(2)$ converts natural-log cross-entropy into bits (if the cross-entropy is expressed in nats). BPB has the convenient operational interpretation of the average number of bits the model needs to encode each byte of the given data under its learned distribution. Lower BPB indicates a stronger byte-level model. By using BPB, the paper can compare tokenizers and byte-based approaches on a common footing: instead of asking how uncertain the model is per token (a measure that depends on the tokenizer), BPB asks how uncertain the model is on the underlying atomic bytes.

# Transformer compute anatomy: attention versus feed-forward costs; block-causal and local attention

To reason about efficiency — which is central to BLT’s design — we must understand how computation is distributed inside a Transformer and how that computation scales with sequence length and model size.

A Transformer layer contains two dominant parts: the self-attention mechanism and the feed-forward network (FFN) that follows it. Self-attention computes query, key, and value (QKV) projections and then forms pairwise attention weights; its cost scales roughly with the square of the length of the sequence in the naive dense form, but in many practical settings attention can be made efficient (sparse, local, or fused) or its cost can be masked by architectural choices. The FFN, by contrast, applies large dense multiplications at each position independently, and its cost scales linearly with sequence length times the width of the model.

The paper emphasizes that at large model scales the dominant compute cost comes from FFNs rather than attention: FFN layers are large and are applied at every position (byte, token, or patch), so reducing the number of positions on which those FFNs run yields large savings. That observation motivates BLT’s central design decision: reduce the number of expensive global Transformer steps by grouping bytes into patches and running the big global model on patches instead of on individual bytes.

BLT’s total estimated FLOPs per byte is computed by summing contributions from the local encoder, the global latent transformer, the local decoder, and the cross-attention blocks that connect byte-level and patch-level representations. The paper writes an operational formula for the forward/backward FLOPs per byte for BLT (here reproduced symbolically):

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}} &= \frac{\text{Transf.\ FL}(h_{\mathcal{G}}, l_{\mathcal{G}}, m = n_{\text{ctx}}/n_p, V=0)}{n_p} \\
&\quad + \text{Transf.\ FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m = w_{\mathcal{E}}, V=0) \\
&\quad + \text{Transf.\ FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m = w_{\mathcal{D}}, V=256) \\
&\quad + \text{CrossAttn.\ FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m = n_p, r = n_p/k) \times \frac{k}{n_p} \\
&\quad + \text{CrossAttn.\ FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m = k, r = k/n_p).
\end{aligned}
\]

Each term corresponds to an architectural component: the first line is the global latent Transformer’s cost (with hidden size $h_{\mathcal{G}}$ and $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers) amortized per byte by dividing by the average patch size $n_p$; the second and third lines are the local encoder and decoder Transformer costs (their sequence lengths are local window sizes $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ and $w_{\mathcal{D}}$, respectively); the final two lines estimate the cost of cross-attention in encoder and decoder, where $r$ denotes the ratio of queries to key/value entries and $k$ denotes how many local splits are concatenated to form a global representation. The decoder’s output projection has vocabulary size $V$ (256 for byte output) and is therefore included only in the decoder term.

Two attention masking patterns that BLT uses deserve special mention. The global latent transformer uses a block-causal attention mask: attention is permitted up to and including the current patch within the current document, but not beyond. This enforces autoregressiveness at the patch level. The local encoder uses a fixed, local block-causal attention window: each byte attends to a fixed number of preceding bytes $w_{\mathcal{E}}$, which may cross the dynamic patch boundaries but does not cross document boundaries. These locality constraints give practical control over attention costs and make it possible to fuse or optimize attention computations in libraries (FlashAttention, FlexAttention in the paper’s implementation).

Putting these elements together, reducing the number of patches $n_p$ — that is, increasing the average patch size — has a direct and substantial effect on the flops expended by the global Transformer (the expensive part). This is the lever BLT uses to trade off inference cost and model capacity: larger patches mean fewer expensive global steps, freeing flops that can be reinvested into wider or deeper global Transformer layers.

# Scaling laws and compute-optimal training regimes (Chinchilla perspective)

Scaling laws relate model performance to compute, number of parameters, and the amount of training data. A principled approach to allocating a fixed compute budget is to choose model size and training data so as to be as close as possible to the compute-optimal regime: the combination of parameters and data for which, given total FLOPs, the model attains the best expected performance. The Chinchilla line of work (which the paper follows in spirit) made this idea operational for subword-tokenized Transformers, and Llama 3 used those estimates to choose training regimes for the baselines in this work.

The BLT paper evaluates models in two complementary regimes. First, it trains BLT and BPE baselines in a parameter-matched, compute-optimal setting determined by prior scaling recommendations and compares the bits-per-byte achieved as a function of training FLOPs. In these matched settings BLT models are able to match or outperform tokenized baselines at scale, suggesting that the compute-optimal parameter/data tradeoffs found for BPE Transformers transfer reasonably well to BLT architectures.

Second, the paper explores a new dimension of scaling that BLT unlocks in practice: inference-flop constrained scaling. Because patch size reduces the number of expensive global steps at inference time, BLT allows you to keep the same inference FLOP budget while making the global model larger and increasing the patch size. Concretely, longer patches reduce the global Transformer’s per-byte cost so that saved compute can be reinvested in increased model capacity. The paper presents inference-FLOP controlled scaling experiments showing that, for fixed inference cost, BLT models with larger patch sizes and larger global models scale better than token-based models that cannot change token size without incurring embedding/output costs.

A final practical point made in the paper is a caution: the compute-optimal ratios used in these experiments were taken from analyses done for BPE-tokenized Transformers. Those scaling laws may not be exactly optimal for BLT; deriving BLT-specific scaling laws is left as future work and could conceivably shift the best choices for model size, patch size, and data volume.

You should now understand the distinction between tokens and bytes, why incrementality matters for generation, how entropy provides a principled signal for segmentation, why Bits-Per-Byte is the appropriate tokenizer-independent metric, how Transformer compute breaks down in practice, and the intuition behind compute-optimal scaling. These concepts are the conceptual foundation underlying BLT’s architecture and experimental claims.

From Individual Bytes to Patches

# What is a patch and a patching function; patch size as a compute dial
A patch is simply a contiguous group of bytes that the model treats as a single step of computation for its global (latent) Transformer. Instead of running the expensive global Transformer on every raw byte, the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) groups bytes into a shorter sequence of patches and runs the latent Transformer on those patch representations. Formally, given a byte sequence $\pmb{x} = \{x_i \mid i=1,\ldots,n\}$, a patching function $f_p$ segments $\pmb{x}$ into a sequence of $m < n$ patches $\pmb{p} = \{p_j \mid j=1,\ldots,m\}$. In the paper this is expressed by mapping each byte index $i$ to an indicator in $\{0,1\}$ where $1$ marks the start of a new patch; the result is a partitioning of the original byte stream into contiguous patches.

The average patch size (bytes per patch), approximately $\frac{n}{m}$, is the primary knob that controls how much work the global Transformer must do. Because the global Transformer is the dominant consumer of FLOPs, larger average patches mean fewer global steps and therefore lower inference and training costs; smaller patches mean more steps and higher cost. This is why the paper emphasizes patch size as a “compute dial”: by changing how many bytes are grouped into each patch (or by changing the rule that decides patch boundaries), BLT can trade computation for finer-grained modeling. Importantly, patches in BLT are not tokens from a fixed finite vocabulary; they are dynamic groups of bytes that are mapped to latent representations via the local encoder and later decoded back to bytes by the local decoder. That decouples the notion of group size from an explicit vocabulary-size cost that tokenizers face.

# Strided patching (every K bytes): simplicity and inefficiencies
Strided patching groups bytes into fixed-length blocks of $k$ bytes (every $k$ bytes starts a new patch). This is the simplest form of patching: it is trivial to implement, it gives a predictable reduction in sequence length (roughly a factor of $k$), and it makes flop accounting straightforward because the number of patches per fixed-length byte window is deterministic. MegaByte uses this approach, and it is useful as a baseline and as a way to explore trade-offs between patch size and model size.

However, fixed-stride patches have important inefficiencies. They are blind to context, so they cannot allocate more compute where the prediction problem is hard and less where it is easy. A fixed stride can waste a global Transformer step on a patch that mostly contains whitespace or highly predictable bytes, while failing to dedicate enough representational capacity to patches containing dense, high-entropy content (for example, a short formula, a number, or the start of a new word or identifier). Fixed stride also produces inconsistent segmentations of the same logical unit: the same word can be split differently depending on its alignment with the fixed grid, which hurts the model’s ability to learn reuseable substructures. These shortcomings motivate moving beyond purely static patching toward context-aware segmentation.

# Space patching: linguistic boundaries, pros and cons across domains and languages
Space patching is a simple, linguistically motivated improvement over fixed-stride patching: start a new patch after any space-like byte. The BLT paper follows the SpaceByte definition of “space-like bytes”: any byte that is not a Latin character, digit, or UTF-8 continuation byte, and with the additional requirement that each patch must contain at least one non-space-like byte. Intuitively, this makes the latent Transformer act roughly at the word level for languages that use whitespace as a word separator, so the model takes one global step per word.

This strategy brings two immediate benefits. First, it creates consistent segmentation of words across different contexts: the same surface word will be grouped the same way, which helps the model reuse learned patterns. Second, it naturally concentrates compute on where it is most useful. For example, the first few bytes after a space are typically harder to predict (they carry choice about what word begins), while the remaining bytes of the same word are often easier once the first character or two are known. By running the latent Transformer once per word, space patching implicitly allocates a global step at the point of higher uncertainty.

Space patching nonetheless has clear limits. It relies on the presence and meaning of “space-like” bytes, so it does not generalize gracefully to languages and scripts that do not use whitespace to delimit words, nor to many non-linguistic domains (dense code tokens, compressed binary formats, or creative punctuation usage). Crucially, space patching is still a rule-based segmentation with a fixed behavior — it cannot smoothly vary the average patch size to trade off compute and fidelity in the same flexible way that an entropy-driven method can.

# Entropy patching: training a small byte LM and computing next-byte entropies
Entropy patching replaces rule-based segmentation by a data-driven method that uses a small byte-level autoregressive language model to estimate how hard each next-byte prediction is. The entropy model defines a conditional distribution $p_e(x_i \mid \pmb{x}_{<i})$ over the byte vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$. The next-byte entropy at position $i$ is computed as

\[
H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_e(x_i{=}v \mid \pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_e(x_i{=}v \mid \pmb{x}_{<i}).
\]

(As presented in the paper, this formula is used directly as the measure to compare against thresholds in order to find boundaries.) Intuitively, $H(x_i)$ is high when the entropy model is uncertain about the next byte and low when the next byte is predictable from the prefix. Entropy patching then chooses patch boundaries at byte positions that the entropy model judges to be high-uncertainty points.

In practice the BLT experiments use a modestly-sized entropy model trained on the same data distribution as the full BLT model. A common configuration in the paper is an entropy transformer with about 100M parameters, 14 layers, hidden size 512, and a sliding-window attention of 512 bytes; the paper also reports experiments with different sizes and receptive fields. One practical advantage is that, when the entropy model’s receptive field is small, the computed entropies can be encoded in an efficient lookup table, making entropy computation cheap in the dataloading step. The result of entropy patching is that patches tend to capture contiguous bytes that have roughly uniform information density: difficult, high-uncertainty bytes become patch starts, and sequences of lower-uncertainty bytes are grouped together.

# Boundary selection methods: global entropy threshold versus approximate monotonicity
The paper explores two concrete rules for turning a sequence of entropies $\{H(x_i)\}$ into boundaries.

The first is a global threshold rule: declare a boundary at position $t$ whenever

\[
H(x_t) > \theta_g,
\]

where $\theta_g$ is a scalar chosen to control how often boundaries occur. This picks absolute peaks of uncertainty: any byte whose predicted entropy exceeds the threshold begins a new patch.

The second rule is an approximate monotonicity rule: declare a boundary at $t$ when the entropy increases sufficiently relative to the previous byte,

\[
H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r,
\]

with a relative threshold $\theta_r$. This can be interpreted as detecting breaks in an approximately monotonically decreasing entropy profile within a patch: typically entropy falls after the start of a new symbol as more bytes are observed, so a relative increase signals the start of a new information-bearing unit.

Each rule has different strengths. The global threshold is straightforward but needs calibrating to the global scale of entropies across a corpus and can be sensitive to long-term shifts in entropy distributions (so-called “entropy drift”). The approximate-monotonic rule is more local and can be more robust to shifts in absolute entropy values because it looks for relative changes rather than absolute magnitudes. In practice the paper finds both approaches useful and sometimes combines them (or prefers the monotonic constraint) to mitigate problems arising from long contexts or repeated content.

# Controlling average patch size via entropy thresholds
Entropy thresholds provide a natural means to set the average patch size. By choosing $\theta_g$ (or $\theta_r$) and measuring the resulting segmentation statistics over the training mix, one can target a desired mean bytes-per-patch on the pretraining corpus. This is important because the average patch size affects how many bytes each model sees per sequence and thus the effective context length seen by the global Transformer.

To keep comparisons fair across different patching schemes or average patch sizes, the BLT experiments ensure that the number of bytes in each training batch remains constant in expectation. Practically, that means that when a model uses larger average patches (fewer patches per fixed number of bytes), the sequence length in terms of bytes is decreased so the total bytes processed per batch match other configurations. For example, on the Llama 2 pretraining mix the experiments use an 8k byte context, while on the BLT-1T mixture they use a 16k byte context, always preserving the same average batch size in bytes (the paper reports using 16M bytes per batch expectation).

At inference time the entropy threshold can also be tuned to trade inference cost for performance. The paper reports that a lower threshold (more patch boundaries) can improve downstream task performance at the expense of more inference steps; for one BLT-Entropy model the authors reduced the threshold from 0.6 to 0.1 at inference to obtain better results, accepting the extra compute. Controlling the entropy threshold therefore gives an explicit and interpretable mechanism to move along the cost–accuracy frontier.

# Incremental patching constraint for generation
A critical operational constraint for patching is that segmentation must be decidable at generation time without seeing future bytes. Formally, a patching scheme $f_p$ must satisfy the incremental property:

\[
f_p(\pmb{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\pmb{x})_{<i},
\]

meaning the segmentation decisions up to position $i$ computed with only the prefix $\pmb{x}_{<i}$ must match the segmentation that would be obtained if the entire full sequence $\pmb{x}$ were available and then truncated. This property ensures that the model can decide whether to invoke the global Latent Transformer (i.e., whether the current position starts a new patch) while generating autoregressively.

Standard BPE tokenization is not incremental in this sense: the same prefix can be tokenized differently depending on how the continuation unfolds, because the tokenizer uses the full string to pick the longest matching subword tokens. (The paper notes that adding a special delimiter token to mark boundaries can make BPE incremental, but that increases sequence length.) Entropy patching, by contrast, uses an autoregressive entropy model that only depends on the prefix when computing $H(x_i)$, so boundaries determined from these entropies can be made incremental and thus compatible with generation.

# Why tokenization is not patching: fixed vocabularies versus dynamic groupings; vocabulary-size versus compute trade-offs
Tokenizers such as BPE and SentencePiece build a finite vocabulary of multi-byte tokens prior to training. A token-based model maps each input token to an embedding and operates on a fixed discrete alphabet. Patches in BLT are categorically different: they are dynamic groupings of raw bytes without a predetermined finite vocabulary. Rather than learning embeddings for every possible multi-byte token, BLT maps arbitrary byte groups into latent patch representations through a lightweight local encoder and reconstructs bytes from patch outputs through a local decoder.

This difference breaks the tight trade-off faced by tokenizers: increasing vocabulary size increases the average token length (so fewer steps) but also increases embedding-table size and the dimension of the final output projection, making the model architecture larger and more expensive in other ways. For example, the paper points out that Llama 3 increased its average token size from 3.7 to 4.4 bytes by also expanding its embedding table about fourfold compared to Llama 2. BLT avoids that coupling: patch size (compute per document) and dimensionality of latent representations are decoupled because the encoder and decoder convert arbitrary byte groups into fixed-sized latent vectors. That design allows BLT to vary patch size freely and to reallocate compute (for example, invest saved flops from running the global Transformer less often into a larger global Transformer) without paying a vocabulary-size penalty.

Another practical consequence is access to raw byte information. Token-based models do not expose raw bytes directly to the model once tokenized; BLT’s local encoder processes bytes explicitly and can therefore make use of sub-byte/character-level cues (helpful for noisy inputs, rare scripts, and orthographic tasks).

# Practical considerations: entropy drift, context resets on newlines, and lightweight preprocessing at dataload time
Entropy patching requires careful practical handling. One observed phenomenon is “entropy drift”: in highly repetitive or very long contexts the entropy model can produce lower entropies for repeated content, which in turn yields unusually large patches in structured repetitive inputs (the paper shows MMLU examples where repeated content interacts with the entropy model context). To mitigate this, BLT uses two practical techniques in large-scale runs. First, the entropy context is reset at document boundaries such as newlines during entropy computation; second, the approximate-monotonic constraint is used instead of a pure global threshold because it is less sensitive to absolute entropy drift. These changes affect only how entropies are computed for segmentation; training of the full BLT model proceeds as before.

Another practical implementation choice is that entropy-based segmentation is applied as a lightweight preprocessing step during dataloading rather than being learned online as a boundary prediction classifier. This offloads most of the segmentation cost away from the heavy global Transformer and avoids the need to backpropagate segmentation decisions. The paper contrasts this with approaches that train a classifier to predict boundaries. Moreover, when the entropy model’s receptive field is small enough, the set of entropy values for common local contexts can be pre-computed and stored in a lookup table to further accelerate preprocessing.

Finally, the variable ratio of bytes to patches produced by dynamic patching necessitates careful batch construction. For efficiency the BLT implementation packs batches of patches to avoid padding steps in the latent Transformer and ensures that every batch has the same number of patches. The training data loader also pads and truncates byte sequences to safe caps (the experiments pad/truncate sequences to 12k and 24k bytes for different datasets) to prevent memory spikes caused by unusually large patches.

# Segmentation stability across domains and scripts
Different patching strategies yield markedly different stability properties across domains and scripts. Strided patching is inherently unstable with respect to logical units: the same word or identifier can be split differently depending on its alignment with the fixed grid. Space patching is stable for whitespace-delimited languages: it consistently groups words in the same way and therefore helps models exploit recurring lexical patterns. But space patching degrades when whitespace is not a reliable word boundary (many languages and scripts) or when domain structure (e.g., code tokens, punctuation-heavy text) uses different orthographic cues.

Entropy patching delivers a middle ground: it produces context-aware and data-driven segmentations that tend to group bytes into units with roughly uniform information density. That makes segmentation more adaptive across languages and domains because it is based on statistical uncertainty rather than a fixed orthographic rule. The trade-off is that entropy-based segmentation depends on the entropy model and on the immediate prefix context, so boundaries can vary with context; this is why ensuring the incremental property and controlling entropy drift are important practical requirements. When these are addressed, entropy patching gives BLT robust, stable segmentations across many kinds of input while retaining the ability to vary patch size to meet compute or accuracy objectives.

# Summary: choosing a patching strategy
Grouping bytes into patches is the fundamental mechanism BLT uses to allocate compute where it matters. Fixed strided patching is simple and controllable but not adaptive; space patching uses a linguistically motivated rule that works well for whitespace languages but cannot adapt its patch size or handle all scripts and domains. Entropy patching is the most flexible: by training a small byte-level LM and thresholding its next-byte entropy (either absolutely or relatively), BLT can create dynamic, context-aware patches that balance information density and compute. Entropy patching requires careful engineering (threshold calibration, handling entropy drift with context resets and monotonic criteria, and packing patches efficiently during training), but it unlocks the key BLT advantages: dynamic compute allocation, improved robustness to rare and noisy byte patterns, and the ability to treat patch size as a genuine compute dial independent of a fixed vocabulary.

The BLT Architecture

# High-level pipeline: Local Encoder → Global Latent Transformer → Local Decoder

The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) organizes computation into three cooperating modules: a Local Encoder that compresses raw bytes into patch-level latents, a Global Latent Transformer that models the sequence of those patch latents autoregressively, and a Local Decoder that expands patch-level outputs back into raw bytes. This three-part decomposition is the defining structural motif of BLT and is designed to match the statistical granularity at which expensive transformer computation should be spent: light-weight, byte-level processing is used where it is cheap and local; the heavy, high-capacity global transformer is invoked only at the coarser patch level.

Concretely, let the input be a byte sequence $\{b_i\}_{i=1}^n$. A patching function groups bytes into $m$ patches $\{p_j\}_{j=1}^m$; the Local Encoder $\mathcal{E}$ maps the bytes within each patch to a patch representation $p_j$. The Global Transformer $\mathcal{G}$ takes the sequence of patch representations and produces output patch representations $o_j$ autoregressively. The Local Decoder $\mathcal{D}$ then consumes the $o_j$ (and the byte-level context) to predict the output bytes $\{y_i\}$. In notation:
- Encoder: $\{b_i\} \xrightarrow{\mathcal{E}} \{p_j\}$,
- Global: $\{p_j\} \xrightarrow{\mathcal{G}} \{o_j\}$,
- Decoder: $\{o_j\}, \{b_{<i}\} \xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}} y_i$.

The encoder and decoder are intentionally lightweight (few layers: $l_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{D}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$) so that the majority of FLOPs is concentrated in the global model. The Local Encoder and Decoder operate at byte resolution (they run on each byte within their local windows), while the Global Transformer operates at patch resolution (it runs once per patch). This design lets BLT control when the expensive global computation happens by selecting how bytes are grouped into patches.

# Global latent transformer: block-causal attention over patches; prioritizing compute where needed

The Global Latent Transformer $\mathcal{G}$ is an autoregressive transformer that consumes the sequence of patch representations and produces updated patch-level outputs. If the encoder produces latent patch inputs $\{p_j\}_{j=1}^m$, then the global model computes
\[
\{o_j\}_{j=1}^m = \mathcal{G}(\{p_j\}_{j=1}^m),
\]
where $\mathcal{G}$ has $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers and internal hidden dimension $h_{\mathcal{G}}$.

Crucially, $\mathcal{G}$ uses a block-causal attention mask: when computing the representation for patch $j$ the attention is restricted to patches up to and including $j$ within the same document. That is, patch $j$ cannot attend to future patches $>j$. This block-causal masking preserves autoregressivity at the patch level while allowing each global step to aggregate information from the entire past of patches. Because the global transformer runs only once per patch, the total number of expensive feed-forward and attention operations is proportional to the number of patches rather than the number of bytes.

By coupling dynamic patching (which chooses where patches begin and end) with a powerful $\mathcal{G}$, BLT concentrates global compute on positions that are informationally dense. In effect, the global transformer is a “when-needed” expense: it is invoked at a rate determined by the patching function so that more model capacity is applied to high-uncertainty regions of the byte stream and less to low-uncertainty (predictable) spans.

# Local encoder: byte embeddings and hash n-gram augmentation for context

The Local Encoder $\mathcal{E}$ is responsible for producing patch-level inputs for $\mathcal{G}$. It is a lightweight transformer stack (few layers) that operates on byte embeddings and augments them with hashed n-gram features to capture local context efficiently.

Each raw byte $b_i$ is embedded via a learnable lookup into a vector $x_i\in\mathbb{R}^{h_{\mathcal{E}}}$ using an embedding matrix of shape $\mathbb{R}^{256\times h_{\mathcal{E}}}$. These per-byte embeddings are augmented by hash-based n-gram embeddings (described in the next section) to produce an initial byte representation $e_i$. The encoder then applies a sequence of alternating transformer layers and cross-attention pooling layers: the transformer layers use a local block-causal attention mask so that each byte attends to a fixed window of $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ preceding bytes (this window can cross patch boundaries but not document boundaries). After one or more transformer updates, a cross-attention block pools the byte-level representations that belong to each patch into a patch representation.

The encoder’s cross-attention block is inserted after each transformer layer (i.e., it repeatedly pools the evolving byte-level features into updated patch latents). For the first cross-attention layer the initial queries for each patch are produced by a simple pooling function over the bytes in that patch (the implementation uses max-pooling to initialize those queries). Across encoder layers the pooled queries are refined via cross-attention with the current byte-level keys/values. The encoder outputs the final set of patch inputs $p_j$ to the global transformer.

# Hash n-gram embeddings (n=3..8): hashing, table sizes, and normalization

To give the encoder richer local context without blowing up the parameter count, BLT augments each byte embedding with hash n-gram embeddings for n in the set $\{3,4,5,6,7,8\}$. For a given position $i$ and n-gram length $n$ define the byte n-gram
\[
g_{i,n} = \{b_{i-n+1}, \dots, b_i\},
\]
omitting sizes $n$ when $i<n$.

Each n-gram is mapped to a small index via a rolling polynomial hash (RollPolyHash) and then reduced modulo the size of the hash table for that $n$:
\[
\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}) = \text{RollPolyHash}(g_{i,n}) \bmod |E_n^{\text{hash}}|,
\]
where $E_n^{\text{hash}}$ is the embedding table for n-grams of length $n$. The hash embedding lookup yields a vector $E_n^{\text{hash}}(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}))$ which is added to the byte embedding.

Putting the pieces together, the augmented byte embedding used as encoder input is
\[
e_i = x_i + \sum_{n=3}^{8} E_n^{\text{hash}}(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n})),
\]
and $e_i$ is normalized by dividing by the number of n-gram sizes plus one (that is, by the count of terms contributing to $e_i$). This normalization keeps the scale consistent whether all n-gram sizes exist for a position or fewer do (near the beginning of a sequence).

In practice BLT uses a single hash function per n-gram and a fixed hash-table size (the paper’s experiments used 500{,}000 hashes in many runs), but the design leaves table size as a tunable hyperparameter. Hash n-grams provide an efficient, parameter-cheap way to expose local multi-byte patterns to the encoder (morphological and orthographic information) without requiring a huge explicit vocabulary.

# Encoder cross-attention: pooling bytes into patch representations with patch-specific masks

The encoder’s cross-attention module is Perceiver-like in structure but adapted to variable-size, data-dependent patches. Instead of a fixed set of latents, each patch corresponds to a latent query that pools the bytes belonging to that patch. Importantly, each patch query only attends to the keys/values formed by the bytes inside that patch; that is, the cross-attention uses a patch-specific mask that restricts attention to the local bytes for that patch.

Formally, initialize patch queries by applying a pooling function $f_{\text{bytes}}$ (e.g., max-pooling) over the encoder byte representations for the bytes in patch $p_j$ and projecting with a (multi-head) linear mapping $\mathcal{E}_C$:
\[
P_{0,j} = \mathcal{E}_C\big(f_{\text{bytes}}(p_j)\big).
\]
Here $\mathcal{E}_C$ projects the pooled byte summary into the multi-head query space (the paper denotes its shape as $\mathcal{E}_C\in\mathbb{R}^{h_{\mathcal{E}}\times(h_{\mathcal{E}}\times U_{\mathcal{E}})}$, where $U_{\mathcal{E}}$ is the number of cross-attention heads used in the encoder).

The cross-attention update for patch latents at layer $l$ is
\[
P_l = P_{l-1} + W_o\Big(\text{softmax}\!\big(\tfrac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\big)\,V\Big),
\]
with
\[
Q_j = W_q(P_{l-1,j}),\qquad K_i = W_k(h_{l-1,i}),\qquad V_i = W_v(h_{l-1,i}),
\]
where $h_{l-1,i}$ are the byte-level hidden states from the encoder’s transformer at the previous layer. The keys and values $K_i,V_i$ are restricted in the attention computation so that the $j$-th query attends only to the bytes that belong to patch $p_j$.

Because the global transformer’s patch-dimensionality $h_{\mathcal{G}}$ is typically larger than the byte-level hidden size $h_{\mathcal{E}}$, BLT maintains the patch latents $P_l$ internally as multiple heads of dimension $h_{\mathcal{E}}$ during cross-attention and later concatenates (or projects) them to form the full $h_{\mathcal{G}}$-dimensional patch representations passed to $\mathcal{G}$. The cross-attention block uses pre-LayerNorm on queries, keys, and values, includes a residual connection around the attention, and — by design — does not add learned positional embeddings inside the cross-attention module.

# Local decoder: cross-attention from bytes to patch states, then byte-level transformer decoding

The Local Decoder $\mathcal{D}$ maps the patch-level outputs of the global transformer back to a sequence of bytes. Like the encoder, it is a lightweight transformer stack that interleaves cross-attention and transformer layers, but with the roles of queries and key/values reversed.

The decoder initializes its byte-level queries from the encoder’s final byte representations:
\[
D_0 = h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}.
\]
Before each decoder transformer layer, a cross-attention block lets each byte query attend to the patch representations $o_j$ produced by the global transformer. The decoder cross-attention update has the form
\[
B_l = D_{l-1} + W_o\Big(\text{softmax}\!\big(\tfrac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\big)\,V\Big),
\]
with
\[
Q_i = W_q(d_{l-1,i}),\qquad K_j = W_k\big(\mathcal{D}_C(o_j)\big),\qquad V_j = W_v\big(\mathcal{D}_C(o_j)\big),
\]
where $\mathcal{D}_C$ is the linear transform (and split) applied to final patch representations to form key/value vectors for the decoder cross-attention. The output $B_l$ has shape compatible with the decoder hidden size (the paper denotes $B\in\mathbb{R}^{h_{\mathcal{D}}\times n_b}$ for $n_b$ output bytes), and the next decoder representations are computed by applying a standard decoder transformer layer:
\[
D_l = \text{Decoder-Transformer-Layer}_l(B_l).
\]

Operationally, the decoder cross-attention is applied before the local decoder’s transformer layer so that the transformer operates on byte representations already conditioned on patch-level context. The decoder transformer applies local autoregressive masking at the byte level to ensure correct causal decoding of bytes. Finally, the decoder projects the top-layer byte representations to a 256-way output (one logit per possible byte) and computes the byte-level cross-entropy loss during training.

# Attention masks and positional encodings: RoPE in self-attention; none in cross-attention

BLT uses three forms of masking/positioning adapted to where each operation runs:

- Self-attention in the byte-level Local Encoder and Local Decoder uses a local block-causal attention mask: each byte may attend to a fixed window of $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ preceding bytes. This enables efficient local modeling while allowing attention to cross patch boundaries within the local window.

- The Global Latent Transformer uses a block-causal attention mask at patch granularity so that each patch representation attends only to earlier patches (and itself) within the same document, preserving autoregressivity at the coarse level.

- Cross-attention modules use dynamic, patch-specific masks: the encoder’s cross-attention for patch $j$ can only read keys/values corresponding to bytes that make up patch $p_j$; the decoder’s cross-attention uses patch outputs as keys/values and byte positions as queries.

For positional encoding, BLT follows a consistent choice: Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE) with $\theta=500000$ are applied in self-attention layers only (both in local and global transformers). Cross-attention modules explicitly use no learned positional embeddings: queries and keys/values in cross-attention are normalized and projected, but positional signals are not added inside the cross-attention block. The cross-attention blocks use pre-LayerNorm on queries, keys, and values and employ residual connections around attention, matching the rest of the transformer design.

From an implementation perspective (reported in the paper), fixed, standard attention masks (local windows, block-causal masks) can be accelerated with FlashAttention, while the dynamic, patch-dependent cross-attention uses FlexAttention to handle fused and mask-varying patterns efficiently.

# Dimensions and heads: h_E and h_G, multi-head splits (k) and residual/normalization choices

BLT uses three characteristic hidden dimensions: $h_{\mathcal{E}}$ for the Local Encoder/Decoder byte-level representations, $h_{\mathcal{G}}$ for the Global Latent Transformer patch-level representations, and $h_{\mathcal{D}}$ for decoder internals (often $h_{\mathcal{D}}\approx h_{\mathcal{E}}$). Typically $h_{\mathcal{E}} \ll h_{\mathcal{G}}$ because the global transformer is the high-capacity module.

A practical implementation detail arises because cross-attention projects between these differently sized spaces: during encoder cross-attention BLT maintains patch latents as multiple sub-vectors (or “splits”) each of size $h_{\mathcal{E}}$ and then concatenates them to obtain an $h_{\mathcal{G}}$-sized vector. The number of such splits is denoted $k$, so that roughly $h_{\mathcal{G}} = k \cdot h_{\mathcal{E}}$ (or the equivalent via projection). In the paper’s illustration $k=2$ is used as an example. Working with multiple smaller heads for cross-attention makes the byte-to-patch and patch-to-byte projections compatible with the byte-level dimensions used inside the local models.

Across attention blocks BLT uses multi-headed attention with the usual per-head key dimension $d_k$, projected by $W_q,W_k,W_v$ and recombined by $W_o$. Transformer layers use SwiGLU activations in feed-forward layers and RMSNorm for normalization. Cross-attention and self-attention blocks are wrapped with pre-LayerNorm and residual connections, preserving the stability properties common to large modern transformer designs.

# Compute gating: when and how the global model is invoked at patch boundaries during training and generation

The Global Latent Transformer is invoked once per patch. Which byte positions start a new patch (and therefore trigger a global step) is determined by the patching function $f_p$. During training, patch boundaries are identified in a lightweight preprocessing step (for entropy-based patching, using entropies produced by a small byte LM); during inference and generation those boundaries must be decided incrementally. Concretely, a patching scheme used for generation must satisfy the incremental property
\[
f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i},
\]
so that the decision of whether position $i$ begins a new patch can be made without access to future bytes. Space-based and entropy-based patchers are incremental in their setup; standard BPE tokenization is not incremental by default, which is why BLT treats tokens and patches differently.

At runtime (training or inference) the pipeline gated by patch boundaries looks like this: bytes flow through the Local Encoder which pools and emits a patch representation when the patching function indicates a patch boundary; that patch representation is then input to the Global Transformer (which produces or extends the autoregressive history at the patch level); the Local Decoder attends to available patch outputs and its own byte-level history to emit bytes. Because the global transformer is the dominant FLOP consumer, reducing the number of times it is invoked (by increasing average patch size when appropriate) reduces inference cost. Conversely, when more fine-grained modeling is needed, smaller patches cause more frequent global steps and concentrate compute on harder regions.

Implementation-wise, BLT packs batches of patches for the latent transformer in training to avoid padding waste inside the expensive $\mathcal{G}$ computation. When using dynamic patching the bytes-to-patches ratio varies across examples, so the training loader ensures that batches of patches are packed to keep the latent transformer’s work dense; additionally, byte sequences are padded/truncated to bounded lengths to avoid pathological memory spikes from unusually large patches.

# Putting the pieces together: an end-to-end view

An end-to-end forward pass in BLT starts from raw bytes and proceeds as:

1. Segment the byte sequence into patches using the chosen patcher $f_p$ (space-based, strided, or entropy-based).

2. Look up per-byte embeddings $x_i$ and compute hash n-gram augmentations to form normalized byte inputs $e_i$.

3. Run the Local Encoder: apply a small stack of byte-level transformer layers (local block-causal attention) interleaved with cross-attention pooling that produces patch latents $p_j$. The encoder produces the inputs to the global model.

4. Run the Global Latent Transformer autoregressively over the patch latents $\{p_j\}$ with block-causal attention to obtain outputs $\{o_j\}$; this is the core high-capacity sequence model.

5. Run the Local Decoder: for each byte position, apply decoder cross-attention (bytes as queries, $\{o_j\}$ as keys/values) and a small transformer stack to decode bytes autoregressively; finally project to 256 logits and compute the byte-level loss.

At generation time the same steps are followed but decisions about patch boundaries must be made online using an incremental patcher; whenever a new patch starts the encoder produces a patch latent and the global model is invoked to extend the patch-level autoregressive state.

This architectural split — light-weight byte modules surrounding a powerful patch-level transformer — is the central engineering idea of BLT. It lets the model keep direct access to raw byte structure through embedding and hash n-gram features, pool local signals into compact, expressive patch representations through cross-attention, and spend the expensive global computation only where the data demands it.

Accounting for Efficiency: FLOPs, BPB, and Cost Drivers

# Why FFNs dominate compute at large scale; impact of fewer global steps

When people talk about "where compute goes" in modern Transformer-based LLMs they usually mean arithmetic operations (floating point operations, FLOPs) that the model performs while processing a sequence. In the Transformer architecture almost every layer contains two qualitatively different kinds of work: attention and the position-wise feed‑forward network (FFN, sometimes called the MLP). The paper emphasizes one empirical point that guides BLT's design: at large model scales the expensive part of the per‑position computation is the FFN layers, not the attention mechanism. Put differently, the dominant cost is the dense matrix multiplies inside the FFNs that are applied independently at every model position.

This observation has a direct operational consequence. If the expensive FFNs are applied at every position, then reducing the number of positions on which the big FFNs run gives a large win. BLT exploits exactly that: it moves most of the heavy, large‑hidden‑dimensional Transformer (the "latent global transformer" with many layers and large hidden size) off of raw bytes and onto patch representations. Each global step now corresponds to a patch rather than to an individual byte. Because the global model consumes the bulk of the flops during pretraining and inference (as stated in the paper), reducing the number of global steps by increasing average patch size or by using dynamic patching produces substantial savings.

Intuitively, the idea is: run the heavy FFN fewer times on aggregated units (patches) and run lightweight byte‑level processing only when necessary. That is why BLT splits computation into a big global Transformer (few but costly steps) plus small local encoders/decoders (many but cheap steps). Fewer global steps therefore translate into large reductions in the dominant component of compute.

# FLOP accounting across BLT modules: local encoder, encoder cross-attn, global transformer, decoder cross-attn, decoder

BLT decomposes work into five pieces that are relevant for FLOP accounting:

1. Local encoder Transformer layers (per‑byte, small): these are the lightweight layers that process byte embeddings with a local window. Their parameters are small ($l_{\mathcal{E}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$, $h_{\mathcal{E}} < h_{\mathcal{G}}$) so their per‑position FFN cost is low, but they run on every byte.

2. Encoder cross‑attention (per‑patch): after each encoder layer, BLT pools bytes in each current patch and runs a multi‑headed cross‑attention that produces patch queries and attends to the bytes that belong to that patch. This block moves information from many byte positions into a single patch representation.

3. Latent global Transformer (per‑patch, large): the global Transformer $\mathcal{G}$ with $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers and large hidden dimensionality is run on the sequence of patch representations. This is the heaviest module per step and consumes the majority of training/inference FLOPs when it is invoked.

4. Decoder cross‑attention (per‑byte): the decoder cross‑attention maps patch (global) representations back onto byte‑level queries so the decoder can produce byte predictions.

5. Local decoder Transformer and final byte projection (per‑byte): this lightweight transformer operates on the decoded byte sequence and finally projects to the byte vocabulary ($V=256$) to produce per‑byte probabilities.

The paper gives a compact expression used to estimate BLT FLOPs per byte. Reproducing that expression helps make the accounting concrete:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}} &= \frac{\text{Transf.FL}(h_{\mathcal{G}},\,l_{\mathcal{G}},\,m = n_{\text{ctx}}/n_p,\,V=0)}{n_p} \\
&\quad + \text{Transf.FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}},\,l_{\mathcal{E}},\,m = w_{\mathcal{E}},\,V=0) \\
&\quad + \text{Transf.FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}},\,l_{\mathcal{D}},\,m = w_{\mathcal{D}},\,V=256) \\
&\quad + \text{CrossAttn.FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}},\,l_{\mathcal{E}},\,m = n_p,\,r = n_p/k)\times \frac{k}{n_p} \\
&\quad + \text{CrossAttn.FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}},\,l_{\mathcal{D}},\,m = k,\,r = k/n_p).
\end{aligned}
\]

Here the important symbols are:
- $n_{\text{ctx}}$: context length in bytes (bytes seen in a sequence),
- $n_p$: average patch size (bytes per patch),
- $w_{\mathcal{E}}, w_{\mathcal{D}}$: local attention window sizes in encoder/decoder,
- $h_{\mathcal{E}}, h_{\mathcal{G}}, h_{\mathcal{D}}$: hidden sizes of encoder, global, decoder,
- $l_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{G}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}$: layer counts,
- $k$: ratio that captures how local patch representations are concatenated to form the global representation (e.g., $k=2$ in the paper's diagrams),
- $r$: a bookkeeping ratio capturing query-to-key/value counts in the cross‑attention accounting,
- $\text{Transf.FL}(\cdot)$ and $\text{CrossAttn.FL}(\cdot)$ denote the transformer and cross‑attention FLOP formulas used in the paper (they follow Chinchilla-style equations, adapted for different context lengths and the special cross‑attention shapes).

Two implementation assumptions in the paper matter for these counts: embedding lookups are treated as efficient table lookups (zero FLOPs in the estimate), and the backward pass is estimated as twice the forward FLOPs. The paper also notes that for fixed mask attention (block causal, fixed-window) they use FlashAttention in practice, but for dynamic cross‑attention masks they rely on FlexAttention to fuse operations — that affects wall‑clock performance but the FLOP accounting uses the standard arithmetic counts.

Interpreting the terms: the first (global) line is the global Transformer's total FLOPs for a sequence of $m = n_{\text{ctx}}/n_p$ patches converted into a per‑byte number (hence dividing by $n_p$). The second and third lines give the per‑byte cost of the local encoder and decoder transformer stacks (they are expressed as per‑position quantities because those layers operate in a sliding/local manner). The fourth and fifth terms are the encoder and decoder cross‑attention costs, converted into per‑byte units. This decomposition isolates the contributions that change with patch size ($n_p$) from those that do not.

# How patch size affects both training and inference FLOPs

Patch size $n_p$ is the central control knob in BLT's cost model. Its effect is qualitatively simple and practically powerful:

- The latent global Transformer cost per byte decreases as patch size increases. In the accounting above the global Transformer term depends on the number of patches $m = n_{\text{ctx}}/n_p$. If you double $n_p$, you roughly halve the number of patches, and since the global model is the dominant consumer of FLOPs (it contains large FFNs and many layers), this reduces the dominant cost substantially.

- Local encoder and decoder Transformer layers are applied at the byte level (or in a local sliding window) and therefore their per‑byte cost is largely independent of $n_p$. Increasing patch size does not eliminate the need to run these local layers on every byte (though the frequency with which the global model is invoked changes).

- Cross‑attention costs sit in between: each encoder cross‑attention maps a patch query to the bytes that make up that patch. Larger $n_p$ increases the number of keys/values for each cross‑attention but reduces the number of queries (fewer patches). The net per‑byte effect is smaller than on the global Transformer and tends to scale more weakly with $n_p$.

Because the global transformer dominates, increasing patch size yields large returns initially, but returns diminish: as $n_p$ grows the global term shrinks and the fixed per‑byte local costs become the floor below which total cost cannot fall. That is why the paper observes diminishing returns in extreme patching: after a point, further increasing patch size saves very little overall FLOPs because local encoders/decoders and cross‑attention dominate.

BLT leverages this tradeoff operationally. For a fixed inference FLOP budget you can either run many small global steps with a smaller model, or run fewer global steps with a larger global Transformer. The paper demonstrates that increasing $n_p$ to reduce global steps and reallocating saved compute into increasing the size of the global Transformer can produce strictly better scaling behavior (the "patches scale better than tokens" finding).

A concrete consequence reported in the paper: average token sizes for popular tokenizers are in the 3–4 byte range (e.g., Llama 2: 3.7 bytes, Llama 3: 4.4 bytes). BLT can use average patch sizes of 6 or 8 bytes. Because inference FLOPs are approximately inversely related to average patch size for the global Transformer term, moving from a 4‑byte average to an 8‑byte average can lead to up to about 50% fewer inference FLOPs in practice (subject to the floor imposed by local modules).

Finally, note that dynamic patching (e.g., entropy‑based patching) means $n_p$ is an average over the data and varies by context: predictable stretches are grouped into longer patches (fewer global steps), while high‑entropy regions are given finer granularity (shorter patches) so the model allocates compute where it matters. This content‑adaptive allocation is a key source of BLT's efficiency and quality gains.

# Shifting output projection to the local decoder (byte vocabulary of 256)

One of the structural cost differences between tokenizer‑based models and BLT is where the final output projection and the embedding tables live. Tokenizer models must choose a finite vocabulary, and that choice affects two heavy objects: the input embedding table and the final output projection. Making the vocabulary larger increases average token size (so fewer steps) but also increases embedding and output projection sizes and thus parameter count and some computation.

BLT eliminates that tradeoff by predicting raw bytes at the very end. The local decoder performs the final projection to the byte vocabulary of size $V=256$. In the FLOP accounting above the only place where $V$ appears is inside the local decoder Transformer term. This design has two practical benefits:

- The final output projection is fixed and small (256 classes), so increasing patch size does not expand the output matrix or its computational cost. BLT therefore decouples the "compress‑into‑fewer‑steps" goal from the "grow the output vocabulary" cost that tokenized models must pay.

- The input embedding in the encoder is implemented as a table lookup (treated as zero FLOPs in the paper's accounting), so BLT does not pay a large input embedding multiply for bytes.

The paper contrasts this with tokenizers: for example, Llama 3 increased average token size from 3.7 to 4.4 bytes but had to increase embedding table memory by about 4× compared to Llama 2. BLT achieves longer effective tokens (patches) without inflating the projection/embedding widths because the byte alphabet at the interfaces is fixed.

# Equalizing context length across models to ensure fair comparisons

When comparing models with different patch sizes or different tokenizations, a subtle fairness issue appears: a model that uses larger patches might implicitly see a larger effective context in terms of semantic content if you keep the same number of model positions. To avoid such confounding effects the paper enforces equality of the number of bytes processed per batch in expectation. Concretely:

- For a given dataset mix, BLT experiments choose an average byte context length (for the Llama 2 data the paper uses an 8k byte context, and for the BLT‑1T dataset they use a 16k byte context) and keep the expected batch byte count constant (they report a 16M byte average batch size).

- If a BLT configuration uses a larger patch size, the number of patches in a sequence falls, so the sequence length passed to the global Transformer is reduced accordingly. To keep the total bytes seen per GPU the same across configurations, they reduce the sequence length for models with larger patches.

This equalization is important because it isolates the effect of patching on computation from the simple effect of exposing the model to more or less context. All reported comparisons in the paper are performed with this bookkeeping so that observed gains are due to BLT architecture and patching strategy rather than to extra bytes of context.

# Patch packing to minimize padding in the latent transformer

Dynamic patching produces sequences with variable numbers of patches. If you naively batch those sequences you either have to pad many sequences to the same large number of patches or you waste compute on short sequences. BLT tackles this with patch packing: during dataloading the implementation groups patches into packed batches so that the latent transformer receives compact patch sequences and padding is minimized.

Two practical implementation details from the paper are worth remembering:

- For efficiency they make every batch have the same number of patches (patch packing), which avoids wasted latent Transformer steps on padding tokens and yields predictable memory behavior.

- To prevent pathological memory spikes due to unusually large patches, they cap sequence lengths at training time (they pad/truncate byte sequences to a maximum that depends on the dataset: 12k bytes for Llama 2 experiments and 24k bytes for BLT‑1T experiments).

Patch packing is therefore a small but essential operational optimization that turns dynamic, variable‑length patching into an efficient training pipeline.

# Interpreting BPB in relation to downstream task accuracy and pass@1

Perplexity is the standard intrinsic measure for language models, but it depends on a fixed tokenization. For tokenizer‑free or byte‑level models the paper uses Bits‑Per‑Byte (BPB), which is a tokenizer‑independent analogue. The paper defines BPB as

\[
\text{BPB}(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \frac{\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\mathbf{x})}{\ln(2)\cdot n_{\text{bytes}}},
\]

where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\mathbf{x})$ is the cross‑entropy loss on example $\mathbf{x}$ (in nats), and $n_{\text{bytes}}$ is the number of bytes in $\mathbf{x}$. Lower BPB means the model assigns higher likelihood (less uncertainty) per byte.

BPB is a useful intrinsic metric for byte models and it correlates with language modeling quality, but it is not a perfect proxy for downstream task performance. The relationship between a single‑number LM objective and downstream metrics such as classification accuracy or pass@1 for code is complex: different tasks emphasize different modeling competencies (e.g., reasoning, factual recall, code synthesis) and decoding strategies interact with model calibration and entropy in nontrivial ways.

Empirically the paper reports that improvements in BPB for BLT (and the advantages of dynamic patching) often carry forward to downstream tasks: the BLT‑Entropy variant exceeded Llama 3 on several benchmarks while being trained on the same number of bytes, and improvements in BPB on long‑tail data relate to better robustness and character‑level skills (CUTE, G2P, low‑resource translation). However, other BLT variants that emphasize larger average patch size for inference‑flop savings (e.g., BLT‑Space with larger patches) showed inferable tradeoffs — they save FLOPs but can be farther from the compute‑optimal operating point and underperform on some benchmarks.

In short: BPB is the right tokenizer‑independent intrinsic metric to monitor for byte models. It is predictive of quality improvements in many cases, but proper evaluation still requires downstream task measurements (classification, pass@1, robustness benchmarks) because architectural choices (patch size, allocation of compute across modules, entropy thresholds at inference time) affect these tasks in task‑specific ways.

# Conclusion and operational takeaways

Putting the pieces together:

- FFNs dominate compute at scale; minimizing how often large FFNs run (fewer global steps) is the primary lever for reducing overall FLOPs.

- BLT's FLOP accounting isolates the dominant global Transformer term and the relatively fixed local terms. The per‑byte global cost scales down as patch size increases (roughly inversely with $n_p$ in the regime where the global term dominates).

- Cross‑attention and local encoder/decoder costs are important but smaller and less sensitive to patch size. As patch size grows, those local costs form a floor for achievable FLOP savings.

- Shifting the byte vocabulary projection to the local decoder (fixed $V=256$) decouples patch size from embedding/projection size and avoids the vocabulary/compute tradeoff tokenizers must manage.

- Fair comparisons require equalizing bytes per batch and careful batching/packing of patches to avoid wasted computation from padding.

- BPB is the appropriate intrinsic metric for byte models; reductions in BPB often correlate with practical gains, but downstream evaluations (accuracy, pass@1, robustness) remain necessary to assess real‑world performance.

These cost drivers explain BLT's effectiveness: by aggregating bytes into patches adaptively and running the heavy global model only at patch granularity, BLT heavily reduces the dominant FFN compute while preserving per‑byte expressivity through local modules and cross‑attention. The result is an architecture that can trade off inference FLOPs against model size and accuracy in a principled way.

Experimental Setup and Implementation Details

# Pretraining datasets: Llama 2 mix and BLT-1T (sources, sizes, quality considerations)

Choosing the pretraining data and how it is mixed is a core part of any fair comparison between architectures. In our experiments we used two distinct datasets with complementary roles.

The Llama 2 dataset is the larger of the two used for scaling-law and compute-optimal experiments. It is a cleaned and filtered mix of publicly available sources totaling roughly 2 trillion tokens. This dataset is used when we want to follow the compute-optimal training regime recommended by prior work (so that models of different sizes are trained using comparable data/parameter budgets). Because it is already processed and filtered, it serves as a stable reference mixture for controlled architectural ablations and scaling studies.

BLT-1T is a separate dataset created for BLT’s full pretraining runs. It contains about 1 trillion tokens compiled from various public sources and includes a subset of the Datacomp-LM pretraining release. We used BLT-1T for the complete BLT 8B run that we compare to Llama 3 on downstream tasks. A deliberate choice was made to avoid using any data gathered from Meta products or services in either dataset.

Why two datasets? The Llama 2 mix gives us a large, stable baseline for controlled scaling experiments (so that we can compare against tokenized baselines under the same compute-optimal assumptions). BLT-1T is a higher-quality, somewhat different mixture that we use for the final large-scale BLT runs and downstream task comparisons. When reporting downstream results, we explicitly note which dataset the model was trained on so comparisons remain clear.

# Tokenizer baselines: Llama 2 vs Llama 3 vocabularies and their effects

When comparing byte-level approaches to tokenized baselines, the choice of tokenizer matters. In our baselines we used the Llama 3 tokenizer (which in our experiments uses a vocabulary of 128K tokens) because it produced stronger baseline performance than the Llama 2 tokenizer on the same training data. Two concrete, related empirical observations appear in the experiments:

- Average token size (bytes per token) changes with the tokenizer. In the Llama 2 / Llama 3 comparison we report average token sizes of approximately 3.7 bytes (Llama 2 tokenizer) and 4.4 bytes (Llama 3 tokenizer) on the training mixtures we used. Increasing average token size reduces the number of transformer steps needed to cover a fixed byte budget, which in turn reduces inference cost.

- Increasing vocabulary size has a parameter-cost side-effect. For example, moving to the Llama 3 tokenizer increased the embedding table (and related final-projection parameter cost) by roughly a factor of 4 compared to Llama 2 in our setups. This is the classic trade-off for tokenized models: making tokens larger (by increasing vocabulary size) reduces sequence length but increases static parameter costs in embedding/projection layers. That trade-off limits how far tokenizers can be tuned to change inference cost without incurring large parameter overhead.

These effects motivate an important part of the BLT argument: patching (no fixed vocabulary) separates sequence-step cost from the cost of a large output projection or embedding table, allowing more flexible control of inference cost without the embedding-size penalty that tokenizers must pay.

# Entropy model design: parameter scales, context windows, training distribution; lookup-table variants

Entropy-based patching relies on accurate estimates of next-byte uncertainty. To obtain those estimates we train a small byte-level, auto-regressive language model (the "entropy model") on the same training distribution as the full BLT model. The entropy model is intentionally lightweight compared to the main BLT global transformer so it can be run efficiently at dataload time.

A typical configuration used in our experiments is a transformer with about 100M parameters, 14 layers, and hidden dimensionality of 512 with a sliding-window attention receptive field of 512 bytes. The entropy used at each byte position is computed under the entropy model distribution \(p_e\) over the byte vocabulary \(\mathcal{V}\) as:

\[
H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i})
\]

(These entropies are computed at dataloading as a lightweight preprocessing step and are used to determine patch boundaries — see the next section.)

We explored the design space of the entropy model in two axes: model size and receptive field. Empirically, scaling the entropy model from very small sizes up toward tens of millions of parameters improved downstream BPB (bits-per-byte) and scaling behavior, with diminishing returns beyond roughly 50M parameters. Similarly, increasing the context window up to a few hundred bytes (we tried windows from 64 up to 512) improved boundary quality. When the entropy model's receptive field is small enough, its mapping from local context to entropy can be implemented as a compact lookup table (a practical optimization), because there are only a limited number of distinct short contexts to index.

Two practical points about running the entropy model in production training:

1. The entropy model is trained on the same data distribution as the main model so its uncertainty estimates reflect the same mixture.  
2. When computing entropies for long repeated contexts (for example, highly repetitive evaluations such as multiple-choice items), we observed so-called "entropy drift" where the entropy model’s long receptive field causes patch boundaries to expand artificially. To mitigate this, in some large-scale BLT-Entropy runs we reset the entropy model context at new lines and used the approximate monotonicity constraint (described below), which reduces sensitivity to drift.

# Estimating thresholds to target specific average patch sizes

Entropy patching converts a stream of bytes into patches by marking boundary bytes where the next-byte uncertainty is relatively high. Two simple, effective conditions we used to identify patch boundaries are:

\[
\text{Global constraint:}\qquad H(x_t) > \theta_g
\]

\[
\text{Approx.\ monotonicity constraint:}\qquad H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r
\]

The global constraint marks bytes whose predicted next-byte entropy exceeds a global threshold \(\theta_g\). The monotonicity constraint marks points where entropy increases sufficiently relative to the immediately preceding byte; this tends to detect local increases in uncertainty that break an approximately decreasing-entropy pattern within a patch.

In practice we choose \(\theta_g\) or \(\theta_r\) by measuring entropies across a representative sample of the pretraining mixture and picking the threshold that yields the desired average patch size (bytes per patch). In other words, threshold selection is an empirical calibration: run the entropy model across sample data, compute the empirical distribution of \(H(x)\), and select \(\theta\) so that the expected number of bytes per patch matches the target. Lowering a threshold (making it easier to trigger a boundary) produces, as expected, smaller patches (more boundaries) and therefore higher inference cost; raising the threshold produces larger patches and lower inference cost.

Because the patching decision must be available during generation, we require an incremental patching property: a patching function \(f_p\) must satisfy

\[
f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i},
\]

i.e., the segmentation up to position \(i\) must be determinable from the prefix alone. Entropy patching computed from \(p_e(x_i \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i})\) satisfies this property, while many learned tokenizers (e.g., BPE) do not.

A practical inference-time usage pattern is to tune \(\theta\) to trade compute for accuracy. For example, in one BLT-Entropy run we used a higher threshold during training (average patch size target \(\sim 4.5\) bytes) but lowered \(\theta\) at inference from 0.6 to 0.1 to obtain better task accuracy at the expense of more inference steps. This demonstrates that entropy thresholds can be treated as a convenient accuracy–cost knob at inference time.

# Controlling context length in bytes; byte-level batching versus patch-level packing

When comparing BLT variants with different average patch sizes (or comparing against tokenized baselines), it is critical to equalize the number of bytes the model sees in expectation. Patch size directly controls the number of transformer steps taken by the global latent transformer; to avoid giving larger-patch models an unfair advantage through longer effective contexts, we equalize at the byte level.

Concretely, we make sure the number of bytes in each batch remains constant in expectation. Two practical consequences follow:

- For training runs on the Llama 2 mix we used an average context length of about 8k bytes per sequence; for BLT-1T experiments we increased the average context to about 16k bytes. In both cases we kept the total batch size measured in bytes constant at about 16M bytes on average. That means for a larger average patch size we reduce the number of patch positions we feed the latent transformer per sequence so that total bytes per batch remain steady.

- In data loading, dynamic patching yields variable numbers of patches for sequences of fixed byte-length. To keep the global (latent) transformer batches efficient we pack patches so that each training batch has the same number of patches (minimizing padding across the expensive latent transformer steps). In other words, we perform patch-level packing inside each batch: patches are grouped to make batches dense in patch positions, which reduces wasted computation due to padding.

To control rare worst cases we also cap sequence byte lengths: during training we pad or truncate sequences to at most 12k bytes for experiments on Llama 2 data and 24k bytes for BLT-1T runs. This avoids memory spikes caused by extremely large patches in long sequences.

These batching choices (equal-bytes batching, patch packing, and safe truncation caps) are essential to make flops- and data-normalized comparisons between BLT and tokenized baselines.

# Attention implementations: Flash Attention and Flex Attention for dynamic masks

BLT mixes three attention regimes and for efficiency we map each to an implementation that best matches its mask structure.

1. Fixed-mask self-attention (global and local transformer self-attention): For self-attention layers that use fixed-standard masks (for example block-causal masks in the latent global transformer or fixed-window block-causal masks in local byte-level transformers), we use FlashAttention. FlashAttention implements attention with minimal memory movement and is highly efficient when the attention mask can be expressed statically per layer. For fixed-window attention (e.g., local windows of size 512) we also use FlashAttention with a window size of 512 for these layers.

2. Dynamic, patch-dependent cross-attention: The encoder and decoder cross-attention layers use masks that depend on the dynamic patch boundaries (each query attends only to the bytes belonging to its patch). Because these masks vary per-batch and per-patch, they cannot be implemented efficiently with a single static kernel. For these layers we use FlexAttention (or equivalent fused implementations) that support dynamic masks and can fuse the required operations to be efficient in practice.

Implementation notes that follow from the architecture:

- Encoder cross-attention: queries are initialized per patch (by pooling byte representations for the patch) and keys/values come from the byte embeddings for the bytes composing that patch; the query-to-key/value attention mask is therefore per-patch.

- Decoder cross-attention: roles are swapped; byte positions are queries and patch representations are keys/values, so again the attention mask depends on patch boundaries.

Because cross-attention is patch-dependent, a specialized fused implementation (FlexAttention) substantially reduces overhead versus a naive per-patch attention loop.

# Shared transformer hyperparameters: SwiGLU, RoPE theta setting, RMSNorm, local windows

To keep the transformer primitives comparable to large, production-quality tokenized models, the BLT implementation adopts most of the same micro-choices used by contemporary Llama-style models:

- Activation: the feed-forward networks use the SwiGLU activation. SwiGLU is adopted throughout the local and global feed-forward layers.

- Rotary positional embeddings (RoPE): RoPE are applied in self-attention layers only and we use the same frequency parameterization \(\theta = 500000\) that is commonly used in Llama-style models.

- Normalization: RMSNorm is used for layer normalization across local and global transformer layers.

- Local windows and masks: the local encoder uses a local block-causal attention mask where each byte attends to a fixed window of \(w_{\mathcal{E}}\) preceding bytes (we commonly use a window size of 512 for such fixed-width attention). The local block-causal mask can cross dynamic patch boundaries but does not cross document boundaries. The global latent transformer uses a block-causal attention mask that restricts attention to the current and preceding patches within the document.

- Cross-attention specifics: cross-attention modules use a pre-LayerNorm on the queries, keys, and values. No positional embeddings are used inside the cross-attention modules. Residual connections are used around cross-attention blocks. When applying multi-headed cross-attention where patch dimensionality differs from byte dimensionality, we maintain multiple heads at the encoder dimensionality and later concatenate to form the global representation.

These shared hyperparameter choices keep BLT’s building blocks aligned with strong tokenized baselines so that observed differences are due to patching and architectural choices rather than low-level transformer recipe differences.

# Optimization details: AdamW, schedules, gradient clipping, batch sizes

Reproducible optimization settings are essential for fair comparisons. Across BLT experiments we used a consistent optimization recipe:

- Optimizer: AdamW with \(\beta_1=0.9\), \(\beta_2=0.95\), and \(\epsilon=10^{-8}\).

- Learning rate: a base learning rate of \(4\mathrm{e}{-4}\) for all BLT and baseline models. This value was chosen after a small hyperparameter sweep in the 400M–1B regime (searching between \(1\mathrm{e}{-3}\) and \(1\mathrm{e}{-4}\)), which showed the same learning rate was near-optimal for both tokenized and BLT models in that range.

- Warm-up and decay: linear warm-up for 2000 steps followed by cosine decay of the learning rate down to 0.

- Regularization: weight decay set to 0.1.

- Gradient clipping: global gradient clipping at a norm threshold of 1.0.

- Batch sizes (data-normalized): for Llama 2 scaling studies we used the batch sizes recommended by Llama 3 (or their byte-equivalent). For the BLT-1T full pretraining runs we maintained an average batch size of about 16M bytes. These byte-normalized batch sizes are critical for fair flop-controlled comparisons.

Practical BLT-specific training knobs that affect convergence:

- N-gram hash embeddings: encoder byte embeddings are augmented with hash n-gram embeddings. In our runs we used 500,000 hashes, a single hash function, and n-gram sizes \(n \in \{3,4,5,6,7,8\}\). The augmented per-byte embedding is normalized by the number of n-gram sizes plus one.

- Encoder initialization of cross-attention queries: for the first encoder cross-attention layer, we initialize per-patch queries using max-pooling over the bytes in the patch.

- Local model sizing: local encoder/decoder sizes are deliberately kept small compared to the global transformer (i.e., \(l_{\mathcal{E}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}\) and \(l_{\mathcal{D}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}\)). In some experiments with larger patch sizes (for example, average patch size 8) we used 3 encoder layers instead of 1 to better support the larger patch representations.

# Inference-time threshold tuning: accuracy–cost trade-offs

One of BLT’s practical advantages is that the entropy threshold used to define patch boundaries is a deploy-time knob that trades compute for performance. Because the entropy-model-based patcher is incremental, the model can change thresholds at inference time without retraining the global transformer.

Key operational points:

- A lower entropy threshold (smaller \(\theta_g\) or \(\theta_r\)) will introduce more patch boundaries, producing smaller patches and therefore more latent-transformer steps; this generally improves accuracy at the cost of increased inference flops.

- Conversely, raising the threshold produces larger patches and reduces inference cost, possibly with some performance degradation.

- We demonstrate this empirically: the BLT-Entropy model used one threshold during training (so average patch size on the training mixture matched the training-flop target) but at inference we could lower the threshold (e.g., from 0.6 to 0.1 in one run) and obtain better downstream-task performance at the expense of additional inference steps. This is a practical lever that allows a single pretrained BLT checkpoint to be tuned for different deployment compute budgets.

- BLT can also be configured to target a specific inference flop budget by increasing patch size and reallocating saved flops to grow the global transformer size. This capability underlies the fixed-inference-flop scaling studies: for a fixed inference budget, we can train larger global transformers by choosing larger patch sizes and thus fewer latent steps.

Because threshold tuning is a purely inference-time change (the segmentation decision depends only on the already-generated prefix), it is an effective and flexible mechanism to adapt models to different latency/throughput budgets without retraining.

---

Summary (practical checklist)

- Use the Llama 2 dataset for controlled compute-optimal scaling studies and a separate BLT-1T mix for full-scale BLT pretraining runs. Exclude proprietary Meta product data in both mixes.

- For entropy patching, train a lightweight byte-level transformer (a good baseline: 100M parameters, 14 layers, hidden 512, 512-byte sliding window) on the same distribution as the main model.

- Compute next-byte entropies at dataloading and choose a global or relative threshold \(\theta_g\) / \(\theta_r\) to hit a target average patch size on the pretraining mixture.

- Equalize the number of bytes per batch in expectation across patching schemes. Pack patches to make latent-transformer batches dense, and cap/truncate sequences (12k / 24k bytes for the two dataset regimes) to avoid memory spikes.

- Use FlashAttention for fixed-mask self-attention (windowed or block-causal) and FlexAttention for dynamic patch-dependent cross-attention masks.

- Adopt shared transformer micro-choices consistent with Llama-style recipes: SwiGLU, RoPE with \(\theta=500000\) in self-attention, RMSNorm, and local windowed attention (512 where used).

- Train with AdamW (\(\beta_1=0.9,\ \beta_2=0.95,\ \epsilon=10^{-8}\)), LR \(4\mathrm{e}{-4}\), 2k-step linear warmup, cosine decay, weight decay 0.1, and global gradient clipping at 1.0. Use byte-normalized batch sizes (e.g., ~16M bytes total per batch on average).

- At inference, tune \(\theta\) as a direct accuracy–cost knob; lowering \(\theta\) increases compute and typically improves accuracy, while increasing \(\theta\) reduces compute and may sacrifice some performance.

Following these dataset, entropy, batching, attention, and optimization conventions ensures that BLT evaluations are measured and compared fairly against strong tokenized baselines while preserving the practical flexibility that patch-based, tokenizer-free models offer.

Scaling Studies: Matching and Surpassing Token-Based Models

# Parameter-matched, compute-optimal comparisons with BPE LLaMA across sizes

A central empirical question for the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) is whether a tokenizer-free, byte-level architecture can match the training and scaling behavior of modern tokenization-based models when both are trained under the same computational constraints. To answer this, BLT was evaluated in a compute-optimal regime: for each model size the experiments follow the parameter-to-data ratio identified as compute-optimal by the Llama 3 study, so that the bpe baselines and BLT variants are compared fairly for a given training FLOP budget. Models were trained on the Llama 2 dataset at multiple sizes (from roughly 1B up to 8B parameters) and performance was measured using a tokenizer-independent language-modeling metric, Bits-Per-Byte (BPB). BPB is computed as
\[
\text{BPB}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\mathbf{x})}{\ln(2)\cdot n_{\text{bytes}}}
\]
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\mathbf{x})$ is the cross-entropy loss over the byte sequence $\mathbf{x}$ and $n_{\text{bytes}}$ its length.

The compute-optimal comparison shows that BLT can match or outperform bpe-based LLaMA models across the trained sizes. In other words, when both model families receive the same FLOP budget and are sized according to the same compute-optimal guideline, BLT follows the same favorable scaling trend as token-based models instead of lagging behind. This result is notable because previous byte-level approaches often required many more FLOPs to approach token-model performance; BLT is, to the authors' knowledge, the first byte-level Transformer architecture to achieve parity in compute-optimal scaling trends up to the studied scales. The experiments also indicate that the notion of a compute-optimal parameter-to-data ratio determined for bpe transformers remains a useful guide for BLT: its models trained under those choices achieve strong results without needing a radically different training-regime prescription.

# Architectural improvements and dynamic patching versus space/strided baselines

Two orthogonal ingredients are critical to BLT’s success: (1) architectural changes that let the model mix byte-level and patch-level signals efficiently, and (2) dynamic, data-dependent patching that groups bytes where it makes sense to do so. To understand their roles, the paper compares BLT to simpler alternatives: fixed-size (strided) patches, and space-based patching (creating boundaries at space-like bytes).

Strided patching (fixed stride of $k$ bytes) is simple to implement and directly controls average patch size, but it wastes compute by assigning the same transformer step cost to predictable and unpredictable bytes alike and can split the same word inconsistently across contexts. Space patching is an improvement: it creates a latent step per word (or per space-like delimiter), so model steps tend to align with linguistic boundaries, allocating attention steps where harder predictions often occur (e.g., the first character of a new word). Empirically, space patching is a close competitor to more sophisticated schemes but it does not allow varying patch size continuously, nor does it directly target prediction uncertainty.

Entropy patching addresses these limitations by letting a small, separately trained byte-level autoregressive language model provide an estimate of the next-byte uncertainty. The entropy used to identify likely patch starts is computed (in the experiments) under the entropy model distribution $p_e$ as
\[
H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v\mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v\mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}),
\]
and two practical boundary rules are tested: a global threshold
\[
H(x_t) > \theta_g,
\]
and an approximate-monotonicity rule
\[
H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r.
\]
Using entropy to choose boundaries creates patches that concentrate model steps on bytes that are intrinsically harder to predict in-context and produce more consistent segmentations for similar substrings.

On the architectural side, BLT departs from a vanilla Transformer-only design by sandwiching a large latent global Transformer between two compact byte-level local models: a light local encoder that aggregates raw bytes into patch representations (using hash n-gram embeddings and per-patch cross-attention) and a light local decoder that expands patch outputs back into bytes. The encoder uses hash n-gram embeddings (maps of recent byte-grams through a rolling polynomial hash into a small embedding table) to enrich byte inputs with short-context statistics. Cross-attention layers in the encoder pool byte-level representations into patch queries; in the decoder the cross-attention is reversed so byte queries retrieve information from patch-level keys/values. Ablations reported in the paper show that the decoder cross-attention is particularly effective and that hash n-gram embeddings materially improve BPB on some domains; moreover, removing these architectural elements (approximating prior static-patching designs) leaves models behind current token-based state of the art. Put together, the architecture plus entropy-driven patching is what closes the gap to bpe models.

# Impact of tokenizer choice for baselines (Llama 2 vs Llama 3)

When comparing BLT to token-based baselines it is important to recognize that not all tokenizers are equal. The paper reports that models trained with the Llama 3 tokenizer (a 128K vocabulary) outperform otherwise equivalent models trained with the earlier Llama 2 tokenizer (a 32K vocabulary) on the same training data. Two concrete numbers reported in the experiments are the average token sizes: $\approx 3.7$ bytes per token for Llama 2 and $\approx 4.4$ bytes per token for Llama 3. Increasing the vocabulary and therefore average token size reduces the number of autoregressive steps a token-based model must execute for a given byte sequence, but it comes at the cost of larger embedding and output projection tables; in Llama 3 that increase was large (the embedding table size grows \textit{substantially}, in the paper expressed as a 4$\times$ embedding table increase compared to Llama 2).

BLT avoids this trade-off because it does not require a fixed finite vocabulary of token types. Patch size (the number of bytes grouped into a patch) can be changed without inflating a parameter-heavy vocabulary table, and the model maps arbitrary byte groups into latent representations through its learned encoder/decoder. This decoupling gives BLT more flexibility: it can select longer average patches to reduce inference cost without paying the large embedding-table penalty that a token-based model would incur by increasing its vocabulary.

# Larger patch sizes (6–8 bytes): inference savings, scale dependence, and convergence behavior

Average patch size is the main mechanical lever controlling how many latent Transformer steps BLT needs to process a fixed number of bytes, and therefore how many FLOPs are consumed by the global model. A key empirical finding is that increasing average patch size reduces inference cost approximately inversely: the paper states that moving to an average patch size of about $8$ bytes can yield \emph{nearly 50\%} inference FLOP savings compared to smaller patches. Concretely, larger patches mean fewer latent Transformer steps per byte of input, and since the global Transformer consumes the bulk of compute at scale, this yields substantial savings.

The experiments uncover an important scale dependence in how different patch sizes behave. With small training budgets (or at smaller model sizes), token-based baselines may outperform BLT variants with very large patches. However, as model size and/or training compute increase, BLT models with larger patch sizes can catch up and then surpass token baselines. For example, an entropy-patching BLT with average patch size $\approx 8$ bytes starts at a weaker point than bpe Llama 2 at the 1B scale but ends up outperforming bpe by about the 7B scale in the studied curves. This behavior suggests an interaction between patch size and model capacity: larger patches save inference steps but shift more of the representational burden onto the global Transformer; when the global Transformer is scaled up (which the patch savings make feasible under a fixed inference budget), the combined system can exceed the performance of smaller models that had more steps but less capacity per step.

Two practical implications follow. First, average patch size is a tunable knob that trades off inference cost against how quickly a model can learn at small scales; second, when planning to train a larger global model under a fixed inference cost, it can be advantageous to increase patch size and reallocate FLOPs to the global Transformer rather than keeping many small steps.

# Beyond compute-optimal: 8B BLT on BLT-1T and downstream task performance

The paper also evaluates BLT when trained beyond the compute-optimal ratios used earlier, by training an $8$B-parameter BLT model on the larger, higher-quality BLT-1T dataset and evaluating on a suite of downstream tasks (commonsense reasoning, world knowledge, and code generation). The chosen benchmarks include ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, PIQA, MMLU (5-shot), MBPP (3-shot), and HumanEval (0-shot). Two variants of BLT were compared to a bpe Llama 3 model trained on the same dataset: BLT-Space (space patching) and BLT-Entropy (entropy patching, with a pragmatic inference-time adjustment to the entropy threshold from $0.6$ down to $0.1$ to increase steps and improve task accuracy at the cost of more inference FLOPs).

Results are summarized qualitatively in the paper: BLT-Entropy outperforms Llama 3 on 4 out of the 7 evaluated tasks under the same training-byte budget. BLT-Space, while more inference-efficient due to an average patch size of about $6$ bytes, generally underperforms Llama 3 on those benchmarks. These outcomes illustrate a trade-off practitioners can make: BLT variants with entropy patching can achieve higher task accuracy when tuned for more steps at inference, while BLT variants that prioritize larger patches (space patching) achieve better inference efficiency but may concede some downstream performance.

# Fixed-inference-budget scaling: simultaneously increasing patch size and model size

One of BLT's most novel capabilities is to expand model capacity while holding a fixed per-inference FLOP budget by increasing patch size and using the compute saved to grow the global Transformer. The paper reports a controlled study where, for two target inference-FLOP classes (one centered on small models like $400\text{M}$ parameters and another on larger classes like $\sim 3.6\text{B}$), the authors construct flop-equivalent models using (a) the Llama 2 tokenizer baseline, (b) the Llama 3 tokenizer baseline, and (c) BLT-Entropy with average patch sizes of $\approx 6$ and $\approx 8$ bytes. For the patch-size-$8$ BLT variant they used a slightly larger local encoder (three encoder layers instead of one) to support the longer patches during encoding.

These models were then trained over a range of training-FLOP budgets. The main empirical pattern is that BLT variants show systematically better scaling trends under a fixed inference-FLOP constraint: given more training compute, BLT can trade saved inference steps (via longer patches) for larger global capacity, and this reallocation yields larger accuracy gains than what token-based models obtain for the same inference cost. Across both inference-FLOP classes, tokenized models win at very small training budgets but are quickly overtaken by BLT models once training FLOPs increase somewhat beyond the compute-optimal point. This demonstrates that practitioners who care about a strict per-request efficiency budget can get better long-run performance by training BLT models that use longer patches and larger global Transformers.

# Crossover points where BLT surpasses BPE at a fixed inference cost

The experiments quantify where on the training-FLOP axis BLT models surpass token-based baselines. In the fixed-inference experiments the paper reports that the crossover — the multiplier over the compute-optimal training budget at which BLT overtakes bpe — depends on the inference-FLOP class and the chosen patch size. For the smaller inference-FLOP class the crossover was observed at roughly $3\times$ the compute-optimal training budget, whereas for the larger inference-FLOP class this crossover moved closer, to about $2.5\times$ the compute-optimal budget. The larger patch-size ($8$-byte) BLT models exhibit steeper scaling curves and therefore overtake baselines earlier still. In practice this means that spending additional training compute (beyond strictly compute-optimal amounts) to grow a BLT global Transformer while simultaneously increasing patch size can pay off faster in settings where inference efficiency is a primary constraint.

# Share of local versus global compute as model scale grows

Understanding why larger patches become increasingly attractive at scale requires examining how different parts of BLT consume FLOPs. The paper provides the per-byte FLOP accounting for BLT as the sum of contributions from the global latent Transformer, the local encoder and decoder Transformers, and the encoder/decoder cross-attention blocks. Formally (as used in the experiments), the per-byte FLOP estimate is written as

\begin{align*}
\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}} &= \frac{\text{Transf.FL}\big(h_{\mathcal{G}}, l_{\mathcal{G}}, m = n_{\text{ctx}} / n_p, V=0\big)}{n_p}
   + \text{Transf.FL}\big(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m = w_{\mathcal{E}}, V=0\big) \\
 &\quad+ \text{Transf.FL}\big(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m = w_{\mathcal{D}}, V=256\big)
   + \text{CrossAttn.FL}\big(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=n_p, r=n_p/k\big)\times \frac{k}{n_p} \\
 &\quad+ \text{CrossAttn.FL}\big(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=k, r=k/n_p\big),
\end{align*}

where $n_{\text{ctx}}$ is the context length in bytes, $n_p$ is the average patch size, $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ and $w_{\mathcal{D}}$ are the byte-level attention window sizes in the encoder and decoder, $h_{\mathcal{G}}, h_{\mathcal{E}}, h_{\mathcal{D}}$ are the hidden dimensions, and $V$ denotes vocabulary size used only in the decoder output projection. The crucial observation from this accounting is that the global Transformer term is the one that scales down roughly inversely with $n_p$ (because the number of patch steps in a sequence is $n_{\text{ctx}}/n_p$), while the local encoder and decoder terms operate at byte granularity and do not shrink with patch size.

As models are scaled up, the global Transformer dominates both parameter count and FLOP consumption. The paper notes that when total parameters grow by a large factor (e.g., about $20\times$ from $\sim 400\text{M}$ to $8\text{B}$), the local model parameters only grow modestly (roughly doubling in the reported setups). That means the relative overhead of the local encoder/decoder becomes smaller at larger model scales, which in turn magnifies the effectiveness of increasing patch size: most saved FLOPs drop out of the dominant global term and can be reallocated to enlarge the global Transformer. This factor contributes directly to why patches "scale better than tokens": by decoupling the number of autoregressive steps from raw bytes, BLT reduces the dominant per-step cost and lets practitioners choose larger per-step capacity under a fixed inference budget.

In short, the scaling story for BLT combines three mechanisms: (1) entropy-driven patching concentrates computation where uncertainty is high, (2) learned encoder/decoder modules and cross-attention let the model translate between byte and patch granularities efficiently, and (3) patch-length is a tunable lever that reduces the dominant global FLOP term and allows reallocation of compute to yield better performance per inference cost as models grow.

# Concluding perspective

The scaling studies in the paper show that a carefully engineered byte-level architecture with dynamic patching can close the historical performance gap against tokenization-based Transformers and, under inference-FLOP constraints, can outperform them as model size and training compute increase. The intuition is straightforward: tokens are a fixed, often suboptimal, pre-processing that imposes a uniform compute-per-token cost, while BLT’s patches let the model decide, based on data uncertainty, where to spend computation and how to compress sequences for the expensive global Transformer. Empirically, BLT matches bpe scaling under compute-optimal regimes and shows superior fixed-inference-budget scaling when patch size and global capacity are co-optimized, especially at larger scales.

Downstream Tasks and Efficiency Trade-offs

# Classification evaluations: ARC-E, ARC-C, HellaSwag, PIQA, MMLU (prompt scoring and protocols)

The paper evaluates BLT on a set of standard multiple-choice classification tasks that probe different aspects of language understanding: common-sense reasoning and world knowledge. The specific benchmarks used are ARC-Easy (ARC-E), ARC-Challenge (ARC-C), HellaSwag, PIQA, and MMLU. These tasks were chosen because they are widely used to measure general reasoning and factual knowledge in language models, and together they form a compact suite for comparing architectural choices under equal training budgets.

Evaluation is carried out with a prompt-scoring protocol. For each multiple-choice example the model is asked to score each candidate answer by computing the model likelihood over the answer characters and then selecting the choice with the highest likelihood. In notation, if the candidate answers for a prompt are C, the model computes for each choice c \in C an unnormalized score proportional to the chain of conditional byte probabilities for the characters in c, and selects argmax_c p(c \mid context). The reported metric is the average accuracy across the examples in each dataset. The authors follow a consistent shot protocol across datasets that they report: ARC-E, ARC-C, HellaSwag, and PIQA are evaluated zero-shot, while MMLU is evaluated in a 5-shot setting (i.e., five in-context examples are provided in the prompt). Using the same prompt-scoring procedure and shot configuration for all models makes the comparisons between BLT variants and BPE baselines directly comparable.

# Code generation: MBPP and HumanEval (pass@1 setup)

To evaluate code generation ability the paper reports results on two standard Python benchmarks: MBPP and HumanEval. MBPP is evaluated in a 3-shot setting and HumanEval in a 0-shot setting. The metric reported for both is pass@1, the common top-1 correctness measure used in code generation benchmarks (the first candidate produced by the model either passes or fails the evaluation tests). These benchmarks stress exact syntactic and functional output and therefore provide a complementary view of model capability to the multiple-choice classification suite: they test the model’s ability to generate correct, runnable code rather than to prefer one of several short answers.

Because BLT operates directly over raw bytes, it has native access to character-level structure (whitespace, punctuation, indentation) that is critical for code. The experiments in the paper report pass@1 for BLT variants and BPE baselines under the same flop and data budgets, so the observed differences reflect the effects of the architecture and patching choices rather than differences in training compute or data.

# Results overview: BLT-Entropy versus BPE baselines and BLT-Space

Across the combined set of seven downstream tasks (classification + code), the BLT variant that uses entropy-based dynamic patching (BLT-Entropy) outperforms the Llama 3 BPE baseline on 4 out of 7 tasks while being trained on the same number of bytes. The paper attributes this advantage to two interacting factors. First, entropy patching uses a small byte-level entropy model to place more computation where next-byte uncertainty is high, so the global latent transformer spends its capacity on the harder, high-information parts of the sequence. Second, the model directly reasons over raw bytes rather than relying on a fixed subword vocabulary, which improves generalization in long-tail and character-sensitive cases.

The BLT-Space variant, which patches on space-like bytes (a simple heuristic), produces larger average patches (about 6 bytes per patch on the training mix) and therefore reduces inference flops substantially. In the reported evaluations BLT-Space underperforms the Llama 3 baseline on most downstream metrics, but it achieves a sizable reduction in inference cost: larger patches mean fewer global transformer steps per input. By contrast, BLT-Entropy and the BPE baseline have similar effective average step sizes on the reported training mixture (roughly 4.5 bytes per step), so BLT-Entropy’s advantage comes from better allocation of the same computational budget rather than from simply taking fewer steps.

Two practical numbers are useful to hold in mind from the experiments: (1) the authors adjusted the entropy threshold used for BLT-Entropy at inference from 0.6 (the training threshold) down to 0.1 and found that this improved downstream task performance at the cost of executing more steps during inference; and (2) moving to still larger patches (e.g., average patch size \approx 8 bytes) reduces inference FLOPs so much that the paper reports nearly a 50% inference-FLOP saving relative to smaller-patch regimes. Finally, because BLT-Space’s larger patch size reduces the per-step cost, the same training-flop budget allows it to cover roughly 30% more raw bytes — a factor that can push the model away from the compute-optimal parameter/data balance for particular tasks.

# Adjusting entropy thresholds at inference: improved accuracy versus more steps

Entropy patching is driven by the per-byte next-token uncertainty estimated by a small, separate byte-level LM. The entropy at position i is computed as

\[
H(x_i) \;=\; \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}),
\]

where \(p_{e}\) is the entropy model’s next-byte distribution over the byte vocabulary \(\mathcal{V}\). Two simple rules can be used to identify patch boundaries from these entropies: a global threshold and an approximate-monotonicity rule,

\[
H(x_t) > \theta_g
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r.
\]

The choice of the global threshold \(\theta_g\) and the relative threshold \(\theta_r\) directly controls the trade-off between computational cost and accuracy. Lowering \(\theta_g\) or \(\theta_r\) makes it easier for a position to be marked as a patch boundary, which creates more (smaller) patches. More patches mean that the global latent transformer is invoked more often for a given input, increasing inference FLOPs and latency, but this finer segmentation concentrates compute on hard decisions and can improve downstream accuracy.

The paper demonstrates this trade-off concretely: for BLT-Entropy the authors use one threshold during preprocessing/training but found that lowering the threshold at inference (from 0.6 to 0.1 in their run) improved task accuracy at the cost of more inference steps. They also note practical measures to stabilize entropy-based decisions: using an approximate-monotonicity constraint and resetting the entropy model’s context at newlines can reduce “entropy drift” when the entropy model’s receptive context changes. In practice, inference-time threshold tuning is a simple and powerful knob: if you need higher accuracy and can afford extra latency, lower the entropy threshold; if you need higher throughput, raise it.

# When to prefer larger patches despite minor metric losses (throughput and latency considerations)

The core operational trade-off in BLT is between per-step computation (the cost of running the global latent transformer) and the number of such steps required to process an input. Because the global transformer is the dominant cost, reducing the number of steps by increasing the average patch size directly reduces inference cost. The paper summarizes this relation qualitatively as

\[
\text{FLOPs}_{\text{inference}} \propto \frac{1}{\text{avg\_patch\_size}},
\]

so, all else equal, doubling the average bytes per patch approximately halves the number of global-transformer steps and therefore roughly halves that dominant component of the inference FLOPs. The experiments show this effect in concrete terms: moving to an average patch size of about 8 bytes produces nearly 50% inference-FLOP savings, while the space-patching variant (avg \approx 6 bytes) enables substantial throughput improvements compared to 4.5-byte regimes.

When should you accept the modest metric losses that sometimes accompany large patches? The paper points to several deployment-driven reasons:

- Throughput-limited systems. If your system must serve many queries per second, reducing per-request FLOPs directly increases throughput. Larger patches are an effective way to shrink per-request cost.

- Latency-sensitive applications. Fewer global steps typically reduce latency because the heavy synchronous work runs less often. If small degradations in accuracy are acceptable for the benefit in response time, larger patches are sensible.

- Scaling model capacity under a fixed inference budget. Larger patches free FLOPs that can be reallocated to grow the global latent transformer’s parameter count while keeping per-request cost fixed. The paper’s fixed-inference-flop scaling experiments show that increasing both patch size and model size can yield better scaling than simply using more steps with a smaller model.

Conversely, keep patches smaller (or lower the entropy threshold) when maximum accuracy is essential or when the task relies on fine-grained character-level distinctions that benefit from more frequent global updates. If you prefer an easy, deterministic trade-off, the BLT-Space variant is a simple way to get larger patches; if you want a finer, data-driven accuracy/cost trade-off, use BLT-Entropy and tune \(\theta_g\) (and optionally \(\theta_r\)) at inference to the point that matches your latency/throughput/accuracy target.

Summary (practical tuning guidance drawn from the experiments): start with BLT-Entropy at the training-time threshold that yields your target average patch size, then tune \(\theta_g\) at inference to find the operating point you want. If you need maximal throughput and can tolerate modest performance drops, adopt larger static patches (BLT-Space) or deliberately raise the inference threshold. If you need maximum accuracy and have room for latency, lower the threshold to let the model take more steps. Finally, if you have a fixed inference FLOP budget and want the best possible model within that budget, consider increasing patch size and reallocating the saved compute to enlarge the global latent transformer; the paper shows this yields favorable scaling trends.

Robustness and Long-Tail Generalization from Bytes

# Why direct byte supervision helps robustness and morphology

One of the clearest conceptual differences between standard token-based language models and byte-level models like the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) is the level of granularity at which the model directly observes and reasons about the input. Tokenizers collapse variable-length byte sequences into elements drawn from a fixed vocabulary; this compression hides the internal character structure of tokens and enforces a particular segmentation chosen before training. That pre-processing step introduces two kinds of brittleness that directly affect robustness and morphological generalization: (1) small character-level changes can produce drastically different token sequences (or out-of-vocabulary behavior), and (2) the static token vocabulary gives unequal coverage across languages and orthographies, which hurts long-tail generalization in low-resource scripts and morphological forms. The BLT paper documents these are practical shortcomings of tokenization (sensitivity to input noise, lack of orthographic knowledge, multilingual inequity) and motivates learning directly from bytes to avoid them.

BLT mitigates these problems by keeping raw bytes as first-class inputs and by using learned mechanisms that expose short character sequences to the model rather than hiding them in fixed tokens. Two architectural components are particularly important here. First, the local encoder augments the basic byte embedding $x_i$ with hash n-gram embeddings so that the local representation explicitly summarizes short character substrings around each byte. Formally, for byte position $i$ and n-gram sizes $n=3\ldots 8$ we form
\[
g_{i,n} = \{b_{i-n+1},\ldots,b_i\}
\]
and then compute an augmented embedding
\[
e_i = x_i + \sum_{n=3}^{8} E_n^{\text{hash}}\big(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n})\big),
\]
normalized by the number of n-gram sizes plus one. These hash n-gram embeddings give the model immediate, explicit access to local orthographic patterns (suffixes, prefixes, common letter clusters) without requiring a particular tokenization scheme. Second, BLT uses cross-attention between bytes and patch latents so that the global latent transformer can operate on variable-length, contextual patch representations while still retaining the underlying byte evidence at decode time. Together these components let BLT represent and manipulate characters and short substrings directly, which is the core reason it is more robust to character-level perturbations and better at morphological phenomena.

A related mechanism that improves robustness is BLT’s patching strategy. Rather than run an expensive global transformer over every byte, BLT groups bytes into patches based on next-byte uncertainty (entropy) estimated by a small byte LM. Given the entropy estimates
\[
H(x_i) = \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} p_e\big(x_i=v\mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}\big)\log p_e\big(x_i=v\mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}\big),
\]
BLT can set boundaries where the next-byte uncertainty is relatively high (for example where $H(x_t)>\theta_g$) or where the entropy increases compared to the previous byte (for example where $H(x_t)-H(x_{t-1})>\theta_r$). Segmenting on uncertainty concentrates the model’s heavier computation on genuinely hard, information-dense bytes (often at morpheme boundaries or at starts of words) while treating easy, predictable runs (infix letters, repetitions) with longer patches. That dynamic allocation both improves efficiency and preserves fidelity around morphological boundaries that matter for tasks like grapheme-to-phoneme mapping or spelling manipulation.

# Noisy-input evaluations: AntSpeak, Drop, RandomCase, Repeat, UpperCase (prompt/completion/both)

To test robustness formally, the BLT paper evaluates models under five distinct character-level noising schemes designed to stress tokenizers and to reveal whether a model can operate when the orthographic surface is changed. The noising strategies are defined as follows in the experiments: AntSpeak converts text into uppercase, space-separated characters; Drop randomly removes 10% of the characters; RandomCase randomly uppercases 50% of characters and lowercases the other 50%; Repeat duplicates 20% of characters up to four times; UpperCase converts the entire text to uppercase. Each noise was applied in isolation to the prompt, to the completion, and to both prompt and completion; results were averaged across those placements so the evaluation measures how the model handles noisy context, noisy targets, or noise in both.

On noised benchmarks such as HellaSwag, BLT models show substantially improved robustness compared to tokenizer-based baselines. The paper reports an average advantage of about 8 percentage points for BLT over a BPE model trained on the same data mix, and even shows improvements relative to a much larger Llama 3.1 model trained on far more data. Intuitively, this robustness arises because small character-level corruptions tend to cause large, non-local changes in tokenized input: a missing letter or a casing change can change token boundaries or map previously common tokens into rare or unknown token combinations, breaking the downstream model’s learned associations. In contrast, the BLT model directly sees the altered bytes, and its local encoder (with n-gram hashing and local attention) preserves usable subword structure even after noise. The entropy-based patching additionally allows the model to adaptively devote more computation around noisy, high-uncertainty bytes, improving predictive accuracy in the regions that matter.

The evaluation protocol—applying noise to prompt/completion/both—also reveals where models are fragile. Tokenizer-based models suffer most when noise changes tokenization of the prompt because that perturbs the conditioning context; BLT degrades less in that scenario because its byte-level representations maintain continuity despite small perturbations. When noise affects the completion, BLT’s advantage remains because the decoder reconstructs bytes from patch latents and has direct access to byte-level features, which helps accurate probability assignments even for unusual or corrupted outputs.

# Orthography and manipulation: CUTE benchmark strengths and remaining gaps

The CUTE benchmark was included to probe the model’s ability to reason about and manipulate orthographic structure. CUTE is composed of three broad task categories: understanding composition (how characters compose into strings), orthographic similarity (measures of how similar two strings are), and sequence manipulation (operations such as deletion, insertion, reordering). These tasks intentionally require models to treat characters and substrings as manipulable primitives—capabilities that token-based models struggle to exhibit reliably because tokens hide internal spelling information.

BLT’s performance on CUTE is a clear demonstration of the practical benefits of byte-level training. The BLT-Entropy variant outperforms both BPE Llama 3 baselines by more than 25 points on the overall benchmark, with especially notable gains on character-manipulation tasks. The paper reports near-perfect performance on spelling tasks (99.9%), showing that BLT has learned to compose and decompose character sequences in ways that are very difficult for tokenizers to acquire without explicit supervision. Importantly, these large gains emerge even though the BLT model was trained on substantially less data than the largest tokenized baselines (the paper notes BLT achieved these gains despite being trained on roughly 1/16 the data of Llama 3.1), which highlights how byte-level supervision provides a stronger inductive bias for orthographic reasoning.

There are, however, measurable gaps where token-based models still have an edge. The paper notes that word deletion and insertion tasks are two areas where BPE models outperform BLT. The likely reason is that tokenizers provide an immediate word- or subword-level discretization that makes certain word-level edit operations very natural to reason about; a byte-level model must infer and reconstitute word boundaries from character sequences, which may be slightly more indirect for these particular operations. The gap is described as not large, and it suggests a plausible path forward: methods that more explicitly expose word boundaries to the byte model (e.g., via auxiliary supervision or targeted decoder inductive biases) could combine BLT’s character-level strengths with the fluency advantages of token-aware operations.

# Phonology: grapheme-to-phoneme few-shot performance and insights

Mapping graphemes (characters) to phonemes (pronunciation units) is a classic problem where character-level information is essential: orthographic-to-phonetic mappings are, by definition, functions of letters or letter-clusters, not of arbitrary token ids. The BLT paper evaluates grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) performance in a few-shot setting using the Phonology Bench. In those experiments BLT outperforms the Llama 3 1T tokenizer-based model under a 5-shot evaluation, demonstrating that direct byte supervision aids phonological mapping.

Two architectural features contribute to this result. First, the local encoder’s n-gram hash embeddings capture common orthographic units—multi-letter patterns such as digraphs and trigraphs—that are often the relevant unit for phonological rules. Second, because BLT maintains byte-level representations through the encode–latent–decode path, it can align byte substrings with output phoneme sequences without being forced to reverse an arbitrary tokenization. Practically, this means that few-shot prompts that exemplify a grapheme-to-phoneme mapping will generalize better when the model directly encodes graphemes, since the mapping is expressed in the same primitive alphabet at both train and test time.

The success on G2P provides a concrete instance of BLT’s broader claim: when the task fundamentally depends on sub-token structure, direct byte supervision yields both sample efficiency and better generalization than token-based approaches that must indirectly reconstruct the same structure from token embeddings.

# Low-resource machine translation: FLORES-101 across diverse families and scripts

A major motivation for byte-level modeling is improved equity across languages and scripts. Tokenizers are often designed to be efficient for the dominant languages in the training mix, which can leave long tails—low-resource languages and uncommon scripts—poorly represented. The BLT experiments evaluate translation quality on FLORES-101 across 21 lower-resource languages spanning several families and scripts, using SentencePiece BLEU as the metric.

The paper reports that BLT outperforms a Llama 3 tokenizer-based model by about 2 BLEU points when translating into English and by about 0.5 BLEU points when translating from English; on many popular language pairs the results are comparable, and on numerous lower-resource pairs BLT shows clear advantages. These improvements align with the intuition that byte-level modeling reduces arbitrary algorithmic bias introduced by vocabulary construction: every script and every byte sequence is treated uniformly as bytes, so rare scripts are not disadvantaged by being represented as awkward token sequences or as a collection of frequent tokens combined in brittle ways.

From an architectural perspective, the combination of byte-level embeddings, n-gram hashes, and entropy-based patching allows BLT to make strong local predictions even in languages with sparse token coverage, while still operating efficiently through patches in predictable runs. In low-resource pairs, where many lexical items are rare or unseen during training, having direct access to characters and character-clusters permits the model to generalize morphological patterns (e.g., affixation, nominalization) that are common across a family even if individual word forms are rare.

# Qualitative examples and error analysis

The empirical gains on benchmarks like CUTE, Phonology Bench, HellaSwag (noised), and FLORES are supported by qualitative patterns that illuminate where byte-level modeling helps and where challenges remain. Examples highlighted in the paper (Figure references) emphasize a recurring phenomenon: tokenizer-based models often fail in ways that are systematically tied to token boundaries—for instance, making entirely different predictions when a single character is removed or when casing changes cause a token split—whereas BLT tends to produce predictions that are locally sensible and degrade gracefully under noise.

Typical qualitative successes for BLT include precise spelling transformations, robust manipulation of character sequences, and accurate phoneme transduction in few-shot contexts. These successes demonstrate that BLT learned manipulable character representations that can be recombined for downstream tasks. Error modes fall into a few categories that suggest directions for future work. First, certain word-level operations (deletion and insertion) are still easier for tokenized models because tokens supply an immediate, fixed granularity for editing; BLT must infer word boundaries from characters and then perform the edit. Second, BLT’s dynamic patching can occasionally suffer from "entropy drift" in long or highly repetitive contexts: when the entropy estimator’s context grows large, repeated content becomes extremely predictable and consequently is grouped into very long patches, which can reduce the number of latent transformer steps and sometimes harm local sensitivity. The paper describes practical mitigations (resetting the entropy model context at newlines and using an approximate monotonicity constraint) that reduce that drift in evaluation, but it also highlights that entropy-based patching needs careful calibration to avoid losing local detail in structured repeats.

Finally, the experiments in the paper show a promising operational route for combining the strengths of large tokenized pretraining with byte-level modeling: initializing BLT’s global transformer from a pretrained Llama 3 model and then training the byte-specific components (encoder and decoder) with a smaller learning rate for the transferred weights. This "byte-ifying" procedure produced a BLT model that substantially outperformed both the tokenized baseline and a BLT trained from scratch under the same flop budget, suggesting that hybrid workflows—using large tokenized pretraining to seed byte-level models—can accelerate convergence and improve performance while preserving the robustness advantages of byte supervision.

# Summary

Across noise robustness, orthographic manipulation, phonological mapping, and low-resource translation, the BLT paper shows that direct byte supervision—combined with architectural choices that expose n-gram structure and allocate computation dynamically—yields measurable and qualitatively different gains from tokenization-based models. The local encoder’s n-gram hashes, the encoder/decoder cross-attention that preserves byte evidence, and entropy-driven patching are the concrete mechanisms that produce these benefits. Remaining challenges are instructive: word-level editing tasks and careful control of patching decisions (to avoid entropy drift) are natural next steps, and the positive results from initializing BLT from large tokenized models indicate an attractive path to combine large-scale token pretraining with byte-level robustness. For learners, the key takeaway is that whenever a task hinges on sub-token structure or when inputs are noisily corrupted, a byte-aware modeling approach like BLT provides both conceptual and empirical advantages.

Ablations and Design Guidelines

# Patching strategies compared: strided, space, BPE-based, and entropy patching

Patching is the single most important design lever in Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) style models because it directly controls how many steps the expensive global Transformer must run. The average patch size (bytes per patch) determines the number of latent steps and therefore the inference and training FLOPs. The ablations in the paper compare four ways to segment bytes into patches: fixed strided patching, space-based patching, BPE tokenization (treated as a form of patching with a fixed vocabulary), and entropy-based dynamic patching. Each method trades off implementation complexity, dynamic allocation of compute, and robustness across domains.

Strided patching groups every $k$ bytes into a patch. It is trivial to implement and gives exact control over average patch size, but it is static: similar byte sequences can be split differently depending on their alignment, and compute is not allocated to the parts of the sequence that are actually difficult to predict. The ablations show that static (strided) patching lags behind the other approaches in modeling performance because it cannot adapt to information density in the data.

Space patching places patch boundaries at space-like bytes (roughly: bytes that are not letters, digits, or UTF-8 continuation bytes) so that words tend to become patches. This simple heuristic often yields much larger average patch sizes than strided patching, is robust for many natural-language corpora, and is easy to implement (no entropy model required). Ablations find that space patching substantially improves over static striding; with additional architectural components (hash n-gram embeddings and cross-attention) it becomes a close competitor to entropy patching. The limitation of space patching is that it cannot vary the patch size adaptively by context and it can fail for languages or domains that do not use space-like delimiters in a way that corresponds to prediction difficulty.

BPE tokenization is not the same as patching in BLT. Tokens are chosen from a finite, precomputed vocabulary and the model never directly observes the underlying raw bytes. Two practical consequences follow: (1) tokens are not incremental in general (the same prefix can be tokenized differently depending on continuation), so tokenization violates the incremental patching requirement
\[
f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i},
\]
and (2) increasing average token size requires increasing embedding/output-table costs, which limits how flexibly a token-based system can trade off step count against parameter overhead. Use BPE if you need compatibility with existing token-based infrastructure or pretrained token models, but be aware of these trade-offs.

Entropy patching uses a small autoregressive byte-level model to estimate the next-byte entropy
\[
H(x_i) = -\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}} p_e(x_i=v\mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_e(x_i=v\mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}),
\]
and places boundaries where the entropy is high. Two simple decision rules were explored in the paper: a global threshold,
\[
H(x_t) > \theta_g,
\]
and an approximate monotonicity condition,
\[
H(x_t)-H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r,
\]
the latter flagging points where entropy increases relative to the immediate past. Entropy patching is computed as a lightweight preprocessing step during data loading and yields contextual, data-driven patch lengths. Ablations show entropy patching produces the best scaling trends overall, because it allocates the global transformer steps where they are most needed, though space patching is a strong and cheaper alternative in many natural-language settings.

Design recommendation summary for patching:
- If you want the best performance and can afford a small entropy model at preprocessing time, choose entropy patching and tune the threshold to match your target average patch size. Use the monotonicity rule when structured repetitive contexts (e.g., repeated question/answer examples) cause entropy “drift”.
- If you want a simple, low-overhead solution that gives big inference savings for typical web text, use space patching together with hash n-gram embeddings and cross-attention (see later sections).
- Avoid pure strided patching except for controlled baselines or when you need an implementation with absolutely minimal infrastructure changes.
- Use BPE only when you must interoperate with tokenizer-only systems; remember BPE is not incremental without extra delimiters.

# Entropy model size and context window: benefits and diminishing returns

Entropy patching depends on a small byte-level language model that provides next-byte entropies used to place patch boundaries. The paper experiments with entropy-model sizes from 1M up to 100M parameters and context windows from 64 up to 512 bytes. The concrete, representative entropy model used in many of the BLT runs had roughly 100M parameters, 14 layers, hidden size 512, and a sliding-window attention of 512 bytes. When the entropy model’s receptive field is small enough, the model can be embedded into an efficient lookup table, providing additional runtime savings.

The ablations show a clear positive correlation between entropy-model capacity (parameters) and context length on BLT scaling performance, but with diminishing returns beyond about 50M parameters. In other words, moving from very small entropy models up to roughly 50M produces meaningful gains in patch quality and downstream modeling, while further increases (e.g., from 50M to 100M) yield smaller incremental improvements. Larger context windows also help up to the ranges tested (512 bytes), because they let the entropy estimator see more local structure that matters for deciding boundaries.

Practical guidance:
- Use an entropy model sized to match the compute budget for preprocessing: a modest-size model (on the order of tens of millions of parameters, e.g., up to \(\sim 50\)M) captures most of the benefit while keeping preprocessing cost small. If you are willing to expend more preprocessing flops, a \(\sim 100\)M model is a sensible upper choice and was used in our large experiments.
- Use context windows of several hundred bytes (up to 512 in the paper) so the entropy estimator can detect local regularities that justify longer patches.
- When working with highly repetitive structured content (e.g., multi-choice stacks, long repeated sequences), entropy estimates can “drift” depending on the model’s wider context. Two practical mitigations that the paper used are (1) resetting the entropy-model context on certain delimiters (for example, newlines) and (2) using the approximate monotonicity constraint instead of a simple global threshold. Both reduce pathological growth of patch lengths in repetitive contexts.
- If latency/per-inference overhead is critical and your entropy receptive field is small, consider precomputing entropies or encoding the estimator as a lookup table (possible when the receptive field is sufficiently small and discrete).

# Cross-attention placement and query initialization: where it helps and when

Cross-attention is the mechanism that connects byte-level representations (the local models) to patch-level latent representations (the global model). BLT inserts cross-attention after each local encoder layer (encoder side) and before each decoder transformer layer (decoder side). The ablations isolate where cross-attention is most useful and how to initialize the cross-attention queries for the encoder.

The main empirical finding is that decoder cross-attention (the cross-attention block that maps global patch outputs back to byte representations in the decoder) is consistently the most beneficial place to include cross-attention. Decoder cross-attention significantly helps the model produce accurate byte-level outputs from the latent patch representations, and this effect shows up across domains.

For encoder cross-attention (pooling bytes into patch queries), the choice of how to initialize the queries matters. The paper evaluated three initializations: (1) the same learned embedding for every global state, (2) a hash embedding of the bytes in the patch, and (3) pooling the encoder hidden representations of the patch bytes at the given encoder layer. The ablations show only a slight encoding benefit when encoder queries are initialized by pooling the encoder hidden states (option 3). In practice this means that if you add encoder cross-attention, initialize queries by pooling the per-byte encoder features; learned constant queries or hash-initialized queries underperform pooling initialization. Cross-attention in the encoder is most useful on datasets such as Common Crawl and when patches are large; it helps the encoder compress the variable-length byte content of a patch into a stable latent representation for the global model.

Practical rules:
- Always include decoder cross-attention. It is the single most effective cross-attention placement and crucial for accurate byte reconstruction from patch latents.
- Include encoder cross-attention when you expect large patches or noisy/heterogeneous input (e.g., Common Crawl, mixed modalities). If you include it, initialize queries by pooling encoder-layer hidden states from the bytes that belong to the patch.
- Use pre-LayerNorm, a residual around cross-attention, no positional embeddings in cross-attention (as done in the BLT design), and multi-headed attention; these details are the implementation choices that accompanied the ablation results in the paper.

# Hash n-gram embedding table sizes: domain-specific gains and saturation effects

Hash n-gram embeddings are a compact way to provide the local encoder with information about recent byte sequences without blowing up memory. For each byte position \(i\) the model forms byte n-grams
\[
g_{i,n}=\{b_{i-n+1},\ldots,b_i\}
\]
for \(n \in \{3,\dots,8\}\), hashes each n-gram using a rolling polynomial hash into a fixed-size embedding table \(E_n^{hash}\), and adds the resulting embeddings to the byte embedding:
\[
e_i = x_i + \sum_{n=3}^{8} E_n^{hash}(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n})),
\]
normalized by the number of n-gram sizes plus one. This design gives the local encoder strong local-context signals with bounded parameters.

The ablations vary the hash-table size (e.g., 0, 100K, 200K, 400K) and demonstrate that n-gram hashes improve BPB across domains, with particularly large gains on Wikipedia and GitHub. In the large-scale regime, the paper used 500K hashes by default. Practical saturation effects were observed: there are diminishing returns beyond roughly 300K hashes, and at 8B scale reducing the table from 500K to 300K changed performance by only about \(0.001\) BPB after 15k steps. Thus while more hashes help, the incremental benefit becomes small past a certain point.

Guidance for choosing hash table sizes:
- Use hash n-gram embeddings by default: they are one of the most important components for bringing BLT performance close to token-model baselines.
- Target a hash table size in the few-hundred-thousand range for large-scale models (e.g., 300k–500k). If memory is constrained, 100k–200k still yields improvements compared to skipping hashes entirely.
- If your data mix is heavy on code or GitHub-like content, allocate a larger hash table because the ablations show stronger gains on those domains.
- Keep n-gram sizes in the \(3\)–\(8\) range as in the paper; these capture useful local byte motifs without becoming prohibitively expensive.

# Local encoder/decoder depth: lightweight encoder with a heavier decoder

The BLT architecture deliberately splits responsibilities between small local models and a large global latent transformer. The local encoder \(\mathcal{E}\) maps bytes to patch representations; the local decoder \(\mathcal{D}\) maps global patch outputs back to bytes. Ablations about depth show a consistent pattern: when hash n-gram embeddings are present, the encoder can be extremely lightweight (often a single transformer layer) while the decoder benefits from being heavier.

Why this asymmetry works: n-gram hash embeddings already provide the encoder with strong local context and surface priors, so the encoder’s job reduces to folding that information into patch queries for the global model. Conversely, the decoder must generate raw bytes conditioned on latent patch outputs and previously decoded bytes; because decoding is performed at byte granularity and includes the 256-way local output projection, it needs more capacity to produce precise byte sequences.

Practical recommendations:
- Start with a 1-layer encoder when you include hash n-gram embeddings; this is computationally cheap and matches the behavior in the paper’s ablations.
- Make the decoder substantially deeper than the encoder (i.e., \(l_{\mathcal{D}} \gg l_{\mathcal{E}}\)). The exact number of decoder layers depends on model scale and patch size; the paper’s experiments show that the decoder should be a nontrivial fraction of local parameters.
- If you increase patch size substantially (for example, moving to average patch size \(8\) bytes), increase encoder depth (the paper used 3 encoder layers in some ps=8 experiments) because larger patches require the encoder to compress more information into each patch representation.
- Keep cross-attention present in decoder blocks (recommended always) and consider encoder cross-attention only when encoder depth is increased or patches are large.

# Practical recipes for different budgets and domains (code, web text, multilingual)

The ablations provide a set of practical, reproducible design patterns that map model and compute budgets to architectural choices. The following recipes collect those findings into actionable starting points. These are not exhaustive prescriptions but reflect what the paper’s ablations and experiments found to work well.

Small-budget / fast-prototype (e.g., models ~400M parameters or limited preprocessing compute):
- Patching: Space patching is a good default if you need simplicity and large average patch size without running an entropy model. It gives large inference savings on web-text-like data.
- Hash n-grams: Use a modest hash-table (e.g., 100k–200k) with n-grams \(3\)–\(8\) to capture local context cheaply.
- Local models: Encoder = 1 layer; Decoder = a few layers (heavier than encoder).
- Entropy model: Skip it to save preprocessing flops; use space patching instead. If you want the dynamic behavior but are tight on compute, use a very small entropy model (a few million parameters) with a small receptive field.
- Use decoder cross-attention; use encoder cross-attention only if you see benefits empirically.

Medium-budget (e.g., models in the 1B–4B parameter range, moderate preprocessing budget):
- Patching: Use entropy patching with an entropy model in the tens of millions of parameters (around 50M is a sensible compromise) and a context window up to 512 bytes. Tune the entropy threshold to achieve an average patch size in the 4–6 byte range depending on desired inference cost.
- Hash n-grams: Use 300k–500k hashes. These table sizes give most of the gains without excessive memory costs.
- Local models: Encoder = 1–2 layers (increase encoder depth for larger patch sizes); Decoder = noticeably deeper than the encoder.
- Cross-attention: Include decoder cross-attention always; include encoder cross-attention initialized by pooling if your patches are large or your data is noisy (e.g., Common Crawl).
- Training: Equalize expected number of bytes per batch so that context length is fair across patch sizes; pack patches to avoid padding in the latent transformer.

Large-budget / high-quality (e.g., models 8B parameters and up, with ample preprocessing and training compute):
- Patching: Entropy patching with an entropy model in the \(\sim 100\)M-parameter class is appropriate if you want the best patch decisions. Use approximate monotonicity and reset the entropy context on structural delimiters (newlines) to avoid entropy drift in repetitive content.
- Patch-size strategy: Consider larger average patch sizes (6–8 bytes). Larger patch sizes can produce nearly 50% inference-FLOP savings and enable you to reallocate compute to grow the global latent transformer — the regime where patches “scale better than tokens.”
- Hash n-grams: Use 300k–500k+ hashes; observe diminishing returns beyond \(\sim 300\)k but allow larger tables for code-heavy corpora.
- Local models: Encoder = 1–3 layers depending on patch size (use 3 for ps=8 in the paper’s experiments); Decoder = substantially deeper.
- Cross-attention: Decoder cross-attention is mandatory; encoder cross-attention with pooled queries helps on large, heterogeneous corpora.
- Distillation / byte-ifying: If you have access to a large pretrained token-model (e.g., Llama 3), consider initializing the global Transformer from it and training the local encoder/decoder (a “byte-ifying” workflow). The paper found that initializing BLT’s global transformer with a pretrained Llama 3 and updating it at a lower learning rate (e.g., \(1/10\) the LR used for local modules) yields faster convergence and strong downstream performance.

Domain-specific notes:
- Code (GitHub, repositories): Code has different tokenization structure and higher local information density. Hash n-gram embeddings showed particularly large improvements on GitHub in the ablations, so allocate a larger hash table and raise encoder depth modestly. Prefer entropy patching so that high-entropy locations in code (operators, identifiers) get more compute; do not rely solely on space patching.
- Web text and natural language (Common Crawl, news): Space patching performs well and is attractive when you want simplicity and large inference savings. Entropy patching still gives the best overall robustness and scaling if you can afford the entropy model.
- Multilingual and low-resource languages: Byte modeling in general helps the long tail; the paper’s FLORES experiments show BLT has advantages on many lower-resource language pairs. Choose entropy patching or space patching with robust hash n-grams; favor somewhat larger hash tables to capture script-specific n-grams.

Additional practical considerations that emerged from the ablations:
- Threshold tuning: Choose the entropy threshold to give your target average patch size on the training mixture. For inference you can lower the threshold (more patch boundaries) to improve quality at the cost of higher inference FLOPs; the paper reports that dropping the inference-time threshold from \(0.6\) to \(0.1\) improved downstream results at the expense of more steps.
- Batch packing and context equalization: To compare architectures fairly or to maintain consistent training behavior, ensure that the expected number of bytes per batch is constant across patching choices; this typically means reducing the sequence length for larger average patch sizes. The paper used padding/truncation (e.g., to 12k/24k bytes in some runs) and packing patches in a batch to avoid wasted latent steps.
- Monitoring and debugging: If you see runaway patch lengths in structured repeated content, apply the approximate monotonicity rule or reset the entropy context on document delimiters.

Concluding note

The ablations in the BLT study reveal a consistent story: (1) dynamic, contextual patching (entropy patching) yields the best trade-off between efficiency and modeling power; (2) compact, hashed n-gram embeddings give the encoder essential local signal and enable an extremely lightweight encoder; (3) decoder cross-attention is critical for decoding accuracy, while encoder cross-attention helps mainly when patches are large or data is noisy; and (4) there are sensible midpoints — e.g., a \(\sim 50\)M entropy model and a few-hundred-thousand hash table — that capture most of the gains without excessive resource requirements. Use these findings as a practical starting point and tune the thresholds, hash sizes, and local depths to the specifics of your data mix and compute budget.

Adapting Pretrained Token Models: Byte-ifying LLaMA

# Initializing BLT’s global transformer from LLaMA 3.1 weights

A practical and effective way to transfer knowledge from a large token-based model to a tokenizer-free BLT is to reuse the pre-trained Transformer stack that encodes high-level, context-dependent information. In the BLT design this component is the latent global transformer, denoted $\mathcal{G}$, which consumes patch representations and carries most of the model capacity and compute. The paper shows that initializing $\mathcal{G}$ with the weights of a pre-trained LLaMA 3.1 Transformer provides a strong starting point: the global model already encodes world knowledge, reasoning abilities, and general sequence modeling priors that remain useful when the front-end and back-end (the byte-level encoder $\mathcal{E}$ and decoder $\mathcal{D}$) are changed to operate on raw bytes and patches.

Concretely, the transfer requires architectural compatibility at the Transformer-block level. The BLT latent transformer must match the pre-trained LLaMA Transformer in layer count $l_{\mathcal{G}}$, hidden dimension $h_{\mathcal{G}}$, attention-head layout, and the same basic block structure (attention + feed-forward + normalization). When those dimensions align, one copies the Transformer block parameters (attention projections, output projection, feed-forward weights, layer-norm/gain parameters, and any rotary/positional parameters that live inside the Transformer blocks). What is not copied are the token embedding table and the token output projection: BLT replaces a tokenizer and large vocabulary head with a byte embedding matrix for the encoder input and a 256-way output projection for bytes in the decoder, and those modules must be initialized and trained from scratch.

Two practical points that the paper emphasizes: (1) BLT’s cross-attention interface (the encoder pooling that produces patch queries) must be configured to output patch vectors of dimension $h_{\mathcal{G}}$ so the copied Transformer weights receive inputs in the same shape they expect; and (2) because LLaMA and BLT use similar Transformer primitives (SwiGLU activations, RoPE, RMSNorm in the experiments), copying weights is straightforward when those choices are matched. The experiments reported in the paper used latent Transformers that match the corresponding BPE Transformer sizes precisely, enabling direct weight initialization.

# Training protocol: lower learning rate for the global module versus local modules

After initializing the global Transformer from a pre-trained LLaMA checkpoint, the BLT training protocol treats the three submodules differently. The local encoder $\mathcal{E}$, the local decoder $\mathcal{D}$, the byte embeddings, and the hash n-gram embeddings are freshly initialized and must learn mappings between raw byte sequences and the patch space. These modules therefore require standard or aggressive learning rates so they can rapidly adapt. By contrast, the global Transformer $\mathcal{G}$ already encodes high-level knowledge and should be adapted conservatively to avoid destroying useful pre-trained structure.

The paper implements this idea by using a two-tier learning rate schedule: the local modules are trained at the usual base learning rate used for BLT runs, while the global Transformer is updated with a learning rate that is one-tenth of the local rate. In symbols, if the learning rate used for the local modules is $\eta_{\text{local}}$, the global Transformer is updated with
\[
\eta_{\text{global}} \;=\; \frac{1}{10}\,\eta_{\text{local}}.
\]
In the BLT experiments the common base learning rate for models trained from scratch is $\eta_{\text{local}} = 4\times 10^{-4}$, so the transferred global Transformer is updated with approximately $\eta_{\text{global}} = 4\times 10^{-5}$. The remaining optimizer settings match the paper’s defaults: AdamW with $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.95$, $\epsilon=10^{-8}$, weight decay $0.1$, 2000-step linear warm-up and cosine decay, and global gradient clipping at 1.0. Training proceeds for the same number of steps used for the LLaMA reference runs (the paper reports using the LLaMA 3 optimal number of steps in these experiments).

The paper also notes an alternative extreme: freezing the global Transformer entirely (zero learning rate), which avoids any gradient computation through $\mathcal{G}$ and thus can produce larger per-step FLOP savings. Both approaches—conservative updating (small $\eta_{\text{global}}$) and freezing—were discussed as viable strategies; the experiments reported use the small-but-nonzero global learning rate to allow limited adaptation while preserving the pre-trained structure.

# Empirical outcomes: gains over from-scratch BLT and gaps to the original token model

Empirically, initializing BLT’s global Transformer from LLaMA 3.1 produced clear training and final-performance benefits compared to training BLT from scratch under the same FLOP budget. The paper reports that a BLT model initialized from a LLaMA 3.1 Transformer and then trained with the reduced global learning rate "significantly outperforms both the LLaMA 3 and BLT baselines, which were trained with the same number of flops" (see Section 7 and Table~\ref{tab:distill} in the paper). In other words, by reusing $\mathcal{G}$’s weights, the BLT variant achieves higher downstream task scores given the same compute budget—this demonstrates faster convergence and a better use of training FLOPs.

A particularly instructive comparison in the paper is with BLT-Entropy, a BLT trained longer or on a larger data mix (the BLT-1T dataset). Even against that model, the BLT initialized from LLaMA 3.1 achieved superior performance on MMLU, despite BLT-Entropy having seen more raw bytes in training. This highlights how transferring high-level knowledge can be more effective than merely providing more byte data to a randomly initialized global Transformer.

At the same time, the paper is careful to place limits on these gains. When comparing the BLT derived from LLaMA 3.1 to the original LLaMA 3.1 model that itself was trained on a much larger corpus (15T tokens in the reference LLaMA 3.1 run), the BLT derived model shows a modest decline on MMLU and HumanEval and a larger decline on other tasks. That gap is not surprising: the original LLaMA 3.1 baseline benefits from orders of magnitude more pretraining data and long, fully token-based pretraining. The empirical takeaway is that byte-ifying a strong tokenizer-based Transformer is an efficient and effective way to bootstrap a BLT model and reduce training cost to get competitive performance, but it does not automatically match the performance of a much larger-data tokenized model without further tuning and possibly additional pretraining.

Why does this transfer work so well in practice? Intuitively, most of the “heavy lifting” for world knowledge, multi-step reasoning and long-range pattern abstraction is stored in the Transformer blocks: attention and feed-forward weights. By reusing these parameters, BLT avoids relearning these general capabilities and can focus its training budget on the byte-specific interface layers (encoder/decoder and the entropy/patching machinery). The practical result demonstrated in the paper is faster convergence and better task performance for a fixed FLOP budget compared to training BLT end-to-end.

# Implications for reducing training FLOPs and practical migration paths

Reusing a pre-trained LLaMA Transformer inside BLT has concrete implications for training cost, practical migration, and engineering trade-offs. The paper’s FLOP accounting (Section 4.6) gives a simple framework to reason about how much compute is concentrated in the global Transformer versus the local encoder/decoder. Let $F_G^{\text{fwd}}$ denote the global Transformer's forward flops per byte and $F_L^{\text{fwd}}$ denote the combined forward flops per byte for the local encoder and decoder. The paper follows the common approximation that the backward pass costs roughly twice the forward pass, so the full training flops per byte for a model trained end-to-end are
\[
F_{\text{train}} \;=\; 3\bigl(F_G^{\text{fwd}} + F_L^{\text{fwd}}\bigr).
\]

If we freeze the global Transformer (no backward through $\mathcal{G}$) but keep it in the forward computation, the per-byte training flops drop to
\[
F_{\text{train, frozen}} \;=\; F_G^{\text{fwd}} + 3 F_L^{\text{fwd}},
\]
because the global module contributes only its forward cost while the local modules still require forward+backward work. The fraction of training flops saved, relative to the fully trained baseline, is therefore
\[
\frac{F_{\text{train}} - F_{\text{train, frozen}}}{F_{\text{train}}}
\;=\;
\frac{2 F_G^{\text{fwd}}}{3\bigl(F_G^{\text{fwd}} + F_L^{\text{fwd}}\bigr)}.
\]
Because the global Transformer’s feed-forward layers dominate compute at large scales, $F_G^{\text{fwd}}$ can be much larger than $F_L^{\text{fwd}}$, and the saved fraction can be substantial (approaching $\tfrac{2}{3}$ in the extreme). The paper makes this point qualitatively: the Transformer feed-forward compute dominates the cost at scale, so avoiding backward passes through the global Transformer can materially reduce training FLOPs.

If instead the global Transformer is updated with a small learning rate (the approach used in the paper), one does not save per-step backward flops, because full gradients are still computed. The savings then come indirectly: because the global Transformer starts from a highly informative initialization, the number of training steps required to reach a target performance can be smaller. The empirical results in the paper show that BLT initialized from LLaMA 3.1 reached notably better performance for the same FLOP budget than BLT trained from scratch, which implies fewer total training FLOPs to reach a given accuracy curve even when global gradients are computed.

Based on the paper’s experiments and configuration choices, a practical migration recipe for “byte-ifying” a LLaMA checkpoint is:

1. Choose a LLaMA checkpoint whose Transformer size (layers, hidden width, head layout, RoPE config) matches the desired BLT global Transformer $\mathcal{G}$; instantiate BLT with a matching $\mathcal{G}$ ($l_{\mathcal{G}}, h_{\mathcal{G}}$).

2. Copy the Transformer block weights from LLaMA into BLT’s $\mathcal{G}$ (attention projections, FFN weights, layer-norm parameters, RoPE internals). Do not copy the token embedding or the token softmax head.

3. Initialize BLT’s local encoder $\mathcal{E}$, decoder $\mathcal{D}$, byte embedding matrix (size $256\times h_{\mathcal{E}}$), and hash n-gram embeddings from scratch. The paper used 500,000 hashes for n-grams of sizes 3–8 as a practical choice.

4. Configure the cross-attention pooling so that the pooled patch representation dimension matches $h_{\mathcal{G}}$, allowing the copied Transformer to accept patch inputs without shape mismatch.

5. Train using differential learning rates: set $\eta_{\text{local}}$ to the BLT default (the experiments used $4\times 10^{-4}$) and set $\eta_{\text{global}} = \eta_{\text{local}}/10$ (approximately $4\times 10^{-5}$ in the experimental setup). Keep other optimizer settings (AdamW, warm-up, cosine decay) consistent with the paper’s configuration.

6. Use the same FLOP-aware batching and patch-packing behavior as the BLT training pipeline (pack patches to avoid padding in the latent Transformer, control average bytes-per-batch to match comparisons).

7. Optionally consider freezing $\mathcal{G}$ entirely if maximal per-step FLOP savings are desired; this requires careful tuning of the encoder/decoder so they can produce and consume patch representations that the frozen $\mathcal{G}$ expects.

Caveats and practical notes from the paper: the converted BLT may still lag a LLaMA model that was trained on much more data (for example, the paper’s reference LLaMA 3.1 trained on 15T tokens), so the converted model is a highly cost-effective middle ground rather than an automatic replacement. Further improvements—better data mixes, adjusted curricula, end-to-end learning of patching, or additional light pretraining—are promising directions to close remaining gaps. The experiments in the paper demonstrate that byte-ifying a strong token-based Transformer is a practical migration path that meaningfully reduces training cost and accelerates convergence while preserving much of the pre-trained model’s capabilities.

Related Work, Limitations, and Future Directions

# Historical context: character RNNs, ByT5, CANINE, MegaByte, SpaceByte, boundary predictors and entropy-based segmentation

Understanding the evolution of byte- and character-level modeling helps clarify why the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) was needed and where it fits. Early work in neural language modeling showed that operating at the level of characters (or raw bytes) has appealing properties: it naturally handles out-of-vocabulary words, captures orthographic and morphological structure, and can be more language-agnostic than word- or subword-based systems. Recurrent neural networks and LSTMs were among the first to demonstrate this potential: character-level RNNs and LSTMs (e.g., the early sequence models) were able to generate text and model languages without an explicit token vocabulary. Researchers also showed that character-level representations can outperform word-level models on morphologically rich languages and certain classification tasks, because characters give the model fine-grained control over subword structure.

When transformers became the dominant architecture for large language models, the field largely moved toward subword tokenization (e.g., BPE) because it dramatically shortens input sequences and therefore reduces compute. Still, there were sustained efforts to bring the benefits of character/byte-level modeling into the transformer era. Several lines of work are especially relevant:

- Character-level transformer efforts tried to close the gap with token-based models. Very deep transformer stacks and auxiliary losses were explored to make character-level transformers competitive, but these approaches often remained substantially more expensive in compute and did not fully close the performance gap on large-scale data. Practical observations from early transformer-era models (e.g., GPT-2 era reports) reiterated that byte-level models were not competitive when sequence lengths grew unconstrained.

- Architectures that downsample characters before applying a large transformer sought to combine both worlds. For example, CANINE and CharFormer use local operations (convolutions or local transformers) to compress character sequences into shorter representations that a global model then consumes. These hybrid strategies reduce sequence length while keeping explicit character-level signals accessible to the model.

- Byte-level encoder–decoder models without any downsampling, such as ByT5, highlighted the benefits of byte-level input (e.g., robustness to noisy inputs) but suffered from extreme computational cost because the transformer (particularly the large feed-forward layers) was still executed at every byte position. This made scaling such models to large parameter counts or massive datasets prohibitively expensive.

- Patching-based approaches were introduced to reduce the per-byte cost. MegaByte proposed a simple static patching strategy: group fixed-size contiguous byte blocks into single patch tokens that are then processed by a Transformer. By reducing the number of expensive global transformer steps, Megabyte reduced FLOPs compared to a pure byte-level Transformer, but static patches do not adapt to variable information density in the input. SpaceByte improved on static striding by creating patches at whitespace boundaries — a linguistically motivated heuristic that better aligns flops with natural word boundaries, but it is still rule-based and cannot smoothly control patch size or allocate compute finer-grained than words.

- Dynamic patching and learned boundary predictors were also explored. Earlier work trained classifiers or boundary predictors to segment bytes into groups, and other papers investigated entropy-based segmentation as a data-driven signal for where to cut. These dynamic approaches begin to address the core inefficiency in naive byte models: not all bytes carry the same predictive burden, so a fixed per-byte application of the big Transformer is wasteful.

- Alternative architectures designed for very long contexts, such as Mamba-based designs, showed that different computation primitives can compensate for long byte sequences; MambaByte used a memory-oriented architecture to handle many bytes without patching, and this worked well at smaller scales.

Taken together, this prior work established two key lessons: (1) raw-byte modeling is attractive for robustness and long-tail coverage but is expensive if every byte is processed by the same large Transformer; (2) patching (static or dynamic), architectural hybrids (local + global), and more efficient attention primitives are necessary to make byte-level models practical. BLT builds directly on these lessons by combining dynamic, entropy-driven patching with a three-part Transformer architecture and additional byte-aware representations.

# How BLT differs: dynamic compute allocation, mixed byte–patch representation, and new scaling axis

BLT departs from prior byte-level or patching architectures in three fundamental ways: the mechanism used to create patches, how byte- and patch-level information are represented and mixed, and how these choices open a new, practical scaling axis.

First, the patching mechanism is data driven and dynamic. BLT segments a byte sequence into patches using next-byte entropy estimates produced by a small byte-level language model. Concretely, the entropy of the next byte under the entropy model distribution $p_e$ is
\[
H(x_i) = -\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_e(x_i=v\mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_e(x_i=v\mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}),
\]
and BLT places boundaries where this entropy crosses a threshold or increases relative to the previous byte (the paper experiments with both a global threshold and an approximate monotonicity constraint):
\[
H(x_t) > \theta_g
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r.
\]
Because the entropy model is causal (it conditions only on the prefix $\mathbf{x}_{<i}$), these boundaries respect the incremental requirement needed for autoregressive generation:
\[
f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i}.
\]
This is important: BPE tokenizers generally do not satisfy this incremental property because the tokenization of a prefix can depend on future bytes. Entropy patching, by relying only on prefix information, allows BLT to decide whether to invoke the larger latent Transformer at generation time without peeking into ungenerated future bytes.

Second, BLT uses a mixed byte–patch representation and a three-part Transformer design to exploit structure at both granularities. The pipeline has (1) a lightweight local encoder that operates on bytes and pools them into patch latents, (2) a large global latent Transformer that operates on those patch latents with a block-causal attention mask, and (3) a lightweight local decoder that maps latent patch outputs back into bytes. The encoder and decoder use cross-attention between byte and patch spaces: the encoder pools bytes into patch queries and attends only to bytes inside the patch, while the decoder attends from bytes to patches to reconstruct or predict raw bytes. Crucially, BLT retains fine-grained byte signals via byte embeddings augmented with hash n-gram embeddings:
\[
e_i = x_i + \sum_{n=3}^{8} E^{\text{hash}}_n(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n})),
\]
where $g_{i,n}$ is the $n$-gram ending at position $i$. These byte-level features ensure the model still has access to sub-token structure (orthography, phonology cues, short n-gram statistics) while enabling the global Transformer to operate on a much shorter patch-level sequence.

Third, BLT unlocks a new scaling axis: the trade-off between average patch size and model size under a fixed inference FLOP budget. Tokenization-based models are constrained by a tight trade-off: increasing vocabulary (and thereby average token size) reduces sequence length but inflates embedding/output projection dimensions and memory costs (the paper notes, for example, that Llama 3 increased average token size from 3.7 to 4.4 bytes at the cost of increasing its embedding table by about 4x compared to Llama 2). Because BLT's patch representations are latent vectors (not direct entries in a fixed output vocabulary), increasing the average patch size reduces the number of times the expensive global Transformer must be run without requiring a proportional increase in final-output projection cost. In practical terms, BLT can choose larger average patches to reduce inference steps and reallocate those FLOPs to grow the global Transformer, resulting in better performance for the same inference budget. The paper demonstrates this with flop-controlled scaling experiments where BLT models with larger patch sizes (e.g., average patch size 6–8 bytes) show steeper scaling trends than tokenization-based counterparts and can realize up to roughly 50% inference FLOP savings in some regimes.

Finally, there are concrete empirical benefits. BLT shows improved robustness to character-level noise, better performance on character manipulation and orthographic tasks, and improved long-tail behavior in low-resource translation — all outcomes that flow naturally from operating directly on bytes and preserving byte-level representations while economizing expensive global computation.

# Engineering limitations: library optimizations and wall-clock performance

While BLT reduces theoretical FLOPs by operating the large Transformer less frequently, engineering realities complicate the translation of FLOP savings into wall-clock speed and deployment efficiency. The paper highlights several practical limitations that a practitioner must bear in mind.

First, commonly used Transformer libraries are heavily optimized for the standard token-based use case: fixed sequence lengths, dense embedding lookups, and attention masks that are static or follow simple causal patterns. BLT introduces dynamic cross-attention masks (the encoder and decoder attend between byte and patch spans that vary per example) and variable-length patch sequences that complicate batching and kernel fusion. To manage this, the BLT implementation uses FlashAttention for standard fixed mask layers and FlexAttention for the dynamic cross-attention blocks; FlexAttention provides fused implementations tailored to dynamic mask patterns. Nevertheless, the ecosystem-level support for such dynamic patterns is less mature than for the canonical token-based stack, and that gap constrains achievable wall-clock efficiency.

Second, batching and memory management become more complex with dynamic patching. BLT trains with a strategy that packs batches of patches to avoid padding the global latent Transformer; batches are constructed to have the same number of patches to prevent wasteful padding in the expensive module. At the same time, to avoid memory spikes from unusually large patches, the implementation pads and truncates byte sequences to fixed maximum lengths (e.g., 12k or 24k bytes for different datasets). These packing and padding strategies are engineering workarounds that add implementation complexity and can complicate distributed training pipelines.

Third, the entropy-based patching mechanism itself introduces overhead. BLT relies on a small entropy model to compute next-byte entropies during preprocessing (or, in principle, online). The entropy model used in experiments is relatively small (e.g., ~100M parameters with a sliding window of 512 bytes), and in some regimes a small receptive field lets the model be implemented as an efficient lookup table. Still, this component adds to the total system complexity, and special care is required to prevent "entropy drift" when the entropy context causes long structured sequences to be over-compressed; the paper notes domain-specific heuristics (e.g., resetting entropy context at newlines) to mitigate such issues in practice.

All of this leads to an important caveat: the FLOP estimates used to compare BLT with token-based models follow standard theoretical formulas (counting multiplications/additions in attention and feed-forward layers) and are necessary for controlled comparisons, but they may not map directly to wall-clock runtime or deployment cost until lower-level library and kernel support for BLT's dynamic patterns matures. The paper flags this as a limitation and calls for further engineering optimizations.

# Scaling-law gaps for BLT and the need for BLT-specific compute-optimal ratios

A central theme of large-scale model design is the notion of compute-optimality: given a fixed compute budget, there is an (empirical) optimal ratio of parameters to training data that yields the best generalization. Classic scaling laws like Chinchilla were derived for token-based Transformers and informed subsequent choices for models such as Llama 3. In BLT, the architecture is meaningfully different in ways that plausibly change the optimal parameter–data trade-offs, so one cannot assume token-based scaling laws are strictly optimal.

Why might BLT require different scaling laws? First, BLT splits work across three modules (encoder, global latent Transformer, decoder) with differing compute characteristics. The expensive part of a typical Transformer training run is the large feed-forward layers in the global Transformer; in BLT, the global Transformer runs at patch granularity, so the effective number of heavy steps depends on average patch size. The encoder and decoder execute per byte (or per small window), but they are much lighter than the global model. Thus, changing patch size modifies the relative compute share of local versus global modules, altering how parameters and training data should be allocated for optimal returns.

Second, BLT introduces additional representational mechanisms (e.g., hash n-gram embeddings) that supply capacity in a different way than a larger global Transformer or a larger embedding matrix. These components may change the marginal utility of more training data versus more parameters.

In the paper, the authors conservatively use Llama 3's compute-optimal choices when designing many experiments to avoid confounds and to make direct comparisons easier. Their controlled studies show that BLT can match or outperform token-based models under these settings, which is a strong result. Still, the authors explicitly leave to future work the calculation of BLT-specific scaling laws. Determining BLT's true compute-optimal parameter–data ratios will require systematic experiments varying (at least) model size, average patch size, and training data quantity while holding total FLOPs constant. Such a study could reveal even more favorable regimes for BLT than the ones reported, particularly at very large scales where larger patch sizes appear to become more effective.

# End-to-end learned patchers, byte-ifying pretrained models, and multilingual equity

The paper outlines several promising avenues that the community can pursue to improve BLT further; three are especially notable: end-to-end learned patchers, conversion of existing big tokenized models into BLT-style models, and implications for multilingual equity.

End-to-end learned patchers. In the current BLT instantiation, patch boundaries are produced by a separately trained small byte-level entropy model and applied in a preprocessing step during dataloading. The paper highlights learning the patching model jointly as a clear future direction. End-to-end patchers would allow the segmentation strategy to be optimized directly for the final language-modeling objective, potentially improving both efficiency and accuracy. Designing such a system raises unique challenges: the patching decisions must be incremental (so generation can proceed causally), and they introduce discrete decisions that complicate gradient propagation. Prior work on boundary predictors and supervised segmentation suggests paths forward (e.g., differentiable boundary predictors, reinforcement learning style objectives, or relaxed/differentiable discretization), but the effective design is open. The potential payoff is large: a learned patcher could adapt to domain, script, or even document-level patterns to allocate global Transformer steps where they most improve the final objective.

Byte-ifying pretrained models. BLT offers a route to leverage existing, large, tokenization-based pretrained models rather than training BLT from scratch. The authors experiment with initializing BLT's global latent Transformer with weights from a pretrained Llama 3.1 model and then training the local encoder and decoder while updating the global model at a much smaller learning rate. This "byte-ifying" procedure yields models that converge faster and, in reported experiments, outperform baselines trained from scratch with the same FLOP budget. Conceptually this is attractive: large token-based models have already learned massive amounts of linguistic knowledge; transferring that knowledge into a tokenizer-free BLT backbone can let models combine the strengths of both pretraining paradigms. The paper shows this initial approach is promising but also notes more work is needed to fully close gaps on certain benchmarks and to develop this workflow into a robust recipe.

Multilingual equity and long-tail coverage. One of the motivations for exploring tokenizer-free models is to mitigate biases introduced by fixed token vocabularies. Tokenizers are typically optimized for frequent languages and scripts, resulting in suboptimal segmentations for low-resource languages and rare scripts — an issue the paper calls "multilingual inequity." Because BLT operates on raw bytes and is not constrained to a fixed vocabulary, it does not privilege particular subword segmentations. Empirically, BLT shows improvements on low-resource machine translation benchmarks (FLORES-101) and substantial gains on character-level benchmarks (CUTE). These results suggest that BLT can better generalize on long-tail byte sequences and reduce some forms of inequity introduced by tokenizers. The paper frames this as an encouraging finding, but it also underscores the need for broader and more systematic evaluations across languages and scripts before strong claims about equity can be made.

# Open problems: patch scheduling policies, hardware-aware training, and long-tail evaluation

BLT opens many research questions. The following are some of the most salient open problems framed by the paper’s results and limitations.

Patch scheduling policies. BLT uses entropy thresholds (global or relative) to decide when to end a patch, and the inference-time threshold can be adjusted to trade off flops for performance. But the paper’s experiments reveal that threshold choice matters: settings that work well for mixed web corpora can fail or produce "entropy drift" on highly repetitive multi-choice tasks unless the entropy context is reset or monotonicity constraints are applied. This suggests a broader design space of patch scheduling policies: per-domain thresholds, adaptive thresholds that depend on recent entropy statistics, learned controllers that trade off latency and accuracy, or policies that explicitly consider downstream tasks (e.g., prefer finer-grained patches in generation tasks requiring high precision). Developing principled, robust scheduling schemes that work across domains, that are efficient to compute at inference, and that preserve the incremental property is an open and practically important problem.

Hardware-aware training and kernels. Converting FLOP savings into wall-clock speed hinges on library and kernel support for BLT's dynamic patterns. Dynamic cross-attention masks, variable-length patches, packing/unpacking overhead, and the need to mix standard and dynamic attention primitives complicate efficient implementation on GPUs and TPUs. The paper already uses FlashAttention and FlexAttention to address parts of this problem, but further work is required: fused kernels for the encoder/decoder cross-attention patterns, efficient batched implementations for variable patch sizes, and scheduler-level techniques for balancing compute and memory across devices during distributed training. Hardware-aware algorithm design—co-design of BLT primitives and kernel implementations—can substantially influence BLT’s real-world adoption.

Systematic evaluation of long-tail and fairness metrics. BLT demonstrates concrete benefits on orthographic and low-resource translation tasks, but more systematic measurement is necessary. Open problems include defining appropriate evaluation suites that stress long-tail behavior across scripts, dialects, and noisy inputs; quantifying how BLT changes error distributions compared to tokenized models; and understanding whether BLT reduces or redistributes model biases. Relatedly, training-data mixtures and sampling strategies may interact with BLT’s segmentation choices in complex ways; designing data mixtures that amplify BLT’s strengths (or mitigate its weaknesses) remains an open empirical question.

Theoretical analysis of patch-size trade-offs. Patch size is a new knob for scaling models: increasing patch size reduces the number of global steps but increases the average information compressed per latent. Formally characterizing how performance scales with patch size, model size, and training data (i.e., deriving BLT-aware scaling laws) is an open theoretical and empirical problem. Such analyses would help practitioners pick patch sizes and model splits for desired deployment budgets.

Robust and low-overhead entropy estimation. BLT’s entropy patching depends on the quality and cost of the entropy model. The paper finds diminishing returns beyond certain entropy-model sizes but also demonstrates failure modes like entropy drift. Designing entropy estimators that are cheap, accurate, and robust across domains — or replacing them with learned but efficient patch predictors trained jointly — is an important direction.

# Summary

BLT sits at the intersection of two long-running goals in language modeling: retain the benefits of byte- or character-level representations (robustness, fine-grained orthographic awareness, equitable handling of diverse languages) while reclaiming the practical efficiency that made tokenization indispensable for large Transformer models. Historically, the community explored many related axes — character RNNs, character-aware Transformers, static and rule-based patching, dynamic boundary predictors, and alternative architectures for long contexts — each contributing important insights. BLT synthesizes these lessons into a concrete, scalable design: entropy-driven dynamic patching, light-weight byte-level encoders/decoders that preserve subword features, and a large latent Transformer that is invoked only when the data’s information density warrants it.

At the same time, BLT raises engineering and scientific challenges. The community still needs better library and kernel support for BLT’s dynamic patterns to convert theoretical FLOP savings into wall-clock benefits, and BLT-specific scaling laws to guide design choices at extreme scales. End-to-end learned patchers and workflows for converting existing large tokenized models into tokenizer-free BLTs are promising directions that could accelerate adoption. Finally, BLT’s early improvements on long-tail and character-level tasks point to meaningful potential gains in multilingual coverage and robustness — but realizing that potential will require broader evaluations and careful design of patching, training data, and deployment strategies.

For a student encountering BLT for the first time, the key takeaways are: BLT demonstrates that patches (dynamically chosen) are a viable and powerful unit of computation for byte-level LLMs; that mixing byte-level features with patch-level latent computation is an effective architectural pattern; and that while theoretical FLOP accounting shows strong advantages, translating these to practical systems is an active area of engineering and research.
