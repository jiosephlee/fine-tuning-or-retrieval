\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

### When initializing from Llama 3.1, was the global transformer frozen or fine-tuned?
Question:
Section 6.2 says you initialize the global transformer from Llama 3.1 and then 'update the weights ... using one-tenth the learning rate,' but later the text also describes 'initializing and freezing the global transformer.' Which protocol did you use for the reported results: fully frozen global transformer, low-LR fine-tuning, or a mix across phases? Any insights on stability and sample efficiency across these choices?
Answer:
Short answer: the numbers reported for "BLT from Llama 3.1" in Section 6.2 were obtained by initializing the global (latent) transformer from Llama 3.1 and fine‑tuning it at a reduced learning rate (the paper states the global transformer was updated with one‑tenth the learning rate used for the local encoder/decoder). The brief mention of “initializing and freezing the global transformer” in Limitations refers to preliminary / follow‑up experiments the authors note were tried, not the main results reported in Table~\ref{tab:distill}.

Why the low‑LR fine‑tune was used (intuition grounded in the paper): you are transferring a very large pretrained model (Llama 3.1) that was trained on token embeddings into a different input pipeline (BLT’s local encoder $\to$ patch representations). That creates an input‑distribution shift at the global transformer. Updating the global transformer at a small learning rate (paper: one‑tenth of the base LR) is a conservative way to (a) preserve the broad knowledge stored in the pretrained weights and avoid catastrophic forgetting, while (b) allowing gentle adaptation so the global stack can accommodate the new latent/patch inputs produced by the BLT encoder. The paper’s empirical claim is that this protocol is sample‑efficient: the BLT initialized from Llama 3.1 and fine‑tuned at the reduced LR “significantly outperforms both the Llama 3 and BLT baselines” for the same flop budget (Section 6.2), showing adaptation with fewer extra training FLOPs.

Practical hyperparameter context the paper gives: their standard LR is $4\times10^{-4}$, so the global transformer’s effective LR under the described protocol would be about $4\times10^{-5}$. Training used a $2k$ step warmup, cosine decay, AdamW, weight decay $0.1$, and global gradient clipping at $1.0$ — these choices help stability when fine‑tuning a large pretrained block at a low LR.

About freezing vs. fine‑tuning vs. mixed schedules: the paper only reports the low‑LR fine‑tuning numbers as the main distillation experiment. Freezing the global transformer (or freezing first, then unfreezing) is described only as an initial/possible approach in Limitations and left for future work. Conceptually, freezing is the most stable and cheap option (fewer trainable params, no risk of overwriting pretrained knowledge), and can be very sample‑efficient if the local encoder can learn to produce representations that the frozen global model accepts. However, freezing can limit final performance when the local encoder cannot perfectly match the pretrained model’s expected embedding geometry. A mixed schedule (train encoder/decoder with the global frozen to bootstrap representation mapping, then unfreeze the global transformer with a small LR) is a commonly effective compromise, but the paper does not report an explicit ablation of that schedule — so it remains a recommended experiment rather than a validated result in this work.

If you want to reproduce or extend their protocol: start by initializing the global transformer from Llama 3.1, train the BLT local encoder/decoder and the global transformer together but use a global LR scaled down by $1/10$ of the encoder/decoder LR (paper example: base LR $4\times10^{-4}$ $\to$ global LR $\approx 4\times10^{-5}$), keep the warmup/decay and gradient clipping settings, and monitor validation BPB / downstream metrics. If you need extra stability or have a tight compute/bandwidth budget, try freezing the global transformer for the early phase and then unfreezing it with a low LR — this is consistent with the paper’s discussion but is not reported there as a primary result.
