\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Chapter 3: From Individual Bytes to Patches

# What is a patch and a patching function; patch size as a compute dial
A patch is simply a contiguous group of bytes that the model treats as a single step of computation for its global (latent) Transformer. Instead of running the expensive global Transformer on every raw byte, the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) groups bytes into a shorter sequence of patches and runs the latent Transformer on those patch representations. Formally, given a byte sequence $\pmb{x} = \{x_i \mid i=1,\ldots,n\}$, a patching function $f_p$ segments $\pmb{x}$ into a sequence of $m < n$ patches $\pmb{p} = \{p_j \mid j=1,\ldots,m\}$. In the paper this is expressed by mapping each byte index $i$ to an indicator in $\{0,1\}$ where $1$ marks the start of a new patch; the result is a partitioning of the original byte stream into contiguous patches.

The average patch size (bytes per patch), approximately $\frac{n}{m}$, is the primary knob that controls how much work the global Transformer must do. Because the global Transformer is the dominant consumer of FLOPs, larger average patches mean fewer global steps and therefore lower inference and training costs; smaller patches mean more steps and higher cost. This is why the paper emphasizes patch size as a “compute dial”: by changing how many bytes are grouped into each patch (or by changing the rule that decides patch boundaries), BLT can trade computation for finer-grained modeling. Importantly, patches in BLT are not tokens from a fixed finite vocabulary; they are dynamic groups of bytes that are mapped to latent representations via the local encoder and later decoded back to bytes by the local decoder. That decouples the notion of group size from an explicit vocabulary-size cost that tokenizers face.

# Strided patching (every K bytes): simplicity and inefficiencies
Strided patching groups bytes into fixed-length blocks of $k$ bytes (every $k$ bytes starts a new patch). This is the simplest form of patching: it is trivial to implement, it gives a predictable reduction in sequence length (roughly a factor of $k$), and it makes flop accounting straightforward because the number of patches per fixed-length byte window is deterministic. MegaByte uses this approach, and it is useful as a baseline and as a way to explore trade-offs between patch size and model size.

However, fixed-stride patches have important inefficiencies. They are blind to context, so they cannot allocate more compute where the prediction problem is hard and less where it is easy. A fixed stride can waste a global Transformer step on a patch that mostly contains whitespace or highly predictable bytes, while failing to dedicate enough representational capacity to patches containing dense, high-entropy content (for example, a short formula, a number, or the start of a new word or identifier). Fixed stride also produces inconsistent segmentations of the same logical unit: the same word can be split differently depending on its alignment with the fixed grid, which hurts the model’s ability to learn reuseable substructures. These shortcomings motivate moving beyond purely static patching toward context-aware segmentation.

# Space patching: linguistic boundaries, pros and cons across domains and languages
Space patching is a simple, linguistically motivated improvement over fixed-stride patching: start a new patch after any space-like byte. The BLT paper follows the SpaceByte definition of “space-like bytes”: any byte that is not a Latin character, digit, or UTF-8 continuation byte, and with the additional requirement that each patch must contain at least one non-space-like byte. Intuitively, this makes the latent Transformer act roughly at the word level for languages that use whitespace as a word separator, so the model takes one global step per word.

This strategy brings two immediate benefits. First, it creates consistent segmentation of words across different contexts: the same surface word will be grouped the same way, which helps the model reuse learned patterns. Second, it naturally concentrates compute on where it is most useful. For example, the first few bytes after a space are typically harder to predict (they carry choice about what word begins), while the remaining bytes of the same word are often easier once the first character or two are known. By running the latent Transformer once per word, space patching implicitly allocates a global step at the point of higher uncertainty.

Space patching nonetheless has clear limits. It relies on the presence and meaning of “space-like” bytes, so it does not generalize gracefully to languages and scripts that do not use whitespace to delimit words, nor to many non-linguistic domains (dense code tokens, compressed binary formats, or creative punctuation usage). Crucially, space patching is still a rule-based segmentation with a fixed behavior — it cannot smoothly vary the average patch size to trade off compute and fidelity in the same flexible way that an entropy-driven method can.

# Entropy patching: training a small byte LM and computing next-byte entropies
Entropy patching replaces rule-based segmentation by a data-driven method that uses a small byte-level autoregressive language model to estimate how hard each next-byte prediction is. The entropy model defines a conditional distribution $p_e(x_i \mid \pmb{x}_{<i})$ over the byte vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$. The next-byte entropy at position $i$ is computed as

\[
H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_e(x_i{=}v \mid \pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_e(x_i{=}v \mid \pmb{x}_{<i}).
\]

(As presented in the paper, this formula is used directly as the measure to compare against thresholds in order to find boundaries.) Intuitively, $H(x_i)$ is high when the entropy model is uncertain about the next byte and low when the next byte is predictable from the prefix. Entropy patching then chooses patch boundaries at byte positions that the entropy model judges to be high-uncertainty points.

In practice the BLT experiments use a modestly-sized entropy model trained on the same data distribution as the full BLT model. A common configuration in the paper is an entropy transformer with about 100M parameters, 14 layers, hidden size 512, and a sliding-window attention of 512 bytes; the paper also reports experiments with different sizes and receptive fields. One practical advantage is that, when the entropy model’s receptive field is small, the computed entropies can be encoded in an efficient lookup table, making entropy computation cheap in the dataloading step. The result of entropy patching is that patches tend to capture contiguous bytes that have roughly uniform information density: difficult, high-uncertainty bytes become patch starts, and sequences of lower-uncertainty bytes are grouped together.

# Boundary selection methods: global entropy threshold versus approximate monotonicity
The paper explores two concrete rules for turning a sequence of entropies $\{H(x_i)\}$ into boundaries.

The first is a global threshold rule: declare a boundary at position $t$ whenever

\[
H(x_t) > \theta_g,
\]

where $\theta_g$ is a scalar chosen to control how often boundaries occur. This picks absolute peaks of uncertainty: any byte whose predicted entropy exceeds the threshold begins a new patch.

The second rule is an approximate monotonicity rule: declare a boundary at $t$ when the entropy increases sufficiently relative to the previous byte,

\[
H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r,
\]

with a relative threshold $\theta_r$. This can be interpreted as detecting breaks in an approximately monotonically decreasing entropy profile within a patch: typically entropy falls after the start of a new symbol as more bytes are observed, so a relative increase signals the start of a new information-bearing unit.

Each rule has different strengths. The global threshold is straightforward but needs calibrating to the global scale of entropies across a corpus and can be sensitive to long-term shifts in entropy distributions (so-called “entropy drift”). The approximate-monotonic rule is more local and can be more robust to shifts in absolute entropy values because it looks for relative changes rather than absolute magnitudes. In practice the paper finds both approaches useful and sometimes combines them (or prefers the monotonic constraint) to mitigate problems arising from long contexts or repeated content.

# Controlling average patch size via entropy thresholds
Entropy thresholds provide a natural means to set the average patch size. By choosing $\theta_g$ (or $\theta_r$) and measuring the resulting segmentation statistics over the training mix, one can target a desired mean bytes-per-patch on the pretraining corpus. This is important because the average patch size affects how many bytes each model sees per sequence and thus the effective context length seen by the global Transformer.

To keep comparisons fair across different patching schemes or average patch sizes, the BLT experiments ensure that the number of bytes in each training batch remains constant in expectation. Practically, that means that when a model uses larger average patches (fewer patches per fixed number of bytes), the sequence length in terms of bytes is decreased so the total bytes processed per batch match other configurations. For example, on the Llama 2 pretraining mix the experiments use an 8k byte context, while on the BLT-1T mixture they use a 16k byte context, always preserving the same average batch size in bytes (the paper reports using 16M bytes per batch expectation).

At inference time the entropy threshold can also be tuned to trade inference cost for performance. The paper reports that a lower threshold (more patch boundaries) can improve downstream task performance at the expense of more inference steps; for one BLT-Entropy model the authors reduced the threshold from 0.6 to 0.1 at inference to obtain better results, accepting the extra compute. Controlling the entropy threshold therefore gives an explicit and interpretable mechanism to move along the cost–accuracy frontier.

# Incremental patching constraint for generation
A critical operational constraint for patching is that segmentation must be decidable at generation time without seeing future bytes. Formally, a patching scheme $f_p$ must satisfy the incremental property:

\[
f_p(\pmb{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\pmb{x})_{<i},
\]

meaning the segmentation decisions up to position $i$ computed with only the prefix $\pmb{x}_{<i}$ must match the segmentation that would be obtained if the entire full sequence $\pmb{x}$ were available and then truncated. This property ensures that the model can decide whether to invoke the global Latent Transformer (i.e., whether the current position starts a new patch) while generating autoregressively.

Standard BPE tokenization is not incremental in this sense: the same prefix can be tokenized differently depending on how the continuation unfolds, because the tokenizer uses the full string to pick the longest matching subword tokens. (The paper notes that adding a special delimiter token to mark boundaries can make BPE incremental, but that increases sequence length.) Entropy patching, by contrast, uses an autoregressive entropy model that only depends on the prefix when computing $H(x_i)$, so boundaries determined from these entropies can be made incremental and thus compatible with generation.

# Why tokenization is not patching: fixed vocabularies versus dynamic groupings; vocabulary-size versus compute trade-offs
Tokenizers such as BPE and SentencePiece build a finite vocabulary of multi-byte tokens prior to training. A token-based model maps each input token to an embedding and operates on a fixed discrete alphabet. Patches in BLT are categorically different: they are dynamic groupings of raw bytes without a predetermined finite vocabulary. Rather than learning embeddings for every possible multi-byte token, BLT maps arbitrary byte groups into latent patch representations through a lightweight local encoder and reconstructs bytes from patch outputs through a local decoder.

This difference breaks the tight trade-off faced by tokenizers: increasing vocabulary size increases the average token length (so fewer steps) but also increases embedding-table size and the dimension of the final output projection, making the model architecture larger and more expensive in other ways. For example, the paper points out that Llama 3 increased its average token size from 3.7 to 4.4 bytes by also expanding its embedding table about fourfold compared to Llama 2. BLT avoids that coupling: patch size (compute per document) and dimensionality of latent representations are decoupled because the encoder and decoder convert arbitrary byte groups into fixed-sized latent vectors. That design allows BLT to vary patch size freely and to reallocate compute (for example, invest saved flops from running the global Transformer less often into a larger global Transformer) without paying a vocabulary-size penalty.

Another practical consequence is access to raw byte information. Token-based models do not expose raw bytes directly to the model once tokenized; BLT’s local encoder processes bytes explicitly and can therefore make use of sub-byte/character-level cues (helpful for noisy inputs, rare scripts, and orthographic tasks).

# Practical considerations: entropy drift, context resets on newlines, and lightweight preprocessing at dataload time
Entropy patching requires careful practical handling. One observed phenomenon is “entropy drift”: in highly repetitive or very long contexts the entropy model can produce lower entropies for repeated content, which in turn yields unusually large patches in structured repetitive inputs (the paper shows MMLU examples where repeated content interacts with the entropy model context). To mitigate this, BLT uses two practical techniques in large-scale runs. First, the entropy context is reset at document boundaries such as newlines during entropy computation; second, the approximate-monotonic constraint is used instead of a pure global threshold because it is less sensitive to absolute entropy drift. These changes affect only how entropies are computed for segmentation; training of the full BLT model proceeds as before.

Another practical implementation choice is that entropy-based segmentation is applied as a lightweight preprocessing step during dataloading rather than being learned online as a boundary prediction classifier. This offloads most of the segmentation cost away from the heavy global Transformer and avoids the need to backpropagate segmentation decisions. The paper contrasts this with approaches that train a classifier to predict boundaries. Moreover, when the entropy model’s receptive field is small enough, the set of entropy values for common local contexts can be pre-computed and stored in a lookup table to further accelerate preprocessing.

Finally, the variable ratio of bytes to patches produced by dynamic patching necessitates careful batch construction. For efficiency the BLT implementation packs batches of patches to avoid padding steps in the latent Transformer and ensures that every batch has the same number of patches. The training data loader also pads and truncates byte sequences to safe caps (the experiments pad/truncate sequences to 12k and 24k bytes for different datasets) to prevent memory spikes caused by unusually large patches.

# Segmentation stability across domains and scripts
Different patching strategies yield markedly different stability properties across domains and scripts. Strided patching is inherently unstable with respect to logical units: the same word or identifier can be split differently depending on its alignment with the fixed grid. Space patching is stable for whitespace-delimited languages: it consistently groups words in the same way and therefore helps models exploit recurring lexical patterns. But space patching degrades when whitespace is not a reliable word boundary (many languages and scripts) or when domain structure (e.g., code tokens, punctuation-heavy text) uses different orthographic cues.

Entropy patching delivers a middle ground: it produces context-aware and data-driven segmentations that tend to group bytes into units with roughly uniform information density. That makes segmentation more adaptive across languages and domains because it is based on statistical uncertainty rather than a fixed orthographic rule. The trade-off is that entropy-based segmentation depends on the entropy model and on the immediate prefix context, so boundaries can vary with context; this is why ensuring the incremental property and controlling entropy drift are important practical requirements. When these are addressed, entropy patching gives BLT robust, stable segmentations across many kinds of input while retaining the ability to vary patch size to meet compute or accuracy objectives.

# Summary: choosing a patching strategy
Grouping bytes into patches is the fundamental mechanism BLT uses to allocate compute where it matters. Fixed strided patching is simple and controllable but not adaptive; space patching uses a linguistically motivated rule that works well for whitespace languages but cannot adapt its patch size or handle all scripts and domains. Entropy patching is the most flexible: by training a small byte-level LM and thresholding its next-byte entropy (either absolutely or relatively), BLT can create dynamic, context-aware patches that balance information density and compute. Entropy patching requires careful engineering (threshold calibration, handling entropy drift with context resets and monotonic criteria, and packing patches efficiently during training), but it unlocks the key BLT advantages: dynamic compute allocation, improved robustness to rare and noisy byte patterns, and the ability to treat patch size as a genuine compute dial independent of a fixed vocabulary.
