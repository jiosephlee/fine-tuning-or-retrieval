\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Chapter 7: Scaling Studies: Matching and Surpassing Token-Based Models

# Parameter-matched, compute-optimal comparisons with BPE LLaMA across sizes

A central empirical question for the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) is whether a tokenizer-free, byte-level architecture can match the training and scaling behavior of modern tokenization-based models when both are trained under the same computational constraints. To answer this, BLT was evaluated in a compute-optimal regime: for each model size the experiments follow the parameter-to-data ratio identified as compute-optimal by the Llama 3 study, so that the bpe baselines and BLT variants are compared fairly for a given training FLOP budget. Models were trained on the Llama 2 dataset at multiple sizes (from roughly 1B up to 8B parameters) and performance was measured using a tokenizer-independent language-modeling metric, Bits-Per-Byte (BPB). BPB is computed as
\[
\text{BPB}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\mathbf{x})}{\ln(2)\cdot n_{\text{bytes}}}
\]
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\mathbf{x})$ is the cross-entropy loss over the byte sequence $\mathbf{x}$ and $n_{\text{bytes}}$ its length.

The compute-optimal comparison shows that BLT can match or outperform bpe-based LLaMA models across the trained sizes. In other words, when both model families receive the same FLOP budget and are sized according to the same compute-optimal guideline, BLT follows the same favorable scaling trend as token-based models instead of lagging behind. This result is notable because previous byte-level approaches often required many more FLOPs to approach token-model performance; BLT is, to the authors' knowledge, the first byte-level Transformer architecture to achieve parity in compute-optimal scaling trends up to the studied scales. The experiments also indicate that the notion of a compute-optimal parameter-to-data ratio determined for bpe transformers remains a useful guide for BLT: its models trained under those choices achieve strong results without needing a radically different training-regime prescription.

# Architectural improvements and dynamic patching versus space/strided baselines

Two orthogonal ingredients are critical to BLT’s success: (1) architectural changes that let the model mix byte-level and patch-level signals efficiently, and (2) dynamic, data-dependent patching that groups bytes where it makes sense to do so. To understand their roles, the paper compares BLT to simpler alternatives: fixed-size (strided) patches, and space-based patching (creating boundaries at space-like bytes).

Strided patching (fixed stride of $k$ bytes) is simple to implement and directly controls average patch size, but it wastes compute by assigning the same transformer step cost to predictable and unpredictable bytes alike and can split the same word inconsistently across contexts. Space patching is an improvement: it creates a latent step per word (or per space-like delimiter), so model steps tend to align with linguistic boundaries, allocating attention steps where harder predictions often occur (e.g., the first character of a new word). Empirically, space patching is a close competitor to more sophisticated schemes but it does not allow varying patch size continuously, nor does it directly target prediction uncertainty.

Entropy patching addresses these limitations by letting a small, separately trained byte-level autoregressive language model provide an estimate of the next-byte uncertainty. The entropy used to identify likely patch starts is computed (in the experiments) under the entropy model distribution $p_e$ as
\[
H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v\mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v\mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}),
\]
and two practical boundary rules are tested: a global threshold
\[
H(x_t) > \theta_g,
\]
and an approximate-monotonicity rule
\[
H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r.
\]
Using entropy to choose boundaries creates patches that concentrate model steps on bytes that are intrinsically harder to predict in-context and produce more consistent segmentations for similar substrings.

On the architectural side, BLT departs from a vanilla Transformer-only design by sandwiching a large latent global Transformer between two compact byte-level local models: a light local encoder that aggregates raw bytes into patch representations (using hash n-gram embeddings and per-patch cross-attention) and a light local decoder that expands patch outputs back into bytes. The encoder uses hash n-gram embeddings (maps of recent byte-grams through a rolling polynomial hash into a small embedding table) to enrich byte inputs with short-context statistics. Cross-attention layers in the encoder pool byte-level representations into patch queries; in the decoder the cross-attention is reversed so byte queries retrieve information from patch-level keys/values. Ablations reported in the paper show that the decoder cross-attention is particularly effective and that hash n-gram embeddings materially improve BPB on some domains; moreover, removing these architectural elements (approximating prior static-patching designs) leaves models behind current token-based state of the art. Put together, the architecture plus entropy-driven patching is what closes the gap to bpe models.

# Impact of tokenizer choice for baselines (Llama 2 vs Llama 3)

When comparing BLT to token-based baselines it is important to recognize that not all tokenizers are equal. The paper reports that models trained with the Llama 3 tokenizer (a 128K vocabulary) outperform otherwise equivalent models trained with the earlier Llama 2 tokenizer (a 32K vocabulary) on the same training data. Two concrete numbers reported in the experiments are the average token sizes: $\approx 3.7$ bytes per token for Llama 2 and $\approx 4.4$ bytes per token for Llama 3. Increasing the vocabulary and therefore average token size reduces the number of autoregressive steps a token-based model must execute for a given byte sequence, but it comes at the cost of larger embedding and output projection tables; in Llama 3 that increase was large (the embedding table size grows \textit{substantially}, in the paper expressed as a 4$\times$ embedding table increase compared to Llama 2).

BLT avoids this trade-off because it does not require a fixed finite vocabulary of token types. Patch size (the number of bytes grouped into a patch) can be changed without inflating a parameter-heavy vocabulary table, and the model maps arbitrary byte groups into latent representations through its learned encoder/decoder. This decoupling gives BLT more flexibility: it can select longer average patches to reduce inference cost without paying the large embedding-table penalty that a token-based model would incur by increasing its vocabulary.

# Larger patch sizes (6–8 bytes): inference savings, scale dependence, and convergence behavior

Average patch size is the main mechanical lever controlling how many latent Transformer steps BLT needs to process a fixed number of bytes, and therefore how many FLOPs are consumed by the global model. A key empirical finding is that increasing average patch size reduces inference cost approximately inversely: the paper states that moving to an average patch size of about $8$ bytes can yield \emph{nearly 50\%} inference FLOP savings compared to smaller patches. Concretely, larger patches mean fewer latent Transformer steps per byte of input, and since the global Transformer consumes the bulk of compute at scale, this yields substantial savings.

The experiments uncover an important scale dependence in how different patch sizes behave. With small training budgets (or at smaller model sizes), token-based baselines may outperform BLT variants with very large patches. However, as model size and/or training compute increase, BLT models with larger patch sizes can catch up and then surpass token baselines. For example, an entropy-patching BLT with average patch size $\approx 8$ bytes starts at a weaker point than bpe Llama 2 at the 1B scale but ends up outperforming bpe by about the 7B scale in the studied curves. This behavior suggests an interaction between patch size and model capacity: larger patches save inference steps but shift more of the representational burden onto the global Transformer; when the global Transformer is scaled up (which the patch savings make feasible under a fixed inference budget), the combined system can exceed the performance of smaller models that had more steps but less capacity per step.

Two practical implications follow. First, average patch size is a tunable knob that trades off inference cost against how quickly a model can learn at small scales; second, when planning to train a larger global model under a fixed inference cost, it can be advantageous to increase patch size and reallocate FLOPs to the global Transformer rather than keeping many small steps.

# Beyond compute-optimal: 8B BLT on BLT-1T and downstream task performance

The paper also evaluates BLT when trained beyond the compute-optimal ratios used earlier, by training an $8$B-parameter BLT model on the larger, higher-quality BLT-1T dataset and evaluating on a suite of downstream tasks (commonsense reasoning, world knowledge, and code generation). The chosen benchmarks include ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, PIQA, MMLU (5-shot), MBPP (3-shot), and HumanEval (0-shot). Two variants of BLT were compared to a bpe Llama 3 model trained on the same dataset: BLT-Space (space patching) and BLT-Entropy (entropy patching, with a pragmatic inference-time adjustment to the entropy threshold from $0.6$ down to $0.1$ to increase steps and improve task accuracy at the cost of more inference FLOPs).

Results are summarized qualitatively in the paper: BLT-Entropy outperforms Llama 3 on 4 out of the 7 evaluated tasks under the same training-byte budget. BLT-Space, while more inference-efficient due to an average patch size of about $6$ bytes, generally underperforms Llama 3 on those benchmarks. These outcomes illustrate a trade-off practitioners can make: BLT variants with entropy patching can achieve higher task accuracy when tuned for more steps at inference, while BLT variants that prioritize larger patches (space patching) achieve better inference efficiency but may concede some downstream performance.

# Fixed-inference-budget scaling: simultaneously increasing patch size and model size

One of BLT's most novel capabilities is to expand model capacity while holding a fixed per-inference FLOP budget by increasing patch size and using the compute saved to grow the global Transformer. The paper reports a controlled study where, for two target inference-FLOP classes (one centered on small models like $400\text{M}$ parameters and another on larger classes like $\sim 3.6\text{B}$), the authors construct flop-equivalent models using (a) the Llama 2 tokenizer baseline, (b) the Llama 3 tokenizer baseline, and (c) BLT-Entropy with average patch sizes of $\approx 6$ and $\approx 8$ bytes. For the patch-size-$8$ BLT variant they used a slightly larger local encoder (three encoder layers instead of one) to support the longer patches during encoding.

These models were then trained over a range of training-FLOP budgets. The main empirical pattern is that BLT variants show systematically better scaling trends under a fixed inference-FLOP constraint: given more training compute, BLT can trade saved inference steps (via longer patches) for larger global capacity, and this reallocation yields larger accuracy gains than what token-based models obtain for the same inference cost. Across both inference-FLOP classes, tokenized models win at very small training budgets but are quickly overtaken by BLT models once training FLOPs increase somewhat beyond the compute-optimal point. This demonstrates that practitioners who care about a strict per-request efficiency budget can get better long-run performance by training BLT models that use longer patches and larger global Transformers.

# Crossover points where BLT surpasses BPE at a fixed inference cost

The experiments quantify where on the training-FLOP axis BLT models surpass token-based baselines. In the fixed-inference experiments the paper reports that the crossover — the multiplier over the compute-optimal training budget at which BLT overtakes bpe — depends on the inference-FLOP class and the chosen patch size. For the smaller inference-FLOP class the crossover was observed at roughly $3\times$ the compute-optimal training budget, whereas for the larger inference-FLOP class this crossover moved closer, to about $2.5\times$ the compute-optimal budget. The larger patch-size ($8$-byte) BLT models exhibit steeper scaling curves and therefore overtake baselines earlier still. In practice this means that spending additional training compute (beyond strictly compute-optimal amounts) to grow a BLT global Transformer while simultaneously increasing patch size can pay off faster in settings where inference efficiency is a primary constraint.

# Share of local versus global compute as model scale grows

Understanding why larger patches become increasingly attractive at scale requires examining how different parts of BLT consume FLOPs. The paper provides the per-byte FLOP accounting for BLT as the sum of contributions from the global latent Transformer, the local encoder and decoder Transformers, and the encoder/decoder cross-attention blocks. Formally (as used in the experiments), the per-byte FLOP estimate is written as

\begin{align*}
\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}} &= \frac{\text{Transf.FL}\big(h_{\mathcal{G}}, l_{\mathcal{G}}, m = n_{\text{ctx}} / n_p, V=0\big)}{n_p}
   + \text{Transf.FL}\big(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m = w_{\mathcal{E}}, V=0\big) \\
 &\quad+ \text{Transf.FL}\big(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m = w_{\mathcal{D}}, V=256\big)
   + \text{CrossAttn.FL}\big(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=n_p, r=n_p/k\big)\times \frac{k}{n_p} \\
 &\quad+ \text{CrossAttn.FL}\big(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=k, r=k/n_p\big),
\end{align*}

where $n_{\text{ctx}}$ is the context length in bytes, $n_p$ is the average patch size, $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ and $w_{\mathcal{D}}$ are the byte-level attention window sizes in the encoder and decoder, $h_{\mathcal{G}}, h_{\mathcal{E}}, h_{\mathcal{D}}$ are the hidden dimensions, and $V$ denotes vocabulary size used only in the decoder output projection. The crucial observation from this accounting is that the global Transformer term is the one that scales down roughly inversely with $n_p$ (because the number of patch steps in a sequence is $n_{\text{ctx}}/n_p$), while the local encoder and decoder terms operate at byte granularity and do not shrink with patch size.

As models are scaled up, the global Transformer dominates both parameter count and FLOP consumption. The paper notes that when total parameters grow by a large factor (e.g., about $20\times$ from $\sim 400\text{M}$ to $8\text{B}$), the local model parameters only grow modestly (roughly doubling in the reported setups). That means the relative overhead of the local encoder/decoder becomes smaller at larger model scales, which in turn magnifies the effectiveness of increasing patch size: most saved FLOPs drop out of the dominant global term and can be reallocated to enlarge the global Transformer. This factor contributes directly to why patches "scale better than tokens": by decoupling the number of autoregressive steps from raw bytes, BLT reduces the dominant per-step cost and lets practitioners choose larger per-step capacity under a fixed inference budget.

In short, the scaling story for BLT combines three mechanisms: (1) entropy-driven patching concentrates computation where uncertainty is high, (2) learned encoder/decoder modules and cross-attention let the model translate between byte and patch granularities efficiently, and (3) patch-length is a tunable lever that reduces the dominant global FLOP term and allows reallocation of compute to yield better performance per inference cost as models grow.

# Concluding perspective

The scaling studies in the paper show that a carefully engineered byte-level architecture with dynamic patching can close the historical performance gap against tokenization-based Transformers and, under inference-FLOP constraints, can outperform them as model size and training compute increase. The intuition is straightforward: tokens are a fixed, often suboptimal, pre-processing that imposes a uniform compute-per-token cost, while BLT’s patches let the model decide, based on data uncertainty, where to spend computation and how to compress sequences for the expensive global Transformer. Empirically, BLT matches bpe scaling under compute-optimal regimes and shows superior fixed-inference-budget scaling when patch size and global capacity are co-optimized, especially at larger scales.
