\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Chapter 1: Introduction: Why Patches Scale Better Than Tokens

# Limitations of fixed-vocabulary tokenization
Modern large language models rely on a short preprocessing step called tokenization: a fixed, finite vocabulary of multi-byte tokens is learned or chosen ahead of training, and every input string is deterministically mapped to a sequence of those tokens. This design gives practical advantages (compact sequences for many languages, small embedding tables, and a fixed discrete output space), but it also imposes several structural limitations that affect performance and robustness.

First, tokenization encodes a particular compression of byte strings that is independent of the model’s prediction problem. That compression is induced by heuristics or algorithms that optimize for frequency/compression, not for how hard it is to predict the next byte under the learned model. As a result, the token boundaries and sizes chosen by a tokenizer are often misaligned with where a model should expend its compute. Tokens therefore introduce domain and modality sensitivity: a static tokenizer that is tuned on one mixture of languages or content types can under-represent rare domains or scripts, producing suboptimal compressions and hurting downstream performance in those modalities.

Second, tokenizers can be brittle to small, character-level perturbations. A tiny edit (an inserted character, casing change, or accidental symbol) can change the tokenization pattern drastically, producing a very different discrete sequence and therefore a very different pattern of compute and learned behaviour. This noise fragility means token-based models can lose performance on noisy inputs unless the tokenizer and training mixture incidentally covered those perturbations.

Third, because token-based models operate on opaque token identifiers, they do not have direct access to the underlying byte-level constituents of their inputs. This makes certain kinds of orthographic reasoning and character-manipulation tasks (spelling, grapheme-to-phoneme mapping, fine-grained string edits) harder to learn: the model must implicitly reconstruct character structure from token ids instead of observing bytes directly.

Finally, these issues combine into multilingual inequity. A fixed token vocabulary gives different effective granularities for different languages and scripts (for example, frequent substrings in high-resource languages are compressed into few tokens, while low-resource scripts may require many tokens per word). Because a model allocates the same compute per token, this uneven compression leads to unequal compute per meaningful linguistic unit across languages.

In short, tokenization fixes where and how much compute is applied, and the tokenization heuristic is not tailored to the actual predictive difficulty of different byte positions in context.

# Why historical byte-level LMs were costly
Training and running models directly on raw bytes avoids many of the above issues, but naive byte-level models face a fundamental cost problem: bytes lengthen sequences. If a word compresses to one token under a tokenizer but to many bytes, the model must process many more positions. Transformer architectures expend non-trivial work per input position, and at large scale the dominant per-position cost comes from the feed-forward (FFN) layers: these large MLPs are applied at every position and quickly dominate total FLOPs as model size grows.

Prior work reduced the attention cost of long byte sequences (for instance by using sparse or specialized attention mechanisms), which helps when attention is the bottleneck. However, when models scale and FFN layers dominate compute, attention-speedups alone are not enough: the expensive FFNs still run once per byte. This is the key computational obstacle to making byte-level models competitive at large scale.

What is needed is a way to reduce the number of positions at which the heavy global computation (the big FFNs inside the main Transformer) must run, while still retaining the ability to read and produce raw bytes. That is the motivation for grouping bytes into larger computation units when the data is predictable and keeping fine-grained steps where the data is complex.

# Core idea: dynamically allocate compute via entropy-aware patches
The central idea of the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) is to make the grouping of bytes into computation units dynamic and data-driven. Formally, a patching function $f_p$ segments a sequence of bytes $\mathbf{x} = \{x_i\}_{i=1}^n$ into a shorter sequence of patches $\mathbf{p} = \{p_j\}_{j=1}^m$ by marking certain byte positions as starts of new patches. Concretely, $f_p$ maps each byte index $i$ to $\{0,1\}$ where a value of $1$ indicates a new patch start, producing $m < n$ patches in practice. The effective computational load of the main Transformer is determined primarily by the number of such patches: fewer patches means fewer heavy Transformer steps.

BLT proposes entropy-aware patching: use a small byte-level next-byte language model to estimate uncertainty at each byte and use those entropy estimates to decide patch boundaries. If $p_e(\cdot\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{<i})$ denotes the entropy model’s predictive distribution over the byte vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ at position $i$, the next-byte uncertainty is computed as

\[
H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_e(x_i=v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_e(x_i=v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}).
\]

(Practically, BLT experiments with two simple decision rules built on these entropies: a global threshold rule $H(x_t) > \theta_g$, and an approximate-monotonicity rule $H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r$ that marks positions where entropy increases relative to the immediate past.)

The intuition is simple and powerful. Many byte predictions in natural data are easy and low-entropy (for example, the trailing letters of a common word once its initial character(s) are known). Predictive complexity is concentrated at a few “hard” bytes (e.g., the start of a new word or the first character of a new token), and those are the positions where we want the large global Transformer to apply more capacity. By starting a new patch at high-entropy positions, BLT allocates more compute to difficult decisions and groups predictable runs of bytes into longer patches that are processed as a single step by the heavy global model.

There are simpler patching alternatives. Fixed-stride patching (group every $k$ bytes) is trivial to implement but cannot adapt to context. Space-based patching (start a new patch after space-like bytes) aligns computation to word boundaries, which is often helpful, but it is a rule-based heuristic that cannot vary patch sizes by uncertainty. Entropy-aware patching is the data-driven mechanism that adapts patch size to local predictability while preserving an important practical constraint: patching must be incremental during generation. BLT requires that $f_p$ be incremental in the sense that

\[
f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i},
\]

so that patch boundaries for a prefix do not depend on unseen future bytes. Standard subword tokenization (BPE) is not an incremental patching scheme: the same prefix can be tokenized differently depending on the continuation, which makes it unsuitable for on-the-fly patch decisions in autoregressive generation without additional markers.

Entropy patching is computed as a lightweight preprocessing step during dataloading, so it is inexpensive to apply during training. Because the entropy model is small and its scope is limited, BLT can afford to compute these entropies on the training data and use them to derive context-sensitive patches without changing the main model’s parameterization.

# BLT at a glance: small local byte models plus a large global latent transformer
BLT is organized around three interacting modules that together let the model operate on bytes while running the heavy global Transformer at a much lower temporal resolution.

At the bottom are two lightweight byte-level models: a local encoder $\mathcal{E}$ and a local decoder $\mathcal{D}$. The encoder maps raw byte embeddings into a sequence of latent patch representations, one per patch. The decoder converts latent patch outputs back into byte-level predictions. Sitting between them is the latent global Transformer $\mathcal{G}$, a large autoregressive Transformer which consumes the sequence of patch representations and models long-range dependencies across patches. The global model uses a block-causal attention mask so that attention is restricted to the current and earlier patches within the document; it is this model that contains the bulk of BLT’s parameters and the majority of the FLOPs during pretraining and inference.

The local encoder is a small Transformer stack ($l_{\mathcal{E}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$) whose byte inputs are embedded with a $256 \times h_{\mathcal{E}}$ embedding matrix to get per-byte vectors $x_i$. To enrich these byte embeddings with contextual string information, BLT augments them with hashed $n$-gram embeddings. For each byte position $i$ and for $n\in\{3,4,5,6,7,8\}$, form the byte-gram

\[
g_{i,n} = \{b_{i-n+1},\ldots,b_i\},
\]

and add a learned hash embedding $E_n^{hash}(\mathrm{Hash}(g_{i,n}))$ to the byte embedding. The augmented embedding is

\[
e_i = x_i + \sum_{n=3}^{8} E_n^{hash}(\mathrm{Hash}(g_{i,n})),
\]

normalized by the number of n-gram sizes plus one. These hash $n$-gram embeddings provide a compact signal about recent byte sequences without requiring a huge discrete vocabulary.

The encoder then converts bytes into patch vectors via a sequence of alternating Transformer layers and a Perceiver-style cross-attention block. Each patch $p_j$ initializes a query by pooling the embeddings of the bytes that belong to that patch and then cross-attends only to those bytes; this produces the latent patch representation used by the global Transformer. The cross-attention update at layer $l$ can be written in the paper’s notation as

\[
P_l = P_{l-1} + W_o\left(\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V\right),
\]

with $Q_j = W_q(P_{l-1,j})$, $K_i = W_k(h_{l-1,i})$, and $V_i = W_v(h_{l-1,i})$, and where the attention mask restricts each query $Q_j$ to attend only to the bytes inside patch $p_j$.

After the local encoder produces patch representations, the global Transformer $\mathcal{G}$ processes the sequence of patches autoregressively. Because patches are typically much fewer than bytes, the global Transformer is invoked far less often than a per-byte Transformer would be, and its heavy FFN computation is amortized over more bytes. Finally, a lightweight local decoder $\mathcal{D}$ uses cross-attention (now with byte positions as queries and patch outputs as keys/values) to reconstruct the byte sequence and produce next-byte probabilities. The decoder layers follow the reverse pattern: cross-attention into patch outputs followed by byte-level Transformer layers that model short-range dependencies in the reconstructed byte sequence.

To understand the FLOP tradeoffs precisely, BLT computes per-byte FLOPs by summing the costs of the encoder Transformer, the global latent Transformer run at the patch rate, the decoder Transformer, and the cross-attention operations that map between bytes and patches. In the paper this is expressed compactly as

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}} &= \frac{\text{Transf.FL}\!\big(h_{\mathcal{G}},\, l_{\mathcal{G}},\, m=n_{\text{ctx}}/n_p,\, V=0\big)}{n_p} \\
&\quad + \text{Transf.FL}\!\big(h_{\mathcal{E}},\, l_{\mathcal{E}},\, m=w_{\mathcal{E}},\, V=0\big) \\
&\quad + \text{Transf.FL}\!\big(h_{\mathcal{D}},\, l_{\mathcal{D}},\, m=w_{\mathcal{D}},\, V=256\big) \\
&\quad + \text{CrossAttn.FL}\!\big(h_{\mathcal{E}},\, l_{\mathcal{E}},\, m=n_p,\, r=n_p/k\big)\times\frac{k}{n_p} \\
&\quad + \text{CrossAttn.FL}\!\big(h_{\mathcal{D}},\, l_{\mathcal{D}},\, m=k,\, r=k/n_p\big),
\end{aligned}
\]

where $n_{\text{ctx}}$ is the byte context length, $n_p$ is average patch size, $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ and $w_{\mathcal{D}}$ are local attention windows, $k$ is a ratio describing how local and global dimensions split/concatenate, and $V$ is the output vocabulary size (only relevant in the decoder). The important qualitative point is that the dominant, high-parameter global Transformer cost is amortized over $n_p$ bytes per step; increasing $n_p$ or selecting longer patches where the text is predictable reduces the heavy per-byte cost.

# Contributions and key results: compute-optimal parity, inference FLOP savings, robustness, and a new scaling axis
BLT makes four interlocking contributions.

First, BLT provides a tokenizer-free LLM architecture that trains end-to-end from raw bytes while matching the compute-optimal scaling trends of token-based models at large scale. The paper reports the first flop-controlled scaling study of byte-level models up to 8B parameters and 4T training bytes, showing that the BLT architecture can achieve parity in training performance with strong tokenizer-based baselines (such as Llama 3) when measured under matched training FLOPs.

Second, BLT improves inference efficiency by dynamically selecting longer patches in predictable contexts. Because the global Transformer runs less often when patches are longer on average, BLT can reduce inference FLOPs substantially: the paper reports up to roughly 50% fewer inference FLOPs for some configurations (for example, average patch sizes of about 8 bytes can lead to nearly 50% inference FLOP savings). Importantly, reducing the number of global steps can be traded for larger global model size while keeping the same inference budget, opening a new scaling axis.

Third, BLT improves robustness and long-tail generalization by directly modeling raw bytes. Empirically, BLT models are more robust to noisy inputs, show stronger character-level understanding (orthographic manipulation, grapheme-to-phoneme tasks), and better generalize on lower-resource and rare byte sequences (improvements on long-tail multilingual translation pairs). These improvements follow from the model’s direct access to byte features plus the encoder’s $n$-gram hash embeddings and cross-attention pooling, which help it learn sub-token structure that tokenizers hide.

Fourth, BLT unlocks a new practical scaling lever: patch size. Because BLT has no fixed discrete vocabulary, it can vary average patch size without the embedding-table tradeoff faced by tokenizers (in token models, larger tokens reduce sequence length but force larger embeddings and output projections). In BLT, longer average patches reduce the number of costly global steps; the saved FLOPs can be reallocated to increase the capacity of the global Transformer, yielding better models for a fixed inference FLOP budget. The paper demonstrates this by training flop-equivalent models with different patch sizes and model sizes and observing that BLT’s scaling curves (with larger patch sizes and larger global Transformers) outperform tokenization-based baselines in fixed-inference experiments.

Beyond these headline claims, BLT is designed with practical considerations that make it usable: entropy patching is computed in a lightweight dataloading step (the entropy model itself is small and can even be encoded as a lookup when its receptive field is tiny), BLT’s encoder and decoder are deliberately kept lightweight so that the dominant cost remains the global Transformer, and the architecture preserves incrementality required for autoregressive generation. The paper also provides empirical ablations tying improvements to specific ingredients (entropy patching, hash $n$-grams, cross-attention design) and releases training and inference code to reproduce the results.

In sum, replacing fixed tokenization with dynamic, entropy-aware patches lets the model allocate its most expensive computations where they are most needed. This reallocation reduces wasted computation on predictable byte runs, increases robustness by preserving byte-level information, and creates a new degree of freedom (patch size) that can be exploited to produce larger, better models under fixed inference budgets. These properties explain why, in the experiments reported, "patches scale better than tokens."
