\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Chapter 2: Background: From Tokens and Bytes to Information and Compute

# Tokens versus bytes: vocabularies, subword units, and out-of-vocabulary handling

Modern language models normally begin by converting raw text into discrete units that the model will process. Those units are most often tokens derived by a tokenizer: a deterministic pre-processing step that maps sequences of bytes into members of a fixed, finite vocabulary. Tokenizers based on subword algorithms compress common byte patterns (words, prefixes, suffixes) into single entries in that vocabulary. The effect is a shorter sequence for the Transformer to process, which reduces the number of autoregressive steps and therefore the nominal compute needed during both training and inference. This is why subword tokenization has been central to scaling up large language models.

However, that very compression also introduces inductive biases. Because tokens are chosen ahead of model training, their boundaries and sizes reflect the heuristic or statistical criteria used to build the tokenizer rather than the downstream model’s notion of where hard predictions occur. The paper documents several practical consequences of this bias: tokenization can be domain- or modality-sensitive, it can make models brittle to small input perturbations, and it can obscure orthographic and character-level structure that is important for some tasks (for example, certain multilingual or low-resource scenarios). Tokenizers also introduce a hard limit on the vocabulary: anything not covered by the trained vocabulary is represented by combinations of tokens rather than by truly atomic raw bytes.

Bytes offer an alternative atomic unit. Working at the byte level removes the need for a precomputed vocabulary: every input is a sequence over the byte alphabet and there is no out‑of‑vocabulary in the usual sense. In the BLT architecture the local encoder begins with an embedding matrix for raw bytes (the paper uses an embedding matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{256 \times h_{\mathcal{E}}}$), which is enough to represent any byte sequence. The cost of byte-level modeling is that sequences become longer than with subword tokenization; every byte must be considered unless additional compression is introduced by the model.

Patches are a middle ground introduced by BLT. A patch is a dynamically-sized group of bytes that the model treats as a single higher-level unit of computation. Unlike tokens, patches are not drawn from a finite fixed vocabulary decided before training. Instead, arbitrary groups of bytes are mapped to latent patch representations by light-weight learned encoder and decoder modules. This design keeps the coverage and robustness advantages of bytes (no fixed vocabulary, direct access to orthographic information) while recovering much of the computational benefit of having fewer high-level steps for the large, expensive parts of the model.

There is an important systems-level tradeoff tied to vocabulary size for token models: increasing the size of the tokenizer vocabulary tends to increase the average number of bytes per token, which decreases the number of Transformer steps needed, but it also increases the embedding and final output projection sizes. The paper gives a concrete illustration: moving from one tokenizer to another raised the average token size from 3.7 to 4.4 bytes while increasing the size of the embedding table by around $4\times$. Patch-based designs like BLT break this coupling: patch size can be varied independently of a fixed output vocabulary because the encoder/decoder map bytes to latent representations rather than indexing into a giant embedding table.

# BPE tokenization and its non-incremental nature

Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) and related subword algorithms are widely used to construct tokenizers. The characteristic feature of BPE-style tokenizers is that they produce a fixed, finite vocabulary before model training. Once that vocabulary is fixed, each byte sequence maps deterministically to a sequence of tokens (given full context). But there is a subtle operational property that matters once we consider autoregressive generation: tokenization is not necessarily prefix-consistent.

To make this precise, the paper introduces a useful formal notion. Let $f_p$ be a segmentation function that partitions a byte sequence $\mathbf{x} = \{x_i \mid i=1,\ldots,n\}$ into patches. We say a segmentation function is incremental (or prefix-consistent) if for every position $i$ the segmentation of the prefix is the prefix of the segmentation of the whole sequence:

\[
f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i}.
\]

In words: if you only have the bytes up to position $i-1$, applying the segmentation function to that prefix yields exactly the same patch boundaries as if you had applied the segmentation function to the entire (unknown) continuation and then truncated to the prefix.

BPE tokenization generally does not satisfy this incremental property. The reason is that the way a prefix is split into subwords can depend on the continuation; the same byte prefix can be partitioned differently depending on what follows. This non-incrementality is acceptable for many offline evaluation setups, but it becomes problematic in autoregressive generation where the model must decide on patch boundaries without seeing the future tokens that would influence the tokenizer’s segmentation. (The paper notes that one can force BPE to be incremental by inserting a special delimiter to mark boundaries, but that increases sequence length and is not necessarily desirable.)

Because of this mismatch, BLT treats tokens and patches differently: it explicitly works with segmentation schemes designed to be incremental (strided patches, whitespace-based patches, or entropy-based patches computed from a next-byte predictor), or it handles the boundary decision in a way that does not rely on future bytes.

# Incremental segmentation: why generation requires prefix-consistent boundaries

Autoregressive generation proceeds left-to-right: at each generation step the model has only the prefix that it has already produced. Any decision that controls whether additional, more expensive computation is invoked (for example, whether to emit a new patch and run the global latent transformer) must therefore be made based only on that prefix. If segmentation were allowed to look at future bytes, generation would require peeking at data that does not yet exist.

Formally, the incremental property $f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i}$ captures this constraint. When a patching scheme satisfies this property it means that the segmentation the model would adopt after observing $i-1$ bytes is exactly the same segmentation you would obtain if you had access to the entire sequence and then truncated it. A segmentation scheme that violates this property cannot be used directly in online generation, unless the generation procedure alters the segmentation logic (for example, by inserting explicit boundary tokens or by maintaining additional state).

BLT’s practical patching options are chosen with incrementality in mind. Fixed-stride patches (take every $k$ bytes) are trivially incremental because the boundary rule depends only on absolute position. Space-based patching (start a new patch at spaces or space-like bytes) is also incremental because the boundary decision depends only on bytes already observed. Entropy-based patching uses next-byte entropies from a small byte-level predictor; to be usable at generation time those entropies must be estimable from just the current prefix (for example, by running the small predictor incrementally or by relying on a sliding window of past bytes). The paper implements entropy patching in a preprocessing step during data loading for training, and for inference there are implementation choices (for instance, a small entropy predictor with sliding window attention) that allow boundary decisions to be made on the fly.

Understanding incrementality is crucial because any architecture that invokes different computational paths depending on segmentation must make those branching decisions only from the prefix. Otherwise, the model could not be used for autoregressive generation without modifying the tokenization protocol.

# Information-theoretic preliminaries: entropy and information density

A central idea behind BLT’s dynamic segmentation is that not all bytes carry the same amount of information. If two different pieces of text require very different effort to predict the next byte, it is wasteful to treat them identically in terms of how much of the model is run for each step. Information theory gives us the right formal quantity to measure this intuition: entropy.

If $p_e(\cdot \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i})$ is the probability distribution assigned by a small, trained byte-level language model (the entropy model) to the next byte $x_i$ given the prefix $\mathbf{x}_{<i}$, then the next-byte entropy is

\[
H(x_i) \;=\; -\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_e(x_i = v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_e(x_i = v \mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}).
\]

This quantity measures the expected information (uncertainty) in bits (if the logarithm is base 2) about the next byte. High entropy means the next byte is hard to predict (high information content); low entropy means it is predictable (low information content).

BLT uses these entropy estimates to segment byte sequences into patches in order to produce groups of bytes that have more uniform information density. The intuition is simple: within many natural linguistic units the uncertainty about each subsequent byte tends to fall as the unit is revealed. For example, once the first character of a word is known the remaining characters of that word are, on average, more predictable. A segmentation that keeps together predictable regions into longer patches and isolates high-uncertainty boundaries as patch starts will reduce the number of times the expensive global transformer must be invoked while concentrating computation on genuinely hard predictions.

The paper experiments with two concrete, local rules to decide where to cut patches using entropies computed by the small predictor. One is a global threshold:

\[
H(x_t) > \theta_g,
\]

which creates a patch boundary whenever the entropy exceeds some fixed threshold $\theta_g$. The second is an approximate monotonicity rule,

\[
H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r,
\]

which triggers a boundary if the entropy rises substantially relative to the previous byte, thereby identifying points that break an approximately decreasing entropy profile inside a patch. Both rules are local and can be implemented by a small predictor that only uses prefix information, making them compatible with the incremental requirement for generation. Practically, the entropy predictor used in the paper is a lightweight byte-level Transformer whose entropies are computed and then used as a preprocessing step during data loading. The entropy model’s size and context window affect the quality of patching: larger entropy models and larger receptive fields reduce noisy segmentation errors, but with diminishing returns beyond a certain size.

# Metrics: perplexity versus bits-per-byte (BPB) for tokenizer-independent evaluation

Perplexity is the standard measure of a model’s uncertainty on a tokenized sequence: it is a monotone transformation of the average cross-entropy per token under the model. But perplexity is defined relative to a particular tokenizer or token vocabulary: changing the tokenizer changes what is considered a token and therefore changes perplexity in ways that are not comparable across tokenizers.

When we want a tokenizer-independent metric that allows direct comparison between byte-level models and token-based models, the paper uses Bits-Per-Byte (BPB). BPB is simply the average cross-entropy per byte, converted to bits. If $\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\mathbf{x})$ denotes the sum of cross-entropy losses of a model on the byte sequence $\mathbf{x}$, and $n_{\text{bytes}}$ denotes the number of bytes in $\mathbf{x}$, then

\[
\text{BPB}(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\mathbf{x})}{\ln(2)\cdot n_{\text{bytes}}}.
\]

The division by $\ln(2)$ converts natural-log cross-entropy into bits (if the cross-entropy is expressed in nats). BPB has the convenient operational interpretation of the average number of bits the model needs to encode each byte of the given data under its learned distribution. Lower BPB indicates a stronger byte-level model. By using BPB, the paper can compare tokenizers and byte-based approaches on a common footing: instead of asking how uncertain the model is per token (a measure that depends on the tokenizer), BPB asks how uncertain the model is on the underlying atomic bytes.

# Transformer compute anatomy: attention versus feed-forward costs; block-causal and local attention

To reason about efficiency — which is central to BLT’s design — we must understand how computation is distributed inside a Transformer and how that computation scales with sequence length and model size.

A Transformer layer contains two dominant parts: the self-attention mechanism and the feed-forward network (FFN) that follows it. Self-attention computes query, key, and value (QKV) projections and then forms pairwise attention weights; its cost scales roughly with the square of the length of the sequence in the naive dense form, but in many practical settings attention can be made efficient (sparse, local, or fused) or its cost can be masked by architectural choices. The FFN, by contrast, applies large dense multiplications at each position independently, and its cost scales linearly with sequence length times the width of the model.

The paper emphasizes that at large model scales the dominant compute cost comes from FFNs rather than attention: FFN layers are large and are applied at every position (byte, token, or patch), so reducing the number of positions on which those FFNs run yields large savings. That observation motivates BLT’s central design decision: reduce the number of expensive global Transformer steps by grouping bytes into patches and running the big global model on patches instead of on individual bytes.

BLT’s total estimated FLOPs per byte is computed by summing contributions from the local encoder, the global latent transformer, the local decoder, and the cross-attention blocks that connect byte-level and patch-level representations. The paper writes an operational formula for the forward/backward FLOPs per byte for BLT (here reproduced symbolically):

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}} &= \frac{\text{Transf.\ FL}(h_{\mathcal{G}}, l_{\mathcal{G}}, m = n_{\text{ctx}}/n_p, V=0)}{n_p} \\
&\quad + \text{Transf.\ FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m = w_{\mathcal{E}}, V=0) \\
&\quad + \text{Transf.\ FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m = w_{\mathcal{D}}, V=256) \\
&\quad + \text{CrossAttn.\ FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m = n_p, r = n_p/k) \times \frac{k}{n_p} \\
&\quad + \text{CrossAttn.\ FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m = k, r = k/n_p).
\end{aligned}
\]

Each term corresponds to an architectural component: the first line is the global latent Transformer’s cost (with hidden size $h_{\mathcal{G}}$ and $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers) amortized per byte by dividing by the average patch size $n_p$; the second and third lines are the local encoder and decoder Transformer costs (their sequence lengths are local window sizes $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ and $w_{\mathcal{D}}$, respectively); the final two lines estimate the cost of cross-attention in encoder and decoder, where $r$ denotes the ratio of queries to key/value entries and $k$ denotes how many local splits are concatenated to form a global representation. The decoder’s output projection has vocabulary size $V$ (256 for byte output) and is therefore included only in the decoder term.

Two attention masking patterns that BLT uses deserve special mention. The global latent transformer uses a block-causal attention mask: attention is permitted up to and including the current patch within the current document, but not beyond. This enforces autoregressiveness at the patch level. The local encoder uses a fixed, local block-causal attention window: each byte attends to a fixed number of preceding bytes $w_{\mathcal{E}}$, which may cross the dynamic patch boundaries but does not cross document boundaries. These locality constraints give practical control over attention costs and make it possible to fuse or optimize attention computations in libraries (FlashAttention, FlexAttention in the paper’s implementation).

Putting these elements together, reducing the number of patches $n_p$ — that is, increasing the average patch size — has a direct and substantial effect on the flops expended by the global Transformer (the expensive part). This is the lever BLT uses to trade off inference cost and model capacity: larger patches mean fewer expensive global steps, freeing flops that can be reinvested into wider or deeper global Transformer layers.

# Scaling laws and compute-optimal training regimes (Chinchilla perspective)

Scaling laws relate model performance to compute, number of parameters, and the amount of training data. A principled approach to allocating a fixed compute budget is to choose model size and training data so as to be as close as possible to the compute-optimal regime: the combination of parameters and data for which, given total FLOPs, the model attains the best expected performance. The Chinchilla line of work (which the paper follows in spirit) made this idea operational for subword-tokenized Transformers, and Llama 3 used those estimates to choose training regimes for the baselines in this work.

The BLT paper evaluates models in two complementary regimes. First, it trains BLT and BPE baselines in a parameter-matched, compute-optimal setting determined by prior scaling recommendations and compares the bits-per-byte achieved as a function of training FLOPs. In these matched settings BLT models are able to match or outperform tokenized baselines at scale, suggesting that the compute-optimal parameter/data tradeoffs found for BPE Transformers transfer reasonably well to BLT architectures.

Second, the paper explores a new dimension of scaling that BLT unlocks in practice: inference-flop constrained scaling. Because patch size reduces the number of expensive global steps at inference time, BLT allows you to keep the same inference FLOP budget while making the global model larger and increasing the patch size. Concretely, longer patches reduce the global Transformer’s per-byte cost so that saved compute can be reinvested in increased model capacity. The paper presents inference-FLOP controlled scaling experiments showing that, for fixed inference cost, BLT models with larger patch sizes and larger global models scale better than token-based models that cannot change token size without incurring embedding/output costs.

A final practical point made in the paper is a caution: the compute-optimal ratios used in these experiments were taken from analyses done for BPE-tokenized Transformers. Those scaling laws may not be exactly optimal for BLT; deriving BLT-specific scaling laws is left as future work and could conceivably shift the best choices for model size, patch size, and data volume.
