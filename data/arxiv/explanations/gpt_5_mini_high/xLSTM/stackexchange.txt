\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### What exactly does the sLSTM normalizer state do, and why divide by n_t?
Question:
In the sLSTM, the forward equations are given as
- cell state: $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$
- normalizer: $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t$
- hidden: $h_t = o_t \cdot (c_t / n_t)$.
I understand this is intended to enable revision via exponential gates, but I'm confused about the precise role of $n_t$.
1) Is $c_t / n_t$ literally a weighted average of past $z_s$ with weights proportional to $i_s \prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u$? Could you show the explicit unrolled form that makes the averaging interpretation clear?
2) Under exponential gating, $i_t>0$ and (optionally) $f_t>0$ and possibly $f_t>1$. How is $n_t$ guaranteed to be nonzero and positive so that division is well-defined? What conditions are required?
3) Conceptually, how does dividing by $n_t$ differ from, say, applying a squashing nonlinearity like $\tanh$ on $c_t$ as in classic LSTM? What stability advantages does the normalization provide for gradient flow compared to $\tanh$?
Answer:
Short answer: the normalizer $n_t$ is exactly the accumulated “retained mass’’ of past writes; it records how much of past inputs survives to time $t$. Dividing $c_t$ by $n_t$ turns the cell readout into an attention‑style weighted average of past inputs (weights = input strength $\times$ future retention). This keeps the readout scale-stable when you allow exponential (possibly $>1$) gates and, importantly, removes the $\tanh$ saturation factor from the main path of gradient flow, which substantially improves stability for learning and for “revision’’ via multiplicative gates.

1) Unrolled form / averaging interpretation. Start from
$$
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t,\qquad
n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t,
$$
and take $c_0,n_0$ arbitrary (often $=0$). Unrolling by induction gives, with the convention $\prod_{u=t+1}^t f_u=1$,
$$
c_t \;=\; \Big(\prod_{u=1}^t f_u\Big)c_0 \;+\; \sum_{s=1}^t \Big(i_s\,z_s\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u\Big),
$$
$$
n_t \;=\; \Big(\prod_{u=1}^t f_u\Big)n_0 \;+\; \sum_{s=1}^t \Big(i_s\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u\Big).
$$
Define the weights
$$
w_{s,t} := i_s\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u .
$$
Then
$$
\frac{c_t}{n_t}
=\frac{\Big(\prod_{u=1}^t f_u\Big)c_0 + \sum_{s=1}^t w_{s,t} z_s}
{\Big(\prod_{u=1}^t f_u\Big)n_0 + \sum_{s=1}^t w_{s,t}}.
$$
In the common initialization $c_0=n_0=0$ this reduces to
$$
\frac{c_t}{n_t} \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^t \frac{w_{s,t}}{\sum_{r=1}^t w_{r,t}}\, z_s,
$$
so $c_t/n_t$ is literally the weighted average of past $z_s$ with weights proportional to $w_{s,t}=i_s\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u$. Intuitively $i_s$ is how strongly you wrote $z_s$ at time $s$, and the product of future $f_u$ says how much of that write is retained until time $t$.

2) Why $n_t>0$ (division well-defined) and numerical safety. In the paper sLSTM sets $i_t=\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ (strictly $>0$) and uses either $f_t=\sigma(\tilde f_t)\in(0,1)$ or $f_t=\exp(\tilde f_t)>0$. If every $i_t>0$ and every $f_t>0$ (and $n_0\ge0$), then every term $w_{s,t}=i_s\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u$ is strictly positive, hence $n_t$ (a sum of positive terms plus a nonnegative initial term) is strictly positive for every $t$. Thus, under the designed exponential/sigmoid gates, division is mathematically safe. If one used gates that could be exactly zero then $n_t$ might be zero in degenerate cases, but the paper’s gate choices avoid that.

Numerically, exponentials can overflow/underflow; the paper introduces the stabilizer state $m_t$ and replaces $i_t,f_t$ by scaled versions $i'_t,f'_t$ (Equations (5)–(7)) which preserve the effective ratios while keeping magnitudes in range. For the vector/matrix mLSTM the paper additionally lower-bounds the retrieval denominator $\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,1\}$ to avoid tiny divisions. In practice it is common to also add a tiny epsilon to scalar denominators for numerical robustness — the paper’s stabilizer + the mLSTM threshold are the principled mechanisms they describe.

3) Conceptual and gradient-flow differences vs. $\tanh$.

Conceptually, $\psi=\tanh$ squashes the raw cell $c_t$ elementwise and introduces a saturating nonlinearity: for large $|c_t|$ the slope $\psi'(c_t)=1-\tanh^2(c_t)$ becomes tiny, which kills gradients. By contrast, dividing by $n_t$ performs normalization (an attention‑style weighted average) rather than saturation. The readout is a relative (scale‑normalized) mixture of past inputs instead of a nonlinear squashed scalar of the accumulated sum.

This difference shows up sharply in the backpropagation factors. For sLSTM with $h_t=o_t(c_t/n_t)$ one finds
$$
\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial c_{t-1}} \;=\; o_t\cdot\frac{\partial (c_t/n_t)}{\partial c_{t-1}}
\;=\; o_t\cdot\frac{f_t}{n_t}.
$$
For a classic LSTM with $h_t=o_t\psi(c_t)$ and $\psi=\tanh$,
$$
\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial c_{t-1}} \;=\; o_t\cdot\psi'(c_t)\cdot f_t
\;=\; o_t\cdot\big(1-\tanh^2(c_t)\big)\cdot f_t,
$$
and $\psi'(c_t)$ can be arbitrarily small when $c_t$ is large. Thus sLSTM replaces the potentially vanishing factor $\psi'(c_t)$ by $1/n_t$. Because $n_t$ is tracking the same gate-mass that created $c_t$, gradients are controlled by gate mass rather than the absolute, possibly-saturated magnitude of $c_t$. Moreover the derivative of $h_t$ with respect to a past write $z_s$ is simple and interpretable:
$$
\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial z_s} \;=\; o_t\cdot\frac{w_{s,t}}{n_t}
\;=\; o_t\cdot \text{(normalized weight of }z_s\text{)},
$$
so gradients flow back to past writes in proportion to their normalized contribution. There is no extra small factor from a squashing nonlinearity, which helps keep useful signal for credit assignment across long ranges.

Intuitively, dividing by $n_t$ decouples “how much total mass is stored’’ from “what the stored content is’’: gates can grow or shrink multiplicatively (allowing strong revision behavior) but the readout stays normalized and bounded. This preserves a form of the constant-error-carousel (additive memory update) while avoiding the gradient-killing saturation of $\tanh$. The stabilization trick in the paper makes this practical even when gates are exponential or when $f_t>1$ is used for revision dynamics.

Taken together: $n_t$ is the bookkeeping variable that makes the sLSTM readout a normalized, attention‑like average of past inputs; its positivity is ensured by the paper’s gate choices and numerical stabilizers; and normalization replaces a saturating squash with a scale‑aware, non‑saturating readout that gives more robust gradients for long‑range credit assignment and for the revision behavior enabled by exponential gating.

### How does the stabilizer m_t make the exponential gates numerically safe without changing outputs or gradients?
Question:
The paper defines a stabilizer state $m_t$ and stabilized gates via
- $m_t = \max(\log f_t + m_{t-1}, \log i_t)$
- $i'_t = \exp(\log i_t - m_t)$, $f'_t = \exp(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t)$,
and then claims that replacing $i_t,f_t$ by $i'_t,f'_t$ does not change the network's outputs nor the gradients of the loss w.r.t. parameters.
Could someone provide a step-by-step derivation that shows:
1) why $c_t$ and $n_t$ computed with $(i'_t,f'_t)$ are exactly proportional to those computed with $(i_t,f_t)$ in such a way that $h_t = o_t (c_t/n_t)$ remains identical, and
2) why the backward pass is also invariant, even though $m_t$ depends on parameters through $\max(\cdot,\cdot)$ (which is nondifferentiable at ties)?
Intuitively this looks like a log-sum-exp style rescaling, but I don't see immediately why the gradients are unaffected.
Answer:
Short answer: the stabilizer is exactly the same algebraic trick as “subtracting the max” in a softmax/log-sum-exp implementation. If you write the cell as a weighted sum of past inputs, the stabilizer divides every weight feeding the current output by the same positive scalar $p_t = \exp(m_t)$ (so the normalized combination is unchanged). For gradients the key identity is that the Jacobian of a softmax (the map from log-weights to normalized weights) annihilates the all-ones vector, so any contribution coming from the dependence of $m_t$ on parameters cancels. Ties (nondifferentiability of max) are handled by subgradients; any valid subgradient of the max sums to $1$, so the same cancellation holds (ties are measure zero in practice).

Below is a short step-by-step derivation.

1) Forward invariance (outputs unchanged)

Define
$p_t := \exp(m_t)$. From the paper’s definition
$m_t = \max(\log f_t + m_{t-1}, \log i_t)$
we get (by exponentiating)
$p_t = \max(f_t p_{t-1}, i_t)$.

The stabilized gates are
$i'_t = \exp(\log i_t - m_t) = \frac{i_t}{p_t}$,
$f'_t = \exp(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t) = \frac{f_t p_{t-1}}{p_t}$.

Write the usual expansion of the cell/normalizer as a weighted sum of past contributions (this identity follows by unrolling the recurrence):
$w_{s,t} := i_s \prod_{u=s+1}^{t} f_u$,  for $s \le t$,
so
$c_t = \sum_{s\le t} w_{s,t} z_s$,   $n_t = \sum_{s\le t} w_{s,t}$.

Observe that $p_t = \max_s w_{s,t}$ (this is exactly what the recursive definition gives: $p_t = \max(i_t, f_t p_{t-1})$ and inductively equals the maximal $w_{s,t}$). If we form the same weights using stabilized gates we get
$w'_{s,t} = i'_s \prod_{u=s+1}^{t} f'_u = \frac{w_{s,t}}{p_t}$,
because each $i_s$ and each $f_u$ is divided by $p_{(\cdot)}$ in a telescoping way that results in an overall division by $p_t$ for every term contributing to time $t$. Therefore
$c'_t = \sum_{s\le t} w'_{s,t} z_s = \frac{1}{p_t}\sum_{s\le t} w_{s,t} z_s = \frac{c_t}{p_t}$,
$n'_t = \frac{n_t}{p_t}$.

Thus the ratio used for the hidden output is unchanged:
$\tilde h_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t} = \frac{c'_t}{n'_t}$,
so $h_t = o_t \tilde h_t$ is identical whether you use $(i,f)$ or $(i',f')$.

(Induction proof of $c'_t = \frac{c_t}{p_t}$ is immediate from the one-step recurrence once you note $i'_t = \frac{i_t}{p_t}$ and $f'_t = \frac{f_t p_{t-1}}{p_t}$.)

2) Backward invariance (gradients unchanged)

It is convenient to work with the log-weights
$u_{s,t} := \log w_{s,t} = \log i_s + \sum_{u=s+1}^{t} \log f_u$.
The normalized weights appearing in the output are
$\alpha_{s,t} := \frac{\exp(u_{s,t})}{\sum_{r\le t}\exp(u_{r,t})}$,
so the output at time $t$ is a linear function of the vector $\alpha_{\cdot,t}$. Stabilization replaces $u$ by
$u'_{s,t} := u_{s,t} - m_t$,
but $\alpha$ depends only on differences of the u’s, so $\alpha(u) = \alpha(u')$ algebraically.

To see why gradients are unchanged even though $m_t$ depends on parameters, compute derivatives via the chain rule. Let $J$ denote the Jacobian of the softmax map with respect to its input vector $u'$ (the usual softmax Jacobian). Its $(i,j)$ entry is
$J_{ij} = \alpha_i (\delta_{ij} - \alpha_j)$,
and a crucial property is $J \mathbf{1} = 0$ (each column of $J$ sums to zero), equivalently the softmax is invariant to adding the same scalar to every component.

Now $u' = u - m_t \mathbf{1}$, so $\frac{\partial u'}{\partial u} = I - \mathbf{1} v^T$ where $v := \frac{\partial m_t}{\partial u}$ is the gradient (or a subgradient) of the scalar max w.r.t. the components of $u$. Note that for the max operator $v$ is a vector with nonnegative entries that sum to $1$ (if there is a unique maximizer $v$ is the one-hot vector at that maximizer; at ties $v$ is any probability vector supported on argmax indices). By chain rule,
$\frac{\partial \alpha}{\partial u} = J\frac{\partial u'}{\partial u} = J(I - \mathbf{1} v^T) = J - J(\mathbf{1} v^T)$.
But $J \mathbf{1} = 0$, so $J(\mathbf{1} v^T) = (J \mathbf{1}) v^T = 0$ and therefore
$\frac{\partial \alpha}{\partial u} = J$.
In words: although $u'$ depends on $u$ through $m_t$, the extra term involving $\frac{\partial m_t}{\partial u}$ is annihilated by the softmax Jacobian. Hence the derivative of the normalized weights $\alpha$ with respect to the underlying log-weights $u$ is the same whether you write $\alpha$ in terms of $u$ or in terms of $u' = u - m_t \mathbf{1}$.

Finally $u$ depends linearly on the gate log-values (log $i$ and log $f$), and those log-values depend on the pre-activations $\tilde i,\tilde f$ (for exponential gates $\log i_t = \tilde i_t$, for sigmoid forget gates you can still use $\log f_t$ but the same linear relations from $\log f$ to $\tilde f$ hold via the chain rule). By composing the equal Jacobians we get identical gradients $\frac{dL}{d\tilde i_t}$ and $\frac{dL}{d\tilde f_t}$ in the stabilized and unstabilized computations.

About nondifferentiability of max: the argument above used an arbitrary subgradient $v = \frac{\partial m_t}{\partial u}$. For the max function any subgradient $v$ satisfies $\sum_j v_j = 1$, so $J \mathbf{1} = 0$ still makes $J(\mathbf{1} v^T)=0$. Thus the cancellation works for any valid subgradient. Ties are a measure-zero event in continuous parameter spaces, and using subgradients at ties is standard; the gradient invariance statement therefore holds almost everywhere (and with any selection of a valid subgradient at ties).

Intuition / final remark

This is exactly the same phenomenon that lets us subtract max(logits) before exponentiating in a numerically stable softmax implementation: subtracting a (data-dependent) scalar from every log-weight does not change the normalized probabilities, and because the softmax Jacobian annihilates the all-ones direction, the dependence of that scalar on parameters does not alter gradients. The xLSTM stabilizer keeps the per-time-step maximal path weight $p_t$ under control (so exponentials never overflow), while algebraically only dividing all path-weights by the same factor for each $t$ — hence both forward values and backward gradients are unaffected.

### Why do exponential gates give better "revision" ability than sigmoid gates in sLSTM?
Question:
The paper argues that exponential gating allows sLSTM to revise stored information (e.g., in the nearest-neighbor search toy task) more effectively than classic sigmoid gates. In the sLSTM we have $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$ with $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ and $f_t$ either sigmoid or exponential, plus normalization by $n_t$.
I'm struggling to see the specific mechanism by which exponentials help revision relative to sigmoids. Even with sigmoids, if a later $i_t$ is high and the product of future $f_u$ terms is moderate, later inputs should be able to dominate. Is the key that exponentials allow a much larger dynamic range of effective weights $i_s \prod_{u>s} f_u$ so a later input can more decisively overwrite earlier content? Or is the normalization by $n_t$ the main factor?
Could someone provide a simple two-step example showing why a later, more relevant $z_t$ can "replace" an earlier one more reliably with exponential gates than with sigmoids?
Answer:
Short version: exponential gates turn the time‑weights that determine how much each past $z_s$ contributes into softmax‑style logits, so a later input can win by having a much larger logit instead of forcing earlier contributions to be driven to zero by tiny forgets. The $n_t$ normalizer is important (it makes $h_t=c_t/n_t$ a normalized mixture of past $z_s$) but it is the exponential nonlinearity that gives the much larger dynamic range and the additive log‑structure that makes decisive “replacement” easy.

Why (algebra + intuition). Expand the sLSTM recurrence once and you get, for two steps with $c_0=n_0=0$,
$$
c_1=i_1 z_1,\qquad n_1=i_1,
$$
$$
c_2=f_2 c_1 + i_2 z_2 = f_2 i_1 z_1 + i_2 z_2,
\qquad
n_2=f_2 n_1 + i_2 = f_2 i_1 + i_2.
$$
So the post‑normalized memory (what the layer actually uses) is
$$
\tilde h_2=\frac{c_2}{n_2} = \frac{f_2 i_1}{f_2 i_1 + i_2} z_1 \;+\; \frac{i_2}{f_2 i_1 + i_2} z_2
      = w_1 z_1 + w_2 z_2,
$$
with effective weights $w_1,w_2$ that sum to $1$. Thus “replacement” of $z_1$ by $z_2$ is exactly the condition $w_2\approx 1$, i.e. $i_2 \gg f_2 i_1$.

Now compare two gate choices.

1) Exponential gates. Take $i_s=\exp(a_s)$ and $f_u=\exp(b_u)$. Then the unnormalized contribution of $z_s$ is
$$
\exp\!\Big(a_s + \sum_{u>s} b_u\Big),
$$
so the normalized weights are exactly a softmax over the additive logits $a_s+\sum_{u>s} b_u$. That representation has an unbounded positive scale: by making $a_2$ large you can make $w_2$ arbitrarily close to $1$ without touching $f_2$ or $i_1$. In other words, you can make the later input dominate by increasing its own logit.

2) Sigmoid gates. With $i_s=\sigma(\alpha_s)\in(0,1)$ and $f_u=\sigma(\phi_u)\in(0,1)$ the same algebra holds, but now the logs $\log i_s$ and $\log f_u$ are bounded above by $0$ (because $\sigma(\cdot)\le 1$). To make $w_2\to1$ you must make $f_2 i_1$ very small (e.g. by pushing $f_2$ or $i_1$ toward $0$) or make $i_2$ approach $1$. Pushing $f_2$ very small has the side effect of erasing earlier content (and can hurt gradients / other uses of that memory), and $i_2$ can only approach $1$ (logarithmically capped), so it cannot by itself create arbitrarily large dominance the way an exponential $i_2=\exp(a_2)$ can.

A concrete numeric two‑step illustration. Let $z_1\neq z_2$ and compare plausible gate values.

- Exponential gates: set $a_1=0\Rightarrow i_1=1$, $b_2=0\Rightarrow f_2=1$, and $a_2=5\Rightarrow i_2\approx 148.4$. Then
  $$
  w_2 = \frac{i_2}{f_2 i_1 + i_2} \approx \frac{148.4}{1+148.4}\approx 0.993,
  $$
  so $z_2$ overwhelmingly replaces $z_1$ while $f_2$ stayed at $1$ (we did not have to “erase” the old content via a tiny forget).

- Sigmoid gates: set $\alpha_1=0\Rightarrow i_1=0.5$, $\phi_2=0\Rightarrow f_2=0.5$. Even if $\alpha_2=5\Rightarrow i_2\approx0.993$, we get
  $$
  w_2=\frac{0.993}{0.993 + 0.5\cdot 0.5} \approx \frac{0.993}{1.243}\approx 0.799,
  $$
  so $z_2$ cannot dominate as strongly unless we also push $f_2$ (or $i_1$) much smaller. Achieving the same 0.99 dominance requires making $f_2 i_1\approx 0.01$, i.e. forcing strong forgetting explicitly.

Role of normalization and stabilization. The normalizer $n_t$ converts the raw, exponentialized contributions into a convex mixture (so we get interpretable “attention” weights and stable outputs). That normalization is necessary for the softmax interpretation and for preventing unbounded hidden values. The paper’s stabilizer $m_t$ is required to avoid numerical overflow when using exponentials, but it does not change the functional form (it merely implements the stable normalized exponent math used in practice).

In short: normalization makes the effect interpretable (a weighted average), but the key enabler of powerful revision is the exponential mapping which gives additive logits and an unbounded positive scale so a late, highly relevant $z_t$ can be made to dominate without having to annihilate earlier contributions via tiny forgets. This is why sLSTM with exponential gating can revise stored information much more decisively than the classic sigmoid gates.

### Is the sLSTM still a "constant error carousel" after introducing normalization c_t / n_t?
Question:
Classic LSTM preserves gradients through the additive update of $c_t$ (the "constant error carousel"). For sLSTM, the hidden state is $h_t = o_t (c_t / n_t)$ with $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$ and $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t$.
Does the division by $n_t$ preserve the favorable gradient flow properties of LSTM? In particular:
1) If I backprop through $c_t/n_t$, under what conditions on $i_t$ and $f_t$ do gradients avoid vanishing or exploding across long time spans?
2) Is there an analogous "constant error carousel" interpretation when we consider both $c_t$ and $n_t$ evolving together?
A short gradient analysis (even in a linearized setting) would be very helpful.
Answer:
Short answer: sLSTM preserves the additive “carousel’’ inside the two-dimensional state $(c_t,n_t)$, so there is still an additive path for gradients (the same product-of-forget-gates factor appears). However the readout $h_t = o_t (c_t / n_t)$ multiplies that path by normalization factors (roughly $1/n_t$ or $c_t/n_t^2$), so whether gradients stay constant, vanish or explode over long spans depends on how the forgets $f_t$ and the accumulated normalizer $n_t$ scale together. In particular, with constant gates you get three clear regimes: exponential decay if $0<f<1$, polynomial $(1/t)$ decay for $f=1$ and constant $i>0$, and potentially constant (bounded) gradients if $f>1$ and numerator and denominator grow at the same exponential rate.

Derivation (linearized / gate-fixed view). Work with a scalar loss that depends on a final $h_T$ (you can insert a multiplier $dL/dh_T$ as needed). From the sLSTM forward rules

$$
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t,\qquad
n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t,\qquad
h_t = o_t \frac{c_t}{n_t},
$$

the direct partials at time $T$ are

$$
\frac{\partial h_T}{\partial c_T} = \frac{o_T}{n_T},\qquad
\frac{\partial h_T}{\partial n_T} = -\,\frac{o_T c_T}{n_T^2}.
$$

The internal additive recurrence implies (for $k<T$)

$$
\frac{\partial c_T}{\partial c_k} = \prod_{u=k+1}^T f_u,\qquad
\frac{\partial n_T}{\partial n_k} = \prod_{u=k+1}^T f_u,
$$

(and cross derivatives like $\partial c_T/\partial n_k$ are zero). By the chain rule therefore

$$
\frac{\partial h_T}{\partial c_k}
= \frac{o_T}{n_T}\,\prod_{u=k+1}^T f_u,
\qquad
\frac{\partial h_T}{\partial n_k}
= -\frac{o_T c_T}{n_T^2}\,\prod_{u=k+1}^T f_u.
$$

Two points follow immediately. First, the familiar product-of-forgets factor $\prod f_u$ — the hallmark of the LSTM constant-error carousel — is still present. Second, the division by $n_T$ inserts extra prefactors ($1/n_T$ or $c_T/n_T^2$) that multiply the carousel factor and can attenuate or amplify gradients depending on the scaling of $n_T$ (and $c_T$).

Simple constant-gate examples (illustrate the regimes). Take constant $f_t = f$ and $i_t = i$, and ignore $o_T$ (assume $0<o_T\le 1$ constant) and inputs for clarity. Then

$$
n_T = f^T n_0 + i\frac{1-f^T}{1-f}\quad(f\neq1),\qquad
n_T = n_0 + iT\quad(f=1).
$$

Hence for the earliest scalar memory $c_0$,

$$
\frac{\partial h_T}{\partial c_0} = \frac{f^T}{n_T}\times o_T.
$$

As $T\to\infty$:

- If $0<f<1$, then $f^T\to0$ while $n_T\to i/(1-f)$ (finite) so the gradient vanishes exponentially (as in standard LSTM when forgets are $<1$).

- If $f=1$ and $i>0$, then $n_T\sim iT$ and the gradient decays only polynomially: $\partial h_T/\partial c_0 \sim O(1/T)$. Intuitively sLSTM behaves like a running average in this case, and the weight of a single past contribution is $O(1/T)$.

- If $f>1$ and $i>0$, then both numerator and denominator scale like $f^T$ and the ratio can converge to a nonzero constant (so the gradient remains bounded away from zero). In other words, exponential growth in $c_T$ is canceled by commensurate growth in $n_T$, preventing explosion and allowing a non-vanishing gradient.

Intuition and “carousel’’ interpretation. The two quantities $(c_t,n_t)$ form a two-dimensional additive memory that obeys the same multiplicative forget dynamics: $s_t := [c_t; n_t]$ satisfies $s_t = f_t s_{t-1} + i_t [z_t;1]$. That additive recurrence is the true “constant error carousel’’: gradients back to $s_k$ carry the $\prod f$ factor and therefore benefit from the LSTM trick. The extra normalization $c_t/n_t$ is a readout that rescales this carousel. So there is an analogous carousel for the internal state vector $s_t$, but the observable gradient to earlier scalar memories (and to model parameters via that readout) is multiplied by the readout Jacobian; this is why you see the extra $1/n_T$ or $c_T/n_T^2$ factors in the formulas above.

Practical implications (from the paper). Because $i_t$ and $f_t$ are input- and state-dependent and the paper allows exponential gating (so $f_t$ can exceed 1), the network can learn gate patterns that keep the effective factor $(\prod f)/n_T$ in a regime where important gradients are preserved. The stabilization trick introduced in the paper (the $m_t$ rescaling) is only a numerical device that does not change outputs or parameter derivatives (see the appendix), so it does not alter the algebraic gradient factors above. The authors also note in Limitations that forget-gate initialization matters: in practice one must choose/learn gate behavior to avoid undesired polynomial or exponential decay.

Takeaway. sLSTM keeps the additive gradient path (so it is not worse than LSTM in principle), but the normalization $c_t/n_t$ means the error seen at the hidden/output layer is a normalized summary of the two-dimensional carousel. If you want a literal constant error carousel for the scalar $h_t$ you need the combined factor $(\prod f)/n_T$ to stay $O(1)$ — achievable if gates are set so numerator and denominator grow together (e.g. $f>1$ with matching $i$) or if $f$ is tuned to keep $n_T$ bounded — otherwise you get polynomial $(1/T)$ or exponential decay as in the standard regimes above.

### Why is the mLSTM denominator clamped: h_t = o_t (C_t q_t) / max(|n_t^T q_t|, 1)?
Question:
In mLSTM, the hidden state is $h_t = o_t \cdot (C_t q_t) / \max(|n_t^T q_t|, 1)$, where $n_t$ is the normalizer state updated by $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t k_t$.
- What is the theoretical motivation for clamping the denominator by 1 instead of adding a small $\epsilon$ as is common in normalization schemes?
- Does this introduce a bias in the scale of the retrieved vector when $|n_t^T q_t| < 1$? If so, why is that preferable to an $\epsilon$ stabilization?
- How sensitive is performance to this threshold, and does it depend on the dimensionality $d$ or the norm statistics of $q_t$ and $k_t$?
Answer:
Short answer: the clamp is a deliberate, scale-aware safety / confidence rule that prevents the mLSTM from amplifying noisy or cancelled match signals. Using $\max(|n_t^T q_t|, 1)$ (with the absolute value) keeps retrievals numerically stable and makes the model treat weak total match mass as low-confidence (shrink the output) instead of blindly normalizing by a tiny number. Adding a tiny $\epsilon$ would not accomplish the same robustness and would be very sensitive to the numerical/scale regime.

Why, mathematically and intuitively. Write
$$
\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t \;=\; \sum_{s\le t} \alpha_s \, \mathbf{v}_s \,(\mathbf{k}_s^\top \mathbf{q}_t),
\qquad
D := \mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t \;=\; \sum_{s\le t} \alpha_s \,(\mathbf{k}_s^\top \mathbf{q}_t),
$$
where the $\alpha_s$ encode the gated decay/input factors. With the unclamped normalization the retrieved vector is
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{D}
\;=\; \sum_s \underbrace{\frac{\alpha_s (\mathbf{k}_s^\top \mathbf{q}_t)}{D}}_{w_s}\mathbf{v}_s,
$$
so for nonzero $D$ this is a weighted average of the stored values $\mathbf{v}_s$ (weights sum to 1). The clamp replaces the exact denominator by $\max(|D|,1)$. Two regimes follow:

- If $|D|\ge 1$ then nothing changes: $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t=(\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t)/D$ — a normalized, confidence-weighted average.
- If $|D|<1$ then $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t=(\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t)/1=\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t = D\cdot(\text{the same weighted average})$. So the clamped output is scaled down by factor $D<1$ relative to the normalized retrieval.

So yes, the clamp does introduce a bias (a shrinkage) whenever the total match mass $|D|$ is less than the threshold; the retrieved direction is the same as the normalized one but its magnitude is reduced by $D$. That bias is intentional: a small $|D|$ signals low confidence (weak matching mass, or cancellation between positive and negative similarities), and shrinking the output is preferable to forcing a full-strength retrieved vector that may be driven by noise or by numerical cancellation in $D$.

Why this is preferable to simply adding $\epsilon$. Adding an $\epsilon$ in a denominator is commonly used to avoid division-by-zero, but it does not express a confidence threshold: dividing by $D+\epsilon$ still normalizes even if $D$ is tiny, and with a tiny $\epsilon$ you get very large, unstable outputs and gradients whenever $D$ is tiny. To avoid that you would have to choose $\epsilon$ on the same scale as typical $|D|$, which makes $\epsilon$ a brittle hyperparameter tied to the data/model scale. The hard clamp to 1 instead implements a clear rule: only normalize when the match-strength is at least order-1; otherwise suppress the retrieval. In this paper that is sensible because the authors deliberately scale keys by $1/\sqrt{d}$ and use layer-norm on inputs, so typical dot-product magnitudes are $O(1)$. Using 1 as the lower bound therefore aligns with their chosen parameterization and with prior work they cite.

About the absolute value and sign: $\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t$ can be near zero because of cancellation between positive and negative contributions (keys are zero-mean). Using $|D|$ avoids sign flips in normalization (dividing by a negative number would flip the retrieved vector's sign), and clamping $|D|$ prevents pathological amplification caused by cancellation.

Sensitivity and dependence on $d$ / norms. The threshold’s effect depends on the expected scale of $D$. Because the paper sets $\mathbf{k}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\mathbf{W}_k\mathbf{x}_t + b_k$ and applies layer-norm, the typical scale of $\mathbf{k}^\top\mathbf{q}$ is roughly constant with $d$, so 1 is a natural, fairly robust choice across model sizes used in the paper. If you change the key/query scaling or remove layer-norm, the expected magnitude of $D$ will change and you should retune the threshold (or equivalently rescale keys by $1/\sqrt{d}$ so that threshold $1$ stays sensible). In practice the behaviour is qualitative: making the threshold much smaller (approaching numerical $\epsilon$) recovers the instabilities the clamp prevents; making it much larger forces the model to suppress many genuine, moderate-strength retrievals and will hurt recall/performance. Empirically the paper follows prior work (they cite Sun et al.) and uses threshold $1.0$; they also stabilize exponential gates separately (the $m_t$ stabilizer) so the whole system stays in a predictable scale regime.

Practical takeaway: the clamp is a scale-aware safety/confidence mechanism (not just a tiny numeric eps). It trades a small, controlled bias (suppressed output when match strength is weak) for much more stable training and inference. With the standard key scaling $1/\sqrt{d}$ and layer-norm used in the paper, threshold $1$ is a natural, robust choice; if you alter key/query parameterization you should retune the threshold or keep the key scaling so the same threshold remains meaningful.

### Are i_t and f_t in mLSTM scalars or vectors, and why this choice?
Question:
The mLSTM equations suggest scalar input and forget gates ($i_t$ and $f_t$), while the output gate $o_t$ is a vector. Is that correct? If so:
- What motivated using scalar $i_t$ and $f_t$ rather than per-dimension gates (e.g., vectors or even diagonal matrices)?
- Would vector gates improve capacity or stability, or would they create optimization/parallelization issues?
- Did you explore a per-head or per-subspace gating variant where different parts of $C_t$ evolve with different $f_t$ values?
Answer:
Short answer: In the paper’s mLSTM as written, both the input gate $i_t$ and the forget gate $f_t$ are scalars, while the output gate $\mathbf{o}_t$ is a vector. You can see this directly in the mLSTM forward equations (Section 2.3): the memory update is
$\mathbf{C}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top$,
and the gates are defined as scalar-valued functions
$i_t=\exp(\tilde i_t)$, $\tilde i_t=\mathbf w_i^\top\mathbf x_t+b_i$,
and $f_t=\sigma(\tilde f_t)$ or $\exp(\tilde f_t)$, whereas
$\mathbf{o}_t=\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf o}_t)$ with $\tilde{\mathbf o}_t=\mathbf W_{\mathbf o}\mathbf x_t+\mathbf b_{\mathbf o}$,
and the hidden state is $\mathbf h_t=\mathbf o_t\odot\tilde{\mathbf h}_t$. That notation and the surrounding text make clear $i_t,f_t\in\mathbb R$ while $\mathbf o_t\in\mathbb R^d$.

Why scalars for $i_t,f_t$?
The paper motivates the matrix memory with a covariance / fast‑weights style update: adding a rank‑one outer product $\mathbf v_t\mathbf k_t^\top$ and applying a single decay (forget) multiplier to the whole matrix is exactly the classic fast‑weights / covariance update view (Section 2.3). Interpreting $f_t$ as a global decay rate and $i_t$ as a global learning rate for the new outer product is natural and compact: a single scalar controls how much of the old matrix survives and how strongly the new rank‑one term is written. This keeps the update interpretable, keeps parameter count low (only a couple of small input projections to produce the scalars), and keeps the implementation and numerical-stabilization logic straightforward (the same stabilizer technique used for scalar exponential gates is applied).

Would vector (per‑dimension) gates help?
In principle, yes: per‑dimension or per‑subspace gates increase representational flexibility — they let different rows/columns (or subspaces) of $\mathbf C_t$ evolve on different timescales or with different learning rates, which can help if the stored facts naturally split into components that need different retention dynamics. However, this is a trade‑off. Vector gates add degrees of freedom (and thus parameters) and require corresponding changes to normalization/stabilization (the paper’s stabilizer $m_t$ would have to be vectorized or otherwise adapted for per‑dim exponential gates). Practically, the dominant compute cost in mLSTM is already the $d\times d$ matrix update and retrieval; adding an $O(d)$ gate vector is not asymptotically worse, but it does increase parameter/gradient noise and tuning complexity. The paper reports ablations on gating mechanisms and finds that making gates learnable and input‑dependent helps, but it does not report experiments that replace the scalar $i_t,f_t$ by full per‑dimension gates in the mLSTM equations.

Parallelization / optimization issues
There is no fundamental barrier to computing vector gates in parallel on modern hardware (elementwise gating is trivially parallel), and the mLSTM is designed to be parallelizable. The practical issues are (a) more parameters and potentially noisier gradients, (b) extra numerical-stability bookkeeping when using exponential activations per dimension, and (c) the need to adapt the normalization logic (the paper normalizes retrieval by $\max\{|\mathbf n_t^\top \mathbf q_t|,1\}$ where $\mathbf n_t$ is a scalar‑weighted sum of keys; per‑dimension gates change $\mathbf n_t$’s structure and may require a different normalization). So vector gating is implementable and parallelizable, but it complicates training and stabilization.

Per‑head / per‑subspace gating
The paper explicitly notes that mLSTM “can have multiple memory cells” and that for mLSTM “multiple heads and multiple cells are equivalent as there is no memory mixing” (Section 2.3). That means a practical way to get differentiated dynamics across subspaces is to split the memory into several smaller matrix memories (heads) and give each head its own scalar $i_t^{(h)},f_t^{(h)}$. This yields per‑subspace behavior while preserving the simpler scalar gating inside each head. The paper does not report a dedicated ablation that replaces the single global $i_t,f_t$ by per‑head or per‑dimension gates inside a single monolithic $\mathbf C_t$; their experiments use combinations of mLSTM and sLSTM blocks and ablate gating styles, but they don’t publish a direct scalar-vs-vector‑gate comparison for mLSTM.

Practical suggestion if you want to experiment
If you want more flexible forgetting/learning dynamics, try (a) multi‑head mLSTM where each head has its own scalar $i_t^{(h)},f_t^{(h)}$ (minimal extra parameters, keeps stabilization simple), or (b) a diagonal (per‑row or per‑column) gate implemented by multiplying $\mathbf C_{t-1}$ by $\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf f_t)$ (this increases gating granularity but requires adapting normalizer and stabilization). Full dense per‑entry gating would be prohibitively expensive. When using exponential activations for gating, remember to adopt per‑dimension stabilization (vectorized versions of the paper’s $m_t$ trick) to avoid numerical overflow.

Summary
The paper’s mLSTM uses scalar $i_t,f_t$ (global learning/decay rates) for interpretability and simplicity in the fast‑weights / covariance update formulation, while keeping $\mathbf o_t$ vector valued to allow per‑coordinate output modulation. Per‑dimension or per‑head gates are conceptually feasible and may increase capacity, and per‑head scalar gates can be implemented by using multiple matrix heads (which the paper says is equivalent to multiple cells), but the paper does not report experiments that directly compare scalar vs. vector gates inside a single monolithic mLSTM memory.

### Why is only the key vector scaled by d^{-1/2} in mLSTM?
Question:
The mLSTM definition uses $k_t \leftarrow d^{-1/2} W_k x_t + b_k$ (keys scaled by $d^{-1/2}$), but $q_t$ and $v_t$ are not scaled in the same way.
- What is the rationale for scaling only $k_t$? Is it to control the magnitude of outer products $v_t k_t^T$ in $C_t$ or to stabilize the denominator $n_t^T q_t$?
- Would scaling $q_t$ and/or $v_t$ similarly help, harm, or be redundant given the normalization via $n_t$?
Answer:
Short answer: the $1/\sqrt{d}$ factor is there for the same reason it appears in Transformer attention — to keep key-query inner products at $O(1)$ as dimension grows — and placing that factor on the keys is a natural, sufficient choice for mLSTM because keys appear both in the outer-product memory updates and in the normalizer. Scaling $q$ instead would be algebraically equivalent for the dot-products, but scaling $v$ is not equivalent and would change the retrieval amplitude.

Explanation grounded in the paper (equations are the paper’s): mLSTM stores outer products and retrieves by
$$
\mathbf{C}_t=\sum_{s\le t} i_s\,\mathbf{v}_s\mathbf{k}_s^\top,\qquad
\mathbf{h}_t \propto \frac{\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|,1\}},\qquad
\mathbf{n}_t=\sum_{s\le t} i_s\,\mathbf{k}_s .
$$
The paper sets $\mathbf{k}_t=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\mathbf{W}_k\mathbf{x}_t+\mathbf{b}_k$. The intuitive and technical points are:

1) Inner products grow with dimension. If vector entries have $O(1)$ variance then a dot product like $\mathbf{k}^\top\mathbf{q}$ has variance proportional to $d$. Dividing the key by $\sqrt{d}$ makes $\mathbf{k}^\top\mathbf{q}$ have variance independent of $d$. This is exactly the “scaled dot‑product” idea used in Transformers: prevent the raw inner products from blowing up with dimension so numbers and gradients stay well‑conditioned.

2) Keys control both the memory update and the normalizer. Because each stored outer product contains one factor of $\mathbf{k}$ and the normalizer $\mathbf{n}_t$ is a sum of keys, putting the $1/\sqrt{d}$ on $\mathbf{k}$ simultaneously tames the magnitude of the stored matrix entries $\mathbf{v}\mathbf{k}^\top$ and of the scalar weights $\mathbf{k}^\top\mathbf{q}$ that appear in both numerator and denominator of the retrieval. Algebraically, if you write $\mathbf{k}_t=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_t$ then
$$
\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_s i_s\,\mathbf{v}_s(\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t),\qquad
\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_s i_s\,(\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t),
$$
so the common $1/\sqrt{d}$ factor cancels inside the ratio (ignoring the lower bound clamp), while the per-term scalars $\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t$ are now $O(1)$ instead of $O(\sqrt{d})$.

3) Why not scale $q$ or $v$ instead? Placing the same $1/\sqrt{d}$ on $q$ would be essentially equivalent for stabilizing the raw inner products $\mathbf{k}^\top\mathbf{q}$ (because only the product matters). However, putting the scale on $k$ has the advantage that it also directly controls the scale of the stored outer products $\mathbf{v}\mathbf{k}^\top$ (so the memory matrix $\mathbf{C}_t$ does not grow with $\sqrt{d}$). Scaling $v$ is different: $\mathbf{v}$ appears in the numerator but not in the normalizer, so scaling $v$ would change the amplitude of retrieved vectors and would not cancel in the normalization. That would either force the model to compensate (through the output gate $\mathbf{o}_t$ or learned weights) or degrade signal‑to‑noise / gradient magnitudes. In short, scaling $v$ is not symmetric with scaling $k/q$ and is generally unnecessary or harmful.

4) Implementation and stability details in the paper. The authors also use a LayerNorm before projections (so inputs have controlled variance) and clamp the denominator by $\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,1\}$ to avoid division by very small scalars — both design choices work together with the $1/\sqrt{d}$ key scaling to keep retrieval numerically stable.

Takeaway: scaling the key by $d^{-1/2}$ is the pragmatic way to (a) keep key–query inner products $O(1)$ with dimension and (b) control the scale of the stored outer products, while leaving $v$ unscaled so that retrieval amplitude is not arbitrarily reduced. Scaling $q$ instead would be mathematically interchangeable for the inner products, but scaling $v$ would not be and is generally not recommended.

### How does mLSTM relate algebraically to fast weight programmers and linear attention?
Question:
mLSTM updates $C_t = f_t C_{t-1} + i_t v_t k_t^T$ and retrieves via $C_t q_t$ (then normalizes). Could someone expand the recurrence to show the explicit sum
$C_t = \sum_{s=1}^t \big( i_s \prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u \big) v_s k_s^T$,
so that $C_t q_t = \sum_{s \le t} w_{s,t} v_s (k_s^T q_t)$ with weights $w_{s,t} = i_s \prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u$? 
This would make the similarity to linear attention (kernel $\phi(x)=x$) and fast weight programmers more transparent. Also, is the normalizer $n_t$ implementing an approximate partition function analogous to linear-attention denominators?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — unrolling the mLSTM recurrence gives exactly a Fast-Weight / outer-product memory and retrieval that is algebraically the same form as causal linear attention with kernel $\phi(x)=x$, up to two important differences: (i) mLSTM multiplies each written outer product by a positive, input-dependent write weight $w_{s,t}$ (the learned “learning rate” and decay), and (ii) the paper’s normalizer is the same weighted projection of keys (with an absolute-value + lower bound clamp for stability), so it plays the role of the linear-attention denominator but modified for stability and the write-weights.

Derivation (unroll the recurrence). Start from the paper’s mLSTM write/update
$$\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top,$$
and assume $\mathbf{C}_0=0$. Expanding inductively gives
$$\mathbf{C}_t=\sum_{s=1}^t \Big(i_s\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u\Big)\,\mathbf{v}_s\mathbf{k}_s^\top.
$$
Define the shorthand write-weight
$$w_{s,t} \coloneqq i_s\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u,
$$
(which is positive because the paper enforces $i_s=\exp(\cdot)>0$ and $f_u$ is either $\sigma(\cdot)\in(0,1)$ or $\exp(\cdot)>0$). Then the stored matrix is
$$\mathbf{C}_t=\sum_{s\le t} w_{s,t}\,\mathbf{v}_s\mathbf{k}_s^\top.
$$

Retrieval and normalizer. Multiplying the memory by the query $\mathbf{q}_t$ gives
$$\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t=\sum_{s\le t} w_{s,t}\,\mathbf{v}_s\big(\mathbf{k}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t\big),
$$
exactly the form “sum of values scaled by key–query inner products”, with an extra multiplicative prior $w_{s,t}$. The paper’s normalizer state
$$\mathbf{n}_t=f_t\mathbf{n}_{t-1}+i_t\mathbf{k}_t
$$
unrolls in the same way to
$$\mathbf{n}_t=\sum_{s\le t} w_{s,t}\,\mathbf{k}_s,
$$
so its projection on the query is
$$\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t=\sum_{s\le t} w_{s,t}\,(\mathbf{k}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t).
$$
The mLSTM then forms the (stabilized) normalized readout
$$\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t=\frac{\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{\,|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|,1\,\}}
=\frac{\sum_{s\le t} w_{s,t}\,(\mathbf{k}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t)\,\mathbf{v}_s}{\max\{\,|\sum_{s\le t} w_{s,t}\,(\mathbf{k}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t)|,1\,\}}.
$$

Connection to linear attention and fast weights. Causal linear attention with kernel $\phi$ typically produces
$$\text{out}_t=\frac{\phi(\mathbf{q}_t)^\top\big(\sum_{s\le t}\phi(\mathbf{k}_s)\mathbf{v}_s^\top\big)}{\phi(\mathbf{q}_t)^\top\big(\sum_{s\le t}\phi(\mathbf{k}_s)\big)}.
$$
If $\phi$ is the identity, this becomes
$$\text{out}_t=\frac{\sum_{s\le t}(\mathbf{k}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t)\,\mathbf{v}_s}{\sum_{s\le t}(\mathbf{k}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t)},
$$
which matches the mLSTM algebraic retrieval when all write-weights $w_{s,t}=1$ and you ignore the absolute/clamp used in the paper. Thus mLSTM is the same algebraic family as linear attention with $\phi(x)=x$, except that mLSTM (a) multiplies each term by a positive, time-dependent write-weight $w_{s,t}$ (the input gate acts like a per-write learning rate and the forget gates implement decay), and (b) stabilizes the denominator by taking absolute value + $\max(\cdot,1)$. The paper explicitly points out the equivalence to Fast Weight Programmers (outer-product updates) by identifying the covariance outer-product update $\mathbf{C}_t\leftarrow f_t\mathbf{C}_{t-1}+i_t\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top$ with that literature.

Interpretation / intuition. Fast-weight programmers keep a fast weight matrix of outer-products that the network writes into and later queries by multiplication; mLSTM is exactly that, but with learned, input-dependent write strength and decay (so it can emphasize or forget particular past writes). Linear attention stores compressed sufficient statistics (sums of outer-products or keyed sums) to produce the same causal, streaming readout; mLSTM stores the same compression but with gated per-write scaling. The normalizer $\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t$ is therefore the natural “partition” or total compatibility scalar appearing in linearized attention, but mLSTM uses the weighted sum and an absolute/clamp to avoid small or sign-changing denominators (the paper motivates and implements that stabilization).

Takeaway. Algebraically mLSTM = Fast Weights = causal linear attention with $\phi(x)=x$ up to (useful) generalizations: learnable, input-dependent write/decay weights $w_{s,t}$, and a stabilized/weighted normalizer. If you set $i_s\!=\!1$, $f_u\!=\!1$, and remove the clamp, mLSTM becomes exactly the $\phi(x)=x$ linear-attention readout (modulo transpose conventions).

### What is the formal equivalence between multiple heads and multiple cells in mLSTM?
Question:
The paper states that "for mLSTM, multiple heads and multiple cells are equivalent as there is no memory mixing." Could you formalize this equivalence?
- If I instantiate $H$ independent mLSTM "heads" each with its own $C_t^{(h)}$, $q_t^{(h)}$, $k_t^{(h)}$, $v_t^{(h)}$, and $n_t^{(h)}$, and then concatenate the outputs, is this exactly the same function class as using a single larger block-diagonal memory $C_t$ with no cross-block mixing?
- Are there any subtle differences due to shared input projections or normalization across heads versus a single larger memory?
Answer:
Short answer: yes—provided you do not introduce any cross-head coupling in the parameters or the normalization/gating, H independent mLSTM heads (each with its own $\mathbf{C}^{(h)}_t,\mathbf{q}^{(h)}_t,\mathbf{k}^{(h)}_t,\mathbf{v}^{(h)}_t,\mathbf{n}^{(h)}_t$) are exactly equivalent to a single mLSTM whose memory is a block–diagonal matrix with those same blocks. The equivalence is just the statement that “no memory mixing” = “independent block dynamics”, so stacking independent blocks is the same as independent heads. Below I state that formally and then point out the small but important caveats (shared projections, global vs per-head gates/normalizers, and numerical/implementation issues).

Formalization. Let head $h\in\{1,\dots,H\}$ have dimension $d_h$. For each head the mLSTM recurrence (written in the paper, but here per-head) is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{C}_t^{(h)} &= f_t^{(h)} \mathbf{C}_{t-1}^{(h)} + i_t^{(h)}\,\mathbf{v}_t^{(h)}\mathbf{k}_t^{(h)\top},\\
\mathbf{n}_t^{(h)} &= f_t^{(h)} \mathbf{n}_{t-1}^{(h)} + i_t^{(h)}\,\mathbf{k}_t^{(h)},\\
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t^{(h)} &= \mathbf{C}_t^{(h)} \mathbf{q}_t^{(h)} \,/\,\mathrm{denom}^{(h)}_t,\qquad
\mathbf{h}_t^{(h)} = \mathbf{o}_t^{(h)}\odot\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t^{(h)},
\end{aligned}
$$
with the usual choices $\mathrm{denom}^{(h)}_t=\max\{|{\mathbf{n}_t^{(h)}}^\top\mathbf{q}_t^{(h)}|,1\}$ (the paper uses that absolute lower-bounded form). Concatenating the heads gives the full output $\mathbf{h}_t=\mathrm{concat}_h(\mathbf{h}_t^{(h)})$.

Now set $D=\sum_h d_h$ and let $E_h\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times d_h}$ be the standard block selector (so $E_h^\top E_h=I_{d_h}$ and $E_i^\top E_j=0$ for $i\neq j$). Define the block–diagonal memory
$$
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; \sum_{h=1}^H E_h \mathbf{C}_t^{(h)} E_h^\top \;=\; \operatorname{blockdiag}(\mathbf{C}_t^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{C}_t^{(H)}),
$$
and concatenate the per-head vectors into big vectors $\mathbf{q}_t=\sum_h E_h\mathbf{q}_t^{(h)}$, $\mathbf{k}_t=\sum_h E_h\mathbf{k}_t^{(h)}$, $\mathbf{v}_t=\sum_h E_h\mathbf{v}_t^{(h)}$, $\mathbf{n}_t=\sum_h E_h\mathbf{n}_t^{(h)}$, and similarly for $\mathbf{o}_t$. Define block-diagonal scalar scalings $S^{(f)}_t=\operatorname{blockdiag}(f_t^{(1)}I_{d_1},\dots,f_t^{(H)}I_{d_H})$ and $S^{(i)}_t=\operatorname{blockdiag}(i_t^{(1)}I_{d_1},\dots,i_t^{(H)}I_{d_H})$. Then the big recurrence can be written exactly as
$$
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; S^{(f)}_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + S^{(i)}_t \,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top,
$$
and retrieval is componentwise blockwise:
$$
\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t \;=\; \sum_{h} E_h\bigl(\mathbf{C}_t^{(h)}\mathbf{q}_t^{(h)}\bigr),
$$
so dividing by the per-head denominators and applying $\mathbf{o}_t$ reproduces the concatenated $\mathbf{h}_t$. Thus any H-head architecture with independent head parameters is realized by a single mLSTM with $\mathbf{C}_t$ block–diagonal and with the large projection matrices built from the per-head projection matrices (e.g. $W_q=\text{row–stack}(W_q^{(1)},\dots,W_q^{(H)})$, etc.). Conversely any block–diagonal single mLSTM decomposes into independent heads by extracting the blocks.

Intuition: because the update is an outer–product ($\mathbf{v}\mathbf{k}^\top$) plus a diagonal-like decay, and there are no recurrent hidden-to-hidden mixes that couple different memory subspaces, disjoint blocks evolve independently and retrieval is independent; concatenation just collects the independent results.

Important caveats (when equivalence breaks):
1. Global vs per-head gates/normalizers. The equivalence requires that the gates and normalizers act independently per block (i.e. you must be able to choose distinct $i_t^{(h)},f_t^{(h)}$ and use per-head denominators). If the model forces a single scalar input/forget gate $i_t,f_t$ applied uniformly to the whole memory, or computes the denominator as the single scalar $|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|$ summing contributions across blocks, then different heads are coupled and the multi-head independent parameterization is strictly more expressive than the constrained single-memory parameterization. (The paper’s equations present scalar gates in the compact form; the multiple-cells / multiple-heads variant assumes you allow per-cell / per-head gating or block-diagonal gating matrices.)

2. Shared input projections or post-concat normalization. If the heads share the same projection matrix (i.e. the same $W_k,W_q,W_v$ used for all heads) or if you apply a normalization operation after concatenation that mixes head components (for example a LayerNorm over the concatenated $\mathbf{q}_t$ that has cross-head statistics), then the H independent-head parameterization is more constrained than the fully free block–structured single mLSTM. Conversely, if you start from H independent heads you can always assemble a single big model whose projection matrices are simply the stacked per-head matrices; so the one-to-one mapping holds only when the single model is allowed the same block-structured freedom.

3. Implementation/finite-precision and training effects. Floating-point order of ops, tieing of biases, initialization, or regularizers can make the trained networks differ even when they are representationally equivalent. But these are practical/engineering differences, not mathematical ones.

Bottom line: mathematically, “multiple heads = multiple cells” for mLSTM is exact if you interpret both architectures as having the same lack of cross-block coupling (i.e. per-head gates/normalizers and block-separable projection parameters). If you impose parameter tying (shared projections, single global gate, or a global normalizer) you introduce coupling and the two architectures are no longer equivalent.

### How exactly is mLSTM parallelized? What is the scan formulation?
Question:
The paper says the mLSTM recurrence can be reformulated in a parallel version, similar to FlashAttention/GLA. Could you spell out the precise scan formulation?
- Given $C_t = f_t C_{t-1} + i_t v_t k_t^T$ and $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t k_t$, what are the associative operators that allow chunked prefix computations?
- How are the stabilizer terms (if using exponential gates) handled across chunk boundaries so that numerical rescaling remains consistent?
- A small pseudo-code example of computing all $h_t$ for a block of length $L$ in $O(\log L)$ reductions would be very helpful.
Answer:
Short answer: mLSTM is an affine linear recurrence with a scalar multiplicative decay and an additive rank-1 update, so it admits a standard prefix‑scan / parallel-scan formulation. The trick is to carry, per element (or per chunk), a small associative summary (a log-decay, a log‑stabilizer, and scaled accumulators). Those summaries compose with a simple associative binary operator; the per-chunk “max” values are exactly the stabilizers from the paper, and composition rescales the chunk accumulators so numerical stabilization stays consistent across chunk boundaries.

Why this works (formula): with
$$
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top,\qquad
\mathbf{n}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{k}_t
$$
one can unroll (assume $\mathbf{C}_0=\mathbf{n}_0=\mathbf{0}$ for simplicity):
$$
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^t \Big(\prod_{j=s+1}^{t} f_j\Big)\, i_s\,\mathbf{v}_s\mathbf{k}_s^\top,\qquad
\mathbf{n}_t \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^t \Big(\prod_{j=s+1}^{t} f_j\Big)\, i_s\,\mathbf{k}_s .
$$
Introduce the log-quantities $g_t=\log f_t,\; a_t=\log i_t$. For each term $s$ in a block ending at index $u$ define the log-weight
$$
T_s^{(u)} = a_s + \sum_{j=s+1}^{u} g_j.
$$
A numerically stable representation factors out the block maximum
$$
M_{p:u} = \max_{s\in[p,u]} T_s^{(u)},
$$
and stores scaled accumulators
$$
\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{p:u} = \sum_{s=p}^{u} \exp\big(T_s^{(u)}-M_{p:u}\big)\,\mathbf{k}_s,\qquad
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{p:u} = \sum_{s=p}^{u} \exp\big(T_s^{(u)}-M_{p:u}\big)\,\mathbf{v}_s\mathbf{k}_s^\top .
$$
The true $\mathbf{n}$ and $\mathbf{C}$ for that prefix are $\mathbf{n}=\exp(M)\,\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}$ and $\mathbf{C}=\exp(M)\,\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}$.

Associative operator (block composition). If A is an earlier block and B a later block, define a block-summary
$$
\text{op}=(G,M,\widetilde{\mathbf{n}},\widetilde{\mathbf{C}})
$$
with $G=\sum g_t$ over the block, $M$ the block max as above, and the two scaled accumulators. The binary compose (A followed by B) is
$$
\begin{aligned}
G_{AB} &= G_A + G_B,\\[4pt]
M_{AB} &= \max\big(M_B,\; M_A + G_B\big),\\[4pt]
\alpha_A &= \exp\big(M_A + G_B - M_{AB}\big),\qquad
\alpha_B = \exp\big(M_B - M_{AB}\big),\\[4pt]
\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{AB} &= \alpha_A\,\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_A \;+\; \alpha_B\,\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_B,\\[4pt]
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{AB} &= \alpha_A\,\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_A \;+\; \alpha_B\,\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_B .
\end{aligned}
$$
This operator is associative (it is just the algebra of shifting and adding the log‑weighted contributions), so you can parallel-scan it in $O(\log L)$ rounds on $L$ elements/chunks.

Relation to the paper’s stabilizer. The per-step stabilizer recurrence in the paper
$$
m_t = \max\big(\log f_t + m_{t-1},\; \log i_t\big)
$$
is exactly the same object as the block $M$ above when the block is a single time step, and the block-composition rule
$$
M_{AB}=\max(M_B,\; M_A+G_B)
$$
is the chunk-level analogue of running that same recurrence across a block boundary. Thus the per-chunk $M$ values are the stabilizers and composition enforces consistent rescaling across chunks.

Numerically stable evaluation of $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t$. After a parallel prefix you obtain for each prefix index $t$ the tuple $(G_t,M_t,\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_t,\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_t)$. Let $\mathbf{q}_t$ and $\mathbf{o}_t$ be the query and output gate at time $t$. Compute
$$
s_t = \widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t,\qquad
u_t = \widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_t\,\mathbf{q}_t.
$$
Recall $\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t=\exp(M_t) s_t$ and $\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t=\exp(M_t) u_t$. The mLSTM output uses
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|,1\}}.
$$
Compute this stably without forming huge/small exponentials by branching on the log condition:
if $M_t + \log(|s_t|+\varepsilon) \ge 0$ (i.e. $\exp(M_t)|s_t|\ge 1$) then
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{u_t}{|s_t|+\varepsilon},
$$
else
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \exp(M_t)\,u_t.
$$
Finally $\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t$. Use a tiny $\varepsilon$ to avoid division by zero; the branch avoids computing $\exp(M_t)$ in the large‑M case because the factor cancels there.

Compact pseudo-code (conceptual, leaves parallel-scan primitive implicit)
```
# Inputs for t=1..L: f[t], i[t], k[t], v[t], q[t], o[t]
g[t] = log(f[t]); a[t] = log(i[t])

# element summaries (in parallel)
op[t].G = g[t]
op[t].M = a[t]
op[t].n_tilde = k[t]                   # vector in R^d
op[t].C_tilde = outer(v[t], k[t])      # d x d matrix (or explicit MV form)

# run an associative parallel prefix using 'combine' defined below:
prefix_ops[1..L] = parallel_scan(op[1..L], combine)

# combine(left,right) implements the formulas in text:
#   G = G_left + G_right
#   M = max(M_right, M_left + G_right)
#   alpha_left = exp(M_left + G_right - M)
#   alpha_right = exp(M_right - M)
#   n_tilde = alpha_left * n_tilde_left + alpha_right * n_tilde_right
#   C_tilde = alpha_left * C_tilde_left + alpha_right * C_tilde_right

# final per-time compute (in parallel)
for t in 1..L:
    M = prefix_ops[t].M
    n_tilde = prefix_ops[t].n_tilde
    C_tilde = prefix_ops[t].C_tilde
    s = dot(n_tilde, q[t])
    u = matmul(C_tilde, q[t])          # vector in R^d
    if M + log(abs(s)+eps) >= 0:
        h_tilde = u / (abs(s)+eps)
    else:
        h_tilde = exp(M) * u
    h[t] = o[t] * h_tilde
```
Complexity and implementation notes: the scan uses the associative combine and therefore can be implemented in $O(\log L)$ parallel rounds (Blelloch or tree-scan); each combine involves scalar exp/logs and additions plus one vector (length $d$) and one matrix ($d\times d$) linear combination. Storing the full $\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}$ as a dense $d\times d$ per partial prefix is straightforward and matches the mLSTM design in the paper (the heavy $d\times d$ cost is what the authors mention). If you want to avoid storing $d\times d$ matrices you can instead keep the sum-of-outer-products form and only materialize $\mathbf{C}\mathbf{q}$ (i.e. accumulate $\sum \alpha_s \mathbf{v}_s ( \mathbf{k}_s^\top \mathbf{q})$); that trades memory for additional reductions but the same associative composition pattern still applies.

Summary (intuitive): think of each time step as producing a little affine operator "multiply previous state by product-of-f's and add these weighted outer-products". Those affine operators compose and you can compute any prefix by associatively combining the compact summaries (log‑scale, scaled accumulators). The per-block max $M$ is precisely the stabilizer from the paper, and combining with the formula above keeps the numerics identical to the sequential stabilized recurrence while permitting $O(\log L)$ parallel reductions.

### Does xLSTM have constant memory in sequence length during training or only at inference?
Question:
The paper claims linear compute and constant memory w.r.t. sequence length. For inference this is clear, but for training, backpropagation through time typically stores activations from each time step, leading to $O(T)$ memory.
- Is the constant-memory claim only about inference? Or do you use checkpointing/recompute/scan tricks to keep training memory sublinear in $T$?
- If so, what is the exact training-time memory complexity and what is the practical overhead?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper’s “constant memory w.r.t. sequence length” statement is best read as a statement about the model’s recurrent working memory and about inference/decoding, not about naive backprop through time. For training with standard autodiff you still need $O(T)$ activation storage unless you deliberately use recomputation/checkpointing or a custom backward; the paper does not explicitly claim it used some magic that makes training memory independent of $T$.

Why (intuitively). xLSTM is a recurrent-style model: its internal state (the sLSTM scalar cells or the mLSTM matrix memory) has a fixed size per layer that does not grow with the number of time steps $T$, so when you step the model forward at inference you only carry a fixed-size state forward. That is exactly the difference the authors highlight compared to Transformers, whose attention caches grow with $T$. So for inference/online decoding the peak memory that depends on the context length is constant (it depends on model dimension $d$, e.g. $O(d)$ for sLSTM or $O(d^2)$ per matrix memory in mLSTM), not on $T$.

Why training is different (technical). Reverse‑mode autodiff (backprop through time) normally needs the forward activations (gate pre‑activations, intermediate vectors, etc.) for every time step to compute gradients. If you run training naively in PyTorch/TensorFlow, peak memory scales like $O(T\cdot A)$ where $A$ is the per‑time‑step activation footprint (vectors $q,k,v$ of size $O(d)$, and — depending on how you implement the outer products — temporaries of size $O(d^2)$ could appear). So standard training memory is $O(T)$ in $T$ (times depend on $d$, batch size, number of layers).

What the paper says (and what it doesn’t). In Section “Memory and Speed Considerations” the authors state that xLSTM “have a linear computation and a constant memory complexity with respect to the sequence length.” The most natural reading in the paper’s text and experiments (they explicitly discuss generation time and maximal decoding throughput) is that this constant‑memory claim is about the recurrent state and inference/decoding, and about the fact that the model’s state does not grow with $T$. The paper does not give a detailed description of a training‑time scheme that magically makes autograd memory independent of $T$, nor does it say they relied on a specific global checkpointing/recompute protocol in the training runs reported. They do note (a) mLSTM “can be reformulated in a parallel version” and (b) they implemented fast CUDA kernels for sLSTM with “GPU memory optimizations to the register level.” Those implementation remarks suggest they took care to avoid huge temporaries (especially for the $d\times d$ outer products) but they do not amount to a general claim that training used constant memory without recomputation.

If you want sublinear or constant memory during training (practical options and costs). To make training memory sublinear you must explicitly use one of the standard techniques:

- Checkpointing / segmental recomputation: divide the sequence into blocks, store only block checkpoints, and recompute activations for each block during backward. A common practical choice is the “$\sqrt{T}$” checkpointing scheme (checkpoint every $\approx\sqrt{T}$ steps) which reduces peak activation memory to $O(\sqrt{T}\cdot A)$ while incurring roughly a $2\times$ compute overhead. More generally, choosing block size $k$ gives peak memory $O(k\cdot A)$ and recomputation cost $\gtrsim$ a small constant $\times T$ (with the $\sqrt{T}$ choice being a good tradeoff).  
- Aggressive recomputation (store almost nothing): you can reduce memory to $O(1)$ in theory but the compute overhead becomes very large (you may need many recomputed forward passes), so this is usually impractical.  
- Optimal checkpointing algorithms (e.g. “revolve”): give better time/memory tradeoffs than naïve schemes but are more complex to implement.  
- Fused custom kernels / adjoint implementations: write a custom forward+backward CUDA kernel for the entire recurrence (or for a block) that keeps only the minimal set of intermediates (or recomputes them inside the kernel). This can get you very low peak memory and good wall‑clock performance, but it is engineering‑heavy and hardware specific. The paper’s note about register‑level GPU optimizations for sLSTM suggests the authors used fused kernels to make inference/training efficient, but the paper does not publish the exact autograd strategy used for reducing training memory.

Concrete bookkeeping (simple formulas). Let $T$ be sequence length, $A$ the per‑time‑step activation size (depends on $d$, number of layers and whether you materialize outer products), and $M_{\text{model}}$ the model / recurrent state size (e.g. $O(d^2)$ per mLSTM memory). Then

- Naive training memory $\approx O(M_{\text{model}} + T\cdot A)$.  
- With block checkpointing of block size $k$ you can get peak memory $\approx O(M_{\text{model}} + k\cdot A)$ with recomputation overhead about $O(T)$ extra forward work (in practice often $\approx 2\times$ wall‑clock). Choosing $k \approx \sqrt{T}$ gives the familiar $O(\sqrt{T})$ memory and $\approx 2\times$ compute tradeoff.  
- To make the $T$ term disappear entirely you must accept large recomputation overhead or implement a fused custom backward/adjoint; the paper does not supply an explicit $T$-independent training algorithm or timings for such a scheme.

Practical overheads reported in the paper. The authors report implementation/runtime observations (these are mostly about inference/throughput): their sLSTM CUDA kernel is “typically less than two times slower than mLSTM,” and their mLSTM implementation is about $4\times$ slower than FlashAttention / the scan used in Mamba (they list these numbers in the “Memory and Speed Considerations” / “Limitations” sections). Those are wall‑clock notes about current implementations; they do not give a training‑memory asymptotic or say they trained with constant active memory in $T$.

Takeaway and what to do if you want to reproduce it. Treat the paper’s constant‑memory claim as an inference/working‑memory claim: xLSTM’s state does not grow with $T$, so decoding memory is constant in $T$ (and this is the reason they can decode with very large batch sizes). For training, assume $O(T)$ activation memory in a standard autograd run; to get sublinear or constant memory you must choose and implement a checkpointing/recompute strategy or write fused backward kernels. If you want exact details of what the authors used in their training runs, check their code at the repository cited in the paper (https://github.com/NX-AI/xlstm) or ask the authors — the paper’s text and appendices do not document a claim that training was done with strictly constant memory in $T$ without recomputation.

### Why is memory mixing necessary for state tracking, and how does sLSTM implement it?
Question:
The paper argues that models without memory mixing (e.g., pure SSMs or Transformers without certain recurrent structure) cannot solve state-tracking problems like parity, whereas sLSTM can.
- Could you give an intuitive explanation for why mixing hidden states over time (via recurrent hidden-to-hidden connections) is required to maintain and update a finite-state machine?
- For sLSTM with multiple cells and heads, what specific recurrent matrices (e.g., $R_z$, $R_i$, $R_f$, $R_o$) are responsible for this capability, and how do exponential gates influence the effective state transitions?
- A tiny worked example (e.g., implementing parity) would clarify this point.
Answer:
Short answer: you need memory mixing because a finite-state machine (FSM) updates its next state by a function of both the previous internal state and the new input. In sLSTM that “both” is realized by feeding the previous hidden vector back into the pre-activations for the cell inputs and for the gates (those are the recurrent matrices). Exponential gates then give you high dynamic range multiplicative control (near on/off writes and strong forgetting) so the model can implement sharp, state-dependent transitions. Below I first make this intuition precise with the sLSTM equations the paper uses, then I point to which recurrent matrices do the work, and finally I give a tiny worked parity implementation that shows the mechanism in action.

How memory mixing implements FSM updates (intuitively + formally)
The defining property of an FSM is that the next internal state $s_{t}$ is some function of the previous state $s_{t-1}$ and the current input $x_{t}$: 
$s_t = \mathcal{F}(s_{t-1},x_t)$. To realize such a conditional update in a neural sequence model you must give the network a direct way to make the new content and the gates depend on the previous internal summary. In sLSTM the vectorized forward pass from the paper is (writing vectors for multiple cells):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{c}_t &= \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{i}_t \odot \mathbf{z}_t,\\
\mathbf{n}_t &= \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{n}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{i}_t,\\
\mathbf{h}_t &= \mathbf{o}_t \odot \bigl(\mathbf{c}_t / \mathbf{n}_t\bigr),
\end{aligned}
$$

with the candidate and gates computed as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_t &= \mathbf{W}_z^\top \mathbf{x}_t \;+\; \mathbf{R}_z \mathbf{h}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{b}_z,\quad
\mathbf{z}_t=\varphi(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_t),\\
\tilde{\mathbf{i}}_t &= \mathbf{W}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t \;+\; \mathbf{R}_i \mathbf{h}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{b}_i,\quad
\mathbf{i}_t=\exp(\tilde{\mathbf{i}}_t),\\
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_t &= \mathbf{W}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t \;+\; \mathbf{R}_f \mathbf{h}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{b}_f,\quad
\mathbf{f}_t=\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_t)\ \text{or}\ \exp(\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_t),\\
\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t &= \mathbf{W}_o^\top \mathbf{x}_t \;+\; \mathbf{R}_o \mathbf{h}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{b}_o,\quad
\mathbf{o}_t=\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t).
\end{aligned}
$$

If the recurrent matrices $\mathbf{R}_\cdot$ are zero, then $\mathbf{z}_t,\mathbf{i}_t,\mathbf{f}_t,\mathbf{o}_t$ depend only on the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ (and biases). That makes the cell update a fixed input-conditioned accumulator: every write and forget is determined by $\mathbf{x}_t$ alone, so the network cannot implement arbitrary conditional transitions that need to route or reinterpret the *previous* state. By contrast, nonzero $\mathbf{R}_z,\mathbf{R}_i,\mathbf{R}_f,\mathbf{R}_o$ feed the previous hidden vector $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ back into candidate and gate pre-activations, so the new content and the gates become direct functions of $(\mathbf{h}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_t)$ — exactly what an FSM transition needs.

Which recurrent matrices do what (roles of $\mathbf{R}_z,\mathbf{R}_i,\mathbf{R}_f,\mathbf{R}_o$)
All four recurrent matrices are the levers of memory mixing:

- $\mathbf{R}_z$ mixes the previous hidden into the cell *candidate* $\mathbf{z}_t$. With $\mathbf{R}_z\neq 0$ you can produce a new candidate value that is an arbitrary linear mixing (and then nonlinear transform) of components of $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$. In practice $\mathbf{R}_z$ is often used to route or permute information among cells (e.g. implement a swap or permutation), so the new write can be a transformed version of the previous memory.

- $\mathbf{R}_i$ lets the input gate $\mathbf{i}_t$ depend on the previous state. This is crucial if you want the network to make write decisions that depend on where you are in the FSM (e.g. “if I’m in state A and I see symbol $x$, then write into cell 3; otherwise ignore”).

- $\mathbf{R}_f$ lets the forget gate $\mathbf{f}_t$ depend on the previous state. This enables state-conditioned retention or erasure: depending on $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ you can choose to keep (or decay) parts of memory.

- $\mathbf{R}_o$ lets the output gate depend on $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$; that changes what the network exposes as the readable hidden state, which in turn changes future recurrent pre-activations.

Put together, these matrices make the step $\mathbf{h}_t = \mathcal{G}(\mathbf{h}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_t)$ expressive enough to implement arbitrary finite-state transitions (up to usual function approximation considerations). The paper emphasizes exactly this point: memory mixing (hidden→hidden) is what gives LSTM-like RNNs the extra expressivity for state-tracking tasks that pure SSMs or vanilla Transformer blocks (without such recurrent mixing) lack.

Why exponential gates matter
The paper replaces some sigmoid gates by exponential gates (e.g. $\mathbf{i}_t=\exp(\tilde{\mathbf{i}}_t)$). Exponentials are positive and have unbounded range, which gives two practical advantages for FSM-style transitions:

1. High dynamic range: $\exp$ can make a gate arbitrarily small or large (in floating-point practice stabilized via the $m_t$ trick in the paper). That lets the model implement near–binary decisions (essential for crisp state switches): one write can be scaled to dominate the memory, or made negligibly small to ignore it.

2. Multiplicative composition over time: because the cell update is multiplicative in gates, products like $\prod f$ naturally appear in the contribution of an older write to the present. Exponentials allow the model to sharply attenuate or amplify contributions across time and, together with the normalizer $\mathbf{n}_t$ (which records the sum of gate strengths), keep retrieval stable.

These two properties are what the paper calls “exponential gating with normalization and stabilization,” and they directly support the ability to perform discrete-like, state-dependent updates and to revise earlier stored values.

A tiny worked parity example (constructive sketch)
We implement parity (toggle on $1$, keep on $0$) with a minimal sLSTM having two memory cells (so the FSM state is one-hot between “even” and “odd”).

Let the two-cell memory be $\mathbf{c}_t\in\mathbb{R}^2$, normalizer $\mathbf{n}_t\in\mathbb{R}^2$, and hidden $\mathbf{h}_t=\mathbf{c}_t/\mathbf{n}_t$ (assume $\mathbf{o}_t=\mathbf{1}$ to simplify). Define the $2\times 2$ swap matrix
$\;S=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\[2pt]1&0\end{pmatrix}\,$.
Choose parameters as follows (this is a constructive idealization — in practice you would use large/small pre-activations and the stabilizer $m_t$ to get near-binary gates):

- Candidate mixing: set $\mathbf{R}_z = S$, $\mathbf{W}_z=\mathbf{0}$, $\mathbf{b}_z=\mathbf{0}$ and let $\varphi$ act approximately linear on the operated regime; then
  $\mathbf{z}_t \approx S\,\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$.

- Input / forget gates depend only on the current bit $b_t\in\{0,1\}$ (the one-hot encoding of input $0/1$). Choose them so that
  when $b_t=0$: $\mathbf{f}_t=\mathbf{1},\ \mathbf{i}_t=\mathbf{0}$ (no write, keep memory);
  when $b_t=1$: $\mathbf{f}_t=\mathbf{0},\ \mathbf{i}_t=\mathbf{1}$ (replace memory with candidate).

With those choices the sLSTM vector update reduces to the simple rule
$$
\mathbf{c}_t =
\begin{cases}
\mathbf{c}_{t-1}, & b_t=0,\\[4pt]
\mathbf{z}_t = S\,\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, & b_t=1.
\end{cases}
$$
If we initialize $\mathbf{c}_0=[1,0]^\top$ and $\mathbf{n}_0=\mathbf{1}$ so $\mathbf{h}_0=[1,0]^\top$ (even), then:

- seeing a $0$ keeps $\mathbf{c}$ the same (state unchanged),
- seeing a $1$ sets $\mathbf{c}_t = S \mathbf{h}_{t-1}$, i.e. it swaps the two coordinates, toggling between $[1,0]$ and $[0,1]$.

Thus parity (XOR of the sequence of bits) is implemented: each $1$ toggles the one-hot memory. The key ingredient that makes this construction possible is $\mathbf{R}_z=S$ (mixing the previous hidden into the new candidate) plus gates that select whether to apply that candidate or keep the old memory. If $\mathbf{R}_z$ were zero (no mixing into $\mathbf{z}_t$), the candidate could not depend on $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ and you could not implement the swap needed for toggling.

Remarks and ties to the paper
This toy shows the generic pattern: use a recurrent matrix to route/permute/transform previous hidden contents into what you will write next, and use gates (the paper’s exponential gates are especially good) to choose *when* and *how strongly* to apply that write. The paper emphasizes that sLSTM’s memory mixing (nonzero $\mathbf{R}_\cdot$) plus exponential gating gives the model precisely the ability to solve state-tracking problems (parity and similar formal-language tasks) where architectures without such hidden→hidden mixing (many SSMs, vanilla Transformer blocks without recurrent state updates) empirically fail on the same tasks.

In short: memory mixing = you can make the *new* candidate and the *gates* depend on the *previous* compressed state; exponential gates = high-dynamic-range multiplicative switches to realize near-discrete transitions; together in sLSTM they implement the FSM-style update needed for parity and other state-tracking problems.

### Recommended initialization for exponential forget/input gates to control timescales?
Question:
With exponential gates, $i_t = e^{\tilde{i}_t}$ and optionally $f_t = e^{\tilde{f}_t}$, the effective timescale depends on the typical magnitude of $f_t$. What initialization strategy ensures stable, useful timescales at the start of training?
- For example, if we target an average forget factor $\bar{f}$ corresponding to a half-life $H$ (so $\bar{f}^H \approx 1/2$), what bias on $\tilde{f}_t$ achieves that given the input distribution and layer norm?
- Do you prefer initializing $\tilde{f}_t$ negatively so that $f_t<1$ initially, and then letting training increase it when needed?
Answer:
Short answer: pick the forget-bias so that the typical pre-activation equals the log of your desired per-step multiplier, and (because you usually use pre-LayerNorm) this is simply
$b_f \approx \ln(\bar{f})$, where for a half-life $H$ you want $\bar{f} = 2^{-1/H}$, so
$b_f = - \ln 2 / H$. In practice you should initialize $b_f$ slightly negative (so $f_t < 1$ initially); training can then push it toward $1$ or above if amplification is useful.

Why and how this follows from the paper. In xLSTM the forget gate can be exponential so $f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)$ (see the sLSTM/mLSTM definitions, eqs. in the paper). Over $H$ steps a memory term is multiplied roughly by $\prod_{k=1}^H f_{t+k}$ so a first-order, “typical” decay is controlled by the sum of logs:
$E\left[\log \prod_{k=1}^H f_{t+k}\right] \approx H \cdot E[\tilde{f}]$. If you want that typical multiplicative factor after $H$ steps equals $1/2$, set $\exp(H \cdot E[\tilde{f}]) = 1/2$, hence
$E[\tilde{f}] = \ln(2^{-1/H}) = - \ln 2 / H$.
If your gate pre-activation has the affine form $\tilde{f}_t = w_f^T x_t + r_f h_{t-1} + b_f$ and the input vector is zero-mean (which is the case when you use pre-LayerNorm residual backbones as in the paper), then $E[\tilde{f}_t] \approx b_f$ and you can directly set
$b_f = - \ln 2 / H$.

Small-but-important corrections. If the pre-activation has non-negligible variance $\sigma^2$ (for example because weights produce spread around the bias), then $f_t$ is log-normal and the mean and median differ: $\operatorname{median}(f_t)=\exp(\text{mean})=\exp(b_f)$ while $E[f_t]=\exp(b_f + \sigma^2/2)$. Which should you match? the dynamics of multiplicative memory are governed by sums of $\log f$ (so the median of the product follows $\exp(H\cdot\text{mean})$), therefore matching the mean of $\tilde{f}$ (i.e. the median of $f$) is the natural choice and gives the simple formula above. If instead you specifically want the expected value $E[f_t]$ to equal $\bar{f}$, set
$b_f = \ln(\bar{f}) - \sigma^2/2$.
In practice with pre-LayerNorm and small initial weights $\sigma^2$ is small, so the uncorrected bias $b_f = - \ln 2 / H$ is a good practical default.

Input-gate and practical defaults. For the exponential input gate $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ you can use the same idea: choose $b_i = \ln(\text{desired per-step write strength})$. A conservative choice is to make $i$ small initially (e.g. $i \approx 0.01\text{--}0.1$), so $b_i \approx \ln(0.01\ldots0.1)$ ($\approx -4.6\ldots -2.3$). Small $i$ and slightly negative $f$ together give stable, slowly-updating memories; the xLSTM stabilizer $m_t$ (eqs. in the paper) prevents numerical overflow but does not remove the need for sensible biasing.

Should you bias $\tilde{f}$ negative at init? Yes. Initializing $\tilde{f}$ negative (so $f_t < 1$) is the safer and usually preferable choice: it produces decaying memory that avoids runaway amplification and numerical growth early in training, and lets gradient descent increase the timescale or even push $f>1$ if the model needs amplification. The paper explicitly warns that forget initialization must be chosen carefully; the formulas above give a principled way to do it.

A short worked example: if you want half-life $H = 512$ tokens then $b_f = - \ln 2 / 512 \approx -0.00135$, so $f \approx \exp(b_f) \approx 0.99865$ (very close to $1$). If your gate pre-activation variance is, say, $\sigma^2 = 0.01$, and you care about mean $f$, subtract $\sigma^2/2 \approx 0.005$ from $b_f$ (so $b_f \approx -0.00635$).

Summary: set $b_f$ so that $E[\tilde{f}] = \ln(\bar{f})$ and use $\bar{f} = 2^{-1/H}$ for a target half-life $H$. With pre-LayerNorm this is just $b_f = - \ln 2 / H$ (with optional small variance correction). Initialize $b_f$ slightly negative ($f<1$) and $b_i$ so $i$ is small; training will adjust timescales as needed.

### If f_t can exceed 1 with exponential gating, what prevents exploding memories?
Question:
In sLSTM and mLSTM the forget gate $f_t$ can be exponential, hence $f_t>1$ is possible. The paper introduces stabilizers and, for mLSTM, a denominator based on $n_t$.
- In sLSTM, if many consecutive $f_t>1$, will $c_t$ and $n_t$ both grow but their ratio remain stable, or can the ratio still blow up?
- In mLSTM, even if $C_t$ grows, does dividing by $|n_t^T q_t|$ guarantee bounded outputs, or can $C_t q_t$ grow faster than the denominator?
- Are there explicit constraints or regularizers used in training to keep these effects in check?
Answer:
Short answer: the xLSTM design prevents unbounded hidden outputs in the normal (intended) regime by construction — the normalizer states make the cell output a weighted average — and the paper adds several numerical/architectural protections (the stabilizer state $m_t$, layer‑norm before key/value projections, key scaling and a lower bound on the denominator). That said, mathematically the raw memories (the scalar cell $c_t$ or the matrix $C_t$) can grow exponentially when many consecutive forget gates exceed $1$; the normalization/thresholding is what prevents the visible output from exploding in typical settings, but adversarial corner cases (e.g. cancellations in mLSTM) can still produce large outputs unless the usual boundedness assumptions on $z,v,k,q$ hold.

More detail and the exact reason why: for sLSTM the normalizer $n_t$ is chosen so that the ratio is a weighted average. If you define
$$
w_{t,s} \;=\; i_s \prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u \qquad(\text{with } \prod_{u=t+1}^t f_u :=1),
$$
then
$$
c_t=\sum_{s\le t} w_{t,s} z_s,\qquad n_t=\sum_{s\le t} w_{t,s}.
$$
Hence
$$
\frac{c_t}{n_t} \;=\; \sum_{s\le t} \frac{w_{t,s}}{n_t}\, z_s,
$$
which is a convex (nonnegative weights that sum to $1$) combination of the past inputs $z_s$. So regardless of how large each individual weight $w_{t,s}$ becomes (for example if many consecutive $f_u>1$), the ratio $c_t/n_t$ remains inside the convex hull of the $\{z_s\}$. In the paper the cell inputs $z_t=\varphi(\tilde z_t)$ are standardly bounded (e.g. $\varphi=\tanh$ or used together with LayerNorm), and the output gate $o_t$ is a sigmoid, so the visible hidden $h_t=o_t(c_t/n_t)$ is likewise bounded. Note also that the stabilizer state $m_t$ in the paper rescales exponentials for numerical safety; as the authors point out, using the stabilized gates does not change the mathematical outputs or gradients — it only prevents floating‑point overflow while computing very large $f_t,i_t$.

For mLSTM the same weighted‑average intuition applies but with an extra inner product factor from keys/queries. Writing the same $w_{t,s}$ you get
$$
C_t q_t \;=\; \sum_{s\le t} w_{t,s}\, v_s (k_s^\top q_t),\qquad
n_t^\top q_t \;=\; \sum_{s\le t} w_{t,s}\, (k_s^\top q_t).
$$
So the unnormalized retrieval is a linear combination of the values $v_s$ with scalar coefficients $\alpha_s := w_{t,s}(k_s^\top q_t)$; the normalization denominator is $\sum_s \alpha_s$. Thus, if all the scalar factors $(k_s^\top q_t)$ scale together, the common factor cancels and you again get a weighted average of bounded $v_s$. To protect against the dot product being very small (e.g. near cancellation of positive and negative terms), the paper uses $|n_t^\top q_t|$ and lower bounds it by $1$: the retrieval is
$$
\tilde h_t \;=\; \frac{\sum_s \alpha_s v_s}{\max\{\,|\sum_s \alpha_s|,\,1\}}.
$$
This prevents division by tiny scalars (numerical blowup). However, in principle there is a pathological scenario in which $\sum_s \alpha_s$ is small because of sign cancellations while the vector sum $\sum_s \alpha_s v_s$ does not cancel (e.g. values $v_s$ aligned so cancellations in the scalar weights do not reduce the vector norm); in that adversarial case dividing by the threshold $1$ can produce a large retrieved vector. The paper mitigates this risk in practice by (i) layer‑normalizing inputs before producing keys/values, (ii) scaling keys by $1/\sqrt{d}$ (attention‑style scaling), (iii) using the absolute value plus a lower threshold on the denominator, and (iv) applying the sigmoid output gate. These measures make extreme mismatches unlikely in practice and keep the produced hidden vectors numerically controlled.

Finally, regarding explicit training regularizers: the paper does not introduce a separate loss term or exotic regularizer whose sole purpose is to cap memory growth. The mechanisms used to keep things well‑behaved are architectural and numerical: the normalizer states $n_t$ (and their division), the stabilizer state $m_t$ for exponentials, layer‑norm before projecting to keys/values, key scaling $1/\sqrt{d}$, the absolute/thresholding of $|n_t^\top q_t|$, and the practical remark that forget‑gate initialization must be chosen carefully (the paper lists that as a limitation and hyperparameter to tune). In short: the model relies on normalization + numeric stabilizers rather than an explicit regularizer on gate magnitudes; under the paper’s boundedness assumptions for projections and activations this is sufficient to prevent exploding visible outputs.

### Is the mLSTM normalizer n_t an analogue of an attention partition function?
Question:
In mLSTM the normalizer evolves as $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t k_t$ and the output uses $(C_t q_t) / \max(|n_t^T q_t|, 1)$. This resembles linear attention, where a denominator approximates the softmax partition.
- Under what assumptions (e.g., keys normalized by layer norm, certain statistics of $k_t$ and $q_t$) does $n_t^T q_t$ approximate the sum of unnormalized attention weights?
- Is there a derivation that makes the connection precise, or is this mainly a heuristic stabilization?
- How critical is the directional alignment between $n_t$ and $q_t$ for good normalization?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — but only in the sense of linear (dot‑product) attention, not softmax attention. The mLSTM normalizer $n_t$ plays exactly the role of the partition / denominator for a linearized attention where the feature map is the identity ($\phi(k)=k$, $\phi(q)=q$). It is not a mathematically exact softmax partition unless you make additional approximation assumptions (or replace the feature map).

Why this is exact for a linear attention interpretation. Write the scalar gating product that each stored key/value receives as
$$
\alpha_s \;=\; i_s \prod_{u=s+1}^{t} f_u,
$$
which follows from iterating the recurrence $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t k_t$ (and similarly for $C_t$). Then the matrix memory and normalizer expand to
$$
\mathbf{C}_t=\sum_{s\le t}\alpha_s\,\mathbf{v}_s\mathbf{k}_s^\top,\qquad
\mathbf{n}_t=\sum_{s\le t}\alpha_s\,\mathbf{k}_s.
$$
Acting on the current query $q_t$ gives
$$
\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t=\sum_{s\le t}\alpha_s(\mathbf{k}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t)\,\mathbf{v}_s,\qquad
\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t=\sum_{s\le t}\alpha_s(\mathbf{k}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t).
$$
Therefore the mLSTM readout before gating,
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t}{\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t},
$$
is exactly the weighted average of stored values with weights proportional to $\alpha_s(\mathbf{k}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t)$. That is precisely the linear (dot‑product) attention readout: scores = $k_s^\top q_t$, per‑item importance = $\alpha_s$, and the denominator $\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t$ is the partition that normalizes those (unnormalized) dot‑product scores. So in the linear attention view the correspondence is exact, not merely heuristic.

Why this is not the softmax partition in general. Standard Transformer attention uses softmax weights $\propto\exp(\mathbf{k}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t)$. To make $\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t$ equal (or close) to $\sum_s \exp(\mathbf{k}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t)$ you would need either (a) a feature map $\phi$ with $\phi(\mathbf{k})^\top\phi(\mathbf{q})\approx\exp(\mathbf{k}^\top\mathbf{q})$ (Performer / random feature approximations try to do exactly this), or (b) a small‑score regime where $\exp(x)\approx 1+x$ and some bookkeeping gives the same form (but that approximation is crude except for very small $x$). mLSTM uses $\phi=\text{id}$ (and gates $\alpha_s>0$), so it matches linearized attention exactly but does not in general equal the softmax partition. The paper therefore rightly describes the resemblance to linear attention.

The paper’s practical design choices that help make the normalizer behave sensibly are worth noting. They apply a LayerNorm before key/value projection (so keys have controlled mean/variance) and they scale keys by $1/\sqrt{d}$ (so dot‑product magnitudes stay $O(1)$). The input/forget gates $i_t, f_t$ are positive (exponential or sigmoid), so the $\alpha_s$ are positive weights. Still, $\mathbf{k}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t$ can be negative or can cancel across stored items, so the scalar $\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t$ can be small or change sign. For that reason the paper uses $\max(|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|,1)$ (and absolute value) as a stabilization: this is a practical heuristic to avoid division by tiny or sign‑flipping denominators and is not derived from a softmax identity.

How important is alignment between $n_t$ and $q_t$? It is critical for the normalization to be meaningful. If the stored keys that matter for a query are coherently aligned with $q_t$ (so each $\mathbf{k}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t$ is positive and they point in similar directions), then $\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t$ will reflect the total unnormalized mass and the normalization yields a sensible convex (or signed) average of values. If stored keys have mixed signs/projections relative to $q_t$, the denominator can shrink by cancellation and the raw numerator can be misleading; that is exactly why the model needs the LayerNorm/scaling and learns gates $\alpha_s$ that emphasize relevant memory entries. In other words, good directional alignment (or learned gating that suppresses negatively projecting keys) makes the normalizer a faithful partition; lack of alignment makes the clamp/absolute stabilization necessary and introduces bias compared to a softmax normalization.

In summary: mLSTM’s $n_t$ is the partition function for dot‑product linear attention (exactly), and the paper’s denominator implements that exact linear normalization plus a pragmatic stabilization (absolute value and lower bound). It becomes an approximation to softmax attention only under additional assumptions (small scores or an explicit exponential/feature approximation). The directional alignment of stored keys with the query is therefore an important factor for the denominator to reflect useful attention mass.

### Why is the sLSTM output nonlinearity \psi removed and replaced by c_t / n_t?
Question:
Classic LSTM uses $h_t = o_t \psi(c_t)$ with a squashing nonlinearity like $\tanh$. In sLSTM the hidden is $h_t = o_t (c_t / n_t)$.
- Why was $\psi$ omitted here? Is the normalization by $n_t$ sufficient to keep $h_t$ in a reasonable range?
- Does removing $\tanh$ improve gradient flow or expressivity, and have you compared against a variant that uses both normalization and a bounded $\psi$?
Answer:
Short answer: $\psi$ (the usual $\tanh$ squash on the cell) was dropped because the new normalizer state $n_t$ already performs the role of scale control: $\tilde{h}_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}$ is a positive-weighted running average of past inputs, so if the inputs $z_t$ are bounded the ratio stays in a reasonable range. Removing the extra $\tanh$ avoids an additional saturating nonlinearity (which hurts gradient flow and can compress stored signals), and the paper stabilizes exponential gates so the normalization is numerically safe. The paper’s ablations attribute the performance gains to the exponential gating + new memory structure (the sLSTM formulation that omits $\psi$ is part of that), but the authors do not report a dedicated experiment that keeps both the normalization and a bounded $\psi$ on the output.

Why the division by $n_t$ replaces $\psi$ (intuitive + algebra). In sLSTM the recurrences are
$$
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t,\qquad
n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t,\qquad
\tilde{h}_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}.
$$
Unrolling these shows
$$
c_t=\sum_{s\le t}\Big(\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u\Big) i_s z_s,\qquad
n_t=\sum_{s\le t}\Big(\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u\Big) i_s,
$$
so
$$
\tilde{h}_t=\frac{c_t}{n_t}=\sum_{s\le t}\alpha_s z_s,\qquad
\alpha_s\ge0,\ \sum_s\alpha_s=1.
$$
Thus $\tilde{h}_t$ is a convex (weighted) average of past cell inputs $z_s$. If $z_s$ is bounded (in practice $\varphi$ often remains a bounded nonlinearity like $\tanh$ on the input path), then $\tilde{h}_t$ is automatically bounded without needing another squashing $\psi$. This is exactly the role the normalizer state $n_t$ plays: it records the total effective gate mass so the quotient cancels out runaway scaling from the (exponential) gates.

Why this helps gradients and retrieval. A squashing $\psi$ (e.g. $\tanh$) can saturate and kill gradients coming back through the output path; by using the linear normalized ratio the derivative from $h_t$ back into $c_t$ behaves like a scaled linear factor (roughly $o_t / n_t$, apart from the dependence of $n_t$ on gates), so you avoid the near-zero derivatives induced by a saturated $\tanh$. At the same time the normalized linear readout preserves relative magnitudes of stored signals, which is important when you want to retrieve or revise stored values precisely (the paper motivates this as important for tasks that require revising storage decisions). The paper also stabilizes exponential gates via the log–max trick (the $m_t$ stabilizer) so large exponentials do not overflow and dividing by $n_t$ remains numerically well-behaved; for mLSTM an analogous denominator clamp (divide by $\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|,1\}$) prevents tiny/zero denominators.

What the paper tested. The ablations reported in the paper show that the major improvements come from exponential gating and the new memory structures (scalar normalizer for sLSTM and matrix/covariance memory for mLSTM). The authors do not present a direct head-to-head of the specific variant $h_t = o_t\cdot\psi(c_t/n_t)$ versus $h_t = o_t\cdot(c_t/n_t)$. Because $\psi$ would reintroduce a saturating nonlinearity on top of the normalized readout, one should expect it to reduce gradient flow and (potentially) retrieval fidelity, but the definite effect would need to be measured empirically.

If you want to experiment: try two simple ablations (1) add a $\psi$ on the normalized readout, $h_t=o_t\psi(c_t/n_t)$, and (2) keep $\psi$ on $c_t$ but without dividing, $h_t=o_t\psi(c_t)$. Monitor validation loss, retrieval accuracy on synthetic tasks, and gradient norms through the memory to see which effect dominates for your task. The paper’s theory and experiments explain why the pure normalized readout is a principled choice for better retrieval and gradient behavior.

### How is sLSTM "multi-head" mixing defined and combined—concatenation, summation, or something else?
Question:
The paper mentions that sLSTM can have multiple heads with memory mixing within each head but not across heads.
- Are the outputs of different sLSTM heads concatenated (like Transformer heads) or summed/averaged? 
- Are the recurrent matrices $R_z, R_i, R_f, R_o$ block-diagonal across heads to prohibit cross-head mixing?
- At what stage (if any) do different heads communicate—only via subsequent feedforward/residual layers?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not spell out a single, formal implementation detail for how sLSTM heads are merged, but it is explicit about the semantic constraint: memory mixing (the recurrent hidden–hidden interactions implemented by the R matrices) is allowed inside each head and forbidden across heads. The simplest and most natural way to implement that is to give each head its own recurrent parameters (or equivalently make the R matrices block‑diagonal) and to combine the per‑head outputs later via a linear projection over the concatenated head outputs (exactly the same recombination pattern used in multi‑head attention). In the xLSTM design heads therefore do not communicate via the recurrent/memory mixing; they only interact through the surrounding linear/pointwise layers (input projection, the up/down projections in the residual block, output projection and subsequent residual/feed‑forward layers).

Why this is the correct reading and what it means practically. The paper states that sLSTM “can have multiple heads with memory mixing within each head but not across heads.” That phrasing implies an architectural partition of the cell state and of the hidden state into head‑wise groups. If there are $H$ heads and each head has a hidden vector of size $d_h$, the global hidden state at time $t$ can be written as
$$
\mathbf{h}_t = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{h}_t^{(1)} \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{h}_t^{(H)}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{H d_h}.
$$
To forbid recurrent mixing across heads you implement the per‑gate recurrent maps so they only see their head’s part of $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ — equivalently the recurrent matrix for a gate (say $R_z$) is block‑diagonal,
$$
\mathbf{R}_z = \mathrm{diag}\!\big(\mathbf{R}_z^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{R}_z^{(H)}\big),
$$
or you store separate per‑head matrices $\mathbf{R}_z^{(k)}$ and never multiply a head’s past hidden vector by another head’s recurrent block. That enforces “no memory mixing across heads” at the recurrence level.

How head outputs are merged is not stated verbatim in the paper, but the xLSTM block structure strongly suggests the same pattern used in multi‑head attention: compute per‑head hidden/outputs independently, concatenate them to form a joint vector, and then apply a linear projection that maps back to the block’s (original) model dimension. Concretely, if each head produces $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_h}$, form the concatenation
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t^{\mathrm{cat}}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t^{(1)}\\\vdots\\\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t^{(H)}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{H d_h},
$$
and then apply an output projection $\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times H d_h}$ to obtain the block output $\mathbf{y}_t=\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t^{\mathrm{cat}}+\mathbf{b}$. This keeps per‑head information separate through the recurrence (no cross‑head contamination) but lets later linear layers recombine head features.

Why concatenation + linear projection is the natural choice. Concatenation preserves the full per‑head information and gives the downstream projection full flexibility to recombine signals, which is important because the paper’s goal is to let residual/FFN layers do high‑dimensional summarization (the sLSTM is used inside a post up‑projection residual block). Summation/averaging would immediately mix head signals and reduce capacity; it would also implicitly allow cross‑head interaction at the moment of summation, which would contradict the paper’s stated “no mixing across heads” at the memory/recurrent level unless the summation is performed only after all recurrence/gating is finished (which would then still be a form of late mixing — i.e., through the output projection or residual path rather than the R matrices).

Where heads can communicate. Following the paper’s description, different heads do not communicate via the recurrent/memory mixing (the R matrices) but they do communicate via (a) shared input projections (all heads typically see the same input embedding or its linear transforms), (b) the output projection / up/down projections inside the xLSTM residual block, and (c) subsequent residual/feed‑forward layers in the stacked architecture. In other words, head isolation is strictly about the internal recurrence; higher layers and linear projections are the channels for cross‑head interaction.

Practical verification. The paper provides code at the linked repository; if you need the exact implementation details (concatenation vs. sum, precise parameter shapes, whether they use explicit block‑diagonal matrices or separate per‑head matrices) check the sLSTM implementation in the repo. Conceptually: treat heads as independent recurrent submodules (separate R matrices) whose outputs are recombined by the block’s linear projections and the residual stack.

### Pre vs. post up-projection: why pre for mLSTM and post for sLSTM?
Question:
The xLSTM block uses post up-projection for sLSTM but pre up-projection for mLSTM, citing capacity and Cover's theorem.
- Could you provide a concise rationale or ablation: why does mLSTM benefit more from summarizing in a higher-dimensional space (pre), whereas sLSTM prefers to summarize in the original space (post)?
- Are there tasks or scales where the opposite configuration works better?
Answer:
Short answer: the choice follows the memory geometry and computational trade‑offs of the two LSTM variants. mLSTM stores a matrix (outer‑product / covariance) memory so you get much more storage and separability by projecting into a larger embedding space before you form keys/values (pre up‑projection). sLSTM relies on scalar (or per‑cell) additive memory plus recurrent memory‑mixing, so you keep the recurrent/gating dynamics compact and only expand after the recurrence (post up‑projection). The paper’s language about Cover’s theorem and “memory capacity becomes larger in the high‑dimensional space” is exactly this point.

Why this is true, intuitively and technically. mLSTM’s update is
$$
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\,\mathbf{k}_t^\top,
$$
so the memory is a $\,d\times d\,$ matrix whose number of “degrees of freedom” (and hence raw associative capacity) grows like $O(d^2)$. If you first up‑project inputs into a bigger dimension $D$ and then form $\mathbf{k},\mathbf{v}$ there, $\mathbf{C}$ lives in $\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}$ and you get a much larger associative store and better linear separability of different histories (Cover’s theorem): keys/values that are produced in higher dimension are easier to separate and produce higher signal/noise under the covariance update and retrieval $\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t$. Because mLSTM purposely abandons hidden–hidden recurrence (it is parallelizable), there is no heavy recurrent matrix to blow up if you work at higher dimension first, so the compute/parameter cost remains concentrated in the feedforward up‑projection — a good match for pre up‑projection.

By contrast sLSTM’s core cell is (scalar per cell)
$$
c_t \;=\; f_t\,c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t\,z_t,
$$
and when you have multiple sLSTM cells the model relies on recurrent memory‑mixing via matrices $\mathbf{R}_{\cdot}$ from the hidden state into gates/inputs. Projecting inputs to a very large dimension before the recurrence would inflate those recurrent matrices (and the cost and instability of recurrent mixing) without yielding the same multiplicative capacity win that a matrix memory gives you. In other words, sLSTM’s useful capacity comes from gated recurrence and structured mixing rather than from a quadratic outer‑product store, so it is more efficient and stable to summarize in the original (compact) space and only then up‑project for the residual/nonlinear transformations (post up‑projection). This also aligns with engineering constraints in the paper: sLSTM is not parallelizable and benefits from keeping its recurrence small; mLSTM is parallelizable and designed to exploit a higher‑dimensional associative store.

About ablations and whether the opposite can ever win. The paper’s ablations emphasize that (i) the matrix memory and (ii) exponential gating are the main contributors to the performance gains, and the experimental default throughout the paper is pre‑up for mLSTM and post‑up for sLSTM. The authors do not report a systematic swap (i.e. pre vs post swapped for each cell type) as a primary ablation in the main text, so there is no direct empirical curve in the paper that says “swap helps on X.” Conceptually, however, one can imagine regimes where the swap might be useful: if you have an sLSTM with many independent heads and can afford very large recurrent matrices (and you care more about separability than about keeping recurrence cheap), pre‑projection could increase separability before gating; conversely, if an mLSTM’s internal dimension is already extremely large or you are compute‑constrained, a post‑projection design might be more efficient and not lose much in practice. Those are trade‑offs of compute, stability, and the kind of memory you need (associative capacity vs. stateful mixing). The paper’s empirical takeaway is that the chosen defaults (pre for mLSTM, post for sLSTM) are practical and robust across the synthetic associative‑recall tasks and the language‑modeling scale experiments they ran.

Practical rule of thumb from the paper: if your cell stores associations via outer products (matrix memory) and you want large retrieval capacity, up‑project first; if your cell’s strength is gated recurrent/stateful mixing, keep the recurrence compact and up‑project after summarizing.

### Is C_t actually a covariance estimator and what assumptions are needed?
Question:
You motivate $C_t \leftarrow C_{t-1} + v_t k_t^T$ as a covariance update rule, assuming layer norm yields zero-mean $k_t$ (and maybe $v_t$).
- True covariance requires centering and also scaling by sample count. Here, you do not divide by count and you also include gating and decay in mLSTM. In what precise sense is $C_t$ a covariance (or proportional to one)?
- Do you rely on approximate whitening from layer norm for the "optimality" claims (maximizing SNR for binary retrieval), or does the argument hold more generally?
Answer:
Short answer: $C_t$ in the paper is not the classical (mean‑centered, $1/N$-scaled) sample covariance; it is an unnormalized, gated outer‑product accumulator — i.e. a weighted second‑moment / cross‑covariance accumulator. Under the paper’s explicit assumption that keys and values are mean‑zero (layer‑norm before the projections) and under the usual associative‑memory assumptions (patterns approximately uncorrelated/isotropic or gates independent of content), $C_t$ is proportional to the (weighted) cross‑covariance matrix. The forget/input gates simply turn the plain outer‑product sum into a content‑weighted (typically exponentially decayed) sum; the paper’s “optimality” claim (maximizing SNR for binary retrieval) is the classical result for the covariance (Hebbian / outer‑product) update under those standard assumptions, and it becomes only approximate when those assumptions are violated.

A few formulae and intuition to make this precise. The mLSTM update is
$$
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top .
$$
Unfolding the recurrence gives
$$
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; \sum_{s\le t} w_{s,t}\,\mathbf{v}_s\mathbf{k}_s^\top,\qquad
w_{s,t}\equiv \Big(\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u\Big)\,i_s,
$$
so $C_t$ is literally a weighted sum of outer products. The normalizer state in the paper is
$$
\mathbf{n}_t \;=\; \sum_{s\le t} w_{s,t}\,\mathbf{k}_s,
$$
and retrieval uses
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t \;=\; \mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t \ /\ \max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|,1\}.
$$

If every $\mathbf{k}_s$ and $\mathbf{v}_s$ is zero‑mean (the paper assumes a LayerNorm before the projections so each projected vector has zero mean across its coordinates), then each outer product $\mathbf{v}_s\mathbf{k}_s^\top$ equals the (uncentered) second moment and its expectation equals the cross‑covariance $E[\mathbf{v}\mathbf{k}^\top]$. If in addition the weights $w_{s,t}$ are independent of the sample content (for example constant or deterministic, or independent in expectation), then
$$
E[\mathbf{C}_t] \;=\; \Big(\sum_{s\le t} E[w_{s,t}]\Big)\,E[\mathbf{v}\mathbf{k}^\top],
$$
so $C_t$ is proportional to the cross‑covariance. In the stationary case with constant forget/input gates this is just an exponential moving average of the outer products (an EWMA covariance estimator); dividing by the sum of weights would give the usual normalized estimator. The paper does not explicitly divide by a scalar count because the retrieval step uses $\mathbf{n}_t$ (and the $\max\{\cdot,1\}$ clamp) to compensate for scale in the readout; so absolute normalization is not necessary for stable retrieval.

About “optimality” (maximizing SNR for binary retrieval): the classical associative‑memory results the paper cites (Sejnowski / Dayan, Fast Weights / outer‑product memories) show that the simple outer‑product (covariance/Hebbian) rule is optimal for linear readout of stored binary or random zero‑mean patterns under the standard assumptions: patterns are zero‑mean, components are uncorrelated or approximately isotropic (so cross‑terms average out), retrieval is linear ($Cq$), and one measures separability / SNR of the desired stored vector versus cross‑talk. Under those assumptions the outer‑product rule maximizes the desired signal relative to the average crosstalk noise among local, biologically/plausibly implementable rules. If those assumptions fail — for example if keys have strong correlations between components (no whitening) or if the gates $i_t,f_t$ are strongly content‑dependent in a way that biases weights — then the strict optimality statement no longer holds. In particular, LayerNorm enforces zero mean (so centering is handled) and rescales variances, but it does not whiten (it does not remove linear correlations between coordinates). Whitening the keys (decorrelating coordinates) would make the outer‑product rule closer to the ideal in correlated regimes; conversely, the pseudoinverse/least‑squares associative rule can eliminate interference entirely but is expensive (requires inverting a Gram matrix), which is why outer‑product rules are attractive: cheap, local, and often near‑optimal under mild randomness/isotropy assumptions.

In practice the picture in the paper is this: LayerNorm removes the per‑sample mean (so centering is satisfied), the linear projections and learned gating produce reasonably behaved, approximately isotropic embeddings, and the readout normalization by $\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t$ stabilizes scale — so interpreting $\mathbf{C}_t$ as a (weighted, unnormalized) covariance makes sense and the Dayan/Sejnowski optimality intuition applies as a useful approximation. If you need strictly unbiased covariance estimates for analysis you would divide by the sum of weights (or explicitly track an effective sample count), and if you face highly correlated keys you should consider a whitening step or a more expensive recursive least‑squares style rule.

### Capacity comparison: how should I think about mLSTM's O(d^2) memory vs. attention's capacity?
Question:
The paper claims mLSTM increases capacity by moving from a scalar to a $d \times d$ matrix memory (so roughly $O(d^2)$ slots), and also notes Transformers can have memory that is exponential in coding dimension in some analyses.
- Is there a simple back-of-the-envelope comparison for the effective number of storable associations for mLSTM versus a Transformer of the same hidden size $d$?
- How do collision/interference and the choice of $f_t,i_t$ affect the practical capacity (e.g., for the Multi-Query Associative Recall task)?
Answer:
Short answer: think of mLSTM’s $d\times d$ matrix as a compressed, low‑rank associative store, not as $d^2$ independent “slots.” For random dense keys/values the number of reliably retrievable key–value pairs scales like $O(d)$ (up to a constant set by your $\mathrm{SNR}$ requirement), even though the memory contains $d^2$ scalars. By contrast, attention/Transformer-style readout is a non‑linear selector that (under favorable coding assumptions used in some theory papers) can in principle separate exponentially many patterns in the embedding dimension $d$; this is why Transformers are the empirical gold standard on associative‑recall benchmarks in the paper.

Why mLSTM looks like $O(d^2)$ but behaves like $O(d)$. The mLSTM cell stores
$$
\mathbf{C}=\sum_{t=1}^{N}\alpha_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^{\!\top}
$$
(with write weights $\alpha_t$ coming from the input/forget gates). Each new pair adds a rank‑1 outer product, so $\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{C})\le \min(d,N)$. That linear algebra fact already says you cannot perfectly encode more than about $d$ linearly independent arbitrary associations: beyond $N\!\sim\!d$ the stored rank saturates and retrieval must trade off signal versus interference. The $d^2$ numbers are the entries of a linear operator $\mathbf{C}:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}^d$, but a linear map can only implement arbitrary targets on at most a $d$-dimensional subspace of queries.

A simple back‑of‑the‑envelope $\mathrm{SNR}$ argument (standard for outer‑product memories) makes the $O(d)$ scaling explicit. Suppose keys are random unit vectors and we query with $q=k_s$. Then
$$
\mathbf{C}q = \alpha_s\mathbf{v}_s + \sum_{t\ne s}\alpha_t \mathbf{v}_t (k_t^\top k_s).
$$
For random keys the cross inner products $k_t^\top k_s$ have zero mean and typical size about $1/\sqrt{d}$. Treating the cross‑terms as noise, the noise standard deviation grows like $\sim\|\mathbf{v}\|\sqrt{N/d}$ while the signal size is $\sim\|\mathbf{v}\|$. Thus the signal‑to‑noise ratio scales like
$$
\mathrm{SNR}\;\approx\;\sqrt{\frac{d}{N}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
N_{\max}\approx \frac{d}{\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{req}}^2}.
$$
If you demand $\mathrm{SNR}\approx 1$ (barely readable), $N_{\max}\sim d$; if you want $\mathrm{SNR}\approx 3$ you must store $\approx d/9$ pairs, etc. So the rule‑of‑thumb: doubling $d$ roughly doubles the number of random associations you can store reliably.

Why Transformers can “beat” that in theory. Attention keeps K/V pairs explicitly and uses a non‑linear softmax selector; in analyses of modern Hopfield/attention models (Ramsauer et al. and related work cited in the paper) one can construct discrete/combinatorial codebooks that make exponentially many patterns (in $d$) mutually separable by a softmax energy landscape. That is a different regime: you rely on special code structure and non‑linear selection instead of linear superposition. The paper correctly notes this as the reason Transformers shine on associative‑recall tasks.

How collisions / interference and gates $(f_t, i_t)$ change the practical capacity. Collisions are just large dot products between different keys: they increase the noise term above and so lower $\mathrm{SNR}$. You can fight collisions in three practical ways that the paper’s mLSTM implements or suggests:

- Make keys more orthogonal or higher‑dimensional (larger $d$): this directly reduces typical cross inner products $\sim 1/\sqrt{d}$ and raises $\mathrm{SNR}$.
- Control write strength (input gate $i_t$) and decay (forget $f_t$): $i_t$ scales the contribution of a new outer product, $f_t$ decays older terms. Smaller or sparse $i_t$ reduces the number of effective writes $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$; lower $f_t$ reduces long‑term accumulation. Both reduce interference but trade off retention. The paper’s exponential gating gives a much larger dynamic range for these weights, so the model can learn a heavy‑tailed write distribution (few strong writes, many near‑zero writes), which helps effective capacity.
- Use normalization / stabilizers (the paper’s $\mathbf{n}_t$ and the denominator $\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top q|,1\}$ and the stabilizer $m_t$). Normalization prevents runaway amplitudes and keeps readout scale well‑conditioned; it doesn’t remove cross‑talk but helps numerical stability and lets the network use larger dynamic ranges safely.

The paper’s experiments on the Multi‑Query Associative Recall task illustrate these points: mLSTM (within xLSTM) improves over scalar LSTM because the matrix memory and exponential gating increase representational power and enable selective writes; xLSTM[1:1] was the best non‑Transformer model and even extrapolated to longer contexts (Section 3.1, experiments). Still, Transformers remained the best on pure associative recall, consistent with the theoretical advantage that non‑linear attention can exploit structured codes and explicit storage.

Practical takeaway. If you want a compact, linear, parallelizable recurrent memory (mLSTM): budget on the order of $d$ reliably retrievable random associations (use the $\mathrm{SNR}$ formula to pick $d$ for your target $N$ and $\mathrm{SNR}$). To push effective capacity without increasing $d$, either make writes sparse/selective via learned gates (exponential gates help), partition memory into heads/cells with different roles, or design keys to reduce collisions. If you need extreme associative recall (very large $N$ or combinatorial codebooks), attention/transformer‑style uncompressed storage is the safer choice, which is exactly what the paper’s experiments show.

### Numerical stability of the sLSTM stabilizer when using sigmoid forget gates
Question:
The stabilizer uses $\log f_t$ even when $f_t$ is sigmoid. For very small $f_t$, $\log f_t$ is large negative. 
- In practice, does $m_t = \max(\log f_t + m_{t-1}, \log i_t)$ avoid underflow reliably, or do you cap $\log f_t$ at some minimum?
- Are there implementation details (e.g., using log1p, fused kernels) necessary to keep this robust across long sequences?
Answer:
Short answer: the max-stabilizer in the paper is the standard log-domain trick and is numerically robust in exact arithmetic; in practice you must (a) compute the log of the sigmoid in a numerically stable way (use a logsigmoid / stable softplus form), (b) keep the log-domain accumulators (the $m_t$ recurrence) in a sufficiently wide format (FP32 / bfloat16) when you are doing mixed‑precision training, and (c) (optionally) clamp values only as a last-resort fallback for very low precision or extremely long sequences. Fusing the recurrence and the gate rescales into a single kernel is recommended for both speed and to avoid extra rounding/cast noise.

Why this works (intuitively + math). The stabilizer is
$$
m_t \;=\; \max\!\big(\log f_t + m_{t-1},\; \log i_t\big),
\qquad
i'_t \;=\; \exp(\log i_t - m_t),\qquad
f'_t \;=\; \exp(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t).
$$
This is exactly the familiar “log-domain” normalization (think log‑sum‑exp / forward algorithm): subtracting $m_t$ makes the exponents $\le 0$, so $\exp(\cdot)$ cannot overflow. If $f_t=\sigma(\tilde f_t)$ then you should not compute $\log f_t$ via $\log(\sigma(\tilde f_t))$ as two separate ops; instead use the numerically stable identity
$$
\log\sigma(\tilde f_t)= -\mathrm{softplus}(-\tilde f_t),
$$
or use your framework’s logsigmoid implementation (PyTorch: F.logsigmoid), which avoids forming tiny $f_t$ first.

Practical gotchas and recommendations
- Compute log-gates in a stable form. For sigmoid forget gates do $\log f_t = \mathrm{logsigmoid}(\tilde f_t) = -\mathrm{softplus}(-\tilde f_t)$ (implemented with stable softplus / log1p internally); for exponential gates $\log f_t=\tilde f_t$ trivially. This prevents an intermediate tiny $f_t$ becoming exactly zero before you take the log.  
- Do these log and $m_t$ operations in FP32 (or bfloat16) even when the rest of the network runs in FP16. FP16’s exponent range is small and will cause overflow/inf / -inf much earlier; using FP32 for the gating/log domain avoids that without breaking the model. The paper uses mixed precision in experiments, and the usual practice is to keep numerically sensitive reductions/normalizers in higher precision.  
- Underflow to zero of $\exp(\text{very negative})$ can still happen in finite precision, but that only means that contribution is numerically negligible (which is usually acceptable). If you need to avoid even tiny nonzero contributions becoming exactly zero (because of gradient flow concerns), keep the exponentials and accumulators in FP32.  
- You generally do not need to “cap $\log f_t$ at some fixed minimum” in correct FP32 implementations; capping is a hack that changes the exact algebraic equivalence (the paper proves the stabilized form is algebraically equivalent). Only use clamping if you are forced to run everything in FP16 / extremely long sequences and see NaNs or infs — then clamp to a reasonable finite range (e.g. a few tens) as a pragmatic fallback.  
- Fuse the recurrence and the gate-rescaling into one kernel when possible. Doing the $\max$, the two exponentials and the cell/normalizer updates in one fused GPU kernel reduces memory traffic, avoids repeated casts, and reduces rounding error accumulation. The paper notes the authors implemented an optimized CUDA kernel for sLSTM, which is exactly the sort of optimization that helps here.  
- Initialization: the paper explicitly warns that forget‑gate initialization matters. Choosing a forget bias that avoids extremely small $\sigma(\tilde f_t)$ at start (standard practice: positive bias around 1.0) reduces the risk of early underflow/vanishing contributions.

In short: the mathematical stabilizer $m_t$ is sound and prevents overflow; the practical steps to make it robust across long sequences are to compute $\log\sigma(\cdot)$ in a numerically stable way, keep the log-domain arithmetic in FP32/bfloat16, and (preferably) fuse the recurrence into a single kernel. Clamping $\log f_t$ is not required if you follow those practices and is only a pragmatic fallback for very low‑precision deployments.

### Output gating in mLSTM: what role does o_t play after normalization?
Question:
mLSTM computes $h_t = o_t \cdot (C_t q_t) / \max(|n_t^T q_t|, 1)$. Given we already apply a form of scale normalization, what additional function does the output gate $o_t$ serve?
- Is $o_t$ mainly feature-wise modulation for conditioning, or is it critical for stability/performance?
- Have you compared against a variant without $o_t$ (or with a scalar $o_t$) to see its marginal benefit?
Answer:
Short answer: $o_t$ is not redundant after the normalization step — it supplies input-conditioned, feature-wise multiplicative control over the retrieved vector and thus plays both a representational and a training-stability role. The paper even phrases this as “the output gate scales the retrieved vector.” Ablations in the paper show that gating in general is beneficial, but the paper does not report a targeted experiment that simply removes $o_t$ or replaces it by a scalar; so the marginal gain of $o_t$ alone is not given numerically there.

Why that matters intuitively and technically. mLSTM computes
$\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t=\dfrac{\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|,1\}}$ and then
$\mathbf{h}_t=\mathbf{o}_t\odot\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t$ with $\mathbf{o}_t=\sigma(\mathbf{W}_o\mathbf{x}_t+\mathbf{b}_o)$. The normalization is a scalar division: it controls a single global scale (and protects against tiny denominators by the lower bound 1), but it does not change the pattern of relative activations inside the vector $\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t$. The output gate is a vector-valued, input-conditioned mask that (1) rescales or zeroes individual dimensions, (2) conditions the readout on the current input, and (3) multiplicatively gates gradients flowing back into the memory/read path. Concretely, the gradient that reaches the retrieved quantity is multiplied per-dimension by $\mathbf{o}_t$, so $\mathbf{o}_t$ functions like an element-wise learned “per-feature learning rate” for the retrieval channel; that both shapes representational capacity and helps stabilize/update dynamics of the memory.

Why this isn’t just “conditioning” or just “stability” — it’s both. Feature-wise gating gives the model the flexibility to expose only the components of a retrieved vector that are relevant for the current input (important for selective readout and for tasks like associative recall or state tracking). At the same time the bounded sigmoid and the multiplicative structure let the network damp large signals or gradients in a fine-grained way that a scalar normalization cannot (especially when the normalization is clipped at 1 or when the normalizer dot is small).

What the paper reports and what it doesn’t. The authors explicitly state that “the output gate scales the retrieved vector,” and their ablation suite attributes much of xLSTM’s gains to exponential gating plus the matrix memory. They also write that “having each gate learnable and influenced by the input has an incremental positive effect.” However, they do not present a clean, single ablation that removes $\mathbf{o}_t$ entirely or replaces it by a single scalar and reports the isolated marginal effect of that change. So if you need hard numbers on that specific change, you would need to run that ablation (see suggestions below).

If you want to test this yourself, the simplest ablations are (a) set $\mathbf{o}_t\equiv\mathbf{1}$ (no output gate), (b) replace $\mathbf{o}_t$ by a learned scalar $s_t$ (shared across dimensions) or by a learned per-head scalar, and (c) use a learnable bias-only gate (no input dependence). Monitor validation perplexity, training loss curves, gradient norms into $\mathbf{C}_t$ (or $\mathbf{q}_t$), and behavior on synthetic tasks that require selective readout (e.g., associative recall / rare-token tasks used in the paper). My expectation (consistent with the paper’s gating-ablation statements and classic LSTM literature) is that removing $\mathbf{o}_t$ will reduce performance on tasks needing selective per-dimension control, and replacing it by a scalar will give a middle-ground: some stability/magnitude control remains, but the model loses per-feature selectivity and will usually perform worse than the full vector gate.

Practical recommendation: keep the vector output gate unless you need to shave parameters/compute and can afford an empirical check. It’s cheap relative to the matrix memory and gives both expressive conditioning and a useful, fine-grained knob for gradient and signal control that a scalar normalization cannot provide.
