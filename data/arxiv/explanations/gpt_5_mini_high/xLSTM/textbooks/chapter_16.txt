Chapter 16: Mathematical Notes and Derivations

# Why normalization by the gate mass preserves scale and gradients

We analyze the sLSTM normalization defined by the coupled recurrences
\[
c_t = f_t\,c_{t-1} + i_t\,z_t,\qquad
n_t = f_t\,n_{t-1} + i_t,
\qquad
h_t = o_t\,\frac{c_t}{n_t},
\]
with \(i_t>0\) and \(f_t>0\) (for sLSTM \(i_t=\exp(\tilde i_t)\), and \(f_t\) is either \(\sigma(\tilde f_t)\) or \(\exp(\tilde f_t)\)). The quantity \(n_t\) is the normalizer or gate mass: it accumulates the effective amount of input mass that has been written into the cell after accounting for forget-decay. The ratio \(c_t/n_t\) is central: it produces a normalized memory content rather than an unbounded accumulated sum.

To see why this normalization preserves scale and stabilizes gradients, expand the recurrences explicitly (assume \(c_0,n_0\) given; the common simplification \(c_0=n_0=0\) is used in many presentations but is not required). By unrolling,
\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t &= \Big(\prod_{s=1}^t f_s\Big)c_0 \;+\; \sum_{j=1}^t \Big(\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s\Big)\, i_j\, z_j,\\
n_t &= \Big(\prod_{s=1}^t f_s\Big)n_0 \;+\; \sum_{j=1}^t \Big(\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s\Big)\, i_j.
\end{aligned}
\]
Define the (nonnegative) unnormalized weights
\[
w_j^{(t)} \;=\; \Big(\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s\Big)\, i_j,
\]
so that
\[
\frac{c_t}{n_t}
\;=\;
\frac{\sum_{j=1}^t w_j^{(t)} z_j \;+\; \big(\prod_{s=1}^t f_s\big)c_0}
     {\sum_{j=1}^t w_j^{(t)} \;+\; \big(\prod_{s=1}^t f_s\big)n_0}.
\]
Thus \(c_t/n_t\) is a weighted average of the past inputs \(z_j\) (and of the initial state), with weights \(w_j^{(t)}\) that combine the write strength \(i_j\) and the subsequent decay factors \(\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s\). Two immediate consequences follow.

First, scale invariance: if all weights \(w_j^{(t)}\) are multiplied by a common positive factor \(\lambda\) then both numerator and denominator are multiplied by \(\lambda\) and the ratio \(c_t/n_t\) is unchanged. More generally, multiplicative rescaling that affects numerator and denominator coherently does not change the normalized memory content. This is the mechanism by which the normalizer removes an overall multiplicative "mass" that would otherwise cause \(c_t\) to grow or shrink uncontrollably; the model's readout \(\tilde h_t = c_t/n_t\) depends only on relative weights among stored items, not on their absolute scale.

Second, gradient stabilization: because the readout is a normalized average, gradients that flow back through the ratio inherit the normalization. Writing \(u_t = c_t\) and \(v_t = n_t\), the derivative of the normalized state with respect to any parameter \(\theta\) takes the quotient form
\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\Big(\frac{u_t}{v_t}\Big)
= \frac{v_t\,\partial_\theta u_t - u_t\,\partial_\theta v_t}{v_t^{\,2}}.
\]
Both \(\partial_\theta u_t\) and \(\partial_\theta v_t\) are linear combinations of the same terms (because both recurrences use the same gate factors \(i_t,f_t\)), so the subtraction in the numerator cancels common large multiplicative factors; the denominator \(v_t^{2}\) scales quadratically with the mass, producing a normalization of gradient magnitudes. Intuitively, when a long run of large multiplicative gates would otherwise produce exploding intermediate \(c_t\), the same multiplicative effect appears in \(n_t\) and cancels in the ratio as well as in most of the gradient numerator. Thus the normalizer removes both forward-scale drift and the corresponding gradient scaling that would lead to instability.

Finally, from an interpretive viewpoint this normalization makes retrieval attention-like: the read \(\tilde h_t\) is a convex-like combination of prior values \(z_j\) (weights are nonnegative), which improves numerical behavior and makes the cell content invariant to a global multiplicative mass that might arise from using exponential gates.

# Log-space stabilizer invariance: sketch of proof

sLSTM and mLSTM use exponential gates which can under naive implementation overflow or underflow in floating point. The paper stabilizes exponentials by maintaining a log-space stabilizer state \(m_t\) and replacing gates \(i_t,f_t\) by scaled versions \(i'_t,f'_t\) (Equations in the paper)
\[
\begin{aligned}
m_t &= \max\big( \log(f_t) + m_{t-1},\ \log(i_t) \big),\\
i'_t &= \exp\big( \log(i_t) - m_t \big),\\
f'_t &= \exp\big( \log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t \big).
\end{aligned}
\]
The claim (stated in the paper and proved in the appendix) is that replacing \(i_t,f_t\) by \(i'_t,f'_t\) in the forward-pass recurrences does not change the network outputs, and therefore does not change gradients with respect to parameters. We give a compact sketch of the algebraic invariance argument; the full details follow the same lines and are standard.

Introduce scaled states
\[
\hat c_t \;=\; e^{-m_t}\, c_t,\qquad
\hat n_t \;=\; e^{-m_t}\, n_t .
\]
We show by induction that \(\hat c_t\) and \(\hat n_t\) evolve under the same recurrence as the original states but with the stabilized gates \(i'_t,f'_t\):
\[
\hat c_t = f'_t \,\hat c_{t-1} + i'_t\, z_t,\qquad
\hat n_t = f'_t \,\hat n_{t-1} + i'_t.
\]
Base case: at \(t=1\) the identity follows by direct substitution. Inductive step: assume the identities hold at \(t-1\). Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
\hat c_t
&= e^{-m_t} c_t
= e^{-m_t}\big(f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t\big)\\
&= e^{\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t}\,(e^{-m_{t-1}} c_{t-1})
 \;+\; e^{\log(i_t)-m_t}\,z_t\\
&= f'_t \,\hat c_{t-1} + i'_t\,z_t,
\end{aligned}
\]
and an identical calculation gives the \(\hat n_t\) recurrence. Because \(h_t=o_t(c_t/n_t)\) and the scaling \(e^{-m_t}\) cancels in the ratio,
\[
\frac{c_t}{n_t} = \frac{e^{m_t}\hat c_t}{e^{m_t}\hat n_t} = \frac{\hat c_t}{\hat n_t},
\]
the readout \(\tilde h_t\) and thus \(h_t=o_t\tilde h_t\) are unchanged by the substitution of \(i'_t,f'_t\) and the internal use of log-space stabilization. Since the forward outputs are identical for every setting of the parameters, the mapping from parameters to loss is identical, and therefore all parameter gradients are identical as well. In other words, the stabilizer transforms the internal computation only by a controllable time-dependent rescaling that cancels exactly in the final readout.

The use of \(m_t=\max(\cdot,\cdot)\) is the standard log-sum / log-space trick: it keeps the exponent arguments in a safe range (so that \(i'_t\le 1\) and \(f'_t\le 1\) in many practical cases) and avoids direct computation of very large/small exponentials while preserving the exact arithmetic relationships that determine outputs.

# Mapping covariance updates to retention- and fast-weight views

mLSTM stores a matrix memory \(\mathbf C_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) with the covariance-style update
\[
\mathbf C_t = f_t\,\mathbf C_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf v_t\mathbf k_t^\top,
\qquad
\mathbf n_t = f_t\,\mathbf n_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf k_t,
\]
and retrieval
\[
\tilde{\mathbf h}_t = \frac{\mathbf C_t\,\mathbf q_t}{\max\big\{|\mathbf n_t^\top\mathbf q_t|,1\big\}}.
\]
Unrolling the matrix recurrence gives the closed-form expansion
\[
\mathbf C_t \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^t \Big(\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s\Big)\, i_j\, \mathbf v_j\mathbf k_j^\top
\;+\; \Big(\prod_{s=1}^t f_s\Big)\mathbf C_0.
\]
Multiplying by \(\mathbf q_t\) gives the retrieval as a weighted sum of past values
\[
\mathbf C_t\,\mathbf q_t
= \sum_{j=1}^t \Big(\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s\Big)\, i_j\, (\mathbf k_j^\top\mathbf q_t)\,\mathbf v_j
\;+\; \Big(\prod_{s=1}^t f_s\Big)\mathbf C_0\,\mathbf q_t.
\]
The same coefficients appear in the normalizer:
\[
\mathbf n_t^\top\mathbf q_t
= \sum_{j=1}^t \Big(\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s\Big)\, i_j\, (\mathbf k_j^\top\mathbf q_t)
\;+\; \Big(\prod_{s=1}^t f_s\Big)\mathbf n_0^\top\mathbf q_t.
\]
Therefore the normalized retrieval
\[
\tilde{\mathbf h}_t
= \frac{\sum_{j=1}^t \alpha_{j,t}\,(\mathbf k_j^\top\mathbf q_t)\,\mathbf v_j}
       {\max\big\{\,\big|\sum_{j=1}^t \alpha_{j,t}\,(\mathbf k_j^\top\mathbf q_t)\big|,\,1\big\}},
\qquad
\alpha_{j,t} \equiv \Big(\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s\Big)\, i_j,
\]
is precisely an attention-like normalized weighted average of values \(\mathbf v_j\) where the weight for item \(j\) is the product of: (i) similarity \(\mathbf k_j^\top\mathbf q_t\), (ii) the write strength \(i_j\), and (iii) the future-decay product \(\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s\).

This algebra makes the connections to related views explicit:

- Fast weights / outer-product learners: the term \(i_j\,\mathbf v_j\mathbf k_j^\top\) is exactly an outer-product (a fast weight) created at time \(j\); subsequent retrievals multiply that fast weight by \(\mathbf q_t\) to produce a contribution \((\mathbf k_j^\top\mathbf q_t)\,i_j\,\mathbf v_j\). The forget gate \(f_s\) implements exponential decay of previously written fast weights.

- Retention / linearized attention: the decay factors and the linearity of the matrix update cause each contribution to be attenuated by a scalar product of decays and to enter retrieval linearly. The normalization by \(\mathbf n_t^\top\mathbf q_t\) plays the role of a summed attention mass, just as softmax normalization does in standard attention, but here computed in a compressive online fashion.

- Attention-like behavior without quadratic memory: \(\mathbf C_t\) stores compressed summary information so that the retrieval approximates the attention output without materializing a \(T\times T\) attention matrix; the cost is the \(d\times d\) state and its \(O(d^2)\) updates and multiplies rather than \(O(T^2)\) with \(T\) the context length.

Because the paper assumes a layer-norm before projecting to keys (zero-mean keys), the covariance update aligns with classical results (cited in the paper) that the covariance rule is optimal for maximal separability (maximizing signal-to-noise) in certain binary-retrieval settings. Intuitively, centering keys increases the orthogonality among stored directions and reduces bias that would otherwise accumulate in the matrix memory.

# Parallel-scan formulation for mLSTM without mixing

A crucial implementation advantage of mLSTM is that, unlike sLSTM, it has no hidden-to-hidden mixing and its matrix recurrence is linear in the previous state with a scalar coefficient. This structure permits a parallel-scan (prefix-sum / parallel reduction) formulation that reduces the sequential dependency depth from \(O(T)\) to \(O(\log T)\) on parallel hardware.

Consider the linear recurrence for a generic vector or matrix \(x_t\):
\[
x_t = A_t\,x_{t-1} + B_t,
\]
where here \(A_t\) is a scalar \(f_t\) and \(B_t\) is either the matrix outer-product \(i_t\mathbf v_t\mathbf k_t^\top\) (for \(\mathbf C_t\)) or the vector \(i_t\mathbf k_t\) (for \(\mathbf n_t\)). The standard associative composition for such recurrences is
\[
(A_2,B_2)\circ(A_1,B_1) \;=\; (A_2A_1,\; A_2B_1 + B_2).
\]
This operator \(\circ\) is associative:
\[
\big((A_3,B_3)\circ(A_2,B_2)\big)\circ(A_1,B_1)
= (A_3A_2A_1,\; A_3A_2B_1 + A_3B_2 + B_3)
= (A_3,B_3)\circ\big((A_2,B_2)\circ(A_1,B_1)\big).
\]
If the initial state \(x_0\) is known (often zero), then the state \(x_t\) after \(t\) steps is the second component of the composed pair
\[
(A_t,B_t)\circ(A_{t-1},B_{t-1})\circ\cdots\circ(A_1,B_1).
\]
Thus computing all prefix composed pairs can be done with any parallel-scan algorithm that reduces the sequence with an associative operator. In practice:

- Build the sequence of pairs \((A_j,B_j)\) where \(A_j=f_j\) and \(B_j=i_j\mathbf v_j\mathbf k_j^\top\) (or \(B_j=i_j\mathbf k_j\) for the normalizer).
- Use a parallel-prefix algorithm (tree reduction) to compute prefix compositions \(\big(\prod_{s=1}^t f_s,\;\sum_{j=1}^t (\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s)\,B_j\big)\) for all \(t\) in \(O(\log T)\) depth.

Because the \(A_j\) are scalars, the multiplications \(A_2A_1\) and the left actions \(A_2B_1\) are cheap and well-defined; the operator handles matrix-valued \(B_j\) naturally via the left-scaling \(A_2B_1\). The same scan yields \(\mathbf C_t\) and \(\mathbf n_t\) in parallel. After the scan, the retrieval step computes \(\tilde{\mathbf h}_t = \mathbf C_t\mathbf q_t/\max\{|\mathbf n_t^\top\mathbf q_t|,1\}\) pointwise.

In summary, mLSTM's lack of hidden-hidden mixing reduces its recurrence to an associative linear-affine form that is amenable to standard parallel-scan parallelization, enabling efficient batched GPU implementations that avoid sequential for-loops over time.

# Memory capacity intuitions and trade-offs (scalar vs. matrix)

Scalar memories (the traditional LSTM cell or sLSTM scalar cells) and matrix memories (mLSTM) represent different points in the capacity–complexity trade-off.

Scalar / vectorized scalar cells:
- Each cell stores a scalar \(c_t^{(i)}\) (or a vector of independent scalars across dimensions). Information must be compressed into scalar channels. Retrieval typically reads each scalar (optionally after a nonlinear squashing \(\psi\)), and memory mixing via recurrent weight matrices \(\mathbf R\) can combine scalar contributions across dimensions.
- Capacity scales roughly linearly with the number of cells \(d\). In tasks requiring associative recall (store many key–value pairs and retrieve by similarity), scalar cells require the network to encode keys and values in compressed scalar-coded form and rely on recurrent mixing to implement retrieval. This is expressive but can be inefficient and limited when many independent associations are required.

Matrix memory (\(\mathbf C\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\)):
- Stores outer-product information; a single update \(i_t\mathbf v_t\mathbf k_t^\top\) directly records an association between \(\mathbf k_t\) and \(\mathbf v_t\). The total number of degrees of freedom held in \(\mathbf C\) is \(d^2\), which can represent many more independent associations than \(d\) scalar channels.
- Retrieval by multiplying \(\mathbf C_t\) by a query \(\mathbf q\) produces a sum of values weighted by the key–query similarities, making associative recall natural and efficient.
- Computational and memory cost: each time step requires \(O(d^2)\) storage (the state \(\mathbf C_t\)) and \(O(d^2)\) arithmetic to update and to multiply by \(\mathbf q_t\). This is heavier than scalar cells, which cost \(O(d)\) per time step. The paper emphasizes that \(\mathbf C_t\) is stateful but parameter-free (the projections to keys/values/queries use parameter matrices whose sizes are linear in \(d\)); the cost is in state maintenance and matrix arithmetic rather than parameter count.

Trade-offs in practice:
- For problems that demand many independent associative links (e.g., the large associative recall tests in the experiments), matrix memory provides substantially better capacity and recall accuracy because it preserves pairwise information explicitly.
- For problems where state tracking and sequential mixing are essential, scalar cells with memory mixing (sLSTM) remain powerful because recurrent mixing enables complex state machines; however, their per-time-step representational capacity is smaller.
- mLSTM provides a middle ground: large associative capacity with parallelizability (since no hidden mixing), at the expense of \(O(d^2)\) compute and storage per layer. The paper's design uses a mixture of mLSTM and sLSTM blocks (heads/cells) to balance capacity and mixing where needed.

One additional point from the paper: Transformers can have an exponential effective memory capacity in coding dimension because attention can, in principle, assign distinct attention patterns over an exponentially large set of histories when keys are combinatorially encoded. The mLSTM covariance rule is optimal in certain SNR senses for separable binary retrieval, but the practical capacity of \(\mathbf C\) still scales with \(d^2\), which explains why Transformers outperform other methods on some associative tasks when the coding dimension is limited.

# Expressivity and state tracking: theoretical context

The paper emphasizes that memory mixing (hidden–hidden recurrent connections) confers the ability to implement stateful computations that some parallel models (vanilla Transformers or some SSMs) cannot easily emulate. At an intuitive level, state tracking requires the model to carry internal variables whose update depends on past hidden states and whose future influence depends on the order of past inputs. Recurrent mixing naturally implements such ordered, history-dependent transforms, because the hidden-to-hidden mapping can be an arbitrary function of the previous hidden state (modulated by gates).

Two concrete aspects where expressivity matters:

1. Revising storage decisions. With only additive gating constrained inside \([0,1]\) (as in sigmoid-based LSTM gates), the network can attenuate old memory but not amplify contributions beyond the incoming new value in the same multiplicative manner. Exponential gates \(i_t=\exp(\tilde i_t)\) permit write strengths that are unbounded above and thus allow the model to robustly overwrite or amplify new inputs relative to old ones, provided the normalizer handles overall scale. Combined with memory mixing that routes hidden-contextual information into gate controls, the model gains the ability to perform structured revisions (e.g., in nearest-neighbor search, to increase the weight of a newly found better match so it dominates previous matches).

2. State tracking and formal-language style tasks. Tasks such as parity or certain regular languages require maintaining discrete or structured internal state across arbitrarily long sequences. Recurrent mixing can implement the finite-state transition logic directly in recurrent weight matrices and gating. The paper reports that xLSTM variants that include memory mixing solve tasks that Transformers and some SSMs without mixing fail on, consistent with theoretical results that RNNs with recurrence can implement certain stateful computations that feed-forward attention-only mechanisms cannot express as naturally.

In sum, expressivity is not solely a matter of per-step representational capacity (matrix vs scalar memory) but also of the model's ability to route, combine, and revise stored information in a history-dependent manner. xLSTM's design—adding exponential gating and offering both sLSTM (mixing) and mLSTM (high-capacity matrix memory) components—targets both axes of expressivity.

# Cover’s Theorem and block design rationale

Cover’s theorem (as cited in the paper) provides the classical intuition: a nonlinear mapping into a higher-dimensional space tends to increase the probability that patterns become linearly separable. For sequence models the idea is used to justify architecture choices that first summarize or transform the past into a higher-dimensional embedding before producing a final prediction.

xLSTM uses two residual block backbones aligned with this idea:

- Post up-projection (Transformer-like): the block first applies a recurrent module in the original ambient dimension (summarizing the past nonlinearly), then projects ("up-projects") into a higher-dimensional space for a nonlinearity and finally projects back down. This scheme is used for sLSTM-based blocks in the paper because sLSTM's memory mixing operates naturally at the ambient dimensionality, and the up-projection postprocesses and enriches the representation for better separability.

- Pre up-projection (SSM-like): the block first maps inputs linearly into a higher-dimensional space, then applies the recurrent/nonlinear summarization in the high-dimensional space, and finally projects back. This layout is used for mLSTM-based blocks because matrix memory benefits from operating in higher-dimensional key/value/query spaces: the covariance memory \(\mathbf C\) grows as \(d\times d\) with the internal dimension \(d\), so placing the memory in an expanded space increases its associative capacity and the chance that different histories map to distinct directions that can be separated linearly at readout.

From the perspective of Cover’s theorem, both designs exploit dimensional expansion to improve separability: the pre-up-projection increases the raw capacity of the associative store (good for mLSTM), while the post-up-projection increases the expressive power of the ambient recurrent mixing (good for sLSTM). These choices are engineering decisions guided by the different roles of the two memory mechanisms.

# Equivalence of mLSTM heads and cells without mixing

The paper states that for mLSTM multiple heads and multiple cells are equivalent because there is no memory mixing across heads/cells. We make this precise.

Suppose we split the overall internal dimension into \(H\) disjoint heads, with head \(h\) of size \(d_h\), so the full dimension \(d=\sum_{h=1}^H d_h\). For each head we maintain an independent matrix memory \(\mathbf C^{(h)}\in\mathbb R^{d_h\times d_h}\) and independent projections to keys/queries/values that act only within that head. The per-head update and retrieval are
\[
\mathbf C^{(h)}_t = f_t^{(h)}\,\mathbf C^{(h)}_{t-1} + i_t^{(h)}\,\mathbf v_t^{(h)}(\mathbf k_t^{(h)})^\top,\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf h}_t^{(h)} = \mathbf C_t^{(h)} \mathbf q_t^{(h)} / \cdots,
\]
and the final \(\tilde{\mathbf h}_t\) is the concatenation of the per-head \(\tilde{\mathbf h}_t^{(h)}\).

Equivalently, we can combine the head-specific matrices into a single block-diagonal matrix \(\mathbf C_t = \operatorname{blockdiag}(\mathbf C_t^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf C_t^{(H)})\), combine keys/values/queries by concatenation, and use a single update
\[
\mathbf C_t = \tilde f_t\,\mathbf C_{t-1} + \tilde i_t\,\tilde{\mathbf v}_t\tilde{\mathbf k}_t^\top,
\]
where \(\tilde f_t\) and \(\tilde i_t\) represent the concatenated scalar gates (or a block-wise scalar applied per block) and \(\tilde{\mathbf v}_t,\tilde{\mathbf k}_t,\tilde{\mathbf q}_t\) are the concatenated vectors. Because there is no cross-head mixing, the block-diagonal single-matrix update reproduces exactly the independent head updates and retrievals. Thus multiple independent mLSTM heads are algebraically the same as a single mLSTM with a block-diagonal memory and appropriately partitioned projections.

The practical implication is that when no mixing across memory partitions is required, using multiple heads is primarily an implementation convenience (e.g., to parallelize or to control per-head dimension) rather than an architectural necessity: heads and cells are interchangeable under the no-mixing constraint. This equivalence fails when recurrent mixing across heads is introduced, which is exactly the situation where sLSTM-style mixing becomes distinctively more expressive.

# Concluding remarks

The algebraic structure introduced by exponential gating, gate-mass normalization, and matrix covariance updates gives xLSTM both numerical robustness and modularity between high-capacity associative memory (mLSTM) and stateful memory-mixing (sLSTM). The key mathematical facts that support the design are:

- The normalizer \(n_t\) converts the raw accumulated memory \(c_t\) (scalar case) or \(\mathbf C_t\) (matrix case) into an attention-like normalized readout that removes global multiplicative scale and stabilizes gradients.

- Log-space stabilizers \(m_t\) provide exact arithmetic invariance: they permit computing equivalent, numerically safe gate factors \(i'_t,f'_t\) without changing outputs or parameter gradients.

- The covariance outer-product update directly implements a fast-weight / associative memory whose retrieval reduces to a weighted sum of stored values, with coefficients that are similarity-weighted and decay-moderated; this bridges attention, retention, and fast-weight perspectives.

- Because the matrix recurrence is affine with scalar left-scaling, it admits an associative composition operator and therefore a parallel-scan implementation, making mLSTM parallelizable despite being a recurrent-style memory.

- Trade-offs between scalar and matrix memory are explicit and interpretable: scalar memories are cheaper but lower capacity; matrix memories increase capacity at \(O(d^2)\) cost but remain compressive in sequence length.

These derivations and identities explain why the architectural choices described in the paper combine to yield models that are both expressive and practical for large-scale sequence modeling.
