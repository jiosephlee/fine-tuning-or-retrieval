\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 4: Exponential Gating and Normalization

# Replacing sigmoid with exponential for input and optionally forget gates

In a classical LSTM the gating functions (input, forget, output) use a sigmoid nonlinearity so that each gate value lies in the interval $(0,1)$. That design enforces a bounded, multiplicative modulation of the memory: gates can attenuate but cannot amplify beyond unity. xLSTM modifies this by replacing the input gate (and optionally the forget gate) with an exponential activation. Concretely, for the scalar sLSTM the relevant pieces of the forward pass are written as

$$
c_t \;=\; f_t \, c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, z_t,
$$

with the input and forget gates given by

$$
i_t \;=\; \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad
\tilde{i}_t \;=\; \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t \;+\; r_i \, h_{t-1} \;+\; b_i,
$$

and

$$
f_t \;=\; \sigma(\tilde{f}_t) \quad\text{or}\quad f_t \;=\; \exp(\tilde{f}_t),
\qquad
\tilde{f}_t \;=\; \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t \;+\; r_f \, h_{t-1} \;+\; b_f.
$$

Replacing the sigmoid by an exponential has two immediate algebraic effects. First, $i_t$ becomes strictly positive and unbounded (rather than constrained to $(0,1)$), so it can scale new inputs by arbitrarily large factors. Second, when we unroll the recurrence this multiplicative behavior of exponentials turns sums and products of gates into exponentials of linear combinations of pre-activations. This change creates a family of weights across time that can vary over many orders of magnitude, allowing the memory update mechanism to perform much more aggressive reweighting of past stored items than the conventional sigmoid gate allows.

Note that the output gate $o_t$ remains a sigmoid in the xLSTM designs presented in the paper; the exponential modification is applied to input gating (and optionally to forget gating) because those gates control how much new information enters and how much past information is decayed — exactly the places where revisability and high dynamic range of the gating values are most useful.

# Normalizer state: tracking gate mass for proper normalization

When gates can produce unbounded positive weights, the raw cell state $c_t$ (a weighted sum of past inputs) can grow without bound. xLSTM adds a companion state, the normalizer $n_t$, which mirrors the structure of the cell update but accumulates the gate-mass rather than the gated inputs. For the scalar sLSTM the normalizer is updated by

$$
n_t \;=\; f_t \, n_{t-1} \;+\; i_t.
$$

Unrolling the recurrences gives a very transparent interpretation. Define the (time-dependent) weight for an input at time $s\le t$ as

$$
w_{s,t} \;=\; i_s \prod_{j=s+1}^{t} f_j.
$$

Then

$$
c_t \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^{t} w_{s,t} \, z_s, \qquad
n_t \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^{t} w_{s,t}.
$$

Thus $n_t$ is exactly the total mass of the contributions currently stored in the cell: it is the sum of the same weights that appear in $c_t$ but without multiplying by the content vectors $z_s$. This bookkeeping is what allows xLSTM to form normalized outputs and to use exponentials safely: by recording how much effective weight is present, the network can divide out that scale when producing the exposed hidden state.

For the matrix-memory mLSTM the normalizer becomes a vector that accumulates keyed mass:

$$
\mathbf{n}_t \;=\; f_t \, \mathbf{n}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, \mathbf{k}_t,
$$

and unrolling yields

$$
\mathbf{n}_t \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^{t} \Big( i_s \prod_{j=s+1}^{t} f_j \Big) \mathbf{k}_s.
$$

In mLSTM the normalizer records the aggregated keys weighted by the gate-mass; it plays the role of the denominator when converting the matrix memory's raw retrieval into a scale-invariant value.

# Normalized hidden state using the normalizer

The normalizer is used to produce a normalized hidden representation that is invariant to overall scaling of stored weights. In sLSTM the network computes

$$
\tilde{h}_t \;=\; \frac{c_t}{n_t}, \qquad
h_t \;=\; o_t \, \tilde{h}_t,
$$

so the exposed hidden state is the cell content scaled by the total gate mass. Because $n_t$ is the sum of the same weights used in $c_t$, the ratio $c_t/n_t$ is a weighted average of past inputs:

$$
\frac{c_t}{n_t} \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^t \frac{w_{s,t}}{\sum_{u=1}^t w_{u,t}} \, z_s,
$$

i.e., a convex combination of the past $z_s$ (assuming $f_t \ge 0$ and $i_t > 0$). This normalization keeps the scale of the hidden state stable even when some $i_s$ become very large, and — crucially for revisability — it turns the memory into a mechanism that can focus on one or a few inputs by making their relative weights dominate the denominator.

For mLSTM the retrieval is matrix-vector based. With the matrix memory

$$
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top,
$$

we obtain the candidate output (before output gating) by multiplying $\mathbf{C}_t$ with a query $\mathbf{q}_t$ and normalizing by the scalar $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ (lower‑bounded and absolute-valued for stability):

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \, \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{\,|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,\,1\,\}}.
$$

Unrolling $\mathbf{C}_t$ shows that $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$ equals a weighted sum of the stored values $\mathbf{v}_s$, with weights proportional to $w_{s,t} (\mathbf{k}_s^\top \mathbf{q}_t)$. Dividing by $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ thus converts this into a query‑conditioned normalized average of the value vectors, making the mLSTM retrieval directly analogous (in structure) to attention-like aggregation but implemented inside a recurrent memory cell.

# Stabilizer state in log-space to prevent overflow

Exponentials grow rapidly, and naively computing $i_t=\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ and (if used) $f_t=\exp(\tilde{f}_t)$ can cause numerical overflow or underflow during both forward and backward passes. xLSTM adopts a log-space stabilizer that prevents such overflow while preserving exact functional behavior. The stabilizer maintains a scalar $m_t$ (per memory) defined by

$$
m_t \;=\; \max\big( \log(f_t) + m_{t-1},\; \log(i_t) \big).
$$

Using $m_t$ we define scaled (stabilized) gate values

$$
i'_t \;=\; \exp\big(\log(i_t) - m_t\big) \;=\; \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t),
$$

$$
f'_t \;=\; \exp\big(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t\big).
$$

Because $m_t$ is the maximum of the two arguments, both exponents $\log(i_t) - m_t$ and $\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t$ are less than or equal to zero; therefore $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ lie in the interval $(0,1]$ and never overflow. This is the same numerical idea that underlies the log-sum-exp trick: factor out the largest exponent to keep exponentiated terms in a stable range.

Operationally, xLSTM uses $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ in the cell and normalizer updates in place of the raw $i_t$ and $f_t$. As we will see, this stable computation does not change the exposed normalized hidden states, because the stabilizer factors out a common scale that cancels in the normalization step.

# Stabilized gates and invariance of network output and gradients

Although the stabilized gates $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ differ from the raw exponentials by multiplicative factors, the final normalized outputs of the cell are identical to those obtained with unstabilized gates. The short inductive proof is straightforward and instructive.

Define $s_t \equiv \exp(m_t)$. From the definitions above we have

$$
i'_t \;=\; \frac{i_t}{s_t}, \qquad
f'_t \;=\; \frac{f_t \, s_{t-1}}{s_t}.
$$

Let $c_t$ and $n_t$ denote the cell and normalizer computed with the original (unstabilized) gates, and let $\hat{c}_t$ and $\hat{n}_t$ denote those computed with the stabilized gates $i'_t,f'_t$. Assume inductively that $\hat{c}_{t-1} = c_{t-1}/s_{t-1}$ and $\hat{n}_{t-1} = n_{t-1}/s_{t-1}$. Then

\begin{align*}
\hat{c}_t
&= f'_t \, \hat{c}_{t-1} + i'_t \, z_t
= \frac{f_t s_{t-1}}{s_t} \cdot \frac{c_{t-1}}{s_{t-1}} + \frac{i_t}{s_t} z_t
= \frac{f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t}{s_t}
= \frac{c_t}{s_t}, \\[6pt]
\hat{n}_t
&= f'_t \, \hat{n}_{t-1} + i'_t
= \frac{f_t s_{t-1}}{s_t} \cdot \frac{n_{t-1}}{s_{t-1}} + \frac{i_t}{s_t}
= \frac{f_t n_{t-1} + i_t}{s_t}
= \frac{n_t}{s_t}.
\end{align*}

Consequently the normalized hidden candidate is identical:

$$
\frac{\hat{c}_t}{\hat{n}_t} \;=\; \frac{c_t/s_t}{n_t/s_t} \;=\; \frac{c_t}{n_t}.
$$

Because the exposed hidden state $h_t$ depends on $c_t/n_t$ (and on output gate $o_t$), the forward outputs of the stabilized and unstabilized recurrences are exactly equal for every input and every set of parameters. From this exact functional equivalence it follows that the gradients of any scalar loss with respect to the parameters are also the same: the two computational graphs implement the same mapping from parameters to outputs, so their Jacobians coincide. Thus the stabilizer prevents overflow without changing the network's learned function or its gradient dynamics.

This invariance is important in practice: we get the safety and numerical soundness of scaled computations while keeping the representational advantages of exponential gating.

# Revisability intuition: why exponential gating matters

One of the core motivations for exponential gating is to enable efficient revision of previously stored memory. Consider the scalar sLSTM expression

$$
\frac{c_t}{n_t} \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^t \alpha_{s,t} \, z_s,
\qquad
\alpha_{s,t} \;=\; \frac{w_{s,t}}{\sum_{u=1}^t w_{u,t}},
\qquad
w_{s,t} \;=\; i_s \prod_{j=s+1}^{t} f_j.
$$

This shows that the normalized cell content is a weighted average of past inputs $z_s$ with weights $\alpha_{s,t}$ determined by the input and forget gates. If the task requires replacing an early stored value by a later, better candidate (as in a sequential nearest‑neighbor search), the model must be able to make the later candidate carry much more mass than the earlier ones. With sigmoid gates bounded in $(0,1)$, the relative change in weights across time is limited; if many earlier items have accumulated mass, the new item cannot easily dominate unless the forget gates strongly decay the entire past.

By using exponential input gates, $i_t=\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$, the network can set $i_{\text{new}}$ many orders of magnitude larger than earlier $i_s$, producing weights $w_{\text{new},t}$ that overwhelm previous contributions. In the extreme, exponentials of large pre-activations approximate an argmax: choosing $\tilde{i}_s$ proportional to some similarity measure and taking a large scaling constant yields a distribution sharply focused on the most similar key. In other words, exponential gating plus normalization produces dynamics closely analogous to attention's softmax numerator: a later item with larger pre-activation can effectively “win” and replace previous stored content in the normalized output.

The normalizer is essential in this picture: without dividing by $n_t$, making a single $i_t$ huge would blow up the scale of $c_t$ and make downstream layers sensitive to absolute magnitudes. By forming the ratio $c_t/n_t$ the model realizes revision as a change in the relative weights (the $\alpha_{s,t}$) rather than as raw additive bias, stabilizing learning and enabling aggressive reweighting.

# Initialization and practical stability considerations

Exponential gates give great expressive power but also require careful numerical and practical handling. The paper highlights that forget‑gate initialization must be chosen carefully; more generally, sensible initializations for the pre-activations $\tilde{i}_t$ and $\tilde{f}_t$ avoid early training regimes where exponentials produce extremely large or tiny values. Layer normalization (or similar preprocessing) before projecting into keys and values (used in mLSTM) ensures that inputs have controlled scale and mean, which tends to reduce the risk of extreme initial activations.

The log-space stabilizer described above is the primary tool to guarantee numerical safety: because $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ are computed as exponentials of non-positive arguments, they remain bounded by $1$ during computation and prevent overflow. In mLSTM an additional practical safeguard is applied on retrieval: the denominator $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ is replaced by $\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,1\}$ (the paper uses 1 as a typical lower bound). This avoids dividing by very small scalars that would amplify noise.

Finally, during model design one may choose whether to exponentiate the forget gate or keep it sigmoid. Using $\exp(\tilde{f}_t)$ allows forget factors larger than $1$ (amplifying past memory), whereas $\sigma(\tilde{f}_t)$ constrains forget factors to be attenuating (in $(0,1)$). The choice interacts with task demands and stability: when forget is exponential, the stabilizer is essential to prevent runs of multiplicative growth; when forget is sigmoid, this particular source of growth is limited, but the model still benefits from exponential input gating for revisability.

# Compatibility with input- and hidden-dependent gating

Crucially, exponential gating in xLSTM is fully compatible with the usual input- and hidden-dependent gate parameterizations inherited from LSTM. The generalized pre-activation for a gate keeps the familiar form

$$
\tilde{i}_t \;=\; \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t \;+\; r_i \, h_{t-1} \;+\; b_i,
$$

so gates can be driven by both the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$. The exponential nonlinearity is applied to $\tilde{i}_t$ (and optionally to $\tilde{f}_t$), and the stabilizer uses the logarithms of the resulting gate values. All of the algebraic steps of stabilization and normalization commute with this dependence: the same inductive invariance argument holds when gates are functions of $h_{t-1}$, because the stabilizer and the subsequent scaling are computed deterministically from the same gate values. In other words, the architectural choices that make LSTM powerful — input dependence, hidden dependence, and learnable biases — are preserved; we only change the output nonlinearity and add numerically safe normalization to unlock broader dynamic range and revisability.

This compatibility extends to multi‑cell and multi‑head variants described in the xLSTM family: each cell or head computes its own stabilized gates and normalizer, and mixing (via recurrent connections in sLSTM) or parallelized retrievals (in mLSTM) proceed with the same stabilized, normalized quantities.

Summary

Exponential gating transforms the LSTM update weights from bounded factors into positive, unbounded weights. To make this change practical and numerically robust, xLSTM introduces the normalizer state that records total gate mass and a log-space stabilizer that prevents overflow by factoring out a running scale. Together these mechanisms let the model realize normalized weighted averages of past inputs where a single item can be made to dominate (enabling revisability), while guaranteeing identical forward outputs and gradients to a hypothetical unstabilized computation. The resulting design preserves input- and hidden-dependent gating patterns of LSTMs, but extends them with a high‑dynamic‑range reweighting capability that is central to xLSTM’s improved memory and revision behavior.
