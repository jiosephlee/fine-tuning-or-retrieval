\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 12: 15B-Token Comparisons and Ablations

# Methods compared: GPT-3/Llama, Mamba/H3, RWKV, GLA, HGRN/HGRN2, Hyena

This section summarizes the families of models that were included in the 15B-token comparison in the paper and recalls the key structural differences that matter when interpreting the ablations.

Transformers (represented here by GPT-3 and Llama) are built around parallel self-attention layers and residual stacks. Their attention mechanism performs pairwise token interactions and, when implemented naively, has quadratic compute and memory cost in the context length. Transformers are the dominant architecture for large language models and provide a useful baseline because they scale well with parameters and data when trained with standard residual/pre-LayerNorm backbones.

State Space Models (SSMs) such as H3 and Mamba are an alternative family that aim to retain long-range dependencies with linear complexity in the context length. SSMs replace attention with linear state updates (often diagonalized or structured) that can be implemented with near-linear or linear-in-time kernels. In the comparisons they serve as the leading non-attention competitors that emphasize long-context efficiency.

Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) in this comparison include variants such as RWKV and the HGRN family (HGRN, HGRN2). These methods inherit recurrence and gating ideas from classical RNNs / LSTMs but adapt them to be competitive in large-language-model settings. RWKV, for example, is a recurrent design specifically engineered to compete with Transformers while maintaining linear computational complexity.

Linear or approximate-attention methods such as GLA (Gated Linear Attention) and Hyena aim to reduce the quadratic scaling of standard attention by either approximating the softmax or replacing it with lower-complexity kernels. They are included because they bridge the conceptual space between full attention and linear-time sequence models.

In the experiments reported for the 15B-token comparison, all models were trained in the same autoregressive setting on 15B tokens from the SlimPajama corpus. Model sizes were matched to a GPT-3-like configuration (approximately 350M parameters: embedding dimension 1024 and 24 residual blocks). Training used mixed precision where applicable; for some methods (RWKV-5/6, GLA, HGRN2), the mixed-precision training did not use the PyTorch automated mixed precision path noted in the paper.

# xLSTM’s validation perplexity advantage and scaling trends

When trained under the same conditions (15B tokens, matched architecture scale), xLSTM achieved lower validation perplexity than the other methods in the study. The paper reports that xLSTM outperforms all compared baselines — Transformers, SSMs, RNNs, and linear-attention variants — on the validation set perplexity for the matched 350M-scale models. This result is presented as the core quantitative outcome of the 15B-token comparison.

Beyond the single-point comparison, the paper examines scaling behavior by plotting validation perplexity versus model size (a standard scaling-law style plot). The reported trend for xLSTM shows the same qualitative power-law scaling regime as the other methods but with a favorable offset: as models grow, xLSTM maintains an advantage in perplexity. The paper summarizes this as an indication that xLSTM will continue to be competitive at larger parameter scales.

Interpreting these trends requires attention to the structural changes in xLSTM. The architecture change is not a single trick but a combination: (i) recasting LSTM-style recurrence into modern residual/pre-LayerNorm blocks and up-projection backbones, (ii) introducing exponential gating (sLSTM), and (iii) introducing matrix (covariance) memory (mLSTM). The validation advantage and the preserved scaling trend indicate that these changes interact well with standard large-scale training recipes and do not merely provide a small-data or toy advantage.

# Ablation path: vanilla LSTM → residual blocks → up-projection

To identify which architectural and algorithmic modifications drive the improvement from a vanilla LSTM to xLSTM, the authors perform a staged ablation: they progressively morph a vanilla LSTM architecture into the full xLSTM architecture and measure the effect of each change.

The first step is to place LSTM layers inside modern residual/pre-LayerNorm backbones. Vanilla LSTMs are often used as standalone recurrent layers. Injecting LSTMs into residual stacks with pre-LayerNorm improves training stability for deep networks and aligns the LSTM-based design with contemporary LLM construction. This change alone improves performance because residual connections and layer normalization facilitate optimization and deepen effective representational power.

The second step introduces an up-projection (and its mirror down-projection) around the recurrent block. There are two variants of this motif mentioned in the paper: a post up-projection (map into a higher-dimensional space after the recurrent summary) and a pre up-projection (map into a high-dimensional space before the recurrent summary). The intuition, drawn from Cover’s Theorem, is that non-linear summaries in higher-dimensional embeddings make different history patterns easier to separate linearly. For sLSTM blocks the paper uses post up-projection; for mLSTM blocks the pre up-projection is preferred because the matrix memory benefits from operating in higher-dimensional key/value/query spaces. Adding these projection steps gives a measurable benefit in the ablation chain because it increases the capacity of each block to separate histories and represent fine-grained contexts.

The final steps are the algorithmic changes inside the recurrent cell itself: (a) replacing bounded sigmoid gates for some gates by exponential gates (sLSTM idea) and (b) replacing scalar memory cells by matrix memories with covariance updates (mLSTM idea). The ablation results reported in the paper show that both of these last two changes yield further reductions in validation perplexity. In other words, the best-performing configuration is not simply the modernized training backbone nor a single cell change; the gains accumulate across these modifications.

# Adding exponential gating: effects on performance

Exponential gating is the first of the two core changes from LSTM to xLSTM. In the scalar sLSTM variant the input gate (and optionally the forget gate) is implemented with an exponential activation rather than a sigmoid. Concretely, the sLSTM forward pass includes the familiar additive memory update

$c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t,$

but with the input gate defined as

$i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$

and the normalizer state tracking the cumulative gate mass

$n_t = f_t \, n_{t-1} + i_t.$

The hidden output is then normalized as

$\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t,$

and $h_t = o_t \, \tilde{h}_t$ where $o_t$ is an output gate (sigmoid). Because $\exp(\cdot)$ is unbounded above, exponential gating gives the model a much larger dynamic range than a sigmoid gate that is restricted to $(0,1)$. This larger range makes it possible to “weight” contributions at different times with a multiplicative factor that can grow or shrink more flexibly over sequences. Intuitively, exponential gates let the model store or emphasize certain inputs more strongly than would be possible with a sigmoid gate, and the normalizer $n_t$ keeps the readout stable by keeping track of the total weight mass that influences $c_t$.

Unbounded exponentials can, however, cause numeric overflow or extremely skewed dynamics. To address this, the sLSTM introduces a stabilizer state $m_t$ and computes numerically stable scaled gates via:

$m_t = \max(\log f_t + m_{t-1}, \log i_t),$

$i'_t = \exp(\log i_t - m_t),$

$f'_t = \exp(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t).$

These stabilized gates $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ replace the raw exponentials in arithmetic, preserving exact output while maintaining numerical stability.

From a performance perspective the ablation studies indicate that exponential gating contributes substantially to the xLSTM improvements. Functionally, exponential gating addresses one of the classical LSTM limitations called out by the authors: the inability to revise storage decisions. With exponential gates and the accompanying normalizer, the model can reallocate attention to past stored information more effectively when new evidence arrives — a capability demonstrated in synthetic “nearest neighbor search” tasks and formal language tasks where revisability and state tracking are essential. In the 15B-token ablation chain, adding exponential gating (after modernizing the backbone and projections) yields a clear perplexity improvement, showing that this mechanism is beneficial at scale for natural language modeling, not only for small synthetic tasks.

# Adding matrix memory: effects on performance

The second major modification is a change in the memory representation: the mLSTM replaces scalar cell states by a matrix-valued memory $\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. The mLSTM stores key-value pairs and updates its memory using a covariance (outer-product) learning rule:

$\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \, \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \, \mathbf{v}_t \, \mathbf{k}_t^\top,$

where $\mathbf{k}_t$ is the key vector, $\mathbf{v}_t$ is the value vector, and $i_t$ and $f_t$ are the input and forget gates (with exponential gating optionally applied to $i_t$). The normalizer for the matrix memory is vector-valued:

$\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \, \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \, \mathbf{k}_t.$

Retrieval at time $t$ is performed by multiplying the memory with a query vector $\mathbf{q}_t$ and normalizing by the projection of the normalizer onto the query:

$\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \dfrac{\mathbf{C}_t \, \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1\}},\qquad
\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t.$

This covariance/outer-product update is closely related to older associative memory formulations (Bidirectional Associative Memories) and to modern “fast weight” or fast-learning mechanisms. It is well suited to storing many key-value associations and retrieving them by content (via $\mathbf{q}_t^\top \mathbf{k}_s$ interactions).

In the experiments the matrix memory produces clear benefits for tasks that require high memory capacity or that hinge on retrieving rare or specific content. The paper points to a “Rare Token Prediction” failure mode for scalar LSTMs — those models have to compress information into scalar cells and thus struggle with rare tokens. The mLSTM’s matrix memory increases representational capacity so that rare tokens and associative recall tasks are handled more effectively. In the specialized associative-recall experiments (the Multi-Query Associative Recall task), xLSTM variants that include mLSTM blocks outperform other non-Transformer models and narrow the gap to Transformers.

Computationally, the matrix memory is heavier: it requires $d \times d$ matrix updates and retrievals. However, because mLSTM abandons the hidden-to-hidden memory mixing (no recurrent hidden-hidden matrices across cells), the mLSTM recurrence can be entirely parallelized across time, making large-batch GPU implementations feasible. The paper reports that, in practice, these matrix operations are parallelizable and have only a minor effect on wall-clock time when implemented efficiently.

# Gate mechanism ablations: learnability and input dependence

Gating is central to all of these recurrent designs, and the authors explicitly ablate different gate variants to understand their contribution. The ablation experiments modify whether gates are learnable or fixed, whether they depend only on hidden state or also on the current input, and whether exponential activation is used.

The high-level conclusion drawn in the paper is straightforward: having each gate be learnable and influenced by the input provides an incremental positive effect on performance. In other words, more flexible gating — where the gate activations are parameterized and can react to the current input vector $\mathbf{x}_t$ — consistently improves perplexity in the ablation chain. This observation aligns with intuition: input-dependent gating allows the model to immediately respond to novel or salient tokens; learnable gating allows the model to adjust the degree of persistence or overwrite in a data-driven way rather than relying on fixed hand-designed gates.

While the paper does not publish every numeric outcome for every gating configuration in the main text, it emphasizes that the gating design matters and that the combination of input-dependent, learnable gates with the exponential activation and normalizers is part of the reason the full xLSTM outperforms simpler gate designs.

# Interpreting where gains come from

Putting together the pieces from the comparisons and the ablations gives a coherent interpretation of why xLSTM improves validation perplexity at the 15B-token training scale.

First, modernizing the LSTM into residual/pre-LayerNorm stacks with projection up/down increases representational capacity and stabilizes optimization. This step aligns LSTM-style recurrence with the proven scaffolding used by large Transformer models and is responsible for a non-trivial portion of the gap closed to state-of-the-art.

Second, exponential gating plus the normalizer addresses a structural limitation of classical LSTM: the inability to revise storage decisions robustly. Exponential gates give a wider dynamic range and, combined with the normalizer, allow stable accumulation and reweighting of past inputs. This improves state-tracking tasks and the ability to override or reinforce earlier stored content as new evidence arrives.

Third, the matrix covariance memory increases raw storage capacity and changes retrieval from a scalar readout to a structured matrix-vector operation. This boosts the model’s ability to memorize and retrieve content-rich items (for example, rare tokens or specific key-value pairs), which is important in natural language where informative signals are often distributed and sparse.

Fourth, the architectural choice about where to place the recurrent unit relative to an up-projection matters: mLSTM benefits from operating in higher-dimensional spaces (pre up-projection), while sLSTM integrates well with post up-projection. The combination — mixing mLSTM and sLSTM blocks in a residual stack — lets the model have both high capacity associative memory and the ability to do structured state tracking with memory mixing.

The ablation chain in the paper shows that none of these components alone is sufficient to achieve the reported best performance: the final gains are cumulative. This explains why xLSTM beats the other model families at matched scale: it combines representational depth (residual stacks and projections), flexible gating for revisability, and high-capacity associative memory — all trained with standard large-scale recipes.

# Lessons for practitioners

The experimental findings and ablations in the paper suggest a number of practical recommendations for practitioners building or evaluating large sequence models.

If you want an RNN-style model that competes with Transformers at language modeling scale, consider a hybrid approach: put modernized recurrent cells into pre-LayerNorm residual blocks and use up-/down-projections to increase within-block capacity. This modernization is not optional — the ablation shows it materially improves optimization and final perplexity.

Use exponential gating cautiously but usefully. Replace selected sigmoid gates with exponential activations (for example, the input gate) but include numerical stabilization (the $m_t$ stabilizer and normalized readout $n_t$) so that training and inference remain numerically safe. Exponential gating enables revisability of stored content and gives the model a larger effective dynamic range for storage weights.

If your task requires high-capacity associative memory (for example, many distinct key-value associations, frequent rare-token modeling, or extreme associative recall), implement a matrix memory like mLSTM. Be prepared for extra $d\times d$ computation and plan GPU parallelism accordingly. In return you get retrieval by content and a robust mechanism for storing vector-valued content.

For mixed workloads (where both state tracking and high-capacity retrieval are important), consider mixing sLSTM and mLSTM blocks in a residual stack (the paper uses notation like xLSTM[7:1] to indicate block ratios). This allows you to keep memory mixing (which sLSTM enables) while leveraging mLSTM’s capacity where parallelism and storage are most helpful.

Be mindful of implementation trade-offs. mLSTM is parallelizable and fits well in vectorized GPU computing, but it multiplies the raw compute per layer. sLSTM retains hidden-hidden memory mixing, which makes it more expressive for state-tracking but harder to parallelize; in the paper the authors implemented a custom CUDA kernel to make sLSTM efficient (reported as less than two times slower than the parallel mLSTM implementation). Also, initialization choices matter (the paper lists careful forget gate initialization as a practical concern).

Finally, the paper’s results show that design choices validated on small synthetic tasks translate to large-scale language modeling when combined with modern backbones and training procedures. The combination of modern residual scaffolding, exponential gating, and matrix memory is what produced the consistent perplexity advantage at 15B tokens. Thus, when exploring new recurrent or hybrid architectures, evaluate them in a staged ablation that mirrors the paper’s path: backbone modernization, projection strategy, gating family, and memory representation.
