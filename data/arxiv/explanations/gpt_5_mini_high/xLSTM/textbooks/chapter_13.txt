\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 13: xLSTM as a Large Language Model (300B Tokens)

This chapter summarizes and explains the experiments and findings reported for xLSTM when scaled and trained as a Large Language Model (LLM) on $300\ \mathrm{B}$ tokens from SlimPajama. It focuses on how xLSTM behaves as an LLM: how well it extrapolates to longer contexts, how its validation perplexity compares across model sizes and to other recent architectures, how it performs on downstream reasoning tasks, how it generalizes across hundreds of text domains, and how it behaves at inference time in latency and throughput. Throughout, the chapter ties these behaviors back to the architectural decisions of xLSTM (exponential gating, scalar and matrix memory variants, and residual stacking) as discussed in the paper.

# Length extrapolation from 2k to 16k context

A central practical question for any LLM is how well a model trained with a particular context length can handle longer contexts at inference time. In the xLSTM experiments, all models compared were trained with context length $2048$ and then evaluated on longer context lengths up to $16384$. This is an $8\times$ extrapolation in context length, a meaningful stress test for long-range retrieval and for architectures that compress past information into internal memory.

Empirically, xLSTM models (in particular the large-scale xLSTM variants trained on $300\ \mathrm{B}$ tokens) maintain low perplexity as context length grows from $2048$ to $16384$, whereas several comparator models degrade more rapidly. The paper reports that xLSTM "maintain low perplexities for longer contexts" in contrast to other methods. This superior extrapolation appears consistently in the 1.3B-sized models that were examined for this experiment.

Intuitively, this robustness to longer contexts is linked to the memory mechanisms inside xLSTM. The mLSTM variant uses a matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t$ and a normalization state $\mathbf{n}_t$ so that retrieval is computed as
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\left\{ |\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,\,1\right\}},
\]
with gates controlling decay ($f_t$) and write strength ($i_t$). The normalizer keeps track of the cumulative gate strengths and is used to stabilize retrieval magnitudes. Because xLSTM’s memory is compressive and governed by gates, it aggregates past information in a way that generalizes beyond the training context length, enabling reasonable retrieval even when evaluating much longer sequences. Additionally, sLSTM’s exponential gating and memory mixing support state-tracking behavior, which helps when longer sequences require preserving and updating structured state rather than re-attending to every token.

# Perplexity across sizes vs. Llama, Mamba, RWKV

To assess xLSTM as an LLM across model sizes, the paper trained model families of sizes $125\ \mathrm{M}$, $350\ \mathrm{M}$, $760\ \mathrm{M}$, and $1.3\ \mathrm{B}$ on the same $300\ \mathrm{B}$ token corpus and compared validation perplexities to representative methods from other families: Llama (Transformer), Mamba (State Space Model), and RWKV-4 (RNN-family). Across those sizes, both xLSTM variants reported in detail — xLSTM[$1$:$0$] (all mLSTM blocks) and xLSTM[$7$:$1$] (mostly mLSTM with some sLSTM) — achieved the best validation perplexities at every model size. The paper explicitly states that “both xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1] are the best models for all model sizes with respect to the validation set perplexity.”

This outcome is consistent with two architectural gains in xLSTM emphasized earlier in the paper. First, exponential gating (input and optionally forget gates with exponential activations, stabilized by auxiliary states) gives the model a more flexible and stronger way to control how new information is written and how old information is decayed or reweighted. Second, the matrix memory of mLSTM increases storage capacity beyond scalar cell states, improving the model’s ability to represent and recall detailed information (including rare or high-dimensional signals) that can reduce next-token prediction error. Ablation experiments reported in the paper attribute the strong performance improvements to both exponential gating and the matrix memory components.

# Downstream tasks (e.g., common-sense reasoning) results

Besides next-token perplexity, the paper evaluated xLSTM models on downstream tasks that measure common-sense reasoning. For the family of sizes listed above, xLSTM models attained superior performance on most of these downstream tasks compared to the selected baseline models. The paper summarizes that “in the vast majority of tasks and across all model sizes xLSTM is the best method,” with one noted exception: on the ARC benchmark (a particular question-answering common-sense dataset), Mamba was sometimes the best performer.

Why might better next-token modeling translate into improved downstream reasoning performance? The paper argues (and shows via experiments) that xLSTM’s combination of higher-capacity memories and stronger gating leads to internal representations that better summarize and separate histories. A residually stacked architecture that nonlinearly maps histories into higher-dimensional spaces (motivated by Cover’s theorem) combined with the memory mechanisms enables xLSTM to form richer state representations useful for reasoning tasks that depend on tracing or composing information across many tokens.

# PALOMA: per-domain perplexity and win rates

A stress-test in the paper is evaluation on PALOMA, a large set of text domains (571 domains ranging from news outlets to specialized Reddit communities). For each domain, next-token prediction perplexity was measured and models compared on a per-domain basis.

Two concrete win-rate statistics are given for xLSTM[$1$:$0$] versus other methods: xLSTM[$1$:$0$] had a lower perplexity than Mamba on $568$ out of $571$ domains ($99.5\%$), a lower perplexity than Llama on $486$ out of $571$ domains ($85.1\%$), and a lower perplexity than RWKV-4 on $570$ out of $571$ domains ($99.8\%$). The paper also reports that xLSTM[$1$:$0$] outperforms xLSTM[$7$:$1$] on the PALOMA language tasks (i.e., the pure mLSTM variant was slightly better on this very broad per-domain evaluation).

These per-domain win rates emphasize that xLSTM’s gains are not limited to a narrow set of domains or styles; rather, the architecture provides broadly better next-token predictions over a highly diverse collection of text distributions. The paper links some of this robustness to the matrix memory’s higher capacity (which was shown earlier to help rare-token prediction), and to the gating and normalization mechanisms that stabilize retrieval across varied inputs.

# Scaling-law fits and offsets across model families

The study also analyzed how model performance scales with model size, following the power-law scaling methodology used in prior works. The paper reports that all evaluated model families exhibit a similar power-law scaling behavior (i.e., validation loss/perplexity decreases as a power of model size), but with different offsets between families. Concretely, xLSTM shows a consistent offset advantage: at matched parameter counts xLSTM attains lower perplexity than the SSM and Transformer baselines studied.

The paper summarizes this qualitatively by saying that “all models share a similar scaling behavior but with different offsets. RWKV performs worst, followed by Llama and Mamba. xLSTM is better than Mamba with a similar margin to Mamba as Mamba has to Llama.” In other words, when performance is plotted against model size on log–log axes, the slopes are similar (the scaling exponent is similar across families) but the curves are vertically displaced; xLSTM’s curve lies below (better) the others. This implies that, extrapolating those fitted power laws, larger xLSTM models are expected to remain competitive or superior to the other families at larger scales, given similar training data regimes.

# Generation latency: linear vs. quadratic scaling

Inference latency for text generation is a critical operational metric. The paper measured text generation time for $1.3\ \mathrm{B}$-parameter models of xLSTM, Mamba, Llama, and RWKV implementations (the latter from HuggingFace). For these experiments the generation workload used batch size $1$ with a pre-fill of $16$ tokens (a setting chosen to be favorable to Transformer-style models with key-value caches). The paper reports that xLSTM and the other recurrent models (Mamba and RWKV-4) displayed linear scaling of generation time with context length, whereas the Transformer (Llama) exhibited quadratic scaling.

This difference follows directly from algorithmic complexity: full Transformer attention is quadratic in context length because each new token’s attention can depend on dot-products with all previous tokens, whereas recurrent or linear-memory mechanisms like mLSTM maintain a compressed state and only perform updates whose cost grows linearly (or stays constant per step). The paper’s measurements confirm that, in wall-clock time, xLSTM’s generation cost scales linearly as context length grows, while Llama’s cost grows roughly quadratically, making xLSTM relatively more efficient for very long contexts.

The experimental setup also notes tooling caveats: at the time of the experiments, full cache compilation of the Transformer model and compilation of the Mamba model with \texttt{torch.compile} did not work; Llama used a static key-value cache for the Transformer to speed decoding. Despite these practicalities, the qualitative conclusion holds: the recurrent/compressive-memory families (including xLSTM) are more favorable for long-context, latency-sensitive generation workloads.

# Decoding throughput gains from constant memory

Throughput in decoding (tokens per second) depends not only on per-step computation but on how memory scales with batch size and context length. Because xLSTM’s memory is compressive and independent of sequence length (the recurrent state or matrix memory does not grow with time the way a full attention cache does), xLSTM can use much higher batch sizes before running into memory limits on a GPU. The paper reports that, in throughput measurements, xLSTM achieves the highest decoding throughput among the compared models because it can run with larger batch sizes while using constant memory for the context.

The throughput experiments varied batch sizes and Transformer prefill settings; the paper states that xLSTM “can use much higher batch sizes than Llama due to its constant memory and thus achieves the highest throughput.” Practically, this means that for server deployments that benefit from batching many requests, or for applications where many parallel decode streams are needed, xLSTM’s memory scaling enables better utilization of hardware and thus higher sustained decoding rates.

# Implications for deployment at scale

Taken together, the experiments paint a coherent story about xLSTM for production LLM deployment. Strengths highlighted in the paper include:

- Robust long-context extrapolation: models trained at context length $2048$ perform well up to $16384$, enabling longer-context use without retraining on much longer sequences.

- Strong validation perplexity and downstream performance: across tested sizes, xLSTM variants achieved the best validation perplexities and generally the best common-sense reasoning performance compared to representative Transformer, SSM, and RNN baselines.

- Broad per-domain generalization: on PALOMA’s $571$ domains xLSTM[$1$:$0$] dominated most domains by large margins in win rates versus the baselines.

- Favorable scaling laws: xLSTM exhibits similar power-law exponents but a better offset, implying competitive advantage at larger parameter scales when trained on comparable data.

- Inference advantages: linear generation-time scaling and constant memory for context enable lower latency for long-context generation and much higher decoding throughput due to larger possible batch sizes.

However, the paper also lists limitations and practical caveats that matter for deployment decisions. Notably, the mLSTM matrix memory involves $d\times d$ matrix updates and retrievals, which are computationally heavier than scalar cell updates and currently result in higher computation cost (the implementation in the paper was about $4\times$ slower than FlashAttention at the time of experiments). The sLSTM’s memory mixing (recurrent hidden-to-hidden connections) is not parallelizable in the same way; although the authors developed a fast CUDA kernel for sLSTM, it remains somewhat slower than the parallel mLSTM implementation (reported as typically less than $2\times$ slower). Other practical concerns include careful initialization of forget gates and the potential for the matrix memory to become overloaded if context length grows without bound (the paper reports no observed issue up to $16\ \mathrm{k}$). Finally, the authors note that the large-scale experiments reported did not exhaustively optimize hyperparameters and implementations; further engineering and tuning could improve both performance and latency.

In short, xLSTM combines improved model capabilities (memory capacity and gating) with algorithmic advantages (linear time, constant memory for context) that make it an attractive candidate for large-scale LLM deployment, particularly in settings where long contexts and high decoding throughput are required. At the same time, practitioners must weigh the current computational overheads of matrix-memory operations and remaining implementation optimization work before replacing highly optimized Transformer stacks in production.
