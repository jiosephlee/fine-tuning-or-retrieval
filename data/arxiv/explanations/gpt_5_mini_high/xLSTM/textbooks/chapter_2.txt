Chapter 2: LSTM Fundamentals

# Recurrent computation: cell state and hidden state

At the core of the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) is a two-part recurrent state that is updated at every time step: the cell state and the hidden state. The cell state, usually denoted $c_t$, acts as the network’s internal memory that carries information forward through time. The hidden state, usually denoted $h_t$, is the gated output derived from that memory and is the signal propagated to other layers or used as input at the next time step.

The canonical, compact form of these two updates is
$$
c_t \;=\; f_t \, c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, z_t,
\qquad
h_t \;=\; o_t \, \psi(c_t).
$$
Here $z_t$ is the new candidate content (the “cell input”) computed from the current input and the previous hidden state, while $i_t$, $f_t$, and $o_t$ are multiplicative gates (input, forget, and output gates, respectively) that control how information flows into, through, and out of the cell. The cell state $c_t$ therefore accumulates information over time in an additive way (the first term carries forward past memory, the second adds new information), and the hidden state $h_t$ is a gated, often squashed, readout of that memory.

This two-tier structure separates storage (the cell state) from output (the hidden state). As a result, the cell can maintain long-lived information internally while the hidden state provides a flexible, gated interface to other parts of the network.

# Constant Error Carousel: additive updates to preserve gradients

One of the key conceptual innovations of the LSTM is the so-called constant error carousel, embodied in the additive update for the cell state:
$$
c_t \;=\; f_t \, c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, z_t.
$$
This additive pathway — the direct carry of $f_t c_{t-1}$ plus an additive input contribution — was introduced specifically to address the vanishing gradient problem that plagued earlier recurrent neural networks. Recurrent architectures that propagate information purely by repeated multiplicative interactions tend to shrink gradients exponentially over many time steps; the additive update provides a path along which gradients can flow with much less attenuation, especially when the forget gate $f_t$ is close to one.

Intuitively, the constant error carousel gives the network a persistent memory channel: information placed in $c_{t-1}$ can be preserved (modulated by $f_t$) and remain available many steps later without being repeatedly multiplied by potentially small factors. This is what makes LSTMs capable of learning long-range dependencies in sequences — the network can keep relevant signals in the cell state and access them later through the output gate.

# Input, forget, and output gates and their roles

Gating is the operational mechanism that gives the constant error carousel its flexibility. The three principal gates in a classic LSTM are the input gate $i_t$, the forget gate $f_t$, and the output gate $o_t$. Each gate is a scalar (or vector, in the multi-cell case) between 0 and 1 that modulates a different part of the cell update or readout.

Concretely, gates and the cell input are computed from current inputs and the previous hidden state via affine preactivations followed by nonlinearities. For example:
\begin{align*}
z_t &= \varphi(\tilde{z}_t), \qquad
\tilde{z}_t = \mathbf{w}_z^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_z \, h_{t-1} + b_z,\\
i_t &= \sigma(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad
\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_i \, h_{t-1} + b_i,\\
f_t &= \sigma(\tilde{f}_t), \qquad
\tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_f \, h_{t-1} + b_f,\\
o_t &= \sigma(\tilde{o}_t), \qquad
\tilde{o}_t = \mathbf{w}_o^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_o \, h_{t-1} + b_o.
\end{align*}
In these equations, $\mathbf{x}_t$ is the external input at time $t$, the $\mathbf{w}_\ast$ terms are input weight vectors, the $r_\ast$ terms are recurrent weights from the previous hidden state, and the $b_\ast$ terms are biases. The gate nonlinearities are sigmoids, so each gate takes values in $(0,1)$.

Each gate has a distinct functional role. The forget gate $f_t$ determines how much of the previous cell state $c_{t-1}$ is retained; the input gate $i_t$ controls how much of the new candidate $z_t$ is written into the cell; and the output gate $o_t$ controls how much of the (typically squashed) cell content is exposed as the hidden state $h_t$. Because gates are computed from both the current input and the previous hidden state, the network can make context-dependent decisions about what to remember, what to discard, and what to reveal.

Historically, the forget gate was introduced after the original LSTM formulation and proved critical for learning to reset or drop irrelevant memory contents dynamically. Together, the gates give the LSTM a rich, learnable mechanism for selective storage and retrieval.

# Activations for cell input and state squashing

The raw candidate that is proposed to be written into the cell is produced by a cell-input nonlinearity, often denoted $\varphi$. The cell state itself is typically passed through a squashing nonlinearity, denoted $\psi$, before being emitted as (part of) the hidden state. The hidden-output computation is commonly written as
$$
\tilde{h}_t \;=\; \psi(c_t), \qquad h_t \;=\; o_t \, \tilde{h}_t.
$$
In practice, both $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are commonly set to $\tanh$, which centers the candidate and the squashed cell values around zero and bounds them in $(-1,1)$. The squashing provided by $\psi$ prevents the hidden output from growing without bound and stabilizes the dynamics that the output gate controls.

The gate activations themselves use the sigmoid $\sigma$, where
$$
\sigma(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{1+\exp(-x)}.
$$
Because the gates multiply through the update and readout equations, their bounded $(0,1)$ range makes them effective soft switches: they can nearly shut off a pathway (value near 0) or almost fully pass it (value near 1), and they can interpolate continuously between those extremes.

# From single cell to multi-cell vectorized LSTM

The original LSTM idea was formulated for a scalar memory cell, but practical models use many memory units in parallel. In later formulations multiple scalar memory cells are collected into a vector
$$
\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d,
$$
with corresponding vector-valued hidden states $\mathbf{h}_t\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and gate vectors $\mathbf{i}_t,\mathbf{f}_t,\mathbf{o}_t$. This vectorization permits the model to store and manipulate multiple independent or interacting memory traces simultaneously and to represent richer internal state.

Vectorized LSTMs replace scalar input weights and recurrent weights by matrices and allow vector-valued nonlinearities. For example, the affine components that computed the scalar preactivations become matrix–vector products in the multi-cell form, and the scalar recurrent coefficients generalize to recurrent matrices. Collecting many cells together in a vector enables the network to learn distributed representations that can capture patterns across different dimensions of the internal state.

This scaling from a single scalar cell to a $d$-dimensional cell vector is what allows modern LSTM layers to be substantial computational building blocks: increasing $d$ increases the model’s representational and memory capacity.

# Memory mixing via hidden-to-hidden recurrent matrices

When multiple memory cells are used, the LSTM can mix information across those cells by using recurrent weight matrices that connect the previous hidden state $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ into the preactivations for the cell inputs and the gates. In vector form there are recurrent matrices corresponding to the different pathways, often written as $\mathbf{R}_{z}$, $\mathbf{R}_{i}$, $\mathbf{R}_{f}$, and $\mathbf{R}_{o}$, which connect $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ to the vectors $\mathbf{z}_t$, $\mathbf{i}_t$, $\mathbf{f}_t$, and $\mathbf{o}_t$, respectively.

This “memory mixing” gives the LSTM the ability to implement complex, state-dependent transitions: information stored in one dimension of $\mathbf{c}_{t-1}$ can influence the gate decisions or candidate updates for other dimensions at the next time step. Such interactions are essential for representing and manipulating structured state, for example in tasks that require tracking multiple entities or carrying counts and flags across a long sequence.

An important practical consequence of memory mixing via hidden-to-hidden connections is that the computation is inherently sequential: the dependence on $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ requires processing time steps in order. This sequential dependency complicates parallelization across time and is one of the reasons why LSTM-based architectures are less trivially parallelizable than attention-based architectures.

# Ablation evidence: why all components matter

Empirical studies that systematically remove or alter components of the LSTM memory cell have shown that its various parts are not merely decorative: each plays a functional role that contributes to the model’s effectiveness. As reported in the literature summarized in the review, ablation studies indicate that the combination of the constant error carousel with the gated write/read mechanisms and the nonlinear squashing is important for the network’s ability to learn long-term dependencies and to remain numerically stable.

Removing the forget gate, eliminating gating from inputs, or altering the squashing nonlinearity tends to degrade performance in representative sequence tasks. The interpretive conclusion is that the additive memory pathway, the gating controls, and the nonlinearities together form a tightly integrated mechanism: the additive path preserves gradients, the gates regulate information flow, and the squashing stabilizes representations. Because of this interdependence, meaningful modifications of LSTM architectures need to respect these core design principles or provide alternative mechanisms that replicate their functional effects.

# Known strengths: abstraction learning and long-term dependencies

LSTMs have enjoyed wide adoption because they combine viable long-range memory with flexible, learnable gating. Historically they have been applied successfully across many domains where sequences and temporal structure matter. The reviewed literature lists numerous applications and achievements: LSTMs were used for text generation and handwriting generation, served as core components of sequence-to-sequence translation systems, and were applied to program evaluation, image captioning, source-code generation, and environmental and hydrological modeling tasks such as rainfall–runoff prediction and flood-warning systems. In reinforcement learning, LSTM-based components have been central to high-profile systems such as AlphaStar for StarCraft II and OpenAI Five for Dota 2, and have been applied to control problems like magnetic controllers for nuclear fusion.

Beyond task performance, LSTMs are noted for their ability to learn abstract, semantically meaningful internal representations. Analyses have revealed units that correlate with syntactic number and structure, linguistic features, and even sentiment; such emergent specialized units demonstrate that LSTMs can compress and maintain abstract information in their memory cells.

These strengths — the ability to store and retrieve information across long intervals and to form abstract internal representations — are precisely why LSTMs remained influential even after newer architectures emerged.

Concluding bridge

The LSTM’s two core ideas — the constant error carousel (additive cell updates) and gating (input, forget, output) — are the foundation for its ability to learn long-term dependencies and abstract representations. Modern multi-cell vectorized implementations with recurrent mixing make LSTMs expressive, but they also introduce limitations: sequential memory mixing constrains parallelization, scalar cell representations can limit storage capacity, and the standard gating shapes decisions that are sometimes hard to revise. These observations motivated the extensions and modifications explored in xLSTM, which keep the essential LSTM principles while addressing specific limitations of the classical design.
