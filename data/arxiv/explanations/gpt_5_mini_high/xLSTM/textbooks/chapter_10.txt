\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 10: Experimental Setup and Evaluation Protocols

# Datasets: SlimPajama (15B and 300B tokens)
All main empirical comparisons and scaling experiments for xLSTM reported in the paper use the SlimPajama corpora as the training source. Two dataset scales are used as the backbone for different experimental goals. First, a smaller-scale regime consisting of $15\ \text{B tokens}$ is used for method comparisons, ablation studies, and certain controlled experiments. Second, a large-scale regime of $300\ \text{B tokens}$ is used to evaluate xLSTM in a large-language-model (LLM) setting, to measure extrapolation, downstream-task performance, and scaling behavior at a scale comparable to recent large-model studies. The choice to run both a mid-size ($15\ \text{B}$) and a large ($300\ \text{B}$) training budget permits (a) fair comparisons among many architectures with reasonable compute, and (b) a final assessment of xLSTM as an LLM trained on a large corpus.

Throughout these experiments, held-out evaluation is performed on the SlimPajama validation set (the same corpus family used for training), with next-token perplexity as the primary language-modeling metric. Code and training artifacts used in the experiments are made available by the authors (see the project repository).

# Tokenization and autoregressive training setup
Across the experiments reported, models are trained in the autoregressive next-token prediction setting: at each position the model conditions on past tokens to predict the next token, and the training objective is the standard autoregressive likelihood (evaluated via validation perplexity). The paper explicitly frames comparisons in this autoregressive setting for all methods being benchmarked, so all reported perplexities and downstream evaluations are directly comparable under that training paradigm.

The paper does not prescribe a single tokenizer design detail in the main text; it emphasizes that all methods are trained in the same autoregressive setup on the same SlimPajama splits when directly compared. For generation-time timing comparisons, a decoding experimental setting is described: generation-speed measurements use batch size $1$ with a prefill of $16$ tokens, a prefill setting chosen to be maximally favorable to Transformer-style models that use a cached key–value mechanism.

# Model scales: $125\ \text{M}$, $350\ \text{M}$, $760\ \text{M}$, $1.3\ \text{B}$ and GPT‑3 $350\ \text{M}$-like baselines
The experiments cover a range of model parameter scales so that both small- and mid-sized behaviors and scaling trends can be observed. Explicit model sizes trained and reported in the $300\ \text{B}$ experiments are $125\ \text{M}$, $350\ \text{M}$, $760\ \text{M}$, and $1.3\ \text{B}$ parameters. For the $15\ \text{B}$-token method-comparison suite, the paper includes a direct comparison to a GPT‑3 style Transformer at approximately the $350\ \text{M}$ scale (the paper notes an embedding dimension of $1024$ and $24$ residual blocks for that GPT‑3–like baseline).

xLSTM architectures are defined in terms of residually stacked xLSTM blocks. To describe mixed architectures made from the two new cell types, the paper uses the notation xLSTM[$a$:$b$] to denote the ratio $a/b$ of mLSTM-based versus sLSTM-based xLSTM blocks. For example, xLSTM[$7$:$1$] means that out of eight blocks seven are mLSTM blocks and one is an sLSTM block; with a common total number of $48$ blocks this corresponds to $42$ mLSTM blocks and $6$ sLSTM blocks. Two extreme points used in experiments are xLSTM[$1$:$0$] (pure mLSTM) and xLSTM[$0$:$1$] (pure sLSTM).

Architecturally, xLSTM blocks are embedded into pre-LayerNorm residual backbones, and the authors select different block-level up-projection strategies for the two cell types: mLSTM blocks are used with a pre up-projection residual block (embedding into a higher-dimensional space before the recurrent update), while sLSTM blocks are used with a post up-projection block (nonlinear summary in the original space, then an up-projection). These backbone choices are kept consistent across the experiments comparing cell variants.

# Context lengths for training and extrapolation
Context length management differs by experiment goal. For the large-scale $300\ \text{B}$ training runs, models are trained with a context length of $2048$ tokens. To assess long-context generalization, trained models are then evaluated at much longer contexts: specifically, sequence-length extrapolation tests evaluate models up to a context length of $16384$ tokens for $1.3\ \text{B}$ models. The paper highlights this extrapolation behavior because one of the key questions is whether the xLSTM variants maintain low perplexity as context length grows beyond what they saw during training.

For the synthetic memory and associative-recall experiments, the authors extended existing tasks to contexts up to $2048$ tokens and to larger numbers of stored key–value pairs (see the MQAR description below). The Long Range Arena experiments and other long-context benchmarks are used to complement these controlled extrapolation studies, measuring behavior across a variety of tasks with naturally long inputs.

# Synthetic suites: formal languages, MQAR, Long Range Arena
The paper uses three synthetic (or semi-synthetic) evaluation suites to isolate different model capabilities that are important for language and sequence modeling.

Formal languages and state-tracking tasks. Formal-language tasks (based on and extended from prior work) are used to test state-tracking and long-range structural capabilities. The authors adapted tasks from prior formal-language benchmarks and extended them to multi-length training for extrapolation tests. These tasks emphasize the ability to revise and maintain internal state across a sequence—a capability that the paper argues sLSTM with exponential gating and memory mixing is designed to improve. For the experiments the authors compare two-block architectures (so each model has two residual blocks) across a wide range of models (pure sLSTM, pure mLSTM, mixed xLSTM, Transformers, SSMs, RWKV, Retention, Hyena, LSTM variants), and they report accuracy scaled between $0$ (random) and $1$ (perfect) on the tokens that are relevant to the task. These comparisons demonstrate that architectures without memory mixing (classic Transformers and many SSMs) fail on some of these formal tasks, whereas xLSTM variants that include memory mixing can succeed.

Multi-Query Associative Recall (MQAR). This task directly probes memory capacity: sequences contain many randomly sampled key–value pairs which the model must memorize and later retrieve. The paper extends the MQAR task to make it more challenging: the number of key–value pairs is increased up to $256$ and context length is increased to $2048$ to stress storage and retrieval. The matrix-memory mLSTM is designed precisely for this setting: it stores key–value pairs via an outer-product (covariance) update and retrieves via a matrix–vector multiply, mirroring associative-memory principles. In experiments comparing a range of models (including Transformer baselines such as Llama, SSMs like Mamba, and RNNs such as RWKV variants), Transformers remain the gold standard owing to their attention-style retrieval, but xLSTM[$1$:$1$] performs best among non-Transformer models and shows particularly strong extrapolation behavior. The experiments show that adding an sLSTM block does not reduce memory capacity and can even help at more difficult task settings.

Long Range Arena (LRA). The Long Range Arena benchmark suite measures performance on a variety of long-context tasks (classification and sequence modeling variants). The paper reports that xLSTM demonstrates consistently strong performance across the LRA tasks, indicating that the xLSTM design is robust to different long-context phenomena. Together with the formal-language and MQAR studies, the LRA experiments provide a rounded view of long-context strengths and weaknesses.

# Validation perplexity and downstream task evaluations
Validation perplexity on next-token prediction is the primary scalar metric used to compare language-modeling performance across methods and scales. The paper organizes comparisons in two major experimental regimes.

Method-comparison and ablation regime ($15\ \text{B}$ tokens). Models (Transformers, SSMs, RWKV variants, linear Transformer variants, and xLSTM architectures) are trained on $15\ \text{B}$ tokens from SlimPajama in a consistent autoregressive setting. For the GPT‑3–like Transformer baseline at this scale the authors match an embedding dimension of $1024$ and $24$ residual blocks so that model sizes are comparable. On this $15\ \text{B}$ training budget, xLSTM variants outperform the set of contemporary baseline architectures in validation perplexity. Ablation studies in this regime proceed by stepwise morphing of a vanilla LSTM architecture into full xLSTM: (1) integrating LSTM layers into pre-LayerNorm residual backbones, (2) embedding those layers into a post up-projection residual block, and (3) adding exponential gating and matrix memory. These staged ablations attribute the largest performance gains to the introduction of exponential gating and to the matrix-memory mechanism.

Large-scale LLM regime ($300\ \text{B}$ tokens). For the LLM-style experiments the authors train multiple model sizes ($125\ \text{M}, 350\ \text{M}, 760\ \text{M}, 1.3\ \text{B}$) on $300\ \text{B}$ tokens and evaluate both next-token perplexity and performance on downstream tasks that probe common-sense reasoning. Across model sizes, xLSTM[$1$:$0$] (pure mLSTM) and xLSTM[$7$:$1$] are reported as the best models in terms of validation perplexity. For downstream tasks, xLSTM is the top performer in the majority of evaluated tasks; the paper notes one exception where Mamba sometimes achieves the best score on the ARC task. These combined results indicate that xLSTM scales favorably both in language-modeling loss and in practical downstream metrics.

Scaling-law analysis. The paper also evaluates scaling behavior following power-law fitting approaches in the spirit of prior work (Kaplan et al., Brown et al.). When plotting validation loss against model size, all compared methods follow similar power-law slopes, but xLSTM enjoys a favorable offset (i.e., a systematically lower loss at a given model size) relative to SSMs and Transformers in the presented experiments. This suggests that, under the same data budget, xLSTM architectures will remain competitive as models are scaled further.

Generation-time and throughput measurements. Beyond training-time metrics, the paper reports generation-time scaling and decoding throughput at the $1.3\ \text{B}$ regime. Measured decoding time for recurrent models including xLSTM scales approximately linearly with context length while Transformer decoding time grows quadratically; empirical generation tests use batch size $1$ with a prefill of $16$ tokens. Because xLSTM has constant memory usage with respect to sequence length, it can sustain much larger decoding batch sizes than Transformers and therefore reaches higher maximal throughput in decoding experiments.

# PALOMA: 571-domain language perplexity benchmark
To inspect domain robustness and per-domain language-modeling quality, the authors evaluate all trained models on the PALOMA benchmark, which provides $571$ distinct text domains (ranging from news sites to niche Reddit communities). The metric used is next-token perplexity measured per domain.

Results reported in the paper for $1.3\ \text{B}$-scale models show strong domainwise performance for xLSTM. In particular, xLSTM[$1$:$0$] (pure mLSTM) achieves lower perplexity than several strong baselines on the vast majority of domains: it has a lower perplexity than Mamba on $568$ out of $571$ domains ($99.5\%$), a lower perplexity than Llama on $486$ out of $571$ domains ($85.1\%$), and a lower perplexity than RWKV-4 on $570$ out of $571$ domains ($99.8\%$). Between xLSTM variants, xLSTM[$1$:$0$] performs better than xLSTM[$7$:$1$] on PALOMA, showing that the matrix-memory–dominant configuration is particularly effective for broad-domain next-token modeling in these experiments.

# Training precision and implementation caveats
The paper documents several practical implementation and precision considerations that affected training and evaluation.

Mixed precision training. Most models in the $15\ \text{B}$ and $300\ \text{B}$ experiments were trained with mixed precision to reduce memory footprint and speed up training. However, the authors note that for some methods (specifically RWKV‑5, RWKV‑6, GLA, and HGRN2) the mixed-precision training reported did not utilize PyTorch’s automated mixed-precision machinery. In the $300\ \text{B}$ experiments, RWKV‑5, RWKV‑6 and HGRN2 had precision and stability settings that were only adjusted after training had already begun; this is an important practical caveat when interpreting cross-method comparisons at very large scale.

sLSTM versus mLSTM parallelism and kernel maturity. The two xLSTM cell types have different parallelization and implementation characteristics. mLSTM abandons memory mixing (no hidden–hidden recurrent weight mixing) and therefore admits a parallel reformulation that is amenable to efficient GPU batch computation. However, the mLSTM implementation used by the authors is not as highly optimized as mature attention kernels: the reported mLSTM CUDA kernels are not yet on par with highly optimized FlashAttention implementations and are roughly four times slower than FlashAttention or the scan implementation used in Mamba in the authors’ profiling. By contrast, sLSTM reintroduces memory mixing and therefore cannot be parallelized in the same way; to compensate, the authors implemented a carefully optimized CUDA kernel for sLSTM with register-level memory optimizations so that sLSTM runs typically at less than two times the wall-clock time of the parallel mLSTM implementation. These implementation statuses affect absolute wall-clock training time but do not change the asymptotic algorithmic properties reported.

Compute and memory trade-offs for the matrix memory. The mLSTM cell stores a $d \times d$ matrix memory and applies a $d \times d$ outer-product update and a $d \times d$ matrix–vector retrieval per step. This design increases per-step arithmetic relative to scalar-cell designs. The memory array itself does not add learnable parameters, but the $d \times d$ operations are computationally expensive. The authors point out that these operations are amenable to GPU parallelism and do not grow with sequence length (so peak memory usage is constant in sequence length), but they caution that the computational overhead is nontrivial in practice until the mLSTM kernels are further optimized.

Other practical limitations. The paper documents additional practical limitations discovered during experiments: (i) the forget-gate initialization must be chosen carefully in practice, (ii) because the mLSTM matrix memory is finite and sequence-length independent, extremely long contexts can in principle overload the memory (the authors did not observe a breakdown up to $16\text{k}$ token contexts in their tests), and (iii) the large-language experiments reported are not fully hyperparameter- or architecture-optimized due to the heavy computational budget required—further tuning could improve absolute results.

# Putting it together: reproducibility and recommended evaluation practice
The experimental protocol used in the paper emphasizes fair, apples-to-apples comparisons: all architectures are trained in the same autoregressive next-token framework on the same SlimPajama splits, evaluated primarily with validation perplexity, and supplemented by synthetic tasks that isolate memory and state-tracking capabilities, domain-wise perplexity on PALOMA, long-context extrapolation tests, and downstream-task evaluations probing reasoning ability. The xLSTM[$a$:$b$] notation provides a concise way to describe mixed-block configurations, and the paper keeps backbone choices (pre-LN residual stacking and the per-cell up-projection strategy) consistent to focus comparisons on the effects of the memory structure and exponential gating innovations.

For readers wanting to reproduce or build on these experiments, the authors provide code and model definitions in their public repository; the combination of $15\ \text{B}$ and $300\ \text{B}$ training regimes, the synthetic-task suite (formal languages, MQAR, LRA), and the PALOMA per-domain evaluation together form a comprehensive evaluation protocol for assessing long-context modeling, memory capacity, and domain robustness of sequential architectures.
