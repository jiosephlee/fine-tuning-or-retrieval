\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Why xLSTM, and Why Now?

# From RNNs to LSTMs to Transformers: a brief history

Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) were the natural starting point for sequence modeling because their recurrent structure directly mirrors sequential data: each time step updates a hidden state that summarizes the past. However, vanilla RNNs suffer from the vanishing and exploding gradient problems, which make it hard to learn long-term dependencies during training. The Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) architecture was introduced to address exactly this issue by changing how information is stored and updated across time steps. The core LSTM idea can be written compactly as
$$
c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t,\qquad
h_t = o_t \, \psi(c_t),
$$
where $c_t$ is the cell (memory) state, $h_t$ is the hidden state (the output of the memory), $z_t$ is a proposed cell input, and $i_t$, $f_t$, $o_t$ are the input, forget and output gates respectively. The additive update in $c_t$ — sometimes called the constant error carousel — prevents gradients from decaying exponentially with time and the multiplicative gates learn how to control information flow.

Because of this mechanism, LSTMs became a dominant tool for many sequence tasks: language modeling and text generation, sequence-to-sequence translation, handwriting and image caption generation, reinforcement learning agents, and more. In particular, LSTM ideas formed the core of some of the earliest large language models (LLMs). Yet in 2017 the Transformer architecture, with parallelizable self-attention at its core, introduced a new paradigm. By exposing token-to-token interactions through attention and enabling much better hardware parallelism, Transformers rapidly scaled to billions of parameters and became the dominant architecture for large-scale language modeling.

# LSTM’s enduring strengths and where they fell behind at scale

LSTMs retain several strengths that have kept them relevant in many domains. Their cell-based memory is well-suited for learning and storing abstract, semantic features of sequences. Practically, LSTMs have demonstrated interpretable neurons (for example neurons encoding number, syntax or sentiment), and in many reinforcement learning settings they still outperform newer sequence models. Their constant-error-carousel mechanism gives them an inductive bias for stateful, compressed storage and gradual updates that is useful in many tasks where a compact, persistent memory is needed.

Despite these strengths, the paper highlights three concrete limitations that explain why LSTMs lost out to Transformers at large scale.

The first limitation is an inability to revise storage decisions effectively. In a classical LSTM the cell update is additive but the gates (typically sigmoid activations constrained to $(0,1)$) moderate how much new information is written and how much old information is forgotten. Once a value has been stored, it can be hard to override it later if a stronger or more relevant signal arrives. The paper illustrates this with a Nearest Neighbor Search synthetic task: when the sequence must be scanned and a later element is more similar to a reference, standard LSTMs struggle to revise the stored value; xLSTM’s modifications address this.

The second limitation is limited storage capacity. Typical LSTM memory cells are scalar (or vectorized but still fundamentally scalar-valued per cell), which forces compression of rich information into limited channels. This shows up empirically: in token prediction experiments LSTMs have higher perplexity on rare tokens than on frequent tokens (e.g., on Wikitext-103), indicating limitations in retaining sparse or infrequent information. Increasing the memory dimensionality in a way that supports structured storage and retrieval is therefore attractive.

The third limitation is lack of parallelizability. LSTMs mix memory and hidden states across time through hidden‑to‑hidden recurrent connections; this enforces a sequential computation pattern that blocks efficient parallel execution across timesteps. In modern large-scale training, the ability to parallelize across tokens and exploit highly optimized kernels is a practical necessity.

These three limitations — revisability, capacity, and parallelizability — are the focus of the xLSTM design choices that follow.

# Transformers’ rise via parallelizable self-attention

Transformers succeeded mainly because self‑attention provides a general, parallelizable mechanism to compute interactions among tokens. Attention computes pairwise influences between tokens in the context, which gives the model the expressive power to relate distant parts of the input directly. Crucially, these interactions are expressed as matrix multiplications and reductions that can be implemented efficiently and in parallel on modern accelerators, allowing Transformers to scale to very large models and very long training runs.

However, self-attention comes with costs: the naive attention mechanism is quadratic in context length, and some tasks where explicit stateful tracking or compact memories are helpful may not be naturally matched by attention alone. This is why alternative lines of work (linearized attention, state-space models, and improved recurrent designs) have continued to appear: they aim to recover linear runtime or constant memory in sequence length while keeping strong modeling power. xLSTM enters this space by rethinking LSTM design to preserve its desirable inductive biases while addressing the three limitations described above.

# What xLSTM changes: exponential gating and new memory structures

The xLSTM family is built from two primary changes to the classic LSTM recipe: (1) replacing or augmenting sigmoid gating with exponential gating together with explicit normalization and numerical stabilization; and (2) enlarging and redesigning the memory structure, giving rise to two variants, sLSTM and mLSTM.

Exponential gating replaces the sigmoid nonlinearity (which outputs values strictly in $(0,1)$) with an exponential activation for some gates. Concretely, in the scalar sLSTM the input gate is computed as
$$
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t),
$$
and the forget gate can be either $\sigma(\tilde{f}_t)$ or $\exp(\tilde{f}_t)$. Because the exponential is strictly positive and unbounded above, it changes the role the gates play: gates are now multiplicative positive weights that can exceed one. To make these weights interpretable and to avoid numerical instability, xLSTM introduces a normalizer state that accumulates the effective total weight of inputs, and divides the accumulated cell by this normalizer when producing the hidden output. For sLSTM the key forward equations are
$$
\begin{aligned}
c_t &= f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t,\\
n_t &= f_t \, n_{t-1} + i_t,\\
\tilde{h}_t &= \frac{c_t}{n_t},\qquad h_t = o_t \, \tilde{h}_t.
\end{aligned}
$$
The normalizer $n_t$ records how much total effective weight has been assigned to stored content; dividing by $n_t$ yields a normalized summary (an effective weighted average), which makes the effect of newly arriving inputs relative to the accumulated past explicit and allows later inputs to outweigh earlier ones when their gating weights are larger. This is the mechanism that mitigates the “inability to revise” problem.

Using exponentials raises obvious numerical concerns because exponentials can grow rapidly. xLSTM includes a stabilizer state $m_t$ to prevent overflow. The stabilizer is updated as
$$
m_t = \max\big(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1},\; \log(i_t)\big),
$$
and the gates are replaced by numerically stable versions
$$
i'_t = \exp(\log(i_t) - m_t),\qquad
f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t).
$$
The paper shows that using these stabilized gates in the forward pass leaves the network outputs and parameter gradients unchanged; the stabilization only changes the numerical representation.

The second major change is to expand the memory from scalars into matrices. The mLSTM stores a matrix-valued memory $\mathbf{C}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and uses a covariance-style outer-product update to record key–value pairs:
$$
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\,\mathbf{k}_t^\top,
$$
where $\mathbf{k}_t$ is a key vector and $\mathbf{v}_t$ is the associated value vector to be stored. Retrieval uses a query $\mathbf{q}_t$ and matrix multiplication:
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t\,\mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{\,|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|,\;1\,\}},
\qquad
\mathbf{n}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{k}_t.
$$
Here $\mathbf{n}_t$ is the normalizer vector analogous to the scalar $n_t$ in sLSTM; dividing by $\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|,1\}$ stabilizes the scale of retrieval, avoiding very small denominators. The keys are projected after a layer normalization (so that they have zero mean) and are scaled by $1/\sqrt{d}$ in the projection, following common attention conventions.

The covariance update is not a random design choice: it connects to classic associative memory models (bidirectional associative memories) and to the “fast weights” literature. The paper notes that the covariance update is optimal for maximizing separability (equivalently maximizing signal-to-noise) under reasonable assumptions, and that it matches mechanisms found in Fast Weight Programmers. In the xLSTM architecture the forget gate acts like a decay rate (dampening previous memory) and the input gate acts like a learning rate for the outer-product update. The matrix memory substantially increases storage capacity compared to scalar cells, at the expense of $d\times d$ matrix operations.

# Two variants: sLSTM (scalar memory with mixing) and mLSTM (matrix memory, fully parallelizable)

xLSTM is not a single monolithic model but a family that includes two complementary LSTM extensions.

sLSTM takes the scalar cell idea from classic LSTMs but augments it with exponential gates, the normalizer $n_t$, the stabilizer $m_t$, and a hidden-state normalization $h_t = o_t (c_t/n_t)$. Crucially, sLSTM retains the recurrent hidden-to-hidden connections (the familiar $\mathbf{R}$ matrices) that permit memory mixing across cells: the hidden state $h_{t-1}$ can influence the gates and candidate input $z_t$ at the next step. This memory mixing is powerful: it permits the network to implement state-tracking logic and to route information dynamically between memory cells. sLSTM can also be extended with multiple scalar memory cells and with a multi‑head structure: heads allow memory mixing within each head but not across heads, giving a new architectural knob for controlling how memories interact.

mLSTM, by contrast, swaps scalar cells for a matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t$ and uses the outer‑product (covariance) update described above. To make the model parallelizable across timesteps, mLSTM drops hidden‑to‑hidden recurrence in the memory update (i.e., no memory mixing). Because the memory update and retrieval are written as linear algebra operations on matrices and vectors, they can be reformulated in a parallel way (similar in spirit to linear attention or fast‑weight approaches). Practically, mLSTM trades off i) substantially higher memory capacity and structured retrieval, for ii) higher per-step computational cost (matrix updates and matrix–vector products of size $d\times d$). In xLSTM architectures, multiple heads or multiple cells in mLSTM are effectively equivalent because there is no inter-head memory mixing to control.

Both variants use the exponential gating/stabilization apparatus and both can be combined inside a single network: the xLSTM[ a : b ] notation in the paper denotes the ratio of mLSTM-based blocks to sLSTM-based blocks. For example, xLSTM[7:1] means out of eight blocks seven use mLSTM and one uses sLSTM.

# xLSTM blocks and residual stacking into full architectures

xLSTM models are built by inserting sLSTM or mLSTM modules into residual block backbones and then stacking these blocks residually to form deep networks. The paper adapts two residual block patterns that are common in modern sequence models.

One block style is the residual block with post up‑projection (resembling Transformer feed‑forward patterns): first summarize the past nonlinearly in the original (model) space, then linearly project up into a higher-dimensional space, apply another nonlinearity, and project back down. The other is the residual block with pre up‑projection (resembling some State Space Model designs): first project up into a higher-dimensional space, then perform the nonlinear, stateful summarization there, before projecting back.

The rationale is tied to Cover’s theorem: nonlinearly embedding histories into a sufficiently high-dimensional space increases the chance that different histories become linearly separable, which aids the final linear readout for predicting the next token. For this reason the paper recommends using the post up‑projection block for sLSTM blocks (it summarizes the past in the original space, then lifts to high dimension) and the pre up‑projection block for mLSTM blocks (the matrix memory benefits directly from working in the higher-dimensional projected space).

Finally, architectures use pre‑LayerNorm residual backbones, consistent with contemporary LLM practice. The layering and residual connections make it straightforward to stack many xLSTM blocks and scale the model depth, while the sLSTM and mLSTM modules provide the stateful, memory‑rich core computations inside each block.

# Headline results vs. state-of-the-art Transformers and SSMs

The paper reports a range of experiments that together argue xLSTM is a viable and competitive direction for large-scale sequence modeling.

On synthetic and formal tasks, xLSTM showcases the practical effect of its architectural choices. Exponential gating with memory mixing (sLSTM behavior) enables state‑tracking solutions that Transformer and many State Space Models (which lack recurrent memory mixing) cannot reliably learn; experiments on formal language tasks show that RNNs with stateful memory outperform architectures that cannot mix hidden states across time on certain regular-grammar tasks such as parity. On associative recall problems that demand storing and later retrieving many arbitrary key–value pairs (the Multi‑Query Associative Recall task), the mLSTM matrix memory substantially improves capacity: xLSTM variants (notably xLSTM[1:1]) perform best among non‑Transformer models even as the number of pairs increases to 256 and context lengths reach 2048. In long‑context benchmarks (Long Range Arena), xLSTM also demonstrates consistently strong performance across varied tasks.

In language modeling experiments on real text, xLSTM shows particularly compelling results. When trained on 15B tokens from the SlimPajama corpus and compared to a variety of methods (Transformers like GPT‑style models and Llama, State Space Models like H3 and Mamba, and recurrent methods like RWKV and HGRN variants), xLSTM achieved the best validation perplexity among the compared models. Ablation studies in the paper trace this improvement to two main contributors: the exponential gating mechanism and the matrix memory.

When scaled further — models trained on 300B tokens — xLSTM continued to show advantages. For models trained with context length 2048 and tested up to length 16384, xLSTM maintained substantially lower perplexity at longer contexts than competing methods, indicating better length extrapolation. Across model sizes from 125M up to 1.3B parameters, xLSTM variants (both xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1]) had the lowest validation perplexity compared to RWKV‑4, Llama, and Mamba. On downstream benchmark tasks for common‑sense reasoning and on the PALOMA suite of 571 real‑world text domains, xLSTM[1:0] often achieved the best next‑token perplexity: it had lower perplexity than Mamba on 568/571 domains, lower than Llama on 486/571, and lower than RWKV‑4 on 570/571. These numbers indicate robust performance across a broad set of natural language domains.

Scaling‑law analyses showed similar power‑law behavior across methods but with xLSTM enjoying a better offset: the extrapolation suggests that as parameter count grows the gap in performance is likely to persist in xLSTM’s favor. From an inference perspective, the paper also highlights that xLSTM (like other recurrent models) has linear runtime scaling in context length, and constant memory requirements independent of sequence length; this lets xLSTM achieve higher batching during decoding and therefore higher throughput than Transformer decoders, whose memory grows with context length. In practical measurements at 1.3B scale, xLSTM’s generation time scales linearly with context length and its decoding throughput benefits from the constant-memory property.

Overall, the experimental suite supports the paper’s central message: by addressing LSTM’s three practical limitations — revisability via exponential gating, storage capacity via matrix memories, and parallelism via the parallelizable mLSTM variant — xLSTM models can reach competitive or superior performance to contemporaneous Transformers and State Space Models across synthetic tasks, standard benchmarks, and large-scale language modeling.

# Code availability and reproducibility pointers

The authors provide their codebase publicly to support reproducibility; the repository is available at https://github.com/NX-AI/xlstm (this link is given in the paper’s abstract). The paper itself contains appendices and more detailed descriptions of model variants, the stabilized gate math and proofs, the parallel reformulation of mLSTM, synthetic task definitions, ablation details, and training procedures. Reproducing the results will require attention to a few practical points the paper calls out: sLSTM’s memory mixing prevents straightforward timestep parallelization (the authors implemented a fast CUDA kernel for it), and the mLSTM implementation in the paper was not fully optimized at the time of writing (so timings can improve with better kernels). The paper also documents training settings (e.g., mixed precision training for many experiments), the xLSTM[ a : b ] notation for mixing mLSTM and sLSTM blocks, and the choice of pre/post up‑projection blocks for the two variants, all of which are important details to match when re‑running experiments.

Taken together, the code release plus the appendices give an explicit path for researchers and practitioners to examine the architectural choices, replicate results, run ablations, and explore further optimizations of the xLSTM family.

# Concluding perspective

xLSTM asks a simple, practical question: if we take the core ideas that made LSTMs powerful and fix the parts that limited them at scale, how far can we go? The design answers are concrete: exponential gating with normalization and stabilization restores revisability of stored information; matrix memories via covariance (outer‑product) updates enormously increase storage capacity and enable associative retrieval; and a parallel mLSTM variant recovers the ability to run efficiently on modern accelerators. By embedding these into modern residual, pre‑LayerNorm backbones and stacking them into deep networks, the paper demonstrates that scaled LSTM variants can compete with — and in many experiments outperform — state‑of‑the‑art Transformers and State Space Models on both synthetic and real language tasks. The work revives the LSTM family with principled modifications that directly address the bottlenecks that limited their use in the Transformer era, and it comes with code and experimental detail to allow others to build on these ideas.

LSTM Fundamentals

# Recurrent computation: cell state and hidden state

At the core of the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) is a two-part recurrent state that is updated at every time step: the cell state and the hidden state. The cell state, usually denoted $c_t$, acts as the network’s internal memory that carries information forward through time. The hidden state, usually denoted $h_t$, is the gated output derived from that memory and is the signal propagated to other layers or used as input at the next time step.

The canonical, compact form of these two updates is
$$
c_t \;=\; f_t \, c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, z_t,
\qquad
h_t \;=\; o_t \, \psi(c_t).
$$
Here $z_t$ is the new candidate content (the “cell input”) computed from the current input and the previous hidden state, while $i_t$, $f_t$, and $o_t$ are multiplicative gates (input, forget, and output gates, respectively) that control how information flows into, through, and out of the cell. The cell state $c_t$ therefore accumulates information over time in an additive way (the first term carries forward past memory, the second adds new information), and the hidden state $h_t$ is a gated, often squashed, readout of that memory.

This two-tier structure separates storage (the cell state) from output (the hidden state). As a result, the cell can maintain long-lived information internally while the hidden state provides a flexible, gated interface to other parts of the network.

# Constant Error Carousel: additive updates to preserve gradients

One of the key conceptual innovations of the LSTM is the so-called constant error carousel, embodied in the additive update for the cell state:
$$
c_t \;=\; f_t \, c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, z_t.
$$
This additive pathway — the direct carry of $f_t c_{t-1}$ plus an additive input contribution — was introduced specifically to address the vanishing gradient problem that plagued earlier recurrent neural networks. Recurrent architectures that propagate information purely by repeated multiplicative interactions tend to shrink gradients exponentially over many time steps; the additive update provides a path along which gradients can flow with much less attenuation, especially when the forget gate $f_t$ is close to one.

Intuitively, the constant error carousel gives the network a persistent memory channel: information placed in $c_{t-1}$ can be preserved (modulated by $f_t$) and remain available many steps later without being repeatedly multiplied by potentially small factors. This is what makes LSTMs capable of learning long-range dependencies in sequences — the network can keep relevant signals in the cell state and access them later through the output gate.

# Input, forget, and output gates and their roles

Gating is the operational mechanism that gives the constant error carousel its flexibility. The three principal gates in a classic LSTM are the input gate $i_t$, the forget gate $f_t$, and the output gate $o_t$. Each gate is a scalar (or vector, in the multi-cell case) between 0 and 1 that modulates a different part of the cell update or readout.

Concretely, gates and the cell input are computed from current inputs and the previous hidden state via affine preactivations followed by nonlinearities. For example:
\begin{align*}
z_t &= \varphi(\tilde{z}_t), \qquad
\tilde{z}_t = \mathbf{w}_z^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_z \, h_{t-1} + b_z,\\
i_t &= \sigma(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad
\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_i \, h_{t-1} + b_i,\\
f_t &= \sigma(\tilde{f}_t), \qquad
\tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_f \, h_{t-1} + b_f,\\
o_t &= \sigma(\tilde{o}_t), \qquad
\tilde{o}_t = \mathbf{w}_o^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_o \, h_{t-1} + b_o.
\end{align*}
In these equations, $\mathbf{x}_t$ is the external input at time $t$, the $\mathbf{w}_\ast$ terms are input weight vectors, the $r_\ast$ terms are recurrent weights from the previous hidden state, and the $b_\ast$ terms are biases. The gate nonlinearities are sigmoids, so each gate takes values in $(0,1)$.

Each gate has a distinct functional role. The forget gate $f_t$ determines how much of the previous cell state $c_{t-1}$ is retained; the input gate $i_t$ controls how much of the new candidate $z_t$ is written into the cell; and the output gate $o_t$ controls how much of the (typically squashed) cell content is exposed as the hidden state $h_t$. Because gates are computed from both the current input and the previous hidden state, the network can make context-dependent decisions about what to remember, what to discard, and what to reveal.

Historically, the forget gate was introduced after the original LSTM formulation and proved critical for learning to reset or drop irrelevant memory contents dynamically. Together, the gates give the LSTM a rich, learnable mechanism for selective storage and retrieval.

# Activations for cell input and state squashing

The raw candidate that is proposed to be written into the cell is produced by a cell-input nonlinearity, often denoted $\varphi$. The cell state itself is typically passed through a squashing nonlinearity, denoted $\psi$, before being emitted as (part of) the hidden state. The hidden-output computation is commonly written as
$$
\tilde{h}_t \;=\; \psi(c_t), \qquad h_t \;=\; o_t \, \tilde{h}_t.
$$
In practice, both $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are commonly set to $\tanh$, which centers the candidate and the squashed cell values around zero and bounds them in $(-1,1)$. The squashing provided by $\psi$ prevents the hidden output from growing without bound and stabilizes the dynamics that the output gate controls.

The gate activations themselves use the sigmoid $\sigma$, where
$$
\sigma(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{1+\exp(-x)}.
$$
Because the gates multiply through the update and readout equations, their bounded $(0,1)$ range makes them effective soft switches: they can nearly shut off a pathway (value near 0) or almost fully pass it (value near 1), and they can interpolate continuously between those extremes.

# From single cell to multi-cell vectorized LSTM

The original LSTM idea was formulated for a scalar memory cell, but practical models use many memory units in parallel. In later formulations multiple scalar memory cells are collected into a vector
$$
\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d,
$$
with corresponding vector-valued hidden states $\mathbf{h}_t\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and gate vectors $\mathbf{i}_t,\mathbf{f}_t,\mathbf{o}_t$. This vectorization permits the model to store and manipulate multiple independent or interacting memory traces simultaneously and to represent richer internal state.

Vectorized LSTMs replace scalar input weights and recurrent weights by matrices and allow vector-valued nonlinearities. For example, the affine components that computed the scalar preactivations become matrix–vector products in the multi-cell form, and the scalar recurrent coefficients generalize to recurrent matrices. Collecting many cells together in a vector enables the network to learn distributed representations that can capture patterns across different dimensions of the internal state.

This scaling from a single scalar cell to a $d$-dimensional cell vector is what allows modern LSTM layers to be substantial computational building blocks: increasing $d$ increases the model’s representational and memory capacity.

# Memory mixing via hidden-to-hidden recurrent matrices

When multiple memory cells are used, the LSTM can mix information across those cells by using recurrent weight matrices that connect the previous hidden state $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ into the preactivations for the cell inputs and the gates. In vector form there are recurrent matrices corresponding to the different pathways, often written as $\mathbf{R}_{z}$, $\mathbf{R}_{i}$, $\mathbf{R}_{f}$, and $\mathbf{R}_{o}$, which connect $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ to the vectors $\mathbf{z}_t$, $\mathbf{i}_t$, $\mathbf{f}_t$, and $\mathbf{o}_t$, respectively.

This “memory mixing” gives the LSTM the ability to implement complex, state-dependent transitions: information stored in one dimension of $\mathbf{c}_{t-1}$ can influence the gate decisions or candidate updates for other dimensions at the next time step. Such interactions are essential for representing and manipulating structured state, for example in tasks that require tracking multiple entities or carrying counts and flags across a long sequence.

An important practical consequence of memory mixing via hidden-to-hidden connections is that the computation is inherently sequential: the dependence on $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ requires processing time steps in order. This sequential dependency complicates parallelization across time and is one of the reasons why LSTM-based architectures are less trivially parallelizable than attention-based architectures.

# Ablation evidence: why all components matter

Empirical studies that systematically remove or alter components of the LSTM memory cell have shown that its various parts are not merely decorative: each plays a functional role that contributes to the model’s effectiveness. As reported in the literature summarized in the review, ablation studies indicate that the combination of the constant error carousel with the gated write/read mechanisms and the nonlinear squashing is important for the network’s ability to learn long-term dependencies and to remain numerically stable.

Removing the forget gate, eliminating gating from inputs, or altering the squashing nonlinearity tends to degrade performance in representative sequence tasks. The interpretive conclusion is that the additive memory pathway, the gating controls, and the nonlinearities together form a tightly integrated mechanism: the additive path preserves gradients, the gates regulate information flow, and the squashing stabilizes representations. Because of this interdependence, meaningful modifications of LSTM architectures need to respect these core design principles or provide alternative mechanisms that replicate their functional effects.

# Known strengths: abstraction learning and long-term dependencies

LSTMs have enjoyed wide adoption because they combine viable long-range memory with flexible, learnable gating. Historically they have been applied successfully across many domains where sequences and temporal structure matter. The reviewed literature lists numerous applications and achievements: LSTMs were used for text generation and handwriting generation, served as core components of sequence-to-sequence translation systems, and were applied to program evaluation, image captioning, source-code generation, and environmental and hydrological modeling tasks such as rainfall–runoff prediction and flood-warning systems. In reinforcement learning, LSTM-based components have been central to high-profile systems such as AlphaStar for StarCraft II and OpenAI Five for Dota 2, and have been applied to control problems like magnetic controllers for nuclear fusion.

Beyond task performance, LSTMs are noted for their ability to learn abstract, semantically meaningful internal representations. Analyses have revealed units that correlate with syntactic number and structure, linguistic features, and even sentiment; such emergent specialized units demonstrate that LSTMs can compress and maintain abstract information in their memory cells.

These strengths — the ability to store and retrieve information across long intervals and to form abstract internal representations — are precisely why LSTMs remained influential even after newer architectures emerged.

Concluding bridge

The LSTM’s two core ideas — the constant error carousel (additive cell updates) and gating (input, forget, output) — are the foundation for its ability to learn long-term dependencies and abstract representations. Modern multi-cell vectorized implementations with recurrent mixing make LSTMs expressive, but they also introduce limitations: sequential memory mixing constrains parallelization, scalar cell representations can limit storage capacity, and the standard gating shapes decisions that are sometimes hard to revise. These observations motivated the extensions and modifications explored in xLSTM, which keep the essential LSTM principles while addressing specific limitations of the classical design.

Where Classic LSTMs Fall Short

Before we examine the specific failure modes, recall the core LSTM update that encodes its memory and gating ideas. At each time step the cell state and hidden state are updated by

\[
c_t \;=\; f_t \, c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, z_t,
\qquad
h_t \;=\; o_t \,\psi(c_t),
\]

where \(i_t\), \(f_t\), and \(o_t\) are the input, forget, and output gates and \(z_t\) is the new cell input. This additive, gated update (the “constant error carousel”) is the reason LSTMs avoid vanishing gradients and why they can store information across many steps. But that same structure also creates three concrete limitations that underlie many modern failures of classic LSTMs. The sections below explain each limitation, give a precise mechanism for why it arises, and connect the limitation to the motivating experiments in the paper.

# Inability to revise storage decisions

The LSTM cell update is additive and gated: everything the network has stored from earlier time steps appears in the current cell state as a sum of past inputs, each attenuated by the forget gates applied since its insertion. Unrolling the recurrence shows this explicitly:

\[
c_T \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^T \Big( i_s z_s \prod_{u=s+1}^T f_u \Big),
\]

so the contribution of an input at time \(s\) to the final state \(c_T\) is scaled by the product of the forget gates that occur after \(s\). Two consequences follow directly.

First, an earlier stored item can only be diminished by the network through the future forget gates \(\{f_{s+1},\dots,f_T\}\). The model cannot directly “go back” and rewrite the remembered representation except by reducing those future multiplicative factors. Practically, this means that revising a previously stored decision (for instance: “the best match so far is X; now a better match Y has arrived — replace X with Y”) requires the model to set the right pattern of forget and input gates at later steps so the later input’s contribution overwhelms the earlier one.

Second, standard LSTM gates are typically sigmoid activations and hence constrained to the interval \((0,1)\). That bounds how sharply a single input can dominate or be suppressed: the only way to make a later input completely replace an earlier one is to set many subsequent forget gates near zero or a single input gate very close to one while all the intervening forget gates are very small. In practice, learning such coordinated gate trajectories is difficult, especially when the decision to revise must depend on fine-grained similarity comparisons between vectors observed at different times.

Together, these factors make classic LSTMs brittle in scenarios that require frequent, selective overwriting of memory. The xLSTM paper addresses this by replacing the usual sigmoid gating for some gates with exponential gating, so that the scale of the input and forget multipliers is not artificially constrained. That change, together with explicit normalization and stabilization, allows the network to produce much larger relative weights for new inputs (or much stronger attenuation for old ones) when the data demands a decisive revision of stored content.

# Nearest Neighbor Search as a failure mode example

A concrete task that exposes the inability to revise storage decisions is the sequential Nearest Neighbor Search described in the paper. The problem is simple to state: given a reference vector \(r\), a sequence of candidate vectors \(\{x_1,\dots,x_T\}\) arrives; each candidate \(x_t\) carries an associated value \(y_t\). The model must scan the sequence and at the final time step emit the value \(y_{t^*}\) associated with the candidate \(x_{t^*}\) that is most similar to the reference \(r\). This task forces the model to maintain “best-so-far” state and to update that state whenever a better match appears.

For an LSTM, implementing “best-so-far” behavior amounts to storing the associated value in the cell and updating it when a new candidate is judged more similar. But the mechanism available is precisely the additive gated update analyzed above: a previous winner’s contribution to the cell persists unless later forget gates repress it sufficiently. When a much better candidate appears late in the sequence, the network must learn to set the gates so the later input’s contribution overtakes the earlier one. With sigmoid-bounded gates and the difficulty of coordinating gates across many steps, the LSTM often fails to revise reliably, producing a large mean-squared error on this task (the paper shows this in the nearest-neighbor figure).

This failure is not merely a training difficulty: it is structural. The additive form turns the memory into a weighted accumulation where older inputs are only attenuated multiplicatively by future forget gates. Exponential gating (as introduced in sLSTM) makes it possible to scale input updates much more flexibly than a sigmoid would allow, enabling decisive overwrites when a later example should replace an earlier stored item. The Nearest Neighbor experiment in the paper highlights precisely this point: classic LSTMs struggle to revise stored values, while xLSTM variants using exponential gates perform much better.

# Limited storage capacity: compression to scalars

The classic LSTM cell stores scalar quantities (or vectors of scalars, where each memory “slot” is ultimately a scalar cell). Even when we use a vector \(\mathbf{c}_t\in\mathbb{R}^d\), each dimension is a scalar accumulator that must encode whatever the model needs to remember. For many tasks this compressive representation creates interference: distinct pieces of information that should be stored separately compete for the same low-dimensional resources, and retrieval degrades because the stored components are mixed.

Two intuitions make the limitation clear. First, a scalar accumulator can represent only so many independent degrees of freedom: long sequences containing many distinct items require either a much higher dimensional \(\mathbf{c}\) or a storage mechanism that can separate items more effectively. Second, many realistic tasks (associative retrieval, rare-event memorization, etc.) require storing individual bindings (e.g., key → value associations) so that a later query can select the correct value. Compressing all bindings into scalar sums creates inevitable collisions and reduces the signal-to-noise ratio for retrieval.

The xLSTM paper addresses this by proposing an mLSTM memory that is a full matrix \(\mathbf{C}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) and is updated with an outer-product (covariance) rule:

\[
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\,\mathbf{k}_t^\top.
\]

Here, \(\mathbf{k}_t\) is a key vector and \(\mathbf{v}_t\) a value vector. Storing the outer product \(\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top\) creates a richer associative structure: retrieval by a query \(\mathbf{q}\) amounts to multiplying \(\mathbf{C}_t\) with \(\mathbf{q}\), i.e.

\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t \;=\; \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t,
\]

which sums up contributions \(\mathbf{v}_s(\mathbf{k}_s^\top \mathbf{q}_t)\). In words, each stored value \(\mathbf{v}_s\) is weighted at retrieval by how similar the stored key \(\mathbf{k}_s\) is to the query \(\mathbf{q}_t\). This is precisely the associative behavior needed to recall the correct value for a given query, and it represents many more degrees of freedom than scalar cells can.

The covariance outer-product update is also principled: as the paper notes, it corresponds to a classical associative-memory update (Bidirectional Associative Memories, Fast Weights) and is optimal in a certain separability sense for binary retrieval. Practically, the matrix memory increases storage capacity dramatically and reduces interference between stored bindings, which addresses the failure modes that arise when scalar compression is forced to represent many distinct associations.

# Rare Token Prediction: performance degradation on low-frequency tokens

A high-impact consequence of limited storage capacity is systematic degradation on rare-token prediction. The paper demonstrates this empirically: when tokens are partitioned by frequency (e.g., common versus rare tokens on Wikitext-103), classic LSTMs show substantially worse perplexity on rare tokens. Intuitively, rare tokens are presented infrequently during training and often need distinct, localized information (specific contexts or rare associations) to be stored and recalled reliably. When the model’s memory must compress many such rare associations into scalar cells, rare-token signals are easily drowned by more frequent patterns or mixed into shared dimensions in ways that harm later retrieval.

The matrix-memory mLSTM mitigates this problem because it stores bindings in a key/value structure rather than forcing all signals to share the same few scalar channels. The outer-product updates accumulate distinct key–value pairs so that, at query time, the right value can be selected by similarity to the query key. Consequently, the xLSTM variants with matrix memory perform better at predicting rare tokens, as shown in the paper’s rare-token partition experiment. This empirical observation directly ties the storage-capacity limitation of scalar cells to a visible performance gap on low-frequency phenomena in language modeling.

# Lack of parallelizability due to memory mixing

A third structural limitation concerns computation and scaling. Classic LSTM architectures include hidden-to-gate recurrent terms such as \(r_z h_{t-1}\), \(r_i h_{t-1}\), etc.; when multiple scalar memory cells are combined into a vector \(\mathbf{c}_t\in\mathbb{R}^d\), recurrent weight matrices \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) are used to mix outputs across cells. These hidden–hidden connections mean that computing the state at time \(t\) requires the exact state at time \(t-1\). The temporal dependence enforces strictly sequential computation: you cannot compute several time steps independently in parallel in a single layer without additional algorithmic machinery.

Sequential processing is a practical bottleneck when training very large models on extremely long sequences: modern hardware and training pipelines obtain much of their speed-up by parallelizing work across tokens (for instance, computing attention across all positions in a Transformer layer in parallel). The recurrence and memory mixing in LSTMs prevent this kind of layer-parallel token processing and hence hinder throughput, distributed training efficiency, and ultimately the ability to scale to very large models and datasets.

The xLSTM paper addresses parallelizability in two ways. The mLSTM variant removes hidden-to-gate recurrent mixing (no hidden-hidden recurrent matrices) and therefore can be reformulated in a parallel-friendly way (the paper notes it is parallelizable similarly to linear attention methods). The sLSTM retains memory mixing and so remains inherently sequential, but the authors developed a specialized CUDA kernel and other optimizations so that, in practice, their sequential sLSTM implementation is not catastrophically slow (it is reported to be typically less than twice as slow as the parallel mLSTM implementation in their experiments). Thus, removing or controlling memory mixing is crucial to get the computational scaling properties required for modern large-scale models.

# Implications for scaling and long-context modeling

Taken together, these three limitations—difficulties in revising stored content, limited scalar-based storage capacity, and enforced sequential computation due to memory mixing—explain why classic LSTMs have struggled to keep pace in large-scale language modeling and other sequence tasks that demand long contexts and massive data.

From an expressivity standpoint, the restriction on how stored items can be overwritten makes certain state-tracking tasks (for example, algorithms that need to update which entity is “current” as new observations arrive) intrinsically hard for vanilla LSTMs. From a memory-capacity perspective, scalar compression leads to performance drops on rare events and on associative tasks that require remembering many distinct bindings across long stretches of input. From an engineering and scaling perspective, sequential dependence reduces parallel throughput, limiting how effectively the model can exploit large GPUs and distributed compute clusters.

These consequences are visible in the experiments reported in the paper: LSTMs lag on nearest-neighbor style tasks and rare-token partitions, and large-scale language modeling at modern LLM scales has been dominated by architectures (Transformers and some State Space Models) that either avoid strict recurrence or restructure it to be parallelizable. The xLSTM proposal is motivated precisely by the desire to remove or ameliorate these bottlenecks while keeping the benefits of LSTM-style gating and memory. Exponential gating provides more decisive, learnable revision mechanisms; matrix memory provides far greater associative capacity; and the option to remove hidden–hidden mixing (in mLSTM) restores layer-level parallelism needed for scaling.

# Why these limitations favored the emergence of Transformers

Historically, LSTMs were the dominant sequence model for many tasks because their gating and additive memory solved the vanishing-gradient problem and allowed reasonably long-range dependencies to be learned. However, as language modeling moved to ever larger training sets and models, two trends made purely recurrent architectures less attractive.

First, parallelizability became crucial. The Transformer architecture replaces recurrence with self-attention, allowing the model to process all tokens in a layer simultaneously. This change unlocks massive wall-clock speedups on modern accelerators and makes it practical to train very large models on huge corpora. Second, self-attention directly computes token-to-token interactions in a way that effectively provides very high-capacity, position-dependent memory: each token can attend to all others and the attention mechanism can represent complex associative patterns without compressing them into a single scalar cell. These computational and representational advantages allowed Transformers to “outpace” LSTMs at scale, as the paper frames it.

The limitations we have described—difficulty revising stored content, limited scalar capacity, and sequential-only computation—are precisely the reasons LSTMs ceded ground. The xLSTM work can be read as an attempt to bring LSTM-style ideas up to date: keep the useful inductive bias of gated, stateful memory (good for state tracking and certain algorithmic behaviors), but augment the gates and memory so the model can revise decisively, store many distinct bindings, and (in the mLSTM case) be computed in parallel. In short, the historical shift toward Transformers was driven by practical and structural issues that the xLSTM modifications explicitly target.

Concluding remark

Understanding these three fundamental failure modes clarifies why the paper introduces exponential gating, matrix-valued memory, and controlled removal of memory mixing. Each modification addresses a specific structural weakness of classic LSTMs: revision of stored content, capacity for associative bindings, and parallelizability for scaling. The experimental examples (nearest-neighbor search, rare-token perplexity partitions, and long-context evaluations) demonstrate how these weaknesses manifest in practice, and why the xLSTM design choices follow naturally from the diagnosis.

Exponential Gating and Normalization

# Replacing sigmoid with exponential for input and optionally forget gates

In a classical LSTM the gating functions (input, forget, output) use a sigmoid nonlinearity so that each gate value lies in the interval $(0,1)$. That design enforces a bounded, multiplicative modulation of the memory: gates can attenuate but cannot amplify beyond unity. xLSTM modifies this by replacing the input gate (and optionally the forget gate) with an exponential activation. Concretely, for the scalar sLSTM the relevant pieces of the forward pass are written as

$$
c_t \;=\; f_t \, c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, z_t,
$$

with the input and forget gates given by

$$
i_t \;=\; \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad
\tilde{i}_t \;=\; \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t \;+\; r_i \, h_{t-1} \;+\; b_i,
$$

and

$$
f_t \;=\; \sigma(\tilde{f}_t) \quad\text{or}\quad f_t \;=\; \exp(\tilde{f}_t),
\qquad
\tilde{f}_t \;=\; \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t \;+\; r_f \, h_{t-1} \;+\; b_f.
$$

Replacing the sigmoid by an exponential has two immediate algebraic effects. First, $i_t$ becomes strictly positive and unbounded (rather than constrained to $(0,1)$), so it can scale new inputs by arbitrarily large factors. Second, when we unroll the recurrence this multiplicative behavior of exponentials turns sums and products of gates into exponentials of linear combinations of pre-activations. This change creates a family of weights across time that can vary over many orders of magnitude, allowing the memory update mechanism to perform much more aggressive reweighting of past stored items than the conventional sigmoid gate allows.

Note that the output gate $o_t$ remains a sigmoid in the xLSTM designs presented in the paper; the exponential modification is applied to input gating (and optionally to forget gating) because those gates control how much new information enters and how much past information is decayed — exactly the places where revisability and high dynamic range of the gating values are most useful.

# Normalizer state: tracking gate mass for proper normalization

When gates can produce unbounded positive weights, the raw cell state $c_t$ (a weighted sum of past inputs) can grow without bound. xLSTM adds a companion state, the normalizer $n_t$, which mirrors the structure of the cell update but accumulates the gate-mass rather than the gated inputs. For the scalar sLSTM the normalizer is updated by

$$
n_t \;=\; f_t \, n_{t-1} \;+\; i_t.
$$

Unrolling the recurrences gives a very transparent interpretation. Define the (time-dependent) weight for an input at time $s\le t$ as

$$
w_{s,t} \;=\; i_s \prod_{j=s+1}^{t} f_j.
$$

Then

$$
c_t \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^{t} w_{s,t} \, z_s, \qquad
n_t \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^{t} w_{s,t}.
$$

Thus $n_t$ is exactly the total mass of the contributions currently stored in the cell: it is the sum of the same weights that appear in $c_t$ but without multiplying by the content vectors $z_s$. This bookkeeping is what allows xLSTM to form normalized outputs and to use exponentials safely: by recording how much effective weight is present, the network can divide out that scale when producing the exposed hidden state.

For the matrix-memory mLSTM the normalizer becomes a vector that accumulates keyed mass:

$$
\mathbf{n}_t \;=\; f_t \, \mathbf{n}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, \mathbf{k}_t,
$$

and unrolling yields

$$
\mathbf{n}_t \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^{t} \Big( i_s \prod_{j=s+1}^{t} f_j \Big) \mathbf{k}_s.
$$

In mLSTM the normalizer records the aggregated keys weighted by the gate-mass; it plays the role of the denominator when converting the matrix memory's raw retrieval into a scale-invariant value.

# Normalized hidden state using the normalizer

The normalizer is used to produce a normalized hidden representation that is invariant to overall scaling of stored weights. In sLSTM the network computes

$$
\tilde{h}_t \;=\; \frac{c_t}{n_t}, \qquad
h_t \;=\; o_t \, \tilde{h}_t,
$$

so the exposed hidden state is the cell content scaled by the total gate mass. Because $n_t$ is the sum of the same weights used in $c_t$, the ratio $c_t/n_t$ is a weighted average of past inputs:

$$
\frac{c_t}{n_t} \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^t \frac{w_{s,t}}{\sum_{u=1}^t w_{u,t}} \, z_s,
$$

i.e., a convex combination of the past $z_s$ (assuming $f_t \ge 0$ and $i_t > 0$). This normalization keeps the scale of the hidden state stable even when some $i_s$ become very large, and — crucially for revisability — it turns the memory into a mechanism that can focus on one or a few inputs by making their relative weights dominate the denominator.

For mLSTM the retrieval is matrix-vector based. With the matrix memory

$$
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top,
$$

we obtain the candidate output (before output gating) by multiplying $\mathbf{C}_t$ with a query $\mathbf{q}_t$ and normalizing by the scalar $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ (lower‑bounded and absolute-valued for stability):

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \, \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{\,|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,\,1\,\}}.
$$

Unrolling $\mathbf{C}_t$ shows that $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$ equals a weighted sum of the stored values $\mathbf{v}_s$, with weights proportional to $w_{s,t} (\mathbf{k}_s^\top \mathbf{q}_t)$. Dividing by $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ thus converts this into a query‑conditioned normalized average of the value vectors, making the mLSTM retrieval directly analogous (in structure) to attention-like aggregation but implemented inside a recurrent memory cell.

# Stabilizer state in log-space to prevent overflow

Exponentials grow rapidly, and naively computing $i_t=\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ and (if used) $f_t=\exp(\tilde{f}_t)$ can cause numerical overflow or underflow during both forward and backward passes. xLSTM adopts a log-space stabilizer that prevents such overflow while preserving exact functional behavior. The stabilizer maintains a scalar $m_t$ (per memory) defined by

$$
m_t \;=\; \max\big( \log(f_t) + m_{t-1},\; \log(i_t) \big).
$$

Using $m_t$ we define scaled (stabilized) gate values

$$
i'_t \;=\; \exp\big(\log(i_t) - m_t\big) \;=\; \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t),
$$

$$
f'_t \;=\; \exp\big(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t\big).
$$

Because $m_t$ is the maximum of the two arguments, both exponents $\log(i_t) - m_t$ and $\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t$ are less than or equal to zero; therefore $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ lie in the interval $(0,1]$ and never overflow. This is the same numerical idea that underlies the log-sum-exp trick: factor out the largest exponent to keep exponentiated terms in a stable range.

Operationally, xLSTM uses $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ in the cell and normalizer updates in place of the raw $i_t$ and $f_t$. As we will see, this stable computation does not change the exposed normalized hidden states, because the stabilizer factors out a common scale that cancels in the normalization step.

# Stabilized gates and invariance of network output and gradients

Although the stabilized gates $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ differ from the raw exponentials by multiplicative factors, the final normalized outputs of the cell are identical to those obtained with unstabilized gates. The short inductive proof is straightforward and instructive.

Define $s_t \equiv \exp(m_t)$. From the definitions above we have

$$
i'_t \;=\; \frac{i_t}{s_t}, \qquad
f'_t \;=\; \frac{f_t \, s_{t-1}}{s_t}.
$$

Let $c_t$ and $n_t$ denote the cell and normalizer computed with the original (unstabilized) gates, and let $\hat{c}_t$ and $\hat{n}_t$ denote those computed with the stabilized gates $i'_t,f'_t$. Assume inductively that $\hat{c}_{t-1} = c_{t-1}/s_{t-1}$ and $\hat{n}_{t-1} = n_{t-1}/s_{t-1}$. Then

\begin{align*}
\hat{c}_t
&= f'_t \, \hat{c}_{t-1} + i'_t \, z_t
= \frac{f_t s_{t-1}}{s_t} \cdot \frac{c_{t-1}}{s_{t-1}} + \frac{i_t}{s_t} z_t
= \frac{f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t}{s_t}
= \frac{c_t}{s_t}, \\[6pt]
\hat{n}_t
&= f'_t \, \hat{n}_{t-1} + i'_t
= \frac{f_t s_{t-1}}{s_t} \cdot \frac{n_{t-1}}{s_{t-1}} + \frac{i_t}{s_t}
= \frac{f_t n_{t-1} + i_t}{s_t}
= \frac{n_t}{s_t}.
\end{align*}

Consequently the normalized hidden candidate is identical:

$$
\frac{\hat{c}_t}{\hat{n}_t} \;=\; \frac{c_t/s_t}{n_t/s_t} \;=\; \frac{c_t}{n_t}.
$$

Because the exposed hidden state $h_t$ depends on $c_t/n_t$ (and on output gate $o_t$), the forward outputs of the stabilized and unstabilized recurrences are exactly equal for every input and every set of parameters. From this exact functional equivalence it follows that the gradients of any scalar loss with respect to the parameters are also the same: the two computational graphs implement the same mapping from parameters to outputs, so their Jacobians coincide. Thus the stabilizer prevents overflow without changing the network's learned function or its gradient dynamics.

This invariance is important in practice: we get the safety and numerical soundness of scaled computations while keeping the representational advantages of exponential gating.

# Revisability intuition: why exponential gating matters

One of the core motivations for exponential gating is to enable efficient revision of previously stored memory. Consider the scalar sLSTM expression

$$
\frac{c_t}{n_t} \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^t \alpha_{s,t} \, z_s,
\qquad
\alpha_{s,t} \;=\; \frac{w_{s,t}}{\sum_{u=1}^t w_{u,t}},
\qquad
w_{s,t} \;=\; i_s \prod_{j=s+1}^{t} f_j.
$$

This shows that the normalized cell content is a weighted average of past inputs $z_s$ with weights $\alpha_{s,t}$ determined by the input and forget gates. If the task requires replacing an early stored value by a later, better candidate (as in a sequential nearest‑neighbor search), the model must be able to make the later candidate carry much more mass than the earlier ones. With sigmoid gates bounded in $(0,1)$, the relative change in weights across time is limited; if many earlier items have accumulated mass, the new item cannot easily dominate unless the forget gates strongly decay the entire past.

By using exponential input gates, $i_t=\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$, the network can set $i_{\text{new}}$ many orders of magnitude larger than earlier $i_s$, producing weights $w_{\text{new},t}$ that overwhelm previous contributions. In the extreme, exponentials of large pre-activations approximate an argmax: choosing $\tilde{i}_s$ proportional to some similarity measure and taking a large scaling constant yields a distribution sharply focused on the most similar key. In other words, exponential gating plus normalization produces dynamics closely analogous to attention's softmax numerator: a later item with larger pre-activation can effectively “win” and replace previous stored content in the normalized output.

The normalizer is essential in this picture: without dividing by $n_t$, making a single $i_t$ huge would blow up the scale of $c_t$ and make downstream layers sensitive to absolute magnitudes. By forming the ratio $c_t/n_t$ the model realizes revision as a change in the relative weights (the $\alpha_{s,t}$) rather than as raw additive bias, stabilizing learning and enabling aggressive reweighting.

# Initialization and practical stability considerations

Exponential gates give great expressive power but also require careful numerical and practical handling. The paper highlights that forget‑gate initialization must be chosen carefully; more generally, sensible initializations for the pre-activations $\tilde{i}_t$ and $\tilde{f}_t$ avoid early training regimes where exponentials produce extremely large or tiny values. Layer normalization (or similar preprocessing) before projecting into keys and values (used in mLSTM) ensures that inputs have controlled scale and mean, which tends to reduce the risk of extreme initial activations.

The log-space stabilizer described above is the primary tool to guarantee numerical safety: because $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ are computed as exponentials of non-positive arguments, they remain bounded by $1$ during computation and prevent overflow. In mLSTM an additional practical safeguard is applied on retrieval: the denominator $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ is replaced by $\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,1\}$ (the paper uses 1 as a typical lower bound). This avoids dividing by very small scalars that would amplify noise.

Finally, during model design one may choose whether to exponentiate the forget gate or keep it sigmoid. Using $\exp(\tilde{f}_t)$ allows forget factors larger than $1$ (amplifying past memory), whereas $\sigma(\tilde{f}_t)$ constrains forget factors to be attenuating (in $(0,1)$). The choice interacts with task demands and stability: when forget is exponential, the stabilizer is essential to prevent runs of multiplicative growth; when forget is sigmoid, this particular source of growth is limited, but the model still benefits from exponential input gating for revisability.

# Compatibility with input- and hidden-dependent gating

Crucially, exponential gating in xLSTM is fully compatible with the usual input- and hidden-dependent gate parameterizations inherited from LSTM. The generalized pre-activation for a gate keeps the familiar form

$$
\tilde{i}_t \;=\; \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t \;+\; r_i \, h_{t-1} \;+\; b_i,
$$

so gates can be driven by both the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$. The exponential nonlinearity is applied to $\tilde{i}_t$ (and optionally to $\tilde{f}_t$), and the stabilizer uses the logarithms of the resulting gate values. All of the algebraic steps of stabilization and normalization commute with this dependence: the same inductive invariance argument holds when gates are functions of $h_{t-1}$, because the stabilizer and the subsequent scaling are computed deterministically from the same gate values. In other words, the architectural choices that make LSTM powerful — input dependence, hidden dependence, and learnable biases — are preserved; we only change the output nonlinearity and add numerically safe normalization to unlock broader dynamic range and revisability.

This compatibility extends to multi‑cell and multi‑head variants described in the xLSTM family: each cell or head computes its own stabilized gates and normalizer, and mixing (via recurrent connections in sLSTM) or parallelized retrievals (in mLSTM) proceed with the same stabilized, normalized quantities.

Summary

Exponential gating transforms the LSTM update weights from bounded factors into positive, unbounded weights. To make this change practical and numerically robust, xLSTM introduces the normalizer state that records total gate mass and a log-space stabilizer that prevents overflow by factoring out a running scale. Together these mechanisms let the model realize normalized weighted averages of past inputs where a single item can be made to dominate (enabling revisability), while guaranteeing identical forward outputs and gradients to a hypothetical unstabilized computation. The resulting design preserves input- and hidden-dependent gating patterns of LSTMs, but extends them with a high‑dynamic‑range reweighting capability that is central to xLSTM’s improved memory and revision behavior.

sLSTM: Scalar Memory with Exponential Gating and New Mixing

# sLSTM forward pass: cell update, normalizer, and normalized output

The sLSTM retains the core LSTM idea of an additive "constant error carousel" update for a scalar memory cell, but augments it with a normalizer state and exponential gating. At each time step $t$ the scalar forward pass of the sLSTM is given by the following set of equations:

$c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, z_t$  (cell state)

$n_t = f_t \, n_{t-1} \;+\; i_t$  (normalizer state)

$h_t = o_t \, \tilde{h}_t,\qquad \tilde{h}_t = \dfrac{c_t}{n_t}$  (hidden state and normalized output)

$z_t = \varphi(\tilde{z}_t),\qquad \tilde{z}_t = \mathbf{w}_z^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_z \, h_{t-1} + b_z$  (cell input)

$i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t),\qquad \tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_i \, h_{t-1} + b_i$  (input gate)

$f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)\ \text{OR}\ \exp(\tilde{f}_t),\qquad \tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_f \, h_{t-1} + b_f$  (forget gate)

$o_t = \sigma(\tilde{o}_t),\qquad \tilde{o}_t = \mathbf{w}_o^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_o \, h_{t-1} + b_o$  (output gate)

These equations should be read together: the cell $c_t$ accumulates past content $c_{t-1}$ attenuated (or amplified) by the forget gate $f_t$, plus the new write $i_t z_t$ modulated by the input gate $i_t$. The novel normalizer $n_t$ accumulates the exact same write weights (forget-modulated input gate strengths) but without the content vectors; dividing $c_t$ by $n_t$ produces $\tilde{h}_t$ — a normalized, content-weighted summary of the past. Finally, the output gate $o_t$ modulates this summary to produce the visible hidden state $h_t$.

To see what the sLSTM is computing over the entire history, unroll the recurrences. With $c_0 = n_0 = 0$ (for simplicity),

$c_t \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^{t} \Bigg( \Big(\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u\Big) \, i_s \, z_s \Bigg), 
\qquad
n_t \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^{t} \Bigg( \Big(\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u\Big) \, i_s \Bigg).$

Thus $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$ is a weighted average of past cell inputs $z_s$ with weights $w_s = i_s \prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u$. The normalizer $n_t$ is exactly the denominator of that average: it tracks the total effective write mass. This representation is central to the sLSTM intuition: the network maintains a compressive summary of the past as a normalized mixture of stored inputs, where the mixture weights are built from input gates and the sequence of subsequent forget gates.

Because sLSTM uses exponential activations for the input gate (and optionally for the forget gate), it necessarily includes numerical stabilization to avoid overflow; that mechanism is described below in the stabilization subsection.

# Gate parameterization: input-, hidden-dependence, and biases

Each pre-activation in the sLSTM — the affine quantities $\tilde{z}_t$, $\tilde{i}_t$, $\tilde{f}_t$, and $\tilde{o}_t$ — follows the classic LSTM pattern: a linear projection of the current input plus a recurrent contribution from the previous hidden state and a bias term. In the scalar presentation above you saw terms like $\mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_i h_{t-1} + b_i$. When sLSTM is generalized to multiple cells, the vectorized versions use matrices in place of the scalar recurrent terms. Concretely, a multi-cell pre-activation takes the form

$\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_t = \mathbf{W}_z \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{R}_z \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_z,$

and analogous equations hold for $\tilde{\mathbf{i}}_t$, $\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_t$, and $\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t$. The matrices $\mathbf{R}_{\cdot}$ are the recurrent mixing matrices that map the previous hidden state back into gate and candidate pre-activations.

There are three levers in this parameterization: (i) input dependence (the $\mathbf{W}_\cdot \mathbf{x}_t$ terms), (ii) hidden dependence (the $\mathbf{R}_\cdot \mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ terms), and (iii) learnable bias terms ($\mathbf{b}_\cdot$). The sLSTM explicitly inherits the original LSTM design choice: any gate can be chosen to depend on input, hidden, both, or neither, and each gate has its own bias. Making a gate hidden-dependent (nonzero $\mathbf{R}_\cdot$) is what enables memory mixing across cells: the previous hidden state carries cross-cell information back into new write and forget decisions. Including a bias is important in practice because it sets the baseline operating point of the exponential or sigmoid nonlinearity (e.g., initializing forget gates to prefer retention or decay).

Because the input gate in sLSTM is exponential, the bias term $\mathbf{b}_i$ influences the baseline magnitude of writes strongly: a modest positive bias in $\tilde{i}_t$ becomes a multiplicative factor via $\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$. Similarly, if the forget gate uses exponential activation then its bias controls multiplicative decay or growth over time; if it uses sigmoid, the bias controls a bounded decay factor. These interactions make gate parameterization choices and initial biases important hyperparameters in training.

# When to use sigmoid vs. exponential for the forget gate

sLSTM deliberately permits two choices for the forget gate nonlinearity: the conventional sigmoid $\sigma(\cdot)$, which maps pre-activations to the interval $(0,1)$, or an exponential $\exp(\cdot)$, which maps to $(0,\infty)$. Each has different qualitative behavior and practical trade-offs.

Using sigmoid for $f_t$ implements the familiar LSTM-style multiplicative decay: forget factors lie between 0 (fully forget) and 1 (fully keep), so the product $\prod f_u$ always shrinks or stays bounded as more steps accumulate. Sigmoid forget gates are numerically stable and require no special handling beyond ordinary training care; they are a conservative choice if you want stable attenuation over time.

Using exponential for $f_t$ gives the forget factor a multiplicative, unbounded positive range. If $f_t=\exp(\tilde f_t)$, then products of forget factors exponentiate sums of pre-activations:

$\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u \;=\; \exp\!\Big(\sum_{u=s+1}^t \tilde f_u\Big).$

This lets the model both attenuate (if the sum is negative) and amplify (if positive) contributions from past writes, giving much larger dynamic range. That dynamic range is precisely what enables the sLSTM to "revise" storage decisions: with exponential forgets and exponential inputs, a late write can be made to dominate earlier writes by producing much larger multiplicative weights, or earlier contributions can be amplified or suppressed by subsequent forgets. The paper demonstrates that this capability materially improves performance on problems that require revising previous storage choices (e.g., the Nearest Neighbor Search toy task).

The downside of exponential forget gates is numerical: exponentials can overflow or produce extremely large/small values during unrolled accumulation. To address this, sLSTM uses a stabilization trick (below) based on working in log-space with a running maximum. If you prefer a simpler, safer default or your task does not require the revising behavior, choosing sigmoid for $f_t$ avoids the stabilization complexity.

One practical note made in the paper is that the initialization of forget-gate parameters matters: because forget dynamics can grow multiplicatively over many time steps (especially with exponential forgets), biases and initial weights must be chosen carefully to avoid pathological growth or rapid collapse of memory.

# Multiple memory cells and recurrent mixing matrices

sLSTM can be scaled from a single scalar memory to multiple scalar memory cells collected into a vector $\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$. The multi-cell sLSTM retains the same structural equations, but all scalar quantities become vectors and elementwise operations are used where appropriate. A compact vectorized form is

$\mathbf{c}_t = \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{i}_t \odot \mathbf{z}_t,$

$\mathbf{n}_t = \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{n}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{i}_t,$

$\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot (\mathbf{c}_t \oslash \mathbf{n}_t),$

with pre-activations

$\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_t = \mathbf{W}_z \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{R}_z \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_z,$

$\tilde{\mathbf{i}}_t = \mathbf{W}_i \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{R}_i \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_i,$

$\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_t = \mathbf{W}_f \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{R}_f \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_f,$

$\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t = \mathbf{W}_o \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{R}_o \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_o.$

Here $\odot$ denotes elementwise multiplication and $\oslash$ elementwise division. The recurrent matrices $\mathbf{R}_\cdot \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ are the explicit mixing mechanisms: they let the previous global hidden state influence every cell's gate and candidate pre-activation, thereby enabling cross-cell interaction at write and forget time.

This recurrent mixing is the principal differentiator between sLSTM and models that lack such hidden–hidden connections. Because the gates themselves (via $\tilde{\mathbf{i}}_t$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_t$) can depend on the entire hidden state, the amount and fate of each cell's stored content can be decided conditionally on a global view of the recent history. In other words, the network can use the hidden state as a routing and coordination signal to direct which cells to write into, which to decay, and how to combine them into output. The presence of exponential gating multiplies the expressivity of these recurrent mixing matrices: a small change in a pre-activation that flows through an exponential can change a cell's contribution multiplicatively by orders of magnitude.

# Multi-head sLSTM: mixing within heads but not across heads

The sLSTM supports a multi-head layout that partitions the set of memory cells into independent groups (heads). Each head contains a subset of the scalar cells and its own set of recurrent mixing matrices. Concretely, if you split the $d$ memory cells into $H$ heads of size $d_h$ each ($d = H d_h$), then the recurrent matrices can be structured so that mixing is only within each head: each head $j$ has its own $\mathbf{R}^{(j)}_z,\mathbf{R}^{(j)}_i,\mathbf{R}^{(j)}_f,\mathbf{R}^{(j)}_o$ that act only on that head's portion of $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$. Equivalent is to let the global recurrent matrices be block-diagonal, with one diagonal block per head.

The crucial design point is that sLSTM allows memory mixing inside each head but explicitly prevents mixing across heads. This architectural bias has two main consequences. First, it compartmentalizes memory so that different heads can specialize on different kinds of temporal patterns or tasks without interfering. Second, because there is no cross-head mixing, the head-level computations can be more readily parallelized across compute units and less prone to degenerate global interference. The authors emphasize that combining heads with exponential gating "establishes a new way of memory mixing": each head can manage a separate multiplicative scale and temporal behavior, and the exponential gates amplify these per-head dynamics.

Note that multi-head sLSTM is not the same as multi-head self-attention; the heads here are groups of recurrent memory cells with independent recurrent dynamics rather than independent attention projections. Still, the head-wise partitioning plays a similar role in letting different subspaces specialize and in structuring how mixing is permitted.

# Exponential gating as a new lever for memory mixing

One of sLSTM's defining ideas is to replace or augment sigmoid gates with exponential activations (at least for the input gate, and optionally for forget). This single change has profound implications for how memory mixing and revision operate.

From the unrolled formula for $c_t$ and $n_t$ we identified weights $w_s = i_s \prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u$ for the contribution of $z_s$ to the normalized output $\tilde{h}_t$. If $i_s=\exp(\tilde{i}_s)$ and $f_u=\exp(\tilde{f}_u)$, then

$w_s = \exp\!\Big( \tilde{i}_s + \sum_{u=s+1}^t \tilde{f}_u \Big).$

This shows two things at once. First, the write weight is multiplicative in the exponentiated pre-activations, so small additive changes in pre-activation space become multiplicative changes in contribution weight. Second, the sequence of forget pre-activations between write time $s$ and read time $t$ accumulates additively in the exponent; later forget decisions can dramatically amplify or attenuate earlier writes. That algebraic structure gives sLSTM fine-grained control over which past writes matter at any later time.

If instead the forgets use sigmoid, then the product $\prod f_u$ is bounded in $(0,1)$ and $w_s$ is a product of positive numbers in a bounded range. This is still multiplicative — early writes can be downweighted — but the dynamic range is far narrower than with exponentials. In practice, exponential gates allow the model to sharply concentrate attention on a small subset of past writes (by making their exponentiated pre-activations large) or to spread attention more evenly; sigmoid forgets tend to enforce smoother decay.

These properties make exponential gating a lever for richer memory mixing: the same recurrent matrices that decide pre-activations now operate in the exponent, so they can produce multiplicative routing effects. Combined with multi-head partitioning, the model can effect very different multiplicative regimes in different subspaces, enabling flexible, adaptive, and sometimes large-scale revisions to the stored summary.

Because these operations can grow or shrink very rapidly, sLSTM introduces a stabilization trick based on a running max (denoted $m_t$ in the paper) that maintains numerical stability when computing exponentials across a time series. Formally the stabilizer updates are

$m_t = \max\!\big( \log(f_t) + m_{t-1} , \, \log(i_t) \big)$

and the stabilized gates become

$i'_t = \exp(\log(i_t) - m_t) = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t),\qquad
f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t).$

These transformations perform the same effective weighting but computed in a numerically safe log-space relative to the current maximum scale. The paper proves that replacing $(i_t,f_t)$ by $(i'_t,f'_t)$ leaves the network outputs and gradients unchanged.

# Expressivity and state tracking advantages

Two structural elements give sLSTM superior expressivity for state-tracking problems compared to models without hidden–hidden mixing: (i) recurrent mixing across cells and (ii) multiplicative, exponential gating combined with normalization. Recurrent mixing ($\mathbf{R}_\cdot$ matrices) enables conditional routing: the decision about which cells to write to or forget can depend on the whole hidden state, allowing the network to implement arbitrarily coupled state-update rules. Exponential gating inflates these conditional decisions into multiplicative, often very selective, weightings of stored content. The normalizer ensures those multiplicative write weights are turned into a stable, comparable summary.

This combination is especially important for tasks that require maintaining and updating discrete or symbolic-like state over time (state tracking), such as formal language recognition or parity/counter-like computations. The paper reports that models lacking memory mixing — including many Transformers and State Space Models — fail on certain formal language tasks because they cannot implement the required sequential state-update logic. In contrast, sLSTM (as part of xLSTM) succeeds on those tasks: the recurrent, gated scalar cells implement internal state variables whose updates and interactions can mimic finite-state machines or counters.

Another way to think about expressivity: sLSTM's normalized mixture form means it can represent a context-dependent center-of-mass of past representations. The network can cause this center-of-mass to shift rapidly (via exponential writes) or to be reweighted by future forget gates; with multiple cells and recurrent mixing, those shifts can be coordinated across many sub-dimensions. That expressive repertoire is what enables stateful, sequential reasoning that is difficult for models without recurrent mixing.

# Computational profile and practical GPU kernel notes

sLSTM trades parallelizability for expressivity. Memory mixing via hidden–hidden recurrent connections implies a true sequential dependency across time: computing $\mathbf{h}_t$ requires $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$, so a straightforward parallel scan over time (as used by many Transformer-like architectures) is not possible. Nevertheless, sLSTM has favorable asymptotics relative to full-attention Transformers: its computation per time step is linear in the model width and independent of sequence length, and its memory usage is constant with respect to sequence length because the model maintains only its compressed cell and normalizer states rather than buffering full per-token keys.

Because sLSTM is sequential, high-performance implementations matter. The authors report having implemented a fast CUDA kernel with register-level memory optimizations that substantially reduces the practical runtime penalty; in their implementation sLSTM is typically less than two times slower than the fully parallelizable mLSTM variant. This difference is relatively modest given the expressivity gains. The implementation also needs to include the stabilizer update ($m_t$) and the log-space arithmetic for exponential gates in order to ensure numerical correctness.

Practical guidance emerging from the paper includes choosing the appropriate forget-gate nonlinearity for the task (sigmoid when you prefer stable decay; exponential when you need revisability and a wide dynamic range), carefully initializing forget-biases, and using the post up-projection residual block when integrating sLSTM into an xLSTM block (the paper uses post up-projection backbones for sLSTM blocks). When using exponential gates, include the stabilization scheme described above; this is essential to avoid overflow/underflow while preserving exact equivalence of outputs and gradients.

In summary, sLSTM is a principled extension of LSTM that keeps the scalar memory cell and recurrent mixing but introduces exponential gating and a normalizer to enable normalized, multiplicatively reweightable summaries of the past. These design choices substantially increase the model's ability to revise and route memory content while still preserving the constant-error carousel that makes LSTMs trainable over long horizons.

mLSTM: Matrix Memory with Covariance Updates

# Matrix memory C: storing key–value pairs via outer products

The core idea of the mLSTM is to replace the scalar LSTM cell with a matrix memory \(\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\). Instead of storing a single scalar per cell, the matrix memory stores associations between key vectors and value vectors. At each time step \(t\) the model produces a key \(\mathbf{k}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and a value \(\mathbf{v}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\). The entry for that key–value pair is recorded in the matrix memory by adding the outer product \(\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top\). Concretely the mLSTM cell update (including gating) is written as
\[
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; f_t \,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \,\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top,
\]
where \(i_t\) and \(f_t\) are the input and forget gates (more on their meaning below). The outer product \(\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top\) stores a mapping from directions in the key space to directions in the value space: when later multiplied by a query \(\mathbf{q}\), that outer product contributes \(\mathbf{v}_t (\mathbf{k}_t^\top \mathbf{q})\), i.e., the stored value scaled by the similarity between the query and the stored key.

A useful algebraic expansion helps make the retrieval mechanism explicit. If we unroll the recurrence (and assume \(\mathbf{C}_0=\mathbf{0}\) for simplicity), then
\[
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^t \left(\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u\right) i_s \,\mathbf{v}_s \mathbf{k}_s^\top.
\]
Multiplying this \(\mathbf{C}_t\) by a query \(\mathbf{q}_t\) yields
\[
\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^t \left(\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u\right) i_s \,\mathbf{v}_s \,(\mathbf{k}_s^\top \mathbf{q}_t),
\]
so the retrieved vector is a weighted linear combination of past values \(\mathbf{v}_s\), with weights given by the product of gates and the key–query inner product \(\mathbf{k}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t\). The outer-product storage therefore implements associative retrieval: a query projects onto stored keys and reconstitutes the corresponding values.

Because the matrix memory is a single \(d\times d\) object that accumulates outer products, its capacity is not tied linearly to the sequence length; it compresses the history into a fixed-size associative structure.

# Covariance update rule and ties to BAM and fast weights

The mLSTM update is a gated and normalized variant of a classical covariance or outer-product learning rule. The plain covariance update (no gates) would be
\[
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; \mathbf{C}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top,
\]
a rule that dates back to early associative memory models such as Bidirectional Associative Memories (BAMs) and related works (Kohonen, Anderson, Nakano, Sejnowski, Dayan, ...). In the mLSTM this basic rule is augmented by the multiplicative gates \(f_t\) and \(i_t\), which respectively implement decay of the old memory and a controllable learning rate for the new outer product.

The paper emphasizes two classical connections. First, when keys are zero-mean (the authors assume a layer-normalization before the key/value projections), the covariance update is optimal in the sense of maximizing separability of retrieved binary vectors, which can be interpreted as maximizing signal-to-noise ratio in retrieval. Second, the outer-product accumulation is equivalent in spirit to Fast Weight Programmers: an explicit, rapidly changing weight matrix is formed from recent inputs (outer products), and it can be read out by multiplying by a query. Many subsequent architectures use the same building block (rank-1 updates, optional decay and learning rates) to build compressive associative memories.

One practical point made in the paper is that higher separability can be achieved only by allowing pairwise interactions of tokens at retrieval time (which leads to quadratic attention). The covariance update rule is a compromise: it yields strong associative capacity with a fixed-size memory and a linear-in-time update at the cost of retaining only second-order (outer-product) correlations rather than full pairwise access to all stored interactions.

# Gate roles: input as learning rate, forget as decay, output as scaling

Gates in mLSTM take on natural interpretations in the matrix-memory setting. The forward formulas from the paper state the gates as
\[
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t),\qquad
f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)\ \text{or}\ \exp(\tilde{f}_t),\qquad
\mathbf{o}_t = \sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t),
\]
with the pre-activations \(\tilde{i}_t,\tilde{f}_t,\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t\) produced by affine projections of the input \(\mathbf{x}_t\). Because \(i_t\) is strictly positive (exponential) it behaves like a positive learning rate: it controls how strongly the new outer product \(\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top\) is added to memory. The forget gate \(f_t\) multiplies the entire previous matrix by a scalar, thereby implementing an exponential decay on all previously stored contributions; older entries are attenuated multiplicatively by the product of future forget gates. The output gate \(\mathbf{o}_t\) is a vector gate that scales the retrieved vector elementwise just before producing the hidden state.

Putting these pieces into the mLSTM memory update and readout,
\[
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top, \qquad
\mathbf{h}_t \;=\; \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t,
\]
makes the analogy clear: \(i_t\) is the per-step learning rate for the fast weight, \(f_t\) is the retention/decay multiplier, and \(\mathbf{o}_t\) shapes the final returned vector dimension-wise. Because the model learns the pre-activations \(\tilde{i}_t,\tilde{f}_t,\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t\) from \(\mathbf{x}_t\), it can adaptively decide when to store, forget, and expose memory to downstream layers.

# Query-based retrieval and division by a guarded normalizer

Retrieval in mLSTM is query-driven: the model projects the current input \(\mathbf{x}_t\) to a query \(\mathbf{q}_t\), which is used to read the associative memory:
\[
\mathbf{q}_t = \mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_q,\qquad
\mathbf{k}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\,\mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_k,\qquad
\mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_v.
\]
The immediate (unnormalized) retrieval is \(\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t\). To keep the scale of the retrieved vector consistent across different histories and gating strengths, the mLSTM divides this retrieval by a scalar normalizer based on a running sum of keys:
\[
\mathbf{n}_t \;=\; f_t \mathbf{n}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \mathbf{k}_t,
\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\big\{\,|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,\;1\,\big\}}.
\]
Here \(\mathbf{n}_t\) collects the keys, weighted by their input gate and decayed by future forget gates; it therefore records the total “mass” of key contributions that are still present in the memory. The scalar \(\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t\) measures how much the current query aligns, in aggregate, with the active keys in memory. Dividing the raw retrieval \(\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t\) by that aggregate alignment produces a scale-invariant readout that behaves roughly like a normalized weighted average of the stored values.

To see this more explicitly, substitute the unrolled forms of \(\mathbf{C}_t\) and \(\mathbf{n}_t\). The numerator is a sum of terms proportional to \(\mathbf{v}_s (\mathbf{k}_s^\top \mathbf{q}_t)\), while the denominator is (up to gating weights) a sum of the scalars \((\mathbf{k}_s^\top \mathbf{q}_t)\). The division therefore yields a value that depends on relative key–query similarities rather than on absolute magnitudes of gate strength or key norms, which stabilizes the output across different contexts.

# Key scaling and denominator thresholding for stability

Two pragmatic choices in the mLSTM design improve numerical stability of the key–query interactions. First, keys are scaled by \(1/\sqrt{d}\) in their projection:
\[
\mathbf{k}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\,\mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_k.
\]
This scaling counteracts the typical growth of dot products with vector dimension \(d\), keeping the distribution of \(\mathbf{k}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t\) well-behaved as \(d\) increases. The practice mirrors the scaled dot-product trick used in Transformer attention and controls variance of inner products.

Second, the denominator in the retrieval formula uses an absolute value and a lower bound:
\[
\max\big\{\,|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,\;1\,\big\}.
\]
There are two reasons for these choices. The absolute value prevents sign cancellations or flips from producing an effectively “negative mass” in the denominator; we are interested in the magnitude of the aggregate key–query alignment as a normalizing quantity. The threshold (commonly chosen to be \(1.0\)) ensures that when the aggregate alignment is very small—due to orthogonal keys, small gate strengths, or other factors—the division will not amplify noise by dividing by a near-zero scalar. This guarded normalization had been adopted in prior work (e.g., Retention) and is a small but important safeguard against unstable large outputs.

# Exponential gate stabilization carried over from sLSTM

mLSTM uses exponential activation for the input gate \(i_t\) (and optionally for the forget gate \(f_t\)), which guarantees positivity and maps the learned pre-activation into an interpretable multiplicative factor (learning rate / decay). However, plain exponentials can produce extremely large values and overflow floating-point representations when pre-activations grow. To avoid this, the authors reuse the stabilization trick introduced for sLSTM.

The stabilizer maintains an auxiliary scalar state \(m_t\) defined by
\[
m_t \;=\; \max\!\big( \log(f_t) + m_{t-1},\; \log(i_t) \big).
\]
Using \(m_t\) we form stabilized gates
\[
i'_t \;=\; \exp\!\big(\log(i_t) - m_t\big) \;=\; \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t),
\qquad
f'_t \;=\; \exp\!\big(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t\big).
\]
Replacing \(i_t\) by \(i'_t\) and \(f_t\) by \(f'_t\) in the forward pass prevents intermediate exponentials from overflowing while preserving the relative ratios that matter for the dynamics. The paper reports (and proves in its appendix) that this substitution does not change the network outputs nor the gradients with respect to parameters; it merely changes the internal numeric pathway to one that is robust to very large or very small gate values. In mLSTM this stabilization is applied in the same way as in sLSTM.

# Multiple cells vs. heads: equivalence without memory mixing

A structural consequence of mLSTM is that multiple cells and multiple heads collapse to equivalent designs in the absence of memory mixing. In architectures like the classic vector LSTM, multiple scalar cells can interact via recurrent hidden–hidden weight matrices, producing memory mixing across cells. In mLSTM there are no recurrent hidden–hidden connections driving memory mixing inside the associative matrix: the matrix memory evolves only by scalar-multiplied persistence and rank-1 updates driven by the input. Because of this independence, splitting the memory into several parallel matrices (multiple cells) or into multiple heads (partitioned subspaces) yields equivalent functional forms—each head or cell simply maintains its own \(\mathbf{C}\)-matrix and processes updates independently, and the model combines the resulting retrievals.

The practical consequence is that designers may choose to realize capacity as either multiple heads or multiple matrices without changing the model class: adding heads is a straightforward way to parallelize computation or to shape the inductive bias (for instance, to encourage specialization), but it does not introduce cross-head memory mixing that would change the expressiveness beyond what independent matrices provide.

# Parallel formulation (scan) and computational complexity (O(d^2))

Two computational facts about mLSTM are important for understanding its trade-offs.

First, the per-step computational and memory complexity is dominated by \(d\times d\) operations. Forming the outer product \(i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top\) and adding it to \(\mathbf{C}_{t-1}\) is an \(O(d^2)\) operation; multiplying \(\mathbf{C}_t\) by the query \(\mathbf{q}_t\) to produce \(\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t\) is another \(O(d^2)\) matrix–vector product. Multiplying the stored matrix by a scalar forget gate is also \(O(d^2)\). Thus the per-step arithmetic cost scales as \(O(d^2)\). On the other hand the memory footprint of the recurrent state is a single \(d\times d\) matrix (or a small number of such matrices when using multiple heads), so the recurrent memory requirement is constant with respect to sequence length—unlike Transformer attention which stores per-token keys and values.

Second, because the mLSTM recurrence does not involve hidden-to-hidden mixing (no recurrent weight matrices that reorder or mix hidden components across time), the update recurrence can be reformulated into a parallel (scan) computation. Conceptually, the update
\[
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top
\]
is a linear recurrence in \(\mathbf{C}\) with a scalar multiplicative factor plus an additive rank-1 term. Such recurrences can be re-expressed using associative prefix operations (scan) so that large segments of the sequence work can be combined in parallel. That property is what the authors mean when they claim mLSTM is “fully parallelizable” (they point to an appendix for a full parallel reformulation). In practice, this means mLSTM can be implemented efficiently on GPUs by casting the updates and retrievals into standard matrix operations that are highly optimized, and that one can apply parallel-scan algorithms if desired to further accelerate across long sequences.

The trade-off is explicit: mLSTM gives a powerful, compressive associative memory with constant memory per sequence length and fully parallel formulation, but this comes at the cost of \(O(d^2)\) per-step computation and a \(d\times d\) recurrent state. The paper notes that the matrix memory itself contains no learned parameters (the projections to keys/values/queries and the gating parameters carry the learned capacity), and that although the computational cost is high, the operations are amenable to GPU parallelism so the wall-clock penalty can be modest with optimized kernels.

# Summary

mLSTM extends the LSTM idea by turning the scalar cell into a matrix associative memory that stores key–value pairs as outer products and reads them via queries. The covariance (outer-product) update is interpreted as a gated fast-weight rule: the input gate acts as a learned, positive learning rate; the forget gate applies scalar decay to previously stored associations; and the output gate scales the retrieved vector. Retrieval is normalized by a guarded normalizer built from the weighted sum of keys, with key scaling and thresholding used to ensure numerical stability. Exponential gates are stabilized using a log-max trick inherited from sLSTM so that the positive multiplicative factors do not overflow. Because there is no hidden–hidden memory mixing, multiple heads are equivalent to multiple independent memory cells and the recurrent recurrence can be re-expressed in a parallel (scan-like) form; the main computational cost per time step is \(O(d^2)\) due to the matrix memory operations, while memory use is constant in sequence length. Together, these choices realize a high-capacity associative memory that is both expressive and amenable to parallel implementation on modern accelerators.

xLSTM Blocks and Full Architectures

# Design goal: nonlinear summarization in high-dimensional spaces

The central design goal of an xLSTM block is to produce a compact, discriminative summary of the past that makes different histories (contexts) easier to separate for a downstream linear predictor. In autoregressive sequence tasks such as language modeling, the predictor needs to distinguish subtle differences in past context in order to produce the correct next token. Rather than keeping raw token streams or shallow linear summaries, each xLSTM block is intended to non-linearly compress the history into a representation where distinct contexts are more linearly separable. This non-linear summarization is the functional role of the block: compress information about the entire prefix into a fixed-dimensional representation that downstream layers can read out effectively.

Practically, achieving strong non-linear summarization means combining (1) a recurrent or memory-centered core that updates an internal state from the new input and previous summary and (2) projection stages that change the working dimensionality before and after the core. Which side of the projection the core operates in (low-dimensional embedding space versus a higher up-projected space) has important consequences for the memory capacity and for which computations are performed in parallel versus sequentially. The remainder of this chapter explains the theoretical motivation for moving computations into higher-dimensional spaces and the two concrete block styles xLSTM uses to realize that goal.

# Cover’s Theorem motivation for up-projection

Cover’s Theorem motivates the use of high-dimensional embeddings for non-linear summarization. In the words used in the paper, Cover’s Theorem states that “in a higher dimensional space non-linearly embedded patterns can more likely be linearly separated than in the original space.” The practical upshot is that a non-linear mapping into a sufficiently high-dimensional space increases the chance that different histories (which may be entangled in the original embedding space) become linearly separable after the mapping.

This insight directly informs the choice to include up-projection and down-projection stages around the recurrent/memory core: by mapping inputs into a larger intermediate dimension, applying non-linear memory updates there, and then projecting back, the block can create high-capacity, highly separable summaries of history. Two canonical choices arise for where to place that up-projection relative to the nonlinear memory core; the xLSTM design uses both in different situations (post up-projection and pre up-projection), selected according to the memory primitive used in the core.

# Post up-projection blocks (Transformer-like) for sLSTM

The post up-projection style is the block shape that mirrors the common “Transformer-like” ordering: the block first non-linearly summarizes the past in the original embedding space, and only afterwards applies an up-projection into a higher-dimensional space, followed by a non-linear activation and a projection back down. In xLSTM this style is used for blocks that contain the sLSTM memory core.

Intuitively, sLSTM extends the classic scalar LSTM cell with exponential gating and a normalizer state, but it retains memory mixing via hidden-to-gate and hidden-to-input recurrent connections. That memory mixing—recurrent interactions between the hidden state and the gates/inputs—means the sLSTM inherently performs sequential, history-dependent computation in the input-space. Placing the core computation in the original space (and then up-projecting) lets the sLSTM perform its state-tracking and memory-mixing behavior in the space where those recurrent connections are defined, while still obtaining the advantages of an up-projection for downstream separability.

A concise description of the sLSTM core (reproduced from the xLSTM formulation) is:
\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t &= f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t,\\
n_t &= f_t \, n_{t-1} + i_t,\\
\tilde{h}_t &= c_t / n_t,\\
h_t &= o_t \, \tilde{h}_t,
\end{aligned}
\]
with gate preactivations and inputs that can depend on the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$, e.g.
\[
\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_i h_{t-1} + b_i,\quad
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t),
\]
and similarly for $\tilde{z}_t$, $\tilde{f}_t$, and $\tilde{o}_t$ (the forget gate can be either $\sigma(\tilde{f}_t)$ or $\exp(\tilde{f}_t)$). Because sLSTM keeps hidden-dependent recurrent terms $r_\cdot h_{t-1}$, it is not fully parallelizable across time; this is why the post up-projection pattern (compute recurrence in the original space, then lift) is the natural choice for sLSTM-containing blocks.

# Pre up-projection blocks (SSM-like) for mLSTM

By contrast, the pre up-projection style maps inputs first into a higher-dimensional space and then performs the non-linear summarization (the memory update) in that larger space before projecting back down. xLSTM uses the pre up-projection block for mLSTM cores.

The mLSTM replaces the scalar cell with a matrix-valued memory $\mathbf{C}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and uses a covariance (outer-product) update rule. Because the mLSTM memory is fundamentally a $d\times d$ structure, the memory capacity grows with the internal dimensionality; therefore it benefits from being operated on in a higher-dimensional working space. Mapping into that space first (pre up-projection) expands the representational capacity available for keys, values and the matrix memory updates, so the covariance update can store richer associations.

The mLSTM core is specified as:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{C}_t &= f_t \, \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \, \mathbf{v}_t \, \mathbf{k}_t^\top,\\
\mathbf{n}_t &= f_t \, \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \, \mathbf{k}_t,\\
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t &= \dfrac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{\,|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|,\,1\,\}},\\
\mathbf{h}_t &= \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t,
\end{aligned}
\]
with linear projections from the input $\mathbf{x}_t$ to query/key/value:
\[
\mathbf{q}_t = \mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_q,\quad
\mathbf{k}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_k,\quad
\mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_v,
\]
and input-only gate preactivations in the parallel formulation, e.g.
\[
\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + b_i,\qquad i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t).
\]
Because mLSTM omits hidden-dependent recurrent mixing in these gate formulas, the updates can be formulated in a parallel (time-wise) fashion, enabling efficient batched computation. The map-then-memory style (pre up-projection) leverages larger internal dimensionality to grow memory capacity via the $d\times d$ matrix $\mathbf{C}_t$.

# Pre-LayerNorm residual backbones

xLSTM architectures are assembled by residually stacking xLSTM blocks into deep networks using the common pre-LayerNorm residual backbone that contemporary large models favor. In practice, each block sits inside a residual frame: the input to the block is first normalized (pre-LayerNorm), the block computes its nonlinear summary (including the sLSTM or mLSTM core and the up/down projections appropriate to that core), and the result is added back to the block input as a residual connection. The paper follows this standard backbone choice because it is the most common practice in modern large language models.

You can view the backbone at a high level as iterating a transform of the normalized state and adding a skip connection; in compact form the layer-to-layer update can be expressed as
\[
\mathbf{x}_{\ell+1} = \mathbf{x}_\ell + \mathrm{Block}\big(\mathrm{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{x}_\ell)\big),
\]
where $\mathrm{Block}(\cdot)$ is an xLSTM block (either pre-up or post-up variant). Using pre-LayerNorm with residual connections keeps individual block inputs well-scaled and supports stable training when many blocks are stacked.

# xLSTM[a:b] notation: mixing mLSTM and sLSTM blocks

To specify a hybrid xLSTM architecture the paper introduces the compact notation xLSTM[$a$:$b$], where $a/b$ denotes the ratio of mLSTM-based blocks versus sLSTM-based blocks in the full model. For instance, xLSTM[7:1] means that out of every 8 blocks, 7 are mLSTM-based and 1 is sLSTM-based. When applying that to a full stack of 48 blocks, xLSTM[7:1] corresponds to 42 mLSTM blocks and 6 sLSTM blocks.

This ratio notation communicates the intended balance between (i) matrix-memory, fully parallelizable blocks (mLSTM) that provide large associative storage and (ii) scalar-memory, memory-mixing blocks (sLSTM) that permit recurrent state-mixing useful for state tracking. Across experiments in the paper, xLSTM instances were built using pre up-projection blocks for mLSTM and post up-projection blocks for sLSTM, consistent with the memory characteristics of each core.

# Stacking strategy and depth considerations

Stacking strategy in xLSTM centers on two choices: the total number of residual blocks (depth) and the allocation of those blocks between mLSTM and sLSTM types (the $a\!:\!b$ ratio). Both choices influence final model capacity, parallelism, compute cost, and the set of tasks the model is well-suited to.

Depth: increasing the number of residually stacked blocks increases the number of nonlinear summarization stages applied to the history. Deeper stacks allow the model to build more expressive hierarchical summaries, which in turn tends to improve modeling power and scaling behavior (as observed for many deep architectures). In experiments reported in the paper, a common large configuration used 48 residual blocks for xLSTM experiments; as a point of comparison, a GPT-3-style 350M model uses 24 residual blocks with embedding dimension 1024.

Mixing ratio: the $a\!:\!b$ choice trades off between memory capacity and state-tracking expressivity. Since mLSTM blocks use matrix memory and are parallelizable, they are a natural choice for the majority of blocks when one wants maximal memory capacity and efficient training/inference. sLSTM blocks, by contrast, add memory mixing via recurrent hidden-to-gate terms which enable state-tracking behaviors that other parallel cores cannot express. The paper’s experiments show that both purely-mLSTM stacks (xLSTM[1:0]) and mixed stacks (e.g., xLSTM[7:1]) perform very well; in practice one can place a small fraction of sLSTM blocks within a predominantly mLSTM stack to give the model explicit sequential memory-mixing ability without losing most of the parallelism and memory capacity.

Practical placement: the $a\!:\!b$ notation does not fix a particular interleaving order; implementations may distribute the sLSTM blocks throughout the stack (sparsely) or group them. The experimental xLSTMs in the paper follow the convention that pre up-projection is used for mLSTM blocks and post up-projection for sLSTM blocks, and they evaluate several mixing ratios to study the trade-offs.

# Practical block components: activations, projections, and norms

Each xLSTM block is constructed from a small set of practical, well-defined components: linear projections (up/down), activation functions for cell inputs, gating nonlinearities, normalizers, and optional stabilizers. The paper specifies these components for both sLSTM and mLSTM cores; here we collect and explain them as they appear inside blocks.

Activations and gates. The sLSTM and mLSTM share the same basic gate design but differ in where recurrent dependence appears. Typical choices include:
- Cell-input and state non-linearities $\varphi$ and $\psi$ (often implemented as $\tanh$).
- Input gate using an exponential activation: $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$.
- Forget gate using either a sigmoid $\sigma(\tilde{f}_t)$ or an exponential $\exp(\tilde{f}_t)$.
- Output gate is a sigmoid: $o_t = \sigma(\tilde{o}_t)$.

Stabilizer for exponential gates. Exponential gates can grow very large numerically; xLSTM uses a stabilizer state $m_t$ (following the formulation in the paper) to avoid overflow while preserving equivalence of outputs and gradients. The stabilizer update and stabilized gates are:
\[
\begin{aligned}
m_t &= \max\big(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1},\ \log(i_t)\big),\\
i'_t &= \exp\big(\log(i_t) - m_t\big) = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t),\\
f'_t &= \exp\big(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t\big).
\end{aligned}
\]
Replacing $i_t$ and $f_t$ by $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ in the forward pass removes numerical instabilities without changing the computed outputs or parameter gradients.

Normalizers and readout scaling. Both sLSTM and mLSTM maintain a normalizer state $n_t$ that keeps track of the effective magnitude of stored contributions (the paper describes it as summing the product of input gate times all future forget gates). In sLSTM this normalizer is scalar and the hidden pre-output is $\tilde{h}_t = c_t/n_t$. In mLSTM the normalizer $\mathbf{n}_t$ is a vector and the readout is scaled by the dot product $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ with a lower bound:
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{\,|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|,\,1\,\}}.
\]
Taking the absolute value and a lower bound (threshold typically set to 1) guards against tiny denominators and was adopted in the paper to stabilize retrieval.

Projections and layer normalization. mLSTM relies on linear projections from input to query/key/value:
\[
\mathbf{q}_t = \mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_q,\quad
\mathbf{k}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_k,\quad
\mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_v.
\]
The paper assumes a layer normalization is applied before projecting inputs to keys and values so that the projected keys and values have (approximately) zero-mean, which is important for the covariance-style update to behave as intended.

Heads and cells. sLSTM supports multiple memory cells and can be arranged with multiple heads: within each head memory mixing (recurrent hidden-to-gate and hidden-to-input connections) is allowed, but there is no memory mixing across heads. This introduces a structured form of memory mixing where heads are independent mixing units. For mLSTM, the paper notes that multiple heads and multiple cells are equivalent because there is no memory mixing across time in the parallel formulation.

Compute/memory considerations. The mLSTM’s matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t$ and its $d\times d$ outer-product update are computationally heavier than scalar-cell updates, but the operations are parameter-free (the memory itself is not parameterized) and are parallelizable across the sequence. The sLSTM’s mixing via recurrent connections prevents a fully parallel time-batched implementation, but the authors report efficient CUDA kernels that keep sLSTM wall-clock overhead moderate in practice.

---

Putting these components together, an xLSTM architecture is formed by choosing a backbone depth, assigning each residual block as either an mLSTM or sLSTM block (and therefore a pre- or post-up-projection layout), and instantiating the practical gate/activation/projection choices described above. The combined effect is a family of networks that preserve the LSTM ideas (constant-error carousel and gating) while leveraging modern architectural practices (residual pre-LayerNorm backbones, projection-based up-sampling, and parallelizable matrix memories) to scale favorably for large language modeling.

Computation, Memory, and Parallelization

# Linear compute and constant memory in sequence length

A fundamental design goal of xLSTM is to keep the work and the working memory required to process a sequence bounded in a way that does not blow up as the sequence gets longer. The paper emphasizes two related claims: (1) xLSTM networks have linear computation with respect to the sequence length, and (2) xLSTM networks have constant memory complexity with respect to the sequence length. It is important to be precise about what those statements mean.

"Linear computation in the sequence length" means that if the model processes a sequence of length $T$, the total amount of arithmetic work grows proportional to $T$ (for fixed model dimensions). In Big‑O notation this is $O(T)$ for fixed hidden dimension(s). This contrasts with architectures whose per‑token work increases with previously seen tokens. For xLSTM the per‑token work is fixed (though it depends on model dimensionality $d$ and on whether a matrix or vector memory is used), so the total compute over a sequence of length $T$ is proportional to $T$.

"Constant memory complexity in the sequence length" means that the working memory the model needs to hold the summarized history does not grow with $T$. In xLSTM the past is summarized (compressed) into a fixed set of internal states: scalar or vector cell states for sLSTM, or a fixed matrix memory for mLSTM. Those states occupy memory that depends on the model dimensions (for example on $d$ or on $d^2$) but not on $T$. Practically, that means that decoding and long‑context inference need not allocate a per‑position key/value cache that grows with the sequence length; rather the historical information is folded into a fixed-size memory.

To anchor the intuition in the paper's equations: in sLSTM the recurrence keeps a (scalar or vector) cell $c_t$ (and a normalizer $n_t$) that summarize all past contributions via multiplicative gates (see the sLSTM forward pass). In mLSTM the memory is a fixed matrix $\mathbf{C}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ that is repeatedly updated by outer products, so the memory footprint is tied to $d$ rather than to $T$. Because each time step updates and/or queries these fixed-size states, total compute across a sequence of length $T$ is $O(T)$ (with per-step cost determined by the memory representation), and the memory used to hold the state is independent of $T$.

This compressive strategy contrasts with Transformer decoders that store per‑position key/value pairs for the whole context. Those per‑position caches cause memory usage to grow with $T$ and contribute to higher generation-time costs for long contexts (see the experimental comparisons in the paper).

# mLSTM’s O(d^2) cost and parallel GPU execution

mLSTM increases the LSTM memory cell from a scalar (or vector) to a matrix $\mathbf{C}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. The matrix memory is updated by a covariance (outer‑product) rule:

\[
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; f_t \,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \,\mathbf{v}_t \,\mathbf{k}_t^\top,
\]

and retrieval is (schematically)

\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \,\mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,\,1\}},
\]

where $\mathbf{k}_t,\mathbf{v}_t,\mathbf{q}_t\in\mathbb{R}^d$ are key, value and query vectors and $\mathbf{n}_t$ is the normalizer. Two operations dominate the per‑step cost: forming the outer product $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ and multiplying the matrix $\mathbf{C}_t$ by the query $\mathbf{q}_t$. Both of these are quadratic in the hidden dimension, i.e., they require on the order of $d^2$ scalar operations. In complexity terms the per‑token arithmetic for mLSTM is $O(d^2)$, so for a full sequence of length $T$ the work is $O(T\cdot d^2)$.

The paper emphasizes that this $d^2$ cost is a tradeoff: raising memory capacity substantially (from scalar or vector memory to matrix memory) increases computational load. However, two important mitigating facts follow from the design. First, the matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t$ is not a parameter tensor; it is a dynamic state that carries information about the sequence but does not inflate the number of learned parameters. Second, the $d\times d$ arithmetic—outer products and matrix–vector multiplications—is composed of standard linear algebra operations that GPUs handle well. Because the updates and retrievals are regular dense matrix operations, they can be expressed and executed in parallel using GPU kernels and batched matrix routines. The paper explicitly notes that the mLSTM recurrence can be reformulated into a parallel version (see appendix), and that the computations parallelize analogously to other GPU‑efficient attention or scan implementations.

Thus, the practical tradeoff for mLSTM is: larger representational capacity (matrix memory, better rare‑token performance and associative recall) versus higher arithmetic cost per token ($O(d^2)$), with the hope that optimized parallel kernels will reduce wall‑clock overhead.

# sLSTM’s sequential nature due to memory mixing

sLSTM augments the LSTM idea with exponential gating and new normalization, while retaining inter‑cell memory mixing through recurrent (hidden‑to‑hidden) connections. In other words, sLSTM variants include terms where the current computation depends on the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$ (for example via recurrent matrices $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{z}},\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{i}},\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{f}},\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{o}}$ in the multi‑cell extension). Those hidden‑to‑hidden connections implement memory mixing across cells and time.

Because each time step's gate activations and inputs depend on the previous hidden state, computation cannot be trivially parallelized across time steps: you must compute $h_{t-1}$ before computing $h_t$. This sequential dependence is the reason sLSTM is not parallelizable in the same way as mLSTM. The paper frames this drawback but also highlights the payoff: memory mixing enables state tracking and other sequence‑sensitive computations that non‑mixing architectures (many SSMs or attention variants) cannot perform as effectively. In empirical terms, sLSTM's memory mixing helped solve formal language and state‑tracking tasks that were challenging for strictly parallel models.

Recognizing the practical cost of sequential processing, the authors implemented a highly optimized CUDA kernel for sLSTM with GPU memory optimizations down to the register level. That implementation narrows the performance gap: the paper reports that their optimized sLSTM kernel is typically less than two times slower than their (parallel) mLSTM implementation. So, while sLSTM enforces sequential computation and so is inherently less parallel across time, careful low‑level implementation can make it competitive in wall‑clock time while preserving the expressivity benefits of memory mixing.

# Parallels to FlashAttention and GLA for efficient scans

The paper draws explicit parallels between mLSTM and GPU‑efficient attention/scan implementations such as FlashAttention and Gated Linear Attention (GLA). The comparison is an analogy about implementation style and parallelizability rather than an identity of algorithms: FlashAttention and GLA are techniques designed to reduce memory overhead and improve GPU throughput by restructuring attention or gated scans into blockwise, cache‑friendly computations. Similarly, mLSTM's matrix memory and its update/retrieval patterns can be expressed as dense linear algebra operations that lend themselves to blocked, parallel execution on GPUs.

This parallelizability is significant because it points to a path for improving mLSTM performance: just as FlashAttention reshaped attention to gain large speedups and lower memory pressure, mLSTM can benefit from the same engineering principles (blocked kernels, attention to memory tiling, fused operations). The paper remarks that faster CUDA kernels for mLSTM could be obtained "in the vein of FlashAttention," indicating that further engineering work—reorganizing the $d\times d$ updates and multiplications into cache‑efficient GPU kernels—should materially close the gap between algorithmic promise and realized throughput.

# CUDA implementation notes and current performance gaps

The paper is explicit about the current state of implementations and practical performance tradeoffs. Two implementation points stand out.

First, sLSTM received a specialized CUDA implementation with register‑level memory optimizations. That implementation reduces the penalty of sequential recurrence: empirically, the optimized sLSTM kernel is typically less than two times slower than the parallel mLSTM kernel used by the authors. This demonstrates that careful kernel engineering can compensate substantially for the lack of timewise parallelism.

Second, mLSTM's CUDA kernels in the presented work are not yet fully optimized. The authors report that their current mLSTM implementation is about four times slower than FlashAttention or the scan used in Mamba. In other words, although mLSTM admits a parallel formulation and is amenable to efficient dense linear algebra, the available kernels in this work left sizable headroom for improvement. The paper explicitly notes that faster kernels could be realized following the strategies used by FlashAttention.

There are also practical caveats from the experimental setup. For example, when comparing generation times the authors used existing implementations from HuggingFace for baselines and included a static key‑value cache for the Transformer baseline; they also mention that compilation/optimization attempts (e.g., full cache compilation of the Transformer model or compiling Mamba with \texttt{torch.compile}) did not work at the time of the experiments. These remarks emphasize that empirical speed comparisons are sensitive to low‑level engineering and that the reported gaps reflect both algorithmic and current‑implementation status.

# Inference behavior: generation latency and throughput

The paper reports inference‑time measurements that illustrate the practical implications of the computation and memory characteristics. Two metrics are highlighted: generation latency as context length grows, and decoding throughput (tokens per second) as batch size and prefill settings vary.

On generation latency, the empirical finding is that xLSTM and other recurrent models (Mamba and RWKV in the comparison) exhibit linear scaling of generation time with respect to context length, while the Transformer baseline (Llama in the experiments) shows quadratic growth in generation time. The experiments used 1.3B‑parameter models and a test condition with batch size 1 and a pre‑fill of 16 tokens (a setting favorable to the Transformer), and nevertheless the xLSTM models showed linear growth in wall‑clock time as the context length increased.

For decoding throughput, the constant memory footprint of xLSTM pays dividends: because xLSTM does not need to allocate a per‑position cache whose size grows with context length, it can support much larger batch sizes during decoding. The paper reports that xLSTM achieves the highest throughput in their throughput measurements precisely because it can use higher batch sizes than the Transformer baseline (whose memory use limits batch size). In practical terms, this means xLSTM can produce more tokens per second when the decoding scenario permits batching, which is an important industrial metric when serving many requests in parallel.

These empirical points tie directly to the theoretical complexity observations: constant memory per request lets you pack more requests or larger batches into the same device memory, and $O(T)$ computational scaling gives predictable latency growth as you extend context.

# Memory footprint vs. KV caches in Transformers

A key cause of Transformers' growing memory footprint with context length is the per‑position key/value cache used during auto‑regressive decoding. Each decoded token typically contributes a key and a value for every attention layer; keeping these cached representations for later attention raises memory usage proportionally to the context length and to the number of layers. In practice this per‑position caching is what forces Transformers to consume substantially more memory for long contexts, and it constrains batch sizes during decoding.

xLSTM takes the opposite design choice: rather than preserving the entire sequence of positional representations, it compresses past information into fixed‑size internal states. For sLSTM, the compressed state is scalar or vector cell states (or multiple cells); for mLSTM, it is a matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t$ of size $d\times d$ that accumulates outer‑product updates. In complexity terms, the Transformer cache memory grows like $O(T)$ (times depth and $d$), whereas xLSTM memory is $O(d)$ for sLSTM (or $O(d^2)$ for mLSTM) and does not include an extra factor of $T$.

That difference explains why xLSTM can sustain larger inference batch sizes and why its per‑request memory remains constant as context length grows. The paper summarizes this succinctly: "Contrary to Transformers, xLSTM networks have a linear computation and a constant memory complexity with respect to the sequence length," and it uses that property to motivate industrial and edge deployments.

# Industrial implications: compressive memory and edge deployment

The computational and memory characteristics of xLSTM give rise to concrete deployment implications that matter in industrial and edge settings. First, the compressive, constant‑size memory is a strong advantage where device memory is constrained. Edge devices (or inference servers that prioritize high concurrency) benefit when the per‑request working set does not grow with the requested context length. Because xLSTM summarises histories into fixed-size states rather than caching full histories, it enables longer effective context handling without proportionally larger memory footprint.

Second, throughput considerations favor xLSTM in batched serving scenarios: with constant memory per request, a single GPU can hold more concurrent requests and thus achieve higher aggregate tokens‑per‑second throughput. The experiments in the paper show that xLSTM achieves the highest throughput among the compared models, driven by the ability to use higher batch sizes than Transformers with KV caches.

Third, there is a nuanced tradeoff between capacity and computational cost. mLSTM delivers richer memory capacity (matrix memory) that improves rare‑token modeling and associative recall, but it incurs an $O(d^2)$ per‑token cost. In production this translates to a choice: allocate more compute to get better memory behavior (mLSTM), or choose a cheaper vector memory variant (sLSTM) and accept different expressivity/parallelism characteristics. The paper notes that mLSTM memory updates do not increase parameter count, so capacity can be increased at runtime by increasing $d$, but with the accompanying quadratic compute growth.

Finally, practical deployment requires attention to low‑level engineering. The authors acknowledge that mLSTM kernels in their work are not yet optimized to the utmost, and that further engineering (following ideas like FlashAttention) should reduce the implementation gap. They also highlight limitations to bear in mind in production: careful forget‑gate initialization is important, the matrix memory can become overloaded if contexts are extremely long (though the paper reports good behavior up to 16k contexts), and extensive architecture and hyperparameter optimization remain to be done.

In short, for industrial cases where memory predictability, long‑context handling, and high concurrency matter, xLSTM's compressive, constant‑memory design is a compelling alternative to per‑position caching Transformers. The final choice between sLSTM and mLSTM (or a hybrid xLSTM stack) should be guided by the workload's sensitivity to state‑tracking, memory capacity needs, and available compute and kernel optimization investment.

Positioning Among Prior Work

# Transformers and quadratic attention vs. linearized variants

Transformers, introduced with parallelizable self-attention as their core mechanism \citep{Vaswani:17}, reshaped sequence modeling and became the dominant architecture for large language models. Their strength comes from the ability to compute interactions between tokens in a way that can be parallelized across positions, which is a major reason they scale so well on modern accelerators and enabled the emergence of the first very large LLMs. At the same time, the pairwise token interactions that self-attention computes imply a cost that grows quadratically with context length; this quadratic dependence on sequence length is the central scalability bottleneck that many later methods attempt to mitigate.

Because of that quadratic scaling, a large body of follow-on work has focused on preserving the modeling power of attention while reducing its computational and memory footprint for long contexts. Some solutions try to approximate or restructure attention to obtain linear (or near-linear) complexity in the context length, while others propose entirely different sequence-processing primitives that are intrinsically linear in the context length. The tension is therefore between (a) the powerful, parallel and expressive token–token interactions of Transformers and (b) the need for linear resource usage for very long contexts or resource-constrained deployments. xLSTM positions itself in this landscape by recovering some of the key LSTM advantages (gating and memory mechanisms) while adopting memory structures and architectural choices that address the practical scaling concerns that motivated the linear/efficient-attention lines of work.

# Linear attention methods: Synthesizer, Linformer, Performer, GLA, Hyena

A first family of responses to the quadratic cost of standard attention consists of approaches that alter the attention computation so that it no longer requires all-pairs interactions. The paper groups several prominent proposals under the “linear attention” heading and briefly summarizes their strategies.

Synthesizer \citep{Tay:20arxiv} abandons explicit token–token similarity in favor of learned synthetic attention weights, thereby removing the requirement for token–token inner products. Linformer \citep{Wang:20arxiv} replaces the full attention matrix by a low-rank factorization that reduces memory and computation. Linear Transformer \citep{Katharopoulos:20} and related methods re-express attention in a way that lets the softmax kernel be linearized; Performer \citep{Choromanski:21} implements such a linearization through positive orthogonal random feature approximations to the softmax kernel. GLA (Gated Linear Attention) \citep{Yang:23arxiv} brings gating mechanisms into linear attention to improve flexibility. Other work replaces attention with long, efficient convolutions; for example, Structured Global Convolution (SGConv) \citep{Li:22} and the Hyena Hierarchy \citep{Poli:23} realize attention-like long-range computations with specially structured convolutions.

All these approaches aim to make the cost scale closer to linearly with the context length, thereby enabling very long-context processing. The trade-offs vary: some are approximations of softmax attention, others adopt structural inductive biases (convolutions, low-rank assumptions), and some add gating to recover flexibility. xLSTM should be read alongside these methods as another approach to linear-time sequence modeling that retains explicit recurrent-style memory but augments it with modern gates and memory structures designed for scalability.

# State Space Models: S4, DSS, GSS, S5, BiGS, H3, Mamba

State Space Models (SSMs) have emerged as a powerful class of sequence models that are intrinsically linear in the context length and which showed inspiring performance on long-context tasks. Representative instances include S4 \citep{Gu:21}, Diagonal State Space (DSS) \citep{Gupta:22}, Gated State Space (GSS) \citep{Mehta:22}, S5 \citep{Smith:22}, Bidirectional Gated SSM (BiGS) \citep{Wang:22}, H3 \citep{Fu:23}, and the recent Mamba \citep{Gu:24arxiv}. These models typically instantiate a linear dynamical system (or variants thereof) whose convolutional kernel or state matrices are engineered or learned so that convolutions over long contexts can be computed efficiently.

SSMs are attractive because they offer linear-time (and often constant-memory per position) algorithms for processing long sequences and can be integrated into residual/block-structured backbones used by contemporary LLMs. Several SSM variants also incorporate gating mechanisms (e.g., GSS, BiGS) to increase modeling flexibility. In the landscape painted by the paper, SSMs are one of the principal alternatives to Transformers and stand as natural competitors to xLSTM when one seeks linear scaling with sequence length.

# RNN families: HGRN/HGRN2, LRUs, RWKV

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) enjoyed dominance before Transformers and have seen a resurgence motivated by the need for efficient, long-context processing and the desire to preserve the recurrent inductive biases (sequential state update, compact internal memory). The paper highlights several recent RNN-derived architectures: Deep Linear Recurrent Units (LRUs) \citep{Orvieto:23, De:24arxiv}, Hierarchically Gated Linear RNNs (HGRN and HGRN2) \citep{Qin:23,Qin:24arxiv}, and the RWKV family \citep{Peng:23arxivshort,Peng:24arxivshort}. RWKV in particular has become a well-known RNN approach for large language modeling, showing that recurrent formulations can be competitive with Transformers when carefully engineered.

These RNN revivals typically emphasize linear complexity in the context length and often bring back or re-imagine gating mechanisms to control information flow. Importantly, they aim to reconcile the classical strengths of RNN-type stateful processing (compact memory, natural sequential updates) with the demands of modern LLM-scale training and inference. xLSTM sits within this cohort: it is an evolution of the LSTM family that borrows modern training and architectural practices while addressing well-known LSTM limitations (revisability of storage, capacity, and parallelizability).

# Covariance/retention-style updates: Fast Weights, Retention, RWKV-5/6

A closely related strand of work augments models with associative, matrix-based memories that implement fast updates of stored key–value pairs. The key mathematical idea is the covariance (outer-product) update:

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top,
\]

where a key vector $\mathbf{k}_t$ and value vector $\mathbf{v}_t$ are written into a matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t$ and later retrieved via a query $\mathbf{q}$. This update rule is a classical idea from associative memory literature and is equivalent to early “Fast Weight Programmers” \citep{Schmidhuber:92ncfastweights,Schlag:21}. The paper points out that, with zero-mean keys (for example after a layer norm), this covariance-rule is optimal in the sense of maximizing separability under certain retrieval objectives \citep{Dayan:91}.

Modern works have adapted this idea for scalable sequence modeling: fast weight updates can be combined with decay (a multiplicative forget/decay applied to $\mathbf{C}_{t-1}$) and scaled by a learning rate (analogous to input gates) \citep{Ba:16}. Retention-like models and recent RWKV variants (RWKV-5 and RWKV-6) adopt similar outer-product update mechanisms. The mLSTM in xLSTM is explicitly built on this covariance-update tradition: it keeps a matrix memory updated by outer products and uses gating (forget gate as decay; input gate as learning rate) and a normalizer state to stabilize retrieval.

A practical detail that appears in the literature and in xLSTM is normalization of retrieved values: because retrieval involves dot products between queries and accumulated key-weight sums, numerical stability and signal-to-noise considerations motivate dividing by a scalar proportional to the accumulated key weight (in xLSTM this is implemented as dividing by $\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|, 1\}$). This is one of several engineering choices that make covariance-style memories usable in large-scale models.

# What’s distinct in xLSTM: exponential gating + memory mixing

The contribution that most clearly distinguishes xLSTM from the prior families is the combination of two ideas applied to the LSTM paradigm: (1) exponential gating with explicit normalization and stabilization, and (2) a reworked memory structure that includes either a scalar, mixed memory (sLSTM) or a matrix, covariance-style memory (mLSTM).

Exponential gating. Traditional LSTM gates use a sigmoid nonlinearity to produce values in $(0,1)$, effectively attenuating contributions. xLSTM introduces exponential activation for gates (e.g., $i_t=\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ and optionally $f_t=\exp(\tilde{f}_t)$), which permits gate outputs in $(0,\infty)$ rather than being bound to $(0,1)$. Because exponentials can grow large, xLSTM adds a stabilizer state $m_t$ and a normalization mechanism that keeps the forward values and gradients numerically well behaved. For the scalar sLSTM the forward updates include a normalizer $n_t$ that accumulates gate-strength-weighted contributions (so the network keeps a running measure of how much effective input has been stored), and the hidden readout is normalized by this normalizer:

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t &= f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t,\\
n_t &= f_t n_{t-1} + i_t,\\
\tilde h_t &= c_t / n_t,\\
h_t &= o_t \tilde h_t .
\end{aligned}
\]

A stabilizer $m_t$ enforces numerically stable exponentials via a log-space max trick and rescales $i_t$ and $f_t$ accordingly (see the paper for the exact formulas). Intuitively, exponential gates plus the normalizer allow the model to revise past storage decisions more flexibly: because gates are not constrained to be attenuators, later inputs can amplify or de-amplify past contributions in a graded multiplicative fashion.

Memory mixing. The sLSTM retains the classical LSTM capability for memory mixing: when multiple scalar memory cells are arranged into a vector, recurrent weight matrices can mix the outputs of those cells, enabling richer state representations and the essential mechanism for state tracking. The paper highlights a new notion of memory mixing introduced for sLSTM: the model may be partitioned into heads, with memory mixing allowed across cells inside a head but not across heads — this provides an additional modularity in how the model distributes and mixes memory.

Matrix memory (mLSTM). To overcome the limited storage capacity of scalar cell-state vectors, xLSTM also introduces the mLSTM variant in which the memory cell is a matrix $\mathbf{C}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ updated by the covariance rule

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top,
\]

and readout uses matrix–vector multiplication $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$ normalized by the dot product between a normalizer vector $\mathbf{n}_t$ and the query $\mathbf{q}_t$. In mLSTM the forget gate plays the role of a decay (multiplicative forgetting of $\mathbf{C}_{t-1}$), while the input gate controls learning rate of the new outer-product update. Because mLSTM deliberately abandons recurrent hidden–to–hidden mixing (no hidden-hidden recurrent matrices), it becomes parallelizable across time steps: the matrix memory is updated and read using matrix operations that can be organized to avoid serial scan-like dependencies. The cost is that $\mathbf{C}_t$ is a $d\times d$ object and so updates are computationally heavier than scalar LSTM updates; but this trade-off is central to the design choices in xLSTM.

By combining exponential gating and these novel memory forms, xLSTM aims to get the best of several worlds: revisable gating that supports dynamic storage decisions, scalar-cell memory mixing when needed for expressivity (sLSTM), large associative storage via matrix memories when capacity is the bottleneck (mLSTM), and, in the mLSTM case, parallelizability that makes it more amenable to modern large-scale training and inference.

# Expressivity and state tracking theory: RNNs vs. SSMs/Transformers

A recurring theme in the paper is that the architectural differences above are not merely implementation choices but have implications for expressivity — what classes of sequence-processing problems a model can represent and solve. In particular, the paper (and recent literature it cites) highlights that RNNs with recurrent hidden–to–hidden mixing (as in classical LSTMs and in sLSTM) can implement state-tracking behaviors that some alternatives cannot.

State tracking refers to the need to maintain and update internal variables that summarize relevant aspects of the past in order to make correct future predictions. Tasks such as evaluating computer programs, tracking entities across a long narrative, or certain formal languages (e.g., parity) require the model not only to remember content but to maintain structured, revisable state that depends on complex interactions over time. The experiments reported in the paper show that models lacking memory mixing — such as many Transformers or SSMs in their standard formulations — fail to solve some of these state-tracking tasks (for example, certain regular-language or parity-style tasks). This empirical finding aligns with theoretical results indicating that, for some families of sequence problems, RNNs with appropriate recurrent structure are strictly more expressive than architectures that only rely on feedforward token–token interactions or linear state-space dynamics \citep{Merrill:24,Deletang:23}.

xLSTM addresses this divide by offering two complementary solutions in the same family: sLSTM reclaims memory mixing and thus the state-tracking expressivity of LSTMs, while mLSTM provides large associative memories and parallelizability. In practice, xLSTM architectures can mix sLSTM and mLSTM blocks so that both state tracking and high-capacity associative memory are available in a single stacked network. This design choice is motivated by the observation that language modeling and related sequence tasks often require both kinds of behavior: robust, revisable state for algorithmic or tracking problems, and large capacity for encoding rare tokens and long-range associations.

# Residual stacking as the shared architectural backbone

Despite the heterogeneity of sequence-modeling primitives (attention, SSMs, RNNs, covariance memories), a common architectural trend in recent large models is to organize processing into residually stacked blocks, typically with pre-layer-norm normalization \citep{Ba:16b}. The paper situates xLSTM within this modern practice: xLSTM variants (sLSTM and mLSTM) are integrated into residual block backbones that resemble the pre-LayerNorm residual modules used in Transformers and SSM-based models.

Two residual block styles are described in the paper: a post up-projection block (Transformer-like) that first nonlinearly summarizes the past in the original representational space and then projects up into a higher-dimensional space for further processing, and a pre up-projection block (SSM-like) that projects first into a high-dimensional space and then performs non-linear summarization there. The choice matters: for xLSTM, sLSTM blocks are paired with post up-projection (reflecting that the scalar cell and its mixing operate naturally in the original space), while mLSTM blocks are paired with pre up-projection (so that the matrix memory lives in the higher-dimensional embedding space, maximizing capacity).

The motivation for embedding summaries into higher-dimensional spaces is classic: Cover’s theorem suggests that nonlinearly mapping data into a higher-dimensional space makes linear separation of different histories or contexts more likely \citep{Cover:65}. For next-token prediction, better separation of distinct pasts translates into easier linear readout. Residual stacking provides the deep, compositional scaffold in which these per-block memory and processing primitives operate. By integrating the xLSTM memory mechanisms into residually stacked, pre-LayerNorm backbones, xLSTM leverages the same architectural backbone that underlies contemporary LLMs while trading in the core processing primitive of a Transformer block for an LSTM-derived block with exponential gating and augmented memory.

# Concluding perspective

When we place xLSTM in the broader landscape painted by the paper, it is best seen as an attempt to reconcile and combine strengths from several prior lines of work. From the LSTM lineage, it inherits gating and the idea of a compact recurrent state that supports state tracking. From covariance/fast-weight traditions, it borrows associative, matrix-based memories that vastly increase per-layer storage capacity. From the efficiency-driven literature (linear attention, SSMs, and RNN revivals), it adopts design choices that allow linear-time processing and, for the mLSTM variant, parallelizable memory operations. Finally, by embedding these primitives into modern residual, pre-LayerNorm block stacks, xLSTM aligns with the architectural practices that enabled the scaling of Transformers to very large model sizes.

The paper argues — and empirically supports — that this synthesis yields models that recover the algorithmic expressivity advantages of recurrent mixing while delivering the capacity and scalability that characterize contemporary long-context architectures. The design comes with trade-offs (e.g., compute cost for $d\times d$ matrix updates, or limited parallelism for the mixed-memory sLSTM), but the overall position of xLSTM in the literature is to inhabit the point of the design space that sits between Transformers, linear-attention approximations, SSMs, and RNN revivals: combining gating, associative memory updates, and residual stacking to target both expressivity and efficiency.

Experimental Setup and Evaluation Protocols

# Datasets: SlimPajama (15B and 300B tokens)
All main empirical comparisons and scaling experiments for xLSTM reported in the paper use the SlimPajama corpora as the training source. Two dataset scales are used as the backbone for different experimental goals. First, a smaller-scale regime consisting of $15\ \text{B tokens}$ is used for method comparisons, ablation studies, and certain controlled experiments. Second, a large-scale regime of $300\ \text{B tokens}$ is used to evaluate xLSTM in a large-language-model (LLM) setting, to measure extrapolation, downstream-task performance, and scaling behavior at a scale comparable to recent large-model studies. The choice to run both a mid-size ($15\ \text{B}$) and a large ($300\ \text{B}$) training budget permits (a) fair comparisons among many architectures with reasonable compute, and (b) a final assessment of xLSTM as an LLM trained on a large corpus.

Throughout these experiments, held-out evaluation is performed on the SlimPajama validation set (the same corpus family used for training), with next-token perplexity as the primary language-modeling metric. Code and training artifacts used in the experiments are made available by the authors (see the project repository).

# Tokenization and autoregressive training setup
Across the experiments reported, models are trained in the autoregressive next-token prediction setting: at each position the model conditions on past tokens to predict the next token, and the training objective is the standard autoregressive likelihood (evaluated via validation perplexity). The paper explicitly frames comparisons in this autoregressive setting for all methods being benchmarked, so all reported perplexities and downstream evaluations are directly comparable under that training paradigm.

The paper does not prescribe a single tokenizer design detail in the main text; it emphasizes that all methods are trained in the same autoregressive setup on the same SlimPajama splits when directly compared. For generation-time timing comparisons, a decoding experimental setting is described: generation-speed measurements use batch size $1$ with a prefill of $16$ tokens, a prefill setting chosen to be maximally favorable to Transformer-style models that use a cached key–value mechanism.

# Model scales: $125\ \text{M}$, $350\ \text{M}$, $760\ \text{M}$, $1.3\ \text{B}$ and GPT‑3 $350\ \text{M}$-like baselines
The experiments cover a range of model parameter scales so that both small- and mid-sized behaviors and scaling trends can be observed. Explicit model sizes trained and reported in the $300\ \text{B}$ experiments are $125\ \text{M}$, $350\ \text{M}$, $760\ \text{M}$, and $1.3\ \text{B}$ parameters. For the $15\ \text{B}$-token method-comparison suite, the paper includes a direct comparison to a GPT‑3 style Transformer at approximately the $350\ \text{M}$ scale (the paper notes an embedding dimension of $1024$ and $24$ residual blocks for that GPT‑3–like baseline).

xLSTM architectures are defined in terms of residually stacked xLSTM blocks. To describe mixed architectures made from the two new cell types, the paper uses the notation xLSTM[$a$:$b$] to denote the ratio $a/b$ of mLSTM-based versus sLSTM-based xLSTM blocks. For example, xLSTM[$7$:$1$] means that out of eight blocks seven are mLSTM blocks and one is an sLSTM block; with a common total number of $48$ blocks this corresponds to $42$ mLSTM blocks and $6$ sLSTM blocks. Two extreme points used in experiments are xLSTM[$1$:$0$] (pure mLSTM) and xLSTM[$0$:$1$] (pure sLSTM).

Architecturally, xLSTM blocks are embedded into pre-LayerNorm residual backbones, and the authors select different block-level up-projection strategies for the two cell types: mLSTM blocks are used with a pre up-projection residual block (embedding into a higher-dimensional space before the recurrent update), while sLSTM blocks are used with a post up-projection block (nonlinear summary in the original space, then an up-projection). These backbone choices are kept consistent across the experiments comparing cell variants.

# Context lengths for training and extrapolation
Context length management differs by experiment goal. For the large-scale $300\ \text{B}$ training runs, models are trained with a context length of $2048$ tokens. To assess long-context generalization, trained models are then evaluated at much longer contexts: specifically, sequence-length extrapolation tests evaluate models up to a context length of $16384$ tokens for $1.3\ \text{B}$ models. The paper highlights this extrapolation behavior because one of the key questions is whether the xLSTM variants maintain low perplexity as context length grows beyond what they saw during training.

For the synthetic memory and associative-recall experiments, the authors extended existing tasks to contexts up to $2048$ tokens and to larger numbers of stored key–value pairs (see the MQAR description below). The Long Range Arena experiments and other long-context benchmarks are used to complement these controlled extrapolation studies, measuring behavior across a variety of tasks with naturally long inputs.

# Synthetic suites: formal languages, MQAR, Long Range Arena
The paper uses three synthetic (or semi-synthetic) evaluation suites to isolate different model capabilities that are important for language and sequence modeling.

Formal languages and state-tracking tasks. Formal-language tasks (based on and extended from prior work) are used to test state-tracking and long-range structural capabilities. The authors adapted tasks from prior formal-language benchmarks and extended them to multi-length training for extrapolation tests. These tasks emphasize the ability to revise and maintain internal state across a sequence—a capability that the paper argues sLSTM with exponential gating and memory mixing is designed to improve. For the experiments the authors compare two-block architectures (so each model has two residual blocks) across a wide range of models (pure sLSTM, pure mLSTM, mixed xLSTM, Transformers, SSMs, RWKV, Retention, Hyena, LSTM variants), and they report accuracy scaled between $0$ (random) and $1$ (perfect) on the tokens that are relevant to the task. These comparisons demonstrate that architectures without memory mixing (classic Transformers and many SSMs) fail on some of these formal tasks, whereas xLSTM variants that include memory mixing can succeed.

Multi-Query Associative Recall (MQAR). This task directly probes memory capacity: sequences contain many randomly sampled key–value pairs which the model must memorize and later retrieve. The paper extends the MQAR task to make it more challenging: the number of key–value pairs is increased up to $256$ and context length is increased to $2048$ to stress storage and retrieval. The matrix-memory mLSTM is designed precisely for this setting: it stores key–value pairs via an outer-product (covariance) update and retrieves via a matrix–vector multiply, mirroring associative-memory principles. In experiments comparing a range of models (including Transformer baselines such as Llama, SSMs like Mamba, and RNNs such as RWKV variants), Transformers remain the gold standard owing to their attention-style retrieval, but xLSTM[$1$:$1$] performs best among non-Transformer models and shows particularly strong extrapolation behavior. The experiments show that adding an sLSTM block does not reduce memory capacity and can even help at more difficult task settings.

Long Range Arena (LRA). The Long Range Arena benchmark suite measures performance on a variety of long-context tasks (classification and sequence modeling variants). The paper reports that xLSTM demonstrates consistently strong performance across the LRA tasks, indicating that the xLSTM design is robust to different long-context phenomena. Together with the formal-language and MQAR studies, the LRA experiments provide a rounded view of long-context strengths and weaknesses.

# Validation perplexity and downstream task evaluations
Validation perplexity on next-token prediction is the primary scalar metric used to compare language-modeling performance across methods and scales. The paper organizes comparisons in two major experimental regimes.

Method-comparison and ablation regime ($15\ \text{B}$ tokens). Models (Transformers, SSMs, RWKV variants, linear Transformer variants, and xLSTM architectures) are trained on $15\ \text{B}$ tokens from SlimPajama in a consistent autoregressive setting. For the GPT‑3–like Transformer baseline at this scale the authors match an embedding dimension of $1024$ and $24$ residual blocks so that model sizes are comparable. On this $15\ \text{B}$ training budget, xLSTM variants outperform the set of contemporary baseline architectures in validation perplexity. Ablation studies in this regime proceed by stepwise morphing of a vanilla LSTM architecture into full xLSTM: (1) integrating LSTM layers into pre-LayerNorm residual backbones, (2) embedding those layers into a post up-projection residual block, and (3) adding exponential gating and matrix memory. These staged ablations attribute the largest performance gains to the introduction of exponential gating and to the matrix-memory mechanism.

Large-scale LLM regime ($300\ \text{B}$ tokens). For the LLM-style experiments the authors train multiple model sizes ($125\ \text{M}, 350\ \text{M}, 760\ \text{M}, 1.3\ \text{B}$) on $300\ \text{B}$ tokens and evaluate both next-token perplexity and performance on downstream tasks that probe common-sense reasoning. Across model sizes, xLSTM[$1$:$0$] (pure mLSTM) and xLSTM[$7$:$1$] are reported as the best models in terms of validation perplexity. For downstream tasks, xLSTM is the top performer in the majority of evaluated tasks; the paper notes one exception where Mamba sometimes achieves the best score on the ARC task. These combined results indicate that xLSTM scales favorably both in language-modeling loss and in practical downstream metrics.

Scaling-law analysis. The paper also evaluates scaling behavior following power-law fitting approaches in the spirit of prior work (Kaplan et al., Brown et al.). When plotting validation loss against model size, all compared methods follow similar power-law slopes, but xLSTM enjoys a favorable offset (i.e., a systematically lower loss at a given model size) relative to SSMs and Transformers in the presented experiments. This suggests that, under the same data budget, xLSTM architectures will remain competitive as models are scaled further.

Generation-time and throughput measurements. Beyond training-time metrics, the paper reports generation-time scaling and decoding throughput at the $1.3\ \text{B}$ regime. Measured decoding time for recurrent models including xLSTM scales approximately linearly with context length while Transformer decoding time grows quadratically; empirical generation tests use batch size $1$ with a prefill of $16$ tokens. Because xLSTM has constant memory usage with respect to sequence length, it can sustain much larger decoding batch sizes than Transformers and therefore reaches higher maximal throughput in decoding experiments.

# PALOMA: 571-domain language perplexity benchmark
To inspect domain robustness and per-domain language-modeling quality, the authors evaluate all trained models on the PALOMA benchmark, which provides $571$ distinct text domains (ranging from news sites to niche Reddit communities). The metric used is next-token perplexity measured per domain.

Results reported in the paper for $1.3\ \text{B}$-scale models show strong domainwise performance for xLSTM. In particular, xLSTM[$1$:$0$] (pure mLSTM) achieves lower perplexity than several strong baselines on the vast majority of domains: it has a lower perplexity than Mamba on $568$ out of $571$ domains ($99.5\%$), a lower perplexity than Llama on $486$ out of $571$ domains ($85.1\%$), and a lower perplexity than RWKV-4 on $570$ out of $571$ domains ($99.8\%$). Between xLSTM variants, xLSTM[$1$:$0$] performs better than xLSTM[$7$:$1$] on PALOMA, showing that the matrix-memory–dominant configuration is particularly effective for broad-domain next-token modeling in these experiments.

# Training precision and implementation caveats
The paper documents several practical implementation and precision considerations that affected training and evaluation.

Mixed precision training. Most models in the $15\ \text{B}$ and $300\ \text{B}$ experiments were trained with mixed precision to reduce memory footprint and speed up training. However, the authors note that for some methods (specifically RWKV‑5, RWKV‑6, GLA, and HGRN2) the mixed-precision training reported did not utilize PyTorch’s automated mixed-precision machinery. In the $300\ \text{B}$ experiments, RWKV‑5, RWKV‑6 and HGRN2 had precision and stability settings that were only adjusted after training had already begun; this is an important practical caveat when interpreting cross-method comparisons at very large scale.

sLSTM versus mLSTM parallelism and kernel maturity. The two xLSTM cell types have different parallelization and implementation characteristics. mLSTM abandons memory mixing (no hidden–hidden recurrent weight mixing) and therefore admits a parallel reformulation that is amenable to efficient GPU batch computation. However, the mLSTM implementation used by the authors is not as highly optimized as mature attention kernels: the reported mLSTM CUDA kernels are not yet on par with highly optimized FlashAttention implementations and are roughly four times slower than FlashAttention or the scan implementation used in Mamba in the authors’ profiling. By contrast, sLSTM reintroduces memory mixing and therefore cannot be parallelized in the same way; to compensate, the authors implemented a carefully optimized CUDA kernel for sLSTM with register-level memory optimizations so that sLSTM runs typically at less than two times the wall-clock time of the parallel mLSTM implementation. These implementation statuses affect absolute wall-clock training time but do not change the asymptotic algorithmic properties reported.

Compute and memory trade-offs for the matrix memory. The mLSTM cell stores a $d \times d$ matrix memory and applies a $d \times d$ outer-product update and a $d \times d$ matrix–vector retrieval per step. This design increases per-step arithmetic relative to scalar-cell designs. The memory array itself does not add learnable parameters, but the $d \times d$ operations are computationally expensive. The authors point out that these operations are amenable to GPU parallelism and do not grow with sequence length (so peak memory usage is constant in sequence length), but they caution that the computational overhead is nontrivial in practice until the mLSTM kernels are further optimized.

Other practical limitations. The paper documents additional practical limitations discovered during experiments: (i) the forget-gate initialization must be chosen carefully in practice, (ii) because the mLSTM matrix memory is finite and sequence-length independent, extremely long contexts can in principle overload the memory (the authors did not observe a breakdown up to $16\text{k}$ token contexts in their tests), and (iii) the large-language experiments reported are not fully hyperparameter- or architecture-optimized due to the heavy computational budget required—further tuning could improve absolute results.

# Putting it together: reproducibility and recommended evaluation practice
The experimental protocol used in the paper emphasizes fair, apples-to-apples comparisons: all architectures are trained in the same autoregressive next-token framework on the same SlimPajama splits, evaluated primarily with validation perplexity, and supplemented by synthetic tasks that isolate memory and state-tracking capabilities, domain-wise perplexity on PALOMA, long-context extrapolation tests, and downstream-task evaluations probing reasoning ability. The xLSTM[$a$:$b$] notation provides a concise way to describe mixed-block configurations, and the paper keeps backbone choices (pre-LN residual stacking and the per-cell up-projection strategy) consistent to focus comparisons on the effects of the memory structure and exponential gating innovations.

For readers wanting to reproduce or build on these experiments, the authors provide code and model definitions in their public repository; the combination of $15\ \text{B}$ and $300\ \text{B}$ training regimes, the synthetic-task suite (formal languages, MQAR, LRA), and the PALOMA per-domain evaluation together form a comprehensive evaluation protocol for assessing long-context modeling, memory capacity, and domain robustness of sequential architectures.

Synthetic Tasks and Long-Context Benchmarks

# Formal languages for state tracking and regular grammars

Synthetic formal-language tasks are a standard way to isolate a model’s ability to implement internal state machines and to track discrete properties of a sequence over time. Unlike natural-language benchmarks, formal languages let us design exact tests of stateful behavior: for example, parity (counting whether the number of 1s seen so far is even or odd), nested bracket matching, and other regular- or context-free-like grammars. These tasks stress whether a model can maintain and update internal counters or toggles, revise stored information, and combine past inputs in ways that are sensitive to order and recurrence.

In the xLSTM study, the authors adapted formal-language tasks (extending the experimental setup of Deletang et al.) to allow multi-length training so that models could be examined both at trained lengths and for extrapolation to longer sequences. They compared small architectures (two residual blocks) across many families — LSTMs, Transformer variants, State Space Models (SSMs), and xLSTM variants. The key experimental conclusion is that models that lack memory mixing (that is, architectures without hidden-to-hidden recurrent mixing of memory cells) fail on certain regular-grammar tasks that require explicit state tracking, such as parity. By contrast, architectures that preserve memory mixing (classic LSTMs and the sLSTM variant in xLSTM) can implement the necessary sequential state updates and solve these tasks reliably. This observation is important because it identifies a structural property — memory mixing — that underlies the ability to implement finite-state computations in learned sequence models.

Intuitively, parity and similar problems require the network to update an internal bit of state whenever a particular input occurs and to carry that state forward across arbitrary gaps. Architectures that only build representations by non-recurrent transformations of the past (e.g., certain SSMs or attention-only variants without recurrent mixing) lack the architectural substrate to implement these toggling updates robustly. The empirical result in the paper confirms this theoretical distinction: without memory mixing, some formal languages cannot be learned, whereas sLSTM-equipped architectures that preserve recurrence and gating can.

# Impact of memory mixing: sLSTM vs. mLSTM vs. no-mixing models

“Memory mixing” refers to the recurrent, hidden-to-hidden interactions that let different scalar memory cells influence one another across time steps. In the canonical multi-cell LSTM this is implemented by recurrent weight matrices that feed the previous hidden state back into gate and input computations; these recurrent connections enable interactions among memory channels and allow the network to implement richer state machines and combinational logic across stored components.

sLSTM retains this memory-mixing capability. With multiple scalar cells, sLSTM uses recurrent matrices (for the inputs and for each gate) so that the current hidden state $h_{t-1}$ can modulate how each cell is updated. This preserves the classic LSTM expressive advantage for state-tracking tasks. In addition, sLSTM introduces exponential gating and a normalizer state (see the next section) that change how contributions from different time steps are weighted and combined.

By contrast, mLSTM abandons hidden–hidden memory mixing to achieve full parallelizability. Its memory is a matrix $\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and updates are performed by outer-product (covariance-like) rules; there are no recurrent hidden-state connections that mix scalar cells. This design trades some expressivity (particularly for finite-state computations that require stepwise mixing of scalar cells) for massive increases in storage capacity and for the ability to parallelize across tokens. Architecturally, mLSTM therefore resembles other “fast-weights” or associative-memory schemes and some linearized attention / SSM approaches that compress past context into a fixed-size associative structure.

Empirically, the experiments show a nuanced picture. For tasks that strictly require state tracking (e.g., parity and certain formal-grammar problems), models without memory mixing (Transformers and many SSMs) fail, while sLSTM succeeds. For associative-memory style tasks and very large-capacity recall, mLSTM (and hybrids that include both mLSTM and sLSTM blocks) can recover strong performance because they store many key–value associations in a matrix structure. The hybrid design (mixing sLSTM and mLSTM blocks in a stack) often gets the best of both worlds: sLSTM supplies state-tracking and sequential logic, while mLSTM supplies large associative memory and parallelism.

# Nearest Neighbor-style revision behavior via exponential gating

A key weakness highlighted for classical LSTMs is the difficulty of revising previously made storage decisions when new information arrives — a behavior that matters in tasks like nearest-neighbor search within a stream. Concretely, consider the following simple problem: a reference vector is given, and a stream of candidate vectors follows; at the end the model must return the value attached to the vector in the stream that is most similar to the reference. To perform well, the model must be able to overwrite or revise its memory whenever it encounters a better match later in the sequence.

sLSTM introduces exponential gating together with a normalizer state that explicitly enables this revisability. The sLSTM forward equations make the mechanism clear:

- additive (constant-error-carousel) cell update and a running normalizer
  $$
  c_t = f_t\, c_{t-1} + i_t\, z_t, \qquad
  n_t = f_t\, n_{t-1} + i_t,
  $$
  where $c_t$ is the scalar cell, $n_t$ is the normalizer, $z_t$ is the candidate input, and $i_t,f_t$ are the input and forget gates.

- normalized output and gating
  $$
  \tilde{h}_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}, \qquad h_t = o_t\,\tilde{h}_t,
  $$
  with $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ and $f_t$ either $\sigma(\tilde{f}_t)$ or $\exp(\tilde{f}_t)$ (and the usual output gate $o_t=\sigma(\tilde{o}_t)$).

Because $c_t$ accumulates weighted contributions and $n_t$ accumulates the same weights, the ratio $c_t/n_t$ behaves like a weighted average of the past inputs $z_\tau$, where each contribution is weighted by the product of the input gate at its insertion time and all future forget gates. Exponential gating makes those weights unboundedly positive, so the network can assign a relatively huge weight to a newly encountered item: when a more similar item appears, the network can set $i_t$ large so that the normalized memory $c_t/n_t$ moves strongly toward the new item and away from older items. In other words, sLSTM can “revise” the remembered best match by changing the relative weighting dynamically.

Numerical stability with exponentials is handled by a simple stabilizer state $m_t$:
$$
m_t = \max\!\big(\log(f_t)+m_{t-1},\ \log(i_t)\big),
$$
and stabilized gates
$$
i'_t = \exp\!\big(\log(i_t)-m_t\big)=\exp(\tilde{i}_t-m_t),\qquad
f'_t = \exp\!\big(\log(f_t)+m_{t-1}-m_t\big).
$$
These adjustments do not change the computed outputs or gradients (they simply factor out a common magnitude), but they keep the exponentials in a numerically safe range.

Empirically the paper reports that classical LSTMs struggle with the nearest-neighbor revision task, while sLSTM (and xLSTM variants that include sLSTM blocks) succeed. The intuition is straightforward: exponential gating plus normalization converts the cell into a mechanism that can compute dynamic weighted averages with flexible, revisable weights; the normalizer $n_t$ is essential because it keeps the aggregation scale-controlled so that later large weights have the intended effect on the normalized memory.

# MQAR: capacity of mLSTM’s matrix memory

The Multi-Query Associative Recall (MQAR) task is designed to stress raw associative memory capacity: a sequence presents many randomly chosen key–value pairs (from a large vocabulary), and at query time the model must recover the value associated with a queried key. To push capacity limits, the experiments increase the number of stored pairs up to 256 and extend context length up to 2048.

mLSTM addresses this class of tasks by replacing scalar cells with a matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ that stores associations via outer products (a covariance-style update). The forward equations summarize the mechanism:

- matrix memory update and normalizer
  $$
  \mathbf{C}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\,\mathbf{k}_t^\top,\qquad
  \mathbf{n}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{k}_t,
  $$
  where $\mathbf{k}_t$ is the key, $\mathbf{v}_t$ the value, and $\mathbf{n}_t$ accumulates weighted keys.

- retrieval (query) and output
  $$
  \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,\;1\}},\qquad
  \mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t,
  $$
  with linear projections $\mathbf{q}_t=\mathbf{W}_q\mathbf{x}_t+\mathbf{b}_q$, $\mathbf{k}_t=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\mathbf{W}_k\mathbf{x}_t+\mathbf{b}_k$, and $\mathbf{v}_t=\mathbf{W}_v\mathbf{x}_t+\mathbf{b}_v$.

The outer-product update $\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top$ is the classic associative-memory (or fast-weight) building block. It stores correlations between keys and values inside a single matrix. At query time, the matrix–vector product $\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t$ sums contributions of stored values weighted by key–query similarities, much like attention but compressed into one matrix rather than a token-indexed cache. The normalizer $\mathbf{n}_t$ and the use of the threshold $\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,1\}$ prevent numerical instability and avoid dividing by tiny scalars when the query aligns weakly with stored keys.

From a theoretical perspective, the covariance update is optimal (in the sense given in prior work) for separability of retrieved binary vectors and maximizes signal-to-noise under certain assumptions. In empirical MQAR experiments, the paper shows that while Transformers remain the “gold standard” (their positional, token-indexed attention can encode an exponential number of associations in coding space), xLSTM variants with mLSTM memory perform very strongly. Specifically, the xLSTM hybrid xLSTM[1:1] (one mLSTM block and one sLSTM block in the small two-block setting) outperformed other non-Transformer baselines (Mamba, RWKV variants) on the extended MQAR tests.

The practical takeaway is that a matrix associative memory implemented inside a recurrent block can provide very high recall capacity. The cost is computational: updating and multiplying $d\times d$ matrices is heavier than scalar-cell RNN operations. But because the operations are parameter-free (the matrix memory is separate from learned weights) and can be implemented with high-parallel GPU matrix algebra, the wall-clock cost is acceptable in many settings.

# Effect of adding sLSTM blocks on associative recall at scale

A natural question is whether introducing sLSTM blocks (which restore memory mixing and sequential recurrence) harms or helps associative recall when combined with mLSTM matrix memory. The experiments deliberately tested hybrid stacks, for example xLSTM[1:1] (one mLSTM block and one sLSTM block in the two-block experiments) and larger mixes in deeper stacks.

Surprisingly — and usefully — adding sLSTM blocks does not reduce associative recall capacity. The paper reports that xLSTM[1:1] performs better than pure mLSTM in many settings among non-Transformer models, and in the hardest MQAR setting (256 key–value pairs) the inclusion of sLSTM blocks seems to leverage the matrix memory rather than impede it. Intuitively, the sLSTM component provides sequential processing and interaction capabilities that can help resolve ambiguities, disambiguate noisy associations, or perform control-flow-like operations on the content retrieved from the matrix memory. In other words, the scalar recurrent mixing and gating augment the raw associative memory with the ability to post-process and route retrieved information before it impacts future updates.

This synergy suggests an architectural design principle: combine a high-capacity associative store (mLSTM) with a recurrent controller that can manage and manipulate stored content (sLSTM). The mLSTM provides dense, parallelizable storage; the sLSTM provides conditional, stateful control. Empirically this hybridization yields strong associative-recall performance at scale and appears to improve extrapolation (see the next section).

# Extrapolation beyond trained context lengths

A major practical test for sequence models is their ability to generalize to longer contexts than those seen during training. The xLSTM experiments addressed extrapolation in two complementary ways: (1) extending MQAR tasks to longer contexts than used in training and (2) a separate language-modeling experiment in which models trained on context length 2048 were evaluated up to 16384 tokens.

The results reported in the paper show that xLSTM variants generalize well to longer contexts. In MQAR extrapolation analyses (detailed in the appendix), xLSTM’s enhanced memory capacities enabled it to continue to recall associations effectively at longer sequence lengths than those seen during training. In the large language experiment, xLSTM models maintained relatively low perplexity as the context grew from 2048 up to 16384, a regime where some Transformer and SSM baselines degraded substantially.

Mechanistically, this extrapolation capacity follows from the two memory design choices. The mLSTM matrix memory has fixed size independent of sequence length: it compresses many associations into a bounded matrix rather than storing token-indexed histories. As long as the matrix does not become saturated (and provided the forget/input gating is appropriately tuned), retrieval can scale to long contexts because the matrix storage and the retrieval operation do not depend linearly on the number of tokens. The sLSTM’s normalized-weight formulation ($c_t/n_t$) produces averaged or weighted aggregates whose form does not blow up with sequence length; instead, the gates control effective memory decay and emphasis, which can be learned to support extrapolation. The authors do note the caveat that an exceedingly long context could eventually overload a fixed-size matrix memory; however, empirically no overload was apparent up to 16k contexts in their experiments.

# Long Range Arena performance and takeaways

The Long Range Arena (LRA) benchmark suite collects diverse tasks that require processing long input sequences — tasks such as long-range sequence classification, retrieval, or algorithmic operations. LRA is useful because it probes different aspects of long-context processing (memory, local vs. global dependencies, and combinational inference).

In the experiments reported, xLSTM shows consistent and strong performance across the LRA tasks. This consistency suggests that the xLSTM design — combining exponential gating, normalized scalar memory (sLSTM), and a matrix associative memory (mLSTM) in the block stack — is broadly effective at the various algorithmic and retrieval demands embodied in LRA. In plain terms: xLSTM’s mechanisms address both the need to remember many associations and the need to perform stateful sequential operations on that memory, which are both present across the LRA tasks.

The practical implication is that xLSTM is not narrowly tuned to a single synthetic task; instead, it offers a robust set of mechanisms that together handle multiple facets of long-context reasoning. For practitioners choosing or designing models for long-sequence problems, this breadth is a key advantage.

# Key insights for architecture selection

The synthetic tasks and long-context benchmarks in the xLSTM paper yield several concrete, experiment-backed guidelines for architectural choices:

- Choose sLSTM when state tracking and revisability matter. If your problem requires toggling internal state, implementing finite-state logic (parity-like tasks), or being able to revise stored choices in light of later inputs, include sLSTM blocks. The scalar-memory sLSTM preserves memory mixing (recurrent hidden-to-hidden interactions) and the exponential-gating plus normalizer mechanism enables revisability.

- Choose mLSTM when raw associative capacity and parallelizability matter. For problems that are essentially key–value storage and later retrieval at query time (MQAR-style tasks, large associative caches), mLSTM’s matrix memory with covariance (outer-product) updates is highly effective. mLSTM admits a parallel formulation and can be implemented with matrix algebra, which is efficient on GPUs.

- Combine them to get the best of both worlds. Hybrid stacks (xLSTM with both mLSTM and sLSTM blocks) are empirically strong: mLSTM provides the associative substrate and sLSTM provides the sequential controller. The experiments show that adding sLSTM does not hurt and often helps associative recall.

- Pay attention to pre/post up-projection choices. In the xLSTM design, sLSTM blocks are used inside a post up-projection residual block and mLSTM inside a pre up-projection block. This reflects the intuition that mLSTM benefits from a higher-dimensional embedding space in which to store many associations, while sLSTM operates naturally in the original embedding space where scalar memory and gating interact closely with the residual stream.

- Mind computational trade-offs. mLSTM requires $d\times d$ matrix updates and matrix–vector products; this raises per-step FLOPs relative to scalar-cell RNNs, but these operations are parameter-free and parallelizable. sLSTM cannot be parallelized across time because of hidden–hidden recurrence, but a carefully tuned CUDA kernel can make it practically fast (the paper reports their sLSTM kernel is typically less than two times slower than their parallel mLSTM implementation). Conversely, unoptimized mLSTM kernels can be slower than highly optimized attention kernels, so implementation effort matters.

- Stabilize and initialize gates carefully. Exponential gates enable new behaviors but need numerical stabilization (the $m_t$ trick) and careful initialization of forget-gate biases so that the memory dynamics start in a good regime.

- Use synthetic probes early in development. The formal-language suite, MQAR, and LRA are informative diagnostics: they reveal whether your design supports state tracking, associative capacity, and long-range generalization. These synthetic tasks can guide architectural tweaks before committing to large-scale training.

In sum, the synthetic-task suite studied in the paper isolates capabilities — revisability, associative capacity, state tracking, and extrapolation — and demonstrates that xLSTM’s combination of exponential gating, scalar memory mixing (sLSTM), and matrix associative memory (mLSTM) addresses these capabilities in complementary ways. Designing an xLSTM stack for a particular application becomes an exercise in matching those capabilities to the application’s functional requirements while balancing computational cost and parallelism constraints.

15B-Token Comparisons and Ablations

# Methods compared: GPT-3/Llama, Mamba/H3, RWKV, GLA, HGRN/HGRN2, Hyena

This section summarizes the families of models that were included in the 15B-token comparison in the paper and recalls the key structural differences that matter when interpreting the ablations.

Transformers (represented here by GPT-3 and Llama) are built around parallel self-attention layers and residual stacks. Their attention mechanism performs pairwise token interactions and, when implemented naively, has quadratic compute and memory cost in the context length. Transformers are the dominant architecture for large language models and provide a useful baseline because they scale well with parameters and data when trained with standard residual/pre-LayerNorm backbones.

State Space Models (SSMs) such as H3 and Mamba are an alternative family that aim to retain long-range dependencies with linear complexity in the context length. SSMs replace attention with linear state updates (often diagonalized or structured) that can be implemented with near-linear or linear-in-time kernels. In the comparisons they serve as the leading non-attention competitors that emphasize long-context efficiency.

Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) in this comparison include variants such as RWKV and the HGRN family (HGRN, HGRN2). These methods inherit recurrence and gating ideas from classical RNNs / LSTMs but adapt them to be competitive in large-language-model settings. RWKV, for example, is a recurrent design specifically engineered to compete with Transformers while maintaining linear computational complexity.

Linear or approximate-attention methods such as GLA (Gated Linear Attention) and Hyena aim to reduce the quadratic scaling of standard attention by either approximating the softmax or replacing it with lower-complexity kernels. They are included because they bridge the conceptual space between full attention and linear-time sequence models.

In the experiments reported for the 15B-token comparison, all models were trained in the same autoregressive setting on 15B tokens from the SlimPajama corpus. Model sizes were matched to a GPT-3-like configuration (approximately 350M parameters: embedding dimension 1024 and 24 residual blocks). Training used mixed precision where applicable; for some methods (RWKV-5/6, GLA, HGRN2), the mixed-precision training did not use the PyTorch automated mixed precision path noted in the paper.

# xLSTM’s validation perplexity advantage and scaling trends

When trained under the same conditions (15B tokens, matched architecture scale), xLSTM achieved lower validation perplexity than the other methods in the study. The paper reports that xLSTM outperforms all compared baselines — Transformers, SSMs, RNNs, and linear-attention variants — on the validation set perplexity for the matched 350M-scale models. This result is presented as the core quantitative outcome of the 15B-token comparison.

Beyond the single-point comparison, the paper examines scaling behavior by plotting validation perplexity versus model size (a standard scaling-law style plot). The reported trend for xLSTM shows the same qualitative power-law scaling regime as the other methods but with a favorable offset: as models grow, xLSTM maintains an advantage in perplexity. The paper summarizes this as an indication that xLSTM will continue to be competitive at larger parameter scales.

Interpreting these trends requires attention to the structural changes in xLSTM. The architecture change is not a single trick but a combination: (i) recasting LSTM-style recurrence into modern residual/pre-LayerNorm blocks and up-projection backbones, (ii) introducing exponential gating (sLSTM), and (iii) introducing matrix (covariance) memory (mLSTM). The validation advantage and the preserved scaling trend indicate that these changes interact well with standard large-scale training recipes and do not merely provide a small-data or toy advantage.

# Ablation path: vanilla LSTM → residual blocks → up-projection

To identify which architectural and algorithmic modifications drive the improvement from a vanilla LSTM to xLSTM, the authors perform a staged ablation: they progressively morph a vanilla LSTM architecture into the full xLSTM architecture and measure the effect of each change.

The first step is to place LSTM layers inside modern residual/pre-LayerNorm backbones. Vanilla LSTMs are often used as standalone recurrent layers. Injecting LSTMs into residual stacks with pre-LayerNorm improves training stability for deep networks and aligns the LSTM-based design with contemporary LLM construction. This change alone improves performance because residual connections and layer normalization facilitate optimization and deepen effective representational power.

The second step introduces an up-projection (and its mirror down-projection) around the recurrent block. There are two variants of this motif mentioned in the paper: a post up-projection (map into a higher-dimensional space after the recurrent summary) and a pre up-projection (map into a high-dimensional space before the recurrent summary). The intuition, drawn from Cover’s Theorem, is that non-linear summaries in higher-dimensional embeddings make different history patterns easier to separate linearly. For sLSTM blocks the paper uses post up-projection; for mLSTM blocks the pre up-projection is preferred because the matrix memory benefits from operating in higher-dimensional key/value/query spaces. Adding these projection steps gives a measurable benefit in the ablation chain because it increases the capacity of each block to separate histories and represent fine-grained contexts.

The final steps are the algorithmic changes inside the recurrent cell itself: (a) replacing bounded sigmoid gates for some gates by exponential gates (sLSTM idea) and (b) replacing scalar memory cells by matrix memories with covariance updates (mLSTM idea). The ablation results reported in the paper show that both of these last two changes yield further reductions in validation perplexity. In other words, the best-performing configuration is not simply the modernized training backbone nor a single cell change; the gains accumulate across these modifications.

# Adding exponential gating: effects on performance

Exponential gating is the first of the two core changes from LSTM to xLSTM. In the scalar sLSTM variant the input gate (and optionally the forget gate) is implemented with an exponential activation rather than a sigmoid. Concretely, the sLSTM forward pass includes the familiar additive memory update

$c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t,$

but with the input gate defined as

$i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$

and the normalizer state tracking the cumulative gate mass

$n_t = f_t \, n_{t-1} + i_t.$

The hidden output is then normalized as

$\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t,$

and $h_t = o_t \, \tilde{h}_t$ where $o_t$ is an output gate (sigmoid). Because $\exp(\cdot)$ is unbounded above, exponential gating gives the model a much larger dynamic range than a sigmoid gate that is restricted to $(0,1)$. This larger range makes it possible to “weight” contributions at different times with a multiplicative factor that can grow or shrink more flexibly over sequences. Intuitively, exponential gates let the model store or emphasize certain inputs more strongly than would be possible with a sigmoid gate, and the normalizer $n_t$ keeps the readout stable by keeping track of the total weight mass that influences $c_t$.

Unbounded exponentials can, however, cause numeric overflow or extremely skewed dynamics. To address this, the sLSTM introduces a stabilizer state $m_t$ and computes numerically stable scaled gates via:

$m_t = \max(\log f_t + m_{t-1}, \log i_t),$

$i'_t = \exp(\log i_t - m_t),$

$f'_t = \exp(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t).$

These stabilized gates $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ replace the raw exponentials in arithmetic, preserving exact output while maintaining numerical stability.

From a performance perspective the ablation studies indicate that exponential gating contributes substantially to the xLSTM improvements. Functionally, exponential gating addresses one of the classical LSTM limitations called out by the authors: the inability to revise storage decisions. With exponential gates and the accompanying normalizer, the model can reallocate attention to past stored information more effectively when new evidence arrives — a capability demonstrated in synthetic “nearest neighbor search” tasks and formal language tasks where revisability and state tracking are essential. In the 15B-token ablation chain, adding exponential gating (after modernizing the backbone and projections) yields a clear perplexity improvement, showing that this mechanism is beneficial at scale for natural language modeling, not only for small synthetic tasks.

# Adding matrix memory: effects on performance

The second major modification is a change in the memory representation: the mLSTM replaces scalar cell states by a matrix-valued memory $\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. The mLSTM stores key-value pairs and updates its memory using a covariance (outer-product) learning rule:

$\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \, \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \, \mathbf{v}_t \, \mathbf{k}_t^\top,$

where $\mathbf{k}_t$ is the key vector, $\mathbf{v}_t$ is the value vector, and $i_t$ and $f_t$ are the input and forget gates (with exponential gating optionally applied to $i_t$). The normalizer for the matrix memory is vector-valued:

$\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \, \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \, \mathbf{k}_t.$

Retrieval at time $t$ is performed by multiplying the memory with a query vector $\mathbf{q}_t$ and normalizing by the projection of the normalizer onto the query:

$\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \dfrac{\mathbf{C}_t \, \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1\}},\qquad
\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t.$

This covariance/outer-product update is closely related to older associative memory formulations (Bidirectional Associative Memories) and to modern “fast weight” or fast-learning mechanisms. It is well suited to storing many key-value associations and retrieving them by content (via $\mathbf{q}_t^\top \mathbf{k}_s$ interactions).

In the experiments the matrix memory produces clear benefits for tasks that require high memory capacity or that hinge on retrieving rare or specific content. The paper points to a “Rare Token Prediction” failure mode for scalar LSTMs — those models have to compress information into scalar cells and thus struggle with rare tokens. The mLSTM’s matrix memory increases representational capacity so that rare tokens and associative recall tasks are handled more effectively. In the specialized associative-recall experiments (the Multi-Query Associative Recall task), xLSTM variants that include mLSTM blocks outperform other non-Transformer models and narrow the gap to Transformers.

Computationally, the matrix memory is heavier: it requires $d \times d$ matrix updates and retrievals. However, because mLSTM abandons the hidden-to-hidden memory mixing (no recurrent hidden-hidden matrices across cells), the mLSTM recurrence can be entirely parallelized across time, making large-batch GPU implementations feasible. The paper reports that, in practice, these matrix operations are parallelizable and have only a minor effect on wall-clock time when implemented efficiently.

# Gate mechanism ablations: learnability and input dependence

Gating is central to all of these recurrent designs, and the authors explicitly ablate different gate variants to understand their contribution. The ablation experiments modify whether gates are learnable or fixed, whether they depend only on hidden state or also on the current input, and whether exponential activation is used.

The high-level conclusion drawn in the paper is straightforward: having each gate be learnable and influenced by the input provides an incremental positive effect on performance. In other words, more flexible gating — where the gate activations are parameterized and can react to the current input vector $\mathbf{x}_t$ — consistently improves perplexity in the ablation chain. This observation aligns with intuition: input-dependent gating allows the model to immediately respond to novel or salient tokens; learnable gating allows the model to adjust the degree of persistence or overwrite in a data-driven way rather than relying on fixed hand-designed gates.

While the paper does not publish every numeric outcome for every gating configuration in the main text, it emphasizes that the gating design matters and that the combination of input-dependent, learnable gates with the exponential activation and normalizers is part of the reason the full xLSTM outperforms simpler gate designs.

# Interpreting where gains come from

Putting together the pieces from the comparisons and the ablations gives a coherent interpretation of why xLSTM improves validation perplexity at the 15B-token training scale.

First, modernizing the LSTM into residual/pre-LayerNorm stacks with projection up/down increases representational capacity and stabilizes optimization. This step aligns LSTM-style recurrence with the proven scaffolding used by large Transformer models and is responsible for a non-trivial portion of the gap closed to state-of-the-art.

Second, exponential gating plus the normalizer addresses a structural limitation of classical LSTM: the inability to revise storage decisions robustly. Exponential gates give a wider dynamic range and, combined with the normalizer, allow stable accumulation and reweighting of past inputs. This improves state-tracking tasks and the ability to override or reinforce earlier stored content as new evidence arrives.

Third, the matrix covariance memory increases raw storage capacity and changes retrieval from a scalar readout to a structured matrix-vector operation. This boosts the model’s ability to memorize and retrieve content-rich items (for example, rare tokens or specific key-value pairs), which is important in natural language where informative signals are often distributed and sparse.

Fourth, the architectural choice about where to place the recurrent unit relative to an up-projection matters: mLSTM benefits from operating in higher-dimensional spaces (pre up-projection), while sLSTM integrates well with post up-projection. The combination — mixing mLSTM and sLSTM blocks in a residual stack — lets the model have both high capacity associative memory and the ability to do structured state tracking with memory mixing.

The ablation chain in the paper shows that none of these components alone is sufficient to achieve the reported best performance: the final gains are cumulative. This explains why xLSTM beats the other model families at matched scale: it combines representational depth (residual stacks and projections), flexible gating for revisability, and high-capacity associative memory — all trained with standard large-scale recipes.

# Lessons for practitioners

The experimental findings and ablations in the paper suggest a number of practical recommendations for practitioners building or evaluating large sequence models.

If you want an RNN-style model that competes with Transformers at language modeling scale, consider a hybrid approach: put modernized recurrent cells into pre-LayerNorm residual blocks and use up-/down-projections to increase within-block capacity. This modernization is not optional — the ablation shows it materially improves optimization and final perplexity.

Use exponential gating cautiously but usefully. Replace selected sigmoid gates with exponential activations (for example, the input gate) but include numerical stabilization (the $m_t$ stabilizer and normalized readout $n_t$) so that training and inference remain numerically safe. Exponential gating enables revisability of stored content and gives the model a larger effective dynamic range for storage weights.

If your task requires high-capacity associative memory (for example, many distinct key-value associations, frequent rare-token modeling, or extreme associative recall), implement a matrix memory like mLSTM. Be prepared for extra $d\times d$ computation and plan GPU parallelism accordingly. In return you get retrieval by content and a robust mechanism for storing vector-valued content.

For mixed workloads (where both state tracking and high-capacity retrieval are important), consider mixing sLSTM and mLSTM blocks in a residual stack (the paper uses notation like xLSTM[7:1] to indicate block ratios). This allows you to keep memory mixing (which sLSTM enables) while leveraging mLSTM’s capacity where parallelism and storage are most helpful.

Be mindful of implementation trade-offs. mLSTM is parallelizable and fits well in vectorized GPU computing, but it multiplies the raw compute per layer. sLSTM retains hidden-hidden memory mixing, which makes it more expressive for state-tracking but harder to parallelize; in the paper the authors implemented a custom CUDA kernel to make sLSTM efficient (reported as less than two times slower than the parallel mLSTM implementation). Also, initialization choices matter (the paper lists careful forget gate initialization as a practical concern).

Finally, the paper’s results show that design choices validated on small synthetic tasks translate to large-scale language modeling when combined with modern backbones and training procedures. The combination of modern residual scaffolding, exponential gating, and matrix memory is what produced the consistent perplexity advantage at 15B tokens. Thus, when exploring new recurrent or hybrid architectures, evaluate them in a staged ablation that mirrors the paper’s path: backbone modernization, projection strategy, gating family, and memory representation.

xLSTM as a Large Language Model (300B Tokens)

This chapter summarizes and explains the experiments and findings reported for xLSTM when scaled and trained as a Large Language Model (LLM) on $300\ \mathrm{B}$ tokens from SlimPajama. It focuses on how xLSTM behaves as an LLM: how well it extrapolates to longer contexts, how its validation perplexity compares across model sizes and to other recent architectures, how it performs on downstream reasoning tasks, how it generalizes across hundreds of text domains, and how it behaves at inference time in latency and throughput. Throughout, the chapter ties these behaviors back to the architectural decisions of xLSTM (exponential gating, scalar and matrix memory variants, and residual stacking) as discussed in the paper.

# Length extrapolation from 2k to 16k context

A central practical question for any LLM is how well a model trained with a particular context length can handle longer contexts at inference time. In the xLSTM experiments, all models compared were trained with context length $2048$ and then evaluated on longer context lengths up to $16384$. This is an $8\times$ extrapolation in context length, a meaningful stress test for long-range retrieval and for architectures that compress past information into internal memory.

Empirically, xLSTM models (in particular the large-scale xLSTM variants trained on $300\ \mathrm{B}$ tokens) maintain low perplexity as context length grows from $2048$ to $16384$, whereas several comparator models degrade more rapidly. The paper reports that xLSTM "maintain low perplexities for longer contexts" in contrast to other methods. This superior extrapolation appears consistently in the 1.3B-sized models that were examined for this experiment.

Intuitively, this robustness to longer contexts is linked to the memory mechanisms inside xLSTM. The mLSTM variant uses a matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t$ and a normalization state $\mathbf{n}_t$ so that retrieval is computed as
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\left\{ |\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,\,1\right\}},
\]
with gates controlling decay ($f_t$) and write strength ($i_t$). The normalizer keeps track of the cumulative gate strengths and is used to stabilize retrieval magnitudes. Because xLSTM’s memory is compressive and governed by gates, it aggregates past information in a way that generalizes beyond the training context length, enabling reasonable retrieval even when evaluating much longer sequences. Additionally, sLSTM’s exponential gating and memory mixing support state-tracking behavior, which helps when longer sequences require preserving and updating structured state rather than re-attending to every token.

# Perplexity across sizes vs. Llama, Mamba, RWKV

To assess xLSTM as an LLM across model sizes, the paper trained model families of sizes $125\ \mathrm{M}$, $350\ \mathrm{M}$, $760\ \mathrm{M}$, and $1.3\ \mathrm{B}$ on the same $300\ \mathrm{B}$ token corpus and compared validation perplexities to representative methods from other families: Llama (Transformer), Mamba (State Space Model), and RWKV-4 (RNN-family). Across those sizes, both xLSTM variants reported in detail — xLSTM[$1$:$0$] (all mLSTM blocks) and xLSTM[$7$:$1$] (mostly mLSTM with some sLSTM) — achieved the best validation perplexities at every model size. The paper explicitly states that “both xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1] are the best models for all model sizes with respect to the validation set perplexity.”

This outcome is consistent with two architectural gains in xLSTM emphasized earlier in the paper. First, exponential gating (input and optionally forget gates with exponential activations, stabilized by auxiliary states) gives the model a more flexible and stronger way to control how new information is written and how old information is decayed or reweighted. Second, the matrix memory of mLSTM increases storage capacity beyond scalar cell states, improving the model’s ability to represent and recall detailed information (including rare or high-dimensional signals) that can reduce next-token prediction error. Ablation experiments reported in the paper attribute the strong performance improvements to both exponential gating and the matrix memory components.

# Downstream tasks (e.g., common-sense reasoning) results

Besides next-token perplexity, the paper evaluated xLSTM models on downstream tasks that measure common-sense reasoning. For the family of sizes listed above, xLSTM models attained superior performance on most of these downstream tasks compared to the selected baseline models. The paper summarizes that “in the vast majority of tasks and across all model sizes xLSTM is the best method,” with one noted exception: on the ARC benchmark (a particular question-answering common-sense dataset), Mamba was sometimes the best performer.

Why might better next-token modeling translate into improved downstream reasoning performance? The paper argues (and shows via experiments) that xLSTM’s combination of higher-capacity memories and stronger gating leads to internal representations that better summarize and separate histories. A residually stacked architecture that nonlinearly maps histories into higher-dimensional spaces (motivated by Cover’s theorem) combined with the memory mechanisms enables xLSTM to form richer state representations useful for reasoning tasks that depend on tracing or composing information across many tokens.

# PALOMA: per-domain perplexity and win rates

A stress-test in the paper is evaluation on PALOMA, a large set of text domains (571 domains ranging from news outlets to specialized Reddit communities). For each domain, next-token prediction perplexity was measured and models compared on a per-domain basis.

Two concrete win-rate statistics are given for xLSTM[$1$:$0$] versus other methods: xLSTM[$1$:$0$] had a lower perplexity than Mamba on $568$ out of $571$ domains ($99.5\%$), a lower perplexity than Llama on $486$ out of $571$ domains ($85.1\%$), and a lower perplexity than RWKV-4 on $570$ out of $571$ domains ($99.8\%$). The paper also reports that xLSTM[$1$:$0$] outperforms xLSTM[$7$:$1$] on the PALOMA language tasks (i.e., the pure mLSTM variant was slightly better on this very broad per-domain evaluation).

These per-domain win rates emphasize that xLSTM’s gains are not limited to a narrow set of domains or styles; rather, the architecture provides broadly better next-token predictions over a highly diverse collection of text distributions. The paper links some of this robustness to the matrix memory’s higher capacity (which was shown earlier to help rare-token prediction), and to the gating and normalization mechanisms that stabilize retrieval across varied inputs.

# Scaling-law fits and offsets across model families

The study also analyzed how model performance scales with model size, following the power-law scaling methodology used in prior works. The paper reports that all evaluated model families exhibit a similar power-law scaling behavior (i.e., validation loss/perplexity decreases as a power of model size), but with different offsets between families. Concretely, xLSTM shows a consistent offset advantage: at matched parameter counts xLSTM attains lower perplexity than the SSM and Transformer baselines studied.

The paper summarizes this qualitatively by saying that “all models share a similar scaling behavior but with different offsets. RWKV performs worst, followed by Llama and Mamba. xLSTM is better than Mamba with a similar margin to Mamba as Mamba has to Llama.” In other words, when performance is plotted against model size on log–log axes, the slopes are similar (the scaling exponent is similar across families) but the curves are vertically displaced; xLSTM’s curve lies below (better) the others. This implies that, extrapolating those fitted power laws, larger xLSTM models are expected to remain competitive or superior to the other families at larger scales, given similar training data regimes.

# Generation latency: linear vs. quadratic scaling

Inference latency for text generation is a critical operational metric. The paper measured text generation time for $1.3\ \mathrm{B}$-parameter models of xLSTM, Mamba, Llama, and RWKV implementations (the latter from HuggingFace). For these experiments the generation workload used batch size $1$ with a pre-fill of $16$ tokens (a setting chosen to be favorable to Transformer-style models with key-value caches). The paper reports that xLSTM and the other recurrent models (Mamba and RWKV-4) displayed linear scaling of generation time with context length, whereas the Transformer (Llama) exhibited quadratic scaling.

This difference follows directly from algorithmic complexity: full Transformer attention is quadratic in context length because each new token’s attention can depend on dot-products with all previous tokens, whereas recurrent or linear-memory mechanisms like mLSTM maintain a compressed state and only perform updates whose cost grows linearly (or stays constant per step). The paper’s measurements confirm that, in wall-clock time, xLSTM’s generation cost scales linearly as context length grows, while Llama’s cost grows roughly quadratically, making xLSTM relatively more efficient for very long contexts.

The experimental setup also notes tooling caveats: at the time of the experiments, full cache compilation of the Transformer model and compilation of the Mamba model with \texttt{torch.compile} did not work; Llama used a static key-value cache for the Transformer to speed decoding. Despite these practicalities, the qualitative conclusion holds: the recurrent/compressive-memory families (including xLSTM) are more favorable for long-context, latency-sensitive generation workloads.

# Decoding throughput gains from constant memory

Throughput in decoding (tokens per second) depends not only on per-step computation but on how memory scales with batch size and context length. Because xLSTM’s memory is compressive and independent of sequence length (the recurrent state or matrix memory does not grow with time the way a full attention cache does), xLSTM can use much higher batch sizes before running into memory limits on a GPU. The paper reports that, in throughput measurements, xLSTM achieves the highest decoding throughput among the compared models because it can run with larger batch sizes while using constant memory for the context.

The throughput experiments varied batch sizes and Transformer prefill settings; the paper states that xLSTM “can use much higher batch sizes than Llama due to its constant memory and thus achieves the highest throughput.” Practically, this means that for server deployments that benefit from batching many requests, or for applications where many parallel decode streams are needed, xLSTM’s memory scaling enables better utilization of hardware and thus higher sustained decoding rates.

# Implications for deployment at scale

Taken together, the experiments paint a coherent story about xLSTM for production LLM deployment. Strengths highlighted in the paper include:

- Robust long-context extrapolation: models trained at context length $2048$ perform well up to $16384$, enabling longer-context use without retraining on much longer sequences.

- Strong validation perplexity and downstream performance: across tested sizes, xLSTM variants achieved the best validation perplexities and generally the best common-sense reasoning performance compared to representative Transformer, SSM, and RNN baselines.

- Broad per-domain generalization: on PALOMA’s $571$ domains xLSTM[$1$:$0$] dominated most domains by large margins in win rates versus the baselines.

- Favorable scaling laws: xLSTM exhibits similar power-law exponents but a better offset, implying competitive advantage at larger parameter scales when trained on comparable data.

- Inference advantages: linear generation-time scaling and constant memory for context enable lower latency for long-context generation and much higher decoding throughput due to larger possible batch sizes.

However, the paper also lists limitations and practical caveats that matter for deployment decisions. Notably, the mLSTM matrix memory involves $d\times d$ matrix updates and retrievals, which are computationally heavier than scalar cell updates and currently result in higher computation cost (the implementation in the paper was about $4\times$ slower than FlashAttention at the time of experiments). The sLSTM’s memory mixing (recurrent hidden-to-hidden connections) is not parallelizable in the same way; although the authors developed a fast CUDA kernel for sLSTM, it remains somewhat slower than the parallel mLSTM implementation (reported as typically less than $2\times$ slower). Other practical concerns include careful initialization of forget gates and the potential for the matrix memory to become overloaded if context length grows without bound (the paper reports no observed issue up to $16\ \mathrm{k}$). Finally, the authors note that the large-scale experiments reported did not exhaustively optimize hyperparameters and implementations; further engineering and tuning could improve both performance and latency.

In short, xLSTM combines improved model capabilities (memory capacity and gating) with algorithmic advantages (linear time, constant memory for context) that make it an attractive candidate for large-scale LLM deployment, particularly in settings where long contexts and high decoding throughput are required. At the same time, practitioners must weigh the current computational overheads of matrix-memory operations and remaining implementation optimization work before replacing highly optimized Transformer stacks in production.

Practical Recipes and Engineering Guidance

This chapter collects the engineering lessons and concrete, experiment-grounded recipes reported in the xLSTM paper. Each section ties a practical decision to the model mechanisms and to the empirical findings in the paper so you can make informed trade-offs when designing, training, and deploying xLSTM models across scales and budgets.

# Choosing the sLSTM:mLSTM block ratio (xLSTM[a:b])

The paper uses the notation xLSTM[$a$:$b$] to denote the ratio $a/b$ of mLSTM-based versus sLSTM-based xLSTM blocks. Concretely, xLSTM[7:1] means that out of 8 blocks, 7 are mLSTM-based and 1 is sLSTM-based; for a total of 48 blocks this corresponds to 42 mLSTM blocks and 6 sLSTM blocks. Use that notation to record and compare variants.

Why this ratio matters
- mLSTM and sLSTM bring different capabilities. mLSTM expands the scalar LSTM memory to a matrix memory $\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ with the covariance-style update
  $\displaystyle \mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \,\mathbf{v}_t \,\mathbf{k}_t^\top$,
  so it provides much larger storage capacity and is designed to be parallelizable. The paper shows mLSTM helps rare-token prediction and improves overall language-model perplexity when scaled.
- sLSTM keeps a scalar memory but preserves memory mixing through recurrent connections and introduces exponential gating plus a normalizer state. That mixing enables state-tracking behavior (formal languages, parity-like tasks) that methods lacking memory mixing cannot solve in the paper’s experiments.

Empirical signals to guide the ratio
- If your task requires explicit state tracking or formal-language-style reasoning (the paper’s synthetic formal-language tests), you need memory mixing: sLSTM-only (xLSTM[0:1]) or hybrids with sLSTM are effective. Models lacking memory mixing failed on such tasks.
- If your primary target is large-scale language modeling (perplexity on SlimPajama) the paper finds mLSTM-heavy configurations perform best. Both xLSTM[1:0] (only mLSTM) and xLSTM[7:1] performed very well in the 15B and 300B token experiments; xLSTM[1:0] gave slightly better results on many language-domain evaluations (PALOMA).
- For associative-recall style memory benchmarks, a hybrid (xLSTM[1:1] in the experiments) gave the best non-Transformer performance—i.e., sLSTM does not necessarily reduce memory capacity and can even help.

Practical recipe
- For a general-purpose language model: start mLSTM-dominant (e.g., xLSTM[1:0] or a high $a:b$ ratio such as xLSTM[7:1]) because that configuration gave the best validation perplexities in the paper’s LM experiments.
- If you expect significant state-tracking, programmatic reasoning, or formal-language behavior: include sLSTM blocks (at least a few) so the network can leverage memory mixing.
- For mixed workloads (some long-range associative memory and some state tracking), use a hybrid mix—this is precisely what the xLSTM[1:1] and xLSTM[7:1] experiments explored.

# Dimensionality, number of cells/heads, and projections

Key equations and structures to keep in mind
- mLSTM memory is a full matrix: $\mathbf{C}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. Keys, values, and queries are $d$‑dimensional vectors:
  \begin{align*}
    \mathbf{q}_t &= \mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_q,\\
    \mathbf{k}_t &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\,\mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_k,\\
    \mathbf{v}_t &= \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_v.
  \end{align*}
  The $1/\sqrt{d}$ key scaling is part of the design reported in the paper.
- The mLSTM covariance update is
  $\displaystyle \mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \,\mathbf{v}_t \,\mathbf{k}_t^\top$,
  and retrieval uses the normalizer $\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{k}_t$ and
  $\displaystyle \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,\,1\}}$.

Implications for dimension and cells
- Because mLSTM stores a $d\times d$ matrix, increasing $d$ increases memory capacity roughly quadratically (and increases compute cost for the memory update and retrieval similarly). The paper explicitly calls out this trade-off: memory capacity vs computational complexity.
- The paper treats “multiple memory cells” and “multiple heads” differently for sLSTM and mLSTM:
  - For mLSTM: multiple heads and multiple cells are equivalent because there is no memory mixing.
  - For sLSTM: multiple memory cells enable memory mixing via recurrent matrices ($\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{z}}$, $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{i}}$, etc.), and multiple heads allow mixing within a head but not across heads. Thus head/cell design matters more for sLSTM.

Projection and block-level choices
- The paper uses different residual block backbones for the two variants: sLSTM is paired with a post up-projection block (non-linear summary in original space, then up-projection to high dimensional space), while mLSTM is paired with a pre up-projection block (linear map to high dimension, then non-linear summarization there). Follow those pairings because they align memory capacity with the embedding dimensionality in the experiments.

Practical recipe
- When you need more memory capacity, increase $d$ on mLSTM blocks, but be mindful of the $d\times d$ cost. Expect higher compute but parallelizable matrix operations; the paper notes this overhead can be handled on GPUs.
- For sLSTM, tune the number of cells/heads to control internal mixing; if state-tracking is important, give sLSTM sufficient internal scalar cells and allow recurrent mixing matrices.
- Use the block pairing from the paper: pre up-projection for mLSTM blocks, post up-projection for sLSTM blocks.

# When to prefer sigmoid vs. exponential forget gates

What the architectures allow
- Both sLSTM and mLSTM permit forget gates $f_t$ to be either sigmoid or exponential:
  \[
    f_t \;=\; \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)\quad\text{OR}\quad f_t \;=\; \exp(\tilde{f}_t),
  \]
  while the input gate is typically exponential in the proposed designs (e.g., $i_t=\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$).

Why exponential gates were introduced
- Exponential gating was introduced to enable the network to “revise storage decisions.” In the paper’s nearest-neighbor search synthetic task, the original LSTM could not revise a stored value when a more similar vector arrived later, whereas xLSTM with exponential gating could. In short: exponential gates increase the range and multiplicative flexibility of storage/removal dynamics, which the experiments show can be important.

Numerical implications and the trade-off
- Exponentials are unbounded and can produce very large values, which can cause overflows and numerical instability. To use exponential gates safely you must implement the stabilization described in the paper (see the next section). The paper shows that with stabilization the exponential gates can be used without changing outputs or derivatives.
- Sigmoid gates are bounded between 0 and 1 and therefore are naturally numerically constrained; they can be chosen when you prefer bounded gating and wish to avoid exponential stabilization mechanisms.

Practical recipe
- Prefer exponential input gating ($i_t=\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$) when you want the stronger revision and learning dynamics introduced in the paper; for the forget gate, use exponential gating together with the stabilization recipe if you need revisability. If you explicitly want simpler bounded gates or want to avoid implementing stabilization, you can retain the sigmoid forget gate variant that is supported by the formulations in the paper.

# Normalizer thresholds and numerical stability tips

The two stabilization/normalization mechanisms to implement exactly as described
1. Stabilizing exponential gates. Because exponentials can overflow, the paper stabilizes input and forget gate products with a running stabilizer $m_t$:
   \begin{align*}
     m_t &= \max\big(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1},\; \log(i_t)\big),\\
     i'_t &= \exp\big(\log(i_t) - m_t\big) = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t),\\
     f'_t &= \exp\big(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t\big).
   \end{align*}
   The paper proves (and uses) the fact that replacing $i_t,f_t$ with $i'_t,f'_t$ in the forward pass neither changes the network’s outputs nor the derivatives of the loss w.r.t. parameters. Implementing $m_t$ exactly (working in log-space and using $\max$) is therefore the intended, numerically-stable path when using exponentials.

2. Normalizer threshold for mLSTM retrieval. The mLSTM uses a normalizer vector $\mathbf{n}_t$ and divides by the dot product magnitude when retrieving:
   \[
     \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,\,1\}}.
   \]
   The paper recommends taking the absolute value of the dot product and clamping the denominator from below; a typical lower bound used in the experiments is $1.0$ (this follows previous work cited in the paper). This prevents near-zero denominators that would amplify noise or lead to numerical blow-up.

Other practical stability notes (as used in the paper)
- Apply a LayerNorm before projecting inputs to keys and values so that key vectors have zero mean; the paper assumes such a layer-norm and notes that zero-mean keys make the covariance update optimal for separability.
- When implementing exponentials and the stabilizer, do the computations in log-space where appropriate and maintain the stabilizer $m_t$ across time steps.

# Initialization (e.g., forget gate bias) and LayerNorm choices

LayerNorm architecture choice
- The xLSTM architectures in the paper use pre-LayerNorm residual backbones (the residual blocks apply LayerNorm before the block, as in many contemporary LLMs). In addition, the paper assumes a layer-norm before projecting to keys and values for mLSTM to produce zero-mean keys (important for the covariance update behavior).

Forget gate initialization
- The paper highlights as a limitation that the initialization of forget gates must be chosen carefully. It does not prescribe a single numeric initialization but signals that this initialization materially affects behavior. Therefore, treat forget-gate initialization as an important hyperparameter to tune in practice (the paper flagged it as something to be careful about).

Practical recipe
- Use pre-LayerNorm residual backbones as the base architecture (this is what the experiments used).
- Be sure to include a LayerNorm before key/value projections in mLSTM blocks to align with the covariance-memory assumptions.
- Pay attention to forget-gate initialization in experiments and monitor training stability/performance as you vary it; it is recognized in the paper as a crucial setting.

# Training schedules, precision, and gradient stability

Precision and mixed-precision training
- The large-scale experiments in the paper were run with mixed precision. The paper also notes practical issues encountered in mixed-precision training for some other models (e.g., RWKV-5/6 and HGRN2), where the automated mixed-precision path (PyTorch AMP) was not used in some runs. This underscores that mixed precision helps training cost, but precision settings can require per-model attention.

Gradient stability and exponential gates
- The stabilization trick for exponential gates (the $m_t$ stabilizer and the transformed gates $i'_t,f'_t$) is not only numerically required but also constructed so that derivatives are preserved. The paper explicitly shows that using the stabilized gates does not change the outputs nor the gradients w.r.t. parameters; so if you implement exponential gating follow the stabilizer recipe to retain correct gradient behavior.

Practical recipe
- Use mixed precision to reduce memory footprint and speed up training, but validate the mixed-precision path for your particular implementation (AMP or manual handling) because some recurrent/block implementations may require special handling (as the paper’s training notes show).
- If you enable exponential gating, implement the stabilizer exactly to ensure correct forward and backward behavior.
- Monitor training for unexpected precision-related instability—especially when porting or re-implementing models—because the paper observed such sensitivities in other architectures during large-scale runs.

# Inference strategies, batching, and memory budgeting

Asymptotic complexity and decoding behavior
- A core practical advantage emphasized in the paper is that xLSTM networks operate with linear computation and constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length (in contrast to the Transformer’s quadratic attention memory with context length). This gives xLSTM an inference advantage for long contexts and for large batch decoding.
- mLSTM’s matrix memory and update are computationally heavy per step (because of $d\times d$ operations), but the memory usage is constant with the sequence length and the compute is parallelizable. sLSTM incurs hidden–hidden recurrent mixing that prevents full parallelization across time steps, which can slow inference; the authors implemented a custom CUDA kernel that reduced the practical slowdown (it was reported to be less than two times slower than the parallel mLSTM implementation in their setup).

Empirical decoding observations from the paper
- In generation time experiments at the 1.3B scale, xLSTM and other recurrent models exhibited linear scaling with context length, while Llama (Transformer) showed quadratic scaling. Because xLSTM has constant memory usage w.r.t. sequence length, it can use much larger batch sizes than Llama and achieved the highest decoding throughput in the authors’ tests.
- The paper used “pre-fill” during generation experiments (they tested batch size 1 and pre-fill 16) and compared to Transformer implementations that use a static key-value cache; the xLSTM variants still showed the linear-scaling, constant-memory benefits.

Memory budgeting caveats
- The mLSTM matrix memory is independent of sequence length: the memory does not grow with the sequence. However, the finite matrix $\mathbf{C}$ can be overloaded if you push to very long contexts beyond what was tested. The paper notes that this did not appear to be a limitation for contexts tested up to 16k tokens.

Practical recipe
- For high-throughput, long-context decoding prioritize mLSTM-dominant or pure-mLSTM configurations because they are parallelizable and enjoy constant memory for context length.
- If state-mixing is required at inference time (e.g., for entity tracking or program evaluation), include sLSTM blocks but anticipate somewhat higher per-token latencies unless you use the specialized CUDA kernel described by the authors.
- When budgeting memory for inference, remember the matrix-memory $d\times d$ cost for mLSTM blocks and that this cost does not grow with context length—this lets you trade compute for capacity while keeping working memory bounded during decoding.

# Interfacing with existing frameworks and the reference codebase

Reference code and implementation notes
- The paper provides a public code repository: https://github.com/NX-AI/xlstm. That repository is the canonical starting point if you want the exact block implementations, the stabilizer logic for exponential gates, and the authors’ CUDA kernels for sLSTM and parallel mLSTM updates.
- The authors implemented a fast CUDA kernel for sLSTM with GPU memory optimizations (down to register level) to mitigate the lack of parallelizability due to hidden–hidden mixing. For mLSTM they emphasize that it is parallelizable and conceptually analogous to approaches such as FlashAttention, though the authors’ kernels were not as optimized as FlashAttention at the time of writing.

Practical recipe for framework integration
- Start from the authors’ repository to ensure you get the stabilizer $m_t$, the normalizer clamping, the pre/post up-projection block choices, and the pre-LayerNorm residual backbone exactly as used in the experiments.
- If you integrate xLSTM blocks into frameworks that expect Transformer-style layers (HuggingFace, etc.), mind the differences: mLSTM blocks are parallelizable and therefore fit more naturally into batched training loops, while sLSTM blocks require the special CUDA kernel or a scan/loop implementation with different performance characteristics.
- If you intend to run large-scale experiments with mixed precision, validate your AMP path (or alternative mixed-precision mechanism) with xLSTM blocks early, because the paper reports that different architectures have different sensitivities to precision handling.

Closing note
The paper’s experiments and ablations tie most of the engineering guidance above to concrete results: exponential gating plus the matrix memory are central contributors to the empirical gains, pre-LayerNorm backbones were used throughout, and the block-level design choices (pre/post up-projection) were fixed per block type. Use those experiment-proven choices as your baseline and treat forget-gate initialization, mixed-precision settings, and the sLSTM/mLSTM ratio as the primary levers to tune for your task and resource envelope. For code-level detail and exact implementations, consult the reference repository linked in the paper.

Limitations, Risks, and Open Research Directions

# sLSTM’s non-parallelizable mixing and performance implications

The sLSTM variant extends the classical scalar LSTM cell by introducing exponential gates together with a normalizer state. In its basic forward pass the sLSTM keeps the familiar additive cell update
\[
c_t \;=\; f_t \, c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, z_t
\]
but it also tracks a normalizer
\[
n_t \;=\; f_t \, n_{t-1} \;+\; i_t
\]
and emits a normalized hidden output
\[
\tilde{h}_t \;=\; \frac{c_t}{n_t}, \qquad h_t = o_t \, \tilde{h}_t .
\]
Crucially, when multiple scalar memory cells are used the sLSTM supports recurrent “memory mixing”: the hidden state vector \(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\) is fed back to the gates and cell inputs through recurrent weight matrices \(\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{z}},\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{i}},\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{f}},\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{o}}\). This hidden–hidden coupling is exactly what gives sLSTM its expressive power for state-tracking problems — the network can revise and remix stored information across cells as the sequence unfolds.

That expressive power comes at a cost: the hidden–hidden dependence enforces a sequential computation that prohibits the kind of straightforward parallelization used in Transformer attention or in the parallel reformulation of the mLSTM. The paper explicitly notes that sLSTM “is not parallelizable due to the memory mixing (hidden–hidden connections).” Practically, this means that wall-clock throughput for long sequences and large models can be limited by the need to iterate time step by time step. In the xLSTM work this was addressed partly by engineering: the authors developed a highly optimized CUDA kernel with register-level memory optimizations; even so, the optimized sLSTM implementation remains, in their reporting, typically under two times slower than the parallel mLSTM implementation.

For a student learning the design trade-offs, the lesson is clear. Memory mixing (sequential recurrence) versus parallelizability is a key axis: sLSTM sits on the side of recurrence and expressivity (state tracking, solving certain formal-language tasks that non-mixing models fail), while this same design limits raw throughput. In architectural practice the paper balances these trade-offs by mixing block types: xLSTM architectures include both mLSTM and sLSTM blocks (notation xLSTM[a:b] denotes the ratio of mLSTM:sLSTM blocks). Using sLSTM sparsely — e.g., only a few sLSTM-based blocks among many mLSTM-based blocks — is therefore a practical way to preserve the state-tracking advantage while keeping much of the model parallelizable.

Open engineering and research directions grounded in the paper’s findings include (a) further low-level optimization of sLSTM kernels to narrow the runtime gap, (b) principled placement strategies for sLSTM blocks inside deep stacks so that recurrence is applied only where it yields the most benefit, and (c) exploiting the sLSTM head design (memory mixing within heads but not across heads) as a means to partition recurrence and recover limited parallelism across independent heads.

# mLSTM’s O(d^2) compute and kernel-optimization needs

The mLSTM variant replaces the scalar cell with a matrix memory \(\mathbf{C}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) and stores information via a covariance-style (outer-product) update:
\[
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; f_t \, \mathbf{C}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, \mathbf{v}_t \, \mathbf{k}_t^\top,
\]
with a matching normalizer
\[
\mathbf{n}_t \;=\; f_t \, \mathbf{n}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, \mathbf{k}_t,
\]
and retrieval
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\big\{\,|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,\,1\,\big\}}, \qquad
\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t .
\]
Because the memory is explicitly a dense \(d\times d\) matrix and updates involve a rank-one outer product \(\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top\), both the update and retrieval operations carry a computational cost scaling on the order of \(d^2\) per time step. This contrasts with scalar-cell LSTMs and many linearized attention schemes whose per-step cost scales like \(d\) (times some small constant).

The benefit of this \(O(d^2)\) investment is a large jump in storage capacity: the matrix memory implements a form of associative memory (covariance memory / fast weights) that can store distributed key–value patterns more richly than scalar cells can. The paper shows that this increased capacity helps in associative-recall-style tasks and rare-token prediction.

However, the compute cost is real. The authors report that their current mLSTM kernels are not yet optimized: the implementation is about four times slower than FlashAttention or the scan used in Mamba. On the positive side, the mLSTM updates are parameter-free matrix operations that can be expressed by standard linear algebra kernels and parallelized on GPUs, so there is substantial room for kernel-level acceleration.

Concrete, paper-grounded directions for improving mLSTM practical performance are therefore primarily engineering-oriented: design and implement fused CUDA kernels for the outer-product update and the \(\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t\) retrieval; borrow ideas from highly optimized attention kernels such as FlashAttention (the paper explicitly suggests “in the vein of FlashAttention”); and exploit model structure (for example, using multiple heads or multiple cells with smaller per-head dimension — the paper notes that “for mLSTM multiple heads and multiple cells are equivalent”) to trade dimension \(d\) against parallelism. Any such optimization reduces the constant factors that turn \(O(d^2)\) into a practical wall-clock cost.

# Memory overload risks at very long contexts

One of the architectural choices that gives mLSTM its strength — the covariance accumulation that compresses many past key–value contributions into a single matrix \(\mathbf{C}_t\) — is also the source of a potential risk when contexts become extremely long. Because \(\mathbf{C}_t\) is updated cumulatively (modulated by forget gates), it is independent of the absolute sequence length and can, in principle, accumulate many contributions. Two interrelated problems can arise.

First, accumulation can cause interference: the outer-product updates of many different key–value pairs can overlap in the same matrix entries, increasing retrieval noise and reducing the signal-to-noise ratio for any particular past item. Second, numerical and scale issues can arise if the effective input magnitudes or gate strengths cause the aggregated matrix to grow or shrink in an uncontrolled way.

The xLSTM design includes several stabilizing mechanisms directly addressing these risks. The normalizer \(\mathbf{n}_t\) records the effective summed key mass (weighted by gates), and the retrieval rescales the readout by \(\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,1\}\) to avoid dividing by very small numbers. The paper also assumes a LayerNorm before the key/value projections so that keys have (approximately) zero mean — this centering helps the covariance update behave more like a zero-mean covariance and therefore stabilizes accumulation. For exponential gating, the stabilizer state \(m_t\) (defined by \(m_t=\max(\log f_t + m_{t-1},\,\log i_t)\)) prevents overflow from very large gate values.

Empirically, the authors did not observe a practical memory overload for the contexts they tested: xLSTM models maintained good performance up to context lengths of 16k. Nonetheless, the paper flags the general risk: “increasing the sequence length might overload the memory for longer context sizes.” This suggests that, as users push to ever-longer contexts, careful attention must be paid to gate dynamics, normalization, and possibly explicit capacity-control mechanisms. Grounded and conservative research directions include exploring gate biasing and scheduling (see the next section), experimenting with different normalizers or thresholding strategies, and testing limits on synthetic workloads where the number of distinct keys grows without bound.

# Sensitivity to forget-gate initialization

The forget gate \(f_t\) is central to both sLSTM and mLSTM dynamics. In the scalar case \(f_t\) multiplies the previous cell content, and in the matrix case it scales the entire matrix memory \(\mathbf{C}_{t-1}\). Thus the initial biasing and learned behavior of the forget gate determine whether stored content is retained, slowly decayed, or rapidly forgotten. The paper points out succinctly that “the initialization of the forget gates must be chosen carefully.”

Why is this a practical issue? If the forget gate is initialized so that \(f_t\) is consistently near zero at the start of training, the model will tend to discard past information aggressively and it may fail to learn long-term dependencies. Conversely, if \(f_t\) is initialized too close to one (or, with exponential gating, to a large value), the memory may retain outdated or noisy signals for too long, slowing down learning or inducing instability. Exponential gating magnifies these sensitivities because small changes in pre-activation yield multiplicative effects after the exponential. Although the stabilizer \(m_t\) mitigates overflow, it does not remove the need for sensible bias initializations and training schedules.

The practical consequence is that training xLSTM models — and especially deep stacks that mix sLSTM and mLSTM blocks — requires targeted experimentation with forget-gate biases and initial parameter configurations. The paper lists this as a limitation and a source of fragility, so an important research and engineering task is to document robust initialization recipes and to test their transfer across scales and datasets.

# Underexplored hyperparameter regimes at larger scales

In their large-scale experiments the authors note a pragmatic constraint: “Due to the expensive computational load for large language experiments, we did neither fully optimize the architecture nor the hyperparameters, especially for larger xLSTM architectures.” This candid admission highlights an important open area.

xLSTM introduces several discrete and continuous design choices that interact: the ratio of mLSTM to sLSTM blocks (xLSTM[a:b]), the placement of sLSTM blocks inside the stack, the per-block projection strategy (pre versus post up-projection), the choice between exponential and sigmoid gating for \(f_t\), the dimensionality \(d\) of the matrix memory, head count, normalization practices, and many optimizer and precision choices used during training. The ablation studies in the paper already demonstrate that exponential gating and the matrix memory both contribute substantially to improved validation perplexity, but the optimal operating point in the huge combinatorial hyperparameter space is still unknown.

For students and researchers, this means there is clear room for targeted hyperparameter studies: scale up systematic sweeps of block ratios and placements, explore per-layer gate initializations and learning-rate schedules, compare mixed-precision recipes, and measure sensitivity of the performance-to-compute trade-off as model size grows. Because xLSTM’s different components (matrix memory, exponential gates, normalizers) interact nonlinearly, careful controlled ablation and scaling studies will be especially important to understand where the model delivers the most benefit per unit of compute.

# Hybrid architectures with attention or SSM components

xLSTM’s block design deliberately echoes current trends: the paper describes two residual block backbones — a post up-projection variant “like Transformers” and a pre up-projection variant “like State Space Models” — and uses these choices differently for sLSTM and mLSTM blocks (post for sLSTM, pre for mLSTM). This architectural kinship suggests a natural research direction: hybrid architectures that combine xLSTM blocks with explicit attention layers or State Space Model (SSM) components.

Why consider hybrids? Each family has complementary strengths. Transformer attention provides flexible token-to-token interactions and has excellent parallelism, SSMs provide linear-in-context algorithms with good long-range inductive bias, and xLSTM brings powerful compressive matrix memory and explicit recurrence that enables state tracking. The paper also empirically demonstrates that Transformers and SSMs struggle on tasks that require memory mixing (state tracking), while xLSTM can solve those tasks. A hybrid model could therefore aim to use attention where token-to-token interactions are most beneficial, SSM components where long-range linear dynamics suffice, and xLSTM blocks where explicit memory mixing and associative storage are needed.

The xLSTM experiments already explore one hybridization dimension — mixing mLSTM and sLSTM blocks in different ratios — and the block pre/post up-projection choices align xLSTM with Transformer and SSM backbones. Extending this to include explicit attention modules or interleaving SSM layers is a grounded and promising avenue for future work: it follows directly from the paper’s architectural motifs and comparative findings.

# Learned decay/learning-rate schedules and adaptive memory

One of the conceptual clarifications in the paper is the interpretation of gates in the mLSTM as dynamical control of memory dynamics: the forget gate acts like a decay rate on stored memory, and the input gate plays the role of a learning rate for the outer-product updates. This links the mLSTM to prior fast-weight and retention models where decay and learning rates are explicit hyperparameters.

A natural open direction is to make those temporal control signals themselves more adaptive and learnable at richer timescales. The present xLSTM already lets gates be functions of the current input (through learned projections), but further possibilities include meta-parameterizations or slower learned schedules that modulate gate biases across layers or training time, or even content-dependent controllers that alter the effective decay when the model recognizes signals that should be preserved longer. The paper points to the connection with Fast Weight Programmers and earlier work that used constant decay and learning rates; replacing fixed scalars with data-dependent, learned controllers is therefore an evolution that is squarely grounded in the paper’s modeling perspective.

Any such adaptive-memory scheme should be designed and evaluated carefully: adaptation promises greater flexibility (e.g., keeping episodic signals longer, accelerating forgetting of spurious correlations), but it also increases model complexity and introduces new stability considerations — the very issues the authors note with forget-gate initialization and exponential gating stabilization.

# Applications beyond language: RL, time series, physical systems

The original LSTM has a long track record across domains beyond language, and the paper highlights several concrete application areas where LSTMs have excelled: reinforcement learning (AlphaStar, OpenAI Five), time-series and hydrological modeling (rainfall–runoff, flooding warnings), controllers for complex physical systems (magnetic controller for nuclear fusion), and generative sequence modeling (text, handwriting, code). The conclusion of the paper explicitly suggests that xLSTM “has the potential to considerably impact other fields like Reinforcement Learning, Time Series Prediction, or the modeling of physical systems.”

Why is xLSTM especially attractive beyond language? Two complementary features stand out. First, the matrix memory and covariance updates give much larger associative-storage capacity than scalar cells, which can be critical for tasks that must memorize large numbers of distinct episodic items (e.g., in long-horizon reinforcement learning or in time-series settings with many distinct regimes). Second, the compressive nature of the memory — the fact that memory size does not grow with sequence length — makes xLSTM attractive for deployment in resource-constrained environments (edge devices) where constant memory complexity is a practical advantage.

The paper’s experiments demonstrate extrapolation and associative recall strengths of xLSTM on synthetic tasks and language benchmarks. Translating those strengths to real-world domains is therefore a natural next step: evaluate xLSTM on RL benchmarks that require long-term credit assignment and state tracking, benchmark it on production time-series datasets (including the hydrology problems cited for LSTMs), and test it on physics-informed control tasks where a compact, associative memory could accelerate learning and inference.

# Summary and prioritized research agenda

The xLSTM paper already addresses several of the promises and pitfalls of scaling LSTM-style memory to modern model sizes. To summarize the limitations and candidate research directions emphasized and supported by the paper:

1. sLSTM parallelization and kernels. Continue low-level optimization of sLSTM kernels; investigate architectural placements (few sLSTM blocks among many mLSTM blocks) and head-partitioning to trade expressivity for parallelism.

2. mLSTM compute optimization. Implement fused, FlashAttention-style CUDA kernels for the outer-product update and retrieval; exploit multiple heads (equate to multiple cells) to reduce per-head dimension \(d\) and reduce \(O(d^2)\) cost in practice.

3. Memory dynamics at scale. Systematically test limits of matrix memory as context increases and study gate behaviors that prevent overload; evaluate normalization strategies and thresholding across extreme-context synthetic setups.

4. Gate initialization and stability. Run controlled experiments on forget-gate biasing schemes and stabilization mechanisms (the \(m_t\) stabilizer already introduced) to establish robust initialization recipes.

5. Hyperparameter and architectural sweeps. Perform targeted searches over block ratios xLSTM[a:b], pre/post up-projection choices, head counts, and gating nonlinearities to find efficient operating points at large scale.

6. Hybrids with attention and SSMs. Design and evaluate hybrid stacks that combine xLSTM blocks with attention or SSM modules to marry state tracking and associative memory with token-wise interactions and efficient long-range dynamics.

7. Learned memory control. Explore richer, possibly slower-timescale controllers for decay and input learning rates (forget and input gates), extending the fixed-decay spirit of fast-weight programmers to data-adaptive controllers.

8. Application-driven evaluation. Carry xLSTM into reinforcement learning benchmarks, production time-series tasks, and control problems to test whether the associative memory and state-mixing benefits translate to practical gains outside language modeling.

All of these directions are practical and grounded in the paper’s technical design, empirical findings, and candid limitations section. They form a concrete roadmap: combine focused kernel engineering and stability work (to fix the existing bottlenecks) with systematic architecture and hyperparameter exploration (to find where xLSTM best leverages its memory advantages), and then translate the resulting models into domains where associative, compressive memory and state tracking are essential.

Mathematical Notes and Derivations

# Why normalization by the gate mass preserves scale and gradients

We analyze the sLSTM normalization defined by the coupled recurrences
\[
c_t = f_t\,c_{t-1} + i_t\,z_t,\qquad
n_t = f_t\,n_{t-1} + i_t,
\qquad
h_t = o_t\,\frac{c_t}{n_t},
\]
with \(i_t>0\) and \(f_t>0\) (for sLSTM \(i_t=\exp(\tilde i_t)\), and \(f_t\) is either \(\sigma(\tilde f_t)\) or \(\exp(\tilde f_t)\)). The quantity \(n_t\) is the normalizer or gate mass: it accumulates the effective amount of input mass that has been written into the cell after accounting for forget-decay. The ratio \(c_t/n_t\) is central: it produces a normalized memory content rather than an unbounded accumulated sum.

To see why this normalization preserves scale and stabilizes gradients, expand the recurrences explicitly (assume \(c_0,n_0\) given; the common simplification \(c_0=n_0=0\) is used in many presentations but is not required). By unrolling,
\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t &= \Big(\prod_{s=1}^t f_s\Big)c_0 \;+\; \sum_{j=1}^t \Big(\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s\Big)\, i_j\, z_j,\\
n_t &= \Big(\prod_{s=1}^t f_s\Big)n_0 \;+\; \sum_{j=1}^t \Big(\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s\Big)\, i_j.
\end{aligned}
\]
Define the (nonnegative) unnormalized weights
\[
w_j^{(t)} \;=\; \Big(\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s\Big)\, i_j,
\]
so that
\[
\frac{c_t}{n_t}
\;=\;
\frac{\sum_{j=1}^t w_j^{(t)} z_j \;+\; \big(\prod_{s=1}^t f_s\big)c_0}
     {\sum_{j=1}^t w_j^{(t)} \;+\; \big(\prod_{s=1}^t f_s\big)n_0}.
\]
Thus \(c_t/n_t\) is a weighted average of the past inputs \(z_j\) (and of the initial state), with weights \(w_j^{(t)}\) that combine the write strength \(i_j\) and the subsequent decay factors \(\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s\). Two immediate consequences follow.

First, scale invariance: if all weights \(w_j^{(t)}\) are multiplied by a common positive factor \(\lambda\) then both numerator and denominator are multiplied by \(\lambda\) and the ratio \(c_t/n_t\) is unchanged. More generally, multiplicative rescaling that affects numerator and denominator coherently does not change the normalized memory content. This is the mechanism by which the normalizer removes an overall multiplicative "mass" that would otherwise cause \(c_t\) to grow or shrink uncontrollably; the model's readout \(\tilde h_t = c_t/n_t\) depends only on relative weights among stored items, not on their absolute scale.

Second, gradient stabilization: because the readout is a normalized average, gradients that flow back through the ratio inherit the normalization. Writing \(u_t = c_t\) and \(v_t = n_t\), the derivative of the normalized state with respect to any parameter \(\theta\) takes the quotient form
\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\Big(\frac{u_t}{v_t}\Big)
= \frac{v_t\,\partial_\theta u_t - u_t\,\partial_\theta v_t}{v_t^{\,2}}.
\]
Both \(\partial_\theta u_t\) and \(\partial_\theta v_t\) are linear combinations of the same terms (because both recurrences use the same gate factors \(i_t,f_t\)), so the subtraction in the numerator cancels common large multiplicative factors; the denominator \(v_t^{2}\) scales quadratically with the mass, producing a normalization of gradient magnitudes. Intuitively, when a long run of large multiplicative gates would otherwise produce exploding intermediate \(c_t\), the same multiplicative effect appears in \(n_t\) and cancels in the ratio as well as in most of the gradient numerator. Thus the normalizer removes both forward-scale drift and the corresponding gradient scaling that would lead to instability.

Finally, from an interpretive viewpoint this normalization makes retrieval attention-like: the read \(\tilde h_t\) is a convex-like combination of prior values \(z_j\) (weights are nonnegative), which improves numerical behavior and makes the cell content invariant to a global multiplicative mass that might arise from using exponential gates.

# Log-space stabilizer invariance: sketch of proof

sLSTM and mLSTM use exponential gates which can under naive implementation overflow or underflow in floating point. The paper stabilizes exponentials by maintaining a log-space stabilizer state \(m_t\) and replacing gates \(i_t,f_t\) by scaled versions \(i'_t,f'_t\) (Equations in the paper)
\[
\begin{aligned}
m_t &= \max\big( \log(f_t) + m_{t-1},\ \log(i_t) \big),\\
i'_t &= \exp\big( \log(i_t) - m_t \big),\\
f'_t &= \exp\big( \log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t \big).
\end{aligned}
\]
The claim (stated in the paper and proved in the appendix) is that replacing \(i_t,f_t\) by \(i'_t,f'_t\) in the forward-pass recurrences does not change the network outputs, and therefore does not change gradients with respect to parameters. We give a compact sketch of the algebraic invariance argument; the full details follow the same lines and are standard.

Introduce scaled states
\[
\hat c_t \;=\; e^{-m_t}\, c_t,\qquad
\hat n_t \;=\; e^{-m_t}\, n_t .
\]
We show by induction that \(\hat c_t\) and \(\hat n_t\) evolve under the same recurrence as the original states but with the stabilized gates \(i'_t,f'_t\):
\[
\hat c_t = f'_t \,\hat c_{t-1} + i'_t\, z_t,\qquad
\hat n_t = f'_t \,\hat n_{t-1} + i'_t.
\]
Base case: at \(t=1\) the identity follows by direct substitution. Inductive step: assume the identities hold at \(t-1\). Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
\hat c_t
&= e^{-m_t} c_t
= e^{-m_t}\big(f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t\big)\\
&= e^{\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t}\,(e^{-m_{t-1}} c_{t-1})
 \;+\; e^{\log(i_t)-m_t}\,z_t\\
&= f'_t \,\hat c_{t-1} + i'_t\,z_t,
\end{aligned}
\]
and an identical calculation gives the \(\hat n_t\) recurrence. Because \(h_t=o_t(c_t/n_t)\) and the scaling \(e^{-m_t}\) cancels in the ratio,
\[
\frac{c_t}{n_t} = \frac{e^{m_t}\hat c_t}{e^{m_t}\hat n_t} = \frac{\hat c_t}{\hat n_t},
\]
the readout \(\tilde h_t\) and thus \(h_t=o_t\tilde h_t\) are unchanged by the substitution of \(i'_t,f'_t\) and the internal use of log-space stabilization. Since the forward outputs are identical for every setting of the parameters, the mapping from parameters to loss is identical, and therefore all parameter gradients are identical as well. In other words, the stabilizer transforms the internal computation only by a controllable time-dependent rescaling that cancels exactly in the final readout.

The use of \(m_t=\max(\cdot,\cdot)\) is the standard log-sum / log-space trick: it keeps the exponent arguments in a safe range (so that \(i'_t\le 1\) and \(f'_t\le 1\) in many practical cases) and avoids direct computation of very large/small exponentials while preserving the exact arithmetic relationships that determine outputs.

# Mapping covariance updates to retention- and fast-weight views

mLSTM stores a matrix memory \(\mathbf C_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) with the covariance-style update
\[
\mathbf C_t = f_t\,\mathbf C_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf v_t\mathbf k_t^\top,
\qquad
\mathbf n_t = f_t\,\mathbf n_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf k_t,
\]
and retrieval
\[
\tilde{\mathbf h}_t = \frac{\mathbf C_t\,\mathbf q_t}{\max\big\{|\mathbf n_t^\top\mathbf q_t|,1\big\}}.
\]
Unrolling the matrix recurrence gives the closed-form expansion
\[
\mathbf C_t \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^t \Big(\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s\Big)\, i_j\, \mathbf v_j\mathbf k_j^\top
\;+\; \Big(\prod_{s=1}^t f_s\Big)\mathbf C_0.
\]
Multiplying by \(\mathbf q_t\) gives the retrieval as a weighted sum of past values
\[
\mathbf C_t\,\mathbf q_t
= \sum_{j=1}^t \Big(\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s\Big)\, i_j\, (\mathbf k_j^\top\mathbf q_t)\,\mathbf v_j
\;+\; \Big(\prod_{s=1}^t f_s\Big)\mathbf C_0\,\mathbf q_t.
\]
The same coefficients appear in the normalizer:
\[
\mathbf n_t^\top\mathbf q_t
= \sum_{j=1}^t \Big(\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s\Big)\, i_j\, (\mathbf k_j^\top\mathbf q_t)
\;+\; \Big(\prod_{s=1}^t f_s\Big)\mathbf n_0^\top\mathbf q_t.
\]
Therefore the normalized retrieval
\[
\tilde{\mathbf h}_t
= \frac{\sum_{j=1}^t \alpha_{j,t}\,(\mathbf k_j^\top\mathbf q_t)\,\mathbf v_j}
       {\max\big\{\,\big|\sum_{j=1}^t \alpha_{j,t}\,(\mathbf k_j^\top\mathbf q_t)\big|,\,1\big\}},
\qquad
\alpha_{j,t} \equiv \Big(\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s\Big)\, i_j,
\]
is precisely an attention-like normalized weighted average of values \(\mathbf v_j\) where the weight for item \(j\) is the product of: (i) similarity \(\mathbf k_j^\top\mathbf q_t\), (ii) the write strength \(i_j\), and (iii) the future-decay product \(\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s\).

This algebra makes the connections to related views explicit:

- Fast weights / outer-product learners: the term \(i_j\,\mathbf v_j\mathbf k_j^\top\) is exactly an outer-product (a fast weight) created at time \(j\); subsequent retrievals multiply that fast weight by \(\mathbf q_t\) to produce a contribution \((\mathbf k_j^\top\mathbf q_t)\,i_j\,\mathbf v_j\). The forget gate \(f_s\) implements exponential decay of previously written fast weights.

- Retention / linearized attention: the decay factors and the linearity of the matrix update cause each contribution to be attenuated by a scalar product of decays and to enter retrieval linearly. The normalization by \(\mathbf n_t^\top\mathbf q_t\) plays the role of a summed attention mass, just as softmax normalization does in standard attention, but here computed in a compressive online fashion.

- Attention-like behavior without quadratic memory: \(\mathbf C_t\) stores compressed summary information so that the retrieval approximates the attention output without materializing a \(T\times T\) attention matrix; the cost is the \(d\times d\) state and its \(O(d^2)\) updates and multiplies rather than \(O(T^2)\) with \(T\) the context length.

Because the paper assumes a layer-norm before projecting to keys (zero-mean keys), the covariance update aligns with classical results (cited in the paper) that the covariance rule is optimal for maximal separability (maximizing signal-to-noise) in certain binary-retrieval settings. Intuitively, centering keys increases the orthogonality among stored directions and reduces bias that would otherwise accumulate in the matrix memory.

# Parallel-scan formulation for mLSTM without mixing

A crucial implementation advantage of mLSTM is that, unlike sLSTM, it has no hidden-to-hidden mixing and its matrix recurrence is linear in the previous state with a scalar coefficient. This structure permits a parallel-scan (prefix-sum / parallel reduction) formulation that reduces the sequential dependency depth from \(O(T)\) to \(O(\log T)\) on parallel hardware.

Consider the linear recurrence for a generic vector or matrix \(x_t\):
\[
x_t = A_t\,x_{t-1} + B_t,
\]
where here \(A_t\) is a scalar \(f_t\) and \(B_t\) is either the matrix outer-product \(i_t\mathbf v_t\mathbf k_t^\top\) (for \(\mathbf C_t\)) or the vector \(i_t\mathbf k_t\) (for \(\mathbf n_t\)). The standard associative composition for such recurrences is
\[
(A_2,B_2)\circ(A_1,B_1) \;=\; (A_2A_1,\; A_2B_1 + B_2).
\]
This operator \(\circ\) is associative:
\[
\big((A_3,B_3)\circ(A_2,B_2)\big)\circ(A_1,B_1)
= (A_3A_2A_1,\; A_3A_2B_1 + A_3B_2 + B_3)
= (A_3,B_3)\circ\big((A_2,B_2)\circ(A_1,B_1)\big).
\]
If the initial state \(x_0\) is known (often zero), then the state \(x_t\) after \(t\) steps is the second component of the composed pair
\[
(A_t,B_t)\circ(A_{t-1},B_{t-1})\circ\cdots\circ(A_1,B_1).
\]
Thus computing all prefix composed pairs can be done with any parallel-scan algorithm that reduces the sequence with an associative operator. In practice:

- Build the sequence of pairs \((A_j,B_j)\) where \(A_j=f_j\) and \(B_j=i_j\mathbf v_j\mathbf k_j^\top\) (or \(B_j=i_j\mathbf k_j\) for the normalizer).
- Use a parallel-prefix algorithm (tree reduction) to compute prefix compositions \(\big(\prod_{s=1}^t f_s,\;\sum_{j=1}^t (\prod_{s=j+1}^t f_s)\,B_j\big)\) for all \(t\) in \(O(\log T)\) depth.

Because the \(A_j\) are scalars, the multiplications \(A_2A_1\) and the left actions \(A_2B_1\) are cheap and well-defined; the operator handles matrix-valued \(B_j\) naturally via the left-scaling \(A_2B_1\). The same scan yields \(\mathbf C_t\) and \(\mathbf n_t\) in parallel. After the scan, the retrieval step computes \(\tilde{\mathbf h}_t = \mathbf C_t\mathbf q_t/\max\{|\mathbf n_t^\top\mathbf q_t|,1\}\) pointwise.

In summary, mLSTM's lack of hidden-hidden mixing reduces its recurrence to an associative linear-affine form that is amenable to standard parallel-scan parallelization, enabling efficient batched GPU implementations that avoid sequential for-loops over time.

# Memory capacity intuitions and trade-offs (scalar vs. matrix)

Scalar memories (the traditional LSTM cell or sLSTM scalar cells) and matrix memories (mLSTM) represent different points in the capacity–complexity trade-off.

Scalar / vectorized scalar cells:
- Each cell stores a scalar \(c_t^{(i)}\) (or a vector of independent scalars across dimensions). Information must be compressed into scalar channels. Retrieval typically reads each scalar (optionally after a nonlinear squashing \(\psi\)), and memory mixing via recurrent weight matrices \(\mathbf R\) can combine scalar contributions across dimensions.
- Capacity scales roughly linearly with the number of cells \(d\). In tasks requiring associative recall (store many key–value pairs and retrieve by similarity), scalar cells require the network to encode keys and values in compressed scalar-coded form and rely on recurrent mixing to implement retrieval. This is expressive but can be inefficient and limited when many independent associations are required.

Matrix memory (\(\mathbf C\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\)):
- Stores outer-product information; a single update \(i_t\mathbf v_t\mathbf k_t^\top\) directly records an association between \(\mathbf k_t\) and \(\mathbf v_t\). The total number of degrees of freedom held in \(\mathbf C\) is \(d^2\), which can represent many more independent associations than \(d\) scalar channels.
- Retrieval by multiplying \(\mathbf C_t\) by a query \(\mathbf q\) produces a sum of values weighted by the key–query similarities, making associative recall natural and efficient.
- Computational and memory cost: each time step requires \(O(d^2)\) storage (the state \(\mathbf C_t\)) and \(O(d^2)\) arithmetic to update and to multiply by \(\mathbf q_t\). This is heavier than scalar cells, which cost \(O(d)\) per time step. The paper emphasizes that \(\mathbf C_t\) is stateful but parameter-free (the projections to keys/values/queries use parameter matrices whose sizes are linear in \(d\)); the cost is in state maintenance and matrix arithmetic rather than parameter count.

Trade-offs in practice:
- For problems that demand many independent associative links (e.g., the large associative recall tests in the experiments), matrix memory provides substantially better capacity and recall accuracy because it preserves pairwise information explicitly.
- For problems where state tracking and sequential mixing are essential, scalar cells with memory mixing (sLSTM) remain powerful because recurrent mixing enables complex state machines; however, their per-time-step representational capacity is smaller.
- mLSTM provides a middle ground: large associative capacity with parallelizability (since no hidden mixing), at the expense of \(O(d^2)\) compute and storage per layer. The paper's design uses a mixture of mLSTM and sLSTM blocks (heads/cells) to balance capacity and mixing where needed.

One additional point from the paper: Transformers can have an exponential effective memory capacity in coding dimension because attention can, in principle, assign distinct attention patterns over an exponentially large set of histories when keys are combinatorially encoded. The mLSTM covariance rule is optimal in certain SNR senses for separable binary retrieval, but the practical capacity of \(\mathbf C\) still scales with \(d^2\), which explains why Transformers outperform other methods on some associative tasks when the coding dimension is limited.

# Expressivity and state tracking: theoretical context

The paper emphasizes that memory mixing (hidden–hidden recurrent connections) confers the ability to implement stateful computations that some parallel models (vanilla Transformers or some SSMs) cannot easily emulate. At an intuitive level, state tracking requires the model to carry internal variables whose update depends on past hidden states and whose future influence depends on the order of past inputs. Recurrent mixing naturally implements such ordered, history-dependent transforms, because the hidden-to-hidden mapping can be an arbitrary function of the previous hidden state (modulated by gates).

Two concrete aspects where expressivity matters:

1. Revising storage decisions. With only additive gating constrained inside \([0,1]\) (as in sigmoid-based LSTM gates), the network can attenuate old memory but not amplify contributions beyond the incoming new value in the same multiplicative manner. Exponential gates \(i_t=\exp(\tilde i_t)\) permit write strengths that are unbounded above and thus allow the model to robustly overwrite or amplify new inputs relative to old ones, provided the normalizer handles overall scale. Combined with memory mixing that routes hidden-contextual information into gate controls, the model gains the ability to perform structured revisions (e.g., in nearest-neighbor search, to increase the weight of a newly found better match so it dominates previous matches).

2. State tracking and formal-language style tasks. Tasks such as parity or certain regular languages require maintaining discrete or structured internal state across arbitrarily long sequences. Recurrent mixing can implement the finite-state transition logic directly in recurrent weight matrices and gating. The paper reports that xLSTM variants that include memory mixing solve tasks that Transformers and some SSMs without mixing fail on, consistent with theoretical results that RNNs with recurrence can implement certain stateful computations that feed-forward attention-only mechanisms cannot express as naturally.

In sum, expressivity is not solely a matter of per-step representational capacity (matrix vs scalar memory) but also of the model's ability to route, combine, and revise stored information in a history-dependent manner. xLSTM's design—adding exponential gating and offering both sLSTM (mixing) and mLSTM (high-capacity matrix memory) components—targets both axes of expressivity.

# Cover’s Theorem and block design rationale

Cover’s theorem (as cited in the paper) provides the classical intuition: a nonlinear mapping into a higher-dimensional space tends to increase the probability that patterns become linearly separable. For sequence models the idea is used to justify architecture choices that first summarize or transform the past into a higher-dimensional embedding before producing a final prediction.

xLSTM uses two residual block backbones aligned with this idea:

- Post up-projection (Transformer-like): the block first applies a recurrent module in the original ambient dimension (summarizing the past nonlinearly), then projects ("up-projects") into a higher-dimensional space for a nonlinearity and finally projects back down. This scheme is used for sLSTM-based blocks in the paper because sLSTM's memory mixing operates naturally at the ambient dimensionality, and the up-projection postprocesses and enriches the representation for better separability.

- Pre up-projection (SSM-like): the block first maps inputs linearly into a higher-dimensional space, then applies the recurrent/nonlinear summarization in the high-dimensional space, and finally projects back. This layout is used for mLSTM-based blocks because matrix memory benefits from operating in higher-dimensional key/value/query spaces: the covariance memory \(\mathbf C\) grows as \(d\times d\) with the internal dimension \(d\), so placing the memory in an expanded space increases its associative capacity and the chance that different histories map to distinct directions that can be separated linearly at readout.

From the perspective of Cover’s theorem, both designs exploit dimensional expansion to improve separability: the pre-up-projection increases the raw capacity of the associative store (good for mLSTM), while the post-up-projection increases the expressive power of the ambient recurrent mixing (good for sLSTM). These choices are engineering decisions guided by the different roles of the two memory mechanisms.

# Equivalence of mLSTM heads and cells without mixing

The paper states that for mLSTM multiple heads and multiple cells are equivalent because there is no memory mixing across heads/cells. We make this precise.

Suppose we split the overall internal dimension into \(H\) disjoint heads, with head \(h\) of size \(d_h\), so the full dimension \(d=\sum_{h=1}^H d_h\). For each head we maintain an independent matrix memory \(\mathbf C^{(h)}\in\mathbb R^{d_h\times d_h}\) and independent projections to keys/queries/values that act only within that head. The per-head update and retrieval are
\[
\mathbf C^{(h)}_t = f_t^{(h)}\,\mathbf C^{(h)}_{t-1} + i_t^{(h)}\,\mathbf v_t^{(h)}(\mathbf k_t^{(h)})^\top,\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf h}_t^{(h)} = \mathbf C_t^{(h)} \mathbf q_t^{(h)} / \cdots,
\]
and the final \(\tilde{\mathbf h}_t\) is the concatenation of the per-head \(\tilde{\mathbf h}_t^{(h)}\).

Equivalently, we can combine the head-specific matrices into a single block-diagonal matrix \(\mathbf C_t = \operatorname{blockdiag}(\mathbf C_t^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf C_t^{(H)})\), combine keys/values/queries by concatenation, and use a single update
\[
\mathbf C_t = \tilde f_t\,\mathbf C_{t-1} + \tilde i_t\,\tilde{\mathbf v}_t\tilde{\mathbf k}_t^\top,
\]
where \(\tilde f_t\) and \(\tilde i_t\) represent the concatenated scalar gates (or a block-wise scalar applied per block) and \(\tilde{\mathbf v}_t,\tilde{\mathbf k}_t,\tilde{\mathbf q}_t\) are the concatenated vectors. Because there is no cross-head mixing, the block-diagonal single-matrix update reproduces exactly the independent head updates and retrievals. Thus multiple independent mLSTM heads are algebraically the same as a single mLSTM with a block-diagonal memory and appropriately partitioned projections.

The practical implication is that when no mixing across memory partitions is required, using multiple heads is primarily an implementation convenience (e.g., to parallelize or to control per-head dimension) rather than an architectural necessity: heads and cells are interchangeable under the no-mixing constraint. This equivalence fails when recurrent mixing across heads is introduced, which is exactly the situation where sLSTM-style mixing becomes distinctively more expressive.

# Concluding remarks

The algebraic structure introduced by exponential gating, gate-mass normalization, and matrix covariance updates gives xLSTM both numerical robustness and modularity between high-capacity associative memory (mLSTM) and stateful memory-mixing (sLSTM). The key mathematical facts that support the design are:

- The normalizer \(n_t\) converts the raw accumulated memory \(c_t\) (scalar case) or \(\mathbf C_t\) (matrix case) into an attention-like normalized readout that removes global multiplicative scale and stabilizes gradients.

- Log-space stabilizers \(m_t\) provide exact arithmetic invariance: they permit computing equivalent, numerically safe gate factors \(i'_t,f'_t\) without changing outputs or parameter gradients.

- The covariance outer-product update directly implements a fast-weight / associative memory whose retrieval reduces to a weighted sum of stored values, with coefficients that are similarity-weighted and decay-moderated; this bridges attention, retention, and fast-weight perspectives.

- Because the matrix recurrence is affine with scalar left-scaling, it admits an associative composition operator and therefore a parallel-scan implementation, making mLSTM parallelizable despite being a recurrent-style memory.

- Trade-offs between scalar and matrix memory are explicit and interpretable: scalar memories are cheaper but lower capacity; matrix memories increase capacity at \(O(d^2)\) cost but remain compressive in sequence length.

These derivations and identities explain why the architectural choices described in the paper combine to yield models that are both expressive and practical for large-scale sequence modeling.
