\title{Blogs about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

# xLSTM in Plain English: Can RNNs Make a Comeback?

Transformers have dominated large‑scale language modeling for several years, but the LSTM family—built on the constant‑error carousel and gating—was the original workhorse for sequence tasks. The paper "xLSTM: Extended Long Short‑Term Memory" asks a simple question: if we bring LSTMs up to date with modern modeling and scaling practices and fix their known weak points, how far can they go? The short answer from the authors: surprisingly far. xLSTM extends LSTMs with two targeted changes (exponential gating and richer memory structures), integrates those into residual blocks, and reports strong empirical results — linear‑time, constant‑memory inference and competitive or better perplexities vs. Transformers and State Space Models (SSMs) on large language modeling tasks.

Below I unpack the two core ideas and the practical consequences in accessible terms, with the minimal math needed to make the mechanisms clear.

## Why revisit LSTMs now?

LSTMs still shine in many sequence problems (and they were the kernel of early large language models), but they have three persistent limitations that helped usher in Transformers:

- Inability to reliably revise previous storage decisions. Once an LSTM has stored something in its scalar cell(s), it can be hard to replace that content cleanly when better information arrives later.
- Limited storage capacity. The classical LSTM cell compresses stored information into scalars (or small vectors), which hurts memorization of many distinct items (e.g., rare tokens or many associative key–value pairs).
- Poor parallelizability. Recurrent hidden–to–hidden mixing forces sequential updates that are harder to parallelize than attention or many SSM formulations.

xLSTM targets these points directly.

## A one‑line LSTM refresher

The LSTM's core idea is an additive memory update (the "constant error carousel") plus gates that control writing, forgetting and reading. In compact form:

- Memory update: $c_t = f_t\, c_{t-1} + i_t\, z_t$
- Output: $h_t = o_t\, \psi(c_t)$

Here $f_t,i_t,o_t$ are forget/input/output gates, $z_t$ is the candidate input, and $\psi$ is a squashing/normalization function (often $\tanh$). Sigmoid gates limit values to $(0,1)$, which is sometimes a practical constraint.

## Core idea 1 — exponential gating: let the model revise what it stored

xLSTM replaces some sigmoid gates with exponential gates and adds normalization/stabilization machinery so the network can safely use unbounded multiplicative updates. The most important consequences are:

- Exponential input gates give the model a flexible, effectively unbounded "learning rate" for new content; that makes it possible to strongly replace previously stored information when appropriate.
- A normalizer state keeps track of the aggregated gate strength so the readout stays well‑scaled.
- A small stabilization trick prevents numerical overflow while keeping gradients and outputs invariant.

The sLSTM (scalar variant) forward pass is written in the paper as:

- cell state: $c_t = f_t\, c_{t-1} + i_t\, z_t$
- normalizer: $n_t = f_t\, n_{t-1} + i_t$
- readout: $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$, and $h_t = o_t\, \tilde{h}_t$
- gates: $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ and $f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)$ or $f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)$ (input/forget pre‑activations are affine functions of input and prior hidden state)

Because exponentials can grow large, the authors add a stabilizer state $m_t$ and normalized gates

- $m_t = \max\!\big(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1},\ \log(i_t)\big)$
- $i'_t = \exp(\log(i_t) - m_t) = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t)$
- $f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t)$

Replacing $i_t,f_t$ by $i'_t,f'_t$ does not change network outputs or gradients but keeps intermediate numbers in a safe range.

Intuition: exponential gating turns gate outputs into multiplicative scaling factors that are not confined to $(0,1)$. The normalizer $n_t$ records the total "mass" of what has been written (weighted by future forgets), and dividing by $n_t$ produces a stable, scale‑normalized readout. That combination allows the network to sharply revise stored content when needed — something vanilla LSTMs struggle with.

## Core idea 2 — matrix memory (mLSTM): increase capacity with associative storage

To address limited storage capacity, xLSTM introduces a matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ that accumulates outer products of values and keys (a covariance or fast‑weight style update). At a high level this stores key–value associations in a way that retrieval is a linear matrix multiply by a query vector.

The mLSTM update and readout (from the paper) are:

- matrix state: $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t\, \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\, \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$
- normalizer: $\mathbf{n}_t = f_t\, \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t\, \mathbf{k}_t$
- retrieval: $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t \ /\ \max\{\,|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,\ 1\,\}$
- output: $\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t$

Here $\mathbf{k}_t$ and $\mathbf{v}_t$ are key and value vectors derived from the input, $\mathbf{q}_t$ is the query, $f_t$ and $i_t$ are forget/input gates (again $i_t$ can be exponential), and the denominator uses the dot product of the normalizer with the query to normalize retrieval strength (clamped to at least 1).

Why this helps: the covariance update $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ is the classic associative memory / fast‑weight mechanism. The forget gate behaves like a decay on old associations and the input (exponential) gate behaves like a learn rate for new associations. Because the mLSTM drops hidden–to–hidden mixing across time (no recurrent mixing inside the matrix memory), its recurrence can be reformulated in a parallel, GPU‑friendly way — unlike sLSTM variants that use hidden mixing.

In short: mLSTM gives an LSTM‑style model a higher‑capacity, attention‑like memory that is independent of sequence length.

## sLSTM vs mLSTM — quick comparison

- sLSTM
  - Scalar memory cell with exponential gating, normalizer and stabilizer.
  - Supports memory mixing via recurrent hidden‑to‑hidden weights (so it can do state tracking).
  - Not parallelizable across time because of the hidden mixing, but the authors implemented a fast CUDA kernel.

- mLSTM
  - Matrix memory that stores key×value outer products (high capacity).
  - No memory mixing; recurrence can be reformulated to run in parallel (good for GPUs).
  - Higher computational cost per layer due to $d\times d$ matrix operations, but memory usage is constant in sequence length.

The xLSTM architecture mixes these building blocks inside residual blocks: mLSTM blocks are typically used with a pre up‑projection backbone (bigger internal dimensionality), while sLSTM blocks use a post up‑projection backbone. The paper denotes mixed stacks as xLSTM[$a$:$b$] meaning the ratio of mLSTM to sLSTM blocks (e.g., xLSTM[7:1]).

## Complexity and practical tradeoffs

- Sequence complexity: xLSTM networks are linear in sequence length and use constant model memory with respect to context length, unlike Transformers whose attention is quadratic in context length.
- Computation: mLSTM incurs $d\times d$ matrix updates per step (higher FLOPs), but these operations are parameter‑free and parallelizable on modern hardware. sLSTM uses hidden mixing which prevents full parallelization, but the authors report an efficient CUDA kernel that is typically less than 2× slower than their parallel mLSTM implementation.
- Engineering: the current mLSTM CUDA kernels are not yet as optimized as FlashAttention; the authors note room for implementation speedups.

## What the experiments show (high level)

The paper evaluates xLSTM across synthetic tasks, controlled memory tests, and large‑scale language modeling.

- Synthetic and formal tasks: exponential gating plus memory mixing lets sLSTM variants solve state‑tracking/formal grammar problems that Transformers and many SSMs (which lack memory mixing) cannot. This supports the claim that mixing + gating improves expressivity for certain algorithmic tasks.
- Associative recall: mLSTM's matrix memory substantially improves associative recall and memorization of many key–value pairs. In a multi‑query associative recall setup (up to 256 key–value pairs and contexts up to 2048) xLSTM[1:1] performed best among non‑Transformer models, and matrix memory helped extrapolate to longer contexts.
- Long Range Arena: xLSTM shows consistent strong performance across tasks designed to test long context handling.
- Language modeling at scale:
  - On 15B tokens of SlimPajama, xLSTM models (mixed mLSTM/sLSTM stacks) achieved lower validation perplexities than a set of baselines including Transformers (GPT‑3 style, Llama), SSMs (H3, Mamba), RNNs (RWKV variants), and several linear/efficient transformer variants.
  - Ablation studies attribute the gains to both exponential gating and the matrix memory.
  - On a larger 300B‑token training run, xLSTM models extrapolated to longer contexts better than the compared methods (tested up to length 16,384) and achieved strong validation perplexities and downstream task performance across multiple model sizes (125M up to 1.3B).
  - On the PALOMA benchmark (571 text domains), xLSTM[1:0] had lower perplexity than Mamba on 568/571 domains (99.5%), lower than Llama on 486/571 (85.1%), and lower than RWKV‑4 on 570/571 (99.8%).
- Inference speed and throughput: because xLSTM’s memory state is compressive and constant w.r.t. context length, generation time scales linearly with output length and xLSTM achieves higher decoding throughput (via larger batch sizes) than a Transformer like Llama, which faces quadratic memory growth in context length.

All empirical claims above are reported in the paper; precise curves and tables live in the figures and appendices of the original work.

## Limitations — what still needs work

The authors are careful to call out several important limitations and engineering needs:

- sLSTM’s memory mixing prevents full parallelization; despite a fast CUDA kernel, it is currently not as fast as a fully parallel model.
- mLSTM kernels are not yet as optimized as state‑of‑the‑art attention kernels; current implementations are about 4× slower than FlashAttention in the authors' experiments.
- The matrix memory in mLSTM incurs $d\times d$ computation, which is heavier than scalar updates.
- Forget‑gate initialization needs careful tuning.
- The matrix memory size is independent of sequence length; extremely long contexts could overload a fixed‑size matrix memory in principle, though experiments up to 16k contexts did not reveal practical problems.
- Large‑scale runs in the paper were expensive and the authors note they did not fully optimize architectures or hyperparameters for the largest xLSTM variants — there is likely additional upside with more engineering and tuning.

## Bottom line

xLSTM is a pragmatic modernization of LSTM ideas. By introducing exponential gating (with normalization and stabilization) and a matrix associative memory, and by integrating these components into modern residual stacks, the authors demonstrate that LSTM‑style recurrent models can be scaled and adapted to match or beat competitive Transformer and SSM baselines on language modeling benchmarks. Important tradeoffs remain (computation vs. memory, parallelizability), and there is engineering work to be done, but xLSTM shows that RNNs are not out of the running — with the right design choices they can still compete in the era of large LMs.
