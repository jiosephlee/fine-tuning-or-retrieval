\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Pre vs. post up-projection: why pre for mLSTM and post for sLSTM?
Question:
The xLSTM block uses post up-projection for sLSTM but pre up-projection for mLSTM, citing capacity and Cover's theorem.
- Could you provide a concise rationale or ablation: why does mLSTM benefit more from summarizing in a higher-dimensional space (pre), whereas sLSTM prefers to summarize in the original space (post)?
- Are there tasks or scales where the opposite configuration works better?
Answer:
Short answer: the choice follows the memory geometry and computational trade‑offs of the two LSTM variants. mLSTM stores a matrix (outer‑product / covariance) memory so you get much more storage and separability by projecting into a larger embedding space before you form keys/values (pre up‑projection). sLSTM relies on scalar (or per‑cell) additive memory plus recurrent memory‑mixing, so you keep the recurrent/gating dynamics compact and only expand after the recurrence (post up‑projection). The paper’s language about Cover’s theorem and “memory capacity becomes larger in the high‑dimensional space” is exactly this point.

Why this is true, intuitively and technically. mLSTM’s update is
$$
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\,\mathbf{k}_t^\top,
$$
so the memory is a $\,d\times d\,$ matrix whose number of “degrees of freedom” (and hence raw associative capacity) grows like $O(d^2)$. If you first up‑project inputs into a bigger dimension $D$ and then form $\mathbf{k},\mathbf{v}$ there, $\mathbf{C}$ lives in $\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}$ and you get a much larger associative store and better linear separability of different histories (Cover’s theorem): keys/values that are produced in higher dimension are easier to separate and produce higher signal/noise under the covariance update and retrieval $\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t$. Because mLSTM purposely abandons hidden–hidden recurrence (it is parallelizable), there is no heavy recurrent matrix to blow up if you work at higher dimension first, so the compute/parameter cost remains concentrated in the feedforward up‑projection — a good match for pre up‑projection.

By contrast sLSTM’s core cell is (scalar per cell)
$$
c_t \;=\; f_t\,c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t\,z_t,
$$
and when you have multiple sLSTM cells the model relies on recurrent memory‑mixing via matrices $\mathbf{R}_{\cdot}$ from the hidden state into gates/inputs. Projecting inputs to a very large dimension before the recurrence would inflate those recurrent matrices (and the cost and instability of recurrent mixing) without yielding the same multiplicative capacity win that a matrix memory gives you. In other words, sLSTM’s useful capacity comes from gated recurrence and structured mixing rather than from a quadratic outer‑product store, so it is more efficient and stable to summarize in the original (compact) space and only then up‑project for the residual/nonlinear transformations (post up‑projection). This also aligns with engineering constraints in the paper: sLSTM is not parallelizable and benefits from keeping its recurrence small; mLSTM is parallelizable and designed to exploit a higher‑dimensional associative store.

About ablations and whether the opposite can ever win. The paper’s ablations emphasize that (i) the matrix memory and (ii) exponential gating are the main contributors to the performance gains, and the experimental default throughout the paper is pre‑up for mLSTM and post‑up for sLSTM. The authors do not report a systematic swap (i.e. pre vs post swapped for each cell type) as a primary ablation in the main text, so there is no direct empirical curve in the paper that says “swap helps on X.” Conceptually, however, one can imagine regimes where the swap might be useful: if you have an sLSTM with many independent heads and can afford very large recurrent matrices (and you care more about separability than about keeping recurrence cheap), pre‑projection could increase separability before gating; conversely, if an mLSTM’s internal dimension is already extremely large or you are compute‑constrained, a post‑projection design might be more efficient and not lose much in practice. Those are trade‑offs of compute, stability, and the kind of memory you need (associative capacity vs. stateful mixing). The paper’s empirical takeaway is that the chosen defaults (pre for mLSTM, post for sLSTM) are practical and robust across the synthetic associative‑recall tasks and the language‑modeling scale experiments they ran.

Practical rule of thumb from the paper: if your cell stores associations via outer products (matrix memory) and you want large retrieval capacity, up‑project first; if your cell’s strength is gated recurrent/stateful mixing, keep the recurrence compact and up‑project after summarizing.
