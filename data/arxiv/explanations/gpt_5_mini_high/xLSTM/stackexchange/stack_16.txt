\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Why is the sLSTM output nonlinearity \psi removed and replaced by c_t / n_t?
Question:
Classic LSTM uses $h_t = o_t \psi(c_t)$ with a squashing nonlinearity like $\tanh$. In sLSTM the hidden is $h_t = o_t (c_t / n_t)$.
- Why was $\psi$ omitted here? Is the normalization by $n_t$ sufficient to keep $h_t$ in a reasonable range?
- Does removing $\tanh$ improve gradient flow or expressivity, and have you compared against a variant that uses both normalization and a bounded $\psi$?
Answer:
Short answer: $\psi$ (the usual $\tanh$ squash on the cell) was dropped because the new normalizer state $n_t$ already performs the role of scale control: $\tilde{h}_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}$ is a positive-weighted running average of past inputs, so if the inputs $z_t$ are bounded the ratio stays in a reasonable range. Removing the extra $\tanh$ avoids an additional saturating nonlinearity (which hurts gradient flow and can compress stored signals), and the paper stabilizes exponential gates so the normalization is numerically safe. The paper’s ablations attribute the performance gains to the exponential gating + new memory structure (the sLSTM formulation that omits $\psi$ is part of that), but the authors do not report a dedicated experiment that keeps both the normalization and a bounded $\psi$ on the output.

Why the division by $n_t$ replaces $\psi$ (intuitive + algebra). In sLSTM the recurrences are
$$
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t,\qquad
n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t,\qquad
\tilde{h}_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}.
$$
Unrolling these shows
$$
c_t=\sum_{s\le t}\Big(\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u\Big) i_s z_s,\qquad
n_t=\sum_{s\le t}\Big(\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u\Big) i_s,
$$
so
$$
\tilde{h}_t=\frac{c_t}{n_t}=\sum_{s\le t}\alpha_s z_s,\qquad
\alpha_s\ge0,\ \sum_s\alpha_s=1.
$$
Thus $\tilde{h}_t$ is a convex (weighted) average of past cell inputs $z_s$. If $z_s$ is bounded (in practice $\varphi$ often remains a bounded nonlinearity like $\tanh$ on the input path), then $\tilde{h}_t$ is automatically bounded without needing another squashing $\psi$. This is exactly the role the normalizer state $n_t$ plays: it records the total effective gate mass so the quotient cancels out runaway scaling from the (exponential) gates.

Why this helps gradients and retrieval. A squashing $\psi$ (e.g. $\tanh$) can saturate and kill gradients coming back through the output path; by using the linear normalized ratio the derivative from $h_t$ back into $c_t$ behaves like a scaled linear factor (roughly $o_t / n_t$, apart from the dependence of $n_t$ on gates), so you avoid the near-zero derivatives induced by a saturated $\tanh$. At the same time the normalized linear readout preserves relative magnitudes of stored signals, which is important when you want to retrieve or revise stored values precisely (the paper motivates this as important for tasks that require revising storage decisions). The paper also stabilizes exponential gates via the log–max trick (the $m_t$ stabilizer) so large exponentials do not overflow and dividing by $n_t$ remains numerically well-behaved; for mLSTM an analogous denominator clamp (divide by $\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|,1\}$) prevents tiny/zero denominators.

What the paper tested. The ablations reported in the paper show that the major improvements come from exponential gating and the new memory structures (scalar normalizer for sLSTM and matrix/covariance memory for mLSTM). The authors do not present a direct head-to-head of the specific variant $h_t = o_t\cdot\psi(c_t/n_t)$ versus $h_t = o_t\cdot(c_t/n_t)$. Because $\psi$ would reintroduce a saturating nonlinearity on top of the normalized readout, one should expect it to reduce gradient flow and (potentially) retrieval fidelity, but the definite effect would need to be measured empirically.

If you want to experiment: try two simple ablations (1) add a $\psi$ on the normalized readout, $h_t=o_t\psi(c_t/n_t)$, and (2) keep $\psi$ on $c_t$ but without dividing, $h_t=o_t\psi(c_t)$. Monitor validation loss, retrieval accuracy on synthetic tasks, and gradient norms through the memory to see which effect dominates for your task. The paper’s theory and experiments explain why the pure normalized readout is a principled choice for better retrieval and gradient behavior.
