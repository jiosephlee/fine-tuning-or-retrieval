\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Why do exponential gates give better "revision" ability than sigmoid gates in sLSTM?
Question:
The paper argues that exponential gating allows sLSTM to revise stored information (e.g., in the nearest-neighbor search toy task) more effectively than classic sigmoid gates. In the sLSTM we have $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$ with $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ and $f_t$ either sigmoid or exponential, plus normalization by $n_t$.
I'm struggling to see the specific mechanism by which exponentials help revision relative to sigmoids. Even with sigmoids, if a later $i_t$ is high and the product of future $f_u$ terms is moderate, later inputs should be able to dominate. Is the key that exponentials allow a much larger dynamic range of effective weights $i_s \prod_{u>s} f_u$ so a later input can more decisively overwrite earlier content? Or is the normalization by $n_t$ the main factor?
Could someone provide a simple two-step example showing why a later, more relevant $z_t$ can "replace" an earlier one more reliably with exponential gates than with sigmoids?
Answer:
Short version: exponential gates turn the time‑weights that determine how much each past $z_s$ contributes into softmax‑style logits, so a later input can win by having a much larger logit instead of forcing earlier contributions to be driven to zero by tiny forgets. The $n_t$ normalizer is important (it makes $h_t=c_t/n_t$ a normalized mixture of past $z_s$) but it is the exponential nonlinearity that gives the much larger dynamic range and the additive log‑structure that makes decisive “replacement” easy.

Why (algebra + intuition). Expand the sLSTM recurrence once and you get, for two steps with $c_0=n_0=0$,
$$
c_1=i_1 z_1,\qquad n_1=i_1,
$$
$$
c_2=f_2 c_1 + i_2 z_2 = f_2 i_1 z_1 + i_2 z_2,
\qquad
n_2=f_2 n_1 + i_2 = f_2 i_1 + i_2.
$$
So the post‑normalized memory (what the layer actually uses) is
$$
\tilde h_2=\frac{c_2}{n_2} = \frac{f_2 i_1}{f_2 i_1 + i_2} z_1 \;+\; \frac{i_2}{f_2 i_1 + i_2} z_2
      = w_1 z_1 + w_2 z_2,
$$
with effective weights $w_1,w_2$ that sum to $1$. Thus “replacement” of $z_1$ by $z_2$ is exactly the condition $w_2\approx 1$, i.e. $i_2 \gg f_2 i_1$.

Now compare two gate choices.

1) Exponential gates. Take $i_s=\exp(a_s)$ and $f_u=\exp(b_u)$. Then the unnormalized contribution of $z_s$ is
$$
\exp\!\Big(a_s + \sum_{u>s} b_u\Big),
$$
so the normalized weights are exactly a softmax over the additive logits $a_s+\sum_{u>s} b_u$. That representation has an unbounded positive scale: by making $a_2$ large you can make $w_2$ arbitrarily close to $1$ without touching $f_2$ or $i_1$. In other words, you can make the later input dominate by increasing its own logit.

2) Sigmoid gates. With $i_s=\sigma(\alpha_s)\in(0,1)$ and $f_u=\sigma(\phi_u)\in(0,1)$ the same algebra holds, but now the logs $\log i_s$ and $\log f_u$ are bounded above by $0$ (because $\sigma(\cdot)\le 1$). To make $w_2\to1$ you must make $f_2 i_1$ very small (e.g. by pushing $f_2$ or $i_1$ toward $0$) or make $i_2$ approach $1$. Pushing $f_2$ very small has the side effect of erasing earlier content (and can hurt gradients / other uses of that memory), and $i_2$ can only approach $1$ (logarithmically capped), so it cannot by itself create arbitrarily large dominance the way an exponential $i_2=\exp(a_2)$ can.

A concrete numeric two‑step illustration. Let $z_1\neq z_2$ and compare plausible gate values.

- Exponential gates: set $a_1=0\Rightarrow i_1=1$, $b_2=0\Rightarrow f_2=1$, and $a_2=5\Rightarrow i_2\approx 148.4$. Then
  $$
  w_2 = \frac{i_2}{f_2 i_1 + i_2} \approx \frac{148.4}{1+148.4}\approx 0.993,
  $$
  so $z_2$ overwhelmingly replaces $z_1$ while $f_2$ stayed at $1$ (we did not have to “erase” the old content via a tiny forget).

- Sigmoid gates: set $\alpha_1=0\Rightarrow i_1=0.5$, $\phi_2=0\Rightarrow f_2=0.5$. Even if $\alpha_2=5\Rightarrow i_2\approx0.993$, we get
  $$
  w_2=\frac{0.993}{0.993 + 0.5\cdot 0.5} \approx \frac{0.993}{1.243}\approx 0.799,
  $$
  so $z_2$ cannot dominate as strongly unless we also push $f_2$ (or $i_1$) much smaller. Achieving the same 0.99 dominance requires making $f_2 i_1\approx 0.01$, i.e. forcing strong forgetting explicitly.

Role of normalization and stabilization. The normalizer $n_t$ converts the raw, exponentialized contributions into a convex mixture (so we get interpretable “attention” weights and stable outputs). That normalization is necessary for the softmax interpretation and for preventing unbounded hidden values. The paper’s stabilizer $m_t$ is required to avoid numerical overflow when using exponentials, but it does not change the functional form (it merely implements the stable normalized exponent math used in practice).

In short: normalization makes the effect interpretable (a weighted average), but the key enabler of powerful revision is the exponential mapping which gives additive logits and an unbounded positive scale so a late, highly relevant $z_t$ can be made to dominate without having to annihilate earlier contributions via tiny forgets. This is why sLSTM with exponential gating can revise stored information much more decisively than the classic sigmoid gates.
