\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Why is the mLSTM denominator clamped: h_t = o_t (C_t q_t) / max(|n_t^T q_t|, 1)?
Question:
In mLSTM, the hidden state is $h_t = o_t \cdot (C_t q_t) / \max(|n_t^T q_t|, 1)$, where $n_t$ is the normalizer state updated by $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t k_t$.
- What is the theoretical motivation for clamping the denominator by 1 instead of adding a small $\epsilon$ as is common in normalization schemes?
- Does this introduce a bias in the scale of the retrieved vector when $|n_t^T q_t| < 1$? If so, why is that preferable to an $\epsilon$ stabilization?
- How sensitive is performance to this threshold, and does it depend on the dimensionality $d$ or the norm statistics of $q_t$ and $k_t$?
Answer:
Short answer: the clamp is a deliberate, scale-aware safety / confidence rule that prevents the mLSTM from amplifying noisy or cancelled match signals. Using $\max(|n_t^T q_t|, 1)$ (with the absolute value) keeps retrievals numerically stable and makes the model treat weak total match mass as low-confidence (shrink the output) instead of blindly normalizing by a tiny number. Adding a tiny $\epsilon$ would not accomplish the same robustness and would be very sensitive to the numerical/scale regime.

Why, mathematically and intuitively. Write
$$
\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t \;=\; \sum_{s\le t} \alpha_s \, \mathbf{v}_s \,(\mathbf{k}_s^\top \mathbf{q}_t),
\qquad
D := \mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t \;=\; \sum_{s\le t} \alpha_s \,(\mathbf{k}_s^\top \mathbf{q}_t),
$$
where the $\alpha_s$ encode the gated decay/input factors. With the unclamped normalization the retrieved vector is
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{D}
\;=\; \sum_s \underbrace{\frac{\alpha_s (\mathbf{k}_s^\top \mathbf{q}_t)}{D}}_{w_s}\mathbf{v}_s,
$$
so for nonzero $D$ this is a weighted average of the stored values $\mathbf{v}_s$ (weights sum to 1). The clamp replaces the exact denominator by $\max(|D|,1)$. Two regimes follow:

- If $|D|\ge 1$ then nothing changes: $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t=(\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t)/D$ — a normalized, confidence-weighted average.
- If $|D|<1$ then $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t=(\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t)/1=\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t = D\cdot(\text{the same weighted average})$. So the clamped output is scaled down by factor $D<1$ relative to the normalized retrieval.

So yes, the clamp does introduce a bias (a shrinkage) whenever the total match mass $|D|$ is less than the threshold; the retrieved direction is the same as the normalized one but its magnitude is reduced by $D$. That bias is intentional: a small $|D|$ signals low confidence (weak matching mass, or cancellation between positive and negative similarities), and shrinking the output is preferable to forcing a full-strength retrieved vector that may be driven by noise or by numerical cancellation in $D$.

Why this is preferable to simply adding $\epsilon$. Adding an $\epsilon$ in a denominator is commonly used to avoid division-by-zero, but it does not express a confidence threshold: dividing by $D+\epsilon$ still normalizes even if $D$ is tiny, and with a tiny $\epsilon$ you get very large, unstable outputs and gradients whenever $D$ is tiny. To avoid that you would have to choose $\epsilon$ on the same scale as typical $|D|$, which makes $\epsilon$ a brittle hyperparameter tied to the data/model scale. The hard clamp to 1 instead implements a clear rule: only normalize when the match-strength is at least order-1; otherwise suppress the retrieval. In this paper that is sensible because the authors deliberately scale keys by $1/\sqrt{d}$ and use layer-norm on inputs, so typical dot-product magnitudes are $O(1)$. Using 1 as the lower bound therefore aligns with their chosen parameterization and with prior work they cite.

About the absolute value and sign: $\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t$ can be near zero because of cancellation between positive and negative contributions (keys are zero-mean). Using $|D|$ avoids sign flips in normalization (dividing by a negative number would flip the retrieved vector's sign), and clamping $|D|$ prevents pathological amplification caused by cancellation.

Sensitivity and dependence on $d$ / norms. The threshold’s effect depends on the expected scale of $D$. Because the paper sets $\mathbf{k}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\mathbf{W}_k\mathbf{x}_t + b_k$ and applies layer-norm, the typical scale of $\mathbf{k}^\top\mathbf{q}$ is roughly constant with $d$, so 1 is a natural, fairly robust choice across model sizes used in the paper. If you change the key/query scaling or remove layer-norm, the expected magnitude of $D$ will change and you should retune the threshold (or equivalently rescale keys by $1/\sqrt{d}$ so that threshold $1$ stays sensible). In practice the behaviour is qualitative: making the threshold much smaller (approaching numerical $\epsilon$) recovers the instabilities the clamp prevents; making it much larger forces the model to suppress many genuine, moderate-strength retrievals and will hurt recall/performance. Empirically the paper follows prior work (they cite Sun et al.) and uses threshold $1.0$; they also stabilize exponential gates separately (the $m_t$ stabilizer) so the whole system stays in a predictable scale regime.

Practical takeaway: the clamp is a scale-aware safety/confidence mechanism (not just a tiny numeric eps). It trades a small, controlled bias (suppressed output when match strength is weak) for much more stable training and inference. With the standard key scaling $1/\sqrt{d}$ and layer-norm used in the paper, threshold $1$ is a natural, robust choice; if you alter key/query parameterization you should retune the threshold or keep the key scaling so the same threshold remains meaningful.
