\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### What exactly does the sLSTM normalizer state do, and why divide by n_t?
Question:
In the sLSTM, the forward equations are given as
- cell state: $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$
- normalizer: $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t$
- hidden: $h_t = o_t \cdot (c_t / n_t)$.
I understand this is intended to enable revision via exponential gates, but I'm confused about the precise role of $n_t$.
1) Is $c_t / n_t$ literally a weighted average of past $z_s$ with weights proportional to $i_s \prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u$? Could you show the explicit unrolled form that makes the averaging interpretation clear?
2) Under exponential gating, $i_t>0$ and (optionally) $f_t>0$ and possibly $f_t>1$. How is $n_t$ guaranteed to be nonzero and positive so that division is well-defined? What conditions are required?
3) Conceptually, how does dividing by $n_t$ differ from, say, applying a squashing nonlinearity like $\tanh$ on $c_t$ as in classic LSTM? What stability advantages does the normalization provide for gradient flow compared to $\tanh$?
Answer:
Short answer: the normalizer $n_t$ is exactly the accumulated “retained mass’’ of past writes; it records how much of past inputs survives to time $t$. Dividing $c_t$ by $n_t$ turns the cell readout into an attention‑style weighted average of past inputs (weights = input strength $\times$ future retention). This keeps the readout scale-stable when you allow exponential (possibly $>1$) gates and, importantly, removes the $\tanh$ saturation factor from the main path of gradient flow, which substantially improves stability for learning and for “revision’’ via multiplicative gates.

1) Unrolled form / averaging interpretation. Start from
$$
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t,\qquad
n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t,
$$
and take $c_0,n_0$ arbitrary (often $=0$). Unrolling by induction gives, with the convention $\prod_{u=t+1}^t f_u=1$,
$$
c_t \;=\; \Big(\prod_{u=1}^t f_u\Big)c_0 \;+\; \sum_{s=1}^t \Big(i_s\,z_s\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u\Big),
$$
$$
n_t \;=\; \Big(\prod_{u=1}^t f_u\Big)n_0 \;+\; \sum_{s=1}^t \Big(i_s\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u\Big).
$$
Define the weights
$$
w_{s,t} := i_s\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u .
$$
Then
$$
\frac{c_t}{n_t}
=\frac{\Big(\prod_{u=1}^t f_u\Big)c_0 + \sum_{s=1}^t w_{s,t} z_s}
{\Big(\prod_{u=1}^t f_u\Big)n_0 + \sum_{s=1}^t w_{s,t}}.
$$
In the common initialization $c_0=n_0=0$ this reduces to
$$
\frac{c_t}{n_t} \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^t \frac{w_{s,t}}{\sum_{r=1}^t w_{r,t}}\, z_s,
$$
so $c_t/n_t$ is literally the weighted average of past $z_s$ with weights proportional to $w_{s,t}=i_s\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u$. Intuitively $i_s$ is how strongly you wrote $z_s$ at time $s$, and the product of future $f_u$ says how much of that write is retained until time $t$.

2) Why $n_t>0$ (division well-defined) and numerical safety. In the paper sLSTM sets $i_t=\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ (strictly $>0$) and uses either $f_t=\sigma(\tilde f_t)\in(0,1)$ or $f_t=\exp(\tilde f_t)>0$. If every $i_t>0$ and every $f_t>0$ (and $n_0\ge0$), then every term $w_{s,t}=i_s\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u$ is strictly positive, hence $n_t$ (a sum of positive terms plus a nonnegative initial term) is strictly positive for every $t$. Thus, under the designed exponential/sigmoid gates, division is mathematically safe. If one used gates that could be exactly zero then $n_t$ might be zero in degenerate cases, but the paper’s gate choices avoid that.

Numerically, exponentials can overflow/underflow; the paper introduces the stabilizer state $m_t$ and replaces $i_t,f_t$ by scaled versions $i'_t,f'_t$ (Equations (5)–(7)) which preserve the effective ratios while keeping magnitudes in range. For the vector/matrix mLSTM the paper additionally lower-bounds the retrieval denominator $\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,1\}$ to avoid tiny divisions. In practice it is common to also add a tiny epsilon to scalar denominators for numerical robustness — the paper’s stabilizer + the mLSTM threshold are the principled mechanisms they describe.

3) Conceptual and gradient-flow differences vs. $\tanh$.

Conceptually, $\psi=\tanh$ squashes the raw cell $c_t$ elementwise and introduces a saturating nonlinearity: for large $|c_t|$ the slope $\psi'(c_t)=1-\tanh^2(c_t)$ becomes tiny, which kills gradients. By contrast, dividing by $n_t$ performs normalization (an attention‑style weighted average) rather than saturation. The readout is a relative (scale‑normalized) mixture of past inputs instead of a nonlinear squashed scalar of the accumulated sum.

This difference shows up sharply in the backpropagation factors. For sLSTM with $h_t=o_t(c_t/n_t)$ one finds
$$
\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial c_{t-1}} \;=\; o_t\cdot\frac{\partial (c_t/n_t)}{\partial c_{t-1}}
\;=\; o_t\cdot\frac{f_t}{n_t}.
$$
For a classic LSTM with $h_t=o_t\psi(c_t)$ and $\psi=\tanh$,
$$
\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial c_{t-1}} \;=\; o_t\cdot\psi'(c_t)\cdot f_t
\;=\; o_t\cdot\big(1-\tanh^2(c_t)\big)\cdot f_t,
$$
and $\psi'(c_t)$ can be arbitrarily small when $c_t$ is large. Thus sLSTM replaces the potentially vanishing factor $\psi'(c_t)$ by $1/n_t$. Because $n_t$ is tracking the same gate-mass that created $c_t$, gradients are controlled by gate mass rather than the absolute, possibly-saturated magnitude of $c_t$. Moreover the derivative of $h_t$ with respect to a past write $z_s$ is simple and interpretable:
$$
\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial z_s} \;=\; o_t\cdot\frac{w_{s,t}}{n_t}
\;=\; o_t\cdot \text{(normalized weight of }z_s\text{)},
$$
so gradients flow back to past writes in proportion to their normalized contribution. There is no extra small factor from a squashing nonlinearity, which helps keep useful signal for credit assignment across long ranges.

Intuitively, dividing by $n_t$ decouples “how much total mass is stored’’ from “what the stored content is’’: gates can grow or shrink multiplicatively (allowing strong revision behavior) but the readout stays normalized and bounded. This preserves a form of the constant-error-carousel (additive memory update) while avoiding the gradient-killing saturation of $\tanh$. The stabilization trick in the paper makes this practical even when gates are exponential or when $f_t>1$ is used for revision dynamics.

Taken together: $n_t$ is the bookkeeping variable that makes the sLSTM readout a normalized, attention‑like average of past inputs; its positivity is ensured by the paper’s gate choices and numerical stabilizers; and normalization replaces a saturating squash with a scale‑aware, non‑saturating readout that gives more robust gradients for long‑range credit assignment and for the revision behavior enabled by exponential gating.
