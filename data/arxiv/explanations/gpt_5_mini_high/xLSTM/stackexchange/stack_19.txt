\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Is C_t actually a covariance estimator and what assumptions are needed?
Question:
You motivate $C_t \leftarrow C_{t-1} + v_t k_t^T$ as a covariance update rule, assuming layer norm yields zero-mean $k_t$ (and maybe $v_t$).
- True covariance requires centering and also scaling by sample count. Here, you do not divide by count and you also include gating and decay in mLSTM. In what precise sense is $C_t$ a covariance (or proportional to one)?
- Do you rely on approximate whitening from layer norm for the "optimality" claims (maximizing SNR for binary retrieval), or does the argument hold more generally?
Answer:
Short answer: $C_t$ in the paper is not the classical (mean‑centered, $1/N$-scaled) sample covariance; it is an unnormalized, gated outer‑product accumulator — i.e. a weighted second‑moment / cross‑covariance accumulator. Under the paper’s explicit assumption that keys and values are mean‑zero (layer‑norm before the projections) and under the usual associative‑memory assumptions (patterns approximately uncorrelated/isotropic or gates independent of content), $C_t$ is proportional to the (weighted) cross‑covariance matrix. The forget/input gates simply turn the plain outer‑product sum into a content‑weighted (typically exponentially decayed) sum; the paper’s “optimality” claim (maximizing SNR for binary retrieval) is the classical result for the covariance (Hebbian / outer‑product) update under those standard assumptions, and it becomes only approximate when those assumptions are violated.

A few formulae and intuition to make this precise. The mLSTM update is
$$
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} \;+\; i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top .
$$
Unfolding the recurrence gives
$$
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; \sum_{s\le t} w_{s,t}\,\mathbf{v}_s\mathbf{k}_s^\top,\qquad
w_{s,t}\equiv \Big(\prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u\Big)\,i_s,
$$
so $C_t$ is literally a weighted sum of outer products. The normalizer state in the paper is
$$
\mathbf{n}_t \;=\; \sum_{s\le t} w_{s,t}\,\mathbf{k}_s,
$$
and retrieval uses
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t \;=\; \mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t \ /\ \max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|,1\}.
$$

If every $\mathbf{k}_s$ and $\mathbf{v}_s$ is zero‑mean (the paper assumes a LayerNorm before the projections so each projected vector has zero mean across its coordinates), then each outer product $\mathbf{v}_s\mathbf{k}_s^\top$ equals the (uncentered) second moment and its expectation equals the cross‑covariance $E[\mathbf{v}\mathbf{k}^\top]$. If in addition the weights $w_{s,t}$ are independent of the sample content (for example constant or deterministic, or independent in expectation), then
$$
E[\mathbf{C}_t] \;=\; \Big(\sum_{s\le t} E[w_{s,t}]\Big)\,E[\mathbf{v}\mathbf{k}^\top],
$$
so $C_t$ is proportional to the cross‑covariance. In the stationary case with constant forget/input gates this is just an exponential moving average of the outer products (an EWMA covariance estimator); dividing by the sum of weights would give the usual normalized estimator. The paper does not explicitly divide by a scalar count because the retrieval step uses $\mathbf{n}_t$ (and the $\max\{\cdot,1\}$ clamp) to compensate for scale in the readout; so absolute normalization is not necessary for stable retrieval.

About “optimality” (maximizing SNR for binary retrieval): the classical associative‑memory results the paper cites (Sejnowski / Dayan, Fast Weights / outer‑product memories) show that the simple outer‑product (covariance/Hebbian) rule is optimal for linear readout of stored binary or random zero‑mean patterns under the standard assumptions: patterns are zero‑mean, components are uncorrelated or approximately isotropic (so cross‑terms average out), retrieval is linear ($Cq$), and one measures separability / SNR of the desired stored vector versus cross‑talk. Under those assumptions the outer‑product rule maximizes the desired signal relative to the average crosstalk noise among local, biologically/plausibly implementable rules. If those assumptions fail — for example if keys have strong correlations between components (no whitening) or if the gates $i_t,f_t$ are strongly content‑dependent in a way that biases weights — then the strict optimality statement no longer holds. In particular, LayerNorm enforces zero mean (so centering is handled) and rescales variances, but it does not whiten (it does not remove linear correlations between coordinates). Whitening the keys (decorrelating coordinates) would make the outer‑product rule closer to the ideal in correlated regimes; conversely, the pseudoinverse/least‑squares associative rule can eliminate interference entirely but is expensive (requires inverting a Gram matrix), which is why outer‑product rules are attractive: cheap, local, and often near‑optimal under mild randomness/isotropy assumptions.

In practice the picture in the paper is this: LayerNorm removes the per‑sample mean (so centering is satisfied), the linear projections and learned gating produce reasonably behaved, approximately isotropic embeddings, and the readout normalization by $\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t$ stabilizes scale — so interpreting $\mathbf{C}_t$ as a (weighted, unnormalized) covariance makes sense and the Dayan/Sejnowski optimality intuition applies as a useful approximation. If you need strictly unbiased covariance estimates for analysis you would divide by the sum of weights (or explicitly track an effective sample count), and if you face highly correlated keys you should consider a whitening step or a more expensive recursive least‑squares style rule.
