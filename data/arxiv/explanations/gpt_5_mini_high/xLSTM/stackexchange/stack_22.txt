\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Output gating in mLSTM: what role does o_t play after normalization?
Question:
mLSTM computes $h_t = o_t \cdot (C_t q_t) / \max(|n_t^T q_t|, 1)$. Given we already apply a form of scale normalization, what additional function does the output gate $o_t$ serve?
- Is $o_t$ mainly feature-wise modulation for conditioning, or is it critical for stability/performance?
- Have you compared against a variant without $o_t$ (or with a scalar $o_t$) to see its marginal benefit?
Answer:
Short answer: $o_t$ is not redundant after the normalization step — it supplies input-conditioned, feature-wise multiplicative control over the retrieved vector and thus plays both a representational and a training-stability role. The paper even phrases this as “the output gate scales the retrieved vector.” Ablations in the paper show that gating in general is beneficial, but the paper does not report a targeted experiment that simply removes $o_t$ or replaces it by a scalar; so the marginal gain of $o_t$ alone is not given numerically there.

Why that matters intuitively and technically. mLSTM computes
$\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t=\dfrac{\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|,1\}}$ and then
$\mathbf{h}_t=\mathbf{o}_t\odot\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t$ with $\mathbf{o}_t=\sigma(\mathbf{W}_o\mathbf{x}_t+\mathbf{b}_o)$. The normalization is a scalar division: it controls a single global scale (and protects against tiny denominators by the lower bound 1), but it does not change the pattern of relative activations inside the vector $\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t$. The output gate is a vector-valued, input-conditioned mask that (1) rescales or zeroes individual dimensions, (2) conditions the readout on the current input, and (3) multiplicatively gates gradients flowing back into the memory/read path. Concretely, the gradient that reaches the retrieved quantity is multiplied per-dimension by $\mathbf{o}_t$, so $\mathbf{o}_t$ functions like an element-wise learned “per-feature learning rate” for the retrieval channel; that both shapes representational capacity and helps stabilize/update dynamics of the memory.

Why this isn’t just “conditioning” or just “stability” — it’s both. Feature-wise gating gives the model the flexibility to expose only the components of a retrieved vector that are relevant for the current input (important for selective readout and for tasks like associative recall or state tracking). At the same time the bounded sigmoid and the multiplicative structure let the network damp large signals or gradients in a fine-grained way that a scalar normalization cannot (especially when the normalization is clipped at 1 or when the normalizer dot is small).

What the paper reports and what it doesn’t. The authors explicitly state that “the output gate scales the retrieved vector,” and their ablation suite attributes much of xLSTM’s gains to exponential gating plus the matrix memory. They also write that “having each gate learnable and influenced by the input has an incremental positive effect.” However, they do not present a clean, single ablation that removes $\mathbf{o}_t$ entirely or replaces it by a single scalar and reports the isolated marginal effect of that change. So if you need hard numbers on that specific change, you would need to run that ablation (see suggestions below).

If you want to test this yourself, the simplest ablations are (a) set $\mathbf{o}_t\equiv\mathbf{1}$ (no output gate), (b) replace $\mathbf{o}_t$ by a learned scalar $s_t$ (shared across dimensions) or by a learned per-head scalar, and (c) use a learnable bias-only gate (no input dependence). Monitor validation perplexity, training loss curves, gradient norms into $\mathbf{C}_t$ (or $\mathbf{q}_t$), and behavior on synthetic tasks that require selective readout (e.g., associative recall / rare-token tasks used in the paper). My expectation (consistent with the paper’s gating-ablation statements and classic LSTM literature) is that removing $\mathbf{o}_t$ will reduce performance on tasks needing selective per-dimension control, and replacing it by a scalar will give a middle-ground: some stability/magnitude control remains, but the model loses per-feature selectivity and will usually perform worse than the full vector gate.

Practical recommendation: keep the vector output gate unless you need to shave parameters/compute and can afford an empirical check. It’s cheap relative to the matrix memory and gives both expressive conditioning and a useful, fine-grained knob for gradient and signal control that a scalar normalization cannot provide.
