\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Why is only the key vector scaled by d^{-1/2} in mLSTM?
Question:
The mLSTM definition uses $k_t \leftarrow d^{-1/2} W_k x_t + b_k$ (keys scaled by $d^{-1/2}$), but $q_t$ and $v_t$ are not scaled in the same way.
- What is the rationale for scaling only $k_t$? Is it to control the magnitude of outer products $v_t k_t^T$ in $C_t$ or to stabilize the denominator $n_t^T q_t$?
- Would scaling $q_t$ and/or $v_t$ similarly help, harm, or be redundant given the normalization via $n_t$?
Answer:
Short answer: the $1/\sqrt{d}$ factor is there for the same reason it appears in Transformer attention — to keep key-query inner products at $O(1)$ as dimension grows — and placing that factor on the keys is a natural, sufficient choice for mLSTM because keys appear both in the outer-product memory updates and in the normalizer. Scaling $q$ instead would be algebraically equivalent for the dot-products, but scaling $v$ is not equivalent and would change the retrieval amplitude.

Explanation grounded in the paper (equations are the paper’s): mLSTM stores outer products and retrieves by
$$
\mathbf{C}_t=\sum_{s\le t} i_s\,\mathbf{v}_s\mathbf{k}_s^\top,\qquad
\mathbf{h}_t \propto \frac{\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|,1\}},\qquad
\mathbf{n}_t=\sum_{s\le t} i_s\,\mathbf{k}_s .
$$
The paper sets $\mathbf{k}_t=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\mathbf{W}_k\mathbf{x}_t+\mathbf{b}_k$. The intuitive and technical points are:

1) Inner products grow with dimension. If vector entries have $O(1)$ variance then a dot product like $\mathbf{k}^\top\mathbf{q}$ has variance proportional to $d$. Dividing the key by $\sqrt{d}$ makes $\mathbf{k}^\top\mathbf{q}$ have variance independent of $d$. This is exactly the “scaled dot‑product” idea used in Transformers: prevent the raw inner products from blowing up with dimension so numbers and gradients stay well‑conditioned.

2) Keys control both the memory update and the normalizer. Because each stored outer product contains one factor of $\mathbf{k}$ and the normalizer $\mathbf{n}_t$ is a sum of keys, putting the $1/\sqrt{d}$ on $\mathbf{k}$ simultaneously tames the magnitude of the stored matrix entries $\mathbf{v}\mathbf{k}^\top$ and of the scalar weights $\mathbf{k}^\top\mathbf{q}$ that appear in both numerator and denominator of the retrieval. Algebraically, if you write $\mathbf{k}_t=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_t$ then
$$
\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_s i_s\,\mathbf{v}_s(\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t),\qquad
\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_s i_s\,(\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t),
$$
so the common $1/\sqrt{d}$ factor cancels inside the ratio (ignoring the lower bound clamp), while the per-term scalars $\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_s^\top\mathbf{q}_t$ are now $O(1)$ instead of $O(\sqrt{d})$.

3) Why not scale $q$ or $v$ instead? Placing the same $1/\sqrt{d}$ on $q$ would be essentially equivalent for stabilizing the raw inner products $\mathbf{k}^\top\mathbf{q}$ (because only the product matters). However, putting the scale on $k$ has the advantage that it also directly controls the scale of the stored outer products $\mathbf{v}\mathbf{k}^\top$ (so the memory matrix $\mathbf{C}_t$ does not grow with $\sqrt{d}$). Scaling $v$ is different: $\mathbf{v}$ appears in the numerator but not in the normalizer, so scaling $v$ would change the amplitude of retrieved vectors and would not cancel in the normalization. That would either force the model to compensate (through the output gate $\mathbf{o}_t$ or learned weights) or degrade signal‑to‑noise / gradient magnitudes. In short, scaling $v$ is not symmetric with scaling $k/q$ and is generally unnecessary or harmful.

4) Implementation and stability details in the paper. The authors also use a LayerNorm before projections (so inputs have controlled variance) and clamp the denominator by $\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,1\}$ to avoid division by very small scalars — both design choices work together with the $1/\sqrt{d}$ key scaling to keep retrieval numerically stable.

Takeaway: scaling the key by $d^{-1/2}$ is the pragmatic way to (a) keep key–query inner products $O(1)$ with dimension and (b) control the scale of the stored outer products, while leaving $v$ unscaled so that retrieval amplitude is not arbitrarily reduced. Scaling $q$ instead would be mathematically interchangeable for the inner products, but scaling $v$ would not be and is generally not recommended.
