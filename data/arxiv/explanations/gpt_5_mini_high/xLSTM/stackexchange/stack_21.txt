\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Numerical stability of the sLSTM stabilizer when using sigmoid forget gates
Question:
The stabilizer uses $\log f_t$ even when $f_t$ is sigmoid. For very small $f_t$, $\log f_t$ is large negative. 
- In practice, does $m_t = \max(\log f_t + m_{t-1}, \log i_t)$ avoid underflow reliably, or do you cap $\log f_t$ at some minimum?
- Are there implementation details (e.g., using log1p, fused kernels) necessary to keep this robust across long sequences?
Answer:
Short answer: the max-stabilizer in the paper is the standard log-domain trick and is numerically robust in exact arithmetic; in practice you must (a) compute the log of the sigmoid in a numerically stable way (use a logsigmoid / stable softplus form), (b) keep the log-domain accumulators (the $m_t$ recurrence) in a sufficiently wide format (FP32 / bfloat16) when you are doing mixed‑precision training, and (c) (optionally) clamp values only as a last-resort fallback for very low precision or extremely long sequences. Fusing the recurrence and the gate rescales into a single kernel is recommended for both speed and to avoid extra rounding/cast noise.

Why this works (intuitively + math). The stabilizer is
$$
m_t \;=\; \max\!\big(\log f_t + m_{t-1},\; \log i_t\big),
\qquad
i'_t \;=\; \exp(\log i_t - m_t),\qquad
f'_t \;=\; \exp(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t).
$$
This is exactly the familiar “log-domain” normalization (think log‑sum‑exp / forward algorithm): subtracting $m_t$ makes the exponents $\le 0$, so $\exp(\cdot)$ cannot overflow. If $f_t=\sigma(\tilde f_t)$ then you should not compute $\log f_t$ via $\log(\sigma(\tilde f_t))$ as two separate ops; instead use the numerically stable identity
$$
\log\sigma(\tilde f_t)= -\mathrm{softplus}(-\tilde f_t),
$$
or use your framework’s logsigmoid implementation (PyTorch: F.logsigmoid), which avoids forming tiny $f_t$ first.

Practical gotchas and recommendations
- Compute log-gates in a stable form. For sigmoid forget gates do $\log f_t = \mathrm{logsigmoid}(\tilde f_t) = -\mathrm{softplus}(-\tilde f_t)$ (implemented with stable softplus / log1p internally); for exponential gates $\log f_t=\tilde f_t$ trivially. This prevents an intermediate tiny $f_t$ becoming exactly zero before you take the log.  
- Do these log and $m_t$ operations in FP32 (or bfloat16) even when the rest of the network runs in FP16. FP16’s exponent range is small and will cause overflow/inf / -inf much earlier; using FP32 for the gating/log domain avoids that without breaking the model. The paper uses mixed precision in experiments, and the usual practice is to keep numerically sensitive reductions/normalizers in higher precision.  
- Underflow to zero of $\exp(\text{very negative})$ can still happen in finite precision, but that only means that contribution is numerically negligible (which is usually acceptable). If you need to avoid even tiny nonzero contributions becoming exactly zero (because of gradient flow concerns), keep the exponentials and accumulators in FP32.  
- You generally do not need to “cap $\log f_t$ at some fixed minimum” in correct FP32 implementations; capping is a hack that changes the exact algebraic equivalence (the paper proves the stabilized form is algebraically equivalent). Only use clamping if you are forced to run everything in FP16 / extremely long sequences and see NaNs or infs — then clamp to a reasonable finite range (e.g. a few tens) as a pragmatic fallback.  
- Fuse the recurrence and the gate-rescaling into one kernel when possible. Doing the $\max$, the two exponentials and the cell/normalizer updates in one fused GPU kernel reduces memory traffic, avoids repeated casts, and reduces rounding error accumulation. The paper notes the authors implemented an optimized CUDA kernel for sLSTM, which is exactly the sort of optimization that helps here.  
- Initialization: the paper explicitly warns that forget‑gate initialization matters. Choosing a forget bias that avoids extremely small $\sigma(\tilde f_t)$ at start (standard practice: positive bias around 1.0) reduces the risk of early underflow/vanishing contributions.

In short: the mathematical stabilizer $m_t$ is sound and prevents overflow; the practical steps to make it robust across long sequences are to compute $\log\sigma(\cdot)$ in a numerically stable way, keep the log-domain arithmetic in FP32/bfloat16, and (preferably) fuse the recurrence into a single kernel. Clamping $\log f_t$ is not required if you follow those practices and is only a pragmatic fallback for very low‑precision deployments.
