\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### If f_t can exceed 1 with exponential gating, what prevents exploding memories?
Question:
In sLSTM and mLSTM the forget gate $f_t$ can be exponential, hence $f_t>1$ is possible. The paper introduces stabilizers and, for mLSTM, a denominator based on $n_t$.
- In sLSTM, if many consecutive $f_t>1$, will $c_t$ and $n_t$ both grow but their ratio remain stable, or can the ratio still blow up?
- In mLSTM, even if $C_t$ grows, does dividing by $|n_t^T q_t|$ guarantee bounded outputs, or can $C_t q_t$ grow faster than the denominator?
- Are there explicit constraints or regularizers used in training to keep these effects in check?
Answer:
Short answer: the xLSTM design prevents unbounded hidden outputs in the normal (intended) regime by construction — the normalizer states make the cell output a weighted average — and the paper adds several numerical/architectural protections (the stabilizer state $m_t$, layer‑norm before key/value projections, key scaling and a lower bound on the denominator). That said, mathematically the raw memories (the scalar cell $c_t$ or the matrix $C_t$) can grow exponentially when many consecutive forget gates exceed $1$; the normalization/thresholding is what prevents the visible output from exploding in typical settings, but adversarial corner cases (e.g. cancellations in mLSTM) can still produce large outputs unless the usual boundedness assumptions on $z,v,k,q$ hold.

More detail and the exact reason why: for sLSTM the normalizer $n_t$ is chosen so that the ratio is a weighted average. If you define
$$
w_{t,s} \;=\; i_s \prod_{u=s+1}^t f_u \qquad(\text{with } \prod_{u=t+1}^t f_u :=1),
$$
then
$$
c_t=\sum_{s\le t} w_{t,s} z_s,\qquad n_t=\sum_{s\le t} w_{t,s}.
$$
Hence
$$
\frac{c_t}{n_t} \;=\; \sum_{s\le t} \frac{w_{t,s}}{n_t}\, z_s,
$$
which is a convex (nonnegative weights that sum to $1$) combination of the past inputs $z_s$. So regardless of how large each individual weight $w_{t,s}$ becomes (for example if many consecutive $f_u>1$), the ratio $c_t/n_t$ remains inside the convex hull of the $\{z_s\}$. In the paper the cell inputs $z_t=\varphi(\tilde z_t)$ are standardly bounded (e.g. $\varphi=\tanh$ or used together with LayerNorm), and the output gate $o_t$ is a sigmoid, so the visible hidden $h_t=o_t(c_t/n_t)$ is likewise bounded. Note also that the stabilizer state $m_t$ in the paper rescales exponentials for numerical safety; as the authors point out, using the stabilized gates does not change the mathematical outputs or gradients — it only prevents floating‑point overflow while computing very large $f_t,i_t$.

For mLSTM the same weighted‑average intuition applies but with an extra inner product factor from keys/queries. Writing the same $w_{t,s}$ you get
$$
C_t q_t \;=\; \sum_{s\le t} w_{t,s}\, v_s (k_s^\top q_t),\qquad
n_t^\top q_t \;=\; \sum_{s\le t} w_{t,s}\, (k_s^\top q_t).
$$
So the unnormalized retrieval is a linear combination of the values $v_s$ with scalar coefficients $\alpha_s := w_{t,s}(k_s^\top q_t)$; the normalization denominator is $\sum_s \alpha_s$. Thus, if all the scalar factors $(k_s^\top q_t)$ scale together, the common factor cancels and you again get a weighted average of bounded $v_s$. To protect against the dot product being very small (e.g. near cancellation of positive and negative terms), the paper uses $|n_t^\top q_t|$ and lower bounds it by $1$: the retrieval is
$$
\tilde h_t \;=\; \frac{\sum_s \alpha_s v_s}{\max\{\,|\sum_s \alpha_s|,\,1\}}.
$$
This prevents division by tiny scalars (numerical blowup). However, in principle there is a pathological scenario in which $\sum_s \alpha_s$ is small because of sign cancellations while the vector sum $\sum_s \alpha_s v_s$ does not cancel (e.g. values $v_s$ aligned so cancellations in the scalar weights do not reduce the vector norm); in that adversarial case dividing by the threshold $1$ can produce a large retrieved vector. The paper mitigates this risk in practice by (i) layer‑normalizing inputs before producing keys/values, (ii) scaling keys by $1/\sqrt{d}$ (attention‑style scaling), (iii) using the absolute value plus a lower threshold on the denominator, and (iv) applying the sigmoid output gate. These measures make extreme mismatches unlikely in practice and keep the produced hidden vectors numerically controlled.

Finally, regarding explicit training regularizers: the paper does not introduce a separate loss term or exotic regularizer whose sole purpose is to cap memory growth. The mechanisms used to keep things well‑behaved are architectural and numerical: the normalizer states $n_t$ (and their division), the stabilizer state $m_t$ for exponentials, layer‑norm before projecting to keys/values, key scaling $1/\sqrt{d}$, the absolute/thresholding of $|n_t^\top q_t|$, and the practical remark that forget‑gate initialization must be chosen carefully (the paper lists that as a limitation and hyperparameter to tune). In short: the model relies on normalization + numeric stabilizers rather than an explicit regularizer on gate magnitudes; under the paper’s boundedness assumptions for projections and activations this is sufficient to prevent exploding visible outputs.
