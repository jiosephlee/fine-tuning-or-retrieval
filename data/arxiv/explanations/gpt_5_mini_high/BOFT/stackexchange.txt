\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why does left-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserve pairwise neuron angles in OFT?
Question:
In the paper, a pretrained linear layer has weights \(\mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}\), and OFT/BOFT form the finetuned weights as \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\) with \(\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) orthogonal. The authors say this preserves the pairwise angles between "neurons" (the columns of \(\mathbf{W}^0\)). I think that means for columns \(\mathbf{w}_i, \mathbf{w}_j\) of \(\mathbf{W}^0\), the cosine \(\cos\theta_{ij} = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{w}_j}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|\,\|\mathbf{w}_j\|}\) remains unchanged after mapping to \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i, \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j\). Could someone show the explicit steps (with shapes) that make this true despite the forward pass being \(\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{x}\)? Also, is there any subtlety if \(\mathbf{W}^0\) has different normalization across columns?
Answer:
Short answer: because an orthogonal matrix is an isometry of Euclidean space: it preserves inner products and hence lengths and angles. Concretely, if $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ satisfies $R^\top R = I_d$ and $w_i, w_j \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are columns (``neurons'') of $W^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, then the cosine between the transformed columns equals the original cosine.

Write the cosine before finetuning as
$$
\cos\theta_{ij}
=\frac{w_i^\top w_j}{\|w_i\|_2\|w_j\|_2},
\qquad w_i, w_j \in \mathbb{R}^d.
$$
After OFT the columns become $R w_i$ and $R w_j$ (since $R W^0$ is $d\times n$ and its $i$-th column is $R w_i$). The inner product and norms transform as
$$
(R w_i)^\top (R w_j) = w_i^\top R^\top R w_j = w_i^\top w_j,
$$
$$
\|R w_i\|_2 = \sqrt{(R w_i)^\top (R w_i)} = \sqrt{w_i^\top R^\top R w_i} = \|w_i\|_2,
$$
so
$$
\frac{(R w_i)^\top (R w_j)}{\|R w_i\|_2 \|R w_j\|_2}
= \frac{w_i^\top w_j}{\|w_i\|_2 \|w_j\|_2}
= \cos\theta_{ij}.
$$
Thus pairwise angles are exactly preserved. Equivalently, the Gram matrix of columns is invariant: $(R W^0)^\top (R W^0) = (W^0)^\top W^0$.

About the forward pass: the fact that the layer computes $z=(R W^0)^\top x$ is just a bookkeeping choice; it is algebraically equal to $z=(W^0)^\top (R^\top x)$. So you can view OFT either as rotating every neuron column by $R$, or as rotating the input $x$ by $R^\top$. Either view makes clear why the neuron-to-neuron geometry (inner products, norms, angles) is unchanged.

Subtleties: different column norms are not a problem — orthogonal $R$ preserves every column's norm, so any initial heterogeneity of norms remains unchanged and does not affect the angle-preservation statement. The only ways angles would change are (i) if $R$ is not truly orthogonal (e.g., only approximately orthogonal because of numerical drift or a relaxed constraint), or (ii) if you subsequently rescale or renormalize columns (or apply non‑isometric operations) after applying $R$. Also be careful about conventions: the paper treats neurons as columns because the forward pass is $z=W^\top x$; if you used a row-wise neuron convention, left-multiplying by $R$ would not preserve pairwise angles of rows (you would need a right-multiplication by an orthogonal matrix to preserve row geometry). Finally, BOFT is a product of orthogonal factors, and a product of orthogonal matrices is itself orthogonal, so the same invariance holds for the BOFT parameterization.

### How exactly are butterfly components made orthogonal via the Cayley transform?
Question:
The paper defines a butterfly factor \(\mathbf{B}^F(k) = \begin{bmatrix} \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2) \\ \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4) \end{bmatrix}\) and then says each butterfly component \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\) can be made orthogonal using the Cayley transform, effectively by parameterizing each \(2\times 2\) (or \(2b\times 2b\)) block as orthogonal. I am confused about the mapping between the diagonal-vector parameterization in \(\mathbf{B}^F(k)\) and a blockwise Cayley parameterization. Do we ignore the diagonal-vector form and instead directly parameterize each \(2\times 2\) (or \(2b\times 2b\)) nonzero block using \(\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) with \(\mathbf{Q}^\top = -\mathbf{Q}\)? If so, what exactly are those blocks (an explicit index-level description would help), and how do we reconcile this with the notation in Equation (1)?
Answer:
Short answer: you do not discard the diagonal-vector notation in Equation (1); that notation is simply a compact way to describe a collection of independent $2\times 2$ subblocks. To make a butterfly component orthogonal you replace each independent $2\times 2$ (or each $2b\times 2b$) subblock by an orthogonal block produced by a Cayley transform applied to a local skew–symmetric matrix. Concretely, the diagonal vectors in (1) index the entries of those small blocks; you simply make those entries depend on the Cayley parameters instead of treating them as unconstrained scalars.

Detail and an explicit index-level mapping. Let $k=2m$ and consider one butterfly factor
$$
\mathbf{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1)&\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2)\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3)&\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)\end{bmatrix}\!,
$$
so $\mathbf{d}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{m}$. This matrix has nonzeros only at positions
$$
(i,i),\quad (i,i+m),\quad (i+m,i),\quad (i+m,i+m)\qquad\text{for }i=1,\dots,m,
$$
i.e. it is made of $m$ independent $2\times 2$ subblocks acting on the index pairs $(i,i+m)$. Write the $i$-th scalar $2\times 2$ subblock as
$$
S^{(i)}=\begin{bmatrix}(\mathbf{d}_1)_i & (\mathbf{d}_2)_i\\[4pt] (\mathbf{d}_3)_i & (\mathbf{d}_4)_i\end{bmatrix}.
$$
To force orthogonality you replace each such scalar block $S^{(i)}$ by an orthogonal $2\times 2$ matrix $R^{(i)}$ obtained from Cayley parameterization. For the scalar $(b=1)$ case take a skew–symmetric
$$
Q^{(i)}=\begin{bmatrix}0 & -q_i\\[3pt] q_i & 0\end{bmatrix},
$$
and form
$$
R^{(i)}=(I+Q^{(i)})(I-Q^{(i)})^{-1}.
$$
Embedding $R^{(i)}$ back into $\mathbf{B}^F(k)$ means
$$
(\mathbf{d}_1)_i \leftarrow R^{(i)}_{11},\quad (\mathbf{d}_2)_i \leftarrow R^{(i)}_{12},\quad
(\mathbf{d}_3)_i \leftarrow R^{(i)}_{21},\quad (\mathbf{d}_4)_i \leftarrow R^{(i)}_{22}.
$$
For example, with the above $Q^{(i)}$ one gets the familiar 2D rotation form (writing $q=q_i$)
$$
R^{(i)}=\frac{1}{1+q^2}\begin{bmatrix}1-q^2 & -2q\\[3pt] 2q & 1-q^2\end{bmatrix},
$$
which corresponds to a rotation with angle $\theta$ via $q=\tan(\theta/2)$; identity initialization corresponds to $q_i=0$ so $R^{(i)}=I$.

For the block case ($b>1$) the same idea generalizes by replacing each scalar entry of the $\mathbf{d}_j$ vectors with a $b\times b$ matrix. Each pair of $b$-sized groups of coordinates becomes a $2b\times 2b$ subblock. Denote a local skew–symmetric $2b\times 2b$ matrix $Q^{(t)}$ for that pair and set
$$
R^{(t)}=(I+Q^{(t)})(I-Q^{(t)})^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{2b\times 2b},
$$
then place the four $b\times b$ blocks of $R^{(t)}$ into the corresponding four diagonal-block positions of $\mathbf{B}^F(k)$. Indexing in the full $d$–dimensional matrix is the same idea but with global indices: if $k$ divides $d$ and there are $s=d/k$ copies of $\mathbf{B}^F(k)$ along the diagonal of $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)$, then for block copy index $u=0,\dots,s-1$ and intra-block pair $j=1,\dots,k/2$ the global pair of indices is
$$
\text{idx}_1 = u\cdot k + j,\qquad \text{idx}_2 = u\cdot k + j + \tfrac{k}{2},
$$
(or the corresponding contiguous groups of $b$ indices in the block case). Put the Cayley-produced $2\times 2$ (or $2b\times 2b$) block on rows/columns $(\text{idx}_1,\text{idx}_2)$ (or the $b$-sized groups).

One practical implementation remark that the paper hints at: every butterfly component’s sparsity pattern is just a permutation of the $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2)$ pattern, so you can either (a) explicitly form the index pairs above and fill each paired block by its Cayley-produced orthogonal block, or (b) apply a permutation that brings the component to a block-diagonal form of independent $2\times 2$ (or $2b\times 2b$) blocks, parameterize that block-diagonal by Cayley, and then undo the permutation. Either way yields a component with exact orthogonality (since Cayley of a skew–symmetric is orthogonal), and the product of these orthogonal components is orthogonal as required.

A short caveat: Cayley parameterization maps skew–symmetric $Q$ to orthogonal $R$ but cannot represent orthogonal matrices having eigenvalue $-1$ (this is standard with the Cayley map). In practice this is not a problem for finetuning because you initialize $Q=0$ (identity $R$) and the product of many blockwise Cayley factors can approximate a wide class of orthogonals (the paper also discusses richer compositions if full coverage is needed).

In sum: Equation (1)’s diagonal-vector form is a compact way to display the sparsity pattern (the independent $2\times 2$ locations). To make those components orthogonal you replace each scalar quadruple $(d_{1,i},d_{2,i},d_{3,i},d_{4,i})$ (or the analogous $b\times b$ quadruple) by the entries of a Cayley-transformed $2\times 2$ (or $2b\times 2b$) skew–symmetric parameter.

### Deriving the BOFT parameter count: why is it \(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm\)?
Question:
The paper claims BOFT\((m,b)\) uses \(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm\) effective trainable parameters for a layer of size \(d \times n\). Could someone derive this count carefully? Specifically: (1) how many independent degrees of freedom does each \(2b\times 2b\) orthogonal block contribute under this butterfly sparsity pattern, (2) how many such blocks appear per butterfly component at dimension \(d\), and (3) how do constraints implied by orthogonality and the product structure reduce the naive parameter count to the stated formula?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper’s count is just "$(\#\ \text{blocks per component}) \times (\text{degrees of freedom per block}) \times (\text{number of components})$". Each atomic orthogonal block of size $s\times s$ (the paper uses both the notation $2b\times 2b$ in one place and later re-labels that block-size as $b$) has $s(s-1)/2$ continuous degrees of freedom (dimension of $O(s)$); a butterfly component is block‑diagonal with $d/s$ such blocks, so one component contributes $(d/s)\cdot s(s-1)/2 = d(s-1)/2$ parameters, and $m$ components give $m\cdot d(s-1)/2$. Using the paper’s final convention $s=b$ this is exactly
$$
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m,
$$
as claimed.

A bit more detail and intuition to connect to your three subquestions. (1) An $s\times s$ real orthogonal matrix obeys $Q^\top Q=I_s$. Starting from $s^2$ raw entries, the orthogonality equations impose $s(s+1)/2$ independent scalar constraints, leaving $s(s-1)/2$ free parameters (this is the well‑known dimension of $O(s)$). If you read the paper’s local block statement as “each $2b\times 2b$ block is made orthogonal”, put $s=2b$ there and the block contributes $s(s-1)/2=(2b)(2b-1)/2$ free parameters. (2) Each butterfly component $\tilde B(\cdot)$ is block‑diagonal with blocks of that same size $s$, so the number of such blocks in a $d\times d$ component is $d/s$ (we assume $s$ divides $d$, as in the radix-2 constructions). (3) Multiply the two counts and then multiply by $m$ components: total free parameters
$$
\frac{d}{s}\cdot\frac{s(s-1)}{2}\cdot m \;=\; \frac{1}{2}(s-1)\,d\,m.
$$
The only subtlety in matching the paper’s displayed formula is notation: the paper later re‑labels the block size $s$ as $b$ (they say “we denote BOFT with $R(m,\tfrac{b}{2})$ as BOFT$(m,b)$”), so substituting $s=b$ gives $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)d m$.

Intuitively, orthogonality is what “reduces” the naive parameter count. If you counted all nonzero scalar entries in the sparse pattern of one component you would get $(d/s)\cdot s^2 = d s$ scalars, but the orthogonality constraints remove $(d/s)\cdot s(s+1)/2 = d(s+1)/2$ degrees, leaving $d(s-1)/2$ effective free parameters per component. The product of $m$ independent orthogonal components does not introduce extra algebraic constraints between components (we parameterize each factor independently, e.g. via Cayley/skew‑symmetric matrices), so the total number of independent trainable scalars is the sum over components, giving the final formula.

Sanity checks: if $m=1$ and $b=d$ (one full orthogonal factor) you get $\tfrac{1}{2}(d-1)d$, the usual $\dim O(d)$. If $b=2$ (atomic $2\times2$ rotations) you get $\tfrac{1}{2}(2-1)d m = \tfrac{1}{2} d m$, the well‑known “one angle per paired entry” count of the radix‑2 butterfly layers.

### In the information-transmission view, what are “free edges” and why are \(d\) edges per level pair considered non-trainable?
Question:
The paper says that for each factor \(\mathbf{B}_i\) to be full rank (and hence potentially orthogonal), we need at least \(d\) edges to form a bijection between levels, and these \(d\) edges "are not trainable (e.g., for a \(d\times d\) orthogonal with \(d\) nonzeros, they can only be \(\pm 1\))." I am not following this. What precisely is a "free edge" versus a non-trainable edge here? Orthogonal matrices with exactly one nonzero per row/column are signed permutations, but once we allow more nonzeros per row/column the values need not be \(\pm 1\). How does this translate into a parameter-counting rule for the product of sparse orthogonal factors?
Answer:
Short answer: a “free edge” is a nonzero entry in a sparse factor that carries a continuous trainable degree of freedom; the authors call the minimum $d$ edges between two adjacent levels non-trainable because those $d$ entries (one per row and column) can only realize a signed permutation when that is the entire support, so they do not provide continuous parameters under the orthogonality constraint. In the BOFT design the remaining (extra) nonzeros are organized into small orthogonal blocks ($2\times 2$ rotations in the radix‑2 case), and counting the continuous degrees of freedom contributed by those blocks yields the reported parameter count $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$.

Explanation and intuition

Think of each factor $\mathbf{B}_i$ as a $d\times d$ adjacency/weight matrix between level $i$ and level $i+1$. An “edge” = a nonzero entry in $\mathbf{B}_i$. The information‑transmission view separates two roles of edges:

- Structural edges required for invertibility/full rank: to make a square factor $\mathbf{B}_i$ full rank you need at least one nonzero in every row and every column, i.e. a perfect matching / bijection across the $d$ nodes. That requires at least $d$ edges.
- Free (trainable) edges: any additional nonzeros beyond that minimum can carry continuous real values and thus provide trainable parameters (subject to the orthogonality constraints that couple them).

Why the minimal $d$ edges are “non‑trainable”
If a $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix has exactly one nonzero in each row and each column, then it must be a signed permutation matrix. Algebraically this is because orthonormality forces each such row/column to be a unit vector with support on a single coordinate, hence that single nonzero must have absolute value $1$; its sign is $\pm 1$. So the pattern with exactly $d$ nonzeros does not allow continuous variation — only discrete sign/permutation choices — and therefore contributes no continuous parameters in the usual smooth parameterizations (Cayley transform, rotation angles, etc.). A tiny example: any $2\times 2$ orthogonal with one nonzero per row/column is either $\begin{pmatrix}\pm1&0\\[2pt]0&\pm1\end{pmatrix}$ or $\begin{pmatrix}0&\pm1\\[2pt]\pm1&0\end{pmatrix}$; no continuous angle lives there.

So when the paper says “these $d$ edges … should not be counted … because these elements are not trainable,” they mean: those edges are the minimal combinatorial connectivity (a permutation) and do not provide continuous degrees of freedom under orthogonality.

How this translates to parameter counting for sparse orthogonal factors
You should not count raw nonzeros as parameters. The correct count is the number of independent continuous degrees of freedom (the dimension of the orthogonal manifold restricted to the given sparsity pattern). A convenient and standard way to realize continuous DOFs is to build each sparse factor out of small orthogonal blocks (e.g. $2\times 2$ Givens rotations or small orthogonal blocks that are parameterized by skew‑symmetric matrices / Cayley transform). In that design:

- For the radix‑2 (smallest) case each factor is a direct sum of $d/2$ independent $2\times 2$ rotation blocks. Each $2\times 2$ rotation has one continuous parameter (an angle), so each factor contributes $d/2$ trainable scalars. With $m$ factors this gives total trainable parameters $(d/2)\,m = \tfrac{1}{2}dm$.
- For the block generalization used in the paper (their BOFT(m,b) notation), each butterfly component is made of block orthogonal units of larger size; the number of continuous parameters per component scales linearly with $d$ and with $(b-1)/2$ per column pair in the chosen blockization, and the paper’s construction leads to the compact count
  $$
    \text{\# trainable scalars} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m.
  $$
  The special case $b=2$ recovers the $\tfrac{1}{2}dm$ radix‑2 count above, and choosing $m=\log d$ and $b=2$ gives the familiar $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameter scaling.

Put another way: parameter counting = sum over factors of (the continuous DOF you explicitly parameterize in each factor). The paper’s BOFT parameterization explicitly parameterizes only the continuous DOFs (small rotations / skew‑symmetric blocks), and it excludes the mandatory $d$ matching edges per factor from the continuous count because those edges, by themselves, are signed permutations and not smoothly adjustable.

Broader connection
This is just a manifestation of two general facts: (1) the orthogonal group $O(d)$ has $\tfrac{d(d-1)}{2}$ continuous dimensions, and (2) sparsity patterns impose algebraic constraints that can reduce those continuous dimensions (sometimes down to zero, as with a signed permutation pattern). A sensible sparse orthogonal parameterization counts only the remaining continuous dimensions; BOFT achieves a dramatically smaller continuous parameter count by arranging those dimensions into many small local rotations (butterfly blocks) so the product still produces a dense final matrix.

### Why does BOFT(1, b) recover block-diagonal OFT and BOFT(1, d) recover full OFT?
Question:
The paper states that BOFT\((1,b)\) reduces to block-diagonal OFT with block size \(b\), and BOFT\((1,d)\) equals the original OFT with a dense \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix. Could someone show this explicitly from the definition of the block butterfly component \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b(d, 2)\)? In particular, how does a single butterfly component with block size \(b\) equal a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix, and why does choosing \(b=d\) remove sparsity altogether?
Answer:
Short answer: by construction a single block-butterfly component is literally a block-diagonal matrix, so when you take just one component ($m=1$) you get exactly the block-diagonal OFT parameterization; if you make the block size equal to the whole dimension there is only one block, so there is no enforced zero pattern any more and you recover a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix (the original OFT). Below I show this explicitly from the paper’s definitions and give a small concrete example to make it obvious.

From the paper. Equation (1) defines a scalar “butterfly factor”
$$
\mathbf{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1) & \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2)\\[4pt]
\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3) & \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
$$
and the (scalar) butterfly component is
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)=\mathrm{diag}\big(\mathbf{B}^F_1(k),\dots,\mathbf{B}^F_{d/k}(k)\big),
$$
i.e. a block‑diagonal matrix with block size $k$. The paper then generalizes to a block-butterfly component $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b(d,k)$ by replacing each scalar entry in the $\mathbf{B}^F(k)$ blocks with a $b\times b$ matrix. Concretely, for $k=2$ each scalar $2\times 2$ butterfly factor becomes a $2\times 2$ block matrix whose block entries are $b\times b$ matrices:
$$
\mathbf{B}^F(2)\;\rightsquigarrow\;
\begin{bmatrix}
A & B\\[4pt]
C & D
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad A,B,C,D\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b},
$$
and the whole component is the block-diagonal concatenation of these $2b\times 2b$ blocks:
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b(d,2)=\mathrm{diag}\big(\mathbf{B}_1,\mathbf{B}_2,\dots,\mathbf{B}_{\,d/(2b)}\big),
\qquad \mathbf{B}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{2b\times 2b}.
$$

Why this is block-diagonal OFT when $m=1$. BOFT builds its orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ as a product of components $\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, and the paper uses the notational convention BOFT$(m,b)$ to mean the same object but with the block‑size bookkeeping described above (see the paragraph after the forward-pass equation). Hence when $m=1$ there is a single factor $\mathbf{R}=\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b(d,2)$, and by the displayed formula this $\mathbf{R}$ is block‑diagonal: it acts independently on disjoint coordinate blocks (each block is an orthogonal $2b\times 2b$ block in the internal math, which the paper’s naming converts to “block size $b$” in the statement BOFT$(1,b)$ reduces to block‑diagonal OFT). Each diagonal block can be parametrized (e.g. by Cayley or 2D rotations) to be orthogonal, so $\mathbf{R}$ is a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix. That is exactly the block‑diagonal OFT parameterization: a direct sum of independent small orthogonal matrices, one per block.

Why setting $b=d$ removes sparsity. The block count is $d/\text{(block size)}$. If you set the block size equal to the whole dimension, there is only a single diagonal block. In symbols, if the component is $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}(d,2)$ and the paper’s naming interprets this as BOFT$(1,B)$ with block size $B=b$ (or equivalently choose the internal block so the physical block size equals $d$), then $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}(d,2)$ has exactly one block of size $d\times d$. That one block is an unconstrained orthogonal $d\times d$ matrix (its internal $b\times b$ submatrices are free), so there are no zero entries forced by the block structure — you recover the standard full OFT orthogonal matrix. Intuitively, increasing the block size merges previously separate groups of coordinates; when the group is the whole vector, there is no group separation left and therefore no sparsity.

A tiny concrete example. Take $d=8$. If you pick BOFT$(1,2)$ (block size $2$) you get
$$
\mathbf{R}=\mathrm{diag}(R_1,R_2,R_3,R_4),\qquad R_i\in O(2),
$$
four independent $2\times 2$ rotations — the block‑diagonal OFT with block size 2. If you pick BOFT$(1,8)$ the decomposition has one block and so
$$
\mathbf{R}=R_1,\qquad R_1\in O(8),
$$
a single $8\times 8$ orthogonal matrix (the full OFT). To get a dense orthogonal matrix from multiple small blocks you would instead compose several butterfly stages (increase $m$); that is exactly how the butterfly product mixes across blocks: several sparse stages composed together can produce dense connectivity (this is the FFT intuition the paper uses).

In short: $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b(d,2)$ is block‑diagonal by definition (each diagonal block is a small orthogonal matrix). Taking a single such component ($m=1$) therefore yields block‑diagonal OFT; making the block size equal to $d$ collapses the block diagonal to a single full $d\times d$ orthogonal block, recovering the original full OFT.

### How is BOFT defined when \(d\) is not a power of two?
Question:
The butterfly recursion is described for \(d = 2^N\). In practice, transformer layers often have dimensions like \(d=768\) or \(d=4096\), which are not exact powers of two or decompose into mixed small primes. What is the exact construction used when \(d\) is not a power of two? Do you pad, use mixed-radix butterfly components, or adopt the "pixelated" generalization cited? A small worked example for, say, \(d=6\) or \(d=12\) would be very helpful.
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not recommend naively padding to the next power of two. Instead it uses the “pixelated” (block) generalization of the radix‑2 butterfly (citing Chen et al.) — i.e. you group coordinates into $b\times b$ blocks so the number of blocks is a power of two, run a radix‑2 butterfly on those blocks, and realize each scalar $2\times 2$ butterfly as a $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal block (parameterized with Cayley/$2\times 2$ rotations). Concretely you write
$$
d = b\cdot 2^n
$$
(choose $b$ so that $t:=d/b$ is a power of two; one natural choice is to take $b$ equal to the odd part of $d$), then use
$$
\bm{R}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\widetilde{\bm B}^b_{(d,i)},\qquad m=\log_2\!\bigg(\frac{2d}{b}\bigg)=n+1,
$$
where every $\widetilde{\bm B}^b_{(d,i)}$ is block‑structured and orthogonal. This is exactly the “block/pixelated” butterfly construction the paper describes: each scalar entry of the radix‑2 butterfly is replaced by a $b\times b$ matrix, and each $2\times 2$ scalar butterfly becomes a $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal block (which you can parametrize by Cayley or by Kroneckerizing a $2\times 2$ rotation).

Why this works and how to think about it: the radix‑2 butterfly requires the number of *blocks* to be a power of two, not the raw dimension. Grouping consecutive channels into blocks makes the radix‑2 recursion valid again while keeping all $d$ coordinates (no wasted padding). Each butterfly stage mixes pairs of blocks; at stage $s$ the elementary $2\times 2$ mixing acts on block pairs of size $b\cdot 2^{s-1}$, which is implemented as a Kronecker product of a $2\times 2$ orthogonal mixing times an identity on the inside‑block coordinates.

Worked examples

1) $d = 6$. Factor $6 = 3\cdot 2^1$. Take $b=3$, $t=2$, $n=1$, so $m=n+1=2$. A minimal block‑butterfly factorization is
$$
\bm{R}=\widetilde{\bm B}_2^3\;\widetilde{\bm B}_1^3,
$$
where you can choose
$$
\widetilde{\bm B}_1^3=\operatorname{diag}(\bm A_1,\bm A_2),\qquad \bm A_1,\bm A_2\in O(3),
$$
and
$$
\widetilde{\bm B}_2^3 = \begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta\,\bm I_3 & -\sin\theta\,\bm I_3\\[4pt]
\sin\theta\,\bm I_3 & \cos\theta\,\bm I_3\end{bmatrix}
= r(\theta)\otimes \bm I_3,
$$
with $r(\theta)$ the usual $2\times 2$ rotation. Both factors are orthogonal; their product is a dense $6\times 6$ orthogonal that has the butterfly sparsity at block level. In BOFT you would parameterize each $\bm A_i$ (and any nontrivial $2b\times 2b$ blocks) via Cayley/skew‑symmetric parameters so orthogonality is exact and initialization at identity is easy.

2) $d = 12$. Factor $12=3\cdot 2^2$. Take $b=3$, $t=4$, $n=2$, so $m=3$. A three‑stage block‑butterfly can be written
$$
\bm{R}=\widetilde{\bm B}_3^3\;\widetilde{\bm B}_2^3\;\widetilde{\bm B}_1^3,
$$
with for example
$$
\widetilde{\bm B}_1^3=\operatorname{diag}(\bm A_1,\bm A_2,\bm A_3,\bm A_4),\qquad \bm A_i\in O(3),
$$
(stage 1: local per‑block orthogonals),
$$
\widetilde{\bm B}_2^3=\operatorname{diag}\big(r(\theta_{1})\otimes\bm I_3,\; r(\theta_{2})\otimes\bm I_3\big)
$$
(stage 2: mix blocks (1,2) and (3,4) pairwise), and
$$
\widetilde{\bm B}_3^3 = r(\theta_{3})\otimes \bm I_6
$$
(stage 3: mix the two half‑groups). Again each stage is orthogonal; choosing the $\bm A_i$ and the rotation angles (or parameterizing full $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks by Cayley) gives the BOFT parameters. This yields the dense final orthogonal while keeping the $O(d\log d)$ parameter count.

Remarks on alternatives and practicality: padding to the next power of two is the simplest brute‑force option but wastes parameters and memory; mixed‑radix Cooley‑Tukey (mixing radix‑3, radix‑2 stages, etc.) is theoretically possible and would build a butterfly-style factorization matching an arbitrary factorization of $d$, but the paper explicitly adopts the pixelated/block generalization (Chen et al.) and parameterizes the $2b\times 2b$ blocks orthogonally. In practice common transformer dims fit neatly: e.g. $768=3\cdot 256$ (use $b=3, t=256, m=\log_2(512)=9$), and $4096$ is already a power of two.

Implementation tips taken from the paper: pick $b$ so that $t=d/b$ is a power of two (often take $b$ = odd part of $d$), set $m=\log_2(2d/b)$, parameterize each active $2b\times 2b$ block with a skew‑symmetric Cayley parameter (so orthogonality is exact and you can initialize to identity), and expand $2\times 2$ rotations to blocks via Kronecker products when you want a simple, low‑parameter choice (as in the small examples above). This is the approach the paper uses for non‑power‑of‑two $d$.

### Are explicit permutation matrices part of BOFT, or is the butterfly pattern achieved without extra permutations?
Question:
Many fast transforms use permutations (bit-reversal, stride permutations) between butterfly stages. The paper mentions that nonzero patterns of butterfly components are permutations of the \(k=2\) case. Are these permutations: (1) fixed, hard-coded reindexings, (2) represented explicitly as learned or fixed permutation matrices, or (3) implicitly realized by how block-diagonal factors are arranged? An explicit statement of whether BOFT includes separate permutation matrices in the product would clarify both expressivity and implementation.
Answer:
Short answer: BOFT does not introduce separate learned permutation matrices. The stagewise “re-wiring” (the bit‑reversal / stride‑style pairings you see in FFT butterflies) is a fixed structural pattern and is realized implicitly by how the block‑diagonal butterfly components are laid out. In other words, BOFT is closest to your option (3): the permutations are built into the placement of the small orthogonal blocks across stages, not represented as extra learned permutation matrices.

Why: the paper writes the BOFT transform as
$$\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)},$$
where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is a block‑diagonal “butterfly component” whose nonzero pattern is a block‑wise permutation of the basic $2\times 2$ (or $2b\times 2b$) pairing pattern. The authors explicitly say “the non‑zero pattern of every butterfly component can be viewed as a permutation of the non‑zero pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$”, and they parameterize the learnable degrees of freedom inside those small orthogonal blocks (via Cayley / skew‑symmetric parameterization). Thus the only learned parameters are the entries (rotation parameters) inside the small blocks; the index re‑pairings between stages are fixed by the chosen butterfly topology.

Implementation note / equivalence: because permutation matrices are themselves orthogonal, you could always factor the same product into explicit permutation matrices times local $2\times 2$ blocks if you wanted. But that would just expose a fixed, non‑learned permutation factor; it adds no learnable parameters and is wasteful to realize as dense matrices. Practically you implement the permutations as fixed index reordering / reshapes (as in FFT implementations) and keep only the small orthogonal blocks as parameterized factors. The paper follows this approach (identity initialization is done by initializing the block skew matrices to zero; multiplicative dropout drops whole blocks/components to identity).

Consequence for expressivity: because the permutations are structural and fixed, BOFT’s expressivity / inductive bias is controlled by the chosen block size $b$ and number of stages $m$; to get universal coverage of the orthogonal group you need richer compositions (the paper mentions kaleidoscope/hierarchical compositions), but those still use fixed stagewise permutations while learning the small orthogonal blocks.

### Unpacking the expressivity theorem: how does the kaleidoscope product span the full orthogonal group?
Question:
Theorem 1 claims BOFT is more expressive than block-diagonal OFT and references a product of butterfly matrices and their transposes (a kaleidoscope hierarchy) that can represent all \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrices when \(m_1 = m_2 = \log d\), \(b_1=b_2=2\), and \(l=d-1\). Could someone provide a proof sketch or an explicit reference explaining why the alternating product \(\mathbf{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\mathbf{B}^\top_{d-1,2}(d)\cdots\mathbf{B}_{1,1}(d)\mathbf{B}^\top_{1,2}(d)\) spans all of \(\mathcal{O}(d)\)? Also, does this require any extra permutations between butterfly factors?
Answer:
Short answer: the claim is a standard “kaleidoscope” / Givens-rotation argument. One shows that (i) the alternating product can realize one perfect matching (a layer of disjoint $2\times 2$ Givens rotations) per repetition, (ii) the complete graph on $d$ vertices can be decomposed into $d-1$ perfect matchings, so $d-1$ such layers provide exactly the right number of continuous parameters, and (iii) those parameters generically span the Lie algebra $so(d)$, hence the family is locally surjective at the identity and—by connectedness—fills $SO(d)$ (and by allowing a single reflection also $O(d)$). Dao et al.’s “kaleidoscope” paper contains the explicit construction and proof; what follows is a short, self-contained sketch of the reasoning behind it.

Think of each $2\times 2$ orthogonal block (a Givens rotation in coordinates $i,j$) as generating a one-parameter subgroup $\exp(\theta E_{ij})$ of $SO(d)$, where $E_{ij}$ is the elementary skew-symmetric matrix with ones in the $(i,j)$ and $(j,i)$ slots (with signs). It is a classical fact (used in QR/Givens algorithms) that such plane rotations generate $SO(d)$: the Lie algebra $so(d)$ is spanned by the set $\{E_{ij} : 1 \le i < j \le d\}$, and an arbitrary orthogonal matrix can be written as a product of plane rotations.

Now build the product from “layers” each consisting of $\frac{d}{2}$ disjoint $2\times 2$ rotations (a perfect matching on the $d$ coordinates). If you have $l$ layers whose matchings together cover every unordered pair $\{i,j\}$ exactly once, then you have introduced exactly $\frac{d}{2}\cdot l$ continuous angle parameters. The complete graph $K_d$ admits a $1$-factorization (a partition of its edges into $d-1$ perfect matchings) when $d$ is even, so choosing $l = d-1$ gives
$$
\frac{d}{2}\cdot (d-1)=\frac{d(d-1)}{2},
$$
which is exactly the (continuous) dimension of $O(d)$ (equivalently $SO(d)$). At the infinitesimal level the derivative of the map from the angles to the matrix at the identity is a linear combination of the conjugates of the elementary skew-symmetric blocks; because each unordered pair occurs in some layer, those conjugates span all $E_{ij}$ and the Jacobian has full rank. By the implicit function theorem the parameterization is locally surjective around the identity; since $SO(d)$ is connected this gives all of $SO(d)$ (and allowing a single reflection in one block gives the full $O(d)$).

Where do butterfly matrices enter? A butterfly matrix is just a product of $\log d$ stages each made of disjoint $2\times 2$ blocks (a special pattern of perfect matchings). Two observations close the circle: (A) any particular perfect matching (or small permutation that reorders wires) can be realized by suitable choices of the $2\times 2$ blocks inside a product of butterfly stages (this is the same routing property used in Beneš/Benes-style networks), and (B) a product of two butterflies (one of them transposed) can be arranged to realize one layer of disjoint $2\times 2$ rotations on an arbitrary perfect matching. Thus each factor pair $B_{i,1}(d) B_{i,2}(d)^\top$ can realize the $i$-th matching in a chosen $1$-factorization. Repeating this for the $d-1$ matchings gives the kaleidoscope product
$$
\mathbf{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\mathbf{B}^\top_{d-1,2}(d)\cdots\mathbf{B}_{1,1}(d)\mathbf{B}^\top_{1,2}(d)
$$
with the required number of free angles, and the Jacobian argument above finishes the proof that the image is the whole orthogonal group. This is exactly the construction and argument given (more formally) in Dao et al. (“Kaleidoscope”) which your paper cites.

About permutations: you do not need to insert extra explicit permutation matrices between butterfly factors. The permutations required to route an arbitrary pair into a given $2\times 2$ block can be realized by choosing certain $2\times 2$ blocks to be swaps (or more generally by the intrinsic routing power of the butterfly/Beneš structure). In some proofs it is convenient to write those routing steps as separate permutation factors (for clarity), but in implementation they are merely special settings of the butterfly $2\times 2$ blocks; the alternating $B$ and $B^\top$ pattern already supplies the needed routing flexibility.

References/places to read next: the Dao et al. “kaleidoscope” construction and proof (cited in your paper) gives the formal combinatorial / differential argument; for the basic facts about Givens rotations and their ability to generate $SO(d)$ see any numerical linear algebra text (e.g. Golub & Van Loan), and for the $1$-factorization / Beneš routing facts see standard combinatorics/network‑routing sources.

### Does left-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserve all singular values, not just the spectral norm?
Question:
The paper emphasizes that BOFT preserves the spectral norm because \(\mathbf{W}^0 = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top\) becomes \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top\) with \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}\) still orthogonal. But then all singular values (not just the largest) are unchanged, right? If so, is there a reason the text highlights only the spectral norm and not the full singular value spectrum being preserved?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — premultiplying (or postmultiplying) a matrix by a square orthogonal matrix preserves the entire singular-value spectrum, not just the largest singular value. The paper singles out the spectral norm because that single number is commonly used in stability / generalization arguments, so it is the most immediate consequence to call out.

Why: if $ \mathbf{W}^0=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top $ is an SVD of $ \mathbf{W}^0 $ and $ \mathbf{R} $ is orthogonal, then
$$
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0 = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U})\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top.
$$
Because $ \mathbf{R} $ and $ \mathbf{U} $ are orthogonal, $ \mathbf{R}\mathbf{U} $ is orthogonal, so the same diagonal $ \mathbf{\Sigma} $ appears in an SVD of $ \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0 $. Equivalently, $ (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)=({\mathbf{W}^0})^\top\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0=({\mathbf{W}^0})^\top\mathbf{W}^0 $, so the eigenvalues of the Gram matrix (and hence the singular values squared) are unchanged. Thus all singular values, the Frobenius norm, the nuclear norm, and any unitarily invariant functional of the singular values are preserved. The left singular vectors are rotated to $ \mathbf{R}\mathbf{U} $, while the right singular vectors $ \mathbf{V} $ and the singular values $ \mathbf{\Sigma} $ remain the same.

Why the paper mentions the spectral norm specifically: the spectral norm (largest singular value) is the operator norm / Lipschitz constant of the linear layer and appears directly in many stability and generalization bounds (see the references the paper cites). Calling out the spectral norm is a compact way to emphasize the training-stability consequence that readers care about, even though the stronger fact (preservation of the whole spectrum) is true and follows from the same simple SVD/Graham argument. A small practical caveat is that this exact invariance requires exact orthogonality (the paper enforces orthogonality analytically via the Cayley parameterization); in finite-precision arithmetic very tiny deviations can occur, but the theoretical property holds.

### How should BOFT’s multiplicative Dropout be scaled to preserve expected activations?
Question:
The proposed multiplicative Dropout randomly replaces a subset of \(2b\times 2b\) orthogonal blocks in some butterfly components by identity. In standard Dropout we scale by \(1/(1-p)\) to preserve the expected activation. Here, should each retained block be scaled to keep \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{R}(m,b)] = \mathbf{I}\) (or some other target), or is no rescaling intended? If no rescaling is used, what is the intuition for why this does not bias training or harm stability?
Answer:
Short answer: No — the paper’s multiplicative Dropout is applied by replacing selected $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks with the identity, and no additional scaling like the usual $\frac{1}{1-p}$ “inverted” Dropout factor is applied. That choice is intentional: scaling would break the orthogonality constraint and destroy the key spectral/stability properties BOFT is designed to preserve, while identity replacement already gives a stable, effective regularizer.

Why scaling would be problematic (technical reason). BOFT enforces each block/component to be orthogonal (via Cayley / $2\times 2$ rotations). If a retained block were multiplied by a scalar $s \neq 1$ to correct the expectation, that scaled block would no longer be orthogonal (its singular values would become $s$), so you would lose the orthogonality constraint and the spectral-norm preservation that follows from it. There is no simple way to multiply a surviving orthogonal block by a scalar and still remain inside the orthogonal manifold used by BOFT.

Why not rescale is acceptable (intuitive + first‑order math). Let each butterfly component be an orthogonal matrix $B_j$, and let the training-time random block-masked version be
$$
\tilde{B}_j = \begin{cases}
B_j & \text{with prob } q = 1 - p,\\
I & \text{with prob } p.
\end{cases}
$$
A sampled BOFT transform is $R_{\text{mask}} = \prod_j \tilde{B}_j$. In general $E[R_{\text{mask}}] \neq I$, so exact expectation-preservation would be violated if that were your metric. However two facts make this benign in BOFT: (1) each sampled $R_{\text{mask}}$ is itself orthogonal (product of orthogonals and identities), hence $\|R_{\text{mask}}\|_2 = 1$ always, so the operator norm (and therefore activation magnitudes) are not amplified or collapsed by the dropout sampling; (2) BOFT is identity-initialized ($B_j$ starts as $I$), so early in training each $B_j = I + \Delta_j$ with small $\Delta_j$ and a first-order expansion gives $E[R_{\text{mask}}] \approx I + q\sum_j \Delta_j + O(\Delta^2)$. In words, the expected deviation from identity is small at the start and grows smoothly with training. Because per-sample transforms preserve norm, there is no instability from scaling bias even though $E[R]$ is not exactly $I$.

Connection to broader regularizers. Multiplicative Dropout in BOFT behaves more like structured DropPath / stochastic-depth style regularization than like standard neuronwise “inverted” Dropout where you scale activations to preserve expectation. The goal here is to regularize the orthogonal transform by occasionally forcing subblocks to be identity (i.e., removing some structured interactions) while keeping each training sample’s transform orthogonal. That preserves the spectral stability advantage of orthogonal finetuning, and the learned full transform (with dropout off at evaluation) is then the ensemble-trained, regularized transform.

Practical implication. Do not try to multiply surviving orthogonal blocks by $\frac{1}{1-p}$ inside the Cayley/orthogonal parameterization — that breaks orthogonality. The usual practice is to apply the multiplicative mask only during training (identity replacements for dropped blocks) and use the learned full $R$ (no mask) at evaluation; this is consistent with the paper’s description (identity initialization + block replacement during training) and preserves BOFT’s stability and spectral properties.

If you really require exact expectation-matching you would need a different parameterization (for example adding a compensating scalar factor and then projecting back to the orthogonal manifold, at extra computation and lost simplicity), but the authors chose the identity-replacement approach because it preserves orthogonality and the desirable stability/generalization behavior described in the paper.

### What is the computational overhead of BOFT during training, asymptotically and in practice?
Question:
In training, BOFT forms \(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\) as a product of \(m\) sparse orthogonal butterfly components and applies it to the left of \(\mathbf{W}^0\). What is the per-layer complexity to multiply \(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\) by a vector and by \(\mathbf{W}^0\) during forward/backward passes? Asymptotically, is it \(\mathcal{O}(dm)\) per application (e.g., \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) for the butterfly case)? In practice, how does this overhead compare to LoRA with rank \(r\) in typical transformer sizes, and are there recommended ways to implement \(\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{x}\) efficiently without materializing dense matrices?
Answer:
Short answer up front: yes — asymptotically applying the BOFT orthogonal factor $R(m,b)$ to a $d$‑vector costs linear in $d$ times the number of factors, and multiplying $R$ into a full weight matrix naively costs an extra factor of $n$ (the output dim). Concretely, applying $R$ (as a product of $m$ block‑butterfly components of block‑size $b$) to a $d$‑vector is $O(m d b)$ (for the usual small $b$ this is $O(m d)$; for the canonical butterfly $b=2$ and $m = O(\log d)$ this is $O(d \log d)$). Naively forming $R W^0$ costs $O(m d b n)$, but you should avoid that in training: compute $R^T x$ first and then use the existing $(W^0)^T$ multiply, so the extra runtime over the frozen layer is only $O(m d b)$ per input. Backpropagation has the same asymptotic scaling (you need to push gradients through the $m$ factors and accumulate parameter gradients), so training overhead is roughly another $O(m d b)$ (constants depend on implementation), i.e. forward+back $\approx$ a small constant times $m d b$ extra work per example.

Why this is true (intuitive + technical): each BOFT component is block‑diagonal into $d/b$ blocks of size $b\times b$. Applying a single component to a vector means doing $d/b$ small $b\times b$ multiplies, costing $O\left((d/b)\cdot b^2\right)=O(d b)$. Doing $m$ components in sequence gives $O(m d b)$. For the common $2\times 2$ block ($b=2$) used in the radix‑2 butterfly, each component is just $d/2$ two‑element pairwise transforms, so one component is $O(d)$ and $m$ components are $O(m d)$ (for $m\approx\log_2 d$ you get the familiar $O(d \log d)$ cost). If you instead multiply each sparse factor into $W^0$ (a dense $d\times n$ matrix) you pay $O(b^2 n)$ per block times $d/b$ blocks, i.e. $O(d b n)$ per factor and $O(m d b n)$ total — that is why you should not materialize $R W^0$ during training.

Comparison to LoRA (rank $r$), typical transformer sizes: LoRA implements an additive low‑rank update $A B$ ($A$: $d\times r$, $B$: $r\times n$). The extra per‑example work LoRA does (beyond the frozen $(W^0)^T x$) is computing $A^T x$ ($O(d r)$) and $B^T$ (that result) ($O(r n)$) — overall $O(r(d+n))$ extra ops per example (for $n\approx d$ this is $\approx 2 r d$). BOFT (with small $b$) adds $\approx m d b$ ($\approx m d$ for $b=2$). Therefore for $b=2$ the crossover is when $2 r d \gtrless m d$, i.e. $r \gtrless m/2$: if LoRA’s rank $r$ is larger than about $m/2$ then LoRA will cost more compute than BOFT; if $r$ is smaller it will cost less. Put another way, for a typical hidden size $d=4096$, $m \approx \log_2 d = 12$ so $BOFT(b=2)$ adds $\approx 12\cdot 4096 \approx 49\text{k}$ multiply‑adds per token; LoRA with $r=8$ adds $\approx 2\cdot 8\cdot 4096 \approx 66\text{k}$ (LoRA heavier), while LoRA with $r=4$ adds $\approx 33\text{k}$ (LoRA lighter). Parameter counts follow the same tradeoff: BOFT’s parameter count per linear layer is $\frac{1}{2}(b-1) d m$ (so for $b=2$ this is $0.5 d m$), while LoRA stores roughly $r(d+n)$ parameters ($\approx 2 r d$ if $n\approx d$). For the $d=4096$, $m=12$ example BOFT parameters $\approx 24.6\text{k}$; LoRA $r=8$ $\approx 65.5\text{k}$; LoRA $r=4$ $\approx 32.8\text{k}$.

Practical implementation advice (avoid materializing dense $R$ and keep it fast): implement $R^T x$ as a sequence of $m$ small block transforms (the paper’s construction). For $b=2$ you can implement each stage as a vectorized pairwise rotation: reshape the batch of vectors into shape $(\text{batch}, d/2, 2)$ and do a batched $2\times 2$ multiply with per‑pair parameters (or store per‑pair angles and compute $\cos/\sin$). For larger $b$ do the analogous reshape to $(\text{batch}, d/b, b)$ and batched $b\times b$ multiplies. These implementations are memory‑light and use $O(m d b)$ work; they avoid the $O(m d b n)$ blowup from materializing $R W^0$. To get good throughput on GPUs you should fuse or batch the tiny matmuls — e.g. use torch.bmm over $(\text{batch}\cdot (d/b))$ small matrices or write a fused CUDA kernel — because many tiny kernels (one $2\times 2$ multiply per pair) can be wasteful if launched individually. For butterfly connectivity that includes permutations between stages, implement those permutations with reshape/transpose or a single index permutation per stage (again batched), which is cheaper than forming dense matrices. For the orthogonality parameterization you can use the simple $2\times 2$ rotation parameterization (angle per block) or the Cayley parameterization applied blockwise; both are block‑local and efficient because $(I - Q)^{-1}$ is trivial for tiny blocks.

Backward pass note: backprop needs to run through the same $m$ stages in reverse (apply transposed blocks to the gradient) and to accumulate parameter gradients from the local inputs/outputs at each block. Asymptotically this is the same $O(m d b)$ scaling; in practice the backward will have a modest constant factor (you’ll see something like $\sim 2\times$ the forward per‑stage work for the transposes and extra reductions to form parameter gradients).

Bottom line and rule of thumb: asymptotically BOFT costs $O(m d b)$ to apply to a vector (so butterfly BOFT is $O(d \log d)$). Do not multiply $R$ into $W^0$; instead compute $y = R^T x$ and feed $y$ through the frozen $(W^0)^T$. Compared to LoRA, BOFT’s extra cost scales with $m$ ($\approx \log d$ for the full butterfly) whereas LoRA scales with $r$; for realistic $d$ (thousands) and common LoRA ranks the two are comparable and BOFT is often cheaper when LoRA uses ranks in the mid/high single‑digits ($r \gtrsim m/2$). The practical performance depends on batched sizes and kernel efficiency, so implement $R^T x$ as batched small‑block multiplies (or a fused kernel) to realize the asymptotic savings in wall time.

### How are OFT/BOFT related to LoRA in the small-update regime?
Question:
With the Cayley parameterization \(\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) and \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\) small, we can expand \(\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q} + 2\mathbf{Q}^2 + \cdots\). Then \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0 - \mathbf{W}^0 \approx 2\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}^0\) to first order. Is there a way to interpret this as a low-rank update like LoRA if \(\mathbf{Q}\) is additionally constrained to be low-rank? Conversely, can LoRA be seen as a special case of OFT/BOFT under some constraints and reparameterizations?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — to first order (the “small‑update” / tangent plane view) OFT/BOFT reduce to an additive low‑rank update and so can look like LoRA if the Cayley generator $\mathbf{Q}$ is low‑rank. But important structural constraints (skew‑symmetry, columnwise orthogonality, and the fact that the additive piece depends on $\mathbf{W}^0$) mean that LoRA is not, in general, an exact special case of OFT/BOFT except under restrictive conditions.

Why: start from the Cayley parameterization used in the paper, $$ \mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1} $$ with $\mathbf{Q}^\top=-\mathbf{Q}$ and $\|\mathbf{Q}\|\ll 1$. Expanding $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}= \mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{Q}^2+\cdots$ gives $$ \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q} + 2\mathbf{Q}^2 + \cdots, $$ so the weight change is $$ \Delta\mathbf{W} \equiv \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0 - \mathbf{W}^0 \approx 2\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}^0 $$ to first order. This is an additive update (like LoRA) whose leading term is the left‑multiplication of $\mathbf{W}^0$ by $\mathbf{Q}$.

If $\mathbf{Q}$ is low rank then $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}^0$ is low rank: by the usual rank bound $\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}^0)\le \min(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{Q}),\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{W}^0))$. Concretely, any real skew‑symmetric low‑rank $\mathbf{Q}$ can be written as a sum of rank‑2 skew outer products, $$ \mathbf{Q}=\sum_{t=1}^k \big(u_t v_t^\top - v_t u_t^\top\big), $$ so $$ \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}^0=\sum_{t=1}^k \big(u_t (v_t^\top \mathbf{W}^0) - v_t (u_t^\top \mathbf{W}^0)\big), $$ a sum of at most $2k$ rank‑1 matrices. Thus, in the small‑$\mathbf{Q}$ regime OFT/BOFT with low‑rank (or effectively low‑rank) generators produces an additive low‑rank correction of the same flavour as LoRA.

Why this is not full equivalence (important caveats)
1. Skew‑symmetry / columnwise orthogonality constraint. For any skew‑symmetric $\mathbf{Q}$ and any column $w_j$ of $\mathbf{W}^0$ the update column $\delta_j\approx 2\mathbf{Q}w_j$ satisfies $$ w_j^\top \delta_j \approx 2 w_j^\top \mathbf{Q} w_j = 0. $$ So the first‑order OFT update changes each column only in a direction orthogonal to the original column. A generic LoRA update $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ does not have to satisfy $w_j^\top (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})_{:,j}=0$, and therefore need not be realizable as $2\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}^0$ for any skew‑symmetric $\mathbf{Q}$. This is a strong structural constraint you get from orthogonality that LoRA does not impose.

2. Dependence on $\mathbf{W}^0$. The OFT linearized update is $2\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}^0$, so the additive correction is tied to $\mathbf{W}^0$. LoRA's parameterization $\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}^0+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ is free (the low‑rank term does not explicitly depend on $\mathbf{W}^0$). Thus LoRA can represent corrections outside the image of the linear map $\mathbf{Q}\mapsto \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}^0$.

3. Higher‑order terms and spectral constraints. The exact multiplicative OFT update contains higher orders ($2\mathbf{Q}^2\mathbf{W}^0+\cdots$). Even when the first‑order pieces match, the multiplicative update differs from a fixed additive LoRA update once $\mathbf{Q}$ is not infinitesimal. Moreover, OFT/BOFT preserve singular values of $\mathbf{W}^0$ exactly (since left‑multiplication by an orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ just rotates left singular vectors), while a LoRA additive update generally changes the singular values; this is a qualitative inductive bias highlighted in the paper and important for stability/generalization.

What about BOFT specifically? BOFT parameterizes $\mathbf{R}$ as a product of sparse orthogonal "butterfly" factors. If every factor is near identity then each factor's Cayley generator is small and the product linearizes to $\mathbf{R}\approx \mathbf{I}+2\sum_i \mathbf{Q}_i$, so BOFT's first‑order correction is again of the form $2(\sum_i\mathbf{Q}_i)\mathbf{W}^0$. If the composite generator $\sum_i\mathbf{Q}_i$ is low‑rank (or well approximated by a low‑rank matrix) then BOFT will behave like a LoRA low‑rank additive update in the small‑step regime. In practice BOFT imposes sparse/butterfly structure (not explicitly low rank), so whether the small‑step update is low rank depends on how those sparse rotations combine; BOFT subsumes the block‑diagonal OFT as the paper notes, but it does not reduce to an unconstrained LoRA in general.

When can LoRA be realized exactly by (linearized) OFT? Solving $2\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}^0=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ for a skew‑symmetric $\mathbf{Q}$ imposes the columnwise orthogonality conditions above. If those conditions hold (and dimension counting / rank conditions permit), you can find a skew‑symmetric $\mathbf{Q}$ (usually non‑unique) that produces the same first‑order change. In general this is a restrictive subset of all LoRA updates, so LoRA is not a generic special case of OFT/BOFT.

Takeaway: think of OFT/BOFT and LoRA as two different structured tangent models for small changes of a pretrained weight: LoRA is an unconstrained additive low‑rank tangent, OFT/BOFT is an orthogonality‑constrained multiplicative tangent whose first‑order term can be low‑rank if you force the Cayley generator to be low‑rank. They coincide approximately when (i) updates are tiny, (ii) the skew generator is low‑rank, and (iii) the LoRA correction satisfies the skew/orthogonality compatibility (columnwise orthogonality) — otherwise they carry different inductive biases (spectral preservation, dependence on $\mathbf{W}^0$, sparsity vs. low rank) and are not equivalent.

### Why can’t a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix approximate classic transforms like the DFT?
Question:
The paper argues that block-diagonal OFT introduces undesirable inductive bias and cannot approximate classic linear transforms (e.g., DFT), whereas butterfly-structured BOFT can. Could someone formalize this limitation? Is it because any block-diagonal matrix preserves independent subspaces that cannot mix coordinates across blocks, and the DFT is dense and highly mixing? A short proof or clear argument would help.
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — your intuition is exactly right. A block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix enforces invariant coordinate blocks and therefore cannot (in general) produce the dense, across‑block mixing that transforms like the DFT (or Hadamard/DCT) require. One can make this precise with a simple lower bound on the approximation error.

Let $d = r b$ and let $F$ be a normalized dense orthonormal transform (for concreteness take the unitary DFT normalized so every entry has magnitude $1/\sqrt{d}$, or a real analogue like the Hadamard/DCT). Let
$R=\operatorname{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r)$ be block‑diagonal with $r$ blocks of size $b$ (each $R_i$ orthogonal). Two immediately useful facts are: (i) every off‑block entry of $R$ is exactly zero, and (ii) every entry of $F$ has magnitude $1/\sqrt{d}$.

Entrywise (Frobenius) lower bound. Count the forced zeros of $R$: there are
$d^2 - r b^2 = d^2 - d b$ off‑block positions. Because each corresponding entry of $F$ has squared magnitude $1/d$, any block‑diagonal $R$ satisfies
$$
\|R-F\|_F^2 \;\ge\; \sum_{\text{off‑block }(i,j)} |0-F_{ij}|^2
    \;=\; (d^2-d b)\cdot\frac{1}{d} \;=\; d-b.
$$
Thus
$$
\|R-F\|_F \ge \sqrt{\,d-b\,},\qquad
\frac{\|R-F\|_F}{\|F\|_F} \ge \sqrt{1-\frac{b}{d}},
$$
since $\|F\|_F=\sqrt{d}$. So unless $b$ is comparable to $d$ (i.e. you give up the parameter savings), the Frobenius error is large. For example, with $d=512$ and $b=16$ the lower bound is $\sqrt{496}\approx 22.3$ while $\|F\|_F=\sqrt{512}\approx 22.6$, so the approximation is basically hopeless.

Operator‑norm (spectral) bound. Pick a basis vector $e_j$ inside some block. The column $F e_j$ has its $\ell_2$ mass spread evenly across all $d$ coordinates, so the fraction of mass inside that one block is exactly $b/d$. Any unit vector supported inside that block (which is where $R e_j$ must lie) has inner product with $F e_j$ of at most $\sqrt{b/d}$ by Cauchy–Schwarz. Hence
$$
\|R e_j - F e_j\|_2^2 = 2 - 2\Re\langle R e_j, F e_j\rangle
    \ge 2 - 2\sqrt{\frac{b}{d}},
$$
so
$$
\|R-F\|_2 \ge \sqrt{2-2\sqrt{b/d}}.
$$
Again, for small $b/d$ this is close to the maximal possible difference between two unit vectors, so block‑diagonal $R$ cannot approximate $F$ in operator norm either.

Intuition / invariant‑subspace viewpoint. A block‑diagonal matrix is exactly an orthogonal map that preserves the direct sum decomposition $\mathbb{R}^d=V_1\oplus\cdots\oplus V_r$ (each $V_i$ the coordinate subspace for a block). That is an enormous structural constraint: no information ever flows between different $V_i$. Dense transforms like the DFT/Hadamard have columns whose support is spread over all coordinate indices, so they do not respect any nontrivial coordinate block decomposition. From the paper’s “information transmission” graph viewpoint, block‑diagonal factorization has no edges between groups and so cannot create the full set of paths needed to connect every input coordinate to every output coordinate; the butterfly topology, by contrast, builds those cross‑block paths with only $O(d\log d)$ sparse factors (this is exactly why Cooley–Tukey decomposes the DFT into butterfly factors).

Bottom line: block‑diagonal OFT forces independent subspaces and therefore has a provable, quantitative lower bound on how well it can match dense, fully mixing transforms (Frobenius and spectral norms give simple, interpretable bounds). To represent or closely approximate DFT‑style mixing you need either blocks of size $b\approx d$ (no parameter saving) or a factorization like the butterfly that creates cross‑block mixing while remaining parameter‑efficient — which is the gap BOFT is designed to close.

### Clarifying the edge-count example: how does a butterfly on \(d=4\) achieve dense connectivity with 8 edges?
Question:
The paper states that in the information-transmission view, a naive dense connection for \(d=4\) needs 12 edges, the example factorization uses 10 edges, and a butterfly network uses 8 edges. Could someone explicitly draw or list the edges for the \(d=4\) butterfly case across all levels, and show how they imply that every source node reaches every sink node? I am trying to match this to the corresponding matrix sparsity patterns.
Answer:
Short answer: the $d=4$ radix-2 butterfly has two stages ($m = \log_2 4 = 2$). Stage‑1 mixes the pairs $(1,2)$ and $(3,4)$; stage‑2 mixes the pairs $(1,3)$ and $(2,4)$. If you write the two $4\times4$ stage matrices with their nonzero patterns and multiply them, the product is generically dense. The paper’s “$8$ edges” count is obtained by counting the extra (nontrivial) nonzeros beyond the $d$ minimal edges per adjacency: each stage has $8$ nonzeros, so $(8-4)+(8-4)=8$. Below I list the edges, show the matrices, and show the product is full.

Label the nodes as Level‑0 sources $s_1,s_2,s_3,s_4$, Level‑1 intermediate nodes $A,B,C,D$ (after stage‑1), and Level‑2 sinks $t_1,t_2,t_3,t_4$ (after stage‑2). The stage connectivity (directed edges between adjacent levels) is:

Stage 1 (Level‑0 $\to$ Level‑1): the two $2\times2$ blocks mix $(1,2)$ and $(3,4)$, so the edges are $s_1\to A$, $s_1\to B$, $s_2\to A$, $s_2\to B$, $s_3\to C$, $s_3\to D$, $s_4\to C$, $s_4\to D$.
Stage 2 (Level‑1 $\to$ Level‑2): the two $2\times2$ blocks mix $(A,C)$ and $(B,D)$, so the edges are $A\to t_1$, $A\to t_3$, $C\to t_1$, $C\to t_3$, $B\to t_2$, $B\to t_4$, $D\to t_2$, $D\to t_4$.

Write these as sparse matrices (use lowercase for the first stage, uppercase for the second). Stage‑1 (block‑diagonal mixing of $(1,2)$ and $(3,4)$):

$$
B_1=\begin{pmatrix}
a & b & 0 & 0\\[2pt]
c & d & 0 & 0\\[2pt]
0 & 0 & e & f\\[2pt]
0 & 0 & g & h
\end{pmatrix},
$$

and stage‑2 (mixing $(1,3)$ and $(2,4)$ in the interleaved pattern):

$$
B_2=\begin{pmatrix}
p & 0 & q & 0\\[2pt]
0 & r & 0 & s\\[2pt]
t & 0 & u & 0\\[2pt]
0 & v & 0 & w
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Each $2\times2$ block (e.g. $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\ c&d\end{pmatrix}$) is orthogonal, so its four entries are constrained but typically nonzero (one free angle parameter per $2\times2$ rotation when using Cayley/rotation parametrization).

Now multiply $R = B_2 B_1$. Multiplying symbolically gives the four rows

$$
R=\begin{pmatrix}
p a & p b & q e & q f\\[2pt]
r c & r d & s g & s h\\[2pt]
t a & t b & u e & u f\\[2pt]
v c & v d & w g & w h
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For generic (nonzero) block entries the $8$ products above are nonzero, so every entry $R_{ij}$ is a sum of nonzero products and hence generically nonzero; i.e. $R$ is dense. Concretely, take source $s_1$: through stage‑1 it reaches intermediate nodes $A$ and $B$; from $A$ it reaches sinks $t_1$ and $t_3$, from $B$ it reaches $t_2$ and $t_4$, so $s_1$ can reach all four sinks. The same argument (by symmetry of the pattern) holds for $s_2,s_3,s_4$, so each source can reach every sink in two hops.

About the paper’s edge counts: the authors count the “extra” nontrivial edges beyond the $d$ minimal edges required to make each adjacency full‑rank. For $d=4$ a single dense mapping (one adjacency) has $16$ nonzeros, and their convention subtracts $4$ trivial edges (giving $12$). For the two‑stage butterfly above each stage has $8$ nonzeros, so the paper reports $(8-4)+(8-4)=8$ edges for the factorization. Note this “edge” count is a count of nonzero positions (connectivity) in the factor matrices, not the number of independent trainable scalar parameters: each orthogonal $2\times2$ block has only one free parameter (a rotation angle), so $BOFT(b=2, m=2)$ has $\frac{d m}{2} = 4$ free parameters in total even though there are $16$ nonzero entries across the two stages.

In short: explicitly listing the stage connections shows the two $2\times2$ mixing layers produce the sparse patterns above; multiplying them yields the dense matrix $R$ shown, and the paper’s “$8$ edges” corresponds to the extra (nontrivial) nonzeros per their counting convention.

### Where does the formula \(m = \log\tfrac{2d}{b}\) come from for BOFT to produce a dense \(\mathbf{R}\)?
Question:
The text says BOFT\((\log\tfrac{2d}{b}, b)\) with block butterfly factors yields a dense orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\). Could someone derive this \(m\) as a function of \(d\) and \(b\) from the recursive definition of the block butterfly components? A step-by-step argument showing that after \(m\) stages all input coordinates can influence all outputs would be great.
Answer:
Short answer: the factor of $\log$ comes from the binary (factor‑2) mixing in a butterfly and the factor of $2/b$ comes from how BOFT groups coordinates into block “lanes”. If you treat the smallest mixing unit as a lane of size $r=\tfrac{b}{2}$, there are $L=\dfrac{d}{r}=\dfrac{2d}{b}$ lanes and each butterfly stage doubles the number of lanes any single input can reach; to reach all lanes you need
$$
m=\log_2 L=\log_2\!\frac{2d}{b}
$$
stages (use $\lceil\cdot\rceil$ if $2d/b$ is not an exact power of two).

Derivation and intuition (step‑by‑step). BOFT is built by replacing the scalar $2\times 2$ “butterfly” atoms of the Cooley–Tukey FFT by block atoms. Concretely, the recursive factors are block‑diagonal matrices whose smallest nontrivial blocks mix two adjacent block‑lanes at a time (a $2\times 2$ structure, but each scalar entry is itself a small matrix). Let the smallest physical vector chunk that gets paired in these $2\times 2$ atoms be of size
$$
r=\frac{b}{2}.
$$
Partition the $d$ coordinates into $L=d/r$ such lanes, so
$$
L=\frac{d}{r}=\frac{2d}{b}.
$$
A single butterfly stage pairs adjacent lanes (so each input lane can reach its partner lane as well). After two stages an input lane can reach four lanes, after three stages eight lanes, and so on: after $t$ stages one input lane can reach exactly $2^t$ lanes. This doubling is the core butterfly property and is proved by a trivial induction: it holds at $t=0$, and one more stage pairs each currently reachable lane with another disjoint set of lanes, doubling the total.

To achieve dense connectivity (every input coordinate can influence every output coordinate) we therefore require that the reach after $m$ stages covers all $L$ lanes:
$$
2^m \ge L \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m \ge \log_2 L = \log_2\!\frac{2d}{b}.
$$
Taking the smallest integer satisfying this gives $m=\lceil\log_2(2d/b)\rceil$, and when the paper writes $m=\log\frac{2d}{b}$ it implicitly assumes the power‑of‑two setting so the logarithm is integral.

Checks / examples. If $b=2$ (the usual scalar butterfly) then $m=\log_2 d$ as in FFT. If $b=d$ (one block covering all dimensions) then $m=\log_2 2=1$, corresponding to the single full orthogonal block (the original OFT). For $d=8$, $b=4$ we have $r=2$, $L=4$, so $m=\log_2 4=2$ stages — after two block‑butterfly layers every input coordinate can reach every output.

Connection to the paper. This is exactly the information‑transmission picture the authors use: lanes = nodes per level, each stage is a layer of edges that pair lanes, and the butterfly’s binary doubling of reach gives the logarithmic depth. The extra factor of $2$ in $2d/b$ comes from the paper’s block‑to‑atom bookkeeping (they use blocks of size $b$ but the atomic $2\times 2$ mixing acts on halves of those blocks), which is why the formula reads $\log_2(2d/b)$ rather than $\log_2(d/b)$.

### Is \(\mathbf{R}\) shared across layers or is there one \(\mathbf{R}\) per layer?
Question:
In one place the paper contrasts BOFT with "butterfly-based sparse training" by stating that BOFT transforms weights with layer-shared weight matrices. Elsewhere, OFT is described as learning an orthogonal matrix for a given layer. I am confused: in practice, do we learn a distinct \(\mathbf{R}\) for each layer (e.g., per attention projection and MLP matrix), or is a single \(\mathbf{R}\) shared across multiple layers? Clarifying this for LLMs and ViTs would help interpret parameter counts and results.
Answer:
Short answer: in this paper each orthogonal transform $\mathbf{R}$ is learned per linear layer (i.e. one $\mathbf{R}$ that is applied to all neurons/columns of that layer). The phrase “layer‑shared” in the text means “shared across the neurons inside a layer” (the same $\mathbf{R}$ is applied to the whole layer), not “a single $\mathbf{R}$ shared across different layers”.

Why: the paper’s OFT formulation explicitly treats $\mathbf{R}$ as a transform for a single pretrained linear layer $\mathbf{W}^0$: they write that OFT “optimizes an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ for a pretrained linear layer $\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$” and changes the forward pass from $\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{x}$ to $\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{x}$. BOFT keeps that per‑layer reparameterization and replaces $\mathbf{R}$ by a product of butterfly components: the forward pass in BOFT is written as
$$
\mathbf{z}=\big(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\,\mathbf{W}^0\big)^\top\mathbf{x},\qquad
\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
$$
which again is applied to a single (frozen) $\mathbf{W}^0$. The paper also states the BOFT parameter count “for finetuning a linear layer of size $d\times n$” which makes clear the accounting is per layer.

Practical implication for counting parameters in LLMs / ViTs: each linear layer you choose to finetune gets its own $\mathbf{R}$ (whose size is governed by that layer’s input dimension $d$). BOFT’s advertised per‑layer parameter cost is
$$
\text{params}_{\text{per layer}}=\tfrac12 (b-1)\,d\,m,
$$
where $m$ and $b$ are the BOFT hyperparameters (for the radix‑2 butterfly you often take $m\approx\log_2 d$, $b=2$, giving $\approx \tfrac12 d\log_2 d$ trainable scalars per layer). The total BOFT parameter budget for a model is simply the sum of that per‑layer cost over all layers where you apply BOFT (or OFT). For example, with $d=1024$, $m=10$, $b=2$ you get $\tfrac12\cdot1024\cdot10=5120$ parameters for each $\mathbf{R}$ you place; if you put one $\mathbf{R}$ on every transformer sub‑layer you will multiply that by the number of such sub‑layers.

Could you instead tie a single $\mathbf{R}$ across multiple layers? Yes, it’s a possible engineering choice (it would save parameters) but it is not the paper’s default and would change the intended per‑layer angle‑preserving property of OFT/BOFT; the paper presents the per‑layer $\mathbf{R}$ design and reports parameter counts accordingly. If you need to verify exactly which matrices the authors finetuned in a particular experiment, check the experiment/appendix or released code (the paper’s formulas and per‑layer cost make the bookkeeping straightforward).

### Numerical stability of Cayley parameterization in BOFT: how is \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) computed safely?
Question:
The Cayley transform requires inverting \(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\). Even though \(\mathbf{Q}\) is skew-symmetric, \(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\) could become ill-conditioned during training. What implementation details ensure stability? For example: (1) is \(\mathbf{Q}\) implicitly blocked into independent \(2\times 2\) (or \(2b\times 2b\)) blocks so inverses are cheap and stable, (2) is there a trust-region or norm constraint on \(\mathbf{Q}\), or (3) do you use an alternative like the exponential map \(\exp(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}^\top)\)?
Answer:
Short answer: BOFT avoids the usual large-matrix instability in practice by applying the Cayley (or rotation) map at the small block level, initializing blocks to identity, and using multiplicative dropout; mathematically (and usefully) for any real skew‑symmetric $Q$ the factor $(I-Q)$ is never singular and has a simple spectral lower bound, so the inversion itself is not the pathological source of instability.

Why that works (intuitively + a bit of algebra). The paper parameterizes each butterfly component as a block-diagonal matrix whose smallest blocks are $2\times 2$ (and the authors also allow block sizes $2b\times 2b$). Each such small block is made orthogonal by a Cayley parameterization (or, for the $2\times 2$ case, by a direct 2‑D rotation). Working blockwise keeps all linear algebra local and cheap, and it sidesteps inverting one big $d\times d$ system every step.

A key linear‑algebra fact you can use to see why $(I-Q)$ is safe: when $Q$ is skew‑symmetric ($Q^T = -Q$) one has
$$
(I-Q)^{T}(I-Q) \;=\; I - Q^2.
$$
Because $Q$ has purely imaginary eigenvalues, $Q^2$ is symmetric negative semidefinite, so $I-Q^2$ is positive definite and its eigenvalues are $\{1+\mu_j^2\}$ (where $\{\pm i\mu_j\}$ are $Q$’s eigenvalues). Therefore every singular value of $(I-Q)$ satisfies
$$
\sigma_j(I-Q)=\sqrt{1+\mu_j^2}\ge 1,
$$
so $(I-Q)$ is always invertible and has no tiny singular values that would directly cause catastrophic division-by‑zero. In the $2\times 2$ block case this is especially transparent: with
$$
Q=\begin{bmatrix}0 & q\\ -q & 0\end{bmatrix},\qquad
(I-Q)^{-1}=\frac{1}{1+q^2}\begin{bmatrix}1 & q\\ -q & 1\end{bmatrix},
$$
and the Cayley output equals the rotation with $\tan(\theta/2)=q$. So for $2\times 2$ blocks you can either compute the closed form inverse (cheap and numerically trivial) or simply parametrize the block by an angle $\theta$ (no matrix inverse needed).

What the paper actually does versus the three options you raised. The paper explicitly says they (i) parameterize the butterfly components as many small orthogonal blocks (the base is $2\times 2$, generalized to $2b\times 2b$), and they use the Cayley transform (or 2‑D rotations) to make those blocks orthogonal. So yes, inverses are done at the block level (cheap and stable). The paper does not propose an explicit trust‑region or norm clipping on $Q$; instead it uses identity initialization (skew‑symmetric parameters initialized to zero so $Q=0$ and $I-Q=I$ at start) and multiplicative dropout that randomly replaces some blocks by identity during training. Those serve as the practical stabilization/regularization mechanisms the authors report. The paper does not use the matrix exponential parameterization; it lists Cayley (or 2‑D rotation) as the chosen parameterizations—exponential maps are a valid alternative in principle, but more costly and not the approach taken in this work.

Practical implications / implementation notes (kept consistent with the paper). Implement $(I-Q)^{-1}$ per block (for $2\times 2$ use closed form or angle parameterization), initialize skew‑symmetric blocks to zero (identity $R$ initially), and apply multiplicative dropout on butterfly components/blocks as described in the paper. If you are worried about extreme parameter excursions in your training run, simple engineering measures (monitor block norms, gradient clipping, or an explicit small weight decay on the skew parameters) are reasonable extra safeguards, but they go beyond what the paper reports.

In short: BOFT avoids the large, global inversion problem by working with many small Cayley blocks (or rotations), starts at identity, and uses multiplicative dropout — and, crucially, because $Q$ is skew‑symmetric the algebra guarantees $(I-Q)$ is always invertible with favorable lower spectral bounds, so the inversion itself is not a fundamental numerical failure mode.

### What exactly is the “weight interpolation” property of BOFT and how is it computed?
Question:
The paper mentions an "intriguing weight interpolation" (referring to a figure) that comes from the matrix factorization. Could someone explain what is being interpolated? Is it an interpolation along the product path of \(\mathbf{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\), e.g., linearly interpolating between partial products, or something like geodesic interpolation on the orthogonal group? How is this property used in practice (e.g., for model selection or regularization)?
Answer:
Short answer: the “weight interpolation” the paper refers to is the canonical path from the identity matrix to the learned BOFT orthogonal matrix that is induced by the factorized representation. Because BOFT represents the final orthogonal update as a product of orthogonal factors
$$
\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
$$
you immediately get a family of intermediate orthogonal matrices by (a) taking partial products (discrete steps along the product path) and (b) turning each factor on continuously by scaling its Cayley parameter (a cheap continuous path that stays on the orthogonal group). This is a multiplicative (product-path) interpolation, not a naive linear mix of full matrices, and it is the interpretation behind the paper’s remarks about a “smoother interpolation between ... identity matrices (regularity) and the full orthogonal group (expressivity).”

How to compute it (two natural, practical choices that follow directly from the paper)

1) Discrete/product-path interpolation (most direct). Take the first k factors and form the partial product
$$
\mathbf{R}_k \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^k \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)},\qquad k=0,1,\dots,m,
$$
with $\mathbf{R}_0=\mathbf{I}$ and $\mathbf{R}_m=\mathbf{R}(m,b)$. Each $\mathbf{R}_k$ is orthogonal (product of orthogonal blocks), and this gives a simple discrete trajectory from identity to the learned transform. The paper’s multiplicative-dropout (replacing components or blocks by identity during training) is exactly a randomized use of this idea and makes it straightforward to examine / regularize intermediate-capacity transforms.

2) Continuous Cayley-scaling interpolation (smooth path staying on $O(d)$). BOFT uses Cayley parameterization for each orthogonal factor: for factor $i$ there is a skew-symmetric matrix $Q_i$ with
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)} = (I+Q_i)(I-Q_i)^{-1}.
$$
A cheap continuous interpolation is to scale the skew-symmetric parameter by $\alpha\in[0,1]$,
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}(\alpha) \;=\; (I+\alpha Q_i)(I-\alpha Q_i)^{-1},
$$
and then assemble
$$
\mathbf{R}(\alpha) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}(\alpha).
$$
For $\alpha=0$ you get $\mathbf{R}(0)=I$; for $\alpha=1$ you recover the trained BOFT matrix. Each $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}(\alpha)$ remains orthogonal (Cayley transform preserves orthogonality for skew-symmetric $Q_i$ when $(I-\alpha Q_i)$ is invertible), hence $\mathbf{R}(\alpha)$ stays on the orthogonal manifold. This is computationally cheap because each factor is block-structured (in practice the inverses are on small blocks).

Why this is different from other interpolations
Linearly interpolating entries, e.g. $(1-t)I+t\mathbf{R}$, does not preserve orthogonality and destroys the spectral guarantees the paper emphasizes. Geodesic interpolation on the orthogonal group (matrix logarithm/exponential $\exp(t\log \mathbf{R})$) is the principled manifold geodesic but is more expensive and is not what BOFT’s factorization naturally gives. The Cayley-scaling/product-path interpolation is cheap, stays orthogonal, and directly leverages the BOFT factorization and identity initialization the authors use.

How it is used in practice (as suggested or enabled by the paper)
The paper explicitly uses identity initialization for factors and proposes multiplicative Dropout that randomly replaces some factors/blocks by identity during training. Those design choices both rely on and exploit the product-path interpolation: you can (i) regularize capacity by leaving certain factors as identity (or dropping them during training), (ii) obtain a discrete family of models $\{\mathbf{R}_k\}$ (or a continuous family $\mathbf{R}(\alpha)$) to sweep over for validation-based model selection, and (iii) schedule capacity (gradually “turn on” factors or ramp $\alpha$ from $0$ to $1$) as a form of curriculum/regularization. The paper emphasizes that this structure produces a “smoother interpolation between ... identity and full orthogonal matrices,” which is the key inductive bias they leverage for better generalization.

Summary
The interpolation is induced by the factorization: you interpolate multiplicatively along the product path (discrete partial products) or continuously by scaling the Cayley skew parameters for each factor. This preserves orthogonality, is cheap to compute given BOFT’s small block factors, and is the mechanism the paper relies on for identity initialization, multiplicative-dropout regularization, and for trading expressivity vs. regularity.

### Which weight matrices in a transformer receive BOFT, and how are dimension mismatches handled?
Question:
For transformers, a layer may contain several linear maps with shapes like \(d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{model}}\), \(d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{ff}}\), and \(d_{\text{model}} \times d_k\) for Q/K/V. Since BOFT left-multiplies a \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix, do we set \(d\) to the row dimension of each weight and learn a separate \(\mathbf{R}\) per matrix? If so, how do you handle cases where some matrices share the same \(d\) and others do not? Are there any tied \(\mathbf{R}\) parameters across Q/K/V within a layer?
Answer:
Short answer: BOFT is applied per linear layer to the input-feature side of that layer. For a pretrained linear weight written in the paper as $\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ (so the forward pass is $z=(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top x$ with $x\in\mathbb{R}^d$), BOFT replaces it by $(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top x$ with $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ orthogonal. In other words, the BOFT $\mathbf{R}$ has size equal to the input dimension of that linear layer (the row count $d$ in the paper's notation) and is learned for that layer.

Concretely for transformer blocks this means: any projection whose input is the hidden state of size $d_{\text{model}}$ (for example Q/K/V when they are implemented as three separate linear layers that each map $d_{\text{model}}\to d_k$, or the first MLP projection $d_{\text{model}}\to d_{\text{ff}}$) will get an $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{model}}}$. The second MLP projection (the one that maps $d_{\text{ff}}\to d_{\text{model}}$) has input dimension $d_{\text{ff}}$ and therefore would get a different $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{ff}}\times d_{\text{ff}}}$. If Q, K and V are implemented as a single fused projection (common in optimized implementations, weight shape $d_{\text{model}}\times 3d_k$), BOFT treats that fused weight as one $\mathbf{W}^0$ and applies a single $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{model}}}$ to the whole fused matrix. If Q/K/V are separate modules, the paper’s formulation applies a separate $\mathbf{R}$ to each module (they are independent parameters).

The paper’s BOFT parameterization itself addresses practical dimension concerns: the formal butterfly construction starts from powers of two, but the authors introduce a block-butterfly generalization $\text{BOFT}(m,b)$ so you can choose block size $b$ and number of stages $m$ to fit the layer’s input dimension (the block-butterfly factors tile the $d$ coordinates). In short, BOFT’s $\mathbf{R}$ is tied to the layer’s input dimension; different input dimensions imply different $\mathbf{R}$ sizes, and whether multiple weights share the same $\mathbf{R}$ depends only on how you treat those weights in the model (fused vs separate). The paper does not report tying a single $\mathbf{R}$ across distinct projection matrices (e.g. forcing one $\mathbf{R}$ to be shared across separate Q/K/V modules); that would be an architectural/PEFT choice (reducing parameters but increasing coupling) outside the standard BOFT recipe.

Finally, remember the motivation: left-multiplying by an orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ acts on the input feature basis and therefore preserves pairwise neuron angles and the spectral norm of the pretrained weights (the paper uses Cayley parameterization / identity initialization for stability). This is why BOFT targets the input dimension of each linear map rather than the output dimension.

### Does BOFT still produce a dense \(\mathbf{R}\) when \(b=2\) but \(m < \log d\)?
Question:
The text states that using the butterfly (\(b=2\), \(m=\log d\)) yields a dense \(\mathbf{R}\). What happens if we choose \(m < \log d\)? Is \(\mathbf{R}\) then guaranteed to be sparse, or merely less expressive (but potentially still dense)? If it is sparse, can you characterize the resulting sparsity pattern?
Answer:
Short answer: No — with the canonical radix-$2$ butterfly used in BOFT (so $b = 2$) and the usual layering of $2\times2$ mixing blocks, taking fewer than $m = \log_2 d$ layers guarantees structural sparsity in $\mathbf{R}$. It is not merely a weaker “expressive” dense matrix; many entries are zero by construction. Concretely, every column (and row) of $\mathbf{R}$ can have at most $2^m$ nonzero entries, so to obtain a fully dense column you need $2^m \ge d$ (i.e. $m \ge \log_2 d$).

Why this holds (intuitive + formal sketch). View each butterfly factor as a sparse connectivity between level $t$ and $t+1$ in the paper’s information‑transmission grid. In the radix-$2$ butterfly each node at one level connects to at most two nodes at the next level, so the set of outputs reachable from a single input doubles at each stage. Formally, let $S_0 = \{j\}$ be the single source index for column $j$, and let $S_{t+1}$ be the set of indices reachable after applying the $(t+1)$-th factor. Because each column of a $2\times2$ block layer has at most two nonzeros, $|S_{t+1}| \le 2|S_t|$; by induction $|S_m| \le 2^m$. The support of column $j$ of $\mathbf{R}$ is $S_m$, so that column has at most $2^m$ nonzeros. If $2^m < d$, some targets are unreachable and the corresponding entries of $\mathbf{R}$ are zero for every choice of numerical values in the factors (values cannot create a nonzero where there is no path).

How the sparsity looks. The sparsity pattern is highly structured (hierarchical/dyadic). For $d = 2^N$ you can index rows/columns by $N$-bit binary addresses; after $m$ stages each input can influence only those outputs whose binary addresses differ from the input in the lower $m$ bits (equivalently: they lie in the same dyadic block of size $2^m$, up to the fixed bit‑reversal/permutation convention used by the particular butterfly ordering). Thus $\mathbf{R}$ is block/skeleton sparse with block size $2^m$ (after the relevant permutation): the matrix is partitioned into $d/2^m$ blocks of height $2^m$, and each column has nonzeros only inside one such block.

A couple of useful corollaries and caveats. The same counting argument generalizes to block size $b$: $\mathrm{BOFT}(m,b)$ has at most $b^m$ nonzeros per column, so achieving density requires $m \gtrsim \log_b d$ (this matches the paper’s statement that $\mathrm{BOFT}(\log(2d/b),b)$ can yield a dense $\mathbf{R}$). Orthogonality constraints can only further reduce the allowed nonzero pattern (they may force additional structural zeros) but cannot create nonzero entries where there is no connecting path in the factor graph. Finally, algebraic cancellation can accidentally zero some entries that are allowed by the connectivity pattern, but that is a measure‑zero event relative to the structural sparsity — the guaranteed zeros come from reachability, not from numerical cancellation.

In short: for the standard $b = 2$ butterfly used in BOFT, $m < \log_2 d$ implies a provably sparse $\mathbf{R}$ with at most $2^m$ nonzeros per column; you need about $\log_2 d$ layers to achieve full (generic) density.

### Is there a lower bound showing \(\Omega(d\log d)\) nonzeros (or parameters) are necessary for dense connectivity via sparse orthogonal products?
Question:
The paper motivates BOFT via the information-transmission view and argues that butterfly structures achieve dense connectivity with \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) edges/parameters. Are there known lower bounds (perhaps from network theory or rearrangeable networks) that show \(\Omega(d\log d)\) is unavoidable to achieve all-to-all mixing under orthogonality constraints, or at least necessary in some reasonable model? A reference would help justify the near-optimality of the butterfly choice.
Answer:
Short answer: yes — under the natural “layered, bounded fan‑in/fan‑out” model that BOFT uses there is a simple combinatorial lower bound showing $\Omega(d \log d)$ nonzeros (hence parameters) are necessary for full all‑to‑all mixing. This matches the butterfly’s $O(d \log d)$ cost up to constants. The same phenomenon is the reason the switching/permutation‑network literature (Benes, Waksman, Leighton, etc.) produces $\Theta(d \log d)$ designs and treats that scale as essentially unavoidable in the constant‑degree model.

Why (informal proof). Consider a factorization
$$
R = B_m B_{m-1}\cdots B_1\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
$$
and suppose every intermediate factor has bounded column fan‑in at most $s$ (so each column of every $B_i$ has at most $s$ nonzeros). Fix one output coordinate (one row of $R$). After applying one factor that row can depend on at most $s$ distinct input coordinates; after two factors it can depend on at most $s^2$ inputs, and after $m$ factors at most $s^m$ inputs. Therefore to have that row depend on all $d$ inputs (i.e. for $R$ to be dense) we must have
$$
s^m \ge d \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m \ge \frac{\log d}{\log s}.
$$
If each factor has $O(d)$ nonzeros (the case of constant $s$, e.g. $s=2$ for a radix‑2 butterfly block), then each factor contributes $\Theta(d)$ parameters, so the total number of nonzeros/parameters is at least
$$
\text{(total)} \;\gtrsim\; d\cdot m \;=\; \Omega\!\big(d\log d\big).
$$
Putting $s=2$ (the BOFT per‑factor sparsity) gives $m\ge\log_2 d$ and a lower bound $\Omega(d\log d)$. Intuitively this is the statement “with constant‑degree local mixing you need logarithmic depth to spread information to all $d$ leaves,” hence depth $\times$ width = $\Omega(d \log d)$ nonzeros.

Relation to network/switching literature. This counting/expansion argument is the same combinatorial core behind classic results about permutation/sorting/switching networks. The Benes/Waksman families realize all permutations with $\Theta(d \log d)$ $2\times 2$ switches, and lower‑bound/optimality arguments in that literature show you cannot do asymptotically better in the constant‑degree layered model. Good surveys/texts are Leighton’s treatment of interconnection networks and routing (see Leighton, Introduction to Parallel Algorithms and Architectures / Introduction to Parallel Computing), and older literature on Benes and Waksman networks; Knuth’s discussion of sorting and permutation networks is also useful background. The paper you cite already points to the same body of ideas (butterfly/topology, broadcasting, etc.).

Caveats and scope. Two important caveats:

1. The $\Omega(d \log d)$ bound above relies on a layered model with bounded per‑column (or per‑row) degree per factor. It is the natural model for BOFT (each factor is sparse with $O(d)$ nonzeros), so within that model butterfly is optimal up to constants. If you abandon the layered/constant‑degree assumption (allow very high degree in some layers, or allow non‑layered algebraic tricks), different trade‑offs are possible; in the extreme you can use a single dense factor and pay $\Theta(d^2)$ parameters, or mix large and small layers but the AM–GM style optimization shows that minimizing total sparsity given the constraint that the product mixes all inputs still pushes you to the logarithmic depth / $\Theta(d \log d)$ regime (the “best” trade is in the middle, not at $m=1$ or $m$ large).

2. Orthogonality itself does not invalidate the expansion argument: orthogonal factors must be invertible and have at least one nonzero per row/column, but that only strengthens the need for enough layers to achieve expansion. Conversely, representing the entire orthogonal group requires $\Theta(d^2)$ degrees of freedom (so you cannot hope to parameterize all orthogonal matrices with $o(d^2)$ parameters); BOFT and butterflies are not claiming universal coverage of $O(d)$ with $o(d^2)$ parameters, they claim dense connectivity (all inputs can influence all outputs) with far fewer parameters, and the lower bound above shows that $\Theta(d \log d)$ is the right scale for that goal in the constant‑degree layered model.

Conclusion. If your model is “product of sparse, constant‑fan‑in orthogonal layers” (exactly the BOFT setup) then a straightforward expansion argument gives a provable $\Omega(d \log d)$ lower bound on the number of nonzeros/parameters needed for full all‑to‑all mixing; the butterfly design attains $O(d \log d)$ and so is near‑optimal in that model. For formal background on the corresponding combinatorial/network lower bounds see the switching/network literature (Benes/Waksman constructions and Leighton’s textbook) and standard treatments of sorting/permutation networks (Knuth, and papers on comparator network lower bounds).
