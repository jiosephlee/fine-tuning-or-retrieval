\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

# Beyond Butterflies: Network Topologies as a New Design Space for PEFT

The BOFT paper reframes efficient orthogonal finetuning as an information-routing problem and shows that a carefully chosen topology — the classical butterfly pattern from FFT — lets you build a dense orthogonal transform with dramatically fewer trainable parameters (roughly $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ instead of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$). That idea is simple and powerful: instead of learning one dense orthogonal matrix $R$, learn a product of sparse orthogonal factors
\[
R = B_m B_{m-1}\cdots B_1,
\]
and choose the sparsity pattern of the $B_i$ so that information from every input coordinate can reach every output coordinate through the $d\times(m+1)$ grid implied by the factorization.

Butterfly is a very natural and effective point in that design space, but it is only one topology. In this post I sketch a practical research agenda motivated by the paper: treat network topologies (the adjacency patterns of the factors) as a core design axis for PEFT. The goal: find topologies that keep inference free (fold the learned orthogonal into the frozen weights), reduce train-time overhead, preserve the spectral and regularity benefits of orthogonal finetuning, and combine well with other PEFT tools (low-rank adapters, pruning, quantization).

## Quick recap (what BOFT gives you)

Orthogonal finetuning (OFT) multiplies a frozen pretrained layer $W^0$ on the left by an orthogonal matrix $R$ so that the layer becomes $R W^0$. BOFT parameterizes $R$ as a product of sparse orthogonal "butterfly components" and enforces orthogonality of each factor (e.g., via Cayley parametrization $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ with $Q=-Q^\top$, or via 2×2 rotations). BOFT($m,b$) denotes using $m$ components with block size $b$; BOFT achieves a dense $R$ with fewer parameters because each component has only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ nonzeros and there are $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ components in the radix-2 butterfly, giving the $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ statistics the paper emphasizes. Important practical points from the paper that we should preserve while designing new topologies:

- Two desiderata from the information-transmission view: dense connectivity (every source node can reach every target node) and minimum free/trainable edges under orthogonality constraints.
- Identity initialization of factors and multiplicative dropout are useful stabilizers in training.
- After finetuning, $R$ can be folded into $W^0$ to incur no inference-time cost.

## Why topology matters (beyond parameter count)

Topology controls several interacting properties that determine whether a factorized orthogonal parameterization is useful in practice:

- Expressivity-per-parameter. Some sparsity patterns let you approximate a large portion of the orthogonal group with few parameters; others cannot.
- Training-time cost. The factorization trades parameters for extra computation. A topology that admits a fast matvec (like the FFT-style butterfly) reduces wall-clock overhead.
- Hardware friendliness. Regular patterns, simple permutations, and block structures map far better to GPUs/TPUs than a sea of tiny scattered nonzeros.
- Compatibility with orthogonality constraints. We need each factor to be orthogonal (or otherwise enforce orthogonality globally); some sparsity patterns make convenient orthogonal parameterizations easier than others.
- Inductive bias. The pattern imposes structure on the allowed transforms; when chosen well this improves generalization on small-data tasks.

The paper uses butterfly because it balances these concerns well. The broader point is: topology is a knob we can tune, and tuning it opens new trade-offs.

## Candidate topologies (what to try and why)

Below are families of topologies that are natural to explore next. I phrase these as hypotheses grounded by the paper's desiderata — each family brings a different mix of expansion (fast mixing), locality (cache-friendly), and regularity (easy parametrization).

- Butterfly (baseline). Already shown to work: $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ nonzeros, fast structured matvec, and connection to many classic fast transforms.
- Expander-like patterns. Sparse graphs with strong expansion properties can mix information quickly with constant degree; they may allow dense connectivity in $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ layers with very few edges per layer.
- Layered permutation/route networks (Clos, Beneš, permutation networks). These networks realize arbitrary permutations with small depth and regular routing patterns, which could be turned into sparse orthogonal layers with good hardware locality.
- Low-diameter algebraic graphs (de Bruijn, Kautz, hypercube). These have logarithmic diameter and regular structure, offering predictable depth vs degree trade-offs.
- Circulant / convolutional-like structures. Circulant matrices are parameter-efficient and admit fast convolution-like algorithms; hybrid designs that mix circulant blocks and sparse factors may yield favorable compute trade-offs.
- Sequences of Givens rotations or Householder reflectors with structured pairings. Givens rotations are naturally 2×2 orthogonal factors. The question is whether there exist pairing schedules (i.e., topologies) that match butterfly-like efficiency while being simpler to implement on hardware.
- Small-world / hierarchical topologies. Combine local dense neighborhoods with a few long-range shortcuts; this can preserve locality (good for cache and parallelism) while enabling global mixing in few steps.

None of these are guaranteed winners; the right choice depends on how many parameters you want, how much compute you can tolerate at training time, and what hardware primitive you want to exploit. The paper’s information-transmission framing gives us a clear evaluation metric: can the topology connect every source to every sink in the chosen depth, and how many trainable elements (free edges) does that require under orthogonality?

## Training-time and hardware considerations

A major practical barrier for BOFT-style methods is training-time overhead: applying and backpropagating through multiple sparse orthogonal factors every step. Here are practical levers to reduce that overhead while preserving the inference-free property:

- Favor topologies with fast structured matvecs. The butterfly admits an FFT-like $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ apply. When exploring topologies, prefer those where factor application reduces to a sequence of regular permutes and small block multiplies rather than wildly scattered sparse GEMMs.
- Tune block size $b$ to trade arithmetic intensity against parameter count (BOFT($m,b$)). Larger blocks increase per-kernel work and GPU efficiency at the cost of more parameters.
- Fuse factor applications. If your topology permits, implement each layer of factors as a batched small-GEMM over contiguous memory with precomputed permutation indexing; this avoids kernel launch overhead.
- Exploit identity initialization and multiplicative dropout during early training to stabilize and allow larger learning rates.
- Use low-precision arithmetic for the factor parameters or their gradients where possible, but be careful: orthogonality constraints are sensitive, so test quantization-aware training or freeze orthogonality with re-projection steps.
- Measure wall-clock, not just FLOPs. On modern accelerators, many small ops are far slower than a single dense op with the same FLOPs.

Topologies that yield regular permutations and dense small blocks will typically map best to current GPU/TPU hardware.

## Composing BOFT with other PEFT ideas

BOFT is a multiplicative reparametrization. It is natural to combine it with complementary PEFT mechanisms:

- BOFT + LoRA (multiplicative + additive). Use a small multiplicative orthogonal preconditioner $R$ to preserve spectral structure and add a tiny low-rank residual (LoRA) to capture fine-grained changes. This keeps most of the pretrained geometry while letting the model adapt in an unrestricted low-dimensional subspace.
- BOFT + structured sparsity / pruning. Start from a richer topology and gradually prune factor entries while maintaining the dense-connectivity constraint. The information-transmission view helps ensure pruned patterns maintain reachability.
- BOFT + quantization. Fold $R$ into $W^0$ at the end and quantize the resulting weights for deployment. For more aggressive memory savings, investigate quantizing factor parameters during training with orthogonality-aware projections or quantization-aware training.
- BOFT + adapters / prompt tuning. Apply BOFT selectively to layers known to influence a task more (e.g., feedforward vs projection heads), and use adapters where multiplicative structure is less helpful.

These hybrids are sensible because BOFT preserves the spectral norm of $W^0$ (left multiplication by orthogonal $R$ leaves singular values unchanged), which can stabilize training; additive low-rank components can then model residual directions without breaking that stability.

## How to search the topology space (practical recipe)

A pragmatic search loop combines simple theory-guided priors with small-scale empirical probes:

1. Define topology families (butterfly, expander, permutation networks, circulant, Givens schedules).
2. For each candidate, fix depth $m$ and block size $b$, parameterize each factor as orthogonal blocks (e.g., 2×2 rotations or Cayley transforms).
3. Proxy test: attempt to approximate a random dense orthogonal matrix of size $d$ and measure approximation error vs effective parameter count (the paper uses this exact proxy). This gives an expressivity curve cheaply.
4. Measure structured matvec cost: benchmark factor-apply throughput and wall-clock time on target hardware.
5. Run a few-shot finetuning experiment on a representative downstream task to assess generalization and stability.
6. Iterate: favor topologies with good expressivity-per-parameter and low wall-clock overhead.

For automated search, use a mixed strategy: rule-based pruning based on theoretical metrics (degree, diameter, expansion) plus black-box optimization (evolutionary search or bandit-based selection) over depth and block-size hyperparameters. Differentiable topology search is possible (relax adjacency masks), but orthogonality constraints complicate gradients and may require special handling.

## Open questions and research directions

- Are there topologies with a strictly better parameter/time/generalization frontier than butterfly for common finetuning regimes?
- Can we derive lower bounds that tie graph-theoretic metrics (degree, expansion, spectral gap, diameter) to the number of layers required to approximate arbitrary orthogonal transforms under orthogonality constraints?
- How does the optimal topology depend on layer type (attention projection vs MLP) and dataset size (few-shot vs many-shot)?
- How do pruning and quantization interact with orthogonality constraints for factorized topologies? Can we maintain provable connectivity under aggressive pruning?
- What hardware primitives (fused small-GEMMs, permutation kernels, in-place 2×2 rotations) give the best training throughput for particular topologies?
- Can we design topology search algorithms that trade off expressivity, train-time throughput, and final generalization in a single optimization loop?

These questions are opportunities for both theoretical work and careful systems+empirical engineering. The BOFT paper gives us a clear conceptual and experimental baseline for these investigations.

## Takeaway

BOFT reframes orthogonal finetuning as a routing problem on a grid of nodes and uses the butterfly topology to get a compelling parameter/expressivity compromise. The next step is to treat the topology itself as a design variable. Graph-theoretic properties (connectivity, expansion, diameter), hardware-mapping considerations (regularity, block structure), and compatibility with orthogonality should guide the search. Doing so opens practical wins: faster training, the same inference-free deployment, and potentially better generalization by tailoring inductive biases to task and hardware.

If you are excited by BOFT, consider running the proxy experiments suggested in the paper (approximate random orthogonals, measure approximation error vs parameters) across a few topology families and profiling factor-apply kernels on your target hardware. Those two signals — expressivity curves and wall-clock cost — will quickly narrow the design space for topologies worth scaling to real finetuning workloads.
