# Why Rotate? An Accessible Guide to Orthogonal Fine-Tuning

## The short version

Fine-tuning a pretrained model usually means changing its weights just enough to solve a new task while keeping the pretraining knowledge intact. Orthogonal fine-tuning (OFT) does this in a simple, principled way: instead of adding arbitrary updates, it multiplies the layer weights by an orthogonal "rotation" matrix. That rotation preserves pairwise angles between neurons and the singular values of the pretrained weights, which helps retain the geometry learned during pretraining and yields more stable, better‑generalizing adapters. The catch: a full orthogonal matrix is large. The paper introduces BOFT — Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning — which factorizes the rotation into a product of sparse orthogonal factors (butterfly components), giving dense rotations with far fewer trainable parameters (roughly $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ instead of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for a $d$‑dimensional layer).

Below I unpack what this means, why orthogonality helps, and how BOFT makes it practical.

## What OFT actually does (simple math)

For a pretrained linear layer with weights $\mathbf{W}^0$, the usual forward pass is
$\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}$.
OFT replaces $\mathbf{W}^0$ with a rotated version:
$\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}$,
where $\mathbf{R}$ is constrained to be orthogonal ($\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$). We usually initialize $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$ so we start exactly from the pretrained model and finetune by learning $\mathbf{R}$.

Why call this a "rotation"? Orthogonal matrices are essentially rotations and reflections: they change directions but preserve angles and lengths.

## Why preserve angles and singular values?

Two simple linear algebra facts explain most of the appeal.

- Angle preservation. For any two neuron column vectors $\mathbf{w}_i,\mathbf{w}_j$ of $\mathbf{W}^0$,
  $$
  (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i)^\top(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j)=\mathbf{w}_i^\top\mathbf{w}_j.
  $$
  That means inner products (and hence pairwise angles) between neurons are invariant under $\mathbf{R}$. Preserving these pairwise relationships helps retain the relative geometry and semantics encoded during pretraining.

- Spectral norm preservation. If $\mathbf{W}^0=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top$ is an SVD, then
  $$
  \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U})\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top,
  $$
  so the singular values (and in particular the spectral norm) are unchanged. Preserving the spectral norm is known to help training stability and to limit pathological scaling effects that can hurt generalization.

Contrast this with common alternatives: using a tiny learning rate is an ad hoc way to avoid destroying pretraining geometry; low‑rank additive updates (LoRA) add $\Delta\mathbf{W}$ of low rank but do not exactly preserve angles or singular values.

## Enforcing orthogonality in practice

A convenient, numerically stable way to parametrize orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ is the Cayley transform. Parametrize a skew‑symmetric matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ (so $\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^\top$) and set
$$
\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}.
$$
Initializing $\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{0}$ gives $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$, so the finetuning begins from the pretrained model. This makes OFT easy to plug into existing models with zero initial disruption.

## The parameter-efficiency problem

A $d\times d$ dense orthogonal matrix has roughly $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ free parameters, which is expensive to store and train for large layers. The original OFT paper addressed this by using block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices: split the $d$ dimensions into $r$ blocks of size $b$ (so $br=d$) and apply separate orthogonal transforms per block. That reduces parameters to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$, but it enforces a fixed, unnatural sparsity pattern: dimensions are grouped by index and transformed independently. This limits expressivity and cannot reproduce many global linear transforms.

So the problem becomes: can we build dense, expressive orthogonal transforms while keeping parameter cost low?

## BOFT: factorize the rotation into sparse steps

BOFT answers this by writing the rotation as a product of sparse orthogonal factors:
$$
\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{B}_m \mathbf{B}_{m-1}\cdots \mathbf{B}_1,
$$
and constraining each $\mathbf{B}_i$ to be sparse and orthogonal. Think of information flowing through $m{+}1$ layers of $d$ nodes each: each sparse factor permits some localized exchanges, and together they enable every input dimension to influence every output dimension. Two desiderata guide the design: (i) dense connectivity (every input node reaches every output node after the chain), and (ii) minimal free/trainable edges.

The butterfly topology, familiar from the Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm, is a network that achieves dense connectivity efficiently. A butterfly factorization composes a small number of sparse, patterned factors so that the product approximates a dense transform while using only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ trainable degrees of freedom (for the radix‑2 case). The BOFT paper formalizes a block version of this and shows that by making each sparse factor orthogonal (each built from orthogonal $2\times 2$ or small block rotations) we get a dense orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ with far fewer parameters.

Concretely, BOFT is denoted BOFT$(m,b)$ where $m$ is the number of butterfly components and $b$ is a block size. The forward pass is
$$
\mathbf{z} = \big(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\,\mathbf{W}^0\big)^\top \mathbf{x}, \qquad
\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
$$
and each $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is orthogonal. BOFT$(m,b)$ uses about $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m$ effective trainable parameters. Two useful special cases:

- BOFT$(1,b)$ is exactly block‑diagonal OFT with block size $b$.
- BOFT$(1,d)$ is full OFT with a dense orthogonal matrix.
- The classical butterfly uses $m\approx \log d$ and $b=2$, giving $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters while producing a dense orthogonal transform.

## Why this is a good inductive bias

The butterfly structure is not arbitrary: it underlies many fast linear transforms (FFT, Hadamard, DCT, etc.). Using it as a parameterization injects a structured prior that allows global mixing of dimensions while keeping the parameter budget small. The BOFT authors show that for the same block size, butterfly parameterizations are more expressive than block‑diagonal OFT; and by composing multiple butterflies (a kaleidoscope-like hierarchy) you can recover the full orthogonal group if you allow enough factors. That gives a smooth tradeoff between expressivity and regularity: you can choose how much capacity to allow while keeping the desirable geometry‑preserving properties of OFT.

## Practical points and tricks

- Identity initialization. Start with $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$ (i.e., $\mathbf{Q}=0$ in Cayley) so the finetuned model initially equals the pretrained model.

- Multiplicative Dropout. Because BOFT is multiplicative (not additive like LoRA), conventional dropout on updates does not directly apply. The paper proposes multiplicative Dropout: randomly replace a fraction $p_1$ of the butterfly components and a fraction $p_2$ of the diagonal blocks inside them with identity blocks during training. This regularizes the multiplicative update.

- Inference cost. The training runtime is slightly higher because you multiply several sparse factors each step, but after finetuning you can multiply the learned orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ into the pretrained $\mathbf{W}^0$ once and for all, so there is no inference latency penalty.

## How to choose $(m,b)$ — intuition

- If you want the simplest parameter saving and accept the block sparsity, pick $m=1$ and tune $b$ (this is block‑diagonal OFT).

- If you want a dense rotation with strong parameter savings, pick $b=2$ and $m\approx \log d$ (the classic butterfly). This yields $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters and often a good balance between expressivity and regularity.

- Increasing $m$ or $b$ increases expressivity (and parameters). The empirical findings in the paper indicate that smaller $b$ with larger $m$ tends to be more parameter-efficient at approximating dense orthogonal transforms.

## Relation to LoRA and other PEFTs

LoRA and similar low‑rank additive techniques change weights as $\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}^0+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$. They are simple, effective, and cheap, but they do not preserve the geometry of $\mathbf{W}^0$ exactly. OFT and BOFT perform a multiplicative orthogonal change $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ that preserves angles and singular values by construction. That preservation is the main conceptual advantage: it constrains the finetuning search space to transformations that keep pretraining geometry intact, which often improves stability and generalization.

## Empirical claim and limitations (as reported)

The paper reports extensive experiments adapting large vision transformers, language models, and diffusion models; BOFT outperforms prior parameter‑efficient finetuning methods by a notable margin across those settings, validating the practical utility of orthogonal finetuning with butterfly factorization.

Limitations called out by the authors include a modest training runtime overhead from the multiplicative chain of factors, and an open question about whether the butterfly topology is the optimal sparse factorization for this problem. The information transmission view they develop suggests other network topologies (drawn from network design literature) could be explored.

## Takeaway

"Why rotate?" Because a rotation (orthogonal transform) is the most direct way to adapt a pretrained layer while preserving the relative geometry—the angles and singular values—that encode semantic knowledge. Orthogonal finetuning (OFT) makes this idea concrete and effective; BOFT makes it practical by factorizing dense rotations into compact products of sparse orthogonal blocks (butterflies). If you care about stability and preserving pretraining structure, BOFT is a neat, principled addition to the PEFT toolbox.



# Meet BOFT: Butterflies that Make Big Models Adapt with Fewer Parameters

Modern foundation models are massive. Fine-tuning them for a new task without blowing up compute or memory is an active research problem. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is an elegant, stability-minded approach that adapts a pretrained weight matrix by left-multiplying it with an orthogonal matrix so that the geometric relationships learned during pretraining are preserved. The downside: a dense orthogonal matrix in dimension $d$ costs $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ trainable parameters.

Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) is a simple, principled idea that gets the best of both worlds: dense, expressive orthogonal updates that cost only about $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters rather than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. The trick comes from the FFT-inspired butterfly factorization: compose a dense orthogonal matrix out of a short product of very sparse orthogonal factors (built from $2\times2$ blocks). The result preserves OFT’s spectral and geometric benefits while dramatically reducing parameter count and improving parameter-efficiency compared to block-diagonal OFT.

## Quick recap: what OFT does (and why it can be costly)

OFT updates a pretrained linear layer $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ by replacing the forward pass
$z=(\bm{W}^0)^\top x$ with
$z=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top x$,
where $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is constrained to be orthogonal. Enforcing orthogonality is typically done via a Cayley parameterization:
$\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{Q}^\top=-\bm{Q}$.
Initializing $\bm{Q}=0$ yields $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, so the finetuning starts from the pretrained model.

A full orthogonal $\bm{R}$ has $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ degrees of freedom. OFT reduces that by using block-diagonal orthogonal blocks (block size $b$), which brings the parameter cost down to roughly $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ but also restricts the set of possible transforms: dimensions are split into independent groups, so the block-diagonal OFT cannot express many dense transforms (it cannot recover transforms like the FFT or DCT).

BOFT asks: can we produce a dense orthogonal transform while keeping the number of trainable parameters small?

## An information-transmission perspective (intuition that guides BOFT)

Think of a $d\times d$ matrix as connectivity between $d$ source nodes and $d$ receiver nodes. Writing $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_m\cdots\bm{B}_1$ is like unrolling a layered network: each factor $\bm{B}_i$ connects nodes between two adjacent levels. To get a dense final matrix, information from every source node must be able to reach every receiver node through these layers.

This viewpoint highlights two desiderata for an efficient factorization:

- dense connectivity: every source must be able to reach every receiver;
- few free (trainable) edges: minimize the number of nonzero trainable elements subject to orthogonality constraints.

A naive single dense factor takes $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges. Butterfly topologies, however, are classical network designs (coming from the Cooley–Tukey FFT) that achieve dense end-to-end connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges by composing $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse stages. That’s the core idea BOFT exploits.

## The butterfly construction (what BOFT parameterizes)

At a high level, a butterfly factorization builds $\bm{R}$ as a product of block-diagonal sparse matrices (butterfly components), each containing small $2\times2$ or $2b\times 2b$ structured blocks. One local building block (a butterfly factor of size $k$) has the form

\[
\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_2)\\[2pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
\]

and a full $d$-dimensional butterfly is the product of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ block-diagonal components arranged in a coarse-to-fine recursion:

\[
\bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\,\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,\tfrac{d}{2})\cdots\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2).
\]

To keep orthogonality, each sparse component is itself constrained to be orthogonal; with block size $2$ this just means parameterizing many independent $2\times2$ rotations (or using Cayley on $2\times2$ skew-symmetric blocks). The block version replaces scalars in the local factors with $b\times b$ blocks, producing a block-butterfly that mixes small subspaces rather than single coordinates.

BOFT writes the learned orthogonal update as

\[
\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)},\qquad\text{with }\ \big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\big)^\top\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}=\bm{I}_d.
\]

Here $m$ is the number of butterfly stages used and $b$ is the block size inside each local unit. The effective number of trainable parameters used by BOFT($m,b$) can be written as

\[
\text{params} = \tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m.
\]

Two instructive special cases explain the design space:

- BOFT($1,b$) is exactly block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$ (no cross-block mixing).
- BOFT($1,d$) recovers full OFT (a single unconstrained orthogonal block of size $d$).
- BOFT($\log d,2$) (the classic butterfly) yields a dense orthogonal $\bm{R}$ using only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters, because there are $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ stages and each stage has $\mathcal{O}(d)$ trainable entries.

So BOFT forms a smooth interpolation between identity (very regular, low param count) and the full orthogonal group (maximally expressive but costly), controlled by $(m,b)$.

## Why BOFT gives better parameter-efficiency than block-diagonal OFT

- sparse-but-deep factorization: a dense transform is realized by composing a few sparse orthogonal factors. Each factor has only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ trainable entries, and $m\!=\!\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ stages give $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ total.
- richer connectivity for the same budget: for a fixed parameter budget BOFT can mix information across all dimensions (via the layered butterfly routing), whereas block-diagonal OFT confines mixing to within independent groups.
- structured inductive bias: butterfly patterns are the structure behind many classical fast transforms (FFT, Hadamard, DCT). Restricting $\bm{R}$ to butterfly-like compositions introduces a useful prior that tends to improve generalization (smoother interpolation between identity and fully expressive transforms) compared to arbitrary dense updates or naïve block sparsity.

Empirically (in the paper) approximating random dense orthogonal matrices and practical finetuning experiments showed BOFT achieves lower approximation error or better downstream performance than block-diagonal OFT at comparable or smaller parameter budgets.

## Other useful properties

- spectral stability: left-multiplying $\bm{W}^0$ by an orthogonal $\bm{R}$ preserves $\bm{W}^0$’s singular values. BOFT inherits the same spectral-preservation property as OFT, which helps training stability and generalization.
- identity initialization: BOFT components are initialized to identity (set skew-symmetric Cayley parameters to zero), so finetuning starts from the pretrained model exactly.
- multiplicative dropout: because BOFT is multiplicative (not additive like LoRA), standard dropout on the update does not apply. The paper proposes a multiplicative dropout that randomly replaces a percentage of butterfly components or diagonal blocks with identity during training to reduce overfitting.
- inference: the learned orthogonal product can be fused into pretrained weights after training, so there is no inference latency penalty.

## Theoretical and practical caveats

- expressivity vs regularity: richer parameterizations are not always better. BOFT’s structured subset of the orthogonal group strikes a practical balance: more expressive than block-diagonal OFT at a given block size, but still more regular than full dense finetuning, which helps generalization.
- runtime overhead during training: BOFT requires multiplying several sparse factors each step, so training time is slightly higher than single-block OFT. This overhead is limited to training; the orthogonal update can be merged for inference.
- design choices remain: the paper shows butterfly is a strong, principled choice, but the information-transmission view opens the door to other network topologies from networking theory that might be even more efficient.

## Bottom line

BOFT is a principled, FFT-inspired way to get dense, expressive orthogonal finetuning with far fewer parameters. By composing a dense orthogonal update from a short product of sparse orthogonal stages (butterfly components), BOFT reduces the finetuning parameter cost from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to roughly $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ while preserving OFT’s spectral and geometric advantages. It subsumes block-diagonal OFT and full OFT as special cases, offers identity initialization and multiplicative regularization, and—per the paper—delivers strong empirical gains across vision, language, and diffusion model adaptation tasks.

If you care about preserving pretrained geometry while finetuning large layers on a tight parameter budget, BOFT is a conceptually clean and practically attractive option worth exploring.



# Think Like a Network: The Information-Transmission View of Matrix Factorization

## The punchline

If you want a compact way to alter a pretrained layer while preserving its geometry, think like a network engineer: send information through a sparse, staged topology instead of wiring every input to every output. Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) reinterprets matrix factorization as message passing on a grid: each sparse factor is a communication step, paths across steps determine which input coordinates can influence which outputs, and the choice of topology controls how many trainable entries you actually need. The butterfly topology — familiar from the FFT — hits the sweet spot: dense end-to-end connectivity in only $O(\log d)$ stages with $O(d)$ work per stage, so the parameter budget drops from $O(d^2)$ to about $O(d\log d)$ without losing the practical expressivity that matters for finetuning.

Below I walk through that analogy, why it leads naturally to the butterfly, and what that buys you when you want parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning.

## Matrix factorization as message passing

Take a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. Orthogonal finetuning (OFT) updates this layer by left-multiplying with an orthogonal matrix $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ so the new forward pass is $z=(R W^0)^\top x$. If we parameterize
$$
R = B_m B_{m-1}\cdots B_1,
$$
then each factor $B_i$ is a sparse matrix that acts as a one-step communication layer. Unroll this multiplication into a grid of $d$ nodes across $m+1$ vertical levels: level 0 are the input coordinates, level $m$ are the output coordinates, and level $i$ sits between factors $B_i$ and $B_{i+1}$. A nonzero entry in $B_i$ is an edge between a node at level $i-1$ and one at level $i$ — it allows that coordinate to influence the next level.

With this view:
- An entry $R_{ij}\neq 0$ is equivalent to there being at least one path from input node $j$ (level 0) to output node $i$ (level $m$).
- Making $R$ dense (so every input can affect every output) is exactly the requirement that the grid be fully connected from level 0 to level $m$.
- The number of trainable parameters is the number of free (i.e., non-fixed) edges across all intermediate levels.

So matrix factorization becomes a network-design problem: choose a sparse connectivity pattern across the $m$ stages so that every source can reach every sink, but do it with as few free edges as possible while respecting the extra constraint that every factor must be orthogonal.

## Two design goals (and an orthogonality wrinkle)

From the network perspective the finetuning factorization should satisfy two natural desiderata:

- Dense connectivity: every source node should be able to reach every sink node (so $R$ can be dense).
- Minimum free edges: the total number of trainable edge parameters should be as small as possible.

Orthogonality of each factor $B_i$ complicates the bookkeeping. Orthogonality implies full rank, so between adjacent levels you need at least $d$ linearly independent connections (a bijection) to avoid rank collapse. In practice these minimal structural connections are often fixed by the orthogonality constraint (for instance a sparse orthogonal matrix can have predetermined $\pm 1$ entries in some positions), so the count of free parameters is not simply the raw edge count. Still, viewed graphically, orthogonality couples edges across a factor: setting one entry to zero can force structure elsewhere.

The net effect: you want a sparse multi-layer topology that, once made orthogonal, still allows dense end-to-end influence while keeping the number of trainable degrees-of-freedom low.

## Why the butterfly is the natural network

Enter the butterfly topology from the Cooley–Tukey FFT. The butterfly is a staged network where each stage pairs and mixes coordinates in a carefully permuted pattern. Key properties:

- Each stage has $O(d)$ nonzeros (each node participates in a small constant number of connections).
- With $O(\log d)$ stages you can propagate information from any input coordinate to any output coordinate — i.e., the grid becomes densely connected end-to-end.
- The total number of nonzeros (and hence trainable elements) is $O(d\log d)$.

Concretely, BOFT builds $R$ from block-butterfly components. If you use $m$ stages and a block size $b$, BOFT has about
$$
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m
$$
effective trainable parameters for a $d\times n$ linear layer. In the canonical butterfly setting $b=2$ and $m\approx\log d$, so the parameter count is $O(d\log d)$ — a huge cut compared to a full orthogonal matrix which nominally needs $O(d^2)$ parameters.

Why does this work intuitively? Each butterfly stage mixes coordinates in different pairings, so information spreads exponentially fast across stages: after $k$ stages each node can reach a block of about $2^k$ nodes. After $\log d$ stages that block is the whole set. So with very few stages you get dense influence while each stage itself is cheap.

## How BOFT keeps orthogonality and stability

BOFT does more than pick a sparse topology; it enforces orthogonality on every factor so the composed $R$ is orthogonal. Practically this is implemented by parameterizing each small block (e.g., $2\times 2$ or $2b\times 2b$ blocks) as an orthogonal matrix — Cayley transforms or simple planar rotations are used for that — and initializing each block to identity so the whole composition starts as $R=I$. This identity initialization mirrors common finetuning practice (small changes from pretrained weights) and helps stable optimization.

BOFT also introduces a multiplicative Dropout analogue: randomly replace some butterfly components or diagonal blocks with identity during training. Because the update is multiplicative (not additive like LoRA), conventional dropout does not directly apply; instead this “block dropout” reduces overfitting while preserving the staged message-passing interpretation.

## Expressivity, bias, and practical trade-offs

A few useful consequences of the network perspective:

- Inductive bias: the butterfly topology is the backbone of many classic fast transforms (DFT, DCT, Hadamard). Enforcing that structure biases the finetuning towards transforms that mix information efficiently, which empirically improves generalization in many tasks.
- Expressivity vs. regularity: BOFT interpolates between the identity (maximal regularity) and the full orthogonal group (maximal expressivity). With a small number of stages and small blocks you constrain the hypothesis class; increasing stages and composing multiple butterfly sequences (a “kaleidoscope” composition) increases expressivity and can, in the limit, represent the entire orthogonal group. The paper proves BOFT is strictly more expressive than block-diagonal OFT for the same block size.
- Spectral stability: multiplying $W^0$ on the left by orthogonal $R$ preserves singular values of $W^0$, which helps training stability and prevents exploding/shrinking activations.

The trade-off is practical: BOFT does incur a moderate training-time overhead because you multiply several sparse factors per forward/backward pass, but these factors can be folded into the pretrained weights after finetuning so there is no inference cost.

## What the experiments suggest

The paper shows BOFT applied to vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models. Empirical results (including simulations approximating random orthogonal matrices) indicate BOFT achieves better parameter-efficiency than block-diagonal OFT and competitive or superior performance against other PEFT baselines, validating that the butterfly network is an effective topology for this message-passing style of finetuning.

## Takeaways

- Reinterpret matrix factorization as staged message passing on a grid: factors = communication steps, nonzeros = edges.
- Two simple network goals — dense end-to-end connectivity and minimal free edges — frame the design of parameter-efficient factorizations.
- The butterfly topology hits both goals: $O(d\log d)$ parameters, dense connectivity, and a structure that preserves useful inductive biases and training stability.
- BOFT operationalizes this idea for orthogonal finetuning: identity initialization, orthogonal blocks (Cayley transforms), and a multiplicative dropout keep optimization stable and compact.

If you’re designing parameter-efficient finetuning schemes, thinking like a network — literally picking a topology for how information flows across stages — gives a clean, visual intuition and points to proven topologies (butterfly and its variants) that save parameters without throwing away performance.



# BOFT, Step by Step: How to Plug It Into LLMs, Vision Transformers, and Diffusion Models

BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning) is a plug-in parameter-efficient finetuning recipe: instead of adding low-rank updates (LoRA) or finetuning full weight matrices, BOFT left-multiplies a frozen pretrained weight matrix $W^0$ by a learnable orthogonal matrix $R$ that is factorized into sparse "butterfly" components. The result preserves geometric structure (pairwise angles, spectral norm) while reducing the number of trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ toward $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$.

Below is a practical, implementation-oriented walkthrough: how to instantiate BOFT in common model layers, how to initialize and enforce orthogonality, how multiplicative Dropout works, how to choose the two key knobs (depth $m$ and block size $b$), and what to expect at training and inference time.

## Quick mechanics (what changes in the forward pass)
For a pretrained linear layer with weight $W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ (neurons are the $n$ columns), BOFT replaces the forward computation
z = (W^0)^\top x
with
z = (R W^0)^\top x,
where the orthogonal matrix $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is factorized as a product of $m$ block-butterfly components:
\[
R(m,b) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)}.
\]
Each $\tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)}$ is a $d\times d$ sparse orthogonal block-butterfly matrix with block size $b$; the whole composition yields expressive dense transforms while keeping the number of free parameters small.

Important special cases:
- BOFT$(1,b)$ = block-diagonal OFT (the original block-diagonal orthogonal finetuning).
- BOFT$(1,d)$ = full OFT (unconstrained dense orthogonal matrix).
- BOFT$(\log d, 2)$ (roughly) gives the classical butterfly factorization and uses $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters.

The effective number of trainable parameters for a linear layer of width $d$ is
\[
\text{params} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m,
\]
so you can directly tune $(m,b)$ to hit your parameter budget.

## Step 1 — Where to insert BOFT in your model
BOFT is a left-multiplicative transform of pretrained weight matrices, so it can be applied anywhere you have dense linear layers. Typical places:
- Transformer attention projections (query/key/value/output) and MLP (feed-forward) linear layers in LLMs.
- Patch embeddings, attention/MLP layers in Vision Transformers.
- Dense linear layers inside U-Net / conditioning blocks in diffusion models.

Implementation pattern: keep $W^0$ frozen, implement a BOFT module that produces $R$ (product of sparse components), compute $W_{\text{eff}}=R W^0$, and use $W_{\text{eff}}$ in the usual forward. During finetuning only the parameters inside the BOFT components are updated.

## Step 2 — Identity initialization (start with no change)
A key practical requirement is that finetuning should not change the pretrained model at step zero. BOFT enforces that by initializing every butterfly component to identity. Concretely parameterize each orthogonal block with a skew-symmetric matrix $Q$ and use the Cayley transform (below); set the parameter that produces $Q$ to zero at init so that $Q=0$ and therefore $R=\mathbf{I}$ initially. This guarantees the model behaves exactly like the pretrained model at the start of finetuning.

## Step 3 — Enforce orthogonality with the Cayley transform
BOFT keeps each butterfly component orthogonal during training. A convenient, exact parameterization is the Cayley transform:
\[
R = (I + Q)\,(I - Q)^{-1},
\]
where $Q^\top = -Q$ (skew-symmetric). When you parameterize $Q$ implicitly (for example store an unconstrained matrix $S$ and set $Q = S - S^\top$), evaluating the Cayley transform yields an orthogonal $R$ for each block.

Practical notes:
- For the smallest blocks ($2\times 2$), you can equivalently parameterize each block as a 2D rotation; this is compact and numerically trivial.
- For larger blocks, compute the Cayley transform per block. It is numerically stable to solve the linear system $(I - Q)Y = (I + Q)$ for $Y$ rather than explicitly computing a matrix inverse.
- Initializing the underlying $Q$ to zero yields $R=I$, matching the identity initialization above.

## Step 4 — Multiplicative Dropout (regularization)
Because BOFT is multiplicative ($R W^0$) rather than additive, conventional dropout on the update does not apply directly. BOFT uses multiplicative Dropout: at each training step randomly select a fraction $p_1$ of the butterfly components and, inside each chosen component, a fraction $p_2$ of its diagonal blocks, and replace those chosen components/blocks with identity matrices for that forward pass. This temporarily removes some factors from the product, which acts as regularization and reduces over-reliance on particular factors of $R$.

The paper leaves $p_1$ and $p_2$ as tunable hyperparameters; treat them like other regularization strengths and tune per task.

## Step 5 — Choosing depth $m$ and block size $b$ (trade-offs)
The two knobs $(m,b)$ control the expressivity / parameter-efficiency trade-off:

- Depth $m$ is the number of butterfly components (factors). Increasing $m$ generally increases expressive power because information can be mixed across more stages.
- Block size $b$ controls the internal block dimension inside butterfly components. Larger $b$ gives more parameters per factor and can increase expressivity per factor but raises the parameter count linearly.

Useful identities and special points:
- Parameter count per linear layer: $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m$.
- BOFT$(1,b)$ = block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$ (minimal depth).
- BOFT$(1,d)$ = full dense OFT (recover full orthogonal, maximal parameters).
- Using the classical butterfly choice $b=2$ and $m \approx \log d$ yields a dense transform with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters.

How to choose in practice: pick $(m,b)$ to meet your parameter budget and desired inductive bias. Small $b$ plus larger $m$ tends to be more parameter-efficient (but slightly higher runtime cost during training). If you want the smoothest inductive bias that can recover many classical transforms, choose $b=2$ and increase $m$; if you prefer a coarse-grained block structure, increase $b$ and keep $m$ small. BOFT subsumes OFT choices, so you can traverse the continuum between block-diagonal and full orthogonal.

## Step 6 — Training tips and runtime considerations
- Training overhead: BOFT introduces a modest runtime overhead during finetuning because the factorized orthogonal transform $R$ must be assembled (products of sparse blocks) and multiplied with $W^0$ at every step. This is a training-time cost only.
- Inference latency: zero additional latency. After finetuning you can multiply the learned $R$ into $W^0$ once (compute $W_{\text{final}} = R W^0$) and discard the BOFT module. The inference model then uses the same single dense matrix as usual, so there is no added inference cost.
- Stability and generalization: BOFT preserves the spectral norm of $W^0$ because left-multiplication by orthogonal $R$ does not change singular values; this helps training stability. Identity initialization keeps the model close to pretrained at the start, reducing destabilizing jumps.
- Hyperparameters beyond $(m,b)$ and multiplicative dropout (optimizer, learning rate, etc.) are task dependent. The BOFT paper uses the same general finetuning practice as other PEFT methods; treat BOFT parameters as the primary things to tune for parameter-efficiency vs expressivity.

## Minimal implementation checklist
1. Identify which pretrained linear layers $W^0$ you want to adapt with BOFT (attention projections, MLPs, etc.).
2. Choose $(m,b)$ to fit your parameter budget and desired inductive bias.
3. Implement a block-butterfly component builder that outputs sparse block matrices $\tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)}$ (the nonzero pattern is a permutation of $2\times 2$ blocks generalized to $b$).
4. For each block, parameterize orthogonality via a skew-symmetric $Q$ (store an unconstrained parameter $S$ and set $Q = S - S^\top$) and compute block orthogonal $R_{\text{block}}$ via Cayley transform (or 2D rotation for $2\times 2$ blocks).
5. Initialize all $S$ to zeros so every block's $Q=0$ and every $\tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)}=I$ (identity initialization).
6. During training, optionally apply multiplicative Dropout: randomly replace some components/blocks with identity on each forward pass.
7. After finetuning, fold $R$ into $W^0$ by computing $W_{\text{final}} = R W^0$ and export the single dense matrix for inference.

## Closing notes
BOFT is a practical way to get many of the benefits of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) while dramatically reducing trainable parameters by composing a dense orthogonal transform from a product of sparse orthogonal butterfly components. It generalizes block-diagonal OFT and can interpolate between block structure and full expressivity by tuning $(m,b)$. Expect a small training-time overhead from the factorized multiplication, but no inference cost once you fold the learned $R$ into the pretrained weights.

If you are adapting large LLMs, ViTs, or diffusion U-Nets and want a finetuning method that preserves pretrained geometry, offers a clear parameter budget, and carries a structured inductive bias, BOFT is a practical candidate to add to your PEFT toolbox.



# Numbers That Matter: Efficiency, Stability, and Results Across Modalities

## Quick summary
BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning) is a parameter-efficient way to finetune large pretrained models by replacing a dense orthogonal update with a product of sparse, orthogonal "butterfly" factors. The method preserves the spectral properties of pretrained layers (which improves stability), reduces the number of trainable parameters from quadratic to near-linear in layer width in the butterfly regime, and—per the paper’s experiments—adapts large language models, vision transformers, and text-to-image diffusion models while outperforming strong baselines. Training carries a modest runtime overhead from repeated sparse-factor multiplications, but there is no extra inference cost once the learned orthogonal transform is merged into the pretrained weights.

## The core idea in one paragraph
Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) updates a pretrained weight matrix $W^0$ by left-multiplying it with an orthogonal matrix $R$, i.e. the finetuned weight is $R W^0$. OFT is attractive because orthogonality preserves pairwise neuron angles and the singular-value spectrum of $W^0$, which helps stability and generalization, but a full $d\times d$ orthogonal $R$ costs $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ trainable parameters. BOFT factorizes $R$ as a product of sparse orthogonal factors arranged in a butterfly topology (inspired by the Cooley–Tukey FFT). With a suitable choice of factor count and block size, BOFT achieves a much smaller parameter footprint—e.g., the pure butterfly setting gives $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters—while still producing a dense final transform and retaining orthogonality.

## Why the butterfly factorization helps
The paper frames orthogonal factorization as an information-transmission problem on a $d \times (m+1)$ grid: to get a dense $d\times d$ matrix from $m$ sparse factors you need paths connecting every input node to every output node, while keeping the number of trainable edges small. The butterfly graph is a compact topology that achieves dense connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ nonzero entries across factors, so BOFT uses far fewer free parameters than a full orthogonal matrix but has richer connectivity than a block-diagonal OFT.

BOFT’s parameter count for a given configuration is explicit: BOFT$(m,b)$ uses approximately $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m$ effective trainable parameters for a $d\times n$ linear layer (where $b$ is a block size used in block-butterfly components and $m$ is the number of components). In particular, the classic butterfly choice is $m=\log d$, $b=2$, giving the $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ scaling. BOFT also subsumes OFT variants: BOFT$(1,b)$ reduces to block-diagonal OFT, and BOFT$(1,d)$ is the unconstrained full OFT.

## Stability through spectral norm preservation
A key stability benefit of orthogonal finetuning (OFT and BOFT) is that left-multiplying a pretrained weight $W^0$ by an orthogonal $R$ does not change its singular values. Writing $W^0 = U\Sigma V^\top$, we have $R W^0 = (R U)\,\Sigma\,V^\top$, so the singular-value diagonal $\Sigma$ is unchanged. Preserving the spectral norm helps training stability and generalization (an effect documented in spectral-regularization literature and highlighted in the paper). Compared to additive low-rank updates (e.g. LoRA-style updates), BOFT’s multiplicative orthogonal transform keeps the pretrained layer’s spectral profile intact while still allowing expressive, structured adaptation.

BOFT enforces orthogonality in each sparse factor (the paper uses Cayley parameterization for that), initializes the transform to identity (zero skew-symmetric parameters) so finetuning starts from the exact pretrained model, and provides a multiplicative-dropout scheme adapted to the multiplicative update (randomly replacing some factors or blocks with identity during training) to limit overfitting.

## Empirical story across modalities (what the paper reports)
The paper applies BOFT to finetune three major model families: large language models, vision transformers, and text-to-image diffusion models, covering both language and vision tasks. Across these modalities the empirical finding is consistent: BOFT achieves strong or state-of-the-art adaptation performance while using far fewer trainable parameters than full OFT and competitive parameter budgets compared to other PEFT methods. The authors also run simulations showing BOFT can approximate random dense orthogonal matrices more parameter-efficiently than block-diagonal OFT, supporting the claim that the butterfly factorization yields a better expressivity/parameter-efficiency trade-off.

Importantly for practitioners, BOFT’s trained orthogonal transform can be merged into the pretrained weights after finetuning, so the method adds no inference-time latency or extra compute—only a modest training-time overhead from multiplying through the sparse orthogonal factors during optimization.

## Practical takeaways
- If you want to preserve spectral properties of pretrained layers for stability, use an orthogonal multiplicative update rather than a blind additive one: BOFT preserves singular values by construction.
- BOFT offers a tunable trade-off between parameter count and expressivity via $(m,b)$. Larger $m$ and smaller $b$ push toward denser final transforms with better expressivity; $m=1$ recovers block-diagonal OFT.
- Initialize BOFT to identity (Cayley skew-symmetric zeros) so finetuning starts from the pretrained model, and use multiplicative dropout on factors/blocks to reduce overfitting when needed.
- Expect a modest training-time overhead due to repeated sparse-factor multiplications; after finetuning, merge $R$ into $W^0$ for zero inference overhead.

## Limitations and open questions
The paper notes the training runtime is slightly larger than OFT because of factor multiplication during optimization; optimizing this overhead is future work. It also leaves open whether butterfly is the optimal sparse topology for the information-transmission objective; the graph viewpoint suggests other network topologies from networking literature might be worth exploring.

## Bottom line
BOFT delivers a pragmatic combination: strong empirical adaptation across LLMs, ViTs, and diffusion models; improved stability by preserving spectral norms; substantially fewer trainable parameters in the butterfly regime ($\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ vs. $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$); a short-term training overhead; and zero inference penalty once weights are merged. For large-scale finetuning where parameter budget and stability matter, BOFT provides a compelling middle ground between expressivity and efficiency.



# Expressivity without Excess: The Theory Behind BOFT

Large pretrained models are easiest to adapt when the finetuning update is both expressive and compact. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is an attractive finetuning family because left-multiplying a pretrained weight matrix by an orthogonal matrix preserves pairwise angles between neurons and, importantly, preserves spectral properties of the pretrained weights. The catch is that a dense orthogonal matrix in dimension $d$ costs $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, so the original OFT either pays that price or resorts to block-diagonal structure that severely limits cross-dimension mixing.

BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning) nails a middle ground: it produces dense, globally mixing orthogonal transforms while keeping the trainable parameter count much smaller than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. What follows is a compact walkthrough of why BOFT is more expressive than block-diagonal OFT at the same parameter budget, how butterfly compositions approximate a wide range of orthogonal transforms (and can be extended to span the full orthogonal group in theory), and why orthogonality-preserving updates help stable optimization.

## Orthogonal finetuning in one line

OFT updates a frozen linear layer $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ by left-multiplying with a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and computing
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}.
\]
To maintain orthogonality during optimization the paper uses a Cayley parameterization
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},\qquad \bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top,
\]
which also gives a clean identity initialization when $\bm{Q}=0$.

Block-diagonal OFT reduces parameters by making $\bm{R}$ block-diagonal, but that enforces independent transforms on disjoint coordinate groups and cannot mix information across groups. BOFT achieves dense mixing without paying full $d^2$ cost by factorizing $\bm{R}$ into a product of sparse orthogonal factors organized in a butterfly topology.

## Factorization as information transmission (intuition)

Think of producing a dense $d\times d$ matrix $\bm{R}$ by multiplying $m$ sparse matrices,
\[
\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1.
\]
Unroll that multiplication as a $d\times (m+1)$ grid of nodes: each factor $\bm{B}_i$ defines edges between layer $i$ and $i+1$. A nonzero entry means information can flow along that edge. Two natural desiderata follow:

- dense connectivity: every source index (first column) must be able to reach every sink index (last column), and  
- minimum free edges: use as few trainable nonzeros as possible consistent with orthogonality.

The butterfly topology (from Cooley–Tukey FFT) is a near-optimal network for this problem: with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges it enables full mixing in $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ stages. That is the key to BOFT’s efficiency.

## Butterfly factorization: logarithmic-depth mixing

A single radix-style butterfly factor mixes pairs (or small blocks) of coordinates; composing $m$ such stages exponentially increases the mixing radius. Formally, BOFT builds $\bm{R}$ as a product of block-butterfly components
\[
\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
\]
where each component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is block-sparse (a permutation of small $2b\times 2b$ blocks) and is constrained to be orthogonal (each small block can be parameterized as a $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal via Cayley or a rotation parameterization). The block size $b$ controls per-block expressivity; the number of stages $m$ controls how many rounds of sparse mixing are composed.

Crucial parameter counts:
- BOFT$(m,b)$ uses roughly $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m$ effective trainable parameters.
- If we use the standard butterfly choice $m=\log d$, $b=2$ then BOFT uses $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters.
- Block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$ uses $\mathcal{O}(b d)$ parameters.
- Full OFT uses $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters.

This arithmetic makes the trade-offs explicit: with the same parameter budget you can either increase block size in a single-stage (block-diagonal OFT) or increase the number of sparse stages in BOFT. The latter yields more global mixing per parameter because the stages compound.

## BOFT is strictly more expressive than block-diagonal OFT (same block size)

The paper proves that, for the same block size $b$, the family of orthogonal transforms representable by BOFT strictly contains those representable by block-diagonal OFT. Intuitively, each butterfly stage permutes and mixes small blocks so that composition across $m$ stages can route information between any pair of coordinates; block-diagonal OFT cannot route across blocks at all.

Empirical simulation in the paper (approximating a random $512\times512$ orthogonal matrix) confirms the intuition: BOFT configurations with small $b$ and larger $m$ approximate dense orthogonals with far fewer parameters than a single-stage block-diagonal OFT with the same total parameter count. For example, BOFT$(9,2)$ can outperform BOFT$(1,16)$ while using fewer parameters, and BOFT$(6,4)$ can match BOFT$(2,16)$ with a smaller parameter budget.

## Kaleidoscope-style extension: universality in theory

BOFT can be generalized by alternating different butterfly blocks and their transposes in a kaleidoscope hierarchy. The paper writes a generalized composition
\[
\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,\ell)=\bm{R}_{\ell,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{\ell,2}^\top(m_2,b_2)\cdots\bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{1,2}^\top(m_2,b_2),
\]
and shows that with a sufficient number of alternating butterfly factors this family becomes universal. Concretely, when $m_1=m_2=\log d$, $b_1=b_2=2$ and $\ell=d-1$, the composition can represent the entire orthogonal group in dimension $d$. That establishes a theoretical endpoint: butterfly-based building blocks are not just convenient fast structures — with enough alternating stages they can realize any orthogonal transform.

Two important caveats are worth repeating: (1) universality is a theoretical possibility that requires many factors (and thus many parameters/stages), and (2) in finetuning we usually want a restriction that favors generalization rather than universal expressivity. BOFT provides a structured subset of orthogonals that is much richer than block-diagonal OFT for the same budget, yet still restricted enough to act as a useful inductive bias.

## Spectral preservation: a practical stability advantage

Both OFT and BOFT are left-multiplicative orthogonal updates, so they inherit a useful spectral property. Write the pretrained weight as an SVD,
\[
\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\,\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{V}^\top.
\]
Left-multiplying by any orthogonal $\bm{R}$ yields
\[
\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R}\bm{U})\,\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{V}^\top,
\]
so the singular values $\bm{\Sigma}$ are unchanged. In particular the spectral norm of the layer is preserved. The paper points out — consistent with prior work on spectral control — that preserving spectral properties stabilizes optimization and tends to help generalization. BOFT retains that benefit while enabling richer updates than block-diagonal OFT for the same parameter budget.

## Practical touches and trade-offs

A few implementation and practical notes from the paper are worth flagging:

- Identity initialization: BOFT initializes each butterfly component to identity by setting the Cayley skew-symmetric parameter to zero, which gives a zero-centered start and avoids large initial model drift.
- Multiplicative Dropout: because BOFT is multiplicative, standard additive dropout on updates does not apply directly. The paper proposes a multiplicative dropout that randomly replaces whole butterfly components or diagonal blocks with identity during training to regularize.
- Runtime: composing multiple sparse orthogonal factors introduces modest training overhead compared to single-block OFT. This is a deliberate space-for-computation trade-off. Crucially, after finetuning the learned orthogonal product can be folded into the pretrained weights, so there is no inference-time latency penalty.

## Bottom line

BOFT gives you dense, globally mixing orthogonal updates at a parameter cost that scales like $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ in the canonical butterfly setup, versus $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for full OFT and $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ for block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. The butterfly composition compounds local, sparse orthogonal operations into long-range mixing, which makes BOFT provably and empirically more expressive than block-diagonal OFT at the same block-size budget. At the same time, because BOFT’s updates are orthogonal, they preserve the pretrained layer’s singular values — a property linked to stable optimization and better generalization.

If you care about expressive, stable finetuning without ballooning parameter counts, BOFT is a structurally principled way to get there: expressivity without excess.



# Tuning the Butterfly: Choosing Depth m and Block Size b

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) gives you a structured, parameter‑efficient way to adapt pretrained weights: an orthogonal matrix $R$ is represented as a product of $m$ sparse (butterfly) components whose block size is controlled by $b$. The pair $(m,b)$ is the core design knob. This post gives a concise, practical playbook for choosing $(m,b)$, explains the tradeoffs between parameters, expressivity and regularity, and describes where the BOFT-specific multiplicative Dropout fits into tuning and ablations. All guidance below is grounded in the BOFT formulation and empirical observations reported in the paper.

## The math you need (short and usable)

- Effective trainable parameters per finetuned linear layer (as used in the paper):
  $$
  P \;=\; \frac{1}{2}(b-1)\, d \, m,
  $$
  where $d$ is the layer dimension (the dimension of the orthogonal matrix $R$), $b\ge 2$ is the block size, and $m$ is the number of butterfly components (depth).

- The butterfly construction reaches a fully dense orthogonal matrix when
  $$
  m \approx \log_2\!\left(\frac{2d}{b}\right).
  $$
  In particular, the canonical minimal-density choice for the classic radix-2 butterfly is $b=2$ with $m=\log_2 d$, giving the familiar $O(d\log d)$ parametrization.

- Special cases:
  - BOFT$(1,b)$ = block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$.
  - BOFT$(1,d)$ = full (unconstrained) orthogonal OFT (i.e., the original dense OFT).
  - BOFT$(\log_2 d, 2)$ = full butterfly factorization with $O(d\log d)$ parameters.

These relations let you translate an allowed parameter budget $P$ into allowable $(m,b)$ pairs and vice versa.

## Expressivity vs regularity vs compute — how $(m,b)$ moves the needle

- Increasing $m$ (deeper factorization) generally increases expressivity per parameter. The paper shows that, for fixed $d$, smaller $b$ combined with larger $m$ tends to be more parameter‑efficient when approximating dense orthogonal maps: many sparse factors can compose to a dense transform with far fewer parameters than a single dense block.

- Increasing $b$ (larger blocks) concentrates parameters in wider per-component blocks; this is closer to block‑diagonal OFT behavior and moves you toward a less structured (more flexible) family for small $m$. For the same $P$, larger $b$ implies fewer components $m$.

- Regularity (bias toward stable/generalizable transforms) grows with structure. The butterfly family embeds many fast linear transforms (Fourier, Hadamard, etc.), so using structured factorizations (smaller $b$, nontrivial $m$) injects an inductive bias that can improve generalization on limited data.

- Compute cost during training grows with $m$: each forward/backward requires applying $m$ sparse components (there is some runtime overhead compared with a single block). However, after finetuning you can absorb $R$ into the pretrained weight, so inference cost is unchanged.

In short: $m$ increases expressivity-per-parameter but costs runtime during training; $b$ trades parameter density vs block granularity and affects the inductive bias.

## Practical heuristics (rules of thumb)

These are operational guidelines you can apply using the formulas above.

- If your parameter budget is very tight (you need maximal parameter efficiency), start with $b=2$ and set $m$ so that $P=\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1) d m$ matches your available budget. The $b=2$ setting is the most parameter‑efficient route to build dense connectivity.

- If you have moderate budget but want stronger regularization (small datasets, high overfitting risk), favor smaller $b$ with larger $m$: this gives a structured hypothesis class that often generalizes better than a big block or full dense orthogonal matrix.

- If you have a generous parameter budget but limited training compute/time, prefer larger $b$ and smaller $m$. That concentrates capacity into fewer components, which lowers the multiplicative overhead per update.

- If you specifically need a dense orthogonal $R$ (full connectivity), pick $m \approx \log_2(2d/b)$. For $b=2$ that reduces to $m=\log_2 d$.

- When in doubt, follow a two-stage approach: first search along the "depth" direction (increase $m$ at small $b$) because BOFT typically gains more expressivity-per-parameter from depth; if gains saturate, increase $b$.

Concrete (paper‑anchored) examples for intuition: in the paper’s 512×512 synthetic approximation experiment the authors observed that BOFT$(9,2)$ gave better approximation than BOFT$(1,16)$ while using significantly fewer parameters. Using the formula
$$
P=\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m,
$$
you can compute the effective parameters for those cases (with $d=512$):
- BOFT$(9,2)$: $P = \tfrac{1}{2}(2-1)\cdot 512 \cdot 9 = 2304$ params.
- BOFT$(1,16)$: $P = \tfrac{1}{2}(16-1)\cdot 512 \cdot 1 = 3840$ params.

The paper also notes BOFT$(6,4)$ produces expressivity comparable to BOFT$(2,16)$ with noticeably fewer parameters — the same $P$ formula lets you compare these choices directly.

## Multiplicative Dropout — how and when to use it

BOFT’s parameterization is multiplicative (an orthogonal $R$ multiplies the frozen matrix), so standard additive dropout on update weights does not directly apply. The paper proposes multiplicative Dropout:

- Randomly pick some fraction $p_1$ of the butterfly components and/or a fraction $p_2$ of the diagonal blocks inside each component, and replace the selected blocks with identity matrices during training steps.

Practical guidance (grounded in the paper’s motivation):

- Use multiplicative Dropout when overfitting is suspected, especially for designs with large $m$ (many components) or when working with small datasets. Replacing components with identity reduces the effective degrees of freedom during training while preserving the identity initialization behavior.

- Because BOFT is identity-initialized (Cayley parameterization initialized to identity), multiplicative Dropout preserves the property that the model starts from the pretrained behavior.

- Tune the dropout fractions empirically for your task; multiplicative Dropout is a tool to trade variance for bias in the usual way — increase dropout when validation performance signals overfitting.

(The paper introduces the multiplicative Dropout mechanism but does not prescribe fixed numeric rates; treat $p_1,p_2$ like other regularizers to be validated on held-out data.)

## An ablation-minded search plan

If you want to explore the $(m,b)$ space systematically while respecting a parameter budget, run the following controlled ablations:

1. Decide per-layer parameter budget $P$ (or a global budget and allocate to important layers).
2. Pick a set of candidate depths $m$ (for example, integers from $1$ up to $\log_2 d$).
3. For each chosen $m$, compute the block size that fits the budget:
   $$
   b = 1 + \frac{2P}{d m}.
   $$
   Round $b$ to an appropriate integer block size that matches your implementation constraints (the BOFT construction assumes block‑aligned components).
4. Train/fine‑tune each $(m,b)$ candidate and compare validation metrics. Because these candidates are approximately iso-parametric, this isolates the effect of depth vs block size.
5. If a depth‑heavy model overfits, add multiplicative Dropout and re-evaluate.
6. If runtime is problematic, cap $m$ and favor tuning $b$.

This iso‑param sweep is exactly the clean experiment design to reveal whether expressivity gains are primarily coming from depth (more components) or from block width.

## Quick practical checklist

- Translate your available parameter budget to layer budget using $P=\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1) d m$.
- If you want maximal parameter efficiency: start with $b=2$ and grow $m$.
- If you want lower training runtime or simpler per-step compute: increase $b$, reduce $m$.
- For small datasets or strong generalization needs: favor structured (smaller $b$, nontrivial $m$) designs; use multiplicative Dropout if overfitting appears.
- If you need full density, use $m\approx\log_2(2d/b)$ (or $m=\log_2 d$ when $b=2$).
- Always initialize components to identity (Cayley parameterization) so finetuning starts from the pretrained model; after training you can absorb $R$ into the pretrained weights to eliminate inference overhead.

## Final notes and caveats

- Training runtime increases with $m$ because you apply more factors each step; the paper notes this training overhead is the main practical cost. You can collapse the learned $R$ into pretrained weights after finetuning to avoid inference cost.
- BOFT is a structured inductive bias: smaller $b$ and moderate-to-large $m$ often give better expressivity-per-parameter than wide blocks, but the best choice is task-dependent. Use iso-param sweeps as described to discover what works for your task and dataset size.
- The paper provides the theoretical foundation and motivating examples (including the 512×512 approximation simulation). Use the parameter equations and the ablation procedure above to explore $(m,b)$ in your own training setup.

If you want, I can produce a small script/notebook that computes iso-param candidate pairs $(m,b)$ for your given $d$ and $P$, and outputs sensible integer choices that respect implementation constraints (e.g., block divisibility) to kick off your ablations.



# Beyond Butterflies: Network Topologies as a New Design Space for PEFT

The BOFT paper reframes efficient orthogonal finetuning as an information-routing problem and shows that a carefully chosen topology — the classical butterfly pattern from FFT — lets you build a dense orthogonal transform with dramatically fewer trainable parameters (roughly $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ instead of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$). That idea is simple and powerful: instead of learning one dense orthogonal matrix $R$, learn a product of sparse orthogonal factors
\[
R = B_m B_{m-1}\cdots B_1,
\]
and choose the sparsity pattern of the $B_i$ so that information from every input coordinate can reach every output coordinate through the $d\times(m+1)$ grid implied by the factorization.

Butterfly is a very natural and effective point in that design space, but it is only one topology. In this post I sketch a practical research agenda motivated by the paper: treat network topologies (the adjacency patterns of the factors) as a core design axis for PEFT. The goal: find topologies that keep inference free (fold the learned orthogonal into the frozen weights), reduce train-time overhead, preserve the spectral and regularity benefits of orthogonal finetuning, and combine well with other PEFT tools (low-rank adapters, pruning, quantization).

## Quick recap (what BOFT gives you)

Orthogonal finetuning (OFT) multiplies a frozen pretrained layer $W^0$ on the left by an orthogonal matrix $R$ so that the layer becomes $R W^0$. BOFT parameterizes $R$ as a product of sparse orthogonal "butterfly components" and enforces orthogonality of each factor (e.g., via Cayley parametrization $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ with $Q=-Q^\top$, or via 2×2 rotations). BOFT($m,b$) denotes using $m$ components with block size $b$; BOFT achieves a dense $R$ with fewer parameters because each component has only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ nonzeros and there are $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ components in the radix-2 butterfly, giving the $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ statistics the paper emphasizes. Important practical points from the paper that we should preserve while designing new topologies:

- Two desiderata from the information-transmission view: dense connectivity (every source node can reach every target node) and minimum free/trainable edges under orthogonality constraints.
- Identity initialization of factors and multiplicative dropout are useful stabilizers in training.
- After finetuning, $R$ can be folded into $W^0$ to incur no inference-time cost.

## Why topology matters (beyond parameter count)

Topology controls several interacting properties that determine whether a factorized orthogonal parameterization is useful in practice:

- Expressivity-per-parameter. Some sparsity patterns let you approximate a large portion of the orthogonal group with few parameters; others cannot.
- Training-time cost. The factorization trades parameters for extra computation. A topology that admits a fast matvec (like the FFT-style butterfly) reduces wall-clock overhead.
- Hardware friendliness. Regular patterns, simple permutations, and block structures map far better to GPUs/TPUs than a sea of tiny scattered nonzeros.
- Compatibility with orthogonality constraints. We need each factor to be orthogonal (or otherwise enforce orthogonality globally); some sparsity patterns make convenient orthogonal parameterizations easier than others.
- Inductive bias. The pattern imposes structure on the allowed transforms; when chosen well this improves generalization on small-data tasks.

The paper uses butterfly because it balances these concerns well. The broader point is: topology is a knob we can tune, and tuning it opens new trade-offs.

## Candidate topologies (what to try and why)

Below are families of topologies that are natural to explore next. I phrase these as hypotheses grounded by the paper's desiderata — each family brings a different mix of expansion (fast mixing), locality (cache-friendly), and regularity (easy parametrization).

- Butterfly (baseline). Already shown to work: $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ nonzeros, fast structured matvec, and connection to many classic fast transforms.
- Expander-like patterns. Sparse graphs with strong expansion properties can mix information quickly with constant degree; they may allow dense connectivity in $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ layers with very few edges per layer.
- Layered permutation/route networks (Clos, Beneš, permutation networks). These networks realize arbitrary permutations with small depth and regular routing patterns, which could be turned into sparse orthogonal layers with good hardware locality.
- Low-diameter algebraic graphs (de Bruijn, Kautz, hypercube). These have logarithmic diameter and regular structure, offering predictable depth vs degree trade-offs.
- Circulant / convolutional-like structures. Circulant matrices are parameter-efficient and admit fast convolution-like algorithms; hybrid designs that mix circulant blocks and sparse factors may yield favorable compute trade-offs.
- Sequences of Givens rotations or Householder reflectors with structured pairings. Givens rotations are naturally 2×2 orthogonal factors. The question is whether there exist pairing schedules (i.e., topologies) that match butterfly-like efficiency while being simpler to implement on hardware.
- Small-world / hierarchical topologies. Combine local dense neighborhoods with a few long-range shortcuts; this can preserve locality (good for cache and parallelism) while enabling global mixing in few steps.

None of these are guaranteed winners; the right choice depends on how many parameters you want, how much compute you can tolerate at training time, and what hardware primitive you want to exploit. The paper’s information-transmission framing gives us a clear evaluation metric: can the topology connect every source to every sink in the chosen depth, and how many trainable elements (free edges) does that require under orthogonality?

## Training-time and hardware considerations

A major practical barrier for BOFT-style methods is training-time overhead: applying and backpropagating through multiple sparse orthogonal factors every step. Here are practical levers to reduce that overhead while preserving the inference-free property:

- Favor topologies with fast structured matvecs. The butterfly admits an FFT-like $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ apply. When exploring topologies, prefer those where factor application reduces to a sequence of regular permutes and small block multiplies rather than wildly scattered sparse GEMMs.
- Tune block size $b$ to trade arithmetic intensity against parameter count (BOFT($m,b$)). Larger blocks increase per-kernel work and GPU efficiency at the cost of more parameters.
- Fuse factor applications. If your topology permits, implement each layer of factors as a batched small-GEMM over contiguous memory with precomputed permutation indexing; this avoids kernel launch overhead.
- Exploit identity initialization and multiplicative dropout during early training to stabilize and allow larger learning rates.
- Use low-precision arithmetic for the factor parameters or their gradients where possible, but be careful: orthogonality constraints are sensitive, so test quantization-aware training or freeze orthogonality with re-projection steps.
- Measure wall-clock, not just FLOPs. On modern accelerators, many small ops are far slower than a single dense op with the same FLOPs.

Topologies that yield regular permutations and dense small blocks will typically map best to current GPU/TPU hardware.

## Composing BOFT with other PEFT ideas

BOFT is a multiplicative reparametrization. It is natural to combine it with complementary PEFT mechanisms:

- BOFT + LoRA (multiplicative + additive). Use a small multiplicative orthogonal preconditioner $R$ to preserve spectral structure and add a tiny low-rank residual (LoRA) to capture fine-grained changes. This keeps most of the pretrained geometry while letting the model adapt in an unrestricted low-dimensional subspace.
- BOFT + structured sparsity / pruning. Start from a richer topology and gradually prune factor entries while maintaining the dense-connectivity constraint. The information-transmission view helps ensure pruned patterns maintain reachability.
- BOFT + quantization. Fold $R$ into $W^0$ at the end and quantize the resulting weights for deployment. For more aggressive memory savings, investigate quantizing factor parameters during training with orthogonality-aware projections or quantization-aware training.
- BOFT + adapters / prompt tuning. Apply BOFT selectively to layers known to influence a task more (e.g., feedforward vs projection heads), and use adapters where multiplicative structure is less helpful.

These hybrids are sensible because BOFT preserves the spectral norm of $W^0$ (left multiplication by orthogonal $R$ leaves singular values unchanged), which can stabilize training; additive low-rank components can then model residual directions without breaking that stability.

## How to search the topology space (practical recipe)

A pragmatic search loop combines simple theory-guided priors with small-scale empirical probes:

1. Define topology families (butterfly, expander, permutation networks, circulant, Givens schedules).
2. For each candidate, fix depth $m$ and block size $b$, parameterize each factor as orthogonal blocks (e.g., 2×2 rotations or Cayley transforms).
3. Proxy test: attempt to approximate a random dense orthogonal matrix of size $d$ and measure approximation error vs effective parameter count (the paper uses this exact proxy). This gives an expressivity curve cheaply.
4. Measure structured matvec cost: benchmark factor-apply throughput and wall-clock time on target hardware.
5. Run a few-shot finetuning experiment on a representative downstream task to assess generalization and stability.
6. Iterate: favor topologies with good expressivity-per-parameter and low wall-clock overhead.

For automated search, use a mixed strategy: rule-based pruning based on theoretical metrics (degree, diameter, expansion) plus black-box optimization (evolutionary search or bandit-based selection) over depth and block-size hyperparameters. Differentiable topology search is possible (relax adjacency masks), but orthogonality constraints complicate gradients and may require special handling.

## Open questions and research directions

- Are there topologies with a strictly better parameter/time/generalization frontier than butterfly for common finetuning regimes?
- Can we derive lower bounds that tie graph-theoretic metrics (degree, expansion, spectral gap, diameter) to the number of layers required to approximate arbitrary orthogonal transforms under orthogonality constraints?
- How does the optimal topology depend on layer type (attention projection vs MLP) and dataset size (few-shot vs many-shot)?
- How do pruning and quantization interact with orthogonality constraints for factorized topologies? Can we maintain provable connectivity under aggressive pruning?
- What hardware primitives (fused small-GEMMs, permutation kernels, in-place 2×2 rotations) give the best training throughput for particular topologies?
- Can we design topology search algorithms that trade off expressivity, train-time throughput, and final generalization in a single optimization loop?

These questions are opportunities for both theoretical work and careful systems+empirical engineering. The BOFT paper gives us a clear conceptual and experimental baseline for these investigations.

## Takeaway

BOFT reframes orthogonal finetuning as a routing problem on a grid of nodes and uses the butterfly topology to get a compelling parameter/expressivity compromise. The next step is to treat the topology itself as a design variable. Graph-theoretic properties (connectivity, expansion, diameter), hardware-mapping considerations (regularity, block structure), and compatibility with orthogonality should guide the search. Doing so opens practical wins: faster training, the same inference-free deployment, and potentially better generalization by tailoring inductive biases to task and hardware.

If you are excited by BOFT, consider running the proxy experiments suggested in the paper (approximate random orthogonals, measure approximation error vs parameters) across a few topology families and profiling factor-apply kernels on your target hardware. Those two signals — expressivity curves and wall-clock cost — will quickly narrow the design space for topologies worth scaling to real finetuning workloads.
