\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 12: Extensions and Variants

# Kaleidoscope hierarchies: alternating products and transposes
One of the cleanest generalizations of the BOFT parameterization presented in the paper is the kaleidoscope-style composition that alternates products of butterfly-parameterized orthogonal blocks and their transposes. The paper introduces this idea explicitly: starting from the BOFT blockwise product notation $\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, we can form a generalized orthogonal matrix by interleaving different BOFT factors and their transposes. Concretely the paper writes this family as
\[
\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,\ell)
= \bm{R}_{\ell,1}(m_1,b_1)\,\bm{R}_{\ell,2}^\top(m_2,b_2)\,
\cdots\,
\bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1)\,\bm{R}_{1,2}^\top(m_2,b_2),
\]
where each $\bm{R}_{i,j}(\cdot,\cdot)$ is itself a BOFT-style orthogonal factor. The paper points out an important expressivity result driven by this construction: when one takes $m_1=m_2=\log d$, $b_1=b_2=2$, and composes $\ell=d-1$ alternating factors, the resulting family is rich enough to represent the entire orthogonal group in dimension $d$. This composition is exactly what the paper refers to as a kaleidoscope hierarchy (and ties back to prior work on kaleidoscope parameterizations).

Intuitively, alternating products and transposes multiplies mixing stages with different alignments so that each stage can re-route and recombine coordinates in ways that a single sequence of butterfly factors cannot. Theorem (Expressivity of BOFT) in the paper formalizes that BOFT with suitable alternation can approximate general orthogonal matrices; the kaleidoscope extension simply makes this explicit and constructive.

The paper also emphasizes a practical caveat: more expressivity is not always better for finetuning. Full expressivity (i.e., the ability to represent any orthogonal matrix) removes regularizing structure and can hurt generalization, just as full finetuning often underperforms more constrained PEFT choices. Thus the kaleidoscope hierarchy is presented in the paper both as a theoretical endpoint (how to reach full orthogonal expressivity) and as a practical knob: it lets practitioners expand BOFT toward full expressivity in a controlled way by increasing $m_1,m_2$ and $\ell$, while being mindful of the trade-off between expressivity and regularity.

# Dynamic selection of m and b per layer or per training stage
A central design pair in BOFT is the number of butterfly components $m$ and the block parameter $b$ (the paper denotes BOFT as BOFT$(m,b)$). These two integers jointly determine both expressivity and parameter cost. The paper gives a compact expression for the effective number of trainable parameters of BOFT on a linear layer of input dimension $d$:
\[
\text{effective parameters} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m.
\]
Several useful special cases are given in the paper: BOFT$(1,b)$ reduces to the block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$; BOFT$(1,d)$ is the original (unconstrained) full OFT orthogonal matrix; and BOFT$\!\big(\log\frac{2d}{b},\,b\big)$ corresponds to using the block butterfly matrix $\bm{B}^{\frac{b}{2}}(d)$ and yields a dense orthogonal matrix while keeping the per-factor structure.

Because $m$ and $b$ directly trade parameter count and connectivity, the paper observes that configurations with smaller $b$ and larger $m$ are often more parameter-efficient in practice: they achieve a given approximation quality with fewer parameters than a single large block. The empirical simulation summarized in the paper shows, for example, that BOFT variants with more stages and smaller blocks (e.g., many $2\times 2$-style blocks) can approximate random orthogonal matrices with fewer parameters than coarse block-diagonal parameterizations.

This explicit control suggests a natural extension — which the paper frames as a design opportunity — to choose $(m,b)$ adaptively across layers or across training stages. Different layers of a pretrained model have different sensitivity and different roles (e.g., early layers tend to represent low-level features, later layers more abstract semantics), and the BOFT parameterization makes it straightforward to allocate more degrees of freedom where they are needed by increasing $b$ or $m$, and to economize where stability/regularity are preferred by keeping $b$ small or $m$ small. Likewise, a staged training schedule that starts close to identity (small effective parameters) and gradually increases $m$ or $b$ can be accommodated within the BOFT family because BOFT naturally supports identity initialization (all components initialized to identity via Cayley transform). The paper itself does not report a layerwise adaptive or stagewise schedule, but its analysis and parameter-count formulas make clear that these dynamic design choices are immediate and well motivated.

# Learnable or data-dependent permutations between components
One of the structural pieces the paper highlights is that butterfly components can be viewed as permutations of the base 2-by-2 interaction pattern: the nonzero pattern of a butterfly component is essentially a permuted arrangement of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$ blocks. The paper explicitly notes that "the butterfly structure along with permutations can perfectly recover many classic linear transforms" (for example, fast Fourier and Hadamard transforms are recoverable when permutations are chosen appropriately). Thus permutations are an integral ingredient in the way BOFT mixes coordinates across stages.

This observation opens two model-design choices discussed in the paper. The first is to fix a canonical permutation schedule (as in the radix-2 Cooley–Tukey FFT) and learn only the continuous entries of the butterfly blocks. The second (presented in prior related work cited by the paper) is to allow permutations to adapt so that the overall topological routing best matches data structure. The paper cites prior works that learn fast linear transforms with butterfly parameterizations, which implicitly exploit permutation flexibility to expand expressivity.

The paper frames learnable or data-dependent permutations as a natural extension: by allowing the permutation wiring between stages to be chosen or adapted (either by discrete selection or by continuous relaxations), one can steer where information mixes early and where it mixes later. Because permutations alter the routing of information but do not change the count of parameters per se, they are a powerful lever for expressivity without necessarily increasing raw parameter budget. The paper, however, does not detail specific algorithms for learning discrete permutations; it presents this as an avenue compatible with the BOFT framework and with the general information-transmission perspective that drives the design.

# Alternative topologies: Beneš, hierarchical, or graph-inspired designs
The information-transmission framing in the paper — representing a factorization $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1$ as a grid of nodes and edges — is deliberately chosen to make contact with classical network topologies studied in computer networking and parallel algorithms. The butterfly topology is one such efficient topology: it achieves dense connectivity from $d$ sources to $d$ receivers using only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ sparse factor blocks. The paper explicitly calls attention to the fact that other topologies from networking and sparse-factorization literature are conceptually available and may be studied through the same lens.

The paper uses the butterfly as its main example because of its proven efficiency (small number of edges/stages for dense connectivity) and because it aligns with many fast linear transforms. But the information-transmission framework treats topologies abstractly: for any choice of per-stage adjacency pattern that ensures every source can reach every sink, one can attempt to parameterize each stage as an orthogonal sparse factor and thereby obtain a dense composite orthogonal matrix. The paper notes that orthogonality imposes constraints (e.g., each stage must have at least $d$ edges to be full-rank), so any candidate topology must be compatible with those algebraic requirements.

In this sense, hierarchical arrangements, permutation networks (e.g., networks known in switching theory), or more general graph-inspired designs can all be ported into the BOFT-style approach. The paper does not claim or evaluate specific alternate topologies, but it highlights the opportunity: the information-transmission viewpoint reduces the search for parameter-efficient orthogonal factorizations to the search for sparse topologies with favorable edge/level trade-offs that respect orthogonality. Exploring such topologies is explicitly suggested as an open direction in the paper.

# Combining BOFT with quantization, pruning, and low-rank methods
The paper positions BOFT as one family of structural parameter-efficiency — namely, sparse factorization leading to orthogonal updates — and contrasts this with the low-rank additive reparameterizations exemplified by LoRA. It explicitly points out that both low-rank matrices and sparse (butterfly-style) matrices are major families of structured matrices that achieve parameter efficiency. This framing motivates natural combinations.

While the paper does not report experiments that fuse BOFT with quantization, pruning, or additive low-rank corrections, it records properties that make such combinations conceptually straightforward. For instance, BOFT preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix (because the learned factor is orthogonal), which yields favorable training stability; after finetuning the orthogonal factors can be multiplied into the pretrained model so there is no inference latency penalty. Those properties make BOFT-friendly to post-processing steps common in deployment: the merged weights can be quantized or pruned in the same way as ordinary dense pretrained weights, and the BOFT parameter budget could be supplemented by low-rank additive corrections (LoRA-style) if a small residual correction is needed beyond a multiplicative orthogonal reweighting.

The paper also introduces multiplicative Dropout as a regularizer tailored to the multiplicative orthogonal structure; this highlights that BOFT admits specific regularization tactics that interact well with other compression methods. The paper leaves detailed mixed-compression recipes as a practical direction rather than as part of the core contribution, but the text establishes conceptual compatibility between BOFT and complementary compression techniques.

# Multi-task and continual learning with shared BOFT components
Because BOFT is a finetuning paradigm where a small, structured orthogonal transform is learned and applied across neurons in a layer, it naturally lends itself to parameter-sharing strategies that are appealing in multi-task and continual learning settings. The paper emphasizes that BOFT learns layer-shared orthogonal matrices to transform the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ as $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, and that $\bm{R}$ is initialized at identity so the pretrained model is preserved at the start. These properties make BOFT a natural adapter: one can imagine reusing a core set of BOFT components across tasks while learning task-specific light-weight components on top.

The paper does not experimentally pursue multi-task or continual learning, but its formulation suggests a few patterns that sit cleanly inside the BOFT family. For example, a shared base BOFT factorization could be learned once and then kept frozen while task-specific BOFT stages (extra factors or small additive corrections) are learned per task; because BOFT factors can be composed and composed transposes are allowed in the kaleidoscope extension, it is straightforward to design a hierarchical sharing strategy. Identity initialization ensures that adding a new task starts from the pretrained behaviour, and the fact that BOFT factors can be multiplied into the base weights after training eliminates per-task inference overhead if desired. The paper frames these multi-task and continual-learning possibilities as natural consequences of the BOFT design without making empirical claims.

# Geodesic or Riemannian optimization variants for orthogonal constraints
The paper enforces orthogonality of each BOFT factor via Cayley parameterization. Concretely it uses the Cayley form
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric ($\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$). This choice guarantees orthogonality for each factor throughout training, allows exact identity initialization (by initializing $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$), and is the parameterization used both in OFT and BOFT in the paper. The Cayley transform therefore plays a practical role: it enforces the orthogonality constraint in a differentiable reparameterization that is compatible with standard gradient updates.

The paper does not pursue manifold-optimization algorithms explicitly (for example, optimization that moves directly along geodesics on the orthogonal group), but the Cayley parameterization is one concrete mechanism the paper relies on to maintain orthogonality while keeping implementation simple. From the paper's perspective, Cayley provides the operational solution used in all experiments and derivations. Exploring other orthogonality-preserving parameterizations or optimization techniques (if desired) is treated as an alternative design choice in the broader space; the paper documents the Cayley choice as the practical instantiation that supports BOFT's identity initialization and orthogonality constraints.

# Summary and practical guidance
The BOFT family introduced in the paper is deliberately modular, and the paper repeatedly frames its contributions in terms of structural choices that control the trade-off between expressivity and regularity. The kaleidoscope hierarchy gives a route to full orthogonal expressivity by alternating BOFT factors and their transposes; the pair $(m,b)$ cleanly parameterizes a continuum from block-diagonal OFT to dense orthogonal transforms; permutations and wiring patterns are explicit design levers captured by the information-transmission perspective; and the Cayley reparameterization affords identity initialization and exact orthogonality during training.

Practical implications emphasized in the paper are these: (i) choose smaller $b$ with larger $m$ when you want parameter efficiency and smoother interpolation to identity; (ii) use BOFT$(1,b)$ when a block-wise orthogonal update suffices (recovering block-diagonal OFT), and increase $m$ or introduce kaleidoscope alternation only when you need more expressivity; (iii) exploit identity initialization to keep the finetuned model close to pretrained behaviour; (iv) consider multiplicative Dropout (as introduced in the paper) to regularize multiplicative orthogonal updates.

Beyond these concrete recommendations, the paper positions BOFT as a flexible framework: the information-transmission view and the butterfly instantiation are stepping stones toward many plausible variants (alternate topologies, learned permutations, mixed compression strategies, and structured sharing for multi-task learning). The paper documents the core mechanics and empirical advantages of BOFT and leaves this ecosystem of extensions as principled directions to explore using the same analytical lens.
