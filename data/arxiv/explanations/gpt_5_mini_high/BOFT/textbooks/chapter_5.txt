\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 5: BOFT: Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning

# Butterfly factor and butterfly component definitions
The basic building block of the BOFT parameterization is the butterfly factor. For an even integer $k$ (in practice we work with powers of two), a butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}$ is written in block form as
\[
\bm{B}^F(k)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_2)\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\]
where each $\bm{d}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}$ and $\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_i)$ denotes the diagonal matrix with entries $\bm{d}_i$. Conceptually, a butterfly factor mixes two equal-size groups of coordinates by applying diagonal (i.e., elementwise) multipliers to each half and then interleaving them; this sparse 2-by-2 block pattern is the canonical local mixing primitive used in classic fast transforms.

A butterfly component at stride $k$ is a block-diagonal matrix that places many copies of such butterfly factors along the diagonal. Writing $d$ for the full dimension (in the paper $d$ is assumed to be a power of two), the component is
\[
\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)=\operatorname{diag}\big(\bm{B}^F_1(k),\bm{B}^F_2(k),\dots,\bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k)\big),
\]
so $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is block-diagonal with $d/k$ blocks each equal in sparsity pattern to $\bm{B}^F(k)$. Each component is therefore sparse (only $O(d)$ nonzero diagonals per component) but will be combined with other components to produce global mixing.

# Recursive construction of butterfly matrices
Butterfly matrices arise by composing a sequence of these components at different scales. When $d=2^N$, the full $d\times d$ (scalar) butterfly matrix is built recursively as
\[
\bm{B}(d)
=
\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\cdot
\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{B}_1(d/2) & \bm{0}\\[4pt]
\bm{0} & \bm{B}_2(d/2)
\end{bmatrix}
\;=\;
\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\,\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d/2)\cdots\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2),
\]
where each $\bm{B}_1(\cdot)$ and $\bm{B}_2(\cdot)$ is itself a smaller butterfly matrix, and each $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ is the block-diagonal component defined above. Viewed this way, the full transform is the product of $\log_2 d$ components with decreasing block sizes: the first component mixes large chunks, the next mixes finer-grained pairs inside those chunks, and so on, until the final $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$ mixes element pairs.

Two points to keep in mind. First, each component is sparse and structured (block-diagonal with small 2-by-2-like blocks), so storage is linear in $d$ per component. Second, because these components are placed at different scales and then multiplied together, the overall matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ can be dense even though every individual component is sparse. That is the key property that lets the butterfly parameterization achieve dense mixing with only $O(d\log d)$ sparse factors.

# From 2x2 to block 2b x 2b orthogonal building blocks
The scalar-valued butterfly factors above can be generalized to block form to control the trade-off between parameter count and expressivity. Replace each scalar entry in the diagonal vectors $\bm{d}_i$ by a $b\times b$ matrix (for an integer $b\ge 1$). This yields a block butterfly component which we denote $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$. Concretely, each $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is replaced by a block version whose entries are $b\times b$ matrices, so each small 2-by-2 scalar factor becomes a $2b\times 2b$ block.

Doing this blockification has two effects that are useful in practice. First, it increases the per-block degrees of freedom (each block can implement a small orthogonal transform rather than just a scalar multiplier), which raises expressivity without blowing up parameter count across the whole layer. Second, it preserves the sparse, structured pattern of butterfly components: for $k>2$ the nonzero pattern of every $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ is just a block-wise permutation of the $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$ pattern. By varying $b$ (block size) and the number of stages $m$ we can smoothly interpolate between highly parameter-efficient but restricted transforms and more expressive ones.

# Ensuring orthogonality via Cayley transforms on small blocks
BOFT imposes orthogonality on the learned transform at every component so that the entire product remains orthogonal (product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal). To guarantee orthogonality in a numerically stable and parameter-efficient way, each small block (the minimal blocks in $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$, i.e., the $2b\times 2b$ blocks) is parameterized to be orthogonal. A convenient and widely used reparameterization is the Cayley transform: any orthogonal matrix without $-1$ eigenvalues can be written as
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric ($\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$). Applying the Cayley transform block-wise lets us parameterize every $2b\times 2b$ block with the independent entries of a skew-symmetric matrix and guarantees exact orthogonality of that block. Because each butterfly component is a permutation of these $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks, the entire component is orthogonal; therefore the product of components
\[
\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}
\]
is orthogonal whenever every $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is orthogonal.

For the smallest scalar case ($b=1$), each $2\times 2$ block can be parameterized as a planar rotation (one scalar angle), which is a special case of the Cayley parameterization. In practice the Cayley parameterization provides a uniform mechanism across block sizes for enforcing orthogonality and for initializing to identity (see below).

# BOFT(m, b): depth m, block size b, and resulting R(m, b)
BOFT is specified by two integers: the number of butterfly stages $m$ (the depth) and the block size parameter $b$. For notational convenience the paper denotes the $i$-th component by
\[
\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)} \coloneqq \tilde{\bm{B}}^b\big(d,\,2^{\,m-i+1}\big),
\]
so the learned orthogonal transform is the product of these $m$ orthogonal components:
\[
\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}.
\]
Given a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, BOFT applies the learned orthogonal transform multiplicatively on the left and leaves $\bm{W}^0$ frozen; the forward pass becomes
\[
\bm{z} = \big(\bm{R}(m,b)\,\bm{W}^0\big)^\top \bm{x},
\]
with $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ the input and $\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^n$ the output. Because each component is orthogonal by construction, $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is orthogonal and the spectral properties of the pretrained weight matrix (e.g., singular values) are preserved up to the orthogonal left multiplication.

Intuitively, $m$ controls how many scales of mixing are available (larger $m$ yields finer mixing and can densify the overall product), while $b$ controls how many degrees of freedom each local mixing unit has (larger $b$ gives bigger local orthogonal blocks and hence more parameters per stage). By choosing $(m,b)$ we trade between parameter cost, computational cost during training (because each component must be multiplied at each forward/backward pass), and expressivity.

# Special cases: BOFT(1, b) equals block-diagonal OFT; BOFT(1, d) equals full OFT
The BOFT family subsumes the OFT variants previously used in the literature as special cases. When $m=1$ there is only a single butterfly component, and that component is block-diagonal: BOFT(1,b) is therefore exactly the block-diagonal Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) with block size $b$. If we further set $b=d$ (one block covering the whole dimension), BOFT(1,d) corresponds to an unconstrained full orthogonal matrix on the $d$-dimensional space and thus matches the original full-matrix OFT. These equivalences show BOFT is a strict generalization: by increasing $m$ (adding more sparse components) one can go from a coarse block-diagonal transform to denser, more expressive transforms without abandoning the orthogonality constraint.

# Dense orthogonals with BOFT(log(2d/b), b)
One of the attractive properties of the butterfly factorization is that with enough stages it can produce a dense transform even though every stage is sparse. Concretely, if $d$ is a power of two and we choose the number of stages
\[
m=\log_2\!\left(\frac{2d}{b}\right),
\]
then BOFT$(m,b)$ implements the (block) butterfly matrix $\bm{B}^{b/2}(d)$ as the overall $\bm{R}$ and the product becomes dense: every input coordinate can influence every output coordinate through paths that traverse the successive sparse components. In the scalar-block limit ($b=2$ in the paper's notation) this reduces to the classical radix-2 butterfly with $m=\log_2 d$ stages, the familiar decomposition that underlies the FFT. In short, increasing $m$ to about $\log_2(d)$ stages achieves dense mixing while still keeping the parameterization built from sparse, structured factors.

# Parameter count formula and scaling: 0.5 × (b − 1) × d × m
A compact expression for the number of trainable scalar parameters in BOFT$(m,b)$ (the effective degrees of freedom that are being learned) is
\[
\text{parameters}(m,b) \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\,(b-1)\,d\,m.
\]
This formula can be understood by counting per-component blocks and the degrees of freedom per block. Each butterfly component contains $d/b$ independent block positions; treating each block as a $b\times b$ orthogonal block, that block has $b(b-1)/2$ degrees of freedom (the dimension of the orthogonal group $O(b)$). Multiplying $d/b$ blocks per component by $b(b-1)/2$ parameters per block gives $d(b-1)/2$ parameters per component, and with $m$ components we obtain the stated total.

Two useful sanity checks of this formula are worth noting. First, if $m=1$ and $b=d$ then
\[
\text{parameters}(1,d)=\tfrac{1}{2}(d-1)d,
\]
which equals the degrees of freedom of a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix (the usual OFT full-matrix case). Second, taking the butterfly extreme $b=2$ with $m=\log_2 d$, the parameter count scales like $O(d\log d)$ (indeed $\frac{1}{2}(2-1)d\log_2 d = \tfrac{1}{2}d\log_2 d$), realizing the paper's main economy: dense orthogonal behavior with near-linear parameter cost multiplied by $\log d$.

Practically, this formula makes clear how to trade parameters: increasing $b$ raises per-stage capacity, while increasing $m$ increases how many scales of mixing are available. BOFT often obtains better parameter-efficiency (expressivity per parameter) than a pure block-diagonal OFT with the same $b$ because the deeper sparse composition can approximate more of the orthogonal group than a single block-diagonal matrix.

# Identity initialization for zero-update start
A desirable property of finetuning is to start from the pretrained model exactly so that, before any training, the finetuned model and the pretrained model coincide. BOFT achieves this by initializing every orthogonal block in every butterfly component to the identity matrix. Under the Cayley parameterization this corresponds simply to initializing each block's skew-symmetric parameter $\bm{Q}$ to the zero matrix (since $(\bm{I}+\bm{0})(\bm{I}-\bm{0})^{-1}=\bm{I}$). With this identity initialization we have $\bm{R}(m,b)=\bm{I}$ at the start of training and the first updates move away from identity in a principled, orthogonality-preserving manner.

# Multiplicative Dropout: blockwise identity replacement for regularization
Because BOFT updates weights multiplicatively ($\bm{W} \leftarrow \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$) rather than additively, conventional element-wise dropout applied to the update does not translate directly. To regularize BOFT in a manner analogous to dropout on additive updates, the paper proposes multiplicative Dropout: during training, randomly select a fraction $p_1$ of the butterfly components and, within the remaining components, randomly select a fraction $p_2$ of the block positions and temporarily replace those selected components / blocks by identity matrices for that forward/backward pass. In effect, multiplicative Dropout stochastically freezes a subset of the multiplicative factors to identity, which reduces overfitting by preventing co-adaptation of too many components at once while preserving the multiplicative orthogonal structure. Because the identity is an orthogonal element, this replacement respects the orthogonality constraint and is straightforward to implement when the components are stored block-wise.

Concluding remark (summary)
BOFT parameterizes a dense $d\times d$ orthogonal transform as a product of $m$ sparse, structured, block-wise-orthogonal butterfly components. By choosing $(m,b)$ appropriately one obtains a flexible continuum from simple block-diagonal OFT up to dense, highly expressive orthogonal transforms, while the parameter count scales as $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$. Identity initialization and blockwise Cayley reparameterization enforce orthogonality throughout training and enable a zero-update start; multiplicative Dropout provides a simple block-level regularizer tailored to the multiplicative nature of the parameterization. Together these design choices realize BOFT's goal: orthogonal finetuning with far fewer trainable parameters than an unconstrained full orthogonal matrix, but with more global mixing than a single block-diagonal OFT.
