# Orthogonal Finetuning: A Gentle Introduction to Keeping Generative Models on Track

Large text-to-image diffusion models—Stable Diffusion, Imagen, GLIDE and friends—are remarkably good at turning words into images. But when you want to adapt such a model for a specific downstream purpose (generate a particular person from a handful of photos, or make the model obey an extra control signal like edges or segmentation), ordinary finetuning can be fragile: too much change and the model loses its pretrained "visual common sense"; too little change and it won't learn the new behavior.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) offers a simple, principled middle path: instead of rewriting weight values, finetune by rotating neuron directions. The rotation is implemented as a small, layer-shared orthogonal transform applied to the pretrained weights. That single change preserves the relationships among neurons that encode semantics, while still letting the model adapt to new tasks. The method is fast, stable, parameter-efficient, and—crucially—does not add runtime cost at inference.

Below I walk through the core ideas, the intuition, how it’s implemented, and what it means in practice.

## The adaptation problems we care about

Two common text-to-image finetuning tasks motivate OFT:

- Subject-driven generation: give the model a few images of a subject (a person, an object) and teach it to generate that subject in new scenes and poses (DreamBooth style).
- Controllable generation: teach the model to respect additional control signals such as Canny edges, segmentation maps, or facial landmarks (ControlNet / T2I-Adapter style).

Both tasks require changing the model just enough to get the new behavior, while preserving its pretrained generative ability (photorealism, compositionality, diversity).

## The core idea: rotate, don’t rewrite

Neural weights can be thought of as vectors (neurons) in high-dimensional space. OFT changes a layer by multiplying the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$:

$ \bm{z} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}, \quad \text{with } \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}. $

Because orthogonal matrices preserve angles and lengths, applying the same orthogonal transform to all neurons in a layer preserves all pairwise angles (and thus certain geometric relationships) among neurons. Those pairwise relationships are closely tied to the model’s semantic knowledge; keeping them intact helps preserve the pretrained model’s generalization and "visual common sense."

In short: instead of adding an unconstrained delta to weights, OFT learns layer-wise rotations that nudge neuron directions in a controlled, structure-preserving way.

## Why angles (directions) matter

Two intuitions from the paper explain why rotating directions is a reasonable way to finetune:

1. Angular features carry semantics. Prior work and a small experiment in the paper show that the angular part of neuron activations often encodes semantic structure far better than magnitudes. An autoencoder toy shows that keeping angular relationships can preserve image content even when magnitudes are scrambled.

2. The 2D Fourier analogy. In images, the phase (angular) spectrum preserves recognizable structure much more than the magnitude spectrum. By analogy, changing neuron directions (phase) is a natural way to alter semantics without destroying the model’s learned structure.

Together these points motivate finetuning methods that adjust directions over changing absolute magnitudes arbitrarily.

## A structural objective: hyperspherical energy

To formalize what "preserve structure" means, OFT uses a measure called hyperspherical energy (HE). For a weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, let $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ be the normalized neuron directions. The hyperspherical energy is

$ \text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}. $

HE captures the global pairwise angular structure (roughly, how uniformly or tightly neurons are arranged on the unit hypersphere). OFT enforces that the finetuned layer keep the same HE as the pretrained layer, which is achieved by adopting an orthogonal transform: if $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0$ with orthogonal $\bm{R}$, then all pairwise angles (and thus HE) are preserved exactly.

Formally, OFT is motivated by minimizing $\|\text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\|$, and a feasible zero is obtained by orthogonal $\bm{R}$.

## Practical implementation: making rotations cheap and stable

Learning a full orthogonal $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ directly is expensive, so the paper uses a few engineering choices:

- Cayley parameterization. Represent orthogonal $\bm{R}$ as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric ($\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$). This enforces orthogonality in a differentiable way.
- Block-diagonal structure. $\bm{R}$ can be set as block-diagonal $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$, which dramatically reduces the number of parameters and computation while keeping orthogonality within each block. Blocks can be shared too.
- Identity initialization. Initialize $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ so the finetuning starts exactly from the pretrained model.
- No inference overhead. At inference time you can fold $\bm{R}$ into the pretrained weights, producing $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ and running the model at the same speed as before.

These choices strike a balance: enough flexibility to learn new behavior, but enough structure to avoid destroying the pretrained model.

## A stability knob: Constrained OFT (COFT)

If you want even safer finetuning, COFT adds an explicit constraint that the orthogonal matrix stays near identity:

$ \|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \le \epsilon. $

Using the Cayley form and the Neumann approximation $\bm{R}\approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$, this can be enforced by constraining $\bm{Q}$ (e.g., via projected gradient). COFT makes the deviation from the pretrained model explicit and tunable, giving an easy way to control the flexibility-vs-stability trade-off.

## A small extension: re-scaled OFT

OFT preserves angles but not per-neuron magnitudes. The paper also considers a re-scaled variant that learns a diagonal scaling $\bm{D}=\text{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ together with $\bm{R}$:

$ \bm{z} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}. $

Scaling does not change hyperspherical energy (because HE uses normalized vectors), but it provides a small extra degree of freedom when needed.

## How OFT compares to other finetuning approaches

- Standard finetuning: update all weights with a tiny learning rate. This can be effective but is sensitive, slow, and can easily overfit or collapse.
- LoRA (low-rank additive updates): add a low-rank matrix $\Delta\bm{W}$ to $\bm{W}^0$ so $\bm{W}=\bm{W}^0+\Delta\bm{W}$. This constrains updates additively with a low-rank assumption.
- Adapter methods (ControlNet, T2I-Adapter): add extra modules or branches that bring controllability but increase inference complexity.

OFT is multiplicative: $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, which can be rewritten as $\bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$—so the effective update is still structured but constrained by orthogonality. Compared with LoRA, OFT tends to:

- Preserve pretrained semantic structure better (because it preserves pairwise angles).
- Converge faster and more stably in the authors’ experiments.
- Require similar or fewer trainable parameters (block-diagonal $\bm{R}$ gives good efficiency).
- Add no extra inference-time modules.

## Empirical highlights (taken from the paper)

The authors finetuned Stable Diffusion v1.5 across two settings: subject-driven generation (DreamBooth-style) and controllable generation (Canny edges, segmentation, landmarks on COCO / ADE20K / CelebA-HQ). Key empirical takeaways reported in the paper:

- OFT and COFT produce better final generation quality and preserve subject identity better than DreamBooth and LoRA in the subject-driven setting.
- OFT / COFT converge faster and more stably than LoRA, ControlNet, and T2I-Adapter in controllable generation tasks. The paper reports an example where LoRA reaches OFT’s performance at epoch 20, while OFT reaches it by epoch 8.
- OFT is more sample efficient: the authors note it can converge well with only 5% of ADE20K in a control task.
- The paper also introduces a control-consistency metric (estimate the control signal from the generated image and compare it to the input control) to quantify controllability; OFT performs strongly on this metric.

These results suggest that rotating neurons is an effective and efficient way to adapt large pretrained diffusion models for structured downstream control.

## What this means for creators and developers

- Faster personalization: OFT can teach a model a new subject or control behavior with fewer training steps and fewer images than many baselines.
- More stable finetuning: preserving neuron geometry makes training less sensitive to hyperparameters like number of iterations and learning rate; COFT gives an explicit safety knob ($\epsilon$).
- No runtime penalty: the learned orthogonal transform can be folded into the model weights so inference cost is unchanged.
- Easy adoption: OFT is model-agnostic in principle—it applies to fully connected, attention, and convolutional layers (the block-diagonal view maps naturally to channel-wise transforms).
- Trade-offs: use smaller blocks or smaller $\epsilon$ for safety and parameter efficiency; enlarge them when you need more flexibility.

## Limitations and open questions

The method is promising but not without issues the paper calls out:

- Cayley parameterization requires a matrix inverse and generates orthogonals with determinant $+1$, which slightly restricts the parameterization and has scalability costs. The block-diagonal trick helps but is a heuristic.
- Block-diagonal structure introduces bias: fewer parameters but less flexibility. Finding better parameter-efficient orthogonal parameterizations is open.
- Compositionality of multiple orthogonal edits (multiplying orthogonal matrices from different finetuning steps) is an intriguing direction: will a single product of orthogonals preserve knowledge from many tasks?
- How to further reduce the computational overhead of enforcing orthogonality in very large layers is an implementation challenge.

## Final thoughts

Orthogonal Finetuning reframes finetuning as a controlled rotation of neuron directions rather than an unconstrained rewrite of weights. That small conceptual shift—enforce geometry instead of suppressing parameter drift—turns out to be powerful: it preserves the pretrained model’s semantic structure (the model’s “visual common sense”) while giving enough flexibility to learn subject-specific or control-guided behavior. For practitioners who want robust, fast, and parameter-efficient finetuning of text-to-image diffusion models, OFT (and its constrained sibling COFT) are practical ideas worth trying.

If you want to experiment: start from attention or MLP layers where LoRA is usually applied, initialize $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, use block-diagonal Cayley parameterization, and try COFT with a modest $\epsilon$ as a safety-first approach. The paper’s experiments used Stable Diffusion v1.5 and demonstrate strong, stable gains across both personalization and control tasks.



# Why Angles Matter: Hyperspherical Energy and the Secret Life of Neurons

TL;DR — When you finetune a big text-to-image model, preserving the directions of neurons (their angles) can be more important than preserving raw weight values. Hyperspherical energy is a compact way to measure those pairwise angles. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) preserves that energy by rotating the neuron basis with layer-shared orthogonal matrices, which the authors show leads to more stable, faster, and higher-quality finetuning for subject-driven and controllable image generation.

## The problem in one sentence

Large pretrained diffusion models are powerful, but naive finetuning can destroy the pretrained model’s general semantic ability. Existing approaches try to stay “close” to the original weights in Euclidean terms, but Euclidean distance does not fully capture the geometric structure that encodes learned semantics. The paper argues that the angular relationships among neurons — their arrangement on a unit hypersphere — are a better object to preserve.

## What is hyperspherical energy (and why should you care)?

Think of each neuron (for a single layer) as a vector in high-dimensional space. Normalize those vectors so they lie on the unit sphere. Hyperspherical energy (HE) is a simple scalar that summarizes how those normalized vectors are arranged with respect to each other. One convenient definition used in the paper is

\[ \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}, \qquad
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i := \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|}, \]

so HE sums inverse pairwise distances between normalized neurons. Equivalent statements could be made in terms of pairwise cosines or angles. Low-level intuition: HE captures the pairwise angular relationships — whether neurons are clustered, evenly spread, or otherwise organized on the hypersphere.

Why does that matter? Prior empirical work and the experiments in this paper show that angular/ directional information encodes semantic structure better than raw magnitudes. Preserving these pairwise angles during finetuning tends to preserve the pretrained model’s semantic generation capabilities.

## Phase vs magnitude: a useful analogy

A helpful analogy is the Fourier decomposition of an image into phase and magnitude. If you keep the phase spectrum and replace the magnitude with random values, you often retain the image’s recognizable structure; the phase carries most of the semantic layout. The paper makes the same point for neurons: a toy autoencoder experiment demonstrates that reconstructing using only angular information (direction of neuron vectors) retains much more semantic information than using only magnitudes. In short: neuron directions behave like "phase" — they carry the semantic signal.

## Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): rotate, don't scramble

If angles matter, how do we finetune without damaging them?

The key observation is that applying the same orthogonal transformation (rotation/reflection) to every neuron in a layer preserves all pairwise angles. Orthogonal Finetuning enforces exactly that: for a pretrained layer with weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^0\), OFT learns a layer-shared orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) and uses

\[ \mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}, \qquad \text{subject to } \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}. \]

Because orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\) preserves distances and inner products, HE is provably unchanged by this transform. In other words, OFT explicitly enforces the finetuned model to have the same hyperspherical energy as the pretrained model, achieving the principle

\[ \min_{\mathbf{W}} \| \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) - \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0) \|, \]

and doing so constructively by restricting \(\mathbf{W}\) to \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\) for orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\).

A few implementation notes from the paper:

- OFT initializes \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}\) so training starts exactly at the pretrained weights.
- To parameterize \(\mathbf{R}\) efficiently and maintain orthogonality, the paper uses the Cayley transform: \(\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) with \(\mathbf{Q}\) skew-symmetric (\(\mathbf{Q}^\top = -\mathbf{Q}\)).
- For large layers, \(\mathbf{R}\) is made block-diagonal with \(r\) blocks to reduce parameter count and regularize the transformation. Blocks can optionally be shared across channels.

Because OFT multiplies the pretrained weight matrix instead of adding arbitrary updates, it preserves the pairwise angular structure while still allowing the network to adapt.

## How OFT compares to other finetuning tricks

- Standard finetuning: updates weights directly; keeping learning rates tiny implicitly tries to stay close in Euclidean space, but this does not guarantee angle preservation and can easily break semantic generalization.
- LoRA: adds a low-rank additive update to weights. This helps limit how much the model can drift, but it does not enforce angle preservation.
- OFT: multiplicative, orthogonality-constrained update that provably preserves hyperspherical energy. Algebraically, OFT is equivalent to

  \[ \mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0 = \mathbf{W}^0 + (\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I})\mathbf{W}^0, \]

  so it can be viewed as a structured additive update where \(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\) is restricted by orthogonality and block structure.

A concrete illustration from the paper: for a \(128\times 128\) weight matrix with low-rank LoRA parameter \(r'=8\), LoRA has about 2,048 trainable parameters, while OFT with block parameter \(r=8\) has about 960 parameters in that example.

## Constrained OFT (COFT): keep angles and stay close

OFT preserves angles but can still rotate the basis substantially. To get the best of both worlds, the paper proposes Constrained OFT (COFT), which enforces both orthogonality and a small deviation from identity:

\[ \|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon. \]

In practice, because \(\mathbf{R}\) is Cayley-parameterized via \(\mathbf{Q}\), the authors use a Neumann-series approximation \(\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2)\) and directly constrain \(\mathbf{Q}\) (projected gradient descent) to keep the update small. COFT improves stability and makes finetuning hyperparameters like learning rate and iteration count less sensitive.

The paper also explores a re-scaled OFT variant that learns a positive diagonal scaling matrix \(\mathbf{D}\) (forward pass \((\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\mathbf{D})^\top \mathbf{x}\)). Scaling does not change HE because HE is computed on normalized vectors, but it adds flexibility by letting magnitudes vary if needed.

## What this buys you in practice

The paper evaluates OFT and COFT on two finetuning scenarios that require preserving pretrained semantics:

- Subject-driven generation (DreamBooth-style): given a few images of a subject, generate the subject in new contexts.
- Controllable generation (ControlNet / T2I-Adapter-style): given an additional control input (edges, segmentation, landmarks), generate images that follow that control.

Empirical findings reported in the paper include:

- OFT and COFT converge faster and more stably than baselines such as DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet, and T2I-Adapter. For example, in a landmark-to-face task the paper notes that LoRA needed 20 epochs to reach the performance that OFT achieved by epoch 8.
- OFT/COFT preserve subject identity better and produce higher-quality images under text constraints. Quantitatively, the paper reports improvements on subject-fidelity metrics (DINO and CLIP image similarity) compared to DreamBooth and LoRA, while maintaining comparable prompt fidelity (CLIP-T) and diversity (LPIPS).
- For controllable generation, the authors introduce a "control consistency" metric (re-extract the control signal from the generated image and compare to the original) and find OFT/COFT yield stronger and more accurate control.
- OFT introduces no inference overhead: the learned \(\mathbf{R}\) can be multiplied into \(\mathbf{W}^0\) ahead of time so runtime is unchanged.

Put simply: preserving angles stabilizes finetuning and retains the pretrained model’s generalization while still allowing meaningful task-specific adaptation.

## Practical takeaways

- If the goal is to adapt a pretrained generative model while keeping its general semantic ability, consider preserving angles rather than just minimizing Euclidean weight shifts.
- OFT is a principled way to do that: learn layer-shared orthogonal transforms, initialize them at identity, and optionally constrain their deviation (COFT) for extra stability.
- Use block-diagonal orthogonal matrices for parameter efficiency; for Transformers apply OFT to the same attention weights where LoRA is usually applied.
- If you need a little more flexibility, add a diagonal scaling matrix (re-scaled OFT) — it doesn’t affect hyperspherical energy but lets magnitudes adapt.

## Limitations and open problems

The paper also lists open issues to keep in mind:

- Cayley parameterization requires a matrix inverse; scalability and efficiency of that step are practical concerns for very large layers.
- OFT’s parameter efficiency depends on block-diagonal structure, which biases the learned transformations; finding less biased compression strategies is an open problem.
- Composability: multiple OFT finetunes produce orthogonal matrices that can be multiplied together, but whether a product of orthogonal matrices preserves all downstream tasks’ knowledge remains to be studied.

## Final thought

Neural networks hide a lot of their learned knowledge in geometry. This paper shows that preserving the geometry — the angles between neurons — is a simple, principled, and effective way to finetune large diffusion models for controlled image generation. If you think of weight vectors as directions on a sphere, finetuning becomes a problem of rotating that sphere carefully, not reshaping it randomly. Orthogonal Finetuning gives you a way to rotate with guarantees, and that turns out to matter for image quality, stability, and data efficiency.



# COFT: Putting Guardrails on Finetuning for Stable, Predictable Results

## TL;DR
Finetuning large text-to-image diffusion models is powerful but fragile: small changes can easily destroy pretrained generative ability. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) puts a simple, principled guardrail around finetuning: learn a layer-shared orthogonal transform but require it to stay within a small “radius” of the identity. This preserves the pairwise neuron geometry that encodes semantic ability, stabilizes training, makes hyperparameters (learning rate, number of iterations) easier to set, and still gives the model enough flexibility to adapt to tasks like subject-driven generation and controllable generation — all without adding inference cost.

## The problem in one paragraph
When you adapt a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model to a downstream task (e.g., inject a new subject from a few images or add an edge/segmentation control), you want two competing things: (1) enough flexibility to learn the new control, and (2) enough fidelity to preserve the pretrained model’s semantic generation ability. Standard finetuning or small additive adapters (e.g., LoRA) give some regularization, but none of them guarantee that the internal geometric relationships among neurons — which actually encode semantic knowledge — are preserved. That’s where orthogonal finetuning and its constrained variant, COFT, come in.

## Quick recap: why orthogonality and hyperspherical energy matter
A key empirical observation is that the directions (angles) of neuron weight vectors encode semantic information better than their magnitudes. The hyperspherical energy of a layer captures the pairwise geometry of normalized neurons on the unit hypersphere. For a weight matrix $W=\{w_1,\dots,w_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ with normalized neurons $\hat w_i = w_i/\|w_i\|$, the hyperspherical energy is
\[
\text{HE}(W) := \sum_{i\ne j} \|\hat w_i - \hat w_j\|^{-1}.
\]
Orthogonal transforms preserve angles, so if you replace $W^0$ (pretrained weights) by $R W^0$ with $R$ orthogonal, you keep the exact same hyperspherical energy:
\[
\text{HE}(R W^0) = \text{HE}(W^0).
\]
Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) leverages this: instead of directly changing weights additively or elementwise, OFT learns a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $R$ and uses
\[
z = (R W^0)^\top x,
\]
so the pairwise neuron geometry is preserved during adaptation.

## What COFT adds: a radius constraint as a guardrail
OFT preserves angles but can still produce large orthogonal rotations that shift the model far from the pretrained parameters. COFT adds an explicit constraint that the learned orthogonal transform stay close to identity:
\[
z = (R W^0)^\top x,\qquad R^\top R = I,\qquad \|R - I\| \le \epsilon.
\]
Intuition:
- The orthogonality keeps the pairwise neuron angles (and thus semantic structure) intact.
- The radius constraint $\|R - I\| \le \epsilon$ limits how far those rotations can move you from the pretrained coordinate frame, preventing destructive changes and avoiding collapse.
- The hyperparameter $\epsilon$ directly trades flexibility vs. conservatism: small $\epsilon$ ≈ pretrained behavior; large $\epsilon$ ≈ OFT.

The paper shows that this explicit radius constraint stabilizes finetuning and reduces sensitivity to learning rate and number of training iterations. With a suitable $\epsilon$, you can largely “set and forget” these other hyperparameters.

## How COFT is implemented (practical details)
Two practical issues need addressing: how to parametrize orthogonal $R$ efficiently, and how to enforce the radius constraint.

- Cayley parameterization. The paper uses the Cayley transform to parametrize orthogonal $R$:
  \[
  R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1},
  \]
  where $Q$ is skew-symmetric ($Q = -Q^\top$). Cayley produces orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$ (the special orthogonal group). Initializing $Q=0$ yields $R=I$, so COFT starts exactly at the pretrained model.

- Enforcing the radius constraint. The Cayley form is nonlinear, but by using a Neumann-series approximation one gets
  \[
  R \approx I + 2Q + \mathcal{O}(Q^2),
  \]
  so the constraint $\|R - I\| \le \epsilon$ can be approximately enforced as a bound on $\|Q\|$. That makes enforcement straightforward via projected gradient steps on $Q$ (the paper enforces a small $\|Q\| \le \epsilon'$ during training). Because $Q$ is initialized to zero, COFT naturally starts conservatively and can expand within the allowed radius.

- Parameter-efficiency via block-diagonal $R$. A full $d\times d$ orthogonal $R$ can be expensive. COFT uses block-diagonal $R=\operatorname{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r)$ to reduce the number of trainable parameters and the cost of the matrix inverse in Cayley. Blocks can also be shared across channels for further savings. This block structure preserves orthogonality within each block and thus still maintains hyperspherical energy invariance for neurons partitioned by block.

- No inference overhead. Once training finishes, you can multiply $R$ into $W^0$ to obtain $W = R W^0$ and discard the separate parametrization; inference uses the same compute as the original model.

## Why COFT improves stability and predictability
COFT is designed around two geometric facts:

1. Semantic information is encoded in neuron directions (angles), so preserving pairwise angles keeps the model’s semantic generation ability intact.
2. Small, structured rotations (orthogonal transforms near identity) give meaningful, controlled ways to adjust neuron directions without "breaking" the pretrained geometry.

Putting these together, COFT provides an explicit guardrail: you adapt directions where it matters, but only within a bounded neighborhood of the pretrained coordinate system. That reduces the chances of sudden model collapse or catastrophic overwrite of pretrained semantics, and it smooths training dynamics so convergence is faster and more predictable. Empirically (per the paper), COFT yielded more stable convergence than vanilla finetuning and adapter-based methods, and it was less sensitive to the number of finetuning iterations.

## Empirical signals (summary from the paper)
- Subject-driven generation: COFT and OFT showed much more stable convergence than DreamBooth and LoRA. They preserved subject identity and achieved better text fidelity while avoiding collapse even at larger iteration counts.
- Controllable generation: COFT converged faster than ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA on several control tasks (Canny edges → image, segmentation → image, landmark → face). COFT also scored better on the paper's “control consistency” metrics (e.g., IoU/F1 for C2I, mean IoU and accuracy for S2I, mean $\ell_2$ landmark error for L2F).
- Sample efficiency: COFT required fewer training images / fewer epochs to reach good control.
- Parameter efficiency: with block-diagonal parametrization COFT uses a modest number of extra parameters and compares favorably to LoRA in practical parameter budgets. (The paper gives concrete parameter-count comparisons for example layer sizes.)

All of these outcomes are a direct consequence of the orthogonality + radius constraints: enough directed flexibility to learn control, but constrained enough to keep pretrained semantics.

## Practical guidance (from the paper)
- Initialize $Q=0$ so $R=I$ and training starts from the pretrained model.
- Choose block size $r$ to trade off parameter cost and expressivity; block sharing reduces parameters further.
- Tune $\epsilon$ (or its proxy $\epsilon'$ on $Q$) to set the level of conservatism: if you see little adaptation, relax $\epsilon$; if you see instability, tighten it. The paper reports COFT is overall less sensitive to learning rate and iteration count once $\epsilon$ is set.
- Apply COFT to the same layers you would target with LoRA (e.g., attention weights) for fair comparison and comparable parameter budgets.
- No inference-time changes are required beyond multiplying $R$ into $W^0$ once training is done.

## Limitations and open problems
The paper notes several practical limitations and interesting directions:
- The Cayley transform requires a matrix inverse; block-diagonal structure alleviates but does not eliminate this cost. More scalable differentiable orthogonal parameterizations would help.
- Block-diagonal $R$ is a bias: it reduces parameters but also limits expressivity. Finding better parameter-efficient orthogonal parameterizations is open.
- Orthogonal matrices compose: if you finetune multiple tasks you can multiply their $R$’s and still get an orthogonal matrix. Whether this composition preserves performance on all tasks (and how to manage interference) is an interesting open question.

## Bottom line
COFT is a simple, geometrically principled way to put guardrails on finetuning: keep neuron angles (hyperspherical energy) intact while forcing rotations to stay in a small radius around identity. That combination stabilizes finetuning, reduces hyperparameter fiddling, accelerates convergence, and preserves pretrained semantic abilities — all with little extra parameter or inference cost. If you're finetuning large text-to-image diffusion models for personalized subjects or additional control signals, COFT is an appealing, easy-to-reason-about option to try.



# Faster, Cheaper, and No Slower at Inference: The Efficiency Story of OFT

## TL;DR
Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) adapts large text-to-image diffusion models by learning small, layer-shared orthogonal transforms $R$ that rotate pretrained neuron weight matrices $W^0$ to new task weights $W = R W^0$. That simple idea buys three practical efficiency wins: faster and more stable training, far fewer trainable parameters via block‑diagonal $R$, and zero extra inference cost (you can fold $R$ into $W^0$ after finetuning). This post explains why these properties hold, how OFT is parameterized in practice (Cayley transform + block diagonalization), and what tradeoffs to watch for.

## The intuitive efficiency picture
Two empirical facts drive OFT’s efficiency:

1. Semantic information in neural layers is largely encoded in neuron directions (angles), not magnitudes. Rotating neuron directions is therefore an effective, low‑dimensional way to change model behavior without destroying pretrained semantic structure.

2. Orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise angles between neurons (and thus the layer’s hyperspherical energy), so they let you edit directionality while keeping the network’s internal relational structure intact. That makes optimization smoother and preserves the pretrained model’s generative ability.

Practically, that means OFT focuses the optimizer on a smaller, more meaningful subspace of weight updates (rotations), rather than unconstrained additive updates. The result: faster convergence, more stable finetuning, and fewer parameters to train.

## How OFT stays cheap: block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices
A full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix has $d(d-1)/2$ degrees of freedom, which can be expensive when $d$ is large. OFT reduces this cost by using a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix with $r$ blocks. If $\bm{R}=\mathrm{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r)$ and each block has size $d/r$, then the number of trainable parameters becomes proportional to
\[
\frac{d(d/r - 1)}{2},
\]
i.e. roughly $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. Two sensible knobs let you control parameter cost vs. flexibility:

- Increase $r$ (more, smaller blocks) → fewer parameters, more regularization.
- Share blocks across channels (set $R_i = R_j$) → further cut parameters (down toward $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ in the extreme).

A concrete numeric illustration from the paper: for a $128\times128$ weight matrix and block count $r=8$, an OFT block parametrization required about 960 trainable parameters; LoRA with rank $r'=8$ used 2,048 trainable parameters. So OFT can be noticeably smaller while remaining expressive.

A useful comparison to LoRA: LoRA models the finetuning update additively with a low‑rank matrix and has $r'(d+n)$ trainable parameters for a $d\times n$ layer. If you choose $(r, r')$ that scale with $d$ (e.g. $r=r'=\alpha d$), LoRA’s parameter count becomes $\mathcal{O}(d^2 + d n)$ while OFT’s block diagonalization can be driven toward $\mathcal{O}(d)$ by taking $r$ proportional to $d$. In short: different design choices give very different scaling behaviors — OFT’s block structure is an effective lever for parameter efficiency.

## Cayley transform: orthogonality without hard constraints
To keep $R$ orthogonal during training, OFT uses the Cayley parameterization:
\[
R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1},
\]
where $Q$ is skew‑symmetric ($Q^\top = -Q$). This parameterization guarantees orthogonality (and produces special orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$). Two practical consequences:

- Initialization is trivial: set $Q=0$ so $R=I$ and training starts exactly from the pretrained weights.
- Orthogonality is maintained by construction, avoiding repeated projections or constrained solvers.

COFT (Constrained OFT), a stability variant, also constrains how far $R$ may deviate from identity: $\|R-I\|\le \epsilon$. Using the Neumann series you can approximate $R \approx I + 2Q + \mathcal{O}(Q^2)$, so the $R$‑deviation constraint is approximated by a $\|Q\|$ bound and enforced via projected gradient steps on $Q$. This keeps finetuning both orthogonal and tightly anchored to the original model for extra stability.

Caveat: Cayley needs a matrix inverse $(I-Q)^{-1}$. That inverse is the primary scalability bottleneck, which is why block‑diagonalization is important in practice (you invert many small blocks instead of one huge matrix). The paper flags efficient differentiable inversion as an open implementation problem.

## Zero inference overhead
This is the cleanest operational win: during inference you do not need to keep any extra adapter, extra control network, or extra forward steps. The learned orthogonal transform can be folded into the pretrained weights:
\[
W = R W^0.
\]
Once you compute $W$ (a one‑time post‑training matrix multiply), the model runs exactly as the original pretrained model with no added layers or runtime cost. That contrasts with methods like ControlNet or adapters that add extra modules at test time and raise inference latency and memory.

If you use the re‑scaled OFT variant ($W = R W^0 D$ with diagonal $D$), you can similarly fold both $R$ and $D$ into $W$ before deployment, so inference still stays unchanged.

## Why optimization is smoother and faster
OFT’s optimization has a few properties that speed up convergence and stabilize training:

- It optimizes on a smooth manifold (orthogonal group) and keeps the pretrained neuron geometry intact (pairwise angles), avoiding destructive updates that break semantics.
- Because it targets neuron directions, it focuses updates on the most semantically effective degrees of freedom.
- Identity initialization makes the early training dynamics conservative and predictable — you start at the pretrained model and gradually rotate only as much as needed.

Empirically, the paper reports consistent speedups and stability across tasks: for the landmark‑to‑face controlled generation task, LoRA needed 20 epochs to reach the performance level that OFT already had at the 8th epoch. OFT also converged well with a small fraction of data (the paper notes good convergence with only 5% of ADE20K in one control task). In subject‑driven generation experiments, OFT/COFT achieved good control in a few hundred iterations and remained stable where other methods sometimes collapsed.

## Practical tips and tradeoffs
- Choose block size $r$ to trade parameter budget vs flexibility. More blocks → fewer params but stricter regularization. Shared blocks are an easy extra savings lever.
- If you need maximum stability, use COFT and pick $\epsilon$ small enough to keep $R$ close to identity; training becomes less sensitive to learning rate and iteration count.
- Initialize $Q=0$ so $R=I$ and finetuning starts from the pretrained model exactly.
- Apply OFT to the same layers where other adapters (like LoRA) are typically used (e.g. attention weights) for fair comparisons and to get the largest ROI for parameter savings.
- Watch runtime during training: block diagonalization reduces cost of the Cayley inverse substantially; fully dense Cayley on huge $d$ is the scalability hotspot.

## Limitations and open problems
- Cayley parameterization requires matrix inversion. Block diagonalization mitigates but does not eliminate the issue; faster differentiable inversion methods would help scale OFT further.
- Block structures introduce inductive biases: extreme blockization reduces flexibility and may limit expressivity in some layers.
- The paper highlights intriguing compositionality questions: orthogonal transforms for different finetuning tasks multiply to another orthogonal matrix, so in principle you could compose task updates — whether that preserves multiple tasks’ knowledge is an open research direction.

## Takeaway
OFT’s core idea — learn small orthogonal rotations of pretrained weights — delivers three aligned efficiency wins: fast/convergent training, low parameter counts through block‑diagonalization, and no added inference cost because the learned transform can be folded into the weights. The combination of principled geometry (angle preservation), a practical orthogonal parameterization (Cayley + block blocks), and identity initialization delivers a robust, deployable finetuning recipe for text‑to‑image diffusion models.



# OFT vs. LoRA vs. ControlNet: When to Use What for Text‑to‑Image Control

Text-to-image diffusion models (Stable Diffusion, Imagen, DALL·E 2, etc.) are great at global text guidance, but real-world use often needs stronger, more reliable control: personalizing a subject from a handful of photos, following an edge/segmentation map, or enforcing a pose/landmark layout. Three practical finetuning strategies are commonly discussed today:

- LoRA – low-rank additive updates to weights;
- ControlNet / T2I‑Adapter – separate adapter modules trained to inject control signals;
- OFT (Orthogonal Finetuning) – a multiplicative, orthogonal transform applied to pretrained neurons (this is the method introduced and studied in the paper summarized here).

Below I summarize how these approaches differ, why OFT works for diffusion finetuning, and practical advice on which method to pick for subject personalization, edge/segmentation control and pose tasks.

## TL;DR
- If you need stable, sample‑efficient finetuning that preserves the pretrained model’s semantic behavior and adds no inference overhead, OFT (or its constrained variant COFT) is often the best choice.
- If you need modular adapters you can enable/disable at inference or you prefer not to modify base weights, ControlNet / T2I‑Adapter are natural — but expect more parameters, slower/stagey convergence and extra inference cost.
- If you want a simple, widely supported, parameter‑efficient transformer trick, LoRA is a sensible baseline, but in the diffusion finetuning experiments in the paper it was less stable and less effective at preserving subject identity and control fidelity.

All observations below come from the comparisons performed in the paper on Stable Diffusion v1.5 across subject‑driven (DreamBooth style) and controllable generation (Canny/Segmentation/Landmark) tasks.

## High‑level differences (what they do, mathematically)
- LoRA assumes the weight update during finetuning is low‑rank, i.e. it parameterizes $M - M^0$ with small matrices so the update is additive and parameter‑efficient. This limits the update’s degrees of freedom but does not enforce any explicit geometric constraint on neuron directions.

- ControlNet / T2I‑Adapter add small neural network modules or conditioning adapters that process the control signal and influence the diffusion model. They are explicit conditioning architectures: extra parameters and extra computation at test time.

- OFT applies a layer‑shared orthogonal transformation to the pretrained neurons. For a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ (columns are neurons) OFT parameterizes the finetuned weights as
  $$
  \bm{W} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0,
  $$
  with $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$. The forward pass becomes $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. Because orthogonal transforms preserve angles between normalized neurons, OFT provably preserves a structural measure called hyperspherical energy.

## Why OFT — intuition and the core invariant
The paper’s key hypothesis is that neuron directions (angles) encode most of the semantic structure a pretrained generator learned. Two motivating facts used in the paper:

1. In a toy autoencoder experiment the angular information of convolutional neurons preserved image semantics much better than magnitudes — suggesting directions matter more for semantic content.

2. Hyperspherical energy (HE) captures the pairwise relational structure among normalized neurons:
   $$
   \mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \big\| \hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j \big\|^{-1},\qquad \hat{\bm{w}}_i=\frac{\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{w}_i\|}.
   $$
   Any orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$ preserves all pairwise angles among normalized neurons, so $\mathrm{HE}(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0) = \mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$.

Thus OFT seeks to adjust neuron directions (what semantically changes images) while avoiding destructive changes to the pretrained directional geometry. COFT (Constrained OFT) adds an explicit small‑deviation constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$ (implemented approximately via the Cayley parameterization) to limit how far the transform can move away from identity.

Practical parameterizations used in the paper:
- Cayley transform to enforce orthogonality: $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$.
- Block‑diagonal $\bm{R}$ to reduce parameter count (and induce further regularization).
- Optional re‑scaled OFT: learn a diagonal scaling $\bm{D}$ so forward pass is $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$.

Initialize $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ (so $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$) to start from the pretrained model exactly.

## Practical tradeoffs: parameters, convergence, inference
- Parameter efficiency: LoRA scales with the chosen low rank $r'$ (parameters $\propto r'(d+n)$ for a $d\times n$ weight), OFT scales with the orthogonal parameterization. Using block‑diagonal and block sharing, OFT can be competitive; example from the paper: for a $128\times128$ weight, LoRA with $r'=8$ uses $2{,}048$ trainable params while OFT with $r=8$ uses $960$ params.

- Convergence and stability: OFT and COFT converge faster and more stably in the paper’s experiments. For example, on the landmark‑to‑face (L2F) task LoRA needed 20 epochs to reach what OFT achieved by epoch 8. In subject personalization (DreamBooth setting) OFT/COFT preserved subject identity more robustly across many training iterations; DreamBooth and LoRA were more sensitive and in some cases produced collapses or identity degradation.

- Inference overhead: OFT introduces no extra runtime cost because the learned orthogonal transform can be folded into the pretrained weights ($\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$). ControlNet / T2I‑Adapter add modules that run at inference and therefore increase latency and memory footprint. (This difference can be decisive for deployment on constrained devices or for high‑throughput services.)

- Control fidelity: the paper introduced a control‑consistency evaluation (e.g., IoU/F1 for edge maps, mIoU/accuracy for segmentation, landmark error for poses). Across Canny edge → image, segmentation → image and landmark → face tasks, OFT/COFT achieved stronger and more accurate control than the baselines in the experiments.

## Which method to pick for common tasks

Subject personalization (DreamBooth style)
- Recommended: OFT or COFT.
- Why: OFT preserves the pretrained model’s directional geometry (hyperspherical energy) so subject identity and generalization are retained. COFT adds explicit radius control ($\epsilon$) if you want an even safer, less flexible finetune. In the paper OFT/COFT outperformed DreamBooth and LoRA on identity metrics (DINO, CLIP‑I) and were less sensitive to the number of finetuning iterations.

Edge / segmentation control (C2I / S2I)
- Recommended: OFT.
- Why: OFT converged faster and gave higher control consistency scores with fewer training examples and epochs compared to ControlNet and T2I‑Adapter. It also produced high‑fidelity images and can be deployed with no extra inference modules.

Pose / landmark‑to‑face (L2F)
- Recommended: OFT (or COFT if you want tight safety).
- Why: Faster, smoother convergence and more accurate pose alignment were observed in the paper. ControlNet achieved similar final accuracy in some settings but required more epochs and produced a “sudden” emergence of controllability; OFT’s convergence was steadier and more predictable.

When you might prefer ControlNet / T2I‑Adapter
- You want modularity: attach multiple separate adapters for different control types and enable/disable them at inference without rewriting base weights.
- You cannot or do not want to modify the base model weights (organizational or product constraints).
- You accept extra inference cost in exchange for a separate control module.

When LoRA is a good fit
- You need an extremely simple, widely supported low‑rank finetuning option (particularly common in transformer NLP workflows).
- LoRA remains parameter‑efficient and easy to plug into existing codebases; but in the diffusion finetuning experiments in this paper it was less robust at preserving subject identity and control fidelity than OFT.

## Implementation tips and practical knobs
- Where to apply OFT: in the paper, OFT was applied to the same layers people often tune with LoRA (e.g., attention weights) for fair comparison. OFT is architecture‑agnostic and can be used on fully connected, convolutional, or Transformer blocks.
- Block size / parameter budget: use block‑diagonal $\bm{R}$ to trade flexibility for parameter efficiency. Sharing blocks reduces parameters further.
- Initialization & safety: initialize the Cayley parameter $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ so $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ and you begin exactly at the pretrained model. If you are worried about unwanted drift, use COFT’s $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$ constraint (implemented by projecting $\bm{Q}$ in the Cayley parameterization).
- Optional: re‑scaled OFT (learn diagonal $\bm{D}$) gives extra flexibility at negligible parameter cost.
- Monitoring: because OFT preserves hyperspherical energy by construction, it is less necessary to tune extremely small learning rates, but you should monitor your usual validation metrics (CLIP/DINO/LPIPS or the paper’s control consistency metrics) to decide training length.

## Caveats and open questions
- Enforcing orthogonality via the Cayley transform involves matrix inverse operations; block‑diagonal structure helps but the method can still be more expensive to compute during training than purely additive low‑rank updates. The paper lists more efficient differentiable orthogonalization as an open problem.
- OFT’s parameter efficiency depends on the block‑diagonal design; that bias limits flexibility compared to unconstrained updates. Finding better parameter‑efficient parametrizations without inducing strong biases is open.
- Compositionality: orthogonal matrices multiply to orthogonal matrices, so in principle OFT finetunings from multiple tasks could be composed. Whether that preserves multiple downstream behaviors in practice is an open direction called out by the authors.

## Bottom line
If your goal is to finetune a text‑to‑image diffusion model for subject personalization or for accurate, deployable control (edges, segmentation, pose) while keeping inference cost minimal and getting fast, stable convergence, OFT / COFT is a very compelling option. ControlNet/T2I‑Adapter still make sense when you want modular adapters or cannot modify base weights. LoRA is a lightweight baseline that is simple and broadly supported, but in the diffusion finetuning experiments reported in the paper it was less reliable at preserving pretrained semantics and control fidelity than OFT.

If you want to try OFT: start with identity initialization, a block‑diagonal Cayley parameterization for $\bm{R}$, and COFT’s small $\epsilon$ if you prefer conservative updates. Monitor your control consistency metrics and CLIP/DINO identity metrics — OFT’s stability usually makes hyperparameter selection much easier than naive finetuning.



# Personalizing Stable Diffusion with Only a Few Photos

Personalizing a large text-to-image model like Stable Diffusion from just a handful of photos is now a common and powerful workflow: give the model a few images of a person or object, finetune it, and then ask for new images of that subject in novel scenes via text prompts. The popular DreamBooth recipe does exactly this, but in practice naive finetuning can be fragile — identity details slip, training can collapse if you run too long, and hyperparameter tuning is tedious.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a simple principled twist on finetuning, tackles these problems. Applied inside the DreamBooth-style pipeline, OFT preserves the pretrained model’s semantic generation ability while giving you faster, more stable personalization that better preserves identity and follows prompts. Below I explain the idea, why it helps, and how it was used in the paper to improve subject-driven generation on Stable Diffusion.

## The core idea — preserve neuron angles, not just weights

Most finetuning methods try to keep the finetuned weights close to pretrained weights in Euclidean space. OFT takes a different structural view: it preserves the pairwise angular relationships among neurons in each layer. The intuition is that neuron directions (angles on the unit sphere) encode semantic information; preserving their relative angles keeps the model’s pretrained generative knowledge intact while letting you rotate that representation to encode a new subject.

Formally, OFT is designed to keep the hyperspherical energy of a layer unchanged. For a weight matrix $W = [w_1,\dots,w_n]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, let $\hat w_i = w_i / \|w_i\|$ and define the hyperspherical energy
$$
\text{HE}(W) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat w_i - \hat w_j\|^{-1}.
$$
OFT aims to finetune without changing this quantity, i.e. minimize $\|\text{HE}(W) - \text{HE}(W^0)\|$ where $W^0$ is the pretrained layer. A sufficient way to achieve that is to apply a global orthogonal transform to the layer:
$$
W = R W^0, \quad\text{with}\quad R^\top R = I.
$$
An orthogonal $R$ preserves all pairwise angles between neuron vectors, so the hyperspherical energy is unchanged.

## Why rotating neurons (angles) makes sense

Two complementary intuitions motivate this choice.

First, empirical work shows angular relationships of features encode semantics well: normalizing neurons to the unit sphere often preserves representational power. The paper also includes a toy autoencoder experiment: when reconstructing images, the angular component of neuron responses preserves most of the semantic information while magnitudes contribute little. This suggests changing neuron directions is the most effective way to change semantic behavior (e.g., to inject subject identity).

Second, orthogonality constrains the finetuning space. A fully unconstrained change to directions is flexible but dangerous: it can easily destroy pretrained structure. An orthogonal transform is a compact, smooth, full-rank way to change directions without scrambling the relational structure among neurons. In practice that gives faster, more stable finetuning.

A convenient analogy is the 2D Fourier decomposition: the phase (angle) of the spectrum carries most semantic content; changing phase changes semantics but preserving relative phase structure keeps consistent content. OFT operates on neuron directions in a similar spirit.

## Implementing OFT efficiently

A few practical details make OFT usable on large models:

- Parameterize the orthogonal matrix $R$ with the Cayley transform: $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ where $Q$ is skew-symmetric ($Q^\top = -Q$). This enforces orthogonality during training while letting us optimize a compact parameter $Q$.
- Use block-diagonal $R$ to reduce parameter count. Partition the neuron dimension $d$ into $r$ blocks and learn smaller orthogonal blocks $\{R_1,\dots,R_r\}$. Block sharing can shrink parameters further.
- Initialize $R=I$ by starting with $Q=0$ so finetuning begins exactly from the pretrained model.
- Optionally learn a positive diagonal rescaling $D=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ and use $W = R W^0 D$ (re-scaled OFT) to give mild extra flexibility. The paper found re-scaled OFT usually helps, but the basic orthogonal idea alone already provides strong gains.

A concrete parameter-efficiency illustration from the paper: for a $128\times128$ weight, LoRA with rank $r'=8$ uses 2,048 trainable parameters, while OFT with $r=8$ blocks uses 960 parameters in the example — OFT is competitive in parameter budget while offering a different inductive bias.

## Constrained OFT (COFT) for extra stability

To further stabilize finetuning, the paper proposes Constrained OFT (COFT), which restricts how far $R$ can deviate from the identity:
$$
\|R - I\| \le \epsilon.
$$
Using the Cayley parameterization and a Neumann-series approximation $R\approx I + 2Q + \mathcal{O}(Q^2)$, this constraint can be enforced approximately as a small-norm constraint on $Q$ (projected gradient updates). COFT thus combines the angle-preserving orthogonality constraint with an explicit radius constraint around the pretrained model, producing an even more conservative and stable personalization.

## Why OFT helps DreamBooth-style personalization

Applied to subject-driven generation (the DreamBooth setting: personalize a text-to-image model from a few photos of a subject), OFT delivers several concrete benefits demonstrated in the paper:

- Better identity preservation. OFT and COFT keep subject-specific details more reliably than both naive DreamBooth finetuning and LoRA-style low-rank updates. In qualitative examples, LoRA sometimes loses identity (e.g., generating a wrong face or fur instead of the target clothing), whereas OFT keeps identity faithful while varying context per prompts.
- Faster and more stable convergence. The paper reports OFT variants reach good control quickly (e.g., by a few hundred iterations), while other methods are slower or unstable. In long runs, naive DreamBooth can start to collapse and LoRA may fail to preserve identity; OFT and COFT remain stable even with many iterations, making the iteration count much less sensitive.
- Stronger metrics for subject fidelity. The authors evaluated subject fidelity with metrics like DINO and CLIP image similarity (CLIP-I). Both OFT and COFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA on those identity metrics by a noticeable margin, while matching or slightly improving prompt fidelity (CLIP-text) and diversity (LPIPS).
- Sample efficiency. OFT converges with fewer training images and epochs in the experiments, meaning you can get a usable personalized model from only a handful of photos and modest compute.

A practical bonus: OFT introduces no extra inference overhead. After training, you can multiply the learned $R$ into the pretrained weights to obtain the final $W=R W^0$ and run exactly the same model at the same runtime cost.

## Practical recipe (what to try)

If you want to personalize Stable Diffusion in a DreamBooth-style setup and try OFT, follow the approach used in the paper:

- Base model: start from a pretrained Stable Diffusion checkpoint (the authors used v1.5).
- Loss / pipeline: keep the DreamBooth reconstruction + prior-preservation losses the same so comparisons are apples-to-apples.
- Where to apply OFT: for fairness to adapter methods like LoRA, the experiments applied OFT to the same attention-weight matrices LoRA targets. OFT can also be applied to other layers (MLP / conv), but attention is a common starting point.
- Parameterization: use the Cayley transform for orthogonality, block-diagonal $R$ to control parameter count, initialize $Q=0$ so $R=I$ initially.
- Stability tip: try COFT (small $\epsilon$) if you want an easier-to-tune, very stable personalization process. COFT makes learning-rate and iteration-count choices less critical.
- Optional: try the re-scaled OFT variant if you want additional flexibility at small parameter cost.

## Limitations and open problems

OFT is a principled and practical finetuning scheme, but it has trade-offs and open issues:

- Cayley transform requires a matrix inverse; block-diagonalization mitigates but does not remove this overhead. Scaling orthogonal parameterizations efficiently to very large layers is still an engineering challenge.
- The block-diagonal structure reduces parameters but adds a bias that may limit ultimate expressivity; finding better parameter-efficient orthogonal parameterizations is an open problem.
- The paper highlights an intriguing compositionality idea: orthogonal transforms from multiple personalization tasks can be multiplied together and remain orthogonal — whether this composes in practice to preserve multiple subjects or tasks is a direction for future work.

## Takeaway

If your goal is to personalize Stable Diffusion from only a few photos and you want results that reliably preserve identity, follow prompts, and train quickly without brittle hyperparameter tuning, Orthogonal Finetuning (and its constrained variant COFT) is a method worth trying. It shifts the finetuning objective away from raw weight drift toward preserving the angular geometry of neuron representations, and that structural inductive bias pays off in more stable, sample-efficient DreamBooth-style personalization — with no inference penalty.



# From Edges, Segments, and Landmarks to Photorealism

Controllable text-to-image models let you steer generation with more than a caption: a Canny edge map, a segmentation layout, or a set of facial landmarks can be used to produce images that follow a desired structure. The challenge is finetuning a large pretrained diffusion model so it obeys those control signals without destroying the model’s pretrained generative abilities (photorealism, diversity, and semantic understanding). Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), and its constrained variant COFT, offer a principled answer: change neuron directions via layer-wise orthogonal transforms so the model learns to respect control inputs while preserving the pretrained internal geometry. This post explains how OFT is applied to three common control tasks — Canny edges (C2I), segmentation maps (S2I), and facial landmarks (L2F) — how the paper measures control accuracy with a new control consistency metric, and why OFT gets stronger, more accurate control with fewer updates.

## The three control tasks, in one paragraph each

- Canny edge → image (C2I). The input is a sparse edge sketch. The generator must “hallucinate” plausible surfaces, textures and object fills that are consistent with these sparse cues. This is a weak, ambiguous control signal that tests semantic completion.

- Segmentation map → image (S2I). The input is a dense per-pixel class layout. Here the task is to produce photorealistic imagery that respects object boundaries, layout and class labels. Success requires accurate spatial structure and realistic rendering within each region.

- Landmarks → face (L2F). The input is a set of facial keypoints. The generator must align face pose, expression and geometry to the landmarks while keeping identity and photorealism intact. This tests fine-grained geometric control.

All three are standard benchmarks in the paper and share the same core problem: how to adapt a pretrained diffusion model so it reliably follows the control signal while keeping its pretrained strengths.

## How the paper measures “how well control is followed”: the control consistency metric

A key contribution of the work is a task-agnostic, practical measure of control fidelity: estimate the control signal from the generated image using off-the-shelf detectors / predictors, then compare that estimate to the original control input. The metric is tailored per task:

- For C2I (edge maps) the pipeline computes the Canny edges from the generated image and compares them to the input edge map with IoU and F1 scores. Higher IoU/F1 means the generated image reproduces the structural strokes.

- For S2I (segmentation maps) the approach runs a segmentation model on the generated image to get predicted class maps and compares those to the input segmentation using mean IoU and mean/overall accuracy. This quantifies layout and class adherence.

- For L2F (landmarks) a facial landmark detector is run on the generated image and the mean $\ell_2$ distance between predicted and input landmark coordinates is computed:
  \[
    \text{mean L2} \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \left\| \hat{\ell}_i - \ell_i \right\|_2,
  \]
  where $\ell_i$ are the target landmark points and $\hat{\ell}_i$ are detections from the generated image. Smaller values indicate better geometric alignment.

Important caveat: these control-consistency scores are upper-bounded by the accuracy of the off-the-shelf detector/segmenter used to extract the signal from the synthesized image, so results are interpreted “up to the accuracy of” those models. Still, the metric gives a practical, interpretable view of how faithfully a finetuned diffusion model follows its control input.

## Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) — the core idea, distilled

At a high level OFT changes neuron directions, not magnitudes, and does so in a way that provably preserves a particular global structure of the pretrained model: hyperspherical energy. A few core pieces:

- Hyperspherical energy (intuitively): for a layer with neurons (weight vectors) $\{\bm{w}_i\}$, normalize each vector $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$. The hyperspherical energy
  \[
    \text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}
  \]
  captures the pairwise angular relationships among neurons (how they are arranged on the unit hypersphere). These pairwise angles encode semantic structure learned during pretraining.

- Orthogonal invariance: if you apply the same orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$ to every neuron in a layer, the pairwise angles are preserved. That is, replacing $\bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ leaves $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$. This is the key invariant OFT exploits.

- OFT forward pass: replace layer weights $\bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ and optimize the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ under $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$. Concretely
  \[
    \bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}, \qquad \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I},
  \]
  and $\bm{R}$ is initialized to the identity so the finetuning starts exactly from the pretrained model.

Why this helps: orthogonal updates let the model move neuron directions (which are highly semantic) while guaranteeing the layer’s global angular structure is unchanged, preventing degradation of pretrained capabilities.

## COFT, Cayley, block-diagonals and practical parameter economy

Two practical refinements the paper uses:

- Constrained OFT (COFT). To avoid drifting too far from the pretrained model, COFT adds an explicit radius constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \le \epsilon$. In practice the Cayley parameterization is used: represent
  \[
    \bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}, \qquad \bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q},
  \]
  with skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$. Using the Neumann expansion one gets $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$, so constraining $\|\bm{Q}\|$ via projected gradient descent implements the COFT radius constraint. COFT tends to be even more stable than OFT during finetuning.

- Block-diagonal parameterization for $\bm{R}$. A full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix has $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. To reduce cost OFT uses a block-diagonal $\bm{R}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$, each block being orthogonal in a smaller dimension. This cuts parameter count roughly by a factor of $r$ (or more if blocks are shared). The paper gives a concrete example: for a $128\times128$ weight matrix, with LoRA rank $r'=8$ the LoRA trainable parameters are $2{,}048$, while OFT with block count $r=8$ uses about $960$ parameters — a favorable trade-off.

A short note on re-scaled OFT: the authors also propose learning a diagonal magnitude matrix $\bm{D}$ (so weights become $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$). Rescaling does not change hyperspherical energy by construction (directions remain comparable), but it adds flexibility with minimal extra parameters.

Finally, unlike ControlNet-style adapters, OFT introduces no runtime overhead: at deployment one can multiply $\bm{R}$ into $\bm{W}^0$ once to obtain $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ and run the model at the original speed.

## Why OFT delivers stronger, faster control (intuition + practical effects)

1. Directions matter more than magnitudes for semantics. The paper draws an analogy to phase information in Fourier transforms: phase (angular information) carries most of the perceptual structure. Empirically, changing neuron directions is a powerful lever for semantic change.

2. Orthogonal transforms preserve the pretrained geometric relationships among neurons. That means OFT can adapt behavior (aligning it to control signals) without destroying the pretrained model’s ability to render photorealistic, semantically coherent images. In contrast, unconstrained finetuning can drift and collapse; additive low-rank updates (LoRA) are effective but operate differently and can still struggle to reach the same stability in some control regimes.

3. Optimization on a smooth manifold. Restricting updates to orthogonal matrices constrains the optimization to a well-behaved manifold, improving convergence and stability in practice.

4. Parameter-efficient yet expressive. Block-diagonal Cayley-parameterized orthogonal matrices strike a balance between expressiveness and parameter budget; COFT can further tighten stability when needed.

Empirical consequences reported in the paper: OFT and COFT converge more quickly and stably on the control tasks than LoRA, T2I-Adapter and ControlNet, often achieving better control-consistency metrics and higher-quality images with fewer training epochs and less data. For example, a reported finding was that LoRA needed 20 epochs to reach a level of landmark error that OFT already achieved by epoch 8. OFT also showed strong data-efficiency in some experiments (converging well with only 5% of ADE20K in a segmentation control test).

## Empirical highlights (what to expect if you try OFT for control)

- Faster, smoother convergence. Training dynamics for OFT/COFT are continuous and predictable; ControlNet-style methods sometimes exhibit “sudden” emergence of controllability after several epochs, while OFT tends to improve steadily.

- Stronger control-consistency scores. Across C2I, S2I and L2F, OFT/COFT gave better control-consistency (IoU/F1 for edges, mean IoU/accuracy for segmentation, lower mean L2 for landmarks) than competing finetuning/adaptation methods in the paper’s experiments.

- Better qualitative output. Generated images matched control signals more faithfully while retaining vivid, photorealistic detail and coherent geometry.

- No runtime penalty. Once trained, the finetuned weights yield the same inference cost as the original model.

## Practical tips

- If you want the safest finetuning (very low drift), use COFT with a modest $\epsilon$ so the transform stays near identity; this makes learning-rate and epoch choices less sensitive.

- For parameter constrained settings, tune the block count $r$ to trade between capacity and memory/compute. Shared blocks further reduce parameter counts.

- For fair comparison to LoRA, the paper applied OFT to the same attention-weight matrices that LoRA targets. That’s a sensible default.

- If you need extra flexibility, consider the re-scaled OFT variant (learnable per-neuron diagonal scale), but the baseline OFT already gives strong gains.

## Limitations and open points

- COFT and OFT use Cayley transforms that involve matrix inversion; scalability and numerical efficiency of that step can be further improved.

- The block-diagonal parameterization introduces a bias (less flexibility) in exchange for parameter efficiency; the best block structure is dataset/model dependent.

- The control-consistency metric depends on off-the-shelf detectors/segmenters; interpretations of absolute scores should account for their accuracy limits.

- Compositionality of multiple OFT finetunings (multiplying orthogonal matrices from different tasks) is an intriguing possibility mentioned in the paper but needs further study.

## Takeaway

If your goal is to adapt a large pretrained diffusion model so it faithfully follows structure-level controls — edges, layouts, or landmarks — Orthogonal Finetuning gives a principled, parameter-efficient, and practically effective route. By restricting updates to layer-wise orthogonal transforms that preserve hyperspherical structure, OFT lets the model learn to obey new control signals with fewer updates, more stable training, and no extra inference cost. The control consistency metric introduced in the work gives you a clear, task-specific way to quantify how faithfully your model follows those external controls.



# Under the Hood: Rotations at Scale with the Cayley Transform

This post peels back the engineering choices behind Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) for text‑to‑image diffusion models: how OFT parameterizes layerwise rotations with the Cayley transform, why a block‑diagonal layout keeps memory and compute practical at large scale, and what trade‑offs and implementation knobs matter when you plug OFT into a diffusion backbone.

## The short picture: why learn rotations at all?

OFT starts from a simple empirical claim: angular relationships among neurons—directions on the unit hypersphere—encode a lot of semantic information. The paper formalizes a per‑layer structural measure, the hyperspherical energy (HE), as
$ \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1} $
where $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i := \mathbf{w}_i / \|\mathbf{w}_i\|$ is a normalized neuron. OFT then asks for finetuning updates that preserve HE (so the pretrained pairwise angular geometry is not destroyed) while still giving enough flexibility to adapt to a downstream task.

Orthogonal matrices do exactly what we want: a common orthogonal transform applied to all neurons of a layer preserves pairwise angles and therefore HE. So OFT parameterizes a layerwise orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ and uses the forward pass
$ \mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x} $
instead of changing $\mathbf{W}^0$ directly. Learning $\mathbf{R}$ is effectively learning a rotation/reflection of the neuron basis rather than arbitrary weight shifts.

## Cayley parameterization: a compact way to build orthogonality

There are many ways to force orthogonality during learning; OFT adopts the Cayley parameterization. Given a skew‑symmetric matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ (so $\mathbf{Q}^\top = -\mathbf{Q}$), the Cayley map produces an orthogonal matrix
$ \mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1} $.
This construction guarantees $\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$, and because of the algebraic form it is differentiable and convenient to optimize end‑to‑end.

Two practical points from the paper:

- Cayley only produces matrices in the special orthogonal group (determinant $+1$). That rules out pure reflections (determinant $-1$), but the authors report this limitation does not harm practical performance.
- Cayley requires computing the inverse $(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}$, so numerical stability and compute cost become considerations at large dimensions. The paper uses the Neumann series
  $ \mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2) $
  under the assumption the series converges (discussion in the paper), and leverages this to relate small deviations in $\mathbf{R}$ to small $\mathbf{Q}$.

Initialize $\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{0}$ to get $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$ and therefore start finetuning exactly from the pretrained model. For the constrained variant COFT, that same $\mathbf{Q}$ space is convenient for imposing an explicit small‑deviation constraint around identity.

## Block‑diagonal layout: keeping rotations memory‑friendly

A full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix has $d(d-1)/2$ free parameters (the degrees of freedom of an orthogonal group), which becomes impractical in large layers. OFT trades off expressivity for efficiency by parameterizing $\mathbf{R}$ as block‑diagonal with $r$ equal blocks:
$ \mathbf{R} = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1,\dots,\mathbf{R}_r),\quad \mathbf{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{(d/r)\times(d/r)}. $
That reduces the number of free parameters to
$ \frac{d}{2}\left(\frac{d}{r}-1\right) = \frac{d(d/r - 1)}{2}, $
i.e. $O(d^2 / r)$. Block sharing (setting all $\mathbf{R}_i$ equal) can reduce the parameter cost even further (paper cites an $O(d^2/r^2)$ regime).

A concrete example from the paper makes the point clear. For a $128\times128$ layer and $r=8$ blocks, OFT needs
$128(128/8 - 1)/2 = 960$ trainable parameters. By contrast, LoRA with rank $r'=8$ adds $r'(d+n) = 8(128+128)=2048$ parameters for the same matrix shape. So the block‑diagonal Cayley parameterization can be more parameter‑efficient than common low‑rank adapters while retaining the orthogonality guarantee.

Two architecture notes made in the paper: (1) for Transformers the authors applied OFT to the same attention weights where LoRA is typically used (for fair comparisons), and (2) for convolutional layers the block structure admits useful interpretations—when the number of blocks equals input channels, each block acts on a channel in a depthwise‑like manner.

## Constraining the rotation: COFT and the Neumann shortcut

OFT is already a regularizer relative to arbitrary weight updates, but the paper introduces Constrained OFT (COFT) to improve stability by explicitly limiting how far $\mathbf{R}$ can deviate from identity:
$ \|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon. $
Using the Neumann approximation $ \mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2) $, the constraint can be transferred into the $\mathbf{Q}$ space as a bound $\|\mathbf{Q}\| \le \epsilon'$, which is easy to enforce with projected gradient descent on $\mathbf{Q}$. Practically, COFT combines the HE‑preserving orthogonality constraint with an explicit radius constraint around the pretrained weights—a helpful stability knob when finetuning on scarce or noisy data.

## Trade‑offs at a glance

- Expressivity vs parameter budget: smaller $r$ (fewer blocks) produces larger per‑block orthogonal transforms and therefore more expressive rotations, but at higher parameter and compute cost. Larger $r$ reduces parameters but restricts the possible transforms.
- Cayley vs full orthogonalization: Cayley is efficient and differentiable but requires inverses and yields only $\det(\mathbf{R})=+1$. The paper finds this acceptable in practice.
- Inversion cost: the inverse in Cayley is the main scalability pain point. Block‑diagonal structure reduces this to $r$ smaller inverses, which is much cheaper than a single full‑rank inverse.
- Flexibility vs preservation: OFT intentionally limits flexibility (compared to free weight updates) to preserve pretrained semantics; COFT tightens that further via an explicit radius parameter.

## Practical tips when integrating OFT into a large diffusion backbone

- Start identity: initialize $\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{0}$ (so $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$) to guarantee you begin from the pretrained model exactly.
- Pick $r$ to match your memory/expressivity budget: the paper’s $128\times128$, $r=8$ example (960 params) is a good reference point—adjust up (more params, more flexibility) or down (fewer params, stronger regularization) from there.
- Use block‑diagonal Cayley to keep inversion cheap: since $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}$ on a block‑diagonal matrix is the blockwise inverse, the matrix inverse cost scales with block size not full $d$.
- If you need extra stability, use COFT: enforce $\|\mathbf{Q}\| \le \epsilon'$ via projected gradient steps (the paper uses this projected approach).
- No inference penalty: after finetuning you can precompute $\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ and use the resulting weight matrix directly at inference time—OFT adds no run‑time overhead.
- Where to apply it: the authors applied OFT to the same weights LoRA targets (attention projections) to allow fair comparisons, and this is a practical starting point.

## Open issues called out by the paper

The authors point to a couple of remaining pain points that are useful to keep in mind if you plan to productionize OFT: Cayley’s matrix inverse is a scalability limiter (block‑diagonal reduces but does not eliminate this cost), and block structure itself introduces bias (it limits the set of achievable transforms). The paper also notes an intriguing composability property—orthogonal matrices multiply to orthogonal matrices—raising the possibility of composing multiple task‑specific OFT matrices, but whether that preserves multiple tasks’ knowledge reliably remains an open question.

## Bottom line

If you want an adapter that explicitly preserves pretrained angular geometry, Cayley‑parameterized OFT is an elegant way to learn layerwise rotations. The combination—skew‑symmetric $\mathbf{Q}$, Cayley map, block‑diagonal layout, and optional COFT radius constraint—gives a clean trade‑off between stability, parameter budget, and finetuning flexibility. The paper’s experiments show this is a practical and effective approach for subject‑driven and controllable image generation with diffusion models, and the implementation recipe above is a compact summary of the choices that make it scale.



# Composing Skills by Multiplying Rotations: The Next Frontier of OFT

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) introduced a simple, principled lever for steering large text‑to‑image diffusion models: learn a layer‑shared orthogonal matrix \(R\) and apply it to pretrained weights \(W^0\) so that the finetuned weights are \(W = R W^0\). Because orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise angles among neurons, OFT provably preserves the model's hyperspherical energy — a structural quantity the paper argues is important for keeping the pretrained semantics intact. In this post I want to explore a natural, forward‑looking idea that the paper itself flags as promising: if each finetuning job produces an orthogonal matrix, we can compose skills simply by multiplying those matrices. Below I summarize what that composition buys you, what it doesn’t, and concrete technical questions and constraints you should care about when you try to turn OFT into a modular “Lego brick” toolchain.

## Quick recap: the math you need in your head
Two short facts from the paper that drive everything below:

- If \(R\) is orthogonal then \(R^\top R = I\). If a layer’s finetuned weights are \(W = R W^0\), then normalized neurons rotate as \(\hat w_i = R \hat w_i^0\), and all pairwise inner products are preserved:
  \[
  \langle \hat w_i, \hat w_j\rangle = \langle R\hat w_i^0, R\hat w_j^0\rangle = \langle \hat w_i^0, \hat w_j^0\rangle.
  \]
  Hence the hyperspherical energy \(\text{HE}(W)\) is unchanged.

- Orthogonal matrices compose: if \(R_1,\dots,R_k\in O(d)\) then
  \[
  R_{\text{comp}} = R_k \cdots R_1 \in O(d),
  \]
  and so \(R_{\text{comp}}\) also preserves hyperspherical energy.

These two observations are exactly why the paper highlights compositionality as an attractive open direction: the product of per‑task rotations is still a valid OFT transform.

## What composing rotations actually gives you
If you finetune separate skills independently and keep their learned orthogonal matrices \(\{R_s\}\), you can form a composed model that applies a selected set of skills by multiplying their \(R\)'s and folding the result into the base weights:
\[
R_{\text{composed}} = R_{s_m}\cdots R_{s_1}, \qquad W_{\text{composed}} = R_{\text{composed}} W^0.
\]
Two practical and useful consequences follow directly from the paper:

- No runtime overhead: once you compute \(W_{\text{composed}}\) you have a single weight matrix. This follows from the same folding trick the paper uses for single‑skill OFT: inference cost is identical to the pretrained model.
- Hyperspherical energy (and therefore the pairwise angular structure the paper argues encodes semantic generalization) remains preserved under composition because the composed transform is orthogonal.

This is the basic “Lego brick” picture: store small rotation bricks per skill and multiply them when you want a combination.

## Ordering, interference, and why composition is not magic
Matrices do not commute in general. Even though the product is orthogonal, \(R_a R_b \neq R_b R_a\) in general, so the sequence in which you compose skills will matter. That has three practical implications:

- Semantics can be order‑dependent. Applying a “style” rotation then a “pose” rotation might produce a different result than the reverse.
- Multiplying many nontrivial rotations can move the total transform far from identity. While each \(R_s\) individually preserves angles, their combined effect may be a large rotation that changes generation behavior in ways that were not anticipated when each skill was trained in isolation.
- Whether a composed rotation actually “retains” the quality of each individual skill (i.e., the composed model performs well on all constituent tasks) is an open empirical question. The paper explicitly raises this as an open problem.

The paper already provides a practical tool to control these effects: Constrained OFT (COFT), which enforces a radius constraint \(\|R - I\| \le \epsilon\) (implemented via a skew‑symmetric Cayley parameter \(Q\) with \(\|Q\|\) small). Training each skill with a small \(\epsilon\) keeps per‑skill rotations near identity, which makes their product less likely to “drift” and might reduce destructive interference when you compose many skills.

## Re‑scaled OFT: adding magnitude without breaking angles
The paper also considers a re‑scaled OFT variant that learns a diagonal magnitude matrix \(D=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)\) in addition to the rotation:
\[
z = (R W^0 D)^\top x.
\]
Because hyperspherical energy is computed on normalized neurons, scaling columns by positive \(s_i\) does not change the pairwise angular structure; the paper notes that rescaling therefore leaves HE invariant while giving extra flexibility.

How this impacts composition is subtle. Rotations compose cleanly (products remain orthogonal). Scaling matrices multiply elementwise on neuron columns, and a naïve composition rule for a stack of skills could be
\[
W_{\text{comp}} = \Big(\prod_{t} R_t\Big) W^0 \Big(\prod_{t} D_t\Big),
\]
i.e., compose rotations on the left and scalings on the right. The paper does not explore this design choice empirically, but it suggests an attractive engineering pattern: store per‑skill \(R_s\) and \(D_s\), compose rotations multiplicatively and aggregate scalings multiplicatively (or via a learned gating) at inference time. This keeps the angle‑preserving core of OFT while letting each skill modulate neuron magnitudes for finer control. Whether that combination reliably preserves multiple skills remains to be tested.

## Parameter efficiency and scaling constraints (practical realities)
The paper is clear about two bottlenecks and trade‑offs:

- Cayley parameterization is a convenient differentiable way to produce orthogonals, but it requires a matrix inverse and currently limits scalability. The authors handle this practically with a block‑diagonal parameterization.
- Block‑diagonal parameterization reduces trainable parameters: an \(r\)-block diagonal \(R\) has parameter complexity \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)\); sharing blocks across channels can further reduce complexity toward \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)\). But block structure introduces bias (less flexibility) and is part of the “flexibility vs regularity” trade‑off the paper emphasizes.

A concrete example from the paper: for a \(128\times 128\) weight matrix, LoRA with \(r'=8\) uses 2,048 trainable parameters, while OFT with \(r=8\) uses 960 parameters. So OFT is competitive in parameter efficiency, but storing many full \(R_s\) matrices for many skills will still add up. The block structure is the principal lever the paper offers for keeping a library of skill rotations compact.

## A short list of concrete experiments to try next
The paper frames compositionality as an open problem. If you want to explore it, here are compact, paper‑grounded experiments that test the key hypotheses:

- Skill composition sanity check. Finetune two skills independently to get \(R_a\) and \(R_b\). Compare the single‑skill models \(R_a W^0\) and \(R_b W^0\) to the composed model \(R_b R_a W^0\). Evaluate with the same metrics the paper uses (e.g., subject fidelity, control consistency).
- Ordering sensitivity. Evaluate \(R_b R_a\) vs \(R_a R_b\) on the same inputs to quantify how much order matters.
- COFT as a composition regularizer. Train skills with COFT for different \(\epsilon\) and measure whether smaller \(\epsilon\) improves composed performance (less interference) at the expense of per‑skill accuracy.
- Re‑scaled OFT composition. Store \((R_s,D_s)\) per skill and test composition rules (e.g., multiply all \(R_s\) and all \(D_s\)). Measure whether combined scalings harm angle preservation or improve multi‑skill fidelity.
- Block‑size sweep. For a fixed set of skills, vary the block count \(r\) and block‑sharing strategy to map the trade‑off between parameter savings and compositional quality.

These are all natural extensions of the paper’s experimental setup — the same Stable Diffusion v1.5 backbone and the same control‑consistency metrics apply.

## High‑level design patterns for a future OFT toolchain
Taking all of the above together, here are a few practical patterns you can build toward:

- Skill library: store per‑layer, per‑skill block‑diagonal Cayley parameters (or \(R_s\) after folding), plus optional diagonal \(D_s\) scalings.
- Compose at load time: pick an ordered list of skills, multiply their \(R\)'s to produce a single \(R_{\text{composed}}\), fold into \(W^0\), and run with zero extra inference overhead.
- Use COFT for multi‑skill robustness: prefer training skills with a constrained radius \(\epsilon\) so compositions remain well behaved.
- Make order explicit: expose skill sequencing in the UI/tooling because composition is generally order‑sensitive.

## Open problems the paper highlights (and why they matter)
The paper calls out, and the composition idea makes more urgent, these unresolved issues:

- Efficient, differentiable orthogonal parameterizations without expensive inverses. Cayley works but can be a bottleneck; block‑diagonalization helps but is a blunt tool.
- Do orthogonal matrices composed post‑hoc preserve all downstream task knowledge? There is no theoretical guarantee that independently optimized \(R_s\) will combine cleanly.
- Parameter efficiency beyond block structure: block‑diagonalization introduces inductive bias. How to represent a rich set of rotations compactly without that bias is open.

Each of these is central to making OFT a practical, scalable system for many skills.

## Closing thoughts
The algebra is simple and elegant: rotations compose, and preserving neuron angles is precisely the inductive bias OFT brings to finetuning. That algebra makes a genuinely new workflow plausible: train small, cheap, angle‑preserving transforms for individual skills, and compose them at inference to assemble multi‑skill models on demand. The paper gives the starting toolkit — OFT, COFT, and re‑scaled OFT — and clearly lays out the parameterization and stability trade‑offs. The next steps are empirical and engineering ones: find parameterizations that scale, measure how composed rotations interact, and build the tooling that lets practitioners pick and sequence "rotation bricks" the way creative users pick stickers in a GUI. If it works, we get a modular, low‑overhead way to teach large generative models many complementary abilities and assemble them like Lego. The payoff would be a compact, interpretable library of skills you can multiply together — literally — to create new capabilities.
