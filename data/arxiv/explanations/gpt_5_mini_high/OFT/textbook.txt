\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

From Text Prompts to Controlled Images: Motivation and Overview

# Limitations of pure text guidance for fine-grained control

Large text-to-image diffusion models have pushed the quality of prompt-driven image synthesis to unprecedented levels. Nevertheless, the paper emphasizes a practical limitation: text alone is often too ambiguous to specify many forms of fine-grained control that users want. A short natural-language prompt can describe a broad scene or a set of attributes, but it typically leaves a vast space of valid images that satisfy those words. As a result, when the goal is precise control — for example, preserving the identity of a specific subject across new contexts, matching a given edge layout or segmentation map, or enforcing a particular facial pose — text guidance by itself can be insufficient.

Two concrete manifestations of this limitation motivate the work. First, when a user wants images of a particular subject (a person, a pet, a product) in new scenes, a text prompt does not uniquely identify the subtle identity cues present in a handful of photographs. Second, when a user supplies a structured control signal (edges, segmentation, landmarks), the pretrained text-to-image model may not respect that signal closely, because the model was not trained to condition on that exact form of control. In both cases, the practical remedy is to adapt the pretrained model to the downstream requirement — but doing so naively risks destroying the rich, general-purpose generative capability learned during pretraining.

# Two target tasks: subject-driven generation and controllable generation

The paper frames two downstream tasks that illustrate the kinds of control users need and that serve as the experimental focus.

Subject-driven generation asks: given a few images of a specific subject, generate new images of that same subject in different contexts under text guidance. The goal is to capture and reproduce the subject-specific appearance (identity, textures, etc.) while letting other aspects (background, pose, lighting) vary according to the prompt. This task is practically important (personalized content creation) and technically challenging because the adaptation must inject subject-specific signals into the generator without erasing the model's general text-conditioned capabilities.

Controllable generation asks: given an additional structured control signal together with a text prompt — examples include Canny edges, segmentation maps, or facial landmarks — generate images that follow the control signal while remaining consistent with the text. Here the model must learn to use the extra control input reliably and to blend it with the text semantics. The difficulty is similar: we must adapt the pretrained model so it accepts and honors the new conditioning without compromising the model's fidelity and diversity.

Both tasks reduce to the same core technical problem: how to finetune a large text-to-image diffusion model so that it gains the desired downstream control while preserving the pretraining generative performance.

# Finetuning desiderata: efficiency and preservation of generalization

When adapting a large pretrained generator, two desiderata stand out in the paper.

The first is training efficiency. Practically, we want methods that modify as few parameters as possible, converge in a small number of training steps or epochs, and are sample efficient (work with few subject images or small control datasets). This keeps finetuning fast, cheap, and usable in realistic settings.

The second is preservation of generalization: the finetuned model should retain the high-fidelity, diverse generative ability learned during pretraining. In other words, finetuning should grant new capabilities (subject identity injection or control signal conditioning) without degrading the model's previously learned semantics or causing collapse to low-diversity outputs.

Existing strategies fall broadly into two classes. One is to update model weights directly with a tiny learning rate (the DreamBooth-style approach). The other is to add a small, reparameterized component such as adapters or low-rank updates (LoRA-style methods). Both strategies have pragmatic value but, as the paper argues, neither provides a principled guarantee that the pretrained generative behavior is preserved. Practitioners typically rely on heuristics — very small updates, careful early stopping, or regularization — because there is no structural measure that captures when the pretrained semantics are preserved.

A common implicit assumption is that keeping the finetuned weights close in Euclidean distance to the pretrained weights ($\|M - M^0\|$ small) suffices to preserve behavior. The paper points out that this Euclidean notion can miss higher-order structure in how neurons encode semantics. What is needed is a structural measure that reflects the relational geometry among neurons, because that geometry is closely tied to what the network represents. The paper introduces such a structural viewpoint based on angular relations among neuron weight vectors.

# Key idea: preserve neuron angular relations via orthogonal transforms

The central conceptual move of the paper is to treat the pairwise angular relations among neurons as an essential carrier of pretrained semantics, and to design finetuning that preserves those relations while allowing controlled, structured changes.

Hyperspherical energy. To formalize the pairwise geometry, the paper uses hyperspherical energy (HE). For a weight matrix $\bm{W} = [\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ whose columns $\bm{w}_i$ are neuron vectors, define the normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$. The hyperspherical energy of the layer is
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
This quantity aggregates pairwise (angular) relationships across the layer: it depends only on the normalized directions of the neurons and therefore captures their arrangement on the unit hypersphere.

Why angular relations matter. Two lines of intuition justify focusing on angles. First, prior empirical observations (e.g., SphereNet-style ideas referenced in the paper) suggest that neuron directions — not magnitudes — often carry the bulk of semantic signal. The authors illustrate this with a toy convolutional autoencoder experiment where, after training with standard inner products, reconstructing images using only angular information (the cosine component between input patches and kernels) preserves semantics far better than using magnitudes alone. Second, the analogy to Fourier decomposition is invoked: in images the phase (angular) spectrum preserves major semantic structure while magnitude contributes less to content identity. Together these points argue that modifying neuron directions is the key lever for altering semantic outputs, while preserving the relational geometry among neurons is important to keep pretrained knowledge intact.

Orthogonal invariance and the OFT principle. Orthogonal matrices act by rotating or reflecting vectors and therefore preserve Euclidean norms and pairwise distances. That simple fact yields a powerful invariance: if we replace $\bm{W}^0$ by $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ for some orthogonal $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ with $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, then each normalized neuron becomes $\widehat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{R}\widehat{\bm{w}}_i^0$, and pairwise distances are unchanged:
\[
\|\widehat{\bm{w}}_i - \widehat{\bm{w}}_j\| = \|\bm{R}\widehat{\bm{w}}_i^0 - \bm{R}\widehat{\bm{w}}_j^0\| = \|\widehat{\bm{w}}_i^0 - \widehat{\bm{w}}_j^0\|.
\]
Consequently $\text{HE}(\bm{W})=\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$, so orthogonal transforms provably preserve hyperspherical energy.

Based on this invariance, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) proposes to finetune a pretrained layer only by learning a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. In a fully connected layer with input $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and original forward pass $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, OFT replaces the forward pass with
\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\qquad\text{subject to }\ \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}.
\]
Initializing $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ guarantees that finetuning starts from the pretrained weights. The paper frames OFT as a way to minimize the hyperspherical energy difference,
\[
\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\right\|,
\]
and observes that an attainable optimum is any orthogonally transformed copy $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$.

Efficient parameterization. A naive unconstrained orthogonal matrix has $d(d-1)/2$ degrees of freedom, which can be large. The paper adopts two practical strategies. First, it uses the Cayley parameterization
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},
\]
where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric ($\bm{Q}^\top=-\bm{Q}$). This parameterization produces orthogonal matrices (in fact special orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$) and is differentiable so it can be optimized by gradient methods. Second, the orthogonal matrix can be structured as block-diagonal with $r$ blocks, each an orthogonal matrix of size $d/r$. The block-diagonal form reduces the number of learnable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, and sharing blocks across groups can reduce parameters further. These choices trade off flexibility and parameter efficiency while retaining orthogonality and therefore HE preservation.

Constrained OFT (COFT). Preserving hyperspherical energy is a strong regularizer, but the authors also argue that explicit control of the magnitude of deviation from the pretrained model helps finetuning stability. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) adds a radial constraint around the identity:
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x},\quad
\text{s.t.}\ \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I},\quad \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon.
\]
Because enforcing the constraint directly on $\bm{R}$ is inconvenient under the Cayley transform, the paper uses a small-$\bm{Q}$ approximation. The Neumann series yields $\bm{R}\approx\bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$, so constraining $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$ can be approximated by constraining $\|\bm{Q}\|\le\epsilon'$, which is amenable to projected gradient steps. COFT thus couples the orthogonality constraint (preserving HE) with an explicit neighborhood constraint that enforces closeness to pretrained coordinates. Empirically, COFT improves finetuning stability relative to OFT alone.

Re-scaling extension. Because hyperspherical energy depends only on directions, the paper also describes a simple extension that allows per-neuron magnitude adaptation without changing HE. Re-scaled OFT introduces a learnable diagonal matrix $\bm{D}=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ with positive entries and uses
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 \bm{D})^\top \bm{x},
\]
permitting limited magnitude changes as an extra degree of freedom. This extension is lightweight in parameters and can improve flexibility while retaining the angular-geometry preservation principle.

Relation to other finetuning methods. It is useful to contrast OFT with common alternatives. LoRA constrains the additive weight update by assuming low rank: $\bm{M}-\bm{M}^0 \approx \bm{A}\bm{B}^\top$ with small rank. OFT updates are multiplicative via $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$, and $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ plays an analogous role to a learned additive update. Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal (and often block-structured), OFT represents a different and complementary path to parameter efficiency: it imposes angular-geometry preservation rather than low-rank structure.

Why this helps optimization and semantics. The paper offers two intuitions for why OFT tends to converge quickly and remain stable. First, changing neuron directions is (empirically) the most effective way to alter semantic outputs, so OFT provides a direct mechanism for the needed adaptations. Second, restricting updates to orthogonal transforms restricts the optimization to a smooth manifold and reduces pathological directions in parameter space, yielding a more favorable optimization landscape and thus faster and more predictable convergence.

# Contributions of OFT and COFT and high-level empirical gains

The paper makes both methodological and empirical contributions, summarized here.

Methodological contributions. The primary contribution is Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a principled finetuning approach that adapts pretrained text-to-image diffusion models by learning layer-shared orthogonal transforms. OFT has the provable property of preserving hyperspherical energy for each transformed layer, which the authors argue is crucial for maintaining the pretrained model's semantic generation ability. The paper also introduces Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which supplements OFT with an explicit radius constraint to keep the learned orthogonal transform close to the identity, improving stability. A simple re-scaled OFT variant allows per-neuron magnitude adjustments without breaking the HE invariance.

Empirical gains. The paper evaluates OFT and COFT on two downstream problems — subject-driven generation (within the DreamBooth paradigm) and controllable generation (matching the setups of ControlNet and T2I-Adapter). High-level empirical claims supported in the paper include:

- Improved stability and faster convergence: OFT/COFT finetune more stably and converge faster than naive finetuning and adapter-based alternatives. For example, OFT-type methods reach good control in fewer iterations/epochs and are less sensitive to the exact number of finetuning steps.
- Better preservation of identity and prompt fidelity in subject-driven tasks: on metrics used in the paper (DINO, CLIP-I for subject fidelity; CLIP-T for prompt fidelity; LPIPS for diversity), OFT and COFT achieve stronger subject preservation while maintaining prompt fidelity and diversity comparable to or better than baselines.
- Stronger and more accurate controllability: on controllable generation tasks (Canny-to-image, segmentation-to-image, landmark-to-face), OFT/COFT yield higher control consistency. The paper introduces a "control consistency" evaluation that recovers a control signal from generated images and compares it to the input control (e.g., IoU/F1 for edges, mean IoU/accuracy for segmentation, landmark $\ell_2$ distance for faces). OFT methods exhibit superior alignment with control inputs.
- Parameter and inference efficiency: with block structure and Cayley parameterization, OFT can match or beat LoRA in parameter efficiency in practical settings (the paper gives a concrete example where a $128\times 128$ weight matrix with rank parameter $r'=8$ gives LoRA $\sim 2048$ parameters while OFT with $r=8$ yields $\sim 960$ parameters). Importantly, OFT introduces no additional inference-time overhead: the learned orthogonal transforms can be multiplied into pretrained weights to yield an equivalent weight matrix $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, so runtime is unchanged.

Practical implications and open issues. Because OFT constrains finetuning to orthogonal transforms and optionally to a small radius around identity, it provides a principled way to trade flexibility for stability. The approach is architecture-agnostic in principle: the paper applies OFT to Transformer attention weights (for a fair comparison to LoRA) and discusses how the block structure naturally fits convolutional layers (e.g., per-channel transforms resemble depthwise kernels). The authors also identify open problems left by the approach: the Cayley transform requires a matrix inverse (a potential scalability bottleneck), understanding how orthogonal transforms compose across multiple finetuning tasks (compositionality), and further improving parameter efficiency without introducing strong biases.

Concluding summary. In short, the paper proposes a simple, principled finetuning paradigm that preserves a structural geometric property of pretrained layers (hyperspherical energy) while enabling the model to acquire downstream control. The orthogonality constraint provides a provable invariance and a regularization that yields faster, more stable finetuning and better preservation of pretrained generative semantics — all without extra test-time cost. The subsequent chapters will unpack the mathematical details of OFT, COFT, the parameterizations used in practice, and the empirical evaluation methodology and results.

Background: Diffusion Models, Conditioning, and Finetuning Strategies

# Denoising diffusion and latent diffusion basics

Diffusion-based generative models have become a dominant paradigm for high-quality image synthesis. At a high level, these models are trained to reverse a gradual corruption process: a clean image is progressively noised and the model learns to map noisy inputs back toward the data distribution. This denoising perspective is the conceptual backbone behind many recent text-to-image systems; the literature cited in the paper places diffusion approaches at the center of recent advances in photorealistic text-guided generation.

Two practical flavors of diffusion model training appear repeatedly in the literature and in the paper's comparisons: pixel-space diffusion and latent-space diffusion. Pixel-space models operate directly on image pixels; prominent examples mentioned in the paper include GLIDE and Imagen, which train the diffusion process in the pixel domain. Latent-space diffusion moves the denoising process into a learned, lower-dimensional representation of images. The advantage of the latent route is computational efficiency: an encoder (or codebook) compresses images to a latent code, the diffusion model operates in that compact latent space, and a decoder reconstructs pixels from the latent. Stable Diffusion and DALL·E 2 are examples of systems that train in such a latent space. Concretely, the paper notes that Stable Diffusion uses VQ-GAN to learn a visual codebook as its latent representation, while DALL·E 2 leverages CLIP to construct a joint embedding for images and text.

Conditioning is central to text-to-image applications: the denoising model must be guided by a textual instruction (and sometimes by extra signals such as edges or segmentation maps). The engineering choices for how to feed conditioning information—whether to jointly train a text encoder or to use a frozen pretrained encoder—vary across systems. The paper observes that GLIDE trains its text encoder jointly with a diffusion prior, whereas Imagen uses a frozen pretrained text encoder. These choices affect both training complexity and how tightly the text representations are aligned with the image generative path.

# Stable Diffusion architecture: UNet and cross-attention conditioning

State-of-the-art text-to-image diffusion systems typically organize the denoising computation in a modular backbone and attach conditioning channels that inject information from language or auxiliary signals into that backbone. In practice, many implementations use a denoising network with an image-processing backbone (frequently a U‑Net style denoiser in the literature) and conditioning mechanisms that let text and other inputs steer the denoising dynamics.

One widely used conditioning mechanism in modern text-to-image models is attention-based conditioning: a text encoder produces token-level representations that are used to modulate image features via attention operations inside the denoiser. From an engineering point of view this means that the denoiser contains components (often implemented as Transformer-style attention blocks or cross-attention layers) whose parameters connect textual representations to image features. Because these attention components are explicit places where text interacts with image generation, they are frequent targets for adaptation when people personalize or control pretrained diffusion models. The paper highlights this operational fact when discussing finetuning comparisons: for fair comparisons with adapter methods like LoRA, the experiments apply OFT to the same attention-related layers where LoRA would normally be attached.

Stable Diffusion, as a representative latent diffusion implementation, therefore combines (1) a learned latent image space (the VQ-GAN codebook referenced above), (2) a denoising backbone operating in that latent space, and (3) conditioning inputs (text encodings and, when used, extra control signals) that modulate the denoiser’s computations. Implementation choices—whether to freeze the text encoder, how to incorporate control signals, and which layers to adapt during finetuning—affect both effectiveness and practical costs such as parameter counts and inference speed.

# Subject-driven generation (e.g., DreamBooth) and its challenges

Subject-driven generation is the personalization task where the model is given a small set of images of a particular subject and the goal is to synthesize new images that faithfully depict that subject in new contexts specified by text prompts. DreamBooth is the canonical framework the paper uses as a baseline for this task. DreamBooth’s practical recipe is to introduce a custom token representing the subject, and then finetune the pretrained text-to-image diffusion model on the small subject-specific dataset using a combination of a reconstruction objective and a class-specific prior preservation loss. The latter is intended to prevent catastrophic drift: while the model should learn the new subject, it should not forget the prior that makes the model generally capable of producing diverse images.

Despite these ingredients, subject-driven finetuning poses several concrete challenges. First, subject fidelity is hard to preserve: finetuning on very few images risks overfitting or producing identity drift, where the synthesized images no longer look like the original subject. Second, finetuning must balance adaptation and preservation: we want the model to acquire the new subject-specific capability while maintaining its pretrained generalization and fidelity to text prompts. The paper abstracts these needs into two desiderata for a finetuning method: (1) training efficiency (few trainable parameters and few epochs) and (2) generalizability preservation (retaining high-fidelity, diverse, pretrained generative behavior).

Existing methods commonly take two approaches: small-weight updates across some parameters (e.g., updating the entire model or a subset with a tiny learning rate) or adding a small reparameterized component (adapter-style modules). DreamBooth itself finetunes the model directly, typically with conservative hyperparameters. However, the paper points out that simply minimizing Euclidean distance of weights to the pretrained checkpoint is an implicit regularization used by many methods, and that Euclidean closeness alone is not a structural measure for whether the model’s semantic generation ability has been preserved.

This motivates looking for measures that better capture the internal relational structure of neurons during finetuning. The paper introduces hyperspherical energy as such a structural measure: for a layer with neurons $ \{\mathbf{w}_i\} $, normalize each neuron to the unit sphere by defining $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i := \mathbf{w}_i / \|\mathbf{w}_i\|$, and then compute
\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
Hyperspherical energy captures pairwise relationships (the angles) among neurons on the unit hypersphere. The paper hypothesizes that preserving this pairwise angular structure is important for maintaining the pretrained model’s semantic capabilities when adapting to a new subject; this insight underpins the orthogonal finetuning approach introduced later.

Evaluating subject-driven generation requires measuring both subject fidelity and prompt fidelity. In the experiments reported in the paper, metrics such as DINO embeddings and CLIP image embeddings (for subject fidelity) and CLIP text-image similarity (for prompt fidelity), together with perceptual diversity metrics like LPIPS, are used to quantify different aspects of the finetuned models’ behavior.

# Controllable generation (e.g., ControlNet, T2I-Adapter) and overhead

Controllable generation refers to the family of tasks where the diffusion model is conditioned not only by text but also by an auxiliary control signal that prescribes structure or attributes the generated image should follow. Typical control signals considered in the paper include Canny edges, segmentation maps, and facial landmarks. The goal is for the model to respect both the text prompt and the image-structure constraint represented by the control input.

Recent works like ControlNet and T2I-Adapter take the pretrained diffusion model and augment or adapt it so that it accepts and respects such control signals. ControlNet, for example, finetunes the diffusion model to take additional control inputs and achieves impressive controllability in practice. Adapter-style approaches like T2I-Adapter insert small modules to inject control information. These methods successfully add controllability but often introduce additional parameters and architectural components. That extra capacity typically translates into added computation or memory during inference: the finetuned model now carries adapter modules or auxiliary networks that must be evaluated when generating images.

A notable practical distinction the paper emphasizes is that orthogonal finetuning (OFT) does not introduce inference-time overhead. OFT learns layer-shared orthogonal transformations that multiply into the pretrained weight matrices; at inference the finite set of learned orthogonal matrices can be multiplied into the original weights to produce an equivalent dense model. Thus, unlike some adapter-based solutions, OFT yields no extra modules to run at inference time.

Measuring the effectiveness of controllable generation requires task-appropriate metrics. The paper introduces a control consistency metric that estimates the control signal from the generated image and compares it to the original control input. Concretely, for Canny-edge-to-image (C2I) tasks the evaluation uses IoU and F1 between detected edges and the original; for segmentation-to-image (S2I) tasks the evaluation uses mean IoU and segmentation accuracy; for landmark-to-face (L2F) tasks the evaluation uses mean $\ell_2$ distance between target landmarks and landmarks predicted from generated images. These metrics quantify how well the generated image adheres to the control signal, complementing perceptual or semantic metrics used elsewhere.

Empirically, the paper reports that adapter-based approaches and ControlNet can be slower to converge and sometimes exhibit sudden or unstable training dynamics, whereas OFT and its constrained variant (COFT) tend to converge faster and more stably while achieving strong control adherence with fewer tunable parameters.

# Standard finetuning, adapter modules, and LoRA: strengths and limits

The landscape of finetuning large pretrained models includes several recurring strategies. The simplest approach is standard finetuning: adjust a subset (or all) of the pretrained weights using gradient descent with a small learning rate. The small learning rate is an implicit form of regularization that keeps the finetuned model near the pretrained checkpoint in Euclidean parameter space; in effect, standard finetuning often aims to keep $\|\mathbf{M} - \mathbf{M}^0\|$ small, where $\mathbf{M}^0$ denotes the pretrained weights. While effective in many applications, standard finetuning can be brittle in large generative models: small changes in parameter space do not always map to semantically small changes in the model’s generative behavior, and tuning hyperparameters such as number of iterations and learning rate becomes delicate.

Adapter-style methods were introduced to reduce the number of trainable parameters and to isolate task-specific changes. One influential adapter approach for transformer-style architectures is LoRA, which parameterizes the weight update as a low-rank additive term. Concretely, LoRA assumes the finetuning update has low rank and learns low-dimensional factors rather than updating the full weight matrix. The paper notes a simple parameter count formula for LoRA: with a low-rank parameter $r'$, the number of trainable parameters scales like $r'(d+n)$ for a weight matrix of size $d\times n$. The appeal of LoRA is parameter efficiency: by restricting updates to a low-rank subspace, it can adapt models with far fewer parameters than full finetuning, and it has been applied successfully to attention weights in Transformer-style modules.

Despite these strengths, adapter methods and low-rate standard finetuning do not offer structural guarantees about what internal geometric properties of the pretrained model they preserve. The paper argues that Euclidean proximity of weights to the pretrained checkpoint is an incomplete proxy for preserving the model’s semantic generation ability. To address this gap the authors introduce the idea of preserving hyperspherical energy, a measure of pairwise angular relations among neurons on the unit hypersphere. As formulated in the paper, the objective of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) can be interpreted as minimizing the difference in hyperspherical energy between the finetuned weights and the pretrained weights:
\[
\min_{\mathbf{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\mathbf{W})-\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)\right\|
\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad
\min_{\mathbf{W}} \bigg\| \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}
- \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_j\|^{-1}\bigg\|,
\]
where $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i := \mathbf{w}_i/\|\mathbf{w}_i\|$.

A mathematical observation underpins OFT: the pairwise angular relationships among vectors (neurons) are preserved if the whole set is transformed by a common orthogonal matrix. Therefore, OFT parametrizes the finetuned layer weights as a shared orthogonal transform applied to the pretrained weights. For a fully connected layer with pretrained weights $\mathbf{W}^0$, OFT replaces the usual forward computation
\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}
\]
with
\[
\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x},
\qquad\text{subject to}\quad \mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^\top=\mathbf{I},
\]
where $\mathbf{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix shared across the neurons in that layer. Initialized at the identity, learning $\mathbf{R}$ deforms the coordinate system of neurons while keeping pairwise angles invariant and thus preserves hyperspherical energy exactly.

Implementationally, maintaining orthogonality can be expensive if done naively. The paper adopts a Cayley parameterization to generate orthogonal matrices:
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\]
where $\mathbf{Q}$ is skew-symmetric ($\mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^\top$). Cayley parameterization produces orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$ (the special orthogonal group) and, in conjunction with a block-diagonal structure for $\mathbf{R}$, yields a practical balance between expressivity and parameter efficiency. Specifically, writing $\mathbf{R} = \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{R}_r)$ with each $\mathbf{R}_i\in \mathrm{O}\!\left(\tfrac{d}{r}\right)$ reduces the number of free parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ down to roughly $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$; sharing blocks further reduces cost to about $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$.

The paper compares parameter complexities qualitatively against LoRA. For a LoRA low-rank $r'$, the trainable parameter count is $r'(d+n)$. When both $r$ and $r'$ scale with $d$ (for example $r=r'=\alpha d$), the paper reports that LoRA’s complexity becomes $\mathcal{O}(d^2 + dn)$ while OFT’s becomes $\mathcal{O}(d)$ under block-sharing assumptions. As a concrete numerical illustration quoted in the paper, for a $128\times 128$ weight matrix with LoRA rank $r'=8$, LoRA has $2{,}048$ trainable parameters, whereas OFT with $r=8$ has $960$ trainable parameters.

Two practical extensions and variants are discussed in the paper. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) adds an explicit small-deviation constraint to keep the learned orthogonal transform close to the identity:
\[
\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon,
\]
which, via a first-order expansion of the Cayley transform ($\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2)$), can be enforced approximately by constraining $\|\mathbf{Q}\| \le \epsilon'$. COFT is intended to improve finetuning stability by making the trade-off between flexibility and preservation explicit. A second extension, re-scaled OFT, allows per-neuron magnitude scaling by including a learnable diagonal matrix $\mathbf{D} = \mathrm{diag}(s_1,\ldots,s_n)$ with positive entries, yielding the forward pass
\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0 \mathbf{D})^\top \mathbf{x}.
\]
Because hyperspherical energy depends only on normalized directions, multiplying by a diagonal magnitude matrix does not change the hyperspherical energy; the scaling increases expressive flexibility with little parameter overhead.

Strengths and limits. The OFT family combines two desirable properties: (1) it enforces a structural invariance (pairwise angles among neurons) that the authors argue is important for preserving pretrained semantic capabilities, and (2) it is parameter-efficient relative to many full-finetuning alternatives. Empirically in the paper, OFT and COFT converge faster and more stably than comparable adapter or fine-update methods and often achieve superior task metrics (both in subject-driven and controllable generation). Limitations noted by the authors include the computational cost of Cayley parameterization (it requires a matrix inverse; block-diagonalization mitigates but does not remove this cost), and the inductive bias introduced by block-diagonal structure (the parameter-efficiency gains come with modeling constraints that may limit flexibility in some regimes). The paper also points to open directions such as efficient scalable orthogonal parametrizations and investigating compositionality of orthogonal transforms across multiple downstream tasks.

# Concluding connections and practical takeaways

Putting the pieces together, a reader should take away a few practical lessons that the paper emphasizes. First, diffusion models provide a powerful generative backbone for text-to-image tasks, and engineering choices such as operating in latent space and how to inject conditioning information materially affect performance and cost. Second, personalization (subject-driven generation) and controllable generation are naturally framed as finetuning problems on top of a strong pretrained diffusion model; however, naive finetuning risks losing pretrained capabilities or being unstable. Third, adapter-style and low-rank update strategies like LoRA are popular because they reduce the number of trainable parameters, but they do not by themselves guarantee preservation of the internal geometric structure the pretrained model uses for semantic generation. Finally, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), together with COFT and re-scaled variants, offers a principled alternative that preserves pairwise neuronal angular relationships (hyperspherical energy) by construction, yields stable and often faster convergence in experiments reported in the paper, and—importantly—introduces no extra inference-time modules because the learned orthogonal transforms can be multiplied into the original weights.

For a student preparing to read the research literature in this area, it is useful to (1) recognize whether a paper trains diffusion in pixel or latent space, (2) note how conditioning is implemented and where adaptations are inserted (attention blocks are a common target), and (3) pay attention to both optimization/regularization choices and structural invariants (like hyperspherical energy) that methods claim to preserve. These perspectives will make it easier to compare methods such as DreamBooth, ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, LoRA, and the OFT family on a principled basis.

Mathematical Foundations: Angles, Hyperspheres, and Orthogonal Transforms

# Cosine similarity and neuron direction versus magnitude

At the core of many neural architectures is the inner product between an input vector and a neuron weight vector. For a neuron with weight $\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and an input $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$, the pre-activation is the inner product
\[
z = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}.
\]
This single scalar can be decomposed into a product of magnitudes and an angular term by the standard identity
\[
\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} = \|\mathbf{w}\| \, \|\mathbf{x}\| \, \cos\theta,
\qquad
\cos\theta := \frac{\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{w}\| \, \|\mathbf{x}\|},
\]
where $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf{w}$ and $\mathbf{x}$. The quantity $\cos\theta$ is commonly called cosine similarity. From this decomposition we immediately see that an inner product mixes two kinds of information: (1) the magnitudes $\|\mathbf{w}\|,\|\mathbf{x}\|$, and (2) the angle $\theta$ between the two vectors.

When we speak of the "direction" of a neuron we mean its normalized vector $\hat{\mathbf{w}} := \mathbf{w}/\|\mathbf{w}\|$. The cosine similarity between a neuron and an input can be written in terms of these normalized vectors as
\[
\cos\theta = \hat{\mathbf{w}}^\top \hat{\mathbf{x}}.
\]
If a network (or a layer) is constructed or trained so that magnitudes are normalized or largely irrelevant, then activations and comparisons reduce to cosine similarities between directions. Prior work (as discussed in the paper) documents that angular differences—comparisons of directions on the unit hypersphere—often encode semantic distinctions more robustly than raw Euclidean differences or un-normalized inner products. Intuitively, if a set of neurons act as detectors for different patterns, whether a particular detector responds strongly depends primarily on how closely aligned the input is to the detector's direction; a global rescaling of either vector changes absolute activation values but not which detectors are most aligned.

This observation motivates treating direction (angles) as the primary carrier of semantic information in neural representations. Practically, this is why methods that normalize features or weights, or otherwise emphasize cosine similarity, tend to reveal semantic structure and why we consider preserving directional relationships among neurons during finetuning to be important.

# Hyperspherical energy: definition and what it measures

To move from pairwise cosine similarity to a single scalar measure of the geometry of all neurons in a layer, the paper uses the hyperspherical energy. Let a layer be represented by its neuron weight matrix
\[
\mathbf{W} = [\mathbf{w}_1,\mathbf{w}_2,\dots,\mathbf{w}_n]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
\]
and write normalized (unit) neurons as $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i := \mathbf{w}_i/\|\mathbf{w}_i\|$. The hyperspherical energy (HE) of the layer is defined as
\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) \;:=\; \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
This definition sums the reciprocal of pairwise Euclidean distances between the unit vectors that represent neuron directions. It therefore penalizes pairs of neurons that are very close on the unit sphere: if two neurons become nearly collinear (small $\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|$), they contribute a large term to the energy.

We can rewrite the pairwise distance in terms of cosine similarity. For unit vectors,
\[
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^2
= \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i\|^2 + \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^2 - 2\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j
= 2(1-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j).
\]
Thus each summand in the hyperspherical energy is
\[
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}
= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j)}}.
\]
The energy therefore increases as $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j$ increases (i.e., as the angle between two neurons decreases). Low hyperspherical energy corresponds to neuron directions that are well spread across the sphere (large pairwise angles); high energy corresponds to clusters of neurons with similar directions.

Why is this a useful summary statistic? First, it captures a global notion of redundancy or uniformity of neuron directions in a layer: if many neurons point in nearly the same direction, the HE will be large. Second, works cited in the paper argue that certain distributions of neuron directions—those avoiding redundancy and achieving a kind of spread—tend to associate with better generalization. In the finetuning context studied in the paper, preserving the pretrained model's HE is hypothesized to help preserve its semantic generation ability: if finetuning alters the angular geometry of neurons too much, the pretrained relationships that encoded semantic mappings may be disrupted.

# Invariance of pairwise angles under a shared orthogonal transform

A key mathematical fact that makes orthogonal finetuning attractive is the behavior of angles and distances under orthogonal transformations. An orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ satisfies
\[
\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^\top = \mathbf{I},
\]
and geometrically represents a rotation and/or reflection in $\mathbb{R}^d$. Orthogonal transforms preserve inner products and norms. Concretely, for any vectors $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^d$,
\[
(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{a})^\top (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{b})
= \mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} \mathbf{b}
= \mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{b},
\]
and therefore
\[
\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{a}\| = \sqrt{(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{a})^\top(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{a})}
= \sqrt{\mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{a}} = \|\mathbf{a}\|.
\]

From these equalities we derive the preservation of pairwise angles and distances on the unit sphere. If $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j$ are unit neurons, then after applying the same orthogonal transform to every neuron in the layer,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\big(\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i}\big)^\top \big(\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j}\big)
&= (\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i)^\top (\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j)
= \hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j,
\\[4pt]
\|\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i} - \widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j}\|
&= \| \mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j \|
= \| \mathbf{R}(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j)\|
= \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|.
\end{aligned}
\]
Because both inner products and pairwise distances among normalized neurons are unchanged, the hyperspherical energy is also unchanged:
\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}) \;=\; \sum_{i\neq j} \|\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}}_i - \widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}
\;=\; \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}
\;=\; \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}).
\]

This invariance is the precise mathematical justification for the central OFT idea: if we finetune a layer by left-multiplying its weight matrix by a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ (so that the finetuned weights are $\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$), then the entire pairwise geometric structure of the neuron directions—angles and distances on the unit sphere—remains exactly the same as in the pretrained model. Geometrically, we have merely rotated (or reflected) the whole configuration of neuron directions; nothing about their relative arrangement changes.

Two small but important points follow from this fact. First, the invariance only holds if all neurons in the same layer undergo the same orthogonal transform. If different neurons were independently rotated, relative angles would change. Second, an orthogonal transform preserves norms, so if one wishes to allow per-neuron magnitude changes (for additional flexibility), one can still do so while keeping directions fixed; this is the rationale for the re-scaled variant (learn a diagonal scaling after the orthogonal transform)—directional geometry (and thus HE) remains unaffected by positive diagonal rescaling because normalized directions do not change.

# Why angular information captures semantics (Fourier phase analogy)

The paper motivates the focus on direction by analogy to a well-known fact in signal processing: a two-dimensional Fourier transform decomposes an image into complex frequency coefficients whose magnitudes and phases have distinct roles. The magnitude (amplitude) spectrum describes how much energy is present at each frequency, while the phase spectrum encodes the relative alignment of sinusoidal components, which directly controls where edges and structures appear in space. Empirically, keeping an image's phase spectrum and replacing its magnitude spectrum with random values often produces an image that preserves the original image's salient structure, whereas keeping the magnitude and randomizing the phase typically obliterates recognizable structure. In that sense, the phase carries much of the perceptual and semantic content.

Translating this intuition to neural representations, the neuron direction (the unit vector on the hypersphere) plays a role analogous to the phase: it tells us how activations align with the basis functions embodied by the neurons, and thus it carries semantic content (which detectors are activated and how they combine). The magnitude of a neuron (its norm) is analogous to the amplitude in the Fourier domain: it scales responses but does not alter which directions are aligned. This is the conceptual reason why rotating directions (changing phases) is an effective lever to change semantics, while simply changing magnitudes is less fundamental to structural content.

However, while changing directions can modify semantics, unrestricted changes of directions (arbitrary, independent rotations per neuron) give the finetuning procedure too much freedom and can destroy the pretrained model's learned structure. Orthogonal finetuning imposes a strong constraint: all neurons are rotated together via a single orthogonal matrix per layer. This preserves the pairwise angular geometry (the "phase relationships" among neurons) while still permitting a coordinated change of basis that can steer model outputs toward the downstream task. Thus orthogonal updates tap into the semantically relevant degrees of freedom (directional changes) while limiting the destructive degrees of freedom (arbitrary per-neuron deviations).

# Toy evidence: angle-only activations reconstruct semantics better than magnitude

The paper presents a compact empirical demonstration that supports the preceding geometric intuition. In a toy experiment, a standard convolutional autoencoder is trained on a dataset of flower images using the usual inner-product based feature computation (that is, during training the encoder produces feature maps via standard convolutional inner products). At test time, with the learned convolutional kernels held fixed, three alternative ways to form the feature maps are compared:

1. Use the original inner products (the same computation used during training).
2. Use only the magnitude information extracted from the weights and inputs (i.e., discard angular detail).
3. Use only the angular information (essentially use normalized weights and inputs and therefore cosine similarities).

Even though the network was trained with inner products, the experiment finds that reconstructing from angle-only features nearly recovers the original images, whereas reconstructing from magnitude-only features produces little usable semantic content. Crucially, the model was not trained with a cosine activation; it was trained with inner products, and the success of angle-only reconstruction demonstrates that the direction of the learned kernels encodes most of the semantic content the autoencoder captured.

This toy result aligns with the Fourier-phase intuition: the directions of convolutional kernels (their orientations and relative phases) capture structures like edges and shapes that are essential for image semantics. The magnitude information—overall energy—contributes little to the identity of those structures.

Taken together, these mathematical facts and empirical observations motivate the Orthogonal Finetuning approach: finetune by learning a layer-shared orthogonal transform so that neuron directions (and their pairwise relations measured by hyperspherical energy) are preserved, while still allowing coordinated directional adjustments that steer generation toward the downstream objective. Preserving hyperspherical energy is thus not an arbitrary constraint but a principled way to keep the semantic geometry learned at pretraining intact while giving the model controlled flexibility to adapt.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Core Formulation

# Objective: minimize change in hyperspherical energy between pretrained and finetuned layers

The central objective of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is to adapt a pretrained layer to a downstream task while preserving the pairwise geometric relationships among that layer's neurons. These pairwise relationships are captured by the hyperspherical energy (HE), a scalar summary of how neurons (weight vectors) are arranged on the unit hypersphere. Concretely, for a weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ (where each column $\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is a neuron), we define the normalized neuron $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$ and the hyperspherical energy
$HE(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.$
HE depends only on the pairwise directions (angles) between neurons, not on their magnitudes.

OFT formulates finetuning as minimizing the change in hyperspherical energy relative to the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$. The optimization target is therefore
$\min_{\bm{W}} \big\| HE(\bm{W}) - HE(\bm{W}^0)\big\|.$
Because HE is completely determined by the angular configuration of the normalized neurons, preserving HE constrains finetuning to keep the pretrained layer's angular (relational) structure intact. The paper shows that the attainable minimum of this objective is zero and that this minimum can be achieved whenever the finetuned weights differ from the pretrained weights only by a rotation or reflection applied to all neurons simultaneously. This observation motivates restricting the finetuned layer to be an orthogonally transformed version of the pretrained layer.

Preserving HE is motivated by the empirical and conceptual observation that angular relationships among neurons encode semantic structure. By minimizing HE change (or making it zero), OFT aims to retain the pretrained layer's semantic generation ability while allowing controlled adaptation to new tasks.

# Forward pass transformation: W' = R · W0 with R orthogonal

OFT implements the HE-preservation objective by parameterizing the finetuned weight matrix $\bm{W}$ as a left multiplication of the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ by a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. The forward pass of a layer that originally computed $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ becomes
$\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},$
subject to the orthogonality constraint
$\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{R}\,\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}.$
Because $\bm{R}$ multiplies on the left, it rotates/reflects each column (neuron) of $\bm{W}^0$ by the same orthogonal transformation.

Two immediate algebraic consequences are important. First, orthogonality preserves Euclidean norms: for any vector $\bm{v}$ we have $\|\bm{R}\bm{v}\| = \|\bm{v}\|$. Second, applying $\bm{R}$ to normalized neurons gives $\widehat{(\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0)} = \bm{R}\,\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0$. Thus pairwise distances among normalized neurons are preserved:
$\|\widehat{(\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0)} - \widehat{(\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0)}\|
= \|\bm{R}(\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0 - \hat{\bm{w}}_j^0)\|
= \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0 - \hat{\bm{w}}_j^0\|,$
and therefore $HE(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0) = HE(\bm{W}^0)$ exactly. By restricting finetuning to the family $\{\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 : \bm{R}\in\text{O}(d)\}$, OFT enforces zero change in HE by construction.

Learning proceeds by optimizing the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ (one per layer, shared across neurons in that layer) with respect to the downstream task loss. Because only $\bm{R}$ is updated, the number of additional trainable parameters and the degrees of freedom are controlled relative to unconstrained finetuning.

# Identity initialization for zero-start finetuning

A critical practical design in OFT is identity initialization: initialize the orthogonal transform to the identity matrix, $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, before any finetuning steps. With this initialization the finetuned weights start exactly equal to the pretrained weights, since $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{W}^0$ at the start. Identity initialization therefore implements a "zero-start" finetuning: the model's initial behavior is identical to the pretrained model and any change emerges gradually through updates to $\bm{R}$.

The identity initialization also simplifies and stabilizes optimization: because the initial point already satisfies the HE-preservation objective and reproduces pretrained behavior, finetuning only needs to introduce the minimal orthogonal deviations required by the downstream loss. In the paper, orthogonality is enforced during training by using a differentiable parameterization of orthogonal matrices (for example, the Cayley transform from a skew-symmetric generator $\bm{Q}$: $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$). With such a parameterization, setting the generator $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ yields $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, so identity initialization is straightforward to implement.

By combining identity initialization with an orthogonality constraint, OFT achieves a principled, well-conditioned starting point that reduces sensitivity to finetuning hyperparameters and helps avoid catastrophic drift from the pretrained solution.

# View as adjusting the neuron coordinate system while preserving pairwise angles

One useful conceptual interpretation of OFT is that it adjusts the coordinate system (basis) in which neurons are expressed, rather than independently changing each neuron. Multiplying the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ on the left by an orthogonal $\bm{R}$ is equivalent to rotating the $d$-dimensional basis used to represent all neurons in that layer. Because the same orthogonal transformation is applied to every neuron, all pairwise angles among neurons are preserved; the neuron configuration is re-expressed in a different orthonormal coordinate frame rather than arbitrarily deformed.

This coordinate-change view is made precise by the preservation of cosine similarities: for any two vectors $\bm{u},\bm{v}$,
$\cos(\angle(\bm{R}\bm{u},\bm{R}\bm{v})) = \frac{(\bm{R}\bm{u})^\top(\bm{R}\bm{v})}{\|\bm{R}\bm{u}\|\,\|\bm{R}\bm{v}\|}
= \frac{\bm{u}^\top\bm{v}}{\|\bm{u}\|\,\|\bm{v}\|} = \cos(\angle(\bm{u},\bm{v})).$
Because angular (directional) relationships are widely observed to carry semantic information in feature representations, preserving these angles retains the pretrained semantic structure while allowing controlled adaptation via rotation.

Intuitively, OFT therefore performs a constrained reparameterization: it keeps the relational geometry (angles and hence HE) intact and only changes how that geometry is aligned with the downstream task. This viewpoint helps explain why OFT can modify semantic outputs (by rotating the basis) yet avoid destroying the pretrained model's generalization behavior.

# Equivalence to a structured weight update: ΔW = (R − I) · W0

Although OFT is implemented multiplicatively as $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0$, it admits an additive interpretation that clarifies its relationship to other finetuning schemes. The finetuned weight can be written as
$\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \Delta\bm{W},\qquad \Delta\bm{W} = (\bm{R}-\bm{I})\,\bm{W}^0.$
Thus OFT is equivalent to adding a structured update $\Delta\bm{W}$ to the pretrained weights, where the additive change is constrained to lie in the column space spanned by $\bm{W}^0$ and is parameterized through $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})$.

This structured form contrasts with an unconstrained additive update used in standard finetuning and with the explicitly low-rank additive parameterization used by methods such as LoRA. LoRA posits $\Delta\bm{W} = \bm{A}\bm{B}$ with small rank, while OFT's $\Delta\bm{W}$ depends on the pretrained $\bm{W}^0$ and the orthogonal generator $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})$. In practice, OFT can also be parameter-efficient: one can parameterize $\bm{R}$ as a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix (shared or not across blocks) so that $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})$ has a sparse structured form. When $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ has low effective rank or block structure, the number of free parameters is small compared to full unconstrained finetuning.

Because $\Delta\bm{W}$ is coupled to $\bm{W}^0$, OFT restricts the finetuning trajectory to a subspace that preserves hyperspherical geometry. This constraint is the key to OFT's stability: the allowed updates are expressive enough to adapt directionality of neurons (where semantic change matters) but constrained so they cannot arbitrarily break the pretrained angular configuration.

# Where to apply OFT in diffusion models (e.g., attention weights)

OFT is fundamentally model-agnostic: any layer whose weights are arranged as a matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ can be finetuned by learning a left-multiplicative orthogonal transform $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. In practice, to make fair comparisons with other parameter-efficient finetuning methods, OFT is often applied to the same locations where competing methods operate. For transformer-style components that appear in many diffusion models (for example, cross-attention and self-attention projection matrices), prior parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA typically modify the attention weight matrices. The paper therefore applies OFT to the attention weights in experiments to ensure a fair comparison.

Beyond fully connected or attention projection matrices, OFT can be used for convolutional layers as well. The block-diagonal parameterization of $\bm{R}$ has a natural interpretation in convolutional settings: if the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, each block acts on a single channel and resembles learning a depthwise transform; if blocks are shared across channels, the same orthogonal rotation is applied channel-wise. This flexibility makes OFT suitable for finetuning a variety of layer types encountered inside diffusion models (U-Net backbones, attention modules, etc.).

A practical operational advantage is that OFT induces no inference-time overhead: after finetuning, the learned orthogonal transform can be folded into the pretrained weights to produce $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, and inference proceeds with the resulting $\bm{W}$ exactly as with any standard layer. This folding means that deploying an OFT-finetuned diffusion model does not require extra runtime computation beyond the original pretrained architecture.

Concluding summary

OFT implements a principled finetuning constraint: learn a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ so that the finetuned weights equal $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, which guarantees exact preservation of hyperspherical energy $HE(\cdot)$. Identity initialization gives a zero-start from the pretrained model, and the multiplicative formulation is equivalent to a structured additive update $\Delta\bm{W}=(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$. OFT is architecture-agnostic (commonly applied to attention weights for fair comparison with LoRA), parameter-efficient via block structure, and introduces no extra inference cost because the learned transform can be folded into the original weights. These properties together make OFT a conceptually simple and provably HE-preserving approach for controlled finetuning of large text-to-image diffusion models.

Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization and Complexity

# Why naive orthogonalization (e.g., Gram–Schmidt) is too costly
A direct way to enforce orthogonality during finetuning is to orthonormalize the columns (or rows) of a weight matrix after each update, for example by Gram–Schmidt or related QR-based procedures. While these procedures are differentiable and mathematically straightforward, they become impractical inside large-scale finetuning loops: they require repeated orthonormalization across all dimensions of a large matrix at every update, which is computationally heavy and memory intensive for the high-dimensional layers used in modern diffusion and Transformer models. The paper therefore treats such "naive" orthogonalization as too expensive to compute in practice and looks for parameterizations that (a) are guaranteed to produce orthogonal matrices, (b) are differentiable, and (c) are far cheaper to optimize than full orthonormalization at every step.

# Cayley transform: R = (I + Q)(I − Q)^{-1} with skew-symmetric Q
A convenient and efficient parameterization used in the paper is the Cayley transform. Concretely, one constructs an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ from a skew-symmetric matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ via
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\qquad\text{with}\qquad
\mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^\top.
\]
Because $\mathbf{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, the Cayley formula yields an orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ by construction. An immediate practical advantage is that we can parametrize $\mathbf{R}$ by the free entries of $\mathbf{Q}$ (the independent entries of a skew-symmetric matrix) and backpropagate through the closed-form expression for $\mathbf{R}$. Initialization is also simple and safe: setting $\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{0}$ gives $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$, so the finetuned model starts exactly from the pretrained weights (important for stable finetuning).

The Cayley transform replaces iterative orthonormalization with a single algebraic mapping from $\mathbf{Q}$ to $\mathbf{R}$. The mapping does require a matrix inverse $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}$, but it avoids the repeated global orthonormalization operations that make naive methods costly; thus the Cayley approach is a practical compromise between exact orthogonality and computational efficiency.

# Consequences of special orthogonal group (determinant +1) and practical impact
A small mathematical restriction of the Cayley parameterization is that it only produces matrices with determinant $+1$, i.e., matrices in the special orthogonal group $\mathrm{SO}(d)$. Orthogonal matrices in general have determinant $\pm 1$; determinant $+1$ corresponds to pure rotations, while determinant $-1$ includes reflections. The Cayley map therefore excludes reflections and can only represent rotations.

Practically, the paper reports that this restriction is not harmful: limiting $\mathbf{R}$ to $\mathrm{SO}(d)$ does not degrade finetuning performance in their experiments. Intuitively, the space of rotations is already rich enough to capture the useful angular re-parameterizations of neurons that OFT relies on, and the excluded reflections do not appear necessary for the downstream control tasks considered.

# Block-diagonal R with r blocks: parameter and compute savings
A full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix has many degrees of freedom: the number of independent parameters is
\[
\frac{d(d-1)}{2},
\]
so storing and optimizing a full Cayley parameter $\mathbf{Q}$ scales as $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. To reduce this cost while preserving orthogonality, the paper adopts a block-diagonal structure for $\mathbf{R}$. Specifically,
\[
\mathbf{R} = \mathrm{diag}\!\big(\mathbf{R}_1,\mathbf{R}_2,\dots,\mathbf{R}_r\big),
\qquad\text{with}\qquad
\mathbf{R}_i \in \mathrm{O}\!\left(\frac{d}{r}\right)\ \text{for }i=1,\dots,r.
\]
When $r=1$ this reduces to the full orthogonal matrix; as $r$ increases the blocks get smaller and the total number of free parameters drops. For an $r$-block diagonal orthogonal matrix the parameter count becomes
\[
\frac{d\big(\frac{d}{r}-1\big)}{2},
\]
so the parameter complexity is $\mathcal{O}\!\big(\tfrac{d^2}{r}\big)$. In other words, partitioning the $d$ dimensions into $r$ independent blocks reduces the quadratic cost by a factor of about $r$.

Beyond parameter count, the block-diagonal structure also reduces the dimension of each matrix inverse in the Cayley transform from $d\times d$ to $(d/r)\times(d/r)$ blocks. This mitigates the practical cost of the $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}$ operation, making the Cayley approach more scalable when block sizes are modest.

# Optional block sharing across channels or heads
The block-diagonal factorization can be tightened further by sharing blocks. One option is to set $\mathbf{R}_i=\mathbf{R}_j$ for all block indices $i\neq j$, which enforces the same orthogonal transform across blocks. Sharing reduces the number of unique parameters even more: with shared blocks the effective parameter complexity falls to roughly $\mathcal{O}\!\big(\tfrac{d^2}{r^2}\big)$ as noted in the paper.

This shared-block design has simple interpretations in common layer types. For convolutional layers, choosing the number of blocks equal to the number of input channels makes each block operate on a single channel, analogous to depthwise processing; sharing all blocks corresponds to applying the same orthogonal transform across channels. For Transformer attention matrices one can likewise apply block partitions aligned with heads or feature splits. The choice—independent blocks, shared blocks, or something in between—gives an explicit trade-off between flexibility (more independent blocks) and parameter efficiency / regularization (more sharing).

# Parameter complexity versus LoRA and concrete 128×128 example
It is useful to compare OFT's parameter cost to the widely-used LoRA finetuning scheme. LoRA models an additive low-rank update to a weight matrix and has $r'(d+n)$ trainable parameters for a $d\times n$ weight matrix, where $r'$ is the LoRA rank. OFT's block-diagonal orthogonal parameterization uses roughly $\dfrac{d(\frac{d}{r}-1)}{2}$ parameters per layer (with the precise constant depending on block sharing choices).

Two concrete comparisons given in the paper make the trade-offs clear:

- If both the OFT block count and LoRA rank scale linearly with $d$, say $r=r'=\alpha d$ for some constant $0<\alpha\le 1$, then LoRA's parameter cost behaves like
  \[
  r'(d+n)=\alpha d (d+n) = \mathcal{O}(d^2 + d n),
  \]
  while OFT's block-diagonal parameter count behaves like
  \[
  \frac{d\big(\frac{d}{r}-1\big)}{2}=\frac{d\big(\frac{1}{\alpha}-1\big)}{2}=\mathcal{O}(d).
  \]
  Thus under this scaling OFT can be far more parameter-efficient.

- For a concrete toy example, consider a weight matrix of size $128\times 128$ and choose $r'=8$ for LoRA and $r=8$ blocks for OFT. LoRA then has
  \[
  r' (d+n) = 8\,(128+128) = 2{,}048
  \]
  trainable parameters, while OFT's block-diagonal orthogonal parameterization has
  \[
  \frac{d\big(\frac{d}{r}-1\big)}{2} = \frac{128\,(16-1)}{2} = 960
  \]
  trainable parameters. This example illustrates that OFT can obtain a smaller number of tunable parameters than LoRA for comparable configuration choices.

Finally, it is worth recalling the conceptual distinction in how the two methods economize on parameters. LoRA enforces a low-rank additive update, i.e., it constrains the change to lie in a low-dimensional linear subspace. OFT instead applies a structured multiplicative change via a (block-)orthogonal transformation, exploiting sparsity and block structure to control parameter count. Both strategies reduce the degrees of freedom compared to dense finetuning, but they do so along different axes of the update space.

Practical remark: once the orthogonal matrices $\mathbf{R}$ are learned, they can be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrices offline so that there is no extra computational overhead at inference time—the resulting weight $\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ can be used directly. The Cayley inverse and any block computations are only needed during finetuning; block-diagonalization helps exactly because it reduces those per-step costs.

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)

# Adding a constraint on deviation from identity: $\|R - I\| \le \varepsilon$

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) augments Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) by explicitly limiting how far the learned orthogonal transform $R$ may move away from the identity matrix. The COFT forward pass for a pretrained layer with weights $W^0$ is written as
\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\qquad\text{subject to}\qquad
\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}, \quad \|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \le \varepsilon.
\]
The orthogonality constraint $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$ preserves pairwise angles among neurons and therefore preserves hyperspherical energy; the new deviation constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\varepsilon$ explicitly bounds how much the finetuned model can drift from the pretrained weights in an angular sense. Intuitively, this radius constraint makes the finetuning problem less prone to catastrophic drift: it restricts the feasible orthogonal transforms to a controlled neighborhood around the identity so the model cannot arbitrarily rotate neuron directions away from the pretrained configuration.

Putting the radius constraint directly into the model makes an otherwise implicit regularization (small learning rates or tiny additive updates) explicit. Practically, COFT therefore gives a clear knob, $\varepsilon$, to trade off preservation of pretrained generative behavior against the ability to adapt to a downstream task. When $\varepsilon$ is very small, COFT enforces only tiny orthogonal deviations and the finetuned model remains close to the pretrained generator; when $\varepsilon$ is large, the method relaxes to OFT and gains more flexibility.

# Relating the constraint to Cayley parameters via Neumann series

COFT uses a computationally convenient parameterization of orthogonal matrices via the Cayley transform. Let $\bm{Q}$ be a skew-symmetric matrix ($\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$). The Cayley parameterization builds an orthogonal matrix as
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q}) (\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]
This construction guarantees orthogonality (and in fact produces matrices in the special orthogonal group with determinant $+1$). Because $\bm{R}$ is expressed through $\bm{Q}$, the radius constraint on $\bm{R}$ can be converted to a constraint on $\bm{Q}$, which is easier to enforce during optimization.

To make that conversion explicit we use the Neumann series expansion of $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$. Under the standard convergence assumption (convergence in operator norm), if $\|\bm{Q}\| < 1$ then
\[
(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \bm{Q}^k.
\]
Plugging this into the Cayley form gives
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})\sum_{k=0}^\infty \bm{Q}^k
= \bm{I} + 2\sum_{k=1}^\infty \bm{Q}^k
= \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2).
\]
Hence for sufficiently small $\bm{Q}$ (so higher-order terms are negligible) we have the first-order approximation
\[
\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q},
\qquad\text{and therefore}\qquad
\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \approx 2\|\bm{Q}\|.
\]
This shows that bounding $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\varepsilon$ is approximately equivalent to bounding $\|\bm{Q}\|\le\varepsilon'$, where $\varepsilon'$ is on the order of $\varepsilon/2$ up to higher-order corrections. The paper therefore treats the radius constraint as an equivalent constraint on the Cayley parameter $\bm{Q}$:
\[
\|\bm{Q}\| \le \varepsilon',
\]
and uses $\varepsilon'$ as the practical hyperparameter during optimization. Because the Neumann-series argument depends on small $\bm{Q}$, this mapping is an approximation; in practice $\varepsilon'$ is treated as an independent tuning parameter that controls the allowed deviation in $\bm{Q}$ space.

# Projected gradient descent on $\bm{Q}$ to enforce $\|\bm{Q}\| \le \varepsilon'$

Once the orthogonal matrix is parameterized by a skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$, the radius constraint can be enforced by simple constrained optimization on $\bm{Q}$. The paper proposes projected gradient descent as an effective way to maintain $\|\bm{Q}\|\le\varepsilon'$ throughout learning. The high-level training loop for COFT is:

1. Parameterize $\bm{R}$ via Cayley: $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{Q}^\top=-\bm{Q}$.
2. Initialize $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ so that $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ and the finetuned model starts exactly at the pretrained weights.
3. On each optimization step compute gradients of the finetuning loss with respect to $\bm{Q}$, take a gradient-descent update, and then project the updated $\bm{Q}$ back into the feasible set $\{\bm{Q}:\|\bm{Q}\|\le\varepsilon'\}$.
4. Recompute $\bm{R}$ from the projected $\bm{Q}$ for the next forward/backward pass.

Projected gradient descent enforces the deviation constraint by clipping the parameter back into the allowed ball after each unconstrained gradient step. The projection operation is conceptually simple: after an update step you replace $\bm{Q}$ by the closest matrix (under the chosen matrix norm) that satisfies $\|\bm{Q}\|\le\varepsilon'$. Because $\bm{Q}$ is constrained to be skew-symmetric by construction, the projected updates are typically implemented so as to maintain that skew-symmetry (for example by parameterizing only the independent skew-symmetric degrees of freedom or re-symmetrizing as part of the projection).

The paper treats the projection step as straightforward to implement and computationally light compared to the overall finetuning. Enforcing the constraint directly on $\bm{Q}$ (rather than trying to project $\bm{R}$ itself) is convenient because $\bm{Q}$ lives in an unconstrained linear space (skew-symmetric matrices) and the Cayley transform maps it to the nonlinear orthogonal manifold.

# Effect of $\varepsilon$: flexibility–stability trade-off and practical tuning

The radius hyperparameter $\varepsilon$ (and its Cayley-space counterpart $\varepsilon'$) governs a clear flexibility–stability trade-off:

- When $\varepsilon$ is very small, COFT tightly restricts $\bm{R}$ to remain near the identity. In this regime the finetuned model changes little from the pretrained generator: hyperspherical structure is preserved and the pretrained generalization behavior is largely maintained. This setting is useful when the downstream task requires only modest, conservative adaptations and when one wants to avoid risking collapse or loss of pretrained capabilities.

- When $\varepsilon$ is large, the radius constraint becomes loose and COFT approaches OFT. This gives more representational freedom and can improve task adaptation but reduces the explicit protection against drifting too far from the pretrained solution.

The paper reports empirical behavior consistent with this intuition: "With larger $\varepsilon$, the COFT-finetuned model resembles the OFT-finetuned model. With smaller $\varepsilon$, the COFT-finetuned model behaves increasingly similar to the pretrained text-to-image diffusion model." In practice, COFT makes hyperparameter choices easier because an appropriate $\varepsilon$ reduces sensitivity to learning rate and to the number of finetuning iterations. The authors note that with a proper $\varepsilon$, both the learning rate and the iteration number become "effortless to set"—COFT allows one to pick a reasonably large iteration budget without risk of catastrophic model collapse that can afflict unconstrained finetuning.

As a practical tuning guideline (consistent with the paper's findings): treat $\varepsilon$ as the primary control for the allowable degree of model adaptation. Start with a conservative (small) value to ensure preservation of pretrained behavior; increase $\varepsilon$ only if the model is failing to adapt adequately to the downstream control signal. Because the Cayley-to-$R$ mapping is nonlinear, the mapping between $\varepsilon$ and task performance is not strictly linear; the paper therefore treats $\varepsilon'$ as an independent hyperparameter to be validated empirically.

# Initialization and stability benefits observed empirically

A key implementation detail and stability advantage of COFT is its identity initialization. Setting $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ at the start yields $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ and therefore recovers exactly the pretrained weights at initialization. This zero-start property gives two practical benefits. First, it guarantees that the finetuning trajectory begins from a known good generator; second, because the radius constraint restricts how far $\bm{R}$ can move, the training dynamics remain stable and predictable as the model adapts.

The empirical results in the paper demonstrate these stability benefits. In subject-driven generation experiments, COFT and OFT converge stably and preserve subject identity while baselines such as LoRA and standard finetuning can either fail to maintain identity or collapse after many iterations. The authors report that after a moderate number of iterations (e.g., a few hundred) OFT and COFT already achieve good control, while standard methods sometimes collapse if training is continued. In controllable generation tasks, COFT also shows faster and more stable convergence than adapter-based baselines: for example, OFT/COFT reach strong control at far fewer epochs than LoRA, and COFT's explicit radius constraint further reduces sensitivity to training schedule and hyperparameter settings.

Another practical advantage is that COFT introduces no additional inference overhead. Once training is finished, the learned orthogonal transform can be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix to produce an equivalent finetuned weight matrix $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$; the resulting model is identical in runtime cost to the original pretrained model. Finally, all parameter-efficiency strategies used for OFT (Cayley parameterization, block-diagonal orthogonal matrices, optional block sharing) are compatible with COFT, so the stability benefits of the radius constraint come at little or no extra parameter or inference cost.

Summary

COFT makes the OFT idea more robust by combining the angle-preserving power of orthogonal transforms with an explicit radius constraint around the identity. The Cayley parameterization gives a practical way to enforce orthogonality, the Neumann-series expansion links the radius on $\bm{R}$ to an approximate ball in Cayley-space for $\bm{Q}$, and projected gradient descent on $\bm{Q}$ enforces the constraint during optimization. Empirically, COFT stabilizes finetuning, reduces sensitivity to iteration count and learning rate, and preserves pretrained generative ability while still enabling effective adaptation for subject-driven and controllable image generation tasks.

Re-scaled OFT and Method Variants

# Adding a positive diagonal scaling D on neuron outputs

Re-scaled OFT extends the basic Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) idea by giving each output neuron an independent magnitude (scale) in addition to the shared orthogonal rotation applied to neuron directions. Concretely, for a pretrained fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ (columns $\bm{w}^0_i$ are the pretrained neurons), re-scaled OFT replaces the usual OFT forward pass
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}
\]
by
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x},
\]
where $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is the layer-shared orthogonal matrix learned by OFT and $\bm{D}=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is a learnable diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries $s_i$ are strictly positive. Each $s_i$ rescales the $i$-th column of $\bm{W}^0$ (after rotation by $\bm{R}$), so re-scaled OFT modifies both angle (via $\bm{R}$) and magnitude (via $\bm{D}$) of neurons.

Two practical points are important to keep in mind. First, to preserve the exact pretrained behavior at the start of finetuning, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is initialized as the identity and the diagonal scaling is typically initialized as $\bm{D}=\bm{I}$ (so the finetuned model starts from $\bm{W}^0$). Second, the constraint $s_i>0$ is enforced conceptually so that scaling does not introduce sign flips per neuron; the paper explicitly assumes the diagonal entries are larger than zero.

# Why scaling does not affect hyperspherical energy

The central analytic observation behind OFT is that hyperspherical energy (HE) depends only on the directions of neurons, not their magnitudes. Re-scaled OFT preserves this key invariance: adding a positive diagonal scaling $\bm{D}$ does not change the hyperspherical energy of the layer. We show this formally.

Recall the hyperspherical energy of a weight matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ is defined using normalized columns $\hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ as
\[
\operatorname{HE}(\bm{W}) \;:=\; \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
Under re-scaled OFT we have $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$. Let $\bm{w}_i^0$ denote the $i$-th column of $\bm{W}^0$ and $s_i$ the corresponding diagonal element of $\bm{D}$. Then the $i$-th column of $\bm{W}$ is
\[
\bm{w}_i \;=\; \bm{R}\,\bm{w}_i^0\,s_i.
\]
Normalize this column:
\[
\hat{\bm{w}}_i \;=\; \frac{\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{w}_i\|} \;=\; \frac{\bm{R}\,\bm{w}_i^0\,s_i}{\|\bm{R}\,\bm{w}_i^0\,s_i\|}.
\]
Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal and $s_i>0$, the norm in the denominator factors as $\|\bm{R}\,\bm{w}_i^0\,s_i\| = s_i\|\bm{w}_i^0\|$, so
\[
\hat{\bm{w}}_i \;=\; \bm{R}\,\frac{\bm{w}_i^0}{\|\bm{w}_i^0\|} \;=\; \bm{R}\,\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0.
\]
Thus every normalized neuron after re-scaling is just the orthogonal transform of the corresponding normalized pretrained neuron. Using orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ (which preserves Euclidean norms), pairwise normalized distances satisfy
\[
\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|
= \|\bm{R}\,\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0 - \bm{R}\,\hat{\bm{w}}_j^0\|
= \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0 - \hat{\bm{w}}_j^0\|.
\]
Plugging this into the HE definition yields
\[
\operatorname{HE}(\bm{W}) \;=\; \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}
\;=\; \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0 - \hat{\bm{w}}_j^0\|^{-1}
\;=\; \operatorname{HE}(\bm{W}^0).
\]
Therefore the diagonal scaling $\bm{D}$ (so long as its entries are positive) does not change hyperspherical energy: per-neuron magnitudes are normalized away and the shared orthogonal rotation preserves the pairwise geometry on the unit hypersphere.

This invariance is the reason re-scaled OFT retains the same structural guarantee of OFT: re-scaled OFT can increase flexibility by adjusting magnitudes without violating the hyperspherical-energy preservation that the OFT framework enforces.

# Forward pass with both R and D, and parameter overhead

The re-scaled OFT forward pass for a layer is
\[
\bm{z} \;=\; (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D})^\top \bm{x},
\]
with learnable $\bm{R}$ (orthogonal, layer-shared) and learnable diagonal $\bm{D}$. In comparison to plain OFT, re-scaled OFT adds only the diagonal degrees of freedom in $\bm{D}$. From a parameter-count perspective this is very light: $\bm{D}$ contains $n$ scalar parameters (one per output neuron or output channel), which is typically negligible compared to the parameters used to represent an orthogonal $\bm{R}$ or the original dense matrix $\bm{W}^0$.

To recall the cost of representing $\bm{R}$: a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix can be parameterized through a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ of size $d\times d$ (Cayley parameterization), and the free parameters are on the order of $d(d-1)/2$, i.e. $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. To save parameters, the paper uses a block-diagonal orthogonal parameterization
\[
\bm{R} = \operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r),
\qquad \bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{(d/r)\times(d/r)},
\]
whose parameter count is
\[
r\cdot \frac{(d/r)((d/r)-1)}{2} \;=\; \frac{d(d/r - 1)}{2},
\]
i.e. $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. The block sharing trick (making all $\bm{R}_i$ identical) can further reduce parameter complexity toward $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. Against that background, the $n$ extra scalars in $\bm{D}$ are small: re-scaled OFT has essentially the OFT parameter cost plus $n$ for the diagonal.

To make this concrete with the example used in the paper: for a $128\times 128$ weight matrix with block parameter $r=8$, the OFT implementation given in the paper uses about $960$ trainable parameters; adding a diagonal $\bm{D}$ of length $n=128$ raises the total to $960+128=1088$ parameters. The paper calls this additional overhead negligible relative to other adapter-style methods (for instance LoRA in the example uses $2048$ trainable parameters with $r'=8$). Thus re-scaled OFT achieves extra modeling flexibility at very small parameter cost.

# When re-scaled OFT helps and interplay with COFT

Why add diagonal scaling at all if orthogonal transforms already preserve hyperspherical energy and capture directional change? The paper frames re-scaled OFT as a lightweight way to give the finetuning procedure slightly more expressive power while keeping the hyperspherical-energy invariance intact. Intuitively, $\bm{R}$ changes neuron directions (angles) — which, as argued in the main OFT discussion, are the primary carriers of semantic structure — while $\bm{D}$ lets the finetuner adjust per-neuron amplitudes to better match downstream targets, without disturbing the pairwise angular geometry that OFT preserves.

Empirically (as reported in the paper), the authors kept most main experiments on the simpler OFT to highlight the role of orthogonal transforms, but they observed that re-scaled OFT is generally better (see the paper's appendix). That matches the conceptual picture: adding a small number of per-neuron magnitudes typically helps optimization and fitting on downstream tasks while preserving the hyperspherical structure that the OFT principle enforces.

Regarding interaction with Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT): COFT augments OFT with an explicit deviation constraint that keeps the learned orthogonal transform near the identity (formally $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq\epsilon$, implemented via a constraint on the skew-symmetric Cayley parameter $\bm{Q}$). Because $\bm{D}$ does not alter hyperspherical energy, COFT's HE-preserving guarantee remains valid when $\bm{D}$ is added. However, COFT was introduced to improve finetuning stability by explicitly limiting deviation from the pretrained weights; re-scaled OFT does change the Euclidean relation between $\bm{W}$ and $\bm{W}^0$ (since the magnitudes are modified), so practitioners who want to control Euclidean closeness as well as angular structure may consider whether to (a) keep the COFT constraint on $\bm{R}$ alone or (b) also constrain $\bm{D}$ (for example, to keep $\bm{D}$ near $\bm{I}$). The paper reports COFT as a practical stability-improving variant; combining it with diagonal scaling is conceptually straightforward and leaves hyperspherical-energy invariance intact, but the main experimental comparisons in the paper focus on the original OFT/COFT choices (the paper notes the re-scaled variant is generally better and provides results in the appendix).

# Implementation note and errata clarification

Two practical implementation notes from the paper are worth calling out. First, the correct re-scaled OFT forward pass used in the authors' implementation and experiments is
\[
\bm{z} \;=\; (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D})^\top \bm{x},
\]
i.e. the diagonal scaling $\bm{D}$ multiplies the pretrained weights on the right, after which the orthogonal rotation $\bm{R}$ is applied on the left. The paper includes an explicit erratum: in the NeurIPS camera-ready version the forward pass for re-scaled OFT was mistakenly printed as $(\bm{D}\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$; the authors clarify that the original implementation used $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$ and the reported experimental results are based on that correct implementation, so the results and appendix are unaffected by the typo.

Second, and related, re-scaled OFT preserves the key OFT guarantee (hyperspherical-energy invariance) only when the diagonal entries are positive; the paper explicitly assumes $\bm{D}=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ with $s_i>0$. In practice one therefore initializes $\bm{D}$ so that it starts at the identity (no scaling) and ensures the diagonal entries remain positive during optimization (the paper states the positivity assumption but leaves exact parameterization details to the implementation).

In summary, re-scaled OFT is a minimal, principled extension of OFT: it keeps the hyperspherical-energy invariance that underlies OFT's semantic preservation while adding very inexpensive per-neuron magnitudes. This preserves the desirable stability and geometry of OFT and provides modest extra capacity that the authors observed to be generally beneficial in practice.

Applying OFT to Subject-driven Generation

# DreamBooth setup: reconstruction and class-preserving losses

Subject-driven generation is the setup where we are given only a few images of a new subject (for example, a person, an object, or a pet) and want the text-to-image model to generate novel images of that same subject placed into new contexts described by text prompts. DreamBooth is a practical framework for this task: it injects a customized token into the text conditioning (so the model can learn to associate that token with the target subject) and finetunes the pretrained diffusion model on a small subject-specific dataset. The finetuning objective used in DreamBooth (and adopted unchanged by OFT in our experiments) combines two complementary losses.

The first is a reconstruction loss: the model must be able to reconstruct the few given subject images when conditioned on prompts that contain the customized token. This encourages the model to learn the visual appearance and details that define the subject, so that later generation with the token will reproduce the subject identity.

The second is a class-specific prior-preservation loss. The purpose of this loss is to prevent the model from “overfitting” the token so strongly that the token loses its relationship to the broader visual class. Concretely, DreamBooth mixes examples of the subject with examples of the general class (for example, other images from the same object or person class) and penalizes deviations from the pretrained model’s prior on that class. This helps the model retain the general generative capability and diversity of the pretrained model while learning the subject-specific details.

OFT integrates into DreamBooth by keeping these same two losses and the same overall training data design (the few subject images plus class examples), but changing how the model parameters are adapted. Instead of updating dense pretrained weights directly (or adding a small additive adapter), OFT parameterizes the change to selected layers using layer-shared orthogonal transformations. This preserves the DreamBooth training signal while enforcing an explicit structural constraint on how the model is allowed to change.

# Finetuning targets (e.g., attention layers) with OFT/COFT

OFT is a parameterization choice for how we adapt pretrained layers. For a given pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ (a matrix of neurons / filters), OFT replaces the forward use of that matrix with a transformed matrix
\[
\bm{W} \;=\; \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0,
\]
so that the layer output for input $\bm{x}$ becomes
\[
\bm{z} \;=\; \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} \;=\; (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\qquad\text{subject to}\quad
\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} \;=\; \bm{I}.
\]
Here $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix that is learned during finetuning while $\bm{W}^0$ remains fixed. By initializing $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ we start the finetuning from exactly the pretrained model, and subsequent updates rotate/reflect the neurons (as a whole) in the layer’s input space.

In the DreamBooth experiments in the paper, OFT is applied only to the same layers that competitor parameter-efficient methods tune for fair comparison — for transformer-style architectures this is typically the attention weight matrices. Constraining which layers are adapted keeps the number of trainable parameters small and isolates the effect of the OFT parameterization. OFT is, however, architecture-agnostic in principle and may be applied to fully connected or convolutional layers; in convolutional settings a block-diagonal structure for $\bm{R}$ has a natural interpretation as channel-wise or shared transforms.

Two practical parameterizations are important in making OFT efficient and stable:

1. Cayley parameterization and skew-symmetric parametrization. To guarantee orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ in a differentiable way, the paper adopts the Cayley transform: parameterize $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$ is skew-symmetric. Cayley produces orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$ (the special orthogonal group), which suffices in practice.

2. Block-diagonal structure. A full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix has $d(d-1)/2$ free parameters, which can be large. OFT reduces parameter cost by using a block-diagonal $\bm{R}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$ with $r$ blocks of size $d/r$. This reduces the number of parameters roughly to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ and can be further reduced by sharing blocks across subspaces.

COFT (Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning) adds an explicit radius constraint around the identity to limit how far the orthogonal transform can deviate from the pretrained coordinate frame:
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x},\qquad
\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I},\quad
\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon.
\]
Because directly constraining the Cayley-form $\bm{R}$ is awkward, the paper uses the Neumann-series approximation $\bm{R}\approx\bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$ to translate the constraint into a simple bound $\|\bm{Q}\|\le\epsilon'$ which can be enforced by projected gradient descent. COFT therefore enforces both orthogonality and a small deviation from identity, combining the hyperspherical-preservation property of orthogonality with an explicit proximity to the pretrained model.

Optionally, OFT can be extended to re-scaled OFT by learning a positive diagonal scaling matrix $\bm{D}=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ and using $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$. Rescaling does not change hyperspherical energy (which depends only on directions), but increases flexibility with few extra parameters. In the DreamBooth experiments, the paper reports results primarily with the original OFT formulation, with re-scaled OFT explored in appendices.

# Stability across iterations versus catastrophic collapse

A central practical problem in subject-driven finetuning is instability: training that initially improves the model can later “collapse,” producing unrealistic images or images that no longer preserve the subject identity. Two common finetuning recipes are (a) vanilla finetuning with a very small learning rate and (b) additive adapter methods (for example, LoRA), both of which try to limit deviation from the pretrained model. Nevertheless, neither strategy guarantees preservation of the pretrained model’s semantic generation ability; with too many iterations or imperfect hyperparameter choices, the model can drift into regions that lose the learned semantics.

OFT addresses this instability through two connected mechanisms. First, an orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$ applied uniformly to all neurons in a layer preserves the pairwise angular relations among the normalized neurons. Let $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ denote normalized neurons. For any orthogonal $\bm{R}$,
\[
\hat{\bm{w}}_i^\top \hat{\bm{w}}_j \;=\; (\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i)^\top (\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_j),
\]
and consequently all pairwise distances on the unit hypersphere are preserved:
\[
\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\| \;=\; \|\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|.
\]
Because hyperspherical energy is computed from these pairwise distances (for a layer $\bm{W}$, $\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W})=\sum_{i\ne j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$), an orthogonal layer transform leaves the hyperspherical energy unchanged. The paper hypothesizes and empirically supports that preserving hyperspherical energy preserves the pretrained model’s semantic generation ability; thus OFT avoids destructive parameter updates that would otherwise break subject identity or the model’s ability to follow prompts.

Second, COFT’s explicit radius constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$ further limits the transform to remain close to identity. In practice this reduces sensitivity to the number of training iterations and the learning rate: COFT behaves increasingly like the pretrained model as $\epsilon$ decreases, and like OFT when $\epsilon$ is large. The paper finds that COFT often yields the best finetuning stability because it combines the angle-preserving guarantee of orthogonality with a controlled deviation from the pretrained coordinate frame.

Empirical observations reported in the paper illustrate the stability differences. In DreamBooth-style subject finetuning runs, OFT and COFT converge quickly and remain stable over many more iterations than competing methods. The paper describes that after only a few hundred iterations both OFT variants achieve good subject control, while LoRA already shows failures to preserve identity; after thousands of iterations vanilla DreamBooth can produce collapsed images, but OFT and COFT remain able to generate the subject faithfully. This insensitivity to iteration count is practically important: with OFT/COFT one can set larger iteration budgets without risk of catastrophic collapse, simplifying hyperparameter tuning.

# Metrics: DINO and CLIP-I for subject fidelity, CLIP-T for prompt fidelity, LPIPS for diversity

Evaluating subject-driven generation requires measuring (at least) three qualities: the degree to which generated images depict the intended subject, the degree to which the images match the text prompt, and the diversity of multiple generated samples for the same token/prompt. The paper uses a small set of automatic metrics to capture these aspects and to compare OFT/COFT to DreamBooth and LoRA.

- Subject fidelity: two embedding-based similarity metrics are used. CLIP-I is the average cosine similarity between CLIP image embeddings of generated images and real subject images; it measures whether generated images are close (in CLIP embedding space) to the actual subject photos. DINO is a similar metric computed using ViT S/16 DINO embeddings instead of CLIP; it provides a complementary embedding view of subject similarity. Higher values indicate better preservation of subject identity.

- Prompt fidelity: CLIP-T is the average cosine similarity between the CLIP text embedding of the prompt and the CLIP image embeddings of the generated images; it measures how faithfully the generated images follow the textual instruction. Again, higher is better for prompt adherence.

- Sample diversity: LPIPS is used as a perceptual measure of diversity among generated samples for the same subject and prompt. The paper evaluates average LPIPS (or LPIPS-based similarity/distance statistics) across multiple generated images; higher LPIPS typically indicates greater perceptual variation among outputs and thus more diversity.

To reduce randomness and report robust comparisons, the experiments in the paper repeat the same finetuning procedure across multiple random seeds (the paper reports 30 different seeds for DreamBooth-style experiments) and report average metric behavior. For qualitative visualization the paper selects, for each method, the model checkpoint that achieved the best validation CLIP metrics and compares images generated from that checkpoint; for quantitative comparison the metrics above are averaged across runs and subjects.

# Empirical patterns: faster convergence, stronger identity preservation, and prompt adherence

Across the DreamBooth-style subject-driven experiments, a consistent empirical pattern emerges in the paper. First, OFT and COFT converge substantially faster than competing parameter-efficient methods: they achieve good subject control in fewer iterations and with fewer finetuning examples. This faster convergence is attributed to OFT directly updating neuron directions (angles), which the paper argues are the most semantically important components of neuron weights. Intuitively, the toy experiment described in the paper (an autoencoder example) showed that angular information of neurons was sufficient to reconstruct inputs, while magnitudes carried little semantic content. OFT’s orthogonal transforms directly act on those directional degrees of freedom and thus are an effective and efficient way to teach the model a new subject.

Second, OFT and COFT show stronger identity preservation. Quantitatively, the paper reports that both OFT and COFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA on the subject-fidelity metrics (DINO and CLIP-I) by a considerable margin, while achieving slightly better or comparable prompt fidelity (CLIP-T) and diversity (LPIPS). Qualitatively, randomly selected comparisons show OFT/COFT images preserving fine identity details where LoRA often fails (for example, producing incorrect textures or losing distinguishing features), and DreamBooth sometimes preserves identity but fails to follow the text prompt as closely.

Third, OFT improves prompt adherence while maintaining identity, balancing the two competing objectives of subject-driven finetuning. The key reason is structural: orthogonal transforms preserve the layerwise hyperspherical structure that encodes pretrained semantic generation ability, so the model remains capable of responding correctly to textual instructions; at the same time, the orthogonal rotation of neuron directions provides an expressive yet constrained way to represent the new subject. COFT tightens this balance by constraining the magnitude of rotation, giving an extra knob to trade flexibility for stability.

Practically, these empirical patterns translate into several useful consequences for applying OFT in DreamBooth-style workflows. OFT requires fewer training examples and fewer finetuning iterations to reach competitive performance, is less sensitive to the exact number of training steps (reducing the need for careful early stopping), and introduces no additional inference cost because the learned orthogonal transforms can be multiplied into the pretrained weights before deployment. COFT further simplifies hyperparameter setting by making both learning-rate and iteration-count less delicate through the explicit proximity constraint.

# Practical summary and recommended procedure

Putting the ideas together, a practical recipe for using OFT in subject-driven generation (as implemented in the paper) is:

1. Prepare the DreamBooth training data: the few images of the subject plus class examples for prior preservation, and build prompts containing a customized token for the subject.

2. Choose which pretrained layers to adapt. For fairness with existing adapter baselines, apply OFT to the attention weight matrices in transformer-based diffusion architectures; OFT can also be applied to other layers.

3. Parameterize each adapted layer by an orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$ so that the effective layer weights are $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Initialize $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$.

4. Implement efficient orthogonality using the Cayley parameterization $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$, and reduce parameter cost with a block-diagonal form for $\bm{R}$.

5. Optionally use COFT by constraining $\|\bm{Q}\|\le\epsilon'$ (projected gradient descent) to keep transforms near identity and increase stability.

6. Train using the same DreamBooth losses (reconstruction plus class-prior preservation), updating only the OFT parameters ($\bm{Q}$ or blockwise representations), and monitor the subject fidelity and prompt-fidelity metrics (DINO, CLIP-I, CLIP-T, LPIPS).

7. At inference time, multiply $\bm{R}$ into the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ to obtain the adapted weights $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, and generate images as usual; there is no extra runtime overhead.

This procedure captures the core contribution of the paper: by restricting finetuning to angle-preserving orthogonal transforms (and optionally constraining deviation from identity), OFT preserves the pretrained model’s hyperspherical structure that correlates with semantic generation ability, while providing a focused and efficient mechanism to teach the model a new subject. The empirical outcomes reported in the paper — faster convergence, improved subject fidelity, and stable finetuning across iterations — make OFT a practical alternative to standard DreamBooth finetuning and additive adapter approaches for subject-driven generation.

Applying OFT to Controllable Generation

# Control signals: Canny edges (C2I), segmentation maps (S2I), facial landmarks (L2F)

Controllable image generation means augmenting a text prompt with an extra, structured signal that constrains the geometric, structural, or semantic content of the output. In the experiments described in the paper, three such control signals are used because each captures a different kind of constraint that users often want to impose.

Canny edges (C2I) are sparse binary maps that mark strong local gradients and outline object boundaries. As a control input they primarily constrain the geometry and silhouette of objects without prescribing class labels or textures. The paper uses Canny edges extracted from natural images (COCO) to ask the diffusion model to “hallucinate” plausible interior content and textures consistent with a given outline. One notable finding is that OFT and COFT can produce semantically coherent images from rough or sparse edge maps, whereas some adapter-based baselines have more difficulty turning a simple edge map into a semantically plausible render.

Segmentation maps (S2I) partition an image into labeled regions (for example, “sky”, “building”, “person”), and thus provide a stronger, semantic layout constraint than edges. When conditioning on segmentation maps (ADE20K in the experiments), the generator must respect not only shapes but also which semantic class occupies each region. The paper reports that OFT and COFT are effective at following segmentation layouts and producing high-fidelity images that match the intended class regions, while some competing finetuning strategies (notably LoRA in the reported comparisons) can fail to follow the segmentation faithfully.

Facial landmarks (L2F) are sparse coordinate sets that encode keypoints on a face (eyes, nose, mouth corners, etc.) and thus act as a precise control for pose and expression. In the CelebA-HQ experiments, landmarks are used to force a subject or a face to adopt a particular pose. Because landmarks are compact and highly geometric, this task tests a model’s ability to translate a small structural signal into consistent pixel-level facial geometry. The paper demonstrates that OFT and COFT yield very accurate pose control on this task.

Across all three signal types the evaluation pipeline is the same in spirit: the model is given a control input and text prompt, it produces images, and then an off-the-shelf estimator is run on the generated image to recover the control signal (edges, segmentation, or landmarks). The recovered control signal is compared to the original input; the comparison is the “control consistency” metric described below. This strategy lets us quantify how well the finetuned model obeys the intended control without requiring human raters for every evaluation.

# Datasets and setups: COCO, ADE20K, CelebA-HQ

Each control task uses a dataset chosen to match the control modality and to stress different generalization aspects of the finetuned model. Canny edge to image (C2I) is evaluated on the COCO dataset, which contains diverse everyday scenes and therefore tests the model’s ability to synthesize varied object types and contexts from outline cues. Segmentation map to image (S2I) uses ADE20K, a dataset with dense pixel-level semantic labels across many scene categories; this forces the generator to respect semantic layouts and object interrelations. Landmark to face (L2F) is evaluated on CelebA-HQ, a high-quality face image dataset that emphasizes fine facial details and pose consistency.

In the experiments reported in the paper, all finetuning methods—including OFT, COFT, ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA—start from the same pretrained Stable Diffusion v1.5 model and are finetuned on the chosen dataset. For fairness, OFT is applied to the same model components that other parameter-efficient methods use (for Transformer-based parts this typically means attention-related weight matrices). Training was run for 20 epochs for the controllable-generation comparisons in the main paper so that convergence curves and final scores could be compared across methods under comparable training budgets.

A practical detail emphasized by the paper is parameter efficiency: OFT and COFT were configured to use a similar number of trainable parameters as LoRA and substantially fewer parameters than ControlNet. Despite this compactness, OFT and COFT reached strong control performance with fewer epochs and less data in several cases, demonstrating both data efficiency and parameter efficiency on these dataset setups.

# Design contrast: OFT versus ControlNet/T2I-Adapter (no extra inference cost)

The key architectural difference between OFT and adapter-based approaches like ControlNet or T2I-Adapter lies in where and how additional capacity is introduced and whether that capacity must be active at inference time.

OFT modifies the pretrained network by learning a layer-shared orthogonal transform applied multiplicatively to the pretrained weights. Concretely, for a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ in a given layer OFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ and uses the transformed weights
\[
\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0,
\qquad\text{subject to}\qquad
\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}.
\]
Because $\bm{R}$ is initialized as the identity and remains orthogonal during training, OFT preserves the hyperspherical structure of the neuron directions while allowing controlled angular changes that adapt the model to the new control signal.

At inference time there is no need to keep a separate module computing a transform—one can pre-multiply and store the modified weight matrix $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ and use it exactly as a standard layer. This is why OFT introduces no additional inference overhead: the learned orthogonal transform has been folded into the base weights and the runtime computation graph and FLOPs remain identical to the original pretrained model.

ControlNet and T2I-Adapter take a different route: they add external networks or adapter modules that process the control signal and inject control-conditioned features into the diffusion model during inference. Those adapters must run at inference time, which increases both parameter count and computational cost. The paper emphasizes that OFT achieves comparable or better controllability without adding such runtime modules; this property makes OFT more practical for deployment where inference latency or memory overhead matters.

A related practical point is that OFT’s parameterization is flexible: the orthogonal matrix can be represented in a block-diagonal form and parameterized efficiently (for example via a Cayley transform of a skew-symmetric matrix), which keeps the number of trainable parameters compact while preserving the orthogonality constraint. COFT further constrains the learned $\bm{R}$ to stay close to the identity (via an $\epsilon$-deviation constraint) to trade off adaptation flexibility and preservation of pretrained generative ability.

# Control consistency metrics: IoU/F1 for edges, mIoU/accuracy for segmentation, landmark L2

To quantify how faithfully a generated image follows its control input the paper proposes a control consistency evaluation: recover the control signal from each generated image using an off-the-shelf estimator and compare it to the original control input. Formally, given a dataset of $N$ examples, for each example $i$ let the original control be $c_i$ and the model output be $I_i^{\text{gen}}$. An extractor function $\mathcal{T}$ computes the recovered control $\hat{c}_i=\mathcal{T}(I_i^{\text{gen}})$. A task-specific distance or similarity $\mathcal{D}$ measures agreement, and the aggregate metric is
\[
\text{consistency}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \mathcal{D}(c_i,\hat{c}_i).
\]
The paper uses standard, interpretable choices of $\mathcal{D}$ for each control modality.

For Canny edges (binary masks) the paper reports Intersection-over-Union (IoU) and F1 score. If $A$ and $B$ are the sets of edge pixels in the original and recovered maps respectively, then
\[
\text{IoU}(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}.
\]
Precision and recall are
\[
\text{precision}=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|B|},\qquad
\text{recall}=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A|},
\]
and the F1 score is the harmonic mean
\[
\text{F1} = \frac{2\cdot\text{precision}\cdot\text{recall}}{\text{precision}+\text{recall}}.
\]

For segmentation maps (multi-class pixel labels) the paper reports mean Intersection-over-Union (mIoU) and mean and overall pixel accuracy. If there are $K$ semantic classes and $\text{IoU}_k$ denotes the IoU computed for class $k$, then
\[
\text{mIoU}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K \text{IoU}_k.
\]
Mean pixel accuracy averages per-class accuracies, and overall accuracy is the fraction of correctly labeled pixels over the whole dataset. Concretely, if $TP_k$ is the number of true positives for class $k$ and $N_k$ is the number of ground-truth pixels of class $k$, one common formulation of mean accuracy is
\[
\text{mean-acc}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K \frac{TP_k}{N_k},
\]
and overall accuracy is
\[
\text{overall-acc}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^K TP_k}{\sum_{k=1}^K N_k}.
\]

For facial landmarks the paper reports the mean $\ell_2$ distance between the input landmark coordinates and the coordinates recovered from the generated face. If each face has $J$ landmark points and landmark $j$ in example $i$ is $l_{i,j}$ while the recovered landmark is $\hat{l}_{i,j}$, then the mean landmark error is
\[
\text{mean-L2}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^J \|l_{i,j}-\hat{l}_{i,j}\|_2.
\]

A crucial practical caveat the paper emphasizes is that these consistency metrics rely on the accuracy of the extractor $\mathcal{T}$ (edge detector, segmentation network, landmark detector). The reported numbers therefore measure how closely the generated images reproduce a control signal that current automated estimators can detect; they are valid and useful comparative metrics but are upper-bounded by the extractor’s own errors.

# Observed benefits: faster, smoother convergence and stronger control

Across the three control tasks the experiments show consistent advantages for OFT (and its constrained variant COFT) over adapter-based and additive finetuning baselines. The benefits appear in three main dimensions: convergence speed, training stability (smoothness), and final control fidelity.

Convergence speed. OFT reaches strong control performance with fewer epochs. A concrete example from the paper is the landmark-to-face (L2F) task: OFT/COFT attain a level of landmark error by epoch 8 that LoRA needs about 20 epochs to reach. This faster convergence was visible both in quantitative curves and in qualitative images as OFT’s generated faces quickly align their pose to the input landmarks. Relatedly, OFT requires fewer training examples in some settings: the paper reports that OFT can converge well even when trained on only 5% of the ADE20K data used in segmentation experiments, showing substantial data efficiency.

Training stability and smoothness. OFT provides smooth, predictable training dynamics. The paper contrasts this with ControlNet, whose controllability often “suddenly emerges” after several epochs in their experiments (a rapid change in behavior that is harder to monitor and tune). By preserving hyperspherical energy, OFT restricts the effective search space of weight updates to orthogonal transforms and hence stabilizes finetuning. COFT adds the explicit $\epsilon$-deviation constraint to further stabilize updates by keeping the learned orthogonal matrix close to identity; smaller $\epsilon$ makes the finetuned model behave more like the pretrained model, while larger $\epsilon$ gives more flexibility.

Stronger control and higher visual fidelity. On the three modalities OFT and COFT produced images that more faithfully followed the input control signals and looked qualitatively better. For segmentation-to-image (S2I) the paper notes that LoRA often fails to reproduce the input segmentation, whereas OFT and COFT follow the segmentation maps and generate realistic textures and geometry. For C2I, OFT and COFT hallucinate plausible interior content from rough Canny outlines; in contrast, T2I-Adapter and LoRA performed worse in the same conditions. For L2F, OFT produced the most accurate pose control even under challenging poses. Overall, OFT delivered higher-fidelity outputs with more vivid details and more coherent geometric structure while using fewer parameters than ControlNet and no extra inference modules.

Why these benefits arise. The paper offers two complementary intuitions. First, angular information among neurons encodes much of the semantic content learned by the pretrained model; changing neuron directions is therefore an efficient way to adapt semantics for a new control signal. Second, orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise neuron angles (hyperspherical energy) and thus maintain the pretrained model’s global relational structure while enabling targeted changes. Together, these effects let OFT make effective, semantically meaningful updates without destroying the pretrained generative ability—this is the heart of why OFT is both effective and stable for controllable generation.

Practical implications. For practitioners, OFT provides an efficient way to add controllability: it is parameter-efficient (comparable to LoRA), data-efficient, converges quickly, and imposes no extra inference overhead because the learned transforms can be folded into the original weights. COFT is a useful variant when conservative adaptation is desired: the $\epsilon$ hyperparameter provides a direct knob to trade off how much the finetuned model may deviate from the pretrained one.

Taken together, the controllable-generation experiments in the paper show that preserving the angular neuron structure while allowing constrained directional adaptation is a powerful recipe for giving pretrained text-to-image diffusion models the ability to follow external control signals accurately and robustly.

Experimental Results and Ablations

# Convergence speed and stability: OFT/COFT vs DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet, T2I-Adapter

One of the clearest empirical findings in the paper is that Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT) converge faster and with greater stability than common baselines for both subject-driven and controllable generation. All experiments in the main paper finetune Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the pretrained model and compare OFT/COFT against DreamBooth and LoRA in subject-driven generation, and against ControlNet, T2I-Adapter and LoRA in controllable generation.

In subject-driven finetuning (DreamBooth-style setup) the dynamics are instructive. The paper reports that after a modest number of optimization iterations (on the order of a few hundred), both OFT and COFT already achieve good subject control, while LoRA often fails to preserve subject identity. As training proceeds, DreamBooth-style full-weight finetuning can eventually begin to produce collapsed images, and LoRA can drift toward incorrect semantic modes (anecdotally producing “yellow fur” instead of the intended “yellow shirt” in one example). By contrast, OFT and COFT remain stable and continue to produce semantically consistent outputs even after many more iterations. Intuition for this stability comes from the OFT constraints: learning a layer-shared orthogonal rotation/reflection preserves pairwise neuron angles (hyperspherical energy) and thus preserves the pretrained network’s relational structure while allowing targeted directional updates. COFT adds an explicit radius constraint around the identity, further limiting drift and making learning-rate / iteration-count choices less sensitive.

For controllable generation (image-conditioned tasks such as Canny→image, segmentation→image, landmark→face), the paper reports faster and smoother convergence for OFT/COFT than for the baselines. A concrete behavioral summary: on the landmark-to-face (L2F) task, OFT/COFT reached the level of control that LoRA obtained only after many more epochs: OFT's performance at epoch 8 was comparable to LoRA at epoch 20. ControlNet and T2I-Adapter show different dynamics — ControlNet in particular exhibits a “sudden emergence” phenomenon where control quality rapidly improves at a late epoch (around the 8th epoch in the reported experiments) rather than gradually; OFT and COFT on the other hand build control more steadily across epochs. This smooth, rapid convergence matters in practice because it reduces the hyperparameter tuning burden (you can run more iterations without catastrophic drift) and reduces wall-clock training time to acceptable control.

Two practical mechanisms underlie the improved convergence and stability. First, OFT focuses on rotating neuron directions rather than arbitrarily changing magnitudes, which (empirically and intuitively) is an effective way to change semantics while retaining pretrained knowledge. Second, constraining optimization to an orthogonal manifold produces a smoother optimization landscape than unconstrained Euclidean updates, which improves optimization behavior and prevents explosive deviation from the pretrained point.

# Final performance on subject fidelity, prompt fidelity, diversity

To quantify final performance in subject-driven generation, the paper evaluates multiple complementary metrics that capture different aspects of generation quality:

- Subject fidelity: measured with DINO features and CLIP image embeddings (referred to as DINO and CLIP-I). These metrics compute embedding similarity between generated images and the reference subject images, capturing how well identity and instance-specific visual cues are preserved.
- Prompt fidelity: measured by CLIP text–image similarity (CLIP-T), which captures how well generated images match the guiding text prompt.
- Diversity: measured via LPIPS between generated samples with the same prompt (higher LPIPS indicates more perceptual diversity).

Across these metrics the main empirical claims are: OFT and COFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA on subject fidelity metrics (DINO and CLIP-I) by a considerable margin, while obtaining comparable or slightly improved prompt fidelity (CLIP-T) and diversity (LPIPS). The evaluation was made robust by repeating finetuning with many random seeds (the paper reports 30 seeds) and selecting models by validation CLIP metrics. Qualitatively, this pattern is consistent with the method design: preserving hyperspherical energy (pairwise neuron angles) helps maintain pretrained semantic priors that underpin identity-preserving visual generation, while orthogonal transformations give enough capacity to adapt appearance to prompts and contexts.

Important practical implication: OFT/COFT improve the combination of subject fidelity and prompt adherence without sacrificing diversity; they therefore address the core trade-off in subject-driven finetuning where naive finetuning can collapse identity fidelity or yield low prompt compliance.

# Control quality across C2I, S2I, L2F with new consistency metrics

To measure controllability in a principled way the authors introduce a control-consistency evaluation methodology: take the generated image, extract a predicted control signal using an off-the-shelf estimator, and compare this predicted control to the original input control. This puts the control assessment in the same signal domain used to condition generation and directly evaluates whether the generated image respects the conditioning.

The concrete metrics applied in each task are:

- Canny edge → image (C2I): compute IoU and F1 between original edge map and edges estimated from the generated image.
- Segmentation map → image (S2I): compute mean IoU, mean accuracy and overall accuracy between the ground-truth segmentation map and the segmentation predicted on the generated image.
- Landmark → face (L2F): compute mean $\ell_2$ distance between the input facial landmarks and landmarks detected on the generated face.

Formally, for a landmark task with $N$ test examples if $l^{(i)}$ denotes the input landmarks and $\hat{l}^{(i)}$ denotes the landmarks predicted from the generated image, the mean landmark error is
\[
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \left\| l^{(i)} - \hat{l}^{(i)} \right\|_2.
\]

Using these task-specific consistency metrics, the paper reports that OFT and COFT consistently yield stronger control than ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA. The paper notes some task-level differences among baselines: for example, LoRA can be competitive on segmentation→image tasks (S2I) but underperforms on C2I and L2F. Adapter-based approaches (ControlNet and T2I-Adapter) tend to converge more slowly and show weaker final consistency in the reported settings. OFT and COFT obtain higher consistency across these varied control tasks, producing images whose recovered control signals closely match inputs.

A caution the authors underline is that the control-consistency metric quality depends on the accuracy of the off-the-shelf estimator used to extract the control signal from generated images. The reported comparisons assume the same estimator across methods, so relative comparisons remain meaningful.

# Data and parameter efficiency, including small-data regimes

Two complementary efficiency axes are emphasized in the paper: data efficiency (how many training images / epochs are needed) and parameter efficiency (how many trainable parameters are required).

On data efficiency, OFT/COFT require fewer epochs and fewer images to obtain good control. Concrete experimental observations include the L2F example where OFT reached LoRA’s final performance substantially earlier (epoch 8 vs epoch 20), and a controllable generation example where OFT converged well when trained with only 5% of ADE20K data — demonstrating strong small-data behavior. In subject-driven generation, OFT is also more robust to the number of finetuning iterations: models can be trained for relatively many iterations without catastrophic collapse, reducing the sensitivity to epoch/iteration choices.

On parameter efficiency, the paper contrasts the parameter counts and asymptotic complexities of OFT and LoRA. LoRA represents the finetuning update additively with a low-rank decomposition, yielding a parameter budget of
\[
\text{params}_{\text{LoRA}} = r'(d+n)
\]
for a layer of shape $d\times n$ with LoRA rank $r'$. OFT parameterizes a layer-wise orthogonal transform $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$, and to reduce cost uses an $r$-block diagonal structure. The number of parameters for an $r$-block diagonal orthogonal matrix scales on the order of $\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{d^2}{r}\right)$, and if blocks are shared this can be further reduced toward $\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{d^2}{r^2}\right)$. A concrete example reported in the paper: for a $128\times 128$ weight matrix, LoRA with $r'=8$ uses $2{,}048$ trainable parameters, while OFT with $r=8$ uses $960$ trainable parameters (no block sharing). In the experiments the OFT/COFT configurations used a number of trainable parameters comparable to LoRA and much fewer than ControlNet, yet OFT achieved better control and faster convergence.

The upshot is that OFT can be both data-efficient and parameter-efficient: it reaches competitive or superior performance with fewer examples and fewer epochs and without inflating the number of finetuned parameters compared to widely used baselines. This combination is particularly valuable for small-data or quick-adaptation scenarios.

# Ablations: number of blocks $r$, block sharing, $\epsilon$ in COFT, re-scaled OFT

The paper includes ablations and analyses that clarify how OFT’s design choices trade off flexibility, capacity and stability.

Number of blocks $r$ and block sharing. OFT parameterizes the orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$ as block-diagonal:
\[
\bm{R}=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r),\quad \bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d/r\times d/r}\text{ orthogonal.}
\]
Intuitively, $r$ controls the granularity of the orthogonal degrees of freedom. Small $r$ (fewer, larger blocks) gives more expressive per-layer transforms (more parameters, closer to a full $\bm{R}\in\mathrm{O}(d)$), while large $r$ (many small blocks) strongly regularizes the transform and reduces parameter cost. Empirically, larger per-layer capacity (smaller $r$) tends to improve ceiling performance but at the risk of increased overfitting or instability; increasing $r$ reduces overfitting and makes finetuning more stable. Block sharing (forcing $\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j$ across blocks) further reduces parameter count (scaling toward $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$) but can introduce bias because the same transform is applied to multiple receptive groups; the authors treat block sharing as a useful but stronger inductive bias that trades capacity for efficiency.

$\epsilon$ in COFT. COFT augments OFT’s orthogonality constraint by constraining the deviation to identity:
\[
\text{s.t.}\quad \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon.
\]
Because OFT uses the Cayley parametrization $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$, the paper approximates the relationship using a Neumann-series expansion:
\[
\bm{R}\approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2),
\]
so the constraint can be enforced approximately by constraining $\|\bm{Q}\|\le\epsilon'$ and projecting $\bm{Q}$ via projected gradient descent. The empirical behavior is intuitive and stable: small $\epsilon$ forces the finetuned model to stay close to pretrained weights (very conservative adaptation), while large $\epsilon$ approaches unconstrained OFT (more flexibility). COFT with a moderately chosen $\epsilon$ produced the best finetuning stability in the experiments, making it easier to choose learning rates and number of iterations robustly.

Re-scaled OFT. The re-scaled OFT variant learns a diagonal re-scaling $\bm{D}=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ in addition to the orthogonal transform, with forward pass
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}.
\]
Because hyperspherical energy is invariant to neuron magnitudes, the diagonal scaling does not break the hyperspherical-energy preservation objective but does add extra flexibility to adjust neuron magnitudes per output channel. The paper reports that re-scaled OFT typically improves results over vanilla OFT (Appendix), with only a small number of added parameters. The paper also notes an erratum that the correct forward pass used in experiments is the above order $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$.

Together, these ablations demonstrate a clear flexibility–regularization trade-off: more degrees of freedom (smaller $r$, no block sharing, larger $\epsilon$, diagonal scaling) increase representational capacity and allow better fit for difficult downstream tasks; stronger regularization (larger $r$, block sharing, smaller $\epsilon$) promotes stability and better preservation of pretrained semantics. Choosing where to land on this spectrum depends on the downstream task, available data, and deployment constraints.

# Preliminary results on convolutional layers and qualitative examples

Although much of the paper’s quantitative comparison focuses on layers where prior adapter methods are typically applied (e.g., attention and fully connected layers), the authors emphasize that OFT is architecture-agnostic and can be applied to convolutional layers. The block-diagonal orthogonal parameterization acquires a particularly natural interpretation in convolutional layers. When the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, each block acts on the corresponding input channel’s neuron vector separately — analogous to a depthwise channel transform. When blocks are shared, the same orthogonal transform is applied across channels, akin to a channel-shared transform. The authors report a preliminary study (in the Appendix) showing that finetuning convolutional layers with OFT is feasible and can be interpreted through these connections to depthwise and channel-shared transforms.

Qualitatively, the generated examples (subject-driven and controllable tasks) demonstrate the strengths of OFT/COFT: better preservation of instance identity, closer adherence to text prompts, and more accurate following of control signals such as segmentation maps, edges, and facial landmarks. In particular, compared to LoRA, OFT/COFT produces fewer failure cases where identity or control is lost; compared to ControlNet and T2I-Adapter, OFT/COFT often yields images with richer details and more coherent geometry while using fewer trainable parameters. The paper also mentions a human evaluation in the Appendix that further validates the qualitative superiority of OFT, consistent with the quantitative metrics.

# No additional inference overhead via weight folding

A practical and important operational advantage of OFT/COFT is that they introduce no extra inference-time overhead. The learned orthogonal transform can be folded into the pretrained weights before deployment. Concretely, if a pretrained layer has weights $\bm{W}^0$ and OFT learns $\bm{R}$, the folded (final) weight is
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0,
\]
so at inference time the model runs with the usual dense weight $\bm{W}$ and no additional runtime modules, memory reads, or computational steps beyond the original network. Re-scaled OFT folds similarly as $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D}$. Thus OFT/COFT yield deployable finetuned models that have identical inference speed to the original pretrained model, unlike some adapter-based approaches that keep an extra conditioning branch at inference time (e.g., certain adapter architectures).

# Practical takeaways and caveats

From the experiments and ablations in the paper a few practical recommendations and caveats emerge:

- If stability and preservation of pretrained semantics are priorities (for example, when subject identity must be preserved across many iterations), COFT is especially attractive because the explicit $\epsilon$-constraint reduces sensitivity to learning rate and number of iterations.
- If final capacity/ceiling performance is the overriding concern and parameter budget is available, use fewer blocks ($r$ small) or disable block sharing and consider the re-scaled OFT variant for extra flexibility; expect improved fitting but monitor for stability.
- If you need strong parameter or compute efficiency, increase $r$ (more, smaller blocks) and/or share blocks; this yields a stronger inductive bias and better small-data stability.
- The choice of the off-the-shelf estimator matters in control-consistency metrics; reported numerical comparisons are relative and assume the same estimator across methods.

Limitations and open engineering points noted in the paper are relevant in practice: the Cayley parametrization used to guarantee orthogonality requires a matrix inverse, which can be expensive for large $d$ (the authors mitigate this with block-diagonalization). Also, while OFT’s block structure is a powerful parameter-efficiency tool, it introduces bias that may limit flexibility in some tasks.

Overall, the empirical results and ablations demonstrate that orthogonal finetuning is a practical, stable, and effective finetuning family for text-to-image diffusion models: it preserves pretrained relational structure (hyperspherical energy), converges quickly, is data-efficient, and produces high-quality, controllable images — all without increasing inference cost.

Conceptual Insights and Connections

# Why angles encode semantics and the manifold view of optimization

A central empirical observation driving Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is that angular relationships among neurons—their directions on a unit hypersphere—carry a disproportionate amount of semantic information. The paper builds on prior observations that hyperspherical (angular) similarity encodes semantic structure well, and it demonstrates with a toy autoencoder experiment that the angular component of neuron responses can almost perfectly recover image content while the magnitudes carry little semantic signal. Intuitively, when a layer produces an output vector $ \bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} $ (with $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}$), the part of the mapping that depends on direction is captured by the normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$. Normalization isolates the angular contribution and shows that much of the discriminative or semantic signal is carried by how $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ aligns with inputs, rather than by the raw lengths $\|\bm{w}_i\|$.

The paper motivates this observation further by analogy to the Fourier transform: the phase spectrum (an angular quantity) preserves the major part of an image's semantics, while the magnitude spectrum is less important. Changing neuron directions is therefore a natural lever for changing semantic behavior of a generative model. The toy experiment reported in the paper supports this claim: replacing the usual inner product with an operation that keeps only angle information preserves reconstruction quality, whereas keeping only magnitude does not.

Viewing neuron directions as points on a unit hypersphere leads naturally to a manifold perspective on finetuning. Directional changes are constrained moves on this hypersphere; orthogonal transformations correspond to global rotations/reflections of the coordinate system in which those directions are expressed. OFT leverages this view by learning a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ and applying the forward pass $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. Because orthogonal transformations are isometries on the hypersphere, they change the absolute coordinates of neurons while preserving their pairwise angular relationships. Practically, constraining updates to this manifold (via orthogonality) both focuses the finetuning on directional adjustments—the aspect most relevant to semantics—and yields a smoother optimization landscape, which the paper argues (and connects to prior work) helps convergence and stability.

# Why minimizing hyperspherical energy is unsuitable for finetuning

Hyperspherical energy (HE) is used in the paper to quantify the pairwise relational structure among neurons in a layer. For a weight matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}$ the paper defines normalized neurons $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ and the hyperspherical energy
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
HE captures how uniformly neurons are spread on the unit hypersphere: smaller HE corresponds to more uniform spacing (less redundancy).

An important algebraic property used by OFT is that HE is invariant under global orthogonal transforms. If $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal (so $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R}=\bm{I}$), then $\widehat{\bm{R}\bm{w}_i} = \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ and, since orthogonal maps preserve Euclidean distances, $\|\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_j\| = \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$. Consequently
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{R}\bm{W}) \;=\; \text{HE}(\bm{W}).
\]
This invariance motivates OFT's design goal: preserve HE during finetuning rather than changing it.

Why not minimize HE, as some prior work does when training classification networks from scratch? Minimizing HE pushes neurons toward a uniformly spaced configuration on the hypersphere, which can be a desirable inductive bias for classification (reducing redundancy and promoting separation). However, for finetuning a large pretrained generative model, that objective can be harmful: the pretrained model stores delicate semantic relationships among neurons, and driving HE toward a new minimum (uniformity) can destroy those relationships and thus the pretrained model's generative behavior. The paper therefore treats HE as a structural quantity to preserve. OFT enforces (provably) zero HE change by restricting updates to orthogonal transforms, and the constrained variant COFT further enforces small deviation from identity to keep the finetuned model near the pretrained weights in Euclidean sense.

# Relationship to LoRA: low-rank additive vs full-rank orthogonal multiplicative updates

LoRA and OFT take different structural approaches to parameter-efficient finetuning. LoRA assumes the update to a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ is well approximated by a low-rank additive term: typically one writes
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B},
\]
with $\bm{A}\bm{B}$ constrained to have small rank $r'$. This additive, low-rank reparameterization reduces the number of trainable parameters and implicitly keeps the finetuned model close (in Euclidean terms) to the pretrained model.

OFT, by contrast, applies a multiplicative, orthogonal reparameterization:
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0,\qquad \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}.
\]
Expanding this shows OFT also can be seen as an additive update:
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0,
\]
so the additive term is $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$. When $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ happens to be low-rank, OFT's update has a low-rank structure similar to LoRA's; but OFT does not impose a low-rank constraint directly. Its defining constraint is orthogonality of $\bm{R}$, which enforces a very different geometric regularity: pairwise neuron angles are preserved exactly. That distinction is the heart of the relationship: LoRA restricts the subspace of possible weight changes (rank), while OFT restricts the geometric nature of the change (an orthogonal coordinate transform). In practice the paper shows that restricting the family of updates by orthogonality yields more stable finetuning and better preservation of pretrained generative behavior for text-to-image diffusion tasks.

# Two routes to parameter efficiency: rank versus structured sparsity

Both LoRA and OFT aim to make finetuning parameter-efficient, but they pursue different parameter compression strategies.

LoRA achieves efficiency by factorizing the additive update as the product of two small matrices and constraining rank. For a weight matrix of size $d\times n$ and LoRA rank $r'$, the number of trainable parameters introduced by LoRA is typically $r'(d+n)$.

OFT achieves efficiency by restricting the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to have structure. A general $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix has $d(d-1)/2$ free parameters (the dimension of the orthogonal Lie group), which can be large. The paper therefore introduces a block-diagonal parameterization
\[
\bm{R} = \operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r),
\qquad \bm{R}_i\in\text{O}(d/r),
\]
which reduces the parameter count. For an $r$-block diagonal orthogonal matrix the parameter count becomes proportional to $d(d/r - 1)/2$, i.e. $\mathcal{O}(d^2 / r)$. Moreover, if blocks are shared across partitions (setting $\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j$ for all $i,j$), the paper shows the effective parameter complexity falls further (they state it becomes $\mathcal{O}(d^2 / r^2)$ in that sharing regime). The paper gives a concrete numerical example: for a $128\times 128$ weight matrix, LoRA with $r'=8$ uses $2{,}048$ trainable parameters, whereas OFT with $r=8$ uses $960$ trainable parameters. The block structure therefore realizes a different route to compactness—structured sparsity / parameter sharing—rather than low-rank factorization.

The paper also remarks (under the assumptions in the text) that if both $r$ and $r'$ scale linearly with $d$ (for example $r=r'=\alpha d$ for some fixed $\alpha$), the asymptotic parameter complexity arguments favor OFT under block-sharing choices (the paper reports that LoRA behaves like $\mathcal{O}(d^2+dn)$ while OFT reduces to $\mathcal{O}(d)$ in that regime). The precise constant factors depend on how blocks are configured and whether block sharing is used, but the conceptual point stands: LoRA reduces rank, OFT reduces degrees of freedom via structured orthogonality.

An additional way to increase flexibility without many parameters is the paper's re-scaled OFT variant, which introduces a diagonal scaling $\bm{D}=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ and uses the forward pass $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}$. Because hyperspherical energy uses normalized neurons, rescaling by $\bm{D}$ does not change HE, so re-scaled OFT increases flexibility with negligible extra parameters.

# Architecture agnosticism and potential for convolutional and transformer layers

OFT is presented in the paper as an architecture-agnostic finetuning strategy: in principle it can be applied wherever a weight matrix or parameter tensor defines a linear map. In practice, the experiments apply OFT to the same parts of the model that existing parameter-efficient methods target so that comparisons are fair (for example, when comparing to LoRA, OFT is applied to attention weights).

For convolutional layers the block-diagonal orthogonal parameterization admits useful interpretations. If the number of blocks equals the number of input channels, then each block acts on a single channel, which is analogous to a per-channel (depthwise) transformation; this interpretation connects OFT's block structure to depth-wise convolutional transformations. If instead a single shared block is used across channels, the same orthogonal transform is applied to all channels. These two extremes illustrate how OFT's block choices trade off channel-wise flexibility against parameter sharing. The paper reports preliminary studies of OFT on convolutional layers in an appendix and highlights these interpretations as motivation.

For Transformer architectures the paper notes that, like LoRA, OFT is naturally applied to attention weight matrices; the multiplicative orthogonal parametrization can be inserted in the same places where additive adapters are commonly introduced. Importantly, OFT does not add inference-time overhead: once finetuning completes one can precompute $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ and use $\bm{W}$ directly at the original inference cost.

# Compositionality of orthogonal transforms across tasks

One particularly attractive algebraic property of orthogonal transforms is compositionality: the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal. If task A produces $\bm{R}^{(A)}$ and task B produces $\bm{R}^{(B)}$, then the composed transform $\bm{R}^{(B)}\bm{R}^{(A)}$ remains orthogonal. This means OFT stores task-specific changes in a way that can be combined (by matrix multiplication) without leaving the orthogonal family. Put differently, OFT represents each finetuning as a coordinate-system rotation; rotations compose by multiplication and remain rotations.

The paper highlights this compositionality as an intriguing opportunity: in principle the orthogonal matrices produced by multiple OFT finetuning tasks can be multiplied together and the result is still orthogonal. Whether such multiplication preserves the knowledge or performance of all constituent downstream tasks is an open question left for future work. The composition property points toward modular and possibly compositional adaptation schemes (e.g., chaining task transforms or building multi-task adaptors by matrix products), but the paper treats this as a promising direction rather than a solved capability.

In addition to compositionality, the paper also flags practical open problems tied to OFT's orthogonal parameterization: Cayley parametrization (used to enforce orthogonality) requires a matrix inverse and is a scalability bottleneck that block structure partially mitigates; and the balance between parameter efficiency (block choices, sharing) and the bias introduced by those choices remains an area for improvement. These points emphasize that OFT's algebraic strengths bring both practical advantages (invariance of HE, composability) and engineering questions to explore.

Concluding remark

Taken together, these conceptual threads explain why OFT is able to be both stable and effective in finetuning large text-to-image diffusion models. Preserving pairwise angular structure (hyperspherical energy) protects pretrained semantic capacity; constraining updates to orthogonal transforms focuses learning on the most semantically relevant degrees of freedom (directions) and yields a smoother manifold for optimization; and structured parameterizations (block-diagonal orthogonality, optional diagonal rescaling, and COFT's radius constraint) provide practical trade-offs between flexibility and regularity. The algebraic properties of orthogonal matrices—distance preservation and composability—provide clear theoretical reasons for the method's behavior and point to several promising directions for future research.

Implementation Guide and Best Practices

# Layer selection (e.g., attention q/k/v/proj) and sharing strategies

Choosing which layers to finetune with OFT/COFT is the first practical decision. The paper demonstrates OFT as a model-agnostic finetuning method and for a fair comparison with LoRA it was applied to the same attention projection matrices that LoRA typically targets. Concretely, in Transformer-style U-Nets or text-to-image denoisers you will usually target the linear projection matrices used by attention (query/key/value/output projections) because these matrices are compact, central to semantic routing, and are the usual place adapter methods are applied. If you have a very tight parameter budget, applying OFT only to those attention projection matrices gives most of the controllability benefit while keeping the number of trainable parameters low.

OFT also applies naturally to fully connected and convolutional layers. For convolutional layers the block-diagonal interpretation of OFT can be particularly convenient: when the number of blocks matches the number of input channels, each block acts on a single channel and behaves similarly to a depth-wise transform. That observation suggests two practical sharing choices for convolutional layers: either give each input channel its own small block (fine control, lower cross-channel flexibility) or share blocks across channels (more bias, fewer parameters). In general, the default recommended strategy is to use a single layer-shared orthogonal matrix per layer (the formulation used in the paper): for each layer you learn one orthogonal transform that is applied to all neurons in that layer. If you need to cut parameters further, use the block-diagonal parameterization described below and optionally share the block submatrix across blocks.

When you want to match a baseline (LoRA or DreamBooth) for fair comparison, apply OFT to the exact same set of weight matrices used by the baseline (for example, the same q/k/v/proj matrices). That keeps the comparison apples-to-apples and simplifies hyperparameter transfer (you can reuse the same training schedule and losses).

# Cayley-parameterized orthogonal module: initialization and stability

The practical orthogonal parameterization used in the paper is the Cayley transform. Implement it exactly as

\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\]

where \(\mathbf{Q}\) is a learnable skew-symmetric matrix (\(\mathbf{Q}^\top = -\mathbf{Q}\)). This parameterization produces a provably orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\) (with determinant \(+1\), i.e., in the special orthogonal group), which is the basic reason to prefer it: the forward pass uses an orthogonal transform and therefore preserves hyperspherical geometry by construction.

Two implementation details are important for stability and correctness. First, initialize \(\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{0}\) so that \(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\) at the start of finetuning. This zero initialization guarantees that the finetuned model begins exactly from the pretrained weights and avoids abrupt early changes. Second, enforce skew-symmetry of \(\mathbf{Q}\) in your parameter pipeline. A convenient implementation pattern is to keep an unconstrained parameter \(\mathbf{A}\) and map it to \(\mathbf{Q}\) via

\[
\mathbf{Q} \leftarrow \tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}^\top),
\]

so skew-symmetry is exact at every step. After you update \(\mathbf{A}\) (or \(\mathbf{Q}\) directly), recompute \(\mathbf{R}\) by forming the Cayley expression and the inverse \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\). For computational efficiency and numerical robustness use a stable linear-algebra routine for the inversion and compute the inverse per-block if \(\mathbf{R}\) is block-diagonal (see next section).

Numerical issues to watch. The Cayley map requires \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})\) to be invertible; if \(\mathbf{Q}\) approaches a matrix that makes this factor nearly singular, inversion becomes unstable. The paper leverages the small-\(\mathbf{Q}\) regime and (optionally) COFT to avoid such problems. In practice you will rarely need to re-orthogonalize \(\mathbf{R}\) after computing it from \(\mathbf{Q}\) because the Cayley form enforces orthogonality analytically; however, floating point round-off can accumulate, so it is good practice to monitor \(\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\) (see the monitoring section below) and to recompute \(\mathbf{R}\) cleanly from \(\mathbf{Q}\) if you detect drift.

Two further practical optimizations. First, when \(d\) (the transform dimension) is large, computing and inverting full \(d\times d\) matrices is expensive; use the block-diagonal parameterization described next to reduce cost. Second, the Neumann-series expansion gives a local approximation

\[
\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2),
\]

so in debugging you can inspect \(\mathbf{Q}\) (which is low-dimensional for small blocks) to reason about how far \(\mathbf{R}\) has moved from identity.

# Choosing r, block sharing, and ε (for COFT)

The block-diagonal parameterization is central for controlling parameter count and flexibility. Let the neuron dimension be \(d\) and choose an integer \(r\) that splits the input dimension into \(r\) equally sized blocks of size \(d/r\). Then

\[
\mathbf{R}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1,\dots,\mathbf{R}_r),\qquad \mathbf{R}_i\in \mathrm{O}\!\left(\tfrac{d}{r}\right).
\]

Counting parameters: a full orthogonal \(d\times d\) matrix has \(\tfrac{d(d-1)}{2}\) degrees of freedom. For the \(r\)-block diagonal orthogonal matrix the number of free parameters is

\[
\text{params}_{\text{OFT},r}=\frac{d\left(\tfrac{d}{r}-1\right)}{2},
\]

and if you share the same block matrix across all blocks (i.e., \(\mathbf{R}_i=\mathbf{R}_j\) for all \(i,j\)) the parameter count reduces roughly to \(\mathcal{O}\!\left(\tfrac{d^2}{r^2}\right)\). For comparison, LoRA with rank \(r'\) has an additive update parameter count

\[
\text{params}_{\text{LoRA}} = r'(d + n)
\]

for a weight matrix of size \(d\times n\). The paper gives a concrete example: for a \(128\times 128\) weight matrix, LoRA with \(r'=8\) uses \(2048\) trainable parameters whereas OFT with \(r=8\) uses \(960\).

How to pick \(r\). The parameter \(r\) controls the trade-off between flexibility and parameter efficiency. Smaller \(r\) means fewer, larger blocks (more flexible, more parameters); larger \(r\) means many small blocks (less flexible, fewer parameters). Practical strategy: pick \(r\) so that your OFT parameter budget is comparable to the competing adapter method you want to match. If you have limited memory, increase \(r\) (more blocks) and consider block sharing. If you need stronger representational capacity, reduce \(r\) (fewer larger blocks).

Choosing \(\epsilon\) for COFT. Constrained OFT (COFT) adds an explicit deviation constraint

\[
\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon,
\]

which the paper moves into the Cayley parameter via the Neumann-series approximation to obtain an equivalent bound \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\le\epsilon'\) for a suitable \(\epsilon'\). The qualitative effect is straightforward: smaller \(\epsilon\) keeps the finetuned model closer to the pretrained weights (stronger regularity, less flexibility), while larger \(\epsilon\) makes COFT behave like unconstrained OFT (more flexibility). The paper reports that COFT yields better finetuning stability and makes learning-rate and iteration choices less sensitive when \(\epsilon\) is set properly. Practical tuning advice: grid-search \(\epsilon\) on a small validation set while monitoring hyperspherical energy and task validation metrics; select the largest \(\epsilon\) that does not degrade the pretrained generative quality while enabling the downstream control you need.

# Optimizers, learning rates, and training schedules

The paper emphasizes that one important desideratum for a finetuning method is stability and sample efficiency. OFT and COFT were trained with the same losses and overall training frameworks used by the baselines (DreamBooth, ControlNet/T2I-Adapter) so the straightforward practical approach is to reuse the baseline’s optimizer and base learning-rate setup for direct comparisons. That said, the paper makes two empirical observations that guide tuning.

First, OFT tends to converge faster than competing parametrizations: in the controllable generation experiments the baselines were trained for 20 epochs while OFT reached similar or better performance in fewer epochs (the paper reports that LoRA took 20 epochs to reach what OFT achieved at epoch 8). For subject-driven generation, the paper shows that OFT achieves good control after 400 iterations while other methods may require longer and can even collapse after 2000 iterations. Use these empirical points as guides: start with the baseline training schedule, evaluate early, and expect OFT to require fewer epochs/iterations for similar or better results.

Second, COFT reduces sensitivity to learning-rate and iteration count. If you find hyperparameter tuning difficult (instability or sudden collapse) start with COFT and a conservative \(\epsilon\); COFT makes the optimization less brittle because it constrains the orthogonal transform close to identity.

Practical operational recipe (in prose). For a given task, prepare the same loss and data splits you would use for DreamBooth/ControlNet finetuning. Replace the parametrized layers with OFT/COFT modules and freeze \(\mathbf{W}^0\). Use the same optimizer as in your existing pipeline and a learning rate in the range that was successful for adapter-style finetuning in that pipeline. Run short pilot training with the same seed for a few validation steps, monitor the metrics described below, and adjust learning rate, \(r\), or \(\epsilon\) according to stability and convergence speed. Because OFT requires fewer iterations on average, prefer earlier validation checks.

# Projected updates for COFT and maintaining invertibility of (I − Q)

COFT requires maintaining the explicit deviation constraint \(\|\mathbf{Q}\| \le \epsilon'\). The paper recommends enforcing that constraint via projected gradient descent on \(\mathbf{Q}\). A practical per-step update looks like this (in words): after computing the gradient step producing an updated unconstrained \(\mathbf{Q}_{\text{raw}}\), first enforce skew-symmetry (set \(\mathbf{Q}\leftarrow\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{Q}_{\text{raw}}-\mathbf{Q}_{\text{raw}}^\top)\)). Then compute the chosen matrix norm \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\) and, if it exceeds \(\epsilon'\), project back onto the norm ball. If you choose the Frobenius-norm ball the projection is the simple scaling

\[
\mathbf{Q}\leftarrow \mathbf{Q}\cdot\frac{\epsilon'}{\|\mathbf{Q}\|_F}\quad\text{if }\|\mathbf{Q}\|_F>\epsilon'.
\]

If you prefer to constrain the operator (spectral) norm to be below \(\epsilon'\) (closer to the theoretical Neumann-series condition), then you must project with respect to the spectral norm (this projection is more expensive because it requires an SVD or power-iteration-based approximation). The paper leaves the norm unspecified and notes that the Neumann-series approximation requires convergence in operator norm; practically, Frobenius projection is simpler to implement and often sufficient, while spectral-norm projection is more conservative with stronger invertibility guarantees.

Why projection matters for invertibility. Maintaining \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\) small reduces the risk that \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})\) becomes singular or ill-conditioned. The Cayley inverse \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) will blow up if \(\mathbf{Q}\) develops an eigenvalue equal to \(1\). Enforcing \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\le\epsilon'\) keeps you in the regime where the Neumann approximation is meaningful and \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})\) remains numerically well-conditioned. If you ever see NaNs during the inversion step, immediately reduce \(\epsilon'\) or the learning rate and re-run the projection logic.

# Monitoring orthogonality and hyperspherical energy preservation

Monitoring is crucial during finetuning: OFT’s whole design goal is preserving the pretrained model’s pairwise neuron relationships (hyperspherical energy) while enabling controlled angular changes. Two sets of diagnostics are essential.

First, orthogonality diagnostic. For each learned \(\mathbf{R}\) (or each block), compute the residual matrix \(\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\) and report its norm, for instance

\[
e_{\text{ortho}}=\big\|\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\big\|_F.
\]

This value should remain close to zero. If it grows unexpectedly, numerical issues have appeared in the Cayley computation (recompute \(\mathbf{R}\) from \(\mathbf{Q}\) carefully, check inverses, or reduce learning rate). Because the Cayley transform enforces orthogonality analytically if implemented exactly, a non-negligible \(e_{\text{ortho}}\) indicates numerical drift or a bug in the implementation (for example, forgetting to re-impose skew-symmetry).

Second, monitor hyperspherical energy (HE). For a weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}=\{\mathbf{w}_1,\dots,\mathbf{w}_n\}\) the per-layer hyperspherical energy used in the paper is

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) \;:=\; \sum_{i\ne j}\big\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\big\|^{-1},\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i := \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|}.
\]

Compute the baseline \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)\) before finetuning and track the difference

\[
\Delta\text{HE} = \big\|\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) - \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)\big\|.
\]

OFT is designed so that \(\Delta\text{HE}\) stays (near) zero; COFT adds extra control on how far weights move from the pretrained value. Use \(\Delta\text{HE}\) as a primary diagnostic of whether you are preserving the pretrained generative structure. If \(\Delta\text{HE}\) drifts upward and the model’s generation quality degrades, respond by tightening \(\epsilon\), increasing block-sharing or reducing learning rate.

Task-level diagnostics. In addition to these mathematical diagnostics, track the downstream metrics used in the paper depending on the task. For subject-driven generation track CLIP-I, DINO and CLIP-T for subject and prompt fidelity and LPIPS for diversity. For controllable generation compute the control-consistency metrics introduced in the paper: for C2I use IoU and F1, for S2I use mean IoU and accuracy, and for L2F use the mean \(\ell_2\) distance between control and predicted landmarks. These practical metrics tell you if finetuning is achieving the intended control goals without destroying pretrained quality.

# Folding R into W^0 for zero-overhead inference

One of OFT’s operational advantages is that the learned orthogonal transform introduces no extra inference cost after folding. After training, compute the effective weight

\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0
\]

and (for re-scaled OFT) \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0\,\mathbf{D}\). Replace the pretrained layer’s stored parameter with this new \(\mathbf{W}\) and remove the OFT modules from the runtime model. For block-diagonal \(\mathbf{R}\), perform the block-wise matrix products and reassemble. For convolutional layers you will need to reshape the convolution kernel into a 2D matrix view that matches your \(\mathbf{R}\) orientation, apply the product, and reshape back; the logical fold remains the same: multiply \(\mathbf{R}\) into the pretrained kernel along the axis you transformed during training.

Do the folding operation with the same numerical dtype as your model weights to avoid precision mismatch (e.g., use float32 if the model uses float32). After folding, validate that forward inference results match the behavior of the finetuned model with explicit \(\mathbf{R}\) (run a few test prompts) to ensure there were no shape/alignment mistakes during folding.

# Common pitfalls and debugging tips

Below are recurring pitfalls observed and remedies suggested by the behavior described in the paper. Read these as a troubleshooting checklist.

Initialization mistakes. If you do not initialize \(\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{0}\) you will not start from the pretrained model and the finetuned weights may drift immediately. Always zero-initialize to get the desired identity start.

Skew-symmetry enforcement. If \(\mathbf{Q}\) is not kept skew-symmetric you break the guarantee of orthogonality for \(\mathbf{R}\). Enforce skew-symmetry by parameterization or explicit projection after gradient steps.

Matrix inverse failures. During Cayley computation \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) may be ill-conditioned or singular if \(\mathbf{Q}\) grows too large. Symptoms are NaNs or Inf values. Remedy: reduce the step size (learning rate), enforce a stricter COFT \(\epsilon'\) on \(\mathbf{Q}\), or increase numerical regularization (for example, add a tiny identity times machine-epsilon before inversion). The simplest practical fix is to project \(\mathbf{Q}\) to a smaller norm ball right after each optimizer step.

Block misalignment in convolutional transforms. If you apply block-diagonal transforms to convolutional kernels be careful about the input-channel ordering and the reshaping direction when you fold \(\mathbf{R}\) into the kernel. A shape mismatch or channel-order swap will produce obviously broken outputs. Test fold-and-inference on a small input batch immediately after implementing a new layer.

Collapsed or degraded generations during long runs. The paper shows DreamBooth and LoRA can suffer collapse at large iteration counts, while OFT and COFT are more stable. If you observe degradation with OFT, switch to COFT with a conservative \(\epsilon\); COFT often restores stability and eliminates the need to carefully tune iteration count.

Parameter-budget mistakes. When matching a LoRA budget, compute OFT parameter counts using the formulas above and choose \(r\) and block sharing accordingly. A common debugging trap is to assume block sharing reduces parameters by \(r\) when in fact the asymptotic scaling differs; check the arithmetic.

Re-scaled OFT positivity. If you use the re-scaled variant with \(\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)\) ensure the diagonal entries stay positive as required by the method statement. Parameterize those scalars in a numerically stable way (for example, learn log-scales or apply a positive-transformation) so the forward pass obeys the assumption that the diagonal elements are positive.

Monitoring and early-validation. Because OFT/COFT aim to preserve pretrained generalizability while enabling control, rely on the two-tier monitoring approach (mathematical diagnostics + task metrics). Run short validation checks frequently; visual inspection of generated samples together with the quantitative diagnostics will usually point quickly to what to change (reduce \(\epsilon\), increase block-sharing, or change the learning rate).

Multi-task composition (caution). The paper notes an intriguing open direction: learned orthogonal matrices from multiple finetuning tasks can be multiplied to remain orthogonal. If you plan to experiment with sequential task composition (multiply \(\mathbf{R}\) matrices), be aware that whether the product preserves all downstream behaviors is an open question and requires careful empirical validation.

A minimal debug workflow in prose. If you see problems: (1) confirm \(\mathbf{Q}\) initialized to zero and \(\mathbf{R}\) starts as identity; (2) check \(\mathbf{Q}\) remains skew-symmetric after optimizer steps; (3) verify \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})\) is invertible (no NaNs in inverse); (4) monitor \(e_{\text{ortho}}\) and \(\Delta\text{HE}\); (5) if instability persists switch to COFT and tighten \(\epsilon\); (6) fold \(\mathbf{R}\) into \(\mathbf{W}^0\) on a test set and confirm identical outputs before deploying.

---

This chapter collected the practical implementation decisions that follow directly from the OFT/COFT method and the experiments reported in the paper: layer targets (attention projections are a good default), Cayley-based orthogonal modules with zero initialization, block-diagonal and block-sharing parameterizations to control parameter cost, COFT projection of \(\mathbf{Q}\) to keep the Cayley inverse safe, monitoring of orthogonality and hyperspherical energy as explicit diagnostics, and folding the learned transforms into \(\mathbf{W}^0\) for zero-overhead inference. These practices reproduce the design principles and empirical stability advantages reported in the paper and provide a concise recipe for bringing OFT/COFT into an existing diffusion finetuning pipeline.

Limitations, Ethics, and Future Directions

# Scalability of Cayley inverse and alternatives (matrix exponential, Householder, Givens)

A core implementation choice in the paper is the Cayley parameterization to guarantee orthogonality of the learned transform. The Cayley map is written in the paper as
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
\qquad \text{with}\quad
\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top,
\]
so that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal and $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$. The authors point out two useful properties of this choice: it is differentiable and it guarantees the orthogonality constraint, and initializing $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ yields $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, so the finetuning starts exactly at the pretrained weights. The Cayley parameterization, however, produces orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$, i.e. elements of the special orthogonal group; the paper notes that in practice this restriction did not harm performance.

The practical bottleneck highlighted in the paper is the matrix inverse $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ that is required by the Cayley transform. The inverse appears inside the forward/backward pass and therefore affects both memory and compute at finetuning time. To reduce this cost the paper adopts a block-diagonal parameterization of $\bm{R}$: rather than a single $d\times d$ orthogonal, $\bm{R}$ is formed as a diagonal of $r$ smaller orthogonal blocks. This reduces the number of free parameters and makes the matrix inverse operate on smaller blocks, which the paper empirically found to be an effective practical trade-off.

The paper quantifies parameter counts for these choices: a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix has $d(d-1)/2$ degrees of freedom; an $r$-block diagonal orthogonal matrix has approximately $d(d/r - 1)/2$ parameters and complexity on the order of $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. If block matrices are shared across blocks, parameter complexity can further drop toward $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. These formulae are used in the paper to argue that the block-diagonal Cayley construction can be made parameter-efficient while preserving the key invariance of hyperspherical energy.

Crucially, the paper frames the matrix-inverse cost as an explicit open problem: "Cayley parametrization which involves a matrix inverse slightly limits the scalability of OFT" and "how to speed up this matrix inverse in a differentiable way remains a challenge." The authors also point readers to other differentiable orthogonalization strategies cited in the paper. In short, the Cayley parameterization is practical and provably orthogonality-preserving, but its matrix inverse motivates further engineering research to make OFT scale to very large layer dimensions without relying heavily on block structure.

# Improving parameter efficiency without strong structural bias

One of the recurring themes in the paper is the trade-off between expressivity and regularization when choosing how to parameterize the orthogonal transform. Block-diagonalization is an attractive lever: it reduces the number of parameters and the size of the Cayley inverses, while still guaranteeing that hyperspherical energy is preserved. But the authors explicitly recognize that the block-diagonal structure introduces a structural bias: when blocks are small or when blocks are shared, the family of allowable rotations is restricted, and this restriction can both help optimization (by regularizing the finetuning) and hamper flexibility (by preventing some transforms that might better suit a downstream task).

The paper contrasts OFT with LoRA to make the point concrete. LoRA parameterizes additive low-rank updates with parameter count $r'(d+n)$ for a weight matrix of size $d\times n$, while OFT’s block-diagonal Cayley parameterization incurs asymptotic parameter complexity on the order of $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ (or $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ under certain sharing choices). The authors give a concrete example: for a $128\times 128$ weight matrix with LoRA rank $r'=8$, LoRA has about $2{,}048$ trainable parameters, whereas OFT with $r=8$ blocks has about $960$ trainable parameters. These comparisons show that OFT can be parameter-competitive, but the savings and their implications depend on the chosen structural choices.

Because parameter efficiency in OFT relies on structure (block sizes and sharing), the paper identifies the need for alternatives that reduce parameter count without imposing strong, potentially harmful biases on the transform family. This is left as an open direction: finding parameterizations or compression strategies that retain OFT’s hyperspherical-energy invariance while being less prescriptive than simple block-diagonalization. The paper also points out application-specific interpretations (for example, in convolutional layers, blocks can line up with channels and resemble depthwise transforms), which suggests that architecture-aware parameterization choices are promising but must be examined for induced biases.

# Compositional finetuning and interference across tasks

One of the most interesting observations in the paper is that the OFT parameterization naturally supports composition: orthogonal matrices are closed under multiplication, so orthogonal transforms learned for separate tasks can in principle be multiplied together to yield another orthogonal transform. Concretely, if task A learns $\bm{R}_A$ and task B learns $\bm{R}_B$, then applying $\bm{R}_B\bm{R}_A$ to the pretrained weights yields another orthogonal transform and thus preserves hyperspherical energy by construction.

The authors raise this compositionality as an open research question: does composing orthogonal transforms preserve the capabilities of all constituent finetuned tasks, or does interference accumulate? The paper does not resolve this question; rather, it points out the algebraic convenience of composition and leaves the empirical and theoretical study of multi-task composition to future work. Several important subtleties are implicit in the paper’s phrasing. For example, orthogonal matrices do not generally commute: $\bm{R}_A\bm{R}_B \neq \bm{R}_B\bm{R}_A$, so the order of composition matters and could affect the resulting behavior. Moreover, composing many task-specific rotations multiplies degrees of freedom into the final transform but does not increase the underlying capacity of the pretrained base model; as more tasks are composed, destructive interference is a plausible failure mode.

The paper frames the broad research questions to explore next: can multiple task-specific orthogonal transforms be combined so that the composite model retains the strengths of each finetuned instance? If not, what mechanisms (for example, structured orthogonal subspaces per task, gating between transforms, or meta-learning to merge transforms) can avoid interference? These are precisely the compositionality and interference questions the authors highlight as important open problems for OFT-style methods.

# Beyond hyperspherical energy: richer measures of semantic preservation

Hyperspherical energy (HE) is central to the paper’s conceptual argument: HE measures an aggregate of pairwise angular relationships among normalized neurons and is defined in the paper as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \big\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\big\|^{-1},
\qquad\text{with}\quad \hat{\bm{w}}_i := \frac{\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{w}_i\|}.
\]
The authors motivate HE as a structural statistic that captures neuron uniformity on the hypersphere and argue (and empirically observe) that preserving HE is important to preserve the pretrained semantic generation ability. Orthogonal transforms provably preserve HE, which is the mathematical justification for OFT’s choice of update family.

At the same time the paper acknowledges that designing a good finetuning strategy also requires practical measures of semantic preservation beyond a single aggregated statistic. The HE captures only pairwise angular relationships within a layer and does not, by itself, guarantee preservation of layer-to-layer interactions, activations produced on natural inputs, or task-level behavior. The authors supplement HE-based reasoning with empirical downstream metrics: for subject-driven generation they report DINO, CLIP-I, CLIP-T and LPIPS; for controllable generation they introduce a control-consistency metric that computes the control signal back from the generated image and compares it to the input control. These practical downstream measures are used in the paper to validate that HE-preserving OFT tends to keep the pretraining semantics while enabling task-specific control.

The paper therefore frames a research agenda: combine structural, parameter-level metrics such as HE with functional, task-level metrics (CLIP-based alignment, segmentation IoU, landmark errors, perceptual diversity) to build a richer and more reliable picture of how much of the pretrained generative ability is retained after finetuning. The authors leave open the problem of designing or identifying better intrinsic measures (possibly layer-wise, activation-distribution based, or function-oriented) that correlate more tightly with generation quality and controllability than HE alone.

# Ethical considerations in personalization and controllable synthesis

The paper focuses on technical advances (OFT and COFT) for two practical finetuning scenarios: subject-driven personalization given a few images, and controllable generation using additional conditional signals. Although the paper itself does not include a dedicated ethics section, the capabilities it develops naturally bring ethical considerations that practitioners and researchers should take seriously when deploying or extending this work.

Personalization via subject-driven finetuning involves training on images of a real individual to produce new images in different contexts. This raises questions of privacy, consent, and potential misuse. Systems that can reliably generate realistic images of a person from a few photos can be used for creative personalization but can also be used to impersonate or produce non-consensual content. Responsible deployment therefore requires attention to data provenance (who provided the images and under what consent), to retention policies (data minimization), and to access controls (who may run finetuning and generate content). The paper demonstrates that OFT can be sample-efficient and stable for few-shot personalization, which increases the need to think carefully about policies around collection and use of personal images.

Controllable synthesis—where a model is guided by edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks—amplifies the ability to create realistic or targeted imagery. This increases the risk that the system could be used to fabricate deceptive visual content or to violate privacy and safety norms. It also raises concerns about bias: pretrained generative models reflect the distributions they were trained on, and finetuning may further amplify or shift biases, potentially producing outputs that are stereotyped or unfair across demographic groups. Practitioners should therefore assess dataset representativeness and evaluate generated outputs across demographic axes where appropriate.

Because OFT improves finetuning stability and enables efficient personalization and control, it is particularly important to think about mitigation strategies. While the paper does not prescribe specific governance measures, possible mitigations consistent with responsible practice include explicit consent mechanisms for personalization data, watermarking or provenance markers for generated content, controlled release of models and checkpoints, and broad evaluation of failure modes and bias. The computational costs and relative ease of finetuning (an advantage of OFT) also compels attention to governance: easier finetuning lowers the barrier to misuse, so procedural and technical safeguards matter. The paper motivates these as important practical concerns for future work and deployment.

# Reproducibility and benchmarking recommendations

The paper contributes a number of experimental choices and metrics that can serve as reproducibility and benchmarking exemplars for future work on finetuning diffusion models. In terms of datasets and tasks, the authors evaluate OFT on standard subject-driven and controllable generation benchmarks: DreamBooth-style few-shot personalization, and control tasks on COCO (Canny edge \(\to\) image), ADE20K (segmentation-map \(\to\) image) and CelebA-HQ (landmark \(\to\) face). They compare against reasonable baselines (DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet, T2I-Adapter) and carefully report downstream metrics.

Concretely, the paper uses a mix of intrinsic and downstream evaluation metrics. For subject fidelity and semantic alignment it reports DINO and CLIP-based measures (CLIP-I and CLIP-T), and for diversity LPIPS. For controllable generation the paper introduces a control-consistency metric that reconstructs the control signal from generated images and computes task-specific scores: IoU and F1 for edge-to-image, mean IoU and accuracy for segmentation-to-image, and mean \(\ell_2\) landmark distance for landmark-to-face. These metric choices illustrate how to measure both fidelity to input controls and semantic quality of generations; the control-consistency idea is a concrete novel contribution of the paper that future benchmarks can adopt.

From the paper’s experimental design we can extract reproducibility recommendations that the authors themselves follow and that are sensible to adopt generally: release exact training recipes (datasets, data splits, learning rates, number of iterations/epochs), report the number of trainable parameters per method and the model initialization strategy, provide random-seed averages (the paper repeats DreamBooth experiments over 30 seeds), and measure both convergence speed and final quality. The authors also emphasize the importance of matching where finetuning is applied for fair comparisons (for example, applying OFT or LoRA to the same layer when comparing against LoRA).

The paper’s discussion also suggests benchmarking axes that future work should include: (1) convergence dynamics (how many iterations/epochs to reach usable control), (2) sensitivity to finetuning hyperparameters such as learning rate and iteration count, (3) sample efficiency (how many finetuning images are needed), (4) stability (tendency to collapse under long finetuning), and (5) computational cost of finetuning (wall-clock and hardware). Collecting and reporting these metrics will make it easier to compare OFT against other parameter-efficient finetuning methods and to evaluate trade-offs in practice.

# Concluding perspective

The paper presents Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and Constrained OFT (COFT) as a principled family of finetuning strategies that preserve hyperspherical energy and thereby tend to retain the semantic abilities of large text-to-image diffusion models while enabling strong controllability. At the same time, the paper is explicit about its limitations and about several directions that deserve further research: alleviating the computational burden of the Cayley inverse, finding more parameter-efficient and less biased orthogonal parameterizations, understanding if and how orthogonal transforms compose across multiple downstream tasks, and developing richer measures of semantic preservation than a single aggregate statistic.

For practitioners and researchers approaching this line of work, the paper provides practical starting points (implementation choices, parameter-count formulas, datasets, and evaluation metrics), but it also clearly signals that several engineering and scientific problems remain open. Addressing these will be necessary both to scale OFT-style approaches to larger models and to ensure their responsible use in personalization and controllable generation scenarios.

Exercises and Mini-Projects

# Implement an OFT layer with Cayley parameterization and block-diagonal structure

The core of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is simple to state: for a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ (a fully connected layer, or a flattened convolutional layer), replace the forward pass
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}
\]
by the OFT forward pass
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{W})^\top\bm{x}=((\bm{R}\bm{W}^0))^\top\bm{x},
\qquad\text{with}\qquad
\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}.
\]
Because an orthogonal $\bm{R}$ preserves pairwise angles among the column vectors of $\bm{W}^0$, this construction is the operational realization of the paper's objective to preserve hyperspherical energy during finetuning.

A numerically convenient parameterization of orthogonal matrices is the Cayley transform. Let $\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ be skew-symmetric so that $\bm{Q}^\top=-\bm{Q}$. The Cayley parameterization builds $\bm{R}$ as
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]
This yields an orthogonal matrix with determinant $+1$ (special orthogonal group). In implementation you therefore need to (1) maintain a skew-symmetric parameter $\bm{Q}$, (2) form $\bm{R}$ by evaluating the Cayley formula, (3) compute $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ for the forward pass, and (4) let automatic differentiation compute gradients back to the parameters used to build $\bm{Q}$.

A common and practical way to represent a skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$ is to store an unconstrained matrix $\bm{A}$ and form $\bm{Q}=(\bm{A}-\bm{A}^\top)/2$ in each forward pass. This guarantees skew-symmetry by construction while keeping optimization straightforward: the actual optimizer updates the entries of $\bm{A}$ and the forward pass enforces the skew constraint.

Directly parameterizing a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix can be expensive when $d$ is large. OFT reduces parameter and compute cost by using a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix
\[
\bm{R}=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r),
\qquad \bm{R}_i\in\text{O}\!\left(\tfrac{d}{r}\right).
\]
Each block $\bm{R}_i$ is generated via an independent Cayley transform from its skew-symmetric block $\bm{Q}_i$. This block structure reduces parameter complexity from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, and further sharing the same block across groups of channels reduces parameters even more (paper: complexity can drop to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ under block sharing). In practice, implement each block separately: reshape the parameter matrices so that each block computes its Cayley transform and its contribution to $\bm{R}$, then assemble $\bm{R}$ as a block-diagonal matrix and multiply it with $\bm{W}^0$.

Initialization is important: set the Cayley skew parameters such that $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ at the start. Then $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{0})(\bm{I}-\bm{0})^{-1}=\bm{I}$, and the finetuned model starts exactly from the pretrained weights. During training you update the free parameters that define $\bm{Q}$, which is equivalent to optimizing on the orthogonal manifold via the Cayley parameterization.

Practical tips. Compute each block inverse $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}_i)^{-1}$ per block; the block-diagonal design keeps the matrix inverse small and efficient. To make the Cayley transform stable, keep the norm of each $\bm{Q}_i$ reasonably small (initialization at zero helps). Implement the skew-symmetric formation $\bm{Q}=(\bm{A}-\bm{A}^\top)/2$ in a numerically stable way (store only the lower triangular elements of $\bm{A}$ if you want to save parameters). During training, compute gradients through the Cayley formula—automatic differentiation handles the required matrix-inverse derivative.

A short algorithmic sketch: at each forward step construct $\bm{Q}_i$ from unconstrained parameters, compute each $\bm{R}_i=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q}_i)(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}_i)^{-1}$, assemble $\bm{R}$, form $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, and use $\bm{W}$ for the model forward pass. Backpropagate to the parameters underlying $\bm{Q}_i$ and update them with your optimizer.

# Replicate the angle-versus-magnitude toy experiment

One of the motivating observations in the paper is that the angular information between kernels (neuron directions) and patches carries most of the semantic content; magnitude contributes little. The inner product decomposes as
\[
\bm{w}^\top\bm{x}=\|\bm{w}\|\,\|\bm{x}\|\,\cos\theta,
\]
so testing magnitude-only or angle-only feature maps is natural.

A minimal experiment to reproduce the paper's toy result is as follows. Train a standard convolutional autoencoder (encoder and decoder built from ordinary convolution layers) on a small collection of flower images as in the paper. Train with the usual inner-product-based convolution (no cosine normalization) and standard reconstruction loss. After training, perform three different reconstructions from the encoder feature maps:

1. The original inner-product feature map: for each convolutional filter $\bm{w}$ and local patch $\bm{x}$ compute $z(\bm{w},\bm{x})=\bm{w}^\top\bm{x}$ and decode using the trained decoder.

2. Magnitude-only feature map: for each pair use
\[
z_{\text{mag}}(\bm{w},\bm{x})=\|\bm{w}\|\,\|\bm{x}\|.
\]
This discards angle information and retains only magnitudes.

3. Angle-only (cosine) feature map: for each pair use
\[
z_{\text{angle}}(\bm{w},\bm{x})=\frac{\bm{w}^\top\bm{x}}{\|\bm{w}\|\,\|\bm{x}\|+\delta}
\]
where a small $\delta>0$ prevents division by zero in practice.

Feed each of these three variants of the feature map through the (fixed) decoder and visualize the resulting reconstructions. The paper's result is that the angle-only reconstruction preserves the semantic content and reconstructs images almost perfectly, whereas the magnitude-only reconstruction contains little useful information.

Implementation notes. When computing $\|\bm{x}\|$ at test time, compute the norm of the local patch corresponding to each sliding window (same receptive field as the convolution). Make sure the decoder expects the same channel and spatial dimensionality as the encoder output; if your decoder expects unnormalized activations, rescale appropriately so that the three variants are comparable. Add a tiny constant $\delta$ in divisions to avoid numerical issues. The goal is primarily qualitative: visualize the three reconstructed images side by side and confirm the angular dominance effect observed in the paper.

# Finetune a small diffusion UNet with OFT vs LoRA and compare convergence

The experiments in the paper compare OFT and its constrained variant (COFT) to baseline finetuning strategies such as DreamBooth finetuning and LoRA. Reproducing a scaled-down version of these comparisons is an excellent mini-project that makes the convergence and stability differences concrete.

Set up a small latent diffusion UNet (or use a publicly available small diffusion UNet checkpoint). For a fair comparison to LoRA, apply both OFT and LoRA to the same weight matrices: in the paper the authors applied OFT to the attention weights when comparing to LoRA, so do the same in your experiment. Implement LoRA's additive low-rank update to the chosen matrices and implement OFT's multiplicative orthogonal transform as described above; make sure the number of trainable parameters is comparable between LoRA and OFT runs (use block sizes $r$ in OFT to match parameter counts).

Choose one downstream finetuning task to begin with. If you mirror the paper, you may try a subject-driven finetuning task in the DreamBooth style (a few images for a subject with a prompt), or a controllable generation task (e.g., canny edge to image) similar to ControlNet. Use the same loss used in the corresponding baseline: the paper follows DreamBooth for subject-driven generation and ControlNet/T2I-Adapter for controllable generation.

Training and evaluation protocol. Train each method with the same optimizer, batch size, and (where applicable) number of iterations/epochs. In the paper, controllable generation finetuning was run for 20 epochs; subject-driven finetuning experiments in the paper observe behavior at iteration scales such as 400 and 2000 iterations. During training log the same evaluation metrics the paper uses: for subject-driven generation log DINO and CLIP-I for subject fidelity, CLIP-T for prompt fidelity, and LPIPS for diversity; for controllable generation compute the control consistency metrics described below. Monitor generated samples at regular checkpoints and plot metric curves versus iterations or epochs.

What to expect. According to the paper's empirical findings, OFT and COFT converge more quickly and more stably than LoRA and naive finetuning. OFT often reaches acceptable control at fewer iterations/epochs and preserves subject identity better. COFT, with an explicit radius constraint, further improves finetuning stability by keeping the learned orthogonal transform close to the identity.

Practical suggestions. Initialize OFT so that $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ and thus $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$. If you use COFT, implement the projection on $\bm{Q}$ (see the COFT section below). To keep comparisons fair, apply OFT and LoRA to the exact same subset of layers (for example, only attention projection matrices) and keep their trainable parameter counts close.

# Ablate r and \epsilon and measure hyperspherical energy preservation

One of the core claims of the paper is that OFT preserves hyperspherical energy (HE) and that controlling the deviation from pretrained weights provides a useful stability lever. Design an ablation study that varies the block count $r$ (the number of diagonal blocks in $\bm{R}$) and the COFT radius parameter $\epsilon$, and measure both a) how well hyperspherical energy is preserved and b) how downstream performance changes.

First, implement a routine to compute hyperspherical energy for a matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\ldots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. Normalize each column vector and compute
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1},
\qquad\text{where}\qquad
\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\frac{\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{w}_i\|}.
\]
During finetuning track layerwise HE and report the difference $\|\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W})-\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\|$ for each layer as training progresses.

Design a grid of $r$ values (the paper uses $r$ to trade parameter efficiency and flexibility; smaller $r$ means larger blocks and more parameters) and COFT radii $\epsilon$ (COFT constrains the deviation of $\bm{R}$ from $\bm{I}$). For each setting run finetuning and log: (1) HE differences per layer and aggregated, (2) downstream generation metrics (DINO, CLIP-I/T, LPIPS for subject-driven or control consistency for controllable tasks), and (3) training curves to examine stability and convergence speed.

Interpretation. Because any exact orthogonal $\bm{R}$ preserves pairwise angles, an ideal OFT implementation will show HE differences near zero (up to numerical precision). COFT adds an explicit constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le \epsilon$, which in practice is enforced by constraining the Cayley skew parameter $\bm{Q}$ (via the linearized approximation $\bm{R}\approx\bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$, the paper enforces $\|\bm{Q}\|\le\epsilon'$ and projects via projected gradient descent). The ablation should reveal the trade-off the paper describes: smaller blocks (larger $r$) reduce parameter count and can limit flexibility, while smaller $\epsilon$ keeps the finetuned weights close to pretrained ones (higher stability but lower task-specific flexibility). Visualize HE difference and downstream metrics as functions of $r$ and $\epsilon$ to make this trade-off explicit.

# Implement and apply control consistency metrics on C2I, S2I, L2F

The paper introduces a practical evaluation approach for controllable generation called control consistency: estimate the control signal from the generated image and compare it to the original control input. Implement and apply these metrics to evaluate how well finetuned models follow control inputs.

For Canny-edge-to-image (C2I) tasks, the control signal is an edge map. For each generated image run the same edge detector used to produce the input control (e.g., Canny with the same thresholds). Compute intersection-over-union (IoU) between the input edge map $E_{\text{in}}$ and the edge map recovered from the generated image $E_{\text{out}}$:
\[
\mathrm{IoU}=\frac{|E_{\text{in}}\cap E_{\text{out}}|}{|E_{\text{in}}\cup E_{\text{out}}|}.
\]
Also compute the F1 score where precision and recall are defined in the usual way in terms of the matching pixels between $E_{\text{in}}$ and $E_{\text{out}}$. These two metrics measure how faithfully the generator reproduced the input edge geometry.

For segmentation-to-image (S2I) tasks, the control is a segmentation map. For each generated image apply an off-the-shelf segmentation model to obtain a predicted segmentation map. Compute per-class IoUs and average them to get mean IoU. Compute per-class accuracy and average these to obtain mean accuracy, and compute overall pixelwise accuracy as
\[
\text{Overall Acc}=\frac{\sum_{p} \mathbb{1}\{ \hat{y}_p=y_p\}}{N_{\text{pixels}}}.
\]
Here $\hat{y}_p$ is the predicted class at pixel $p$ and $y_p$ is the input control class at pixel $p$. These are standard segmentation consistency metrics and capture whether the generated image contains the semantic regions implied by the input segmentation.

For landmark-to-face (L2F) tasks, the control is a set of facial landmark coordinates. Run a face landmark detector on the generated image to obtain predicted landmarks and compute the mean Euclidean distance between predicted and input control landmark coordinates, averaged over landmarks and images:
\[
\text{Mean L2}=\frac{1}{N_{\text{images}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{images}}}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\| \hat{\ell}_{i,k}-\ell_{i,k} \|_2.
\]
Lower values indicate better compliance with the control pose.

Implementation guidance. Use the same preprocessing, resolution, and detector thresholds for control extraction from generated images as used to produce the input control signals. For S2I and L2F tasks the paper used off-the-shelf models to estimate the recovered control signal; follow the same approach. Evaluate metrics over the full validation set for robust comparisons.

# Compose multiple OFT transforms and study interference

A theoretically attractive property is that the composition (matrix product) of orthogonal matrices is still orthogonal. This leads to an interesting empirical mini-project: finetune separate OFT transforms for two different tasks and study whether composing them retains each task's behavior, or whether the tasks interfere.

Concretely, start from the same pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$. Finetune with OFT on task A to obtain orthogonal transform $\bm{R}_A$ so that the finetuned weights are $\bm{W}_A=\bm{R}_A\bm{W}^0$. Independently finetune another copy on task B to obtain $\bm{R}_B$ and $\bm{W}_B$. Now consider the composed transform $\bm{R}_{\text{comp}}=\bm{R}_B\bm{R}_A$ and the composed weights
\[
\bm{W}_{\text{comp}}=\bm{R}_{\text{comp}}\bm{W}^0=(\bm{R}_B\bm{R}_A)\bm{W}^0.
\]
Because $\bm{R}_{\text{comp}}$ is orthogonal, hyperspherical energy is preserved relative to $\bm{W}^0$, but it is an open empirical question whether $\bm{W}_{\text{comp}}$ will simultaneously satisfy the requirements of both tasks.

Design experiments that compare three models on both tasks: (1) single-task $\bm{W}_A$ for task A and $\bm{W}_B$ for task B, (2) sequential finetuning where you finetune for task A and then finetune the result (starting from $\bm{W}_A$) for task B, and (3) composed transform $\bm{W}_{\text{comp}}$. Evaluate each model on the task-specific metrics (subject-driven metrics, control consistency metrics, etc.) and report how performance trades off. The paper mentions this compositionality as an open problem; your experiments will explore whether simple multiplication of orthogonal transforms yields a practical route to multi-tasking without catastrophic forgetting, or whether interference occurs.

Report both objective metrics and qualitative examples. Given the theoretical preservation of pairwise angles, analyze whether and how the two tasks are compatible in the same rotated coordinate system.

# Extend OFT to convolutional layers (depth-wise vs shared blocks)

Although OFT is described for fully connected layers, the paper highlights that OFT naturally extends to convolutional layers by interpreting the convolution kernel as a matrix whose columns are neurons. Concretely, a convolutional weight tensor shaped $(\text{out\_channels},\ \text{in\_channels},\ k, k)$ can be reshaped into a matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ with $d=\text{in\_channels}\cdot k\cdot k$ and $n=\text{out\_channels}$. Applying an orthogonal transform $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ to this matrix as $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ corresponds to rotating the input-kernel basis for all output channels.

Two interesting block strategies are particularly worth studying as mini-project variants. First, set the number of blocks $r$ equal to the number of input channels so that each block has size $k\cdot k$. Each block $\bm{R}_i$ then acts on the spatial kernel weights of one input channel only; this is analogous to an orthogonal depth-wise transform per input channel. Second, share the same orthogonal block among multiple channels so that the same spatial transform applies to several channels; this reduces the parameter count and imposes a different inductive bias.

Implement both variants: (a) reshape the convolutional kernels into a matrix, apply OFT with $r=\text{in\_channels}$ so that each block is small (depth-wise), (b) apply OFT with fewer blocks and shared blocks across input channels. Retrain or finetune on the same downstream tasks and compare both performance and parameter counts. The paper indicates that the depth-wise block setting maps naturally to depth-wise convolution interpretations and can be parameter-efficient.

Practical considerations. Reshaping and reassembling convolutional weights correctly is crucial. When converting back to convolutional weight shapes, ensure channel order and kernel layout match the original framework conventions (e.g., channel-first vs channel-last). Use block-diagonal Cayley transforms to avoid inverting a huge $(d\times d)$ matrix; operate on small per-block inverses. Measure both final downstream metrics and HE preservation to ensure orthogonality is maintained.

# Suggested project sequence, deliverables, and reporting

A pedagogically effective sequence is: first implement a correct OFT layer with Cayley and block-diagonal structure and verify that with $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ the model reproduces pretrained outputs. Second, reproduce the angle-vs-magnitude toy experiment to internalize why direction matters. Third, implement COFT projection and run small-scale finetuning comparisons between OFT, COFT, and LoRA on a toy diffusion UNet; plot metric curves and visualize samples across checkpoints. Fourth, conduct the $r$ and $\epsilon$ ablations and produce HE-preservation plots and downstream metric trade-off plots. Fifth, implement the control consistency metrics and evaluate on the C2I, S2I, and L2F settings. Sixth, explore composition of orthogonal transforms and extensions to convolutional layers.

For each mini-project deliver: (1) code for the implemented OFT/COFT layer(s), (2) a short experimental script that runs a chosen finetuning and evaluation protocol, (3) plots of training curves (HE difference and downstream metrics), and (4) representative qualitative samples illustrating success or failure modes. Where possible follow the paper's evaluation practices: for subject-driven generation the paper repeated finetuning across many random seeds (30) to reduce randomness; report mean and variance of metrics where feasible. For controllable generation, follow the paper's practice of reporting control consistency metrics such as IoU, F1, mean IoU, accuracy, and mean L2 landmark distance.

These exercises are intentionally open-ended so you can explore implementation variants and empirical trade-offs. The paper provides the guiding principles and the mathematical definitions you need: OFT's multiplicative orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$, Cayley parameterization $(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$, block-diagonal parameterization for efficiency, COFT's small-radius constraint implemented as a projection on $\bm{Q}$ using the approximation $\bm{R}\approx\bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$, and the hyperspherical energy
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
Following these steps will let you reproduce and extend the empirical findings in the paper, and will give hands-on experience with the trade-offs between flexibility and regularity that underlie OFT and COFT.
