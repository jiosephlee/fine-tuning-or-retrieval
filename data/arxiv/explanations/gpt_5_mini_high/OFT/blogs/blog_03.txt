\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# COFT: Putting Guardrails on Finetuning for Stable, Predictable Results

## TL;DR
Finetuning large text-to-image diffusion models is powerful but fragile: small changes can easily destroy pretrained generative ability. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) puts a simple, principled guardrail around finetuning: learn a layer-shared orthogonal transform but require it to stay within a small “radius” of the identity. This preserves the pairwise neuron geometry that encodes semantic ability, stabilizes training, makes hyperparameters (learning rate, number of iterations) easier to set, and still gives the model enough flexibility to adapt to tasks like subject-driven generation and controllable generation — all without adding inference cost.

## The problem in one paragraph
When you adapt a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model to a downstream task (e.g., inject a new subject from a few images or add an edge/segmentation control), you want two competing things: (1) enough flexibility to learn the new control, and (2) enough fidelity to preserve the pretrained model’s semantic generation ability. Standard finetuning or small additive adapters (e.g., LoRA) give some regularization, but none of them guarantee that the internal geometric relationships among neurons — which actually encode semantic knowledge — are preserved. That’s where orthogonal finetuning and its constrained variant, COFT, come in.

## Quick recap: why orthogonality and hyperspherical energy matter
A key empirical observation is that the directions (angles) of neuron weight vectors encode semantic information better than their magnitudes. The hyperspherical energy of a layer captures the pairwise geometry of normalized neurons on the unit hypersphere. For a weight matrix $W=\{w_1,\dots,w_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ with normalized neurons $\hat w_i = w_i/\|w_i\|$, the hyperspherical energy is
\[
\text{HE}(W) := \sum_{i\ne j} \|\hat w_i - \hat w_j\|^{-1}.
\]
Orthogonal transforms preserve angles, so if you replace $W^0$ (pretrained weights) by $R W^0$ with $R$ orthogonal, you keep the exact same hyperspherical energy:
\[
\text{HE}(R W^0) = \text{HE}(W^0).
\]
Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) leverages this: instead of directly changing weights additively or elementwise, OFT learns a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $R$ and uses
\[
z = (R W^0)^\top x,
\]
so the pairwise neuron geometry is preserved during adaptation.

## What COFT adds: a radius constraint as a guardrail
OFT preserves angles but can still produce large orthogonal rotations that shift the model far from the pretrained parameters. COFT adds an explicit constraint that the learned orthogonal transform stay close to identity:
\[
z = (R W^0)^\top x,\qquad R^\top R = I,\qquad \|R - I\| \le \epsilon.
\]
Intuition:
- The orthogonality keeps the pairwise neuron angles (and thus semantic structure) intact.
- The radius constraint $\|R - I\| \le \epsilon$ limits how far those rotations can move you from the pretrained coordinate frame, preventing destructive changes and avoiding collapse.
- The hyperparameter $\epsilon$ directly trades flexibility vs. conservatism: small $\epsilon$ ≈ pretrained behavior; large $\epsilon$ ≈ OFT.

The paper shows that this explicit radius constraint stabilizes finetuning and reduces sensitivity to learning rate and number of training iterations. With a suitable $\epsilon$, you can largely “set and forget” these other hyperparameters.

## How COFT is implemented (practical details)
Two practical issues need addressing: how to parametrize orthogonal $R$ efficiently, and how to enforce the radius constraint.

- Cayley parameterization. The paper uses the Cayley transform to parametrize orthogonal $R$:
  \[
  R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1},
  \]
  where $Q$ is skew-symmetric ($Q = -Q^\top$). Cayley produces orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$ (the special orthogonal group). Initializing $Q=0$ yields $R=I$, so COFT starts exactly at the pretrained model.

- Enforcing the radius constraint. The Cayley form is nonlinear, but by using a Neumann-series approximation one gets
  \[
  R \approx I + 2Q + \mathcal{O}(Q^2),
  \]
  so the constraint $\|R - I\| \le \epsilon$ can be approximately enforced as a bound on $\|Q\|$. That makes enforcement straightforward via projected gradient steps on $Q$ (the paper enforces a small $\|Q\| \le \epsilon'$ during training). Because $Q$ is initialized to zero, COFT naturally starts conservatively and can expand within the allowed radius.

- Parameter-efficiency via block-diagonal $R$. A full $d\times d$ orthogonal $R$ can be expensive. COFT uses block-diagonal $R=\operatorname{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r)$ to reduce the number of trainable parameters and the cost of the matrix inverse in Cayley. Blocks can also be shared across channels for further savings. This block structure preserves orthogonality within each block and thus still maintains hyperspherical energy invariance for neurons partitioned by block.

- No inference overhead. Once training finishes, you can multiply $R$ into $W^0$ to obtain $W = R W^0$ and discard the separate parametrization; inference uses the same compute as the original model.

## Why COFT improves stability and predictability
COFT is designed around two geometric facts:

1. Semantic information is encoded in neuron directions (angles), so preserving pairwise angles keeps the model’s semantic generation ability intact.
2. Small, structured rotations (orthogonal transforms near identity) give meaningful, controlled ways to adjust neuron directions without "breaking" the pretrained geometry.

Putting these together, COFT provides an explicit guardrail: you adapt directions where it matters, but only within a bounded neighborhood of the pretrained coordinate system. That reduces the chances of sudden model collapse or catastrophic overwrite of pretrained semantics, and it smooths training dynamics so convergence is faster and more predictable. Empirically (per the paper), COFT yielded more stable convergence than vanilla finetuning and adapter-based methods, and it was less sensitive to the number of finetuning iterations.

## Empirical signals (summary from the paper)
- Subject-driven generation: COFT and OFT showed much more stable convergence than DreamBooth and LoRA. They preserved subject identity and achieved better text fidelity while avoiding collapse even at larger iteration counts.
- Controllable generation: COFT converged faster than ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA on several control tasks (Canny edges → image, segmentation → image, landmark → face). COFT also scored better on the paper's “control consistency” metrics (e.g., IoU/F1 for C2I, mean IoU and accuracy for S2I, mean $\ell_2$ landmark error for L2F).
- Sample efficiency: COFT required fewer training images / fewer epochs to reach good control.
- Parameter efficiency: with block-diagonal parametrization COFT uses a modest number of extra parameters and compares favorably to LoRA in practical parameter budgets. (The paper gives concrete parameter-count comparisons for example layer sizes.)

All of these outcomes are a direct consequence of the orthogonality + radius constraints: enough directed flexibility to learn control, but constrained enough to keep pretrained semantics.

## Practical guidance (from the paper)
- Initialize $Q=0$ so $R=I$ and training starts from the pretrained model.
- Choose block size $r$ to trade off parameter cost and expressivity; block sharing reduces parameters further.
- Tune $\epsilon$ (or its proxy $\epsilon'$ on $Q$) to set the level of conservatism: if you see little adaptation, relax $\epsilon$; if you see instability, tighten it. The paper reports COFT is overall less sensitive to learning rate and iteration count once $\epsilon$ is set.
- Apply COFT to the same layers you would target with LoRA (e.g., attention weights) for fair comparison and comparable parameter budgets.
- No inference-time changes are required beyond multiplying $R$ into $W^0$ once training is done.

## Limitations and open problems
The paper notes several practical limitations and interesting directions:
- The Cayley transform requires a matrix inverse; block-diagonal structure alleviates but does not eliminate this cost. More scalable differentiable orthogonal parameterizations would help.
- Block-diagonal $R$ is a bias: it reduces parameters but also limits expressivity. Finding better parameter-efficient orthogonal parameterizations is open.
- Orthogonal matrices compose: if you finetune multiple tasks you can multiply their $R$’s and still get an orthogonal matrix. Whether this composition preserves performance on all tasks (and how to manage interference) is an interesting open question.

## Bottom line
COFT is a simple, geometrically principled way to put guardrails on finetuning: keep neuron angles (hyperspherical energy) intact while forcing rotations to stay in a small radius around identity. That combination stabilizes finetuning, reduces hyperparameter fiddling, accelerates convergence, and preserves pretrained semantic abilities — all with little extra parameter or inference cost. If you're finetuning large text-to-image diffusion models for personalized subjects or additional control signals, COFT is an appealing, easy-to-reason-about option to try.
