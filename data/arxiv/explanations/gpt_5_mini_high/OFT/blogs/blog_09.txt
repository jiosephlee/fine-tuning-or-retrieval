\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# Composing Skills by Multiplying Rotations: The Next Frontier of OFT

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) introduced a simple, principled lever for steering large text‑to‑image diffusion models: learn a layer‑shared orthogonal matrix \(R\) and apply it to pretrained weights \(W^0\) so that the finetuned weights are \(W = R W^0\). Because orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise angles among neurons, OFT provably preserves the model's hyperspherical energy — a structural quantity the paper argues is important for keeping the pretrained semantics intact. In this post I want to explore a natural, forward‑looking idea that the paper itself flags as promising: if each finetuning job produces an orthogonal matrix, we can compose skills simply by multiplying those matrices. Below I summarize what that composition buys you, what it doesn’t, and concrete technical questions and constraints you should care about when you try to turn OFT into a modular “Lego brick” toolchain.

## Quick recap: the math you need in your head
Two short facts from the paper that drive everything below:

- If \(R\) is orthogonal then \(R^\top R = I\). If a layer’s finetuned weights are \(W = R W^0\), then normalized neurons rotate as \(\hat w_i = R \hat w_i^0\), and all pairwise inner products are preserved:
  \[
  \langle \hat w_i, \hat w_j\rangle = \langle R\hat w_i^0, R\hat w_j^0\rangle = \langle \hat w_i^0, \hat w_j^0\rangle.
  \]
  Hence the hyperspherical energy \(\text{HE}(W)\) is unchanged.

- Orthogonal matrices compose: if \(R_1,\dots,R_k\in O(d)\) then
  \[
  R_{\text{comp}} = R_k \cdots R_1 \in O(d),
  \]
  and so \(R_{\text{comp}}\) also preserves hyperspherical energy.

These two observations are exactly why the paper highlights compositionality as an attractive open direction: the product of per‑task rotations is still a valid OFT transform.

## What composing rotations actually gives you
If you finetune separate skills independently and keep their learned orthogonal matrices \(\{R_s\}\), you can form a composed model that applies a selected set of skills by multiplying their \(R\)'s and folding the result into the base weights:
\[
R_{\text{composed}} = R_{s_m}\cdots R_{s_1}, \qquad W_{\text{composed}} = R_{\text{composed}} W^0.
\]
Two practical and useful consequences follow directly from the paper:

- No runtime overhead: once you compute \(W_{\text{composed}}\) you have a single weight matrix. This follows from the same folding trick the paper uses for single‑skill OFT: inference cost is identical to the pretrained model.
- Hyperspherical energy (and therefore the pairwise angular structure the paper argues encodes semantic generalization) remains preserved under composition because the composed transform is orthogonal.

This is the basic “Lego brick” picture: store small rotation bricks per skill and multiply them when you want a combination.

## Ordering, interference, and why composition is not magic
Matrices do not commute in general. Even though the product is orthogonal, \(R_a R_b \neq R_b R_a\) in general, so the sequence in which you compose skills will matter. That has three practical implications:

- Semantics can be order‑dependent. Applying a “style” rotation then a “pose” rotation might produce a different result than the reverse.
- Multiplying many nontrivial rotations can move the total transform far from identity. While each \(R_s\) individually preserves angles, their combined effect may be a large rotation that changes generation behavior in ways that were not anticipated when each skill was trained in isolation.
- Whether a composed rotation actually “retains” the quality of each individual skill (i.e., the composed model performs well on all constituent tasks) is an open empirical question. The paper explicitly raises this as an open problem.

The paper already provides a practical tool to control these effects: Constrained OFT (COFT), which enforces a radius constraint \(\|R - I\| \le \epsilon\) (implemented via a skew‑symmetric Cayley parameter \(Q\) with \(\|Q\|\) small). Training each skill with a small \(\epsilon\) keeps per‑skill rotations near identity, which makes their product less likely to “drift” and might reduce destructive interference when you compose many skills.

## Re‑scaled OFT: adding magnitude without breaking angles
The paper also considers a re‑scaled OFT variant that learns a diagonal magnitude matrix \(D=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)\) in addition to the rotation:
\[
z = (R W^0 D)^\top x.
\]
Because hyperspherical energy is computed on normalized neurons, scaling columns by positive \(s_i\) does not change the pairwise angular structure; the paper notes that rescaling therefore leaves HE invariant while giving extra flexibility.

How this impacts composition is subtle. Rotations compose cleanly (products remain orthogonal). Scaling matrices multiply elementwise on neuron columns, and a naïve composition rule for a stack of skills could be
\[
W_{\text{comp}} = \Big(\prod_{t} R_t\Big) W^0 \Big(\prod_{t} D_t\Big),
\]
i.e., compose rotations on the left and scalings on the right. The paper does not explore this design choice empirically, but it suggests an attractive engineering pattern: store per‑skill \(R_s\) and \(D_s\), compose rotations multiplicatively and aggregate scalings multiplicatively (or via a learned gating) at inference time. This keeps the angle‑preserving core of OFT while letting each skill modulate neuron magnitudes for finer control. Whether that combination reliably preserves multiple skills remains to be tested.

## Parameter efficiency and scaling constraints (practical realities)
The paper is clear about two bottlenecks and trade‑offs:

- Cayley parameterization is a convenient differentiable way to produce orthogonals, but it requires a matrix inverse and currently limits scalability. The authors handle this practically with a block‑diagonal parameterization.
- Block‑diagonal parameterization reduces trainable parameters: an \(r\)-block diagonal \(R\) has parameter complexity \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)\); sharing blocks across channels can further reduce complexity toward \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)\). But block structure introduces bias (less flexibility) and is part of the “flexibility vs regularity” trade‑off the paper emphasizes.

A concrete example from the paper: for a \(128\times 128\) weight matrix, LoRA with \(r'=8\) uses 2,048 trainable parameters, while OFT with \(r=8\) uses 960 parameters. So OFT is competitive in parameter efficiency, but storing many full \(R_s\) matrices for many skills will still add up. The block structure is the principal lever the paper offers for keeping a library of skill rotations compact.

## A short list of concrete experiments to try next
The paper frames compositionality as an open problem. If you want to explore it, here are compact, paper‑grounded experiments that test the key hypotheses:

- Skill composition sanity check. Finetune two skills independently to get \(R_a\) and \(R_b\). Compare the single‑skill models \(R_a W^0\) and \(R_b W^0\) to the composed model \(R_b R_a W^0\). Evaluate with the same metrics the paper uses (e.g., subject fidelity, control consistency).
- Ordering sensitivity. Evaluate \(R_b R_a\) vs \(R_a R_b\) on the same inputs to quantify how much order matters.
- COFT as a composition regularizer. Train skills with COFT for different \(\epsilon\) and measure whether smaller \(\epsilon\) improves composed performance (less interference) at the expense of per‑skill accuracy.
- Re‑scaled OFT composition. Store \((R_s,D_s)\) per skill and test composition rules (e.g., multiply all \(R_s\) and all \(D_s\)). Measure whether combined scalings harm angle preservation or improve multi‑skill fidelity.
- Block‑size sweep. For a fixed set of skills, vary the block count \(r\) and block‑sharing strategy to map the trade‑off between parameter savings and compositional quality.

These are all natural extensions of the paper’s experimental setup — the same Stable Diffusion v1.5 backbone and the same control‑consistency metrics apply.

## High‑level design patterns for a future OFT toolchain
Taking all of the above together, here are a few practical patterns you can build toward:

- Skill library: store per‑layer, per‑skill block‑diagonal Cayley parameters (or \(R_s\) after folding), plus optional diagonal \(D_s\) scalings.
- Compose at load time: pick an ordered list of skills, multiply their \(R\)'s to produce a single \(R_{\text{composed}}\), fold into \(W^0\), and run with zero extra inference overhead.
- Use COFT for multi‑skill robustness: prefer training skills with a constrained radius \(\epsilon\) so compositions remain well behaved.
- Make order explicit: expose skill sequencing in the UI/tooling because composition is generally order‑sensitive.

## Open problems the paper highlights (and why they matter)
The paper calls out, and the composition idea makes more urgent, these unresolved issues:

- Efficient, differentiable orthogonal parameterizations without expensive inverses. Cayley works but can be a bottleneck; block‑diagonalization helps but is a blunt tool.
- Do orthogonal matrices composed post‑hoc preserve all downstream task knowledge? There is no theoretical guarantee that independently optimized \(R_s\) will combine cleanly.
- Parameter efficiency beyond block structure: block‑diagonalization introduces inductive bias. How to represent a rich set of rotations compactly without that bias is open.

Each of these is central to making OFT a practical, scalable system for many skills.

## Closing thoughts
The algebra is simple and elegant: rotations compose, and preserving neuron angles is precisely the inductive bias OFT brings to finetuning. That algebra makes a genuinely new workflow plausible: train small, cheap, angle‑preserving transforms for individual skills, and compose them at inference to assemble multi‑skill models on demand. The paper gives the starting toolkit — OFT, COFT, and re‑scaled OFT — and clearly lays out the parameterization and stability trade‑offs. The next steps are empirical and engineering ones: find parameterizations that scale, measure how composed rotations interact, and build the tooling that lets practitioners pick and sequence "rotation bricks" the way creative users pick stickers in a GUI. If it works, we get a modular, low‑overhead way to teach large generative models many complementary abilities and assemble them like Lego. The payoff would be a compact, interpretable library of skills you can multiply together — literally — to create new capabilities.
