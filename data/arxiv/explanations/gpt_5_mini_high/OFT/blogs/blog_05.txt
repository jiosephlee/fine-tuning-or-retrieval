\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# OFT vs. LoRA vs. ControlNet: When to Use What for Text‑to‑Image Control

Text-to-image diffusion models (Stable Diffusion, Imagen, DALL·E 2, etc.) are great at global text guidance, but real-world use often needs stronger, more reliable control: personalizing a subject from a handful of photos, following an edge/segmentation map, or enforcing a pose/landmark layout. Three practical finetuning strategies are commonly discussed today:

- LoRA – low-rank additive updates to weights;
- ControlNet / T2I‑Adapter – separate adapter modules trained to inject control signals;
- OFT (Orthogonal Finetuning) – a multiplicative, orthogonal transform applied to pretrained neurons (this is the method introduced and studied in the paper summarized here).

Below I summarize how these approaches differ, why OFT works for diffusion finetuning, and practical advice on which method to pick for subject personalization, edge/segmentation control and pose tasks.

## TL;DR
- If you need stable, sample‑efficient finetuning that preserves the pretrained model’s semantic behavior and adds no inference overhead, OFT (or its constrained variant COFT) is often the best choice.
- If you need modular adapters you can enable/disable at inference or you prefer not to modify base weights, ControlNet / T2I‑Adapter are natural — but expect more parameters, slower/stagey convergence and extra inference cost.
- If you want a simple, widely supported, parameter‑efficient transformer trick, LoRA is a sensible baseline, but in the diffusion finetuning experiments in the paper it was less stable and less effective at preserving subject identity and control fidelity.

All observations below come from the comparisons performed in the paper on Stable Diffusion v1.5 across subject‑driven (DreamBooth style) and controllable generation (Canny/Segmentation/Landmark) tasks.

## High‑level differences (what they do, mathematically)
- LoRA assumes the weight update during finetuning is low‑rank, i.e. it parameterizes $M - M^0$ with small matrices so the update is additive and parameter‑efficient. This limits the update’s degrees of freedom but does not enforce any explicit geometric constraint on neuron directions.

- ControlNet / T2I‑Adapter add small neural network modules or conditioning adapters that process the control signal and influence the diffusion model. They are explicit conditioning architectures: extra parameters and extra computation at test time.

- OFT applies a layer‑shared orthogonal transformation to the pretrained neurons. For a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ (columns are neurons) OFT parameterizes the finetuned weights as
  $$
  \bm{W} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0,
  $$
  with $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$. The forward pass becomes $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. Because orthogonal transforms preserve angles between normalized neurons, OFT provably preserves a structural measure called hyperspherical energy.

## Why OFT — intuition and the core invariant
The paper’s key hypothesis is that neuron directions (angles) encode most of the semantic structure a pretrained generator learned. Two motivating facts used in the paper:

1. In a toy autoencoder experiment the angular information of convolutional neurons preserved image semantics much better than magnitudes — suggesting directions matter more for semantic content.

2. Hyperspherical energy (HE) captures the pairwise relational structure among normalized neurons:
   $$
   \mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \big\| \hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j \big\|^{-1},\qquad \hat{\bm{w}}_i=\frac{\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{w}_i\|}.
   $$
   Any orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$ preserves all pairwise angles among normalized neurons, so $\mathrm{HE}(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0) = \mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$.

Thus OFT seeks to adjust neuron directions (what semantically changes images) while avoiding destructive changes to the pretrained directional geometry. COFT (Constrained OFT) adds an explicit small‑deviation constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$ (implemented approximately via the Cayley parameterization) to limit how far the transform can move away from identity.

Practical parameterizations used in the paper:
- Cayley transform to enforce orthogonality: $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$.
- Block‑diagonal $\bm{R}$ to reduce parameter count (and induce further regularization).
- Optional re‑scaled OFT: learn a diagonal scaling $\bm{D}$ so forward pass is $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$.

Initialize $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ (so $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$) to start from the pretrained model exactly.

## Practical tradeoffs: parameters, convergence, inference
- Parameter efficiency: LoRA scales with the chosen low rank $r'$ (parameters $\propto r'(d+n)$ for a $d\times n$ weight), OFT scales with the orthogonal parameterization. Using block‑diagonal and block sharing, OFT can be competitive; example from the paper: for a $128\times128$ weight, LoRA with $r'=8$ uses $2{,}048$ trainable params while OFT with $r=8$ uses $960$ params.

- Convergence and stability: OFT and COFT converge faster and more stably in the paper’s experiments. For example, on the landmark‑to‑face (L2F) task LoRA needed 20 epochs to reach what OFT achieved by epoch 8. In subject personalization (DreamBooth setting) OFT/COFT preserved subject identity more robustly across many training iterations; DreamBooth and LoRA were more sensitive and in some cases produced collapses or identity degradation.

- Inference overhead: OFT introduces no extra runtime cost because the learned orthogonal transform can be folded into the pretrained weights ($\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$). ControlNet / T2I‑Adapter add modules that run at inference and therefore increase latency and memory footprint. (This difference can be decisive for deployment on constrained devices or for high‑throughput services.)

- Control fidelity: the paper introduced a control‑consistency evaluation (e.g., IoU/F1 for edge maps, mIoU/accuracy for segmentation, landmark error for poses). Across Canny edge → image, segmentation → image and landmark → face tasks, OFT/COFT achieved stronger and more accurate control than the baselines in the experiments.

## Which method to pick for common tasks

Subject personalization (DreamBooth style)
- Recommended: OFT or COFT.
- Why: OFT preserves the pretrained model’s directional geometry (hyperspherical energy) so subject identity and generalization are retained. COFT adds explicit radius control ($\epsilon$) if you want an even safer, less flexible finetune. In the paper OFT/COFT outperformed DreamBooth and LoRA on identity metrics (DINO, CLIP‑I) and were less sensitive to the number of finetuning iterations.

Edge / segmentation control (C2I / S2I)
- Recommended: OFT.
- Why: OFT converged faster and gave higher control consistency scores with fewer training examples and epochs compared to ControlNet and T2I‑Adapter. It also produced high‑fidelity images and can be deployed with no extra inference modules.

Pose / landmark‑to‑face (L2F)
- Recommended: OFT (or COFT if you want tight safety).
- Why: Faster, smoother convergence and more accurate pose alignment were observed in the paper. ControlNet achieved similar final accuracy in some settings but required more epochs and produced a “sudden” emergence of controllability; OFT’s convergence was steadier and more predictable.

When you might prefer ControlNet / T2I‑Adapter
- You want modularity: attach multiple separate adapters for different control types and enable/disable them at inference without rewriting base weights.
- You cannot or do not want to modify the base model weights (organizational or product constraints).
- You accept extra inference cost in exchange for a separate control module.

When LoRA is a good fit
- You need an extremely simple, widely supported low‑rank finetuning option (particularly common in transformer NLP workflows).
- LoRA remains parameter‑efficient and easy to plug into existing codebases; but in the diffusion finetuning experiments in this paper it was less robust at preserving subject identity and control fidelity than OFT.

## Implementation tips and practical knobs
- Where to apply OFT: in the paper, OFT was applied to the same layers people often tune with LoRA (e.g., attention weights) for fair comparison. OFT is architecture‑agnostic and can be used on fully connected, convolutional, or Transformer blocks.
- Block size / parameter budget: use block‑diagonal $\bm{R}$ to trade flexibility for parameter efficiency. Sharing blocks reduces parameters further.
- Initialization & safety: initialize the Cayley parameter $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ so $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ and you begin exactly at the pretrained model. If you are worried about unwanted drift, use COFT’s $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$ constraint (implemented by projecting $\bm{Q}$ in the Cayley parameterization).
- Optional: re‑scaled OFT (learn diagonal $\bm{D}$) gives extra flexibility at negligible parameter cost.
- Monitoring: because OFT preserves hyperspherical energy by construction, it is less necessary to tune extremely small learning rates, but you should monitor your usual validation metrics (CLIP/DINO/LPIPS or the paper’s control consistency metrics) to decide training length.

## Caveats and open questions
- Enforcing orthogonality via the Cayley transform involves matrix inverse operations; block‑diagonal structure helps but the method can still be more expensive to compute during training than purely additive low‑rank updates. The paper lists more efficient differentiable orthogonalization as an open problem.
- OFT’s parameter efficiency depends on the block‑diagonal design; that bias limits flexibility compared to unconstrained updates. Finding better parameter‑efficient parametrizations without inducing strong biases is open.
- Compositionality: orthogonal matrices multiply to orthogonal matrices, so in principle OFT finetunings from multiple tasks could be composed. Whether that preserves multiple downstream behaviors in practice is an open direction called out by the authors.

## Bottom line
If your goal is to finetune a text‑to‑image diffusion model for subject personalization or for accurate, deployable control (edges, segmentation, pose) while keeping inference cost minimal and getting fast, stable convergence, OFT / COFT is a very compelling option. ControlNet/T2I‑Adapter still make sense when you want modular adapters or cannot modify base weights. LoRA is a lightweight baseline that is simple and broadly supported, but in the diffusion finetuning experiments reported in the paper it was less reliable at preserving pretrained semantics and control fidelity than OFT.

If you want to try OFT: start with identity initialization, a block‑diagonal Cayley parameterization for $\bm{R}$, and COFT’s small $\epsilon$ if you prefer conservative updates. Monitor your control consistency metrics and CLIP/DINO identity metrics — OFT’s stability usually makes hyperparameter selection much easier than naive finetuning.
