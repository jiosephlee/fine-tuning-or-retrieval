\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# Orthogonal Finetuning: A Gentle Introduction to Keeping Generative Models on Track

Large text-to-image diffusion models—Stable Diffusion, Imagen, GLIDE and friends—are remarkably good at turning words into images. But when you want to adapt such a model for a specific downstream purpose (generate a particular person from a handful of photos, or make the model obey an extra control signal like edges or segmentation), ordinary finetuning can be fragile: too much change and the model loses its pretrained "visual common sense"; too little change and it won't learn the new behavior.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) offers a simple, principled middle path: instead of rewriting weight values, finetune by rotating neuron directions. The rotation is implemented as a small, layer-shared orthogonal transform applied to the pretrained weights. That single change preserves the relationships among neurons that encode semantics, while still letting the model adapt to new tasks. The method is fast, stable, parameter-efficient, and—crucially—does not add runtime cost at inference.

Below I walk through the core ideas, the intuition, how it’s implemented, and what it means in practice.

## The adaptation problems we care about

Two common text-to-image finetuning tasks motivate OFT:

- Subject-driven generation: give the model a few images of a subject (a person, an object) and teach it to generate that subject in new scenes and poses (DreamBooth style).
- Controllable generation: teach the model to respect additional control signals such as Canny edges, segmentation maps, or facial landmarks (ControlNet / T2I-Adapter style).

Both tasks require changing the model just enough to get the new behavior, while preserving its pretrained generative ability (photorealism, compositionality, diversity).

## The core idea: rotate, don’t rewrite

Neural weights can be thought of as vectors (neurons) in high-dimensional space. OFT changes a layer by multiplying the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$:

$ \bm{z} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}, \quad \text{with } \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}. $

Because orthogonal matrices preserve angles and lengths, applying the same orthogonal transform to all neurons in a layer preserves all pairwise angles (and thus certain geometric relationships) among neurons. Those pairwise relationships are closely tied to the model’s semantic knowledge; keeping them intact helps preserve the pretrained model’s generalization and "visual common sense."

In short: instead of adding an unconstrained delta to weights, OFT learns layer-wise rotations that nudge neuron directions in a controlled, structure-preserving way.

## Why angles (directions) matter

Two intuitions from the paper explain why rotating directions is a reasonable way to finetune:

1. Angular features carry semantics. Prior work and a small experiment in the paper show that the angular part of neuron activations often encodes semantic structure far better than magnitudes. An autoencoder toy shows that keeping angular relationships can preserve image content even when magnitudes are scrambled.

2. The 2D Fourier analogy. In images, the phase (angular) spectrum preserves recognizable structure much more than the magnitude spectrum. By analogy, changing neuron directions (phase) is a natural way to alter semantics without destroying the model’s learned structure.

Together these points motivate finetuning methods that adjust directions over changing absolute magnitudes arbitrarily.

## A structural objective: hyperspherical energy

To formalize what "preserve structure" means, OFT uses a measure called hyperspherical energy (HE). For a weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, let $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ be the normalized neuron directions. The hyperspherical energy is

$ \text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}. $

HE captures the global pairwise angular structure (roughly, how uniformly or tightly neurons are arranged on the unit hypersphere). OFT enforces that the finetuned layer keep the same HE as the pretrained layer, which is achieved by adopting an orthogonal transform: if $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0$ with orthogonal $\bm{R}$, then all pairwise angles (and thus HE) are preserved exactly.

Formally, OFT is motivated by minimizing $\|\text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\|$, and a feasible zero is obtained by orthogonal $\bm{R}$.

## Practical implementation: making rotations cheap and stable

Learning a full orthogonal $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ directly is expensive, so the paper uses a few engineering choices:

- Cayley parameterization. Represent orthogonal $\bm{R}$ as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric ($\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$). This enforces orthogonality in a differentiable way.
- Block-diagonal structure. $\bm{R}$ can be set as block-diagonal $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$, which dramatically reduces the number of parameters and computation while keeping orthogonality within each block. Blocks can be shared too.
- Identity initialization. Initialize $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ so the finetuning starts exactly from the pretrained model.
- No inference overhead. At inference time you can fold $\bm{R}$ into the pretrained weights, producing $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ and running the model at the same speed as before.

These choices strike a balance: enough flexibility to learn new behavior, but enough structure to avoid destroying the pretrained model.

## A stability knob: Constrained OFT (COFT)

If you want even safer finetuning, COFT adds an explicit constraint that the orthogonal matrix stays near identity:

$ \|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \le \epsilon. $

Using the Cayley form and the Neumann approximation $\bm{R}\approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$, this can be enforced by constraining $\bm{Q}$ (e.g., via projected gradient). COFT makes the deviation from the pretrained model explicit and tunable, giving an easy way to control the flexibility-vs-stability trade-off.

## A small extension: re-scaled OFT

OFT preserves angles but not per-neuron magnitudes. The paper also considers a re-scaled variant that learns a diagonal scaling $\bm{D}=\text{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ together with $\bm{R}$:

$ \bm{z} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}. $

Scaling does not change hyperspherical energy (because HE uses normalized vectors), but it provides a small extra degree of freedom when needed.

## How OFT compares to other finetuning approaches

- Standard finetuning: update all weights with a tiny learning rate. This can be effective but is sensitive, slow, and can easily overfit or collapse.
- LoRA (low-rank additive updates): add a low-rank matrix $\Delta\bm{W}$ to $\bm{W}^0$ so $\bm{W}=\bm{W}^0+\Delta\bm{W}$. This constrains updates additively with a low-rank assumption.
- Adapter methods (ControlNet, T2I-Adapter): add extra modules or branches that bring controllability but increase inference complexity.

OFT is multiplicative: $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, which can be rewritten as $\bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$—so the effective update is still structured but constrained by orthogonality. Compared with LoRA, OFT tends to:

- Preserve pretrained semantic structure better (because it preserves pairwise angles).
- Converge faster and more stably in the authors’ experiments.
- Require similar or fewer trainable parameters (block-diagonal $\bm{R}$ gives good efficiency).
- Add no extra inference-time modules.

## Empirical highlights (taken from the paper)

The authors finetuned Stable Diffusion v1.5 across two settings: subject-driven generation (DreamBooth-style) and controllable generation (Canny edges, segmentation, landmarks on COCO / ADE20K / CelebA-HQ). Key empirical takeaways reported in the paper:

- OFT and COFT produce better final generation quality and preserve subject identity better than DreamBooth and LoRA in the subject-driven setting.
- OFT / COFT converge faster and more stably than LoRA, ControlNet, and T2I-Adapter in controllable generation tasks. The paper reports an example where LoRA reaches OFT’s performance at epoch 20, while OFT reaches it by epoch 8.
- OFT is more sample efficient: the authors note it can converge well with only 5% of ADE20K in a control task.
- The paper also introduces a control-consistency metric (estimate the control signal from the generated image and compare it to the input control) to quantify controllability; OFT performs strongly on this metric.

These results suggest that rotating neurons is an effective and efficient way to adapt large pretrained diffusion models for structured downstream control.

## What this means for creators and developers

- Faster personalization: OFT can teach a model a new subject or control behavior with fewer training steps and fewer images than many baselines.
- More stable finetuning: preserving neuron geometry makes training less sensitive to hyperparameters like number of iterations and learning rate; COFT gives an explicit safety knob ($\epsilon$).
- No runtime penalty: the learned orthogonal transform can be folded into the model weights so inference cost is unchanged.
- Easy adoption: OFT is model-agnostic in principle—it applies to fully connected, attention, and convolutional layers (the block-diagonal view maps naturally to channel-wise transforms).
- Trade-offs: use smaller blocks or smaller $\epsilon$ for safety and parameter efficiency; enlarge them when you need more flexibility.

## Limitations and open questions

The method is promising but not without issues the paper calls out:

- Cayley parameterization requires a matrix inverse and generates orthogonals with determinant $+1$, which slightly restricts the parameterization and has scalability costs. The block-diagonal trick helps but is a heuristic.
- Block-diagonal structure introduces bias: fewer parameters but less flexibility. Finding better parameter-efficient orthogonal parameterizations is open.
- Compositionality of multiple orthogonal edits (multiplying orthogonal matrices from different finetuning steps) is an intriguing direction: will a single product of orthogonals preserve knowledge from many tasks?
- How to further reduce the computational overhead of enforcing orthogonality in very large layers is an implementation challenge.

## Final thoughts

Orthogonal Finetuning reframes finetuning as a controlled rotation of neuron directions rather than an unconstrained rewrite of weights. That small conceptual shift—enforce geometry instead of suppressing parameter drift—turns out to be powerful: it preserves the pretrained model’s semantic structure (the model’s “visual common sense”) while giving enough flexibility to learn subject-specific or control-guided behavior. For practitioners who want robust, fast, and parameter-efficient finetuning of text-to-image diffusion models, OFT (and its constrained sibling COFT) are practical ideas worth trying.

If you want to experiment: start from attention or MLP layers where LoRA is usually applied, initialize $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, use block-diagonal Cayley parameterization, and try COFT with a modest $\epsilon$ as a safety-first approach. The paper’s experiments used Stable Diffusion v1.5 and demonstrate strong, stable gains across both personalization and control tasks.
