\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# Personalizing Stable Diffusion with Only a Few Photos

Personalizing a large text-to-image model like Stable Diffusion from just a handful of photos is now a common and powerful workflow: give the model a few images of a person or object, finetune it, and then ask for new images of that subject in novel scenes via text prompts. The popular DreamBooth recipe does exactly this, but in practice naive finetuning can be fragile — identity details slip, training can collapse if you run too long, and hyperparameter tuning is tedious.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a simple principled twist on finetuning, tackles these problems. Applied inside the DreamBooth-style pipeline, OFT preserves the pretrained model’s semantic generation ability while giving you faster, more stable personalization that better preserves identity and follows prompts. Below I explain the idea, why it helps, and how it was used in the paper to improve subject-driven generation on Stable Diffusion.

## The core idea — preserve neuron angles, not just weights

Most finetuning methods try to keep the finetuned weights close to pretrained weights in Euclidean space. OFT takes a different structural view: it preserves the pairwise angular relationships among neurons in each layer. The intuition is that neuron directions (angles on the unit sphere) encode semantic information; preserving their relative angles keeps the model’s pretrained generative knowledge intact while letting you rotate that representation to encode a new subject.

Formally, OFT is designed to keep the hyperspherical energy of a layer unchanged. For a weight matrix $W = [w_1,\dots,w_n]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, let $\hat w_i = w_i / \|w_i\|$ and define the hyperspherical energy
$$
\text{HE}(W) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat w_i - \hat w_j\|^{-1}.
$$
OFT aims to finetune without changing this quantity, i.e. minimize $\|\text{HE}(W) - \text{HE}(W^0)\|$ where $W^0$ is the pretrained layer. A sufficient way to achieve that is to apply a global orthogonal transform to the layer:
$$
W = R W^0, \quad\text{with}\quad R^\top R = I.
$$
An orthogonal $R$ preserves all pairwise angles between neuron vectors, so the hyperspherical energy is unchanged.

## Why rotating neurons (angles) makes sense

Two complementary intuitions motivate this choice.

First, empirical work shows angular relationships of features encode semantics well: normalizing neurons to the unit sphere often preserves representational power. The paper also includes a toy autoencoder experiment: when reconstructing images, the angular component of neuron responses preserves most of the semantic information while magnitudes contribute little. This suggests changing neuron directions is the most effective way to change semantic behavior (e.g., to inject subject identity).

Second, orthogonality constrains the finetuning space. A fully unconstrained change to directions is flexible but dangerous: it can easily destroy pretrained structure. An orthogonal transform is a compact, smooth, full-rank way to change directions without scrambling the relational structure among neurons. In practice that gives faster, more stable finetuning.

A convenient analogy is the 2D Fourier decomposition: the phase (angle) of the spectrum carries most semantic content; changing phase changes semantics but preserving relative phase structure keeps consistent content. OFT operates on neuron directions in a similar spirit.

## Implementing OFT efficiently

A few practical details make OFT usable on large models:

- Parameterize the orthogonal matrix $R$ with the Cayley transform: $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ where $Q$ is skew-symmetric ($Q^\top = -Q$). This enforces orthogonality during training while letting us optimize a compact parameter $Q$.
- Use block-diagonal $R$ to reduce parameter count. Partition the neuron dimension $d$ into $r$ blocks and learn smaller orthogonal blocks $\{R_1,\dots,R_r\}$. Block sharing can shrink parameters further.
- Initialize $R=I$ by starting with $Q=0$ so finetuning begins exactly from the pretrained model.
- Optionally learn a positive diagonal rescaling $D=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ and use $W = R W^0 D$ (re-scaled OFT) to give mild extra flexibility. The paper found re-scaled OFT usually helps, but the basic orthogonal idea alone already provides strong gains.

A concrete parameter-efficiency illustration from the paper: for a $128\times128$ weight, LoRA with rank $r'=8$ uses 2,048 trainable parameters, while OFT with $r=8$ blocks uses 960 parameters in the example — OFT is competitive in parameter budget while offering a different inductive bias.

## Constrained OFT (COFT) for extra stability

To further stabilize finetuning, the paper proposes Constrained OFT (COFT), which restricts how far $R$ can deviate from the identity:
$$
\|R - I\| \le \epsilon.
$$
Using the Cayley parameterization and a Neumann-series approximation $R\approx I + 2Q + \mathcal{O}(Q^2)$, this constraint can be enforced approximately as a small-norm constraint on $Q$ (projected gradient updates). COFT thus combines the angle-preserving orthogonality constraint with an explicit radius constraint around the pretrained model, producing an even more conservative and stable personalization.

## Why OFT helps DreamBooth-style personalization

Applied to subject-driven generation (the DreamBooth setting: personalize a text-to-image model from a few photos of a subject), OFT delivers several concrete benefits demonstrated in the paper:

- Better identity preservation. OFT and COFT keep subject-specific details more reliably than both naive DreamBooth finetuning and LoRA-style low-rank updates. In qualitative examples, LoRA sometimes loses identity (e.g., generating a wrong face or fur instead of the target clothing), whereas OFT keeps identity faithful while varying context per prompts.
- Faster and more stable convergence. The paper reports OFT variants reach good control quickly (e.g., by a few hundred iterations), while other methods are slower or unstable. In long runs, naive DreamBooth can start to collapse and LoRA may fail to preserve identity; OFT and COFT remain stable even with many iterations, making the iteration count much less sensitive.
- Stronger metrics for subject fidelity. The authors evaluated subject fidelity with metrics like DINO and CLIP image similarity (CLIP-I). Both OFT and COFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA on those identity metrics by a noticeable margin, while matching or slightly improving prompt fidelity (CLIP-text) and diversity (LPIPS).
- Sample efficiency. OFT converges with fewer training images and epochs in the experiments, meaning you can get a usable personalized model from only a handful of photos and modest compute.

A practical bonus: OFT introduces no extra inference overhead. After training, you can multiply the learned $R$ into the pretrained weights to obtain the final $W=R W^0$ and run exactly the same model at the same runtime cost.

## Practical recipe (what to try)

If you want to personalize Stable Diffusion in a DreamBooth-style setup and try OFT, follow the approach used in the paper:

- Base model: start from a pretrained Stable Diffusion checkpoint (the authors used v1.5).
- Loss / pipeline: keep the DreamBooth reconstruction + prior-preservation losses the same so comparisons are apples-to-apples.
- Where to apply OFT: for fairness to adapter methods like LoRA, the experiments applied OFT to the same attention-weight matrices LoRA targets. OFT can also be applied to other layers (MLP / conv), but attention is a common starting point.
- Parameterization: use the Cayley transform for orthogonality, block-diagonal $R$ to control parameter count, initialize $Q=0$ so $R=I$ initially.
- Stability tip: try COFT (small $\epsilon$) if you want an easier-to-tune, very stable personalization process. COFT makes learning-rate and iteration-count choices less critical.
- Optional: try the re-scaled OFT variant if you want additional flexibility at small parameter cost.

## Limitations and open problems

OFT is a principled and practical finetuning scheme, but it has trade-offs and open issues:

- Cayley transform requires a matrix inverse; block-diagonalization mitigates but does not remove this overhead. Scaling orthogonal parameterizations efficiently to very large layers is still an engineering challenge.
- The block-diagonal structure reduces parameters but adds a bias that may limit ultimate expressivity; finding better parameter-efficient orthogonal parameterizations is an open problem.
- The paper highlights an intriguing compositionality idea: orthogonal transforms from multiple personalization tasks can be multiplied together and remain orthogonal — whether this composes in practice to preserve multiple subjects or tasks is a direction for future work.

## Takeaway

If your goal is to personalize Stable Diffusion from only a few photos and you want results that reliably preserve identity, follow prompts, and train quickly without brittle hyperparameter tuning, Orthogonal Finetuning (and its constrained variant COFT) is a method worth trying. It shifts the finetuning objective away from raw weight drift toward preserving the angular geometry of neuron representations, and that structural inductive bias pays off in more stable, sample-efficient DreamBooth-style personalization — with no inference penalty.
