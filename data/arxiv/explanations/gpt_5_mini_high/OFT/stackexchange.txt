\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### What exactly is "hyperspherical energy" in OFT—cosine-sum or inverse-distance potential?
Question:
In the OFT paper, hyperspherical energy is first described as the sum of hyperspherical similarity (e.g., cosine similarity) between all neuron pairs. But later it is defined as \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i\ne j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}\) with \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \mathbf{w}_i / \|\mathbf{w}_i\|\). For unit vectors, cosine similarity and Euclidean distance are related by \(\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^2 = 2(1 - \cos \theta_{ij})\). Are the authors using the inverse-distance potential as the actual HE, or are both forms used interchangeably? If inverse-distance is the real objective/invariant, can you explain why this choice (as opposed to a direct sum over cosines) and whether the invariance arguments rely on this specific form?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper’s formal definition (and what they preserve) is the inverse-distance Riesz-type potential, not a raw sum of cosines. The earlier phrase “sum of hyperspherical similarity (e.g., cosine similarity)” is an informal description of the idea that hyperspherical pairwise relations matter. For unit-normalized neurons the two views are tightly related but not identical; the invariance argument, however, only needs that the quantity depends solely on pairwise angles/distances, so orthogonal transforms preserve it regardless of which of those equivalent-angle functions you use.

Explanation and math grounded in the paper. In Eq. (1) the authors define
$$
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W})=\sum_{i\ne j}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1},
\qquad \hat{\mathbf{w}}_i=\mathbf{w}_i/\|\mathbf{w}_i\|.
$$
For unit vectors the distance and cosine are related by
$$
\|\hat{w}_i-\hat{w}_j\|^2 = 2(1-\cos\theta_{ij}),
$$
so the HE used in the paper can be written as
$$
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W})=\sum_{i\ne j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1-\cos\theta_{ij})}}.
$$
Thus the inverse-distance potential is a specific (nonlinear, singular) function of the pairwise cosines.

Why that choice and what it implies. The inverse-distance (Riesz) energy is standard in the “hyperspherical energy” literature the paper cites: it behaves like a repulsive potential that blows up when two points coincide, so it strongly penalizes collapse/near-coincidence and emphasizes uniform spreading on the sphere. By contrast a direct sum of cosines, $\sum_{i\ne j}\cos\theta_{ij}$, is a simpler, bounded statistic (linear in inner products) and is less sensitive to very small separations. So the two are related (monotone in each pairwise $\cos\theta_{ij}$ for unit vectors) but have different numerical/gradient behavior; choosing the inverse-distance ties the paper to the Riesz-energy notion of “energy” and to its repulsive interpretation.

What the invariance argument actually uses. Orthogonal matrices preserve inner products and Euclidean norms: if $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$ and $\hat{w}'_i=\mathbf{R}\hat{w}_i$ then
$$
(\hat{w}'_i)^\top(\hat{w}'_j)=\hat{w}_i^\top\hat{w}_j,\qquad
\|\hat{w}'_i-\hat{w}'_j\|=\|\hat{w}_i-\hat{w}_j\|.
$$
So any quantity that depends only on pairwise angles or distances (including the cosine-sum or the inverse-distance sum) is preserved by the same orthogonal transform. Therefore the paper’s key claim — that a single layer-shared orthogonal transform preserves the hyperspherical pairwise relations — does not rely on the special algebraic form $1/\|\hat{w}_i-\hat{w}_j\|$; it only requires that the measure is a function of pairwise angles/distances. Practically, the authors choose and state the inverse-distance form (Eq. (1)) because it is the conventional hyperspherical energy and has the desirable repulsive/singular properties discussed above.

A final practical note: OFT does not try to minimize $\mathrm{HE}$; it enforces preservation by parameterizing weight updates as orthogonal transforms (so $\mathrm{HE}$ is preserved exactly). If instead one used $\mathrm{HE}$ as a soft regularizer, the choice between inverse-distance versus cosine-sum would matter a lot for optimization dynamics (because the gradients and sensitivity to near-collisions differ).

### Why does left-multiplying weights by an orthogonal matrix preserve pairwise neuron angles?
Question:
The paper considers a layer with weights \(\mathbf{W}^0 = [\mathbf{w}_1^0,\dots,\mathbf{w}_n^0] \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) and applies OFT as \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0\) where \(\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is orthogonal. I want to verify that for normalized columns \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \mathbf{w}_i/\|\mathbf{w}_i\|\), the pairwise angles are preserved, i.e., \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j = (\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0})^\top (\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j^0})\). Is the preservation due to orthogonality maintaining both norms and inner products, so normalization commutes with \(\mathbf{R}\)? Does this also hold for convolutional layers once they are reshaped into \(d\times n\) matrices?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — because an orthogonal matrix is an isometry of Euclidean space, it preserves inner products and norms, so normalization “commutes” with left-multiplication by the orthogonal matrix and all pairwise angles (or cosine similarities) are exactly preserved. The same argument applies to convolutional kernels after you flatten them into column vectors.

Proof and intuition. If $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is orthogonal then $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$. For any vectors $u,v\in\mathbb{R}^d$ this gives
$$
(\mathbf{R}u)^\top(\mathbf{R}v)=u^\top \mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R} v = u^\top v,
$$
so inner products are preserved; taking $u=v$ shows norms are preserved: $\|\mathbf{R}u\|=\|u\|$. Now let the pretrained column be $\mathbf{w}_i^0$ and the OFT-transformed column be $\mathbf{w}_i=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0$. Because norms are preserved we have
$$
\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0}
= \frac{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0}{\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0\|}
= \frac{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0}{\|\mathbf{w}_i^0\|}
= \mathbf{R}\,\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_i^0,
$$
so normalization and $\mathbf{R}$ commute in this sense. Therefore for any pair $i,j$
$$
(\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0})^\top(\widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j^0})
= (\mathbf{R}\,\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_i^0)^\top(\mathbf{R}\,\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_j^0)
= (\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_i^0)^\top \widehat{\mathbf{w}}_j^0,
$$
which is exactly the statement you wanted to verify. Geometrically $\mathbf{R}$ just rotates/reflections the whole set of unit vectors on the hypersphere, so pairwise angles and Euclidean distances on the sphere are unchanged. Because hyperspherical energy is a function of those pairwise (normalized) distances, OFT therefore preserves hyperspherical energy.

Convolutional layers. The same math applies after you reshape a convolutional weight tensor into a matrix whose columns are flattened filters: treat each output-channel kernel as a vector in $\mathbb{R}^d$ (with $d = \text{in\_channels}\times\text{kernel\_height}\times\text{kernel\_width}$) and form $\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. Left-multiplying by an orthogonal $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ rotates every flattened kernel in the same way, so pairwise angles among the flattened columns are preserved. The paper’s block-diagonal parameterization simply restricts $\mathbf{R}$ to an orthogonal block structure (e.g. per-input-channel blocks); each block is still orthogonal, so the invariance property still holds for that choice as well.

Caveats. This is exact only if $\mathbf{R}$ is exactly orthogonal and columns are nonzero (normalization is undefined for a zero column). In practice numerical error or approximate orthogonality (e.g. from a soft penalty) will make the preservation only approximate. Also, if you apply different orthogonal transforms to different columns (not a single layer-shared $\mathbf{R}$), or you transform along a different axis (right-multiplication by a non-orthogonal matrix), the pairwise angles will generally change.

This is exactly the mathematical reason the paper uses OFT: orthogonal left-multiplication is an isometric change of basis that preserves pairwise neuron angles (and thus hyperspherical energy).

### If OFT preserves all pairwise neuron angles, how can it still change the model’s behavior?
Question:
OFT preserves the hyperspherical energy by keeping all pairwise neuron angles the same within a layer via \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\). Intuitively that sounds like preserving most of the layer’s "geometry." Yet the method achieves different behavior (e.g., stronger controllability) after fine-tuning. What is changing if all inter-neuron angles are fixed? Is the key that angles between neurons and the layer inputs \(\mathbf{x}\) are not preserved (since \(\mathbf{x}\) is not rotated), so the alignment of features with the input distribution changes even though neuron-neuron geometry stays the same? Some intuition or a simple example would help.
Answer:
Short answer: your intuition is correct. OFT preserves pairwise neuron angles (the hyperspherical energy) because it replaces a pretrained weight matrix by a left-multiplication with an orthogonal matrix, $\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$. That guarantees the Gram matrix among neurons is unchanged, but it does not preserve the inner products between neurons and a fixed input $\mathbf{x}$. Those neuron–input alignments are exactly what determine the layer’s outputs, so rotating the whole neuron set can produce very different activations and thus different downstream behavior.

Here is the algebra that makes this immediate. With OFT the layer output is
$$\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top(\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{x}).$$
The neuron–neuron Gram matrix is preserved because
$$\mathbf{W}^\top\mathbf{W}=(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0=(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{W}^0,$$
so all pairwise neuron inner products (and therefore angles) stay the same. But the layer sees $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{x}$ instead of $\mathbf{x}$: unless the input distribution is rotationally symmetric, the set of dot products $\{\mathbf{w}_i^\top\mathbf{x}\}$ will change, and that changes activations after the nonlinearity and the whole downstream computation.

A tiny 2D example makes this concrete. Take $\mathbf{W}^0=[(1,0),(0,1)]$ (two orthonormal neurons) and rotate by $\theta=\pi/4$. The rotated neurons are $\mathbf{w}_1'=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$, $\mathbf{w}_2'=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$; their mutual inner product remains 0, so their angle is still $90^\circ$. For input $\mathbf{x}=(1,0)$ the original pre-activations were $(1,0)$; after rotation they are $\bigl(\cos\theta,\,-\sin\theta\bigr)\approx(0.707,\,-0.707)$. That sign flip and magnitude change will typically alter ReLU thresholds, normalization statistics, and every downstream layer that depends on these activations — so the network’s outputs change substantially even though neuron–neuron angles did not.

Two important nuances that the paper also alludes to are worth noting. First, if the input distribution were exactly rotation-invariant (e.g., isotropic Gaussian), then rotating neurons would not change the distribution of activations in expectation, so OFT would have little effect; real image distributions are not rotation-invariant, so OFT can change behavior by re-aligning neurons to the data. Second, because activations pass through nonlinearities and later layers, even a modest rotation of neuron directions can be amplified into a large functional change; COFT (the constrained variant) and the re-scaled OFT (adding per-neuron magnitudes) are precisely ways the paper uses to control how much alignment to the inputs is changed while still preserving hyperspherical structure.

In short: OFT preserves internal geometry among neurons (good for keeping pretrained semantics stable) but deliberately manipulates the neurons’ absolute orientation relative to the fixed input space (the degrees of freedom that actually change the mapping from $\mathbf{x}$ to $\mathbf{z}$). That is why OFT can both keep the model stable and still produce controlled behavioral changes.

### Is OFT equivalent to rotating inputs by \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\)? What are the implications across layers?
Question:
Given \(\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top (\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{x})\), it seems OFT on a given layer is equivalent to rotating the layer’s input by \(\mathbf{R}^\top\). Does this interpretation hold in practice inside a UNet/Transformer block with residual connections and normalization? If multiple layers each learn their own \(\mathbf{R}_\ell\), does this behave like inserting orthogonal "preconditioners" between nonlinearities, or could some \(\mathbf{R}_\ell\) effects cancel out? Any guidance on how this interacts with layer norm and residual paths?
Answer:
Short answer: locally — yes; globally inside a real model — not in any trivial way. Algebraically OFT on a single linear layer is exactly the same as rotating that layer’s input, but once you put that layer inside a UNet/Transformer block (with LayerNorm, nonlinearities and residual sums) the rotation becomes a layer-local change of basis rather than a global “rotate everything” preconditioner, and different learned rotations across layers generally do not just cancel out.

Why the single-layer equivalence is exact. If you replace a pretrained weight matrix by the OFT parameterization
$$\mathbf{W}=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0),$$
then for that layer
$$\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top(\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{x}).$$
So viewed in isolation the layer computes the same output as the original weights applied to a rotated input $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{x}$. That is why the paper describes OFT as “adjusting the canonical coordinate system” for the neurons and why orthogonality preserves hyperspherical energy (angles).

Why this does not mean a global rotate-that-fixes-everything. The algebraic identity above uses only linearity. In real blocks you have one or more of: LayerNorm, nonlinearities (GELU, SiLU, ReLU), residual sums (skip connections), softmax attention, etc. None of these operations commute with arbitrary orthogonal rotations in the same simple way:

- Residuals: a residual block typically produces $y = x + f(x)$. If you only change $f$ by replacing its internal linear layer with $(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top$, you get $y = x + (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top(\mathbf{R}^\top x)$; there is no way to factor a single $\mathbf{R}^\top$ out of the whole sum because the identity branch remains unrotated. So OFT behaves as a layer-local re-basing of the sublayer outputs and does not simply rotate the entire residual-block input.

- Nonlinearities: a nonlinear activation $\phi(\cdot)$ breaks the ability to push a rotation past it; generally $\phi(\mathbf{R}^\top x)\neq \mathbf{R}^\top\phi(x)$. So rotations sandwiched by nonlinearities are not mergeable and cannot cancel through the nonlinearity.

- LayerNorm: LayerNorm normalizes statistics across channels (per token). Orthogonal rotations mix channels and change per-channel means/variances, so LayerNorm does not commute with arbitrary rotations. (Orthogonals preserve total norm and pairwise angles, but not per-coordinate mean/variance used by LN.)

Because of these ingredients, each learned $\mathbf{R}_\ell$ is best thought of as a learned, layer-local change of basis (a “coordinate transform”) between that layer’s inputs and its neurons, not as a global preconditioner that will be pushed or cancelled through the whole block.

What about multiple $\mathbf{R}_\ell$ and possible cancellation? Two facts are useful.

1. If you had a purely linear stack (no nonlinearities, no normalization, and no residual sums), orthogonal matrices multiply and can be collapsed: the product of layer rotations is another orthogonal, so several $\mathbf{R}_\ell$ can be combined or, in principle, cancel if they happen to be inverses. Concretely, for two consecutive linear layers with OFT,
$$h = ( \mathbf{R}_1\mathbf{W}_1^0)^\top x = \mathbf{W}_1^{0\top}\mathbf{R}_1^\top x,\qquad
z=(\mathbf{R}_2\mathbf{W}_2^0)^\top h = \mathbf{W}_2^{0\top}\mathbf{R}_2^\top\mathbf{W}_1^{0\top}\mathbf{R}_1^\top x,$$
so if all intermediate maps were identity-like one could combine $\mathbf{R}_2^\top\mathbf{R}_1^\top$ into a single rotation. But that relies on linearity.

2. In practice, because of nonlinearities/LN/residuals you cannot generally get exact cancellation. Two independently trained $\mathbf{R}_\ell$ could accidentally oppose each other on some subspace, but that would be a fragile, non-robust way to represent a task and the paper’s constrained variant (COFT) explicitly discourages large, arbitrary rotations (it enforces $\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\le \epsilon$ in the Cayley parametrization). The paper also points out (as an open question) that orthogonal matrices can be multiplied together across tasks because the group is closed, but whether those compositions preserve all tasks’ knowledge is nontrivial.

Are these rotations “preconditioners”? Not in the numerical linear algebra sense. A classical preconditioner rescales eigenvalues / alters singular values to improve conditioning. Orthogonal matrices are isometries: multiplying a linear map on the right by an orthogonal matrix does not change its singular values. If $M' = M R^\top$ then
$$M'M'^\top = M R^\top R M^\top = M M^\top,$$
so spectral norms and conditioning remain the same. Conceptually OFT is a learned change of coordinates (a basis change) between nonlinear layers; this can help optimization or representation by aligning neurons with task structure, but it does not act like a diagonal/rescaling preconditioner.

Practical implications / tips (grounded in the paper and the architecture facts above). Treat OFT as a per-layer, multiplicative adapter (a learned orthonormal re-basing) rather than a global rotation you can push through the model. In Transformers and UNets the paper uses block-diagonal $\mathbf{R}$ (and sometimes per-head or per-channel blocks) precisely to avoid destructive cross-channel mixing and to be parameter-efficient. If you want to preserve attention inner products, make $\mathbf{R}$ consistent across the projections you care about (e.g. share or align the rotation for Q and K projections) — that helps keep dot-product structure stable. If LayerNorm / residual stability is a concern, use the constrained variant COFT (small $\epsilon$) so rotations stay near identity; empirically the paper finds COFT improves finetuning stability. Finally, remember you can multiply learned $\mathbf{R}$ into $\mathbf{W}^0$ at inference time (no runtime overhead), which is why OFT is implemented as a reparameterization rather than a costly runtime transform.

Concluding intuition: OFT gives you lightweight, angle-preserving re-basis transforms at each layer (hence preserving hyperspherical energy and semantic structure). That is powerful for controlled finetuning because it changes directions (angles) of neurons while keeping their relational geometry; but because real networks are nonlinear and use skips/LN, the learned rotations act locally and interact in a nontrivial way across layers rather than simply cancelling or becoming a single global rotation.

### Won’t stacking OFT matrices across layers cancel out and leave the network unchanged?
Question:
If layer \(\ell\) uses \(\mathbf{W}_\ell = \mathbf{R}_\ell \mathbf{W}_\ell^0\), and the next layer uses \(\mathbf{W}_{\ell+1} = \mathbf{R}_{\ell+1} \mathbf{W}_{\ell+1}^0\), could there be regimes where the cumulative effect approximately cancels (e.g., if \(\mathbf{R}_{\ell+1}^\top\approx\mathbf{R}_\ell\)) so the overall function change is small? How do the nonlinearities and attention mechanisms prevent trivial cancellations, and is there any empirical evidence about learned \(\mathbf{R}_\ell\) matrices across depth (e.g., do they align or diverge)?
Answer:
Short answer: mathematically, two orthogonal re-parameterizations can cancel in the degenerate linear case, but in the actual diffusion / Transformer stacks used in the paper trivial cancellation is extremely unlikely. Nonlinearities (pointwise activations), normalization, residual / skip connections, multi‑head attention and the COFT near‑identity constraint all break the algebraic conditions needed for a layer‑to‑layer undoing of rotations. The paper does not report an explicit per‑layer alignment analysis of the learned $R_\ell$’s (the authors flag composition of orthogonal transforms as an open question), but the strong, stable task-level effects of OFT/COFT in the experiments are indirect evidence that learned rotations are producing nontrivial functional change rather than canceling out.

Why cancellation is possible in principle (and what it would require).  Consider two consecutive linear layers without nonlinearity:
$$
y = W_{2}^\top (W_{1}^\top x).
$$
With OFT you replace $W_1\leftarrow R_1 W_1^0$ and $W_2\leftarrow R_2 W_2^0$, so the overall linear map becomes
$$
y = (W_2^0)^\top R_2^\top R_1^\top (W_1^0)^\top x.
$$
To get exactly the original map $(W_2^0)^\top (W_1^0)^\top x$ for all $x$ you need
$$
R_2^\top R_1^\top = I \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad R_2 = R_1^\top.
$$
So in a purely linear stack an orthogonal transform on one layer can be undone by a coordinated orthogonal transform on the next layer.

Why that cancellation $\approx$ never happens in practice. Real diffusion / Transformer networks are not sequences of two naked linear maps. There are several mechanisms that block the algebraic cancellation above:

1. Pointwise nonlinearities. With an activation $\phi$ between layers the output is
$$
y=(W_2^0)^\top R_2^\top \phi\big((W_1^0)^\top R_1^\top x\big).
$$
Equaling the original $(W_2^0)^\top \phi\big((W_1^0)^\top x\big)$ for every input would require the extremely strong identity
$$
\phi\big((W_1^0)^\top R_1^\top x\big)=R_2^{-\top}\,\phi\big((W_1^0)^\top x\big)
$$
for all $x$, which cannot hold in general for nonlinear $\phi$ (ReLU/SiLU/etc.) unless the rotations and activations conspire in a very special, highly constrained way (e.g. permutations that preserve activation patterns). In practice the nonlinearities change signs/thresholds and so a rotation before $\phi$ changes activation patterns that an inverse rotation after $\phi$ cannot globally undo.

2. Normalization and residual/skip structure. LayerNorm/BatchNorm and residual connections mix coordinates and statistics across channels. A rotation inside one branch will alter per‑channel statistics and residual sums; undoing that effect would require coordinating rotations and normalization statistics across branches and across many parameter groups — again a very constrained requirement.

3. Attention mechanics and multi‑head structure. In attention blocks the model computes Q/K/V projections, a scaled dot‑product + softmax, and then value aggregation and output projection. If one rotated the Q and K projections in exactly the same way within a single attention block, raw dot products might be preserved, but values and the final projection are still affected; if rotations differ across heads or across Q/K/V, attention patterns change. Canceling a rotation across layers would therefore require coordinated rotations across many distinct parameter matrices (Q, K, V, and the feed‑forward layers that follow). The architecture’s branching (multi‑head) makes the precise undoing of a prior rotation on every path combinatorially unlikely.

4. COFT (the constrained variant) explicitly keeps each $\,R\,$ close to identity ($\|R-I\|\le\epsilon$ in the paper). That both limits the magnitude of per‑layer rotations and biases training toward small, locally meaningful adjustments rather than large rotations that would then need to be undone layer‑by‑layer.

Empirical evidence in the paper. The paper does not show a per‑layer study of how $R_\ell$ matrices align with each other (there is no table or figure of $R_\ell^\top R_{\ell+1}$ statistics). The authors do note compositionality as an open problem — “the orthogonal matrices produced by multiple OFT finetuning tasks can be multiplied together … Whether this set … preserve the knowledge of all the downstream tasks remains an interesting direction to study.” Practically, however, the experiments show that OFT/COFT produce substantial, stable task‑level changes (faster convergence, better controllability, preserved semantics). Those task outcomes are an empirical signal that the learned orthogonal updates are effectful, not trivially canceled across depth.

If you want to check alignment yourself (suggested experiments consistent with the paper). Compute simple diagnostics across layers (or across blocks if you use block‑diagonal $R$): (a) pairwise closeness to identity, e.g. $\|R_{\ell+1}^\top R_\ell - I\|_F$; (b) layerwise distance to identity $\|R_\ell-I\|_F$ (COFT will show small values); (c) geodesic distance on $O(d)$ via matrix log: $\| \log(R_{\ell+1}^\top R_\ell)\|_F$; (d) principal angles / singular values of $R_{\ell+1}^\top R_\ell$ to see whether they are near 1 (alignment) or spread (divergence). Because the paper uses block diagonal parameterizations, do these statistics per‑block. I would expect to see that $R_\ell$ are not arranged to systematically undo each other — especially across nonlinear/residual/attention boundaries — but the paper does not report those numbers, so this is a concrete minor study you could run.

Bottom line: yes, exact cancellation is a real algebraic possibility for chains of pure linear maps, but the realistic building blocks used in Stable Diffusion / Transformers and the COFT regularization make such trivial cancellations extremely unlikely. The paper’s empirical improvements are consistent with meaningful, non‑cancelling rotations; a per‑layer alignment analysis is left as future work / open problem in the paper.

### Which norm is used in COFT’s constraint \(\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\le \epsilon\), and how is projection performed?
Question:
COFT imposes \(\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon\) alongside orthogonality. Which norm is used here (Frobenius norm or operator/spectral norm)? When translating this to the Cayley parameter \(\mathbf{Q}\) via the Neumann approximation \(\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2)\), how exactly is the projection implemented: do you project \(\mathbf{Q}\) onto a ball in the chosen norm each step? Any practical guidance for choosing \(\epsilon\) (or the corresponding \(\epsilon'\) on \(\mathbf{Q}\)) for stability without underfitting?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not explicitly spell out which matrix norm is used for the constraint $\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\le\epsilon$. However, the text motivates the Neumann-series linearization by saying “the Neumann series converges in the operator norm,” so the most natural reading is that the authors intended an operator (spectral)‑norm control. In the paper they then move the constraint inside the Cayley parameterization and state that the constraint on $\mathbf{Q}$ can be “easily enforced by projected gradient descent” — i.e. you project $\mathbf{Q}$ onto a norm ball after each update.

Why operator norm is the natural mathematical choice. The Cayley map is
$$
\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
$$
with $\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^\top$. The Neumann expansion $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}=\sum_{k\ge0}\mathbf{Q}^k$ requires $\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{\text{op}}<1$ for convergence; expanding gives
$$
\mathbf{R} \;=\; \mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2).
$$
That derivation is explicitly invoked in the paper, so controlling the operator norm of $\mathbf{Q}$ is the most direct way to guarantee the Cayley approximation and invertibility behavior used in COFT.

How to implement the projection in practice. The paper’s recipe is projected gradient descent on $\mathbf{Q}$: after a gradient step on the Cayley parameters, project back into the allowed ball. Concretely, two practical projections are common and simple to implement:

- Frobenius‑norm clipping. Compute $\|\mathbf{Q}\|_F$. If $\|\mathbf{Q}\|_F>\epsilon'$ then set $\mathbf{Q}\leftarrow \mathbf{Q}\cdot(\epsilon'/\|\mathbf{Q}\|_F)$. This is very cheap and preserves skew‑symmetry if you enforce $\mathbf{Q}\leftarrow(\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{Q}^\top)/2$ after the update.

- Spectral (operator)‑norm clipping. Compute the largest singular value $\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{Q})$ (per block if you use block diagonal $\mathbf{Q}$). If $\sigma_{\max}>\epsilon'$ then scale $\mathbf{Q}\leftarrow\mathbf{Q}\cdot(\epsilon'/\sigma_{\max})$. Computing $\sigma_{\max}$ can be done by a small number of power‑iterations or by SVD for small blocks; scaling preserves skew‑symmetry.

Either of these implements “projected gradient descent” in the loose sense used by the paper (clip then continue). If you want the exact orthogonal projection onto the spectral‑norm ball with respect to Frobenius distance you would perform an SVD and clamp singular values, but for the routine goal of enforcing a spectral bound a simple scale‑clip by $\sigma_{\max}$ is typically adequate and much cheaper.

Two implementation details that follow from the paper and help stability: parameterize $\mathbf{Q}$ as skew‑symmetric explicitly (store a free matrix $A$ and set $\mathbf{Q}=(A-A^\top)/2$), perform projection per block if $\mathbf{R}$ is block‑diagonal (this is exactly how the paper makes the method parameter‑efficient), and compute $\mathbf{R}$ via a stable linear solve $\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})\,(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}=\text{solve}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})$ rather than forming an explicit inverse.

Mapping $\epsilon$ on $\mathbf{R}$ to $\epsilon'$ on $\mathbf{Q}$. Using the first‑order relation $\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\approx 2\mathbf{Q}$, a reasonable starting rule is
$$
\epsilon' \approx \frac{\epsilon}{2},
$$
but remember this is only first‑order: higher‑order terms $\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2)$ make the relationship nonlinear. More robust rules are (a) impose a strict operator‑norm cap $\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{\text{op}}<1$ to ensure Cayley invertibility/Neumann convergence, and (b) choose $\epsilon'$ considerably smaller than 1 so the linear approximation is accurate (so that $\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|$ is in practice close to $2\|\mathbf{Q}\|$). In short: $\epsilon'$ must be small; a conservative conversion is $\epsilon'=\epsilon/2$ and then tune around that.

Practical advice for choosing $\epsilon$ (and $\epsilon'$). COFT’s role is to trade flexibility for stability: smaller $\epsilon$ keeps the finetuned model nearer to pretrained weights (less collapse, more stability) but can underfit; larger $\epsilon$ approaches unconstrained OFT and gives more flexibility. The paper shows this tradeoff qualitatively (their Figure on $\epsilon$ effects). In practice you should treat $\epsilon$ as a small, tunable hyperparameter and pick it by validation: start conservative (small) and increase until you see sufficient adaptation without loss of pretrained capabilities. If you use block diagonal $\mathbf{Q}$, tune per‑block scale or use the same $\epsilon'$ per block but normalize by block size if you use Frobenius norm (because $\|\cdot\|_F$ grows with dimension). If you want a rule‑of‑thumb mapping from the linearized relation, start with $\epsilon'$ so that $\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{\text{op}}$ is comfortably below 1 (e.g. target an initial $\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{\text{op}}$ on the order of 0.05–0.3 to keep higher‑order terms small), then adjust by validation. Also consider adding a soft penalty (e.g., $\lambda\|\mathbf{Q}\|_F^2$) instead of a hard projection if you prefer a smoother regularizer.

Summary. The paper implies an operator‑norm viewpoint (because of the Neumann/operator‑norm comment) and explicitly says to enforce the Q constraint by projected gradient descent. In implementation you may project $\mathbf{Q}$ after each update either in spectral norm (mathematically natural for Cayley/Neumann) or in Frobenius norm (computationally cheap); enforce skew‑symmetry and block structure before/after projection; use the approximation $\epsilon'\approx\epsilon/2$ as a starting point but tune $\epsilon$ on validation because of higher‑order terms and the flexibility/stability tradeoff.

### Is the Cayley transform well-conditioned during training, and can it become numerically unstable?
Question:
The orthogonal matrix is parameterized as \(\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) with \(\mathbf{Q}^\top = -\mathbf{Q}\). While \(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\) is always invertible for real skew-symmetric \(\mathbf{Q}\), could it become poorly conditioned as \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\) grows, leading to unstable gradients? Do you regularize or cap \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\) beyond the COFT constraint, and does the block-diagonal structure materially improve conditioning (beyond reducing matrix sizes)?
Answer:
Short answer: No — the Cayley parametrization used in the paper is not a source of exploding / ill-posed gradients in the way you fear. For a real skew‑symmetric $\mathbf{Q}$ the matrix $\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}$ is always invertible and the mapping $ \mathbf{Q}\mapsto \mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}$ is well behaved: the forward map is smooth and the backpropagated gradients are uniformly bounded (you do not get unbounded gradient amplification from the inverse). The paper also gives an explicit stability knob: COFT enforces a small deviation constraint $\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\le\epsilon$, which (via the Neumann approximation $\mathbf{R}\approx \mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q}+O(\mathbf{Q}^2)$) is implemented as a cap on $\|\mathbf{Q}\|$ enforced by projected gradient descent.

Why this is true (intuitive + math). Real skew‑symmetric matrices have purely imaginary eigenvalues. If the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{Q}$ are $i\lambda_k$ with real $\lambda_k$, then the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}$ are $1-i\lambda_k$ and their moduli are
$$
|1-i\lambda_k|=\sqrt{1+\lambda_k^2}\ge 1.
$$
Thus $\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}$ is never singular, and its smallest singular value is at least $1$. That rules out the kind of near‑singularity that typically causes exploding inverses.

A short calculation of the differential makes the effect on gradients explicit. Differentiating the Cayley map gives
$$
d\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{R})\,d\mathbf{Q}\,(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}.
$$
By the usual adjoint (chain‑rule) for gradients,
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{Q}}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{R})^\top\!\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{R}}\,(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-T}.
$$
Use operator‑norm bounds $\|\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{R}\|_2\le \|\mathbf{I}\|_2+\|\mathbf{R}\|_2\le 2$ (since $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal) and $\|(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\|_2\le 1$ (from the singular‑value formula above) to get
$$
\big\|\tfrac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{Q}}\big\|_F \le 2\big\|\tfrac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{R}}\big\|_F.
$$
So the Cayley inverse does not amplify gradients by an unbounded factor — in fact the mapping is (at most) a small constant‑factor amplifier. That is the key mathematical reason you shouldn’t expect exploding gradients coming from the Cayley inverse.

What the paper does about it. The paper (section “Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning”) explicitly acknowledges the inverse and shows two practical mitigations. First, they initialize $\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{0}$ so $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$ at start (zero‑change initialization). Second, COFT adds the explicit constraint $\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\le\epsilon$. Using the Neumann series approximation $\mathbf{R}\approx\mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q}+O(\mathbf{Q}^2)$ they move that constraint inside the Cayley parameter and enforce an equivalent bound $\|\mathbf{Q}\|\le\epsilon'$ (the paper denotes the mapped hyperparameter $\epsilon'$) via projected gradient descent. So yes — the authors do cap $\mathbf{Q}$ in COFT; in vanilla OFT the only implicit cap is small learning rates and the identity initialization, but empirically OFT was already stable and COFT gives even stronger stability.

Does the block‑diagonal structure help conditioning (beyond cost and parameter reduction)? Block diagonalization primarily helps two things that improve training in practice: it reduces the matrix sizes you invert (so lower compute and less accumulated rounding), and it strongly regularizes the family of allowable rotations (fewer degrees of freedom), which empirically improves stability and reduces overfitting. Algebraically, block‑diagonal $\mathbf{Q}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{Q}_1,\dots,\mathbf{Q}_r)$ simply makes the Cayley inverse act per block; the spectral bounds above hold per block, and the worst‑case conditioning of the full matrix is the worst of the blocks. Thus block‑diagonalization does not change the fundamental invertibility / singular‑value lower bound (those are still $\ge 1$ per block), but it does limit how large block eigenvalues can grow under the same amount of parameter freedom and numerical noise. In short: block structure helps computationally and acts as effective regularization (and so helps numerical behaviour empirically), but it is not a magic cure that changes the Cayley spectral facts.

Practical caveats. The theoretical bounds assume exact arithmetic and exact skew‑symmetry. In floating‑point practice one can still see noisy behaviour if Q entries become astronomically large or if you use very aggressive learning rates; the paper’s COFT projection (and identity initialization) is meant exactly to avoid that. The authors also note (in the discussion and open problems) that the matrix inverse has a computational cost and that speeding up a differentiable inverse remains an implementation challenge — which is why block diagonalization is used in practice.

Takeaway. From a linear‑algebra and autodiff viewpoint the Cayley map is safe: $\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}$ cannot become singular for skew‑symmetric $\mathbf{Q}$, the backpropagated gradients are uniformly bounded (no explosion due to inversion), and COFT gives an explicit, implemented way to cap $\|\mathbf{Q}\|$ if you want an even stronger guarantee. The block‑diagonal choice is mainly an efficiency/regularization device that also helps empirical stability.

### What is the actual compute cost of Cayley parameterization compared to LoRA at training time?
Question:
The paper focuses on parameter counts (e.g., OFT has \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)\) parameters for an \(r\)-block diagonal \(\mathbf{R}\)), but training-time cost also includes forming or solving with \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})\) per block (potentially \(\mathcal{O}((d/r)^3)\) if naively inverted). How is this implemented efficiently in practice (linear solves, batched small inverses, or iterative methods), and how does wall-clock time compare to LoRA’s two low-rank multiplications in large SD v1.5 UNets?
Answer:
Short answer: Cayley introduces an implicit small dense matrix inverse per orthogonal block, so a naive implementation costs cubic time in the block size; in practice you avoid explicit full inverses and implement Cayley with batched linear solves / cached factorizations or with a Neumann-series approximation when the skew matrix is small. Per training step LoRA’s extra work is just two cheap low‑rank multiplies (linear in the rank), so LoRA is typically much cheaper per step; OFT can be made practical by (1) choosing small blocks, (2) amortizing the factorization across many RHS (batch / spatial tokens / sequence positions), and/or (3) using the Neumann approximation in the COFT regime — but the paper does not publish per‑step wall‑clock numbers, it only notes the inverse as a scalability concern and uses block‑diagonal Cayley to mitigate it.

Why and how this matters (formula + intuition). The Cayley map used in the paper is
$$
\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},\qquad \mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^\top,
$$
and the authors parameterize $\mathbf{R}$ as block‑diagonal with $r$ blocks of size $b=d/r$. If you literally form a dense inverse per block you pay $\mathcal{O}(b^3)$ per block, so naive cost for all blocks is $\mathcal{O}(r b^3)=\mathcal{O}(d^3/r^2)$ just to form the transforms. More usefully, you can avoid forming full inverses and instead compute the action of $\mathbf{R}$ on many vectors by solving linear systems: to compute $\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{x}$ you can form $\mathbf{b}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})^\top\mathbf{x}$ and then solve $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^\top \mathbf{v}=\mathbf{b}$. A direct LU/QR factorization per block costs $\mathcal{O}(b^3)$ but that factorization can be reused for all columns (all spatial/sequence positions / batch entries) in the forward pass; applying the factorized solver to $M$ right‑hand sides costs $\mathcal{O}(b^2 M)$ per block. So a realistic per‑step cost for OFT (forward) is roughly
$$
\mathcal{O}\!\big(r b^3 + r b^2 M\big)=\mathcal{O}\!\Big(\frac{d^3}{r^2}+\frac{d^2}{r}M\Big),
$$
where $M$ is the number of activation columns you multiply by $\mathbf{R}$ (batch size times spatial/sequence positions). The corresponding extra cost for LoRA (rank $r'$) is
$$
\mathcal{O}\big(M r'(d+n)\big)
$$
because LoRA computes two small low‑rank matmuls per activation (first $A x$ then $B(\cdot)$). When $M$ is large (many tokens / spatial locations), the $\mathcal{O}((d^2/r)M)$ term usually dominates OFT’s overhead; when $M$ is small the one‑time factorization term $\mathcal{O}(d^3/r^2)$ matters more.

Practical implementation patterns. The paper explicitly uses Cayley and block‑diagonalization and remarks that the matrix inverse “slightly limits scalability” (and that block structure is used to mitigate this), and it also uses the Neumann‑series expansion to motivate the small‑Q / COFT constraint ($\mathbf{R}\approx \mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2)$). From that you get the practical options people use in implementations:

- Do not form $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}$ explicitly. Use a batched direct solver (LU/QR) to compute $\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{x}$ (or $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ if you prefer), factorize $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})$ once per optimization step / forward and apply it to many RHS (cheap if $M$ large). Modern GPU libraries provide efficient batched small‑matrix factorization/solve kernels which are the usual choice.

- Cache factorizations across the entire forward pass and reuse them in the backward pass; the backward requires similar solves but can reuse the cached LU, so the extra cost is roughly a small constant factor above forward cost.

- If you operate in the COFT regime (small $\|\mathbf{Q}\|$), truncate the Neumann series $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\approx \sum_{k=0}^{K}\mathbf{Q}^k$ for small $K$. That replaces cubic work with a small number $K$ of matrix‑times‑matrix or matrix‑times‑vector products (complexity $\mathcal{O}(K b^2 M)$), and is attractive when you can tolerate the approximation.

- Keep block size $b=d/r$ small. The cubic term is $b^3$, so choosing more blocks (larger $r$) reduces the heavy cost and trades compute for parameter constraints. The paper follows exactly this tradeoff (block‑diagonal Cayley).

Wall‑clock comparison (what to expect). Because the paper does not publish microbenchmarks, you must treat this qualitatively. Per training iteration LoRA’s extra cost is usually tiny (two low‑rank multiplications per activation) and is therefore substantially cheaper than a naive Cayley implementation. With a careful Cayley implementation (batched solves + block sizes chosen so that $b$ is small and the factorization is amortized over many RHS) the per‑step overhead can be reduced to a modest factor above the baseline layer cost. As a rough numeric illustration (purely illustrative): if $d\!\approx\!1024$, $n\!\approx\!1024$, $r\!=\!8$ (so $b=128$) and $r'\!=\!8$, then the asymptotic ratio of OFT extra work to LoRA extra work for the per‑column term is roughly $\approx d/(2 r r') \approx 8$, i.e. OFT could be several times heavier per forward in that regime — but the factorization is amortized over many RHS and the real measured gap depends on how many columns $M$ you multiply by $\mathbf{R}$, on kernel implementations, and on whether you use the Neumann shortcut in COFT. Importantly, the paper reports much faster convergence (fewer iterations) for OFT/COFT; therefore the total wall‑clock to reach a given validation target can be competitive or even better for OFT despite higher per‑step cost.

Recommendation. If you care about minimal per‑step latency, LoRA is the cheaper plug‑in. If you want the stability/semantic preservation OFT promises, implement Cayley with (1) block‑diagonalization to keep $b$ small, (2) batched direct factorization + triangular solves (cache factorizations across forward/backward), and/or (3) a Neumann approximation for COFT if you can tolerate it. Benchmark per‑step time and number of iterations to convergence for your SD v1.5 UNet and chosen $r,r'$ — the right tradeoff is empirical and depends on block sizes, batch/patch counts ($M$), and kernel efficiency on your GPU.

Summary sentence rooted in the paper: the paper itself flags the inverse as the main scalability concern and mitigates it by block‑diagonal Cayley; it does not publish wall‑clock comparisons versus LoRA, so you should expect higher per‑step cost for OFT unless you use the practical tricks above, but the faster convergence the paper reports can offset that in total training time.

### Why claim OFT is \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) when \(r=\alpha d\), but LoRA is \(\mathcal{O}(d^2+dn)\)? Is this comparison fair?
Question:
The paper compares asymptotics assuming \(r=r'=\alpha d\), concluding OFT is \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) while LoRA is \(\mathcal{O}(d^2+dn)\). But in typical LoRA usage, \(r'\ll d\) is chosen to be a small constant. Could you provide an apples-to-apples comparison where both methods use the same parameter budget (e.g., equal trainable parameter counts), and report the resulting compute and quality trade-offs?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper’s $O(d)$ vs $O(d^2+dn)$ statement is true under the specific scaling assumption $r = r' = \alpha d$ (both OFT block count and LoRA rank proportional to the neuron dimension). That assumption is a particular asymptotic regime, not the usual practical LoRA setting (practitioners typically pick $r'$ a small constant). If you want a fair, “apples‑to‑apples” comparison fix the same number of trainable parameters $P$ for both methods; you can then solve for the implied LoRA rank $r'$ and OFT block count $r$ and compare per‑step compute and empirical convergence. Below I give the simple algebra, an intuitive interpretation, a small numeric example, and the paper’s empirical takeaways.

Why the paper wrote $O(d)$ vs $O(d^2+dn)$
The OFT parameter count (block‑diagonal orthogonal $R$ with $r$ blocks) is $P_{\text{OFT}} = \frac{d\big(\tfrac{d}{r}-1\big)}{2} \approx \frac{d^2}{2r}$, so if $r$ scales like $\alpha d$ you get $P_{\text{OFT}} = O(d^2/(\alpha d)) = O(d)$. LoRA’s trainable parameters are $P_{\text{LoRA}} = r'(d+n)$, so with $r' = \alpha d$ you get $P_{\text{LoRA}} = \alpha d(d+n) = O(d^2 + dn)$. Thus the asymptotic statement is simply the result of assuming both parameterizations grow linearly with $d$. That is a legitimate asymptotic comparison but not what most LoRA users pick in practice.

Apples‑to‑apples mapping (same trainable parameter budget $P$)
If you fix the same trainable parameter budget $P$, solve the two equalities for $r'$ (LoRA) and $r$ (OFT):

- LoRA: $P = r'(d+n) \Rightarrow r' = \dfrac{P}{d+n}$.
- OFT: $P = \dfrac{d\big(\tfrac{d}{r}-1\big)}{2} \Rightarrow r = \dfrac{d^2}{2P + d}$.

These give the internal hyperparameters (rank or number of blocks) you should pick to make the two methods use the same number of trainable parameters.

Per‑step extra compute (training-time)
During training the extra forward cost (beyond the baseline $W^0 x$) is roughly:

- LoRA: cost $\approx r'(d+n) = P$ (because you compute $U^T x$ then $V^T(\ldots)$, costing $O(d r' + n r')$).
- OFT: cost to apply $R^T x$ is $\approx \dfrac{d^2}{r}$. Substituting $r$ from the previous line yields $\dfrac{d^2}{r} = 2P + d$, so OFT’s extra forward cost $\approx 2P + d \approx 2P$ for $P \gg d$.

Intuition: equal parameter budget $P \Rightarrow$ LoRA spends those parameters as a rank-$r'$ additive update; its forward cost is about $P$ flops. OFT stores an orthogonal transform whose forward cost (apply $R$ to the input) scales like $d^2/r$, which algebraically becomes about $2P$ here, so OFT costs roughly twice as many extra flops per step as LoRA for the same $P$.

Small concrete example (to make numbers tangible)
Take $d = n = 128$ and choose $P = 2048$ trainable params (the paper uses a $128\times128$ toy to illustrate parameter counts). Then

- LoRA: $r' = \dfrac{P}{d+n} = \dfrac{2048}{256} = 8$ (this matches the paper’s example: LoRA with $r'=8$ is 2048 params).
- OFT: $r = \dfrac{d^2}{2P + d} = \dfrac{16384}{4096 + 128} \approx 3.9 \Rightarrow$ choose $r = 4$ blocks, which gives $P_{\text{OFT}} \approx 1984 \approx 2048$ (close). The paper’s example earlier ($r=8$) gave 960 params for OFT; that was an unequal-budget comparison.

Per‑step extra flops: LoRA $\approx 2048$, OFT $\approx 2P + d \approx 4224$ (or directly compute $R^T x$ cost $\approx 4096$). So OFT takes $\sim 2\times$ extra flops per step at equal $P$. But see the next paragraph about convergence.

Quality / convergence tradeoffs (what the paper actually reports)
The paper’s empirical results intentionally compare with similar numbers of trainable parameters (they say “to ensure a fair comparison to LoRA, we only apply OFT or COFT to the same layer where LoRA is used” and “OFT and COFT use a similar number of trainable parameters to LoRA”). In those experiments OFT/COFT converge faster and achieve better final quality on subject‑driven and controllable generation tasks: for example, on the L2F task the paper reports LoRA needed $\approx 20$ epochs to reach the performance that OFT reached at epoch $8$. Because OFT tends to converge in fewer epochs, the larger per‑step cost can be (and in the paper’s experiments was) offset by fewer steps required. Numerically, with the toy numbers above LoRA total extra work $\approx 20\times P$ while OFT $\approx 8\times (2P) \approx 16\times P$ — OFT can still be cheaper overall and ends up with better stability and sample quality in the paper’s tasks.

Practical conclusions and recommendations
The asymptotic $O(d)$ vs $O(d^2+dn)$ claim is correct under the $r\propto d$ assumption, but that assumption is not the usual LoRA setting. For fair comparisons you should fix the trainable parameter budget $P$ and use the mapping formulas above to pick $r'$ and $r$. Under equal $P$ you’ll find:

- LoRA has about $P$ extra flops per forward; OFT about $2P$ (so LoRA is cheaper per step).
- OFT typically converges in fewer steps and preserves angular/neuron geometry (hyperspherical energy), which in this paper led to better stability and higher final quality for the tasks studied.
- Both methods can be merged into the base weights at inference time, so neither adds inference overhead once you bake weights.

If you care most about wall‑clock training FLOPs and you must keep $r'$ tiny, LoRA will be cheaper per step. If you care about stability, faster convergence (fewer epochs), and final generation quality (and can accept $\approx 2\times$ training flops per parameter), OFT (or COFT) is attractive — the paper’s experiments show this regimen wins in the text‑to‑image tasks they study.

### How exactly is OFT applied to attention weights in Stable Diffusion?
Question:
For fairness to LoRA, the paper says OFT is applied to the same attention weights. Concretely: which matrices receive \(\mathbf{R}\) (\(\mathbf{W}_q, \mathbf{W}_k, \mathbf{W}_v, \mathbf{W}_o\))? Is \(\mathbf{R}\) shared across heads or per-head? Is it shared across layers or unique per layer? Are block-diagonal \(\mathbf{R}\) blocks aligned with head dimensions? Some code-level clarification would help replication.
Answer:
Short answer: the paper applies OFT to the same attention projection weights that LoRA was attached to in the baseline experiments — i.e. the linear/$1\times 1$-conv projection matrices inside each attention block (the projection layers that implement query/key/value/output). OFT learns one orthogonal transform per layer (the paper’s phrase is “layer‑shared”), not one single $R$ shared across the whole network. Whether that per‑layer $R$ acts “per‑head” or “across all heads” is controlled by the block‑diagonal parameterization: the authors parameterize an orthogonal matrix $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and optionally make it block‑diagonal with $r$ blocks. If you set $r=\text{num\_heads}$ and keep each block independent you effectively get a per‑head transform (block size $d/r$ equals head dimension); if you keep $r=1$ or you force all blocks to be identical you get a single transform shared across heads. The paper explicitly notes both options (independent blocks or shared blocks) and that they used a layer‑shared orthogonal transform in their experiments.

How to implement this in code (formula + practical choices). OFT replaces the original forward
$$
z=(W^0)^\top x
$$
by
$$
z=(R W^0)^\top x,
$$
which by algebra is equivalent to
$$
z=(W^0)^\top (R^\top x).
$$
In practice this gives two convenient implementation choices:

1) absorb $R$ into the stored weight once (inference or if you want to materialize the new weight):
compute $W\leftarrow R W^0$ and use the usual linear layer with that weight. This produces no runtime overhead at inference.

2) avoid rewriting weights and avoid a full $d\times d$ multiply each step by pre‑transforming the input: compute $x' = x\,R$ (or $x' = R^\top x$ depending on row/column layout) and then call the original frozen linear. Concretely in PyTorch terms where a Linear has weight `W_p = $(W^0)^\top$` and computes `out = x @ W_p.T`, you can do `x2 = x @ $R$` and then `out = F.linear(x2, W_p)`; this keeps the pretrained weight frozen and only applies the small orthogonal transform to the activations.

Parameterizing and enforcing orthogonality. The paper uses Cayley parameterization per (full or block) orthogonal matrix:
$$
R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1},
$$
with $Q$ skew‑symmetric ($Q=-Q^\top$). To implement this stably, parameterize each block's $Q$ by an unconstrained matrix $A$ and set $Q=A-A^\top$, initialize $A=0$ so $Q=0$ and $R=I$ (zero change at start). For the Constrained OFT (COFT) add a post‑step projection $\|Q\|\le\epsilon'$ (or clip $A$) to realize the $\|R-I\|\le\epsilon$ neighbourhood (the paper uses a Neumann approximation to relate the two). When you use block diagonalization set block_size = $d/r$; to align with multi‑head attention choose $r=\text{num\_heads}$ so block_i maps exactly to head_i’s subspace. If you want fewer trainable params you can either increase $r$ (smaller blocks) or share blocks across positions (the paper mentions the option $R_i=R_j$).

Practical replication tips that follow the paper’s statements. The paper says “we only apply OFT to finetune the attention weights” and “to ensure a fair comparison to LoRA, we only apply OFT or COFT to the same layer where LoRA is used.” Thus to reproduce their comparison you should attach OFT to exactly the same attention projection layers that you would attach LoRA to in your baseline (many LoRA recipes attach to the attention projection matrices — commonly the query and/or value projections, but the safe replication rule from the paper is: apply OFT to whatever projection(s) you used LoRA on). During training keep the pretrained weight $W^0$ frozen and only update the Cayley parameters (the $Q$ blocks or their parametrization). At inference you can multiply $R$ into $W^0$ once to produce a single updated weight and remove any runtime extra ops.

### How is OFT applied to convolutional layers with shape \(C_{out}\times C_{in}\times k_h\times k_w\)?
Question:
The paper mentions interesting interpretations for conv layers and claims certain block structures are analogous to depth-wise transforms. How do you reshape conv weights \(\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{out}\times C_{in}\times k_h\times k_w}\) into a \(d\times n\) matrix to apply \(\mathbf{R}\)? If using \(r=C_{in}\) blocks, what are the exact block dimensions and how does this correspond to mixing within an input channel only? Could you provide a small example (e.g., \(C_{in}=2, k_h=k_w=3\)) to illustrate the mapping and the effect on the convolution?
Answer:
Short answer: treat each output-channel kernel as a $d$-dimensional neuron where $d = C_{in} \cdot k_h \cdot k_w$, so reshape the conv tensor into a matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times C_{out}}$ (columns = output-channel kernels). If you choose $r = C_{in}$ blocks then $R$ is block‑diagonal with $C_{in}$ blocks, each of size $(k_h k_w) \times (k_h k_w)$; each block rotates only the $k_h k_w$ spatial entries of a single input channel, i.e. it mixes spatial positions inside a channel but never mixes across different input channels (this is why the paper says it is analogous to depth‑wise transforms).

More concretely, let $W^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{out} \times C_{in} \times k_h \times k_w}$ be the pretrained conv weights. For $j = 1, \dots, C_{out}$ form the flattened column

$$
w_j := \operatorname{vec}\big(W^0[j,:,:,:]\big) \in \mathbb{R}^{d}, \qquad d = C_{in} k_h k_w,
$$

and stack them $W_{\text{mat}} = [w_1, \dots, w_{C_{out}}] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times C_{out}}$. Choose

$$
\mathbf{R} = \operatorname{diag}(R_1, \dots, R_{C_{in}}),
$$

with each block $R_c \in \mathbb{R}^{(k_h k_w) \times (k_h k_w)}$ orthogonal. The OFT update is

$$
W_{\text{mat}}' = \mathbf{R}\, W_{\text{mat}},
$$

and then reshape each column back to $(C_{in}, k_h, k_w)$ to recover the new conv tensor.

Elementwise this means for output channel $j$ and input channel $c$ at spatial position $(u,v)$

$$
W'_{j,c,u,v}
= \sum_{p=1}^{k_h} \sum_{q=1}^{k_w} \big(R_c\big)_{(u,v),(p,q)}\; W^0_{j,c,p,q},
$$

so each new $3\times 3$ (in your example) kernel for input channel $c$ is a linear orthogonal combination of the old $3\times 3$ entries for that same channel — no cross‑channel mixing.

Equivalently, in the convolution forward pass a local input patch $x \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ (the concatenation of the $C_{in}$ per‑channel $k_h \times k_w$ patches) produces outputs $z = W_{\text{mat}}'^{\top} x = W_{\text{mat}}^{\top}(\mathbf{R}^{\top} x)$. Thus applying block‑diagonal $R$ to weights is the same as applying the block‑wise orthogonal transform $R^{\top}$ to each input channel’s spatial patch before the original kernels are applied — another way to see the “depth‑wise” interpretation.

Small explicit example ($C_{in} = 2$, $k_h = k_w = 3$). Here $d = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 = 18$ and each column $w_j$ has the layout

$$
w_j = \begin{bmatrix} u_{j,1}\\[4pt] u_{j,2}\end{bmatrix},\qquad u_{j,c} \in \mathbb{R}^9
$$

where $u_{j,c}$ is the flattened $3\times 3$ kernel for input channel $c$. With $R = \operatorname{diag}(R_1, R_2)$, $R_1, R_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{9 \times 9}$,

$$
R w_j = \begin{bmatrix} R_1 u_{j,1}\\[4pt] R_2 u_{j,2}\end{bmatrix}.
$$

If, for example, $R_1$ is the $9\times 9$ permutation that flips the $3\times 3$ spatial grid, then every entry of the output channel $j$ that used to read the top‑left weight from input channel 1 will now read the bottom‑right weight (i.e. mixing only spatial positions within channel 1); input channel 2 is transformed independently by $R_2$.

Implementation note: to implement OFT on a conv layer you (a) reshape conv weights to $(d, C_{out})$, (b) apply either the full $R$ or per‑channel small $R_c$ blocks (the latter is cheaper), and (c) reshape back to $(C_{out}, C_{in}, k_h, k_w)$. Using block‑diagonal $R$ with $r = C_{in}$ gives the parameter‑efficient, depthwise‑like behaviour emphasized in the paper and still yields a global orthogonal map (so the hyperspherical angles among the output‑channel neurons are preserved).

### Does re-scaled OFT with a diagonal \(\mathbf{D}\) actually preserve \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})\)? Any side effects on normalization?
Question:
Re-scaled OFT uses \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0\,\mathbf{D}\) with \(\mathbf{D}=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n), s_i>0\), and claims that scaling does not change HE because columns are normalized when computing \(\text{HE}(\cdot)\). Can you provide a short proof that \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\mathbf{D}) = \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)\)? Also, since \(\mathbf{D}\) changes pre-activations, does it interact poorly with LayerNorm/GroupNorm or require additional stabilization (e.g., constraints on \(s_i\) or learning rate)?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — re‑scaled OFT with a positive diagonal $\mathbf{D}=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ preserves hyperspherical energy, and the proof is one line; however $\mathbf{D}$ does change pre‑activation magnitudes and so can interact with normalization layers (LayerNorm/GroupNorm/BatchNorm). In practice you should initialize and constrain the scales (or use a smaller LR / regularizer) to avoid destabilizing activations — the paper notes re‑scaled OFT helps empirically but does not claim any special normalization trick, so a little care is advised.

Proof that $\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\mathbf{D})=\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)$ (short and self‑contained). Let $\mathbf{W}^0=[\mathbf{w}^0_1,\dots,\mathbf{w}^0_n]$ and form $\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\mathbf{D}$. The $i$th column of $\mathbf{W}$ is
$\mathbf{w}_i=\mathbf{R}(s_i\mathbf{w}^0_i)=s_i\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^0_i$. The normalized column used in the hyperspherical energy is
$$
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i=\frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|}=\frac{s_i\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^0_i}{s_i\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^0_i\|}
=\frac{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^0_i}{\|\mathbf{w}^0_i\|}
=\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_i,
$$
where we used $\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u}\|=\|\mathbf{u}\|$ for orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$. Hence for any $i\neq j$
$$
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|=\|\mathbf{R}(\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_j)\|
=\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_j\|.
$$
Since $\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}$, the pairwise distances (and therefore the $\mathrm{HE}$ sum) are identical for $\mathbf{W}$ and $\mathbf{W}^0$. The $s_i>0$ assumption is important: a negative $s_i$ flips direction before normalization and can change angles between columns, so the paper restricts $s_i>0$.

About LayerNorm / GroupNorm / other normalizations. The preservation of $\mathrm{HE}$ is purely a geometric statement about normalized columns and does not imply that the network’s forward / backward numerical behavior is unchanged. Multiplying the $i$th column by $s_i$ multiplies that channel’s pre‑activation $z_i=\mathbf{w}_i^\top x$ by $s_i$, so activation statistics seen by any downstream normalization (and by nonlinearities and residual sums) do change. Whether this is a problem depends on where the normalization sits and whether it has trainable affine parameters. If the linear layer you re‑scale is immediately followed by a LayerNorm/GroupNorm with trainable per‑channel gains, those gains can partially absorb the effect of $D$; if the norm is upstream or frozen, per‑channel scaling will alter the normalized outputs and can affect training stability or require the optimizer to re‑calibrate other parameters.

Practical recommendations (simple, conservative rules consistent with the paper’s presentation). Initialize all $s_i=1$ (so you start exactly at the pretrained network), enforce positivity (e.g. parametrize $s_i=\exp(t_i)$ or $s_i=\text{softplus}(t_i)$ with $t_i\!=\!0$ init), and use milder updates for the scales than for the orthogonal transform and other weights: either a smaller learning rate for the $t_i$, a small weight decay / penalty on $\log s_i$, or a hard/soft clamp such as $s_i\in[1/\alpha,\alpha]$ (typical $\alpha$ might be 1.5–2). Alternatively, share scales across blocks (per‑group instead of per‑neuron) to reduce degrees of freedom. If you observe instability, freeze $D$ for a few warmup steps or add a small regularizer on $\log s_i$ (so the optimizer prefers staying near 1). These are standard stabilizations when you add per‑channel multiplicative freedom; the paper reports that re‑scaled OFT generally helps in their experiments but doesn’t report special normalization fixes, so the above are pragmatic safeguards rather than requirements.

In short: mathematically $\mathrm{HE}$ is preserved (proof above), but because $\mathbf{D}$ changes pre‑activation magnitudes it can interact with normalization layers and the optimizer; initialize $s_i=1$, keep positivity, and control the learning dynamics of $s_i$ (smaller LR, regularization or clipping, or coarser per‑block scales) to avoid instabilities.

### What prevents the hyperspherical energy from blowing up when neurons coincide?
Question:
Because \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i\ne j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}\), it diverges if \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i=\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\). In OFT, this is avoided by construction since orthogonal transforms preserve the original distances. But in practice, with finite precision, could near-collisions cause numerical instability when estimating HE (e.g., for diagnostics)? Do you compute HE explicitly anywhere, and if so do you clip the inverse distances or add \(\delta\)-regularization?
Answer:
Short answer: nothing in OFT creates the singularity because an orthogonal map preserves unit‑sphere distances exactly, so if the pretrained neurons are not coincident then OFT cannot introduce a zero denominator in the hyperspherical energy (HE). In practice the only numerical hazards are (1) pre‑existing very small pairwise distances in the pretrained matrix, and (2) floating‑point roundoff when you explicitly evaluate the HE sum. The paper does not report using HE as a direct training objective (OFT enforces HE preservation by construction via $ \bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 $ with $ \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$), so no special clipping was needed in the method itself; for diagnostics you should compute HE in a numerically stable way (or monitor inner products / Gram matrices instead).

Why OFT avoids blow‑up (exact math). On the unit hypersphere an orthogonal matrix $ \bm{R} $ preserves norms and inner products, so for normalized neurons $ \hat{\bm w}_i $ and $ \hat{\bm w}_j $:
$$
\| \bm{R}\hat{\bm w}_i - \bm{R}\hat{\bm w}_j\| = \|\hat{\bm w}_i - \hat{\bm w}_j\|,
\qquad
(\bm{R}\hat{\bm w}_i)^\top(\bm{R}\hat{\bm w}_j)=\hat{\bm w}_i^\top\hat{\bm w}_j.
$$
Therefore, for $ \text{HE}(\bm{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\|^{-1}$ we have
$$
\text{HE}(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)=\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0),
$$
so OFT cannot introduce a divergence unless the pretrained HE already diverged (this is the statement behind Eq.(1) and the OFT forward pass in Eq.(2) in the paper).

Finite‑precision caveats. In floating point you do not have exact algebraic equality, but usual round‑off errors are tiny compared to typical pairwise distances; the implementation choices in the paper (Cayley parameterization with $ \bm{Q} $ skew‑symmetric, block‑diagonal blocks, and COFT’s projection of $ \bm{Q} $ to keep $ \|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| $ small) all act to keep $ \bm{R} $ well‑behaved and make $ (I-\bm{Q}) $ far from singular. The real risk is if two normalized neurons in the pretrained weights are already extremely close (or identical because of tying/quantization); OFT will preserve that closeness and thus preserve a very large HE value.

Practical, numerically stable diagnostics. The paper uses HE as the motivating measure but enforces it via orthogonality rather than minimizing the raw HE during training, so it does not describe special clipping. If you want to compute HE for monitoring, do one (or more) of the following to avoid blow‑ups:

- Work in the inner‑product domain (numerically safest). Use the Gram / cosine matrix $G_{ij}=\hat{\bm w}_i^\top\hat{\bm w}_j$ and monitor a bounded metric such as $\|G-G^0\|_F$ or $\max_{i,j}|G_{ij}-G^0_{ij}|$. This directly measures the preserved angles (the property OFT enforces) and has no singularities because $G_{ij}\in[-1,1]$.

- If you must compute the original HE, compute it from inner products and clamp the small denominator. Using $\|\hat u-\hat v\|=\sqrt{2(1-\hat u^\top\hat v)}$, define a stabilized HE, for example
$$
\text{HE}_\varepsilon(\bm{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\max\big(1-\hat{\bm w}_i^\top\hat{\bm w}_j,\ \varepsilon\big)}}
\quad\text{or}\quad
\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{\max(\|\hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\|,\ \delta)}.
$$
Choose $\varepsilon,\delta$ relative to your floating‑point precision (e.g. start with $\delta\sim 10^{-6}$ in float32) or compute HE in float64 for diagnostics.

- Avoid the full $O(n^2)$ sum on very wide layers: sample random pairs or use a trimmed / percentile sum (ignore the extreme few smallest distances or report medians) to get robust trends instead of the raw sum.

Implementation note about Cayley / COFT. The Cayley parameterization in Section 3.3 uses $ (I+\bm{Q})(I-\bm{Q})^{-1} $, so numerically you should ensure $I-\bm{Q}$ is well conditioned. COFT explicitly keeps $\bm{Q}$ small (via projection) and initializes $\bm{Q}=0$, which both reduce inversion risk; block‑diagonalization also reduces dimension and condition‑number issues.

Bottom line: OFT does not make HE blow up by construction; if you monitor HE explicitly, prefer monitoring Gram/cosine differences (safe and directly tied to OFT’s invariance) or compute HE with a small additive/clipping epsilon and/or in higher precision.

### Why should preserving \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})\) correlate with preserving generative semantics?
Question:
The paper argues that pairwise angles among neurons carry semantic structure, so preserving \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})\) should help preserve pretrained capabilities. Is there empirical evidence correlating \(\|\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})-\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)\|\) with metrics like CLIP-I/CLIP-T or FID during finetuning? Could there exist weight configurations with identical HE but very different behaviors, and if so, why isn’t that a concern here?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not show a single-layer scatter plot that directly correlates the scalar difference $\|\mathrm{HE}(W)-\mathrm{HE}(W^0)\|$ to CLIP‑I/CLIP‑T or FID. Instead it gives strong, indirect empirical evidence: the Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) construction provably preserves hyperspherical energy and pairwise angles, and the OFT/COFT finetuned models consistently beat baselines on the CLIP/DINO/LPIPS/control‑consistency metrics and exhibit much more stable training. Theoretical caveats are real (many different weight configurations can share the same scalar HE), but they are precisely the reason the paper enforces a much stronger constraint (an orthogonal transform that preserves the full pairwise inner‑product structure) and also uses a radius constraint (COFT) to keep the network close in Euclidean space. Together those design choices and the ablations in the paper are the authors’ empirical answer to the concern.

Why OFT is more than “preserve one scalar (HE)”: hyperspherical energy for a layer is
$$
\mathrm{HE}(W)=\sum_{i\neq j}\big\|\hat{w}_i-\hat{w}_j\big\|^{-1},\qquad \hat{w}_i=\frac{w_i}{\|w_i\|},
$$
so it is a single global summary of pairwise angular geometry. An orthogonal matrix $R$ satisfies $R^\top R=I$, and therefore for normalized neurons
$$
\big\|R\hat{w}_i-R\hat{w}_j\big\|=\big\|\hat{w}_i-\hat{w}_j\big\|,
$$
hence $\mathrm{HE}(RW)=\mathrm{HE}(W)$. More strongly, an orthogonal left‑multiplication preserves every pairwise inner product:
$$
(R\hat{w}_i)^\top(R\hat{w}_j)=\hat{w}_i^\top\hat{w}_j,
$$
so OFT preserves the entire Gram matrix of normalized neurons in a layer, not just the scalar HE. That is the key difference between “minimize a scalar HE difference” and “enforce an orthogonal transform”: OFT gives a far stronger structural invariance.

What the paper actually shows empirically: the authors report that OFT/COFT (which by construction preserve pairwise angles) attain better subject fidelity and prompt fidelity (metrics reported include CLIP‑I, CLIP‑T and DINO) and produce more stable convergence than DreamBooth and LoRA. They also include a toy autoencoder experiment showing that angular information carries most semantic content, and an ablation where removing the orthogonality constraint breaks generation. These pieces of evidence are all consistent with the hypothesis that preserving angular structure helps preserve pretrained generative semantics. The paper does not, however, present a line‑by‑line numerical correlation (e.g., scatter plots of per‑layer $\|\mathrm{HE}(W)-\mathrm{HE}(W^0)\|$ against CLIP or FID) in the main text.

Can identical HE still give different behavior? Yes, in principle. HE is a single scalar; many different pairwise angle matrices can produce the same HE value. Two concrete failure modes are (1) different distributions of pairwise angles that sum to the same HE, and (2) arbitrary per‑neuron radial scaling (HE uses normalized vectors so it ignores magnitudes), which can change network function even when HE is identical. Because of that theoretical non‑uniqueness, preserving the scalar HE alone would not guarantee identical behavior.

Why that non‑uniqueness is not the practical concern here: OFT was not designed to freeze a single scalar; it enforces layerwise orthogonal transforms which preserve the full set of pairwise inner products (the Gram matrix) and thus retain a much stronger fingerprint of the layer’s geometry. COFT adds an explicit $\|R-I\|\le\epsilon$‑type constraint (implemented as a small constraint on the Cayley parameter) so the finetuned weights also remain close in Euclidean terms to the pretrained ones. Empirically, those two design choices (full pairwise preservation via orthogonality, plus a radius constraint) are sufficient for the generation tasks evaluated: OFT/COFT maintain pretrained capability better than baselines, converge faster, and avoid collapse—which is the practical desideratum.

What would be useful follow‑ups (if you want a tighter empirical linkage)? The natural next experiments the paper doesn’t fully report are (a) per‑layer plots of HE difference (and full Gram‑matrix Frobenius difference) over finetuning steps for each method, (b) scatter plots between those per‑layer differences and downstream metrics (CLIP‑I, CLIP‑T, DINO, FID) across seeds and checkpoints, and (c) representational similarity measures (CKA or RSA) between hidden activations of pretrained and finetuned models. Those analyses would quantify how tightly HE (or the full Gram matrix) tracks semantic performance, and would separate the contributions of angular preservation versus Euclidean closeness.

In short: the paper provides convincing indirect empirical evidence that preserving neuron angles (via orthogonal transforms that preserve the full pairwise inner products) correlates with preserving generative semantics; the theoretical possibility that identical scalar HE masks different behaviors is real, but OFT’s orthogonality + COFT’s radius constraint were chosen to remove that ambiguity in practice and the experiments show this suffices for the finetuning tasks studied.

### Toy autoencoder: what are the exact formulas for "magnitude-only" and "angle-only" feature maps?
Question:
In the toy experiment, at test time you compare (a) the inner product used in training, (b) magnitude-only, and (c) angular-only activations, and show that the angular information nearly reconstructs the input. Could you specify precisely what (b) and (c) compute? For a filter \(\mathbf{w}\) and patch \(\mathbf{x}\), is (b) \(z=\|\mathbf{w}\|\,\|\mathbf{x}\|\) and (c) \(z=\cos \angle(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|\,\|\mathbf{x}\|}\)? If so, how is the decoder trained to handle these alternative feature maps at test time, given it only saw dot products during training?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — in the toy autoencoder the three test-time alternatives come from the exact factorization of the dot product. For a filter $\mathbf{w}$ and a local input patch $\mathbf{x}$ the trained activation is
$$
z = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} = \|\mathbf{w}\|\,\|\mathbf{x}\|\,\cos\theta,
\qquad \theta=\angle(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}).
$$
At test time the paper replaces this $z$ by either (b) the magnitude-only scalar
$$
z_{\text{mag}}=\|\mathbf{w}\|\,\|\mathbf{x}\|,
$$
or (c) the angular (cosine) scalar
$$
z_{\text{ang}}=\cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|\,\|\mathbf{x}\|}.
$$
The authors explicitly note that the network was trained using the ordinary inner products $z=\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}$ and that the cosine/normalized maps were only used at test time.

Why the decoder still works when you feed these alternative maps. Write the channel vector of activations for a patch as $\mathbf{z} = [z_j]_j = \mathbf{m}\odot\mathbf{a}$ where $m_j=\|\mathbf{w}_j\|\,\|\mathbf{x}\|$ and $a_j=\cos\theta_j$. Feeding the decoder the magnitude-only maps replaces $\mathbf{z}$ by $\mathbf{m}$ (discarding $\mathbf{a}$); feeding the decoder the angular-only maps replaces $\mathbf{z}$ by $\mathbf{a}$ (discarding $\mathbf{m}$). Empirically the paper shows that $\mathbf{a}$ (the directions / cosines) preserves most of the semantic and spatial structure of the patch, whereas $\mathbf{m}$ does not.

Intuitively this happens for two related reasons. First, $\mathbf{a}$ encodes the relative alignment of the patch with many filters (a phase-like, sign-and-pattern signal), and edges / shapes in images are primarily carried by relative orientations rather than absolute per-filter magnitudes. Second, $\mathbf{m}$ often factorizes as $\|\mathbf{x}\|$ times a per-filter constant $\|\mathbf{w}_j\|$; that common scalar $\|\mathbf{x}\|$ collapses much of the useful channel-wise variation, so magnitude-only maps become low-information for reconstruction. The decoder trained on full dot-products is therefore robust: when given $\mathbf{a}$ it still sees the informative cross-channel pattern that it learned to decode, and whatever global scale information is missing can be (partly) absorbed/compensated by biases, channel mixing in the decoder or by spatial context.

Practical notes: computing $\cos\theta$ requires dividing by norms so in implementation one adds a small $\varepsilon$ for numerical stability. Also, this is an empirical demonstration (the paper stresses it): the decoder was not re-trained on cosine activations — the near-perfect reconstruction from $z_{\text{ang}}$ is evidence that neuron direction (angle) carries the bulk of the semantic signal, which is exactly the intuition the paper uses to motivate orthogonal finetuning.

### How does OFT enable controllable generation without extra inference modules like ControlNet?
Question:
For controllable generation (e.g., edges or segmentation maps as control), ControlNet and T2I-Adapter add extra networks or adapters. The paper claims OFT adds no inference-time overhead. How are control signals injected into Stable Diffusion in OFT: additional input channels to the UNet, conditioning via cross-attention (e.g., tokenizing the control), or some other mechanism? If extra inputs are added, why is there no additional computation compared to the vanilla model?
Answer:
Short answer: OFT does not add a new inference-time module (like ControlNet’s extra conditioned UNet or T2I‑Adapter blocks). Instead OFT is a re-parameterization of existing layers: during finetuning it learns a layer-wise orthogonal transform $R$ so that the finetuned weight is

$$W = R W^0$$,

with $R$ orthogonal ($R^T R = I$). At test time you simply multiply $R$ into the pretrained weights once (or materialize $W$ offline) and run the original UNet forward as usual. That is why OFT has no extra per‑step inference cost.

Why this answers your two subquestions. The paper is deliberately architecture‑agnostic about “how” the control signal is presented — OFT is a finetuning method that can be applied to whatever weights are used to process the control in your setup (attention projection matrices or convolutional kernels). Concretely, in the experiments the authors applied OFT to the same layers where LoRA would be applied (they say “To compare fairly to LoRA, we only apply OFT to finetune the attention weights in our experiments”), so the model learns to use the control through the existing conditioning pathway (i.e. the cross‑attention / attention projections). More generally, if you feed the control as extra spatial channels to the UNet, you can apply OFT to the convolution weights that consume those channels; if you encode the control as extra conditioning tokens, you apply OFT to the attention weights. OFT itself does not prescribe a new encoder or an extra branch — it just adapts the existing weights so the original network can consume the control signal.

A bit more intuition and technical detail. OFT constrains the finetuning to orthogonal rotations (optionally block‑diagonal, Cayley‑parameterized for efficiency), which preserves pairwise neuron angles (the paper’s hyperspherical energy argument). During training you update the small parameterization of $R$; after training you fold $R$ into $W^0$ to get the final $W$. Folding is a one‑time weight update (or you can materialize $W$ when exporting the model); after that the runtime forward pass is identical to the vanilla UNet: same number of layers, same number of FLOPs, same memory pattern. (Note: if your conditioning pipeline requires an external encoder to convert a raw control map into tokens or a latent, that encoder’s cost still exists — OFT removes extra UNet branches, not the cost of any necessary preprocessing.)

So in short: OFT injects control capability by changing the pretrained weights (multiplicatively, orthogonally) in the layers that process conditioning. Because the learned transforms are absorbed into the base weights at test time ($W = R W^0$), there is no additional inference module or per‑step computation compared to the vanilla model.

### Details of the "control consistency" metrics for C2I, S2I, and L2F
Question:
The paper proposes a control consistency evaluation: (1) For Canny-to-Image, how are edges extracted from generated images (which Canny parameters or edge detector), and how exactly are IoU and F1 computed and thresholded? (2) For Segmentation-to-Image, which segmentation model is used to parse the generated images, and are metrics (mIoU, mean/overall accuracy) computed on the model’s argmax outputs or probabilities? (3) For Landmark-to-Face, which landmark detector is used, how are faces aligned or cropped, and how is the \(\ell_2\) distance normalized (e.g., inter-ocular distance)? How sensitive are the reported gains to these choices?
Answer:
Short answer: the main paper only states the high-level evaluation protocol — C2I is measured with $\text{IoU}$ and $\text{F1}$, S2I with mean $\text{IoU}$ and mean/overall accuracy, and L2F with mean $\ell_2$ distance between control and detected landmarks — and refers readers to Appendix A (Appendix~\ref{app:settings} in the paper) for “more details regarding the consistency metrics.” The paper also explicitly cautions that the control-consistency numbers are valid “up to the accuracy of the off-the-shelf segmentation/detection model.” The appendix (or the project code, if available) is where the exact detector, Canny parameters, any cropping/alignment, and normalization choices are given. Below I explain the standard, canonical implementations of these metrics, how they are computed in practice, and how sensitive the reported gains typically are to those implementation choices.

Canny-to-Image (C2I). In practice you extract edges from a generated image with an edge detector (classic Canny or a learned edge detector). Classic Canny returns a binary map after non-maximum suppression and hysteresis thresholding controlled by two thresholds (low and high) and often a Gaussian smoothing sigma. The evaluation then compares that binary edge map to the input control edge map. The two scalar metrics the paper names are computed in the usual pixel-wise way: with true positives ($\text{TP}$), false positives ($\text{FP}$) and false negatives ($\text{FN}$), $$\text{IoU}=\frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP}+\text{FP}+\text{FN}},\qquad \text{F1}=\frac{2\text{TP}}{2\text{TP}+\text{FP}+\text{FN}}.$$ Because edges are extremely thin and sensitive to small spatial shifts, many papers apply a small tolerance when matching edge pixels (count predicted edge pixel as $\text{TP}$ if it lies within $r$ pixels of any GT edge pixel), or they dilate one of the maps by a few pixels before computing $\text{TP}$/$\text{FP}$/$\text{FN}$. If the authors used plain pixel-wise $\text{IoU}$ on nondilated Canny maps it will be stricter (lower $\text{IoU}$) and very sensitive to small misalignments; if they used tolerance/dilation it is more robust. The Canny thresholds (and whether Canny vs. a learned edge detector was used) will change absolute $\text{IoU}$/$\text{F1}$ numbers but, if the same edge-extraction pipeline is used for all methods, relative comparisons (who is better) are usually stable.

Segmentation-to-Image (S2I). The paper says they “compute mean $\text{IoU}$, mean and overall accuracy” by parsing the generated image with an off-the-shelf segmentation model (they point to Appendix for details). The standard definitions are: per-class $\text{IoU}$ for class $c$ is $$\text{IoU}_c=\frac{\text{TP}_c}{\text{TP}_c+\text{FP}_c+\text{FN}_c},$$ and mean $\text{IoU}$ ($\text{mIoU}$) is the average over $C$ classes, $\text{mIoU}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_c \text{IoU}_c$. Mean accuracy is usually the mean of per-class pixel accuracies $\frac{1}{C}\sum_c \frac{\text{TP}_c}{\text{TP}_c+\text{FN}_c}$, and overall accuracy is the total correct pixels divided by total pixels, $$\text{overall accuracy}=\frac{\sum_c \text{TP}_c}{\text{total pixels}}.$$ Most implementations take the segmentation model’s per-pixel class probabilities and convert them to hard labels with $\arg\max$ before computing these discrete metrics; this is the standard $\text{mIoU}$ pipeline the paper likely uses (unless they explicitly say they used soft/expected $\text{IoU}$). Using soft (probability-weighted) $\text{IoU}$ or thresholding on probabilities can change numbers slightly; the dominant factor in sensitivity is the particular pretrained segmentation network (its taxonomy/classes, label mapping to ADE20K classes, and robustness to synthetic/generated image artifacts). Again, if the same parser is used for all methods the ranking is robust, but absolute $\text{mIoU}$ values depend on the chosen model.

Landmark-to-Face (L2F). The paper reports “mean $\ell_2$ distance between control landmarks and predicted landmarks.” The generic formula is, for each image with $K$ landmarks, $$\text{mean-}\ell_2 = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K \lVert p_k - g_k\rVert_2,$$ averaged over images. The main implementation questions are which landmark detector is used, whether images are first cropped/aligned by a face detector, and whether the raw Euclidean distance is normalized. The paper does not specify these details in the main text; the appendix or code typically lists the detector (common choices are dlib 68-point, FAN, MediaPipe, or HRNet-based detectors). Practically, to make distances comparable across face size and image resolution you normalize by a scale factor $s$ (common choices are inter-ocular distance, distance between outer eye corners, or the face bounding-box diagonal). A normalized error looks like $$\text{norm-}\ell_2=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K \frac{\lVert p_k - g_k\rVert_2}{s}.$$ If the paper reports un-normalized pixel $\ell_2$ then results depend on image scale/cropping; if they normalize by inter-ocular distance the metric becomes scale-invariant and more interpretable. Cropping/alignment (e.g., detecting face box and resizing to a canonical size before running the landmark detector) reduces variance and is standard.

How sensitive are the reported gains? The paper itself states the caveat that the control-consistency scores are valid “up to the accuracy of the off-the-shelf segmentation/detection model,” which is exactly the point: absolute metric values move around with detector choice, thresholds, and pre-processing, but comparative gains between finetuning methods are usually robust so long as you use the same exact parsing pipeline for all methods in the comparison. The main failure modes are (a) the parser fails systematically on images produced by one method but not others (for example artifacts that break a segmentation model), which can produce an artificial advantage or penalty; (b) for edge evaluation small misalignments matter unless you allow a tolerance radius or dilation; and (c) different landmark detectors have different pose/occlusion robustness, which changes absolute $\ell_2$ and potentially ranking for hard poses.

If you want reproducible, stable comparisons, I recommend checking the Appendix/code for the exact detector and parameters used in the paper; if you re-run experiments, keep the parsing pipeline identical across methods, and add robustness checks: vary Canny thresholds or use a small spatial tolerance for edge matching; evaluate S2I with two different segmentation networks or report per-class $\text{IoU}$ as well as $\text{mIoU}$; and for L2F report both raw $\ell_2$ and normalized $\ell_2$ (inter-ocular normalization) and state the face-cropping/alignment procedure. Those checks will show whether the observed improvements are implementation artifacts or true gains.

If you want, paste Appendix~\ref{app:settings} (or a link to the authors’ code) and I’ll extract the exact detectors/parameters they used and comment on how much those specific choices could have affected the paper’s reported numbers.

### Under what conditions is \((\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I})\mathbf{W}^0\) effectively low-rank, as suggested in the LoRA connection?
Question:
The paper notes \(\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{W}^0 + (\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I})\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}\) and says the update term is analogous to LoRA. However, \(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\) is generally full-rank if \(\mathbf{R}\ne\mathbf{I}\). When does \(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\) become low-rank in practice? Does the block-diagonal structure of \(\mathbf{R}\) imply anything about the rank of \((\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I})\mathbf{W}^0\), or is the analogy purely conceptual rather than a strict rank constraint?
Answer:
Short answer: $(R - I) W^0$ is low‑rank only when the multiplicative perturbation $R - I$ itself is (or acts like) a low‑rank operator, or when $W^0$ is rank‑deficient. Because the pretrained weight $W^0$ is typically full‑rank, the rank of the additive update is limited by the rank of $R - I$ (indeed $\operatorname{rank}((R-I)W^0)\le\min(\operatorname{rank}(R-I),\operatorname{rank}(W^0))$). The paper’s “connection to LoRA” is therefore mainly conceptual: both produce an additive correction to $W^0$, but OFT enforces orthogonality / block structure rather than an explicit low‑rank factorization. Below I give the linear‑algebra intuition and the practical regimes where the OFT update can be (or look) low‑rank.

Why the rank is controlled by $R-I$. Because
$$\operatorname{rank}((R-I)W^0)\le\min\big(\operatorname{rank}(R-I),\operatorname{rank}(W^0)\big),$$
and the paper states $W^0$ is typically full rank, a low rank additive update requires $R-I$ to be low rank (or that $W^0$ has a small effective rank). So the question reduces to: when is $R-I$ low‑rank (or effectively low‑rank)?

Practical conditions that make $R-I$ low‑rank or “effectively” low‑rank:
1. $R$ is an identity plus a low‑rank perturbation. If one could write $R=I+UV^\top$ (or more generally $R=I+$ a matrix of low column/row dimension), then $R-I=UV^\top$ is low rank and $(R-I)W^0$ is low rank. Note: exact orthogonality constrains this form, so constructing an orthogonal $R$ with an exact $I+UV^\top$ decomposition is nontrivial; however small perturbations can approximate this situation.
2. Cayley param + low‑rank skew perturbation. The paper uses the Cayley map $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ with $Q^\top=-Q$ and notes the Neumann expansion $R\approx I+2Q+O(Q^2)$ for small $Q$. If $Q$ is chosen to be low‑rank (e.g. built from a few independent $2\times 2$ Givens/skew blocks), then $R-I\approx 2Q$ is low‑rank (or dominated by a few singular directions). So a low‑rank skew‑symmetric $Q$ gives an (approximate) low‑rank $R-I$.
3. Block‑diagonal pattern with few active blocks. The paper recommends a block‑diagonal $R=\operatorname{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r)$. That enforces structured sparsity, but it does not by itself guarantee low rank: each block difference $R_i-I$ can still be full rank (up to the block size). However if only a small number of blocks deviate from identity (or those deviating blocks are themselves low‑rank perturbations), then $R-I$ has reduced rank equal to the sum of the (small) ranks of the deviating blocks. In other words, block structure gives a lever to concentrate changes into a limited subspace.
4. Small‑norm / constrained deviations (COFT) produce effective low rank. COFT constrains $\|R-I\|\le\epsilon$. Small operator norm does not force low algebraic rank, but in practice a small, structured perturbation often has fast spectral decay so $(R-I)W^0$ can be “effectively” low‑rank (only a few singular values matter). Initialization at $R=I$ and tiny updates (common in finetuning) make the additive update numerically low‑energy and often compressible.
5. Alignment with $W^0$’s singular directions. Even if $R-I$ is not low rank, if its action lies mostly in directions where $W^0$ has small singular values (or if $W^0$ itself is approximately low‑rank in practice), the product can have fast singular‑value decay and be effectively low‑rank.

Why the LoRA link is mainly conceptual. LoRA enforces an explicit additive low‑rank parametrization $A B^\top$ for the update. OFT writes the forward pass as $W^0+(R-I)W^0$, so the additive term looks similar, but OFT does not impose a strict rank constraint on $R-I$ — it imposes orthogonality and (optionally) block structure and small deviation. Those are different inductive biases: LoRA reduces rank directly; OFT reduces degrees of freedom by orthogonality and sparsity of blocks. Thus the resemblance to LoRA is useful intuition (both are small-parameter additive corrections) but not a formal equivalence unless you explicitly constrain $R-I$ to be low rank (for example by choosing a low‑rank skew $Q$, using only a few Givens rotations, or leaving most blocks equal to identity).

If you need a strict low‑rank guarantee while staying in the OFT style, you would have to alter the parameterization: e.g. parameterize $Q$ as a low‑rank skew‑symmetric factor (so $Q$ has only $k\ll d$ active dimensions), or only allow a few small rotation blocks to differ from identity. The paper does not enforce those specific low‑rank parametrizations, which is why the authors frame the LoRA connection as an analogy rather than a proof that OFT enforces low rank.

Summary: mathematically the rank of the OFT additive update is bounded by $\operatorname{rank}(R-I)$, so low rank requires $R-I$ to be low rank (or $W^0$ to be rank‑deficient). Block‑diagonal parameterization gives structure and parameter efficiency but does not automatically make $(R-I)W^0$ low‑rank; small deviations from identity, low‑rank skew perturbations (via Cayley), or concentrating changes into a few blocks/subspaces are the practical ways OFT can produce an effectively low‑rank update — hence the LoRA analogy is useful but not a strict identity.

### Does OFT preserve angles only within a layer, and could cross-layer relationships be disrupted?
Question:
HE is defined per layer as a function of pairwise angles among that layer’s neurons. If OFT preserves each layer’s intra-layer angles but different layers learn different \(\mathbf{R}_\ell\), could this disrupt cross-layer feature alignments that matter for diffusion models (e.g., encoder-decoder skip connections)? Have you measured any notion of cross-layer "angle structure" or is preserving per-layer HE empirically sufficient?
Answer:
Short answer: OFT guarantees preservation of intra‑layer angles exactly, but it does not mathematically guarantee preservation of cross‑layer alignments when different orthogonal transforms are learned for different layers. In the paper we did not introduce or report an explicit cross‑layer “angle” statistic — instead we validated sufficiency of per‑layer hyperspherical energy preservation by showing that OFT/COFT keeps the pretrained model’s generative behavior (CLIP/DINO/quality metrics, convergence and control consistency) on the diffusion tasks. COFT (the radius/identity constraint) and the block/tied parameterizations are practical knobs that reduce the chance of harmful cross‑layer misalignment.

Why OFT preserves only intra‑layer angles (and how to see possible cross‑layer change). By construction OFT replaces a pretrained layer $W^0$ by $W = R W^0$ with $R$ orthogonal. For two normalized neurons in that layer $\hat w_i^0, \hat w_j^0$ we have
$$
\langle R\hat w_i^0,\,R\hat w_j^0\rangle
=\hat w_i^{0\top} R^\top R \hat w_j^0
=\hat w_i^{0\top}\hat w_j^0,
$$
so all pairwise cosines (and hence the hyperspherical energy) within that layer are unchanged. That is the provable invariance the paper relies on.

However, cross‑layer composition can change unless the orthogonal transforms are compatible. For two successive linear layers (with matching feature dimension) $W_1^0$ and $W_2^0$, after OFT we have $W_1 = R_1 W_1^0$ and $W_2 = R_2 W_2^0$, and the two‑layer linear map becomes
$$
(W_2)^\top (W_1)^\top
=(W_2^0)^\top R_2^\top R_1 (W_1^0)^\top.
$$
So a factor $R_2^\top R_1$ is inserted into the composition. Only when $R_2^\top R_1 = I$ (for example $R_2 = R_1$) is the cross‑layer linear mapping preserved exactly. In other words, independently learned $R_\ell$ can change how representations produced by one layer align to the next. The same point applies to skip connections in U‑Net style diffusion models: if an encoder feature is rotated by $R_{\text{enc}}$ and the matching decoder receives features in a different coordinate frame rotated by $R_{\text{dec}}$, a direct add/concat can be misaligned unless the rotations are compatible or small.

What the paper actually measured and what was observed empirically. The paper does not report an explicit cross‑layer “angle structure” statistic (no measurements of e.g. $R_{l+1}^\top R_l$ or inter‑layer cosine matrices). Instead the evaluation is task‑level: subject fidelity (DINO/CLIP‑I), prompt fidelity, control consistency, convergence and qualitative image quality on Stable Diffusion (which uses a U‑Net with skip connections). Across those diffusion tasks OFT and COFT worked very well and were more stable than baselines. That empirical success indicates that—at least for the layers and parameterizations used in the paper (attention layers, block‑diagonal $R$, COFT’s small deviation constraint when used)—preserving per‑layer hyperspherical energy was practically sufficient for the tasks tested.

Practical implications and remedies if you worry about cross‑layer misalignment. If your model or task is especially sensitive to precise encoder–decoder alignment (or you see failures), there are simple ways to reduce the risk: (1) use COFT to enforce $\|R-I\|\le\epsilon$ so each rotation is small and relative misalignment is limited; (2) tie or share the same orthogonal $R$ across layers that must remain aligned (for example tie encoder/decoder pairs or skip‑paired layers); (3) add an explicit cross‑layer regularizer such as minimizing $\|R_{l+1}^\top R_l - I\|_F$ for adjacent/paired layers; (4) measure representational similarity (CKA/SVCCA) or directly compute per‑pair neuron cosines before/after finetuning to detect misalignment; or (5) apply OFT only to layers whose intra‑layer geometry matters but that do not form critical direct links (this is what we did for fair comparison to LoRA in the experiments: we applied OFT to the same attention layers LoRA targets).

If you want to test cross‑layer alignment yourself, straightforward diagnostics are: compute $\|R_{l+1}^\top R_l - I\|_F$ for connected layers, compare pre/post finetuning canonical correlations or CKA between corresponding layer activations, or compute pairwise cosines between encoder feature channels and decoder receptive directions used in skip connections.

Bottom line: OFT’s theoretical guarantee is intra‑layer only; cross‑layer alignments are not guaranteed but can be controlled in practice (COFT, block/tied parameterizations, small rotations) and the downstream empirical results in the paper show that per‑layer HE preservation was sufficient for the diffusion tasks evaluated. If you suspect tasks where strict cross‑layer alignment matters, try tying transforms or add a small cross‑layer consistency regularizer and monitor the simple diagnostics above.

### How should we choose the number of blocks \(r\) in block-diagonal \(\mathbf{R}\)?
Question:
The parameter count and compute scale with the number of blocks \(r\). Are there heuristics to set \(r\) relative to the layer width \(d\), data size, or task difficulty? Do you have ablations showing the stability/flexibility trade-off as \(r\) varies (e.g., convergence speed, final controllability, and risk of collapse)?
Answer:
Short answer: treat $r$ as an explicit regularizer that trades off flexibility (smaller $r$, bigger blocks, more parameters) vs stability/simplicity (larger $r$, smaller blocks, fewer parameters). The paper gives the parameter-count formulas and the conceptual trade‑off, but it does not present a full sweep/ablation over $r$ in the main text — it reports that (1) increasing the number of trainable parameters improves performance (i.e. moving toward fewer blocks) and (2) the block‑diagonal factorization itself acts as a useful regularizer that improves stability. Use an architecture‑aware starting point (blocks aligned with channels/heads), then sweep $r$ while monitoring controllability and training stability; if you need extra stability use COFT (small $\epsilon$) or increase $r$.

Why $r$ matters (math + intuition). For a layer of width $d$ a full orthogonal $d\times d$ matrix has $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ degrees of freedom ($\mathcal{O}(d^2)$). With $r$ equal blocks of size $d/r$ the number of learnable parameters in the orthogonal factor is
$$
p_R \;=\; r\cdot\frac{(d/r)\,(d/r-1)}{2}
     \;=\; \frac{d(d/r-1)}{2}
     \approx \frac{d^2}{2r}\qquad (d\gg r).
$$
If you share the same block across the diagonal the parameter count drops further to
$$
p_{R,\text{shared}} \;=\; \frac{(d/r)\,(d/r-1)}{2}\approx \frac{d^2}{2r^2}.
$$
So increasing $r$ reduces parameters (and hence the transform’s capacity) roughly as $1/r$ (or $1/r^2$ with block sharing). Intuitively, fewer blocks (small $r$) let the orthogonal transform be more expressive (closer to a full rotation) which helps hard/finely controlled tasks but increases the risk of overfitting or destabilizing the pretrained geometry; more blocks (large $r$) restrict the degrees of freedom and act like a structural regularizer, improving stability and sample efficiency.

Heuristics to pick $r$ (practical guidance grounded in the paper)
- Budget / parameter targets: choose $r$ so $p_R$ fits your trainable-parameter budget. The paper demonstrates this trade‑off explicitly (example: $d\!=\!128$, $r\!=\!8$ gives $960$ params for OFT), and it compares OFT to LoRA by matching parameter budgets. If you want parity with an existing adapter (LoRA) use $r$ that yields similar trainable-parameter count.
- Data size / task difficulty: for low-data or easy tasks, increase $r$ (smaller per‑block dimension) to regularize. For high-data or very fine-grained control tasks, decrease $r$ to give OFT more capacity. The paper reports that OFT’s performance improves as the number of trainable parameters grows (i.e., by reducing $r$), but also that block‑diagonalization and COFT improve stability when flexibility is limited.
- Architecture alignment: use structure-aware blocks. For convolution layers the paper explicitly notes that setting the number of blocks equal to the number of input channels makes each block act like a per‑channel (depth‑wise) orthogonal transform; this is a natural inductive bias. By analogy, for Transformer attention one can make block sizes align with head dimensions so each block covers one head’s subspace — this preserves natural modularity and is often a sensible starting point.
- Extra knobs: if you need both angle‑preservation and some per‑neuron scaling, use the re‑scaled OFT variant (learn diagonal $\bm{D}$) rather than only enlarging blocks; if you want stronger stability regardless of $r$, use COFT and tune $\epsilon$ to control deviation from identity.

What the paper reports about the trade‑off (and what it does not). The paper frames exactly this flexibility/stability trade‑off: block‑diagonalization is explicitly called a stronger regularization and COFT adds an explicit deviation constraint for more stable finetuning. Empirically the paper shows OFT/COFT converge faster and more stably than LoRA and DreamBooth with comparable or fewer parameters, and it observes that performance still improves as the number of trainable parameters increases (i.e. the authors did not see a hard saturation). However, the main text does not present a dedicated, systematic ablation plot that sweeps $r$ and reports convergence speed, final control‑consistency metric, and collapse probability as a function of $r$. The authors list improving parameter‑efficiency and the bias introduced by block structure as open problems, so a thorough $r$–sweep is left to future work.

A practical tuning recipe you can run quickly. Pick a small set of candidate block sizes and measure both convergence curves and the paper’s controllability/quality metrics (control‑consistency, CLIP/DINO for subject fidelity, LPIPS for diversity). It is convenient to parametrize by block size $s=d/r$ and try powers-of-two divisors of $d$ (for example try $s\in\{d, d/2, d/4, d/8\}$ or equivalently $r\in\{1,2,4,8\}$ when they divide $d$). For each candidate monitor: (i) validation control‑consistency (the paper’s metric), (ii) convergence speed (metric vs epoch), (iii) hyperspherical energy difference (the paper’s preservation quantity), and (iv) signs of collapse (sudden drop in fidelity or degenerate samples). If you see instability/collapse increase $r$ or switch to COFT with a smaller $\epsilon$; if you see underfitting, decrease $r$ (bigger blocks) or add the diagonal rescaling.

Concise summary. $r$ controls a clear capacity–stability axis: smaller $r$ = higher capacity (better final controllability given enough data/computation) ; larger $r$ = stronger regularization and better stability/sample efficiency. The paper gives the parameter scaling formulas and demonstrates that more OFT capacity tends to help, while block structure and COFT improve stability — but it does not publish a full ablation grid over $r$, so the exact sweet spot will usually be task- and budget-dependent; follow an architecture‑aware starting point and then sweep $r$ with the metrics suggested above.

### What quantitative criterion defines "collapse" in the subject-driven experiments?
Question:
The paper states that DreamBooth collapses after 2000 iterations while OFT/COFT remain stable. What metric is used to define collapse (e.g., reconstruction loss explosion, CLIP-I degradation, DINO score drop, or visual artifacts)? Is there a reproducible threshold or procedure to detect collapse across subjects?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not give a single formal numeric definition of “collapse.” In the subject-driven experiments the authors label DreamBooth as having “collapsed” based on the visible degradation of generated samples (identity loss / visual artifacts) and this qualitative failure is corroborated by the subject‑fidelity metrics they report (DINO and CLIP‑I). For model selection and reporting they explicitly monitor validation CLIP metrics (and report DINO / CLIP‑I for subject fidelity, CLIP‑T for prompt fidelity and LPIPS for diversity), and they also average results across $30$ random seeds. There is no fixed scalar threshold in the paper that says “below $X = \text{collapse}$.”

What this means in practice (and how to reproduce the paper’s observation) is straightforward: keep a held‑out validation set of subject images, sample validation prompts periodically during finetuning, generate images, and track the mean DINO and CLIP‑I similarity between generated images and the real subject images. The authors state they pick the final checkpoint by best validation CLIP metrics, and the reported DreamBooth “collapse after $2000$ iterations” is the point where generated images visibly degrade (identity replaced / hallucinated textures) and the subject‑fidelity metrics stop improving and begin to worsen. The paper does not report tracking a exploding reconstruction loss or any other single training‑loss signal as the collapse criterion.

If you want a reproducible automated detector (the paper doesn’t prescribe one), a simple procedure consistent with the paper is: hold out validation images, evaluate mean CLIP‑I and/or DINO every $N$ iterations, and treat a sustained drop from the peak value (e.g., a relative drop of several percent or a drop greater than a few standard deviations of your validation runs sustained for $K$ checkpoints) as collapse. Calibrate the exact numeric thresholds per dataset and model because absolute cosine values depend on evaluation setup. Also follow the paper’s practice of multiple random seeds so you can distinguish noisy fluctuations from true degradation. Finally, always inspect example images: the paper’s collapse description is grounded in qualitative failures (identity loss, wrong textures like “yellow fur” instead of “yellow shirt”) as much as in the metric trends.

Conceptually this matches why OFT/COFT behave better in the paper: they preserve neuron angular structure (hyperspherical energy) and—especially COFT—enforce a small deviation from pretrained weights, which prevents the kind of semantic drift that produces the visual/metric degradation labeled as “collapse.”

### Does restricting to \(\det(\mathbf{R})=1\) (Cayley) hurt compared to allowing reflections?
Question:
Cayley parameterization yields \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathrm{SO}(d)\) with \(\det(\mathbf{R})=1\). Do you expect any benefit from also allowing \(\det(\mathbf{R})=-1\) (reflections) by using, say, Householder products or the matrix exponential parameterization \(\mathbf{R}=\exp(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}^\top)\)? Did you try alternatives (e.g., Lezcano’s parametrizations) and see differences in convergence or final metrics?
Answer:
Short answer: In this paper we used the Cayley parametrization (so $R\in\mathrm{SO}(d)$, $\det(R)=1$) and found that its restriction to rotations did not hurt empirical performance. The paper explicitly notes “the Cayley parameterization can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$ … Fortunately, we find that such a limitation does not affect the performance in practice.” We did not report a head‑to‑head ablation that replaces Cayley with a Householder/product parameterization or Lezcano-style alternatives, so a rigorous comparison is left for future work — but there are simple, conceptual reasons why restricting to $\det=1$ is unlikely to be a major practical bottleneck for OFT.

Why that is so: the full orthogonal group $O(d)$ has two connected components, the rotations $SO(d)$ (determinant $+1$) and the “reflections” component (determinant $-1$). Both kinds of orthogonal maps preserve pairwise angles, and hence both preserve the hyperspherical energy that OFT is built to conserve. Cayley and the skew‑symmetric exponential ($R=\exp(S)$ with $S=-S^\top$) both produce elements of $SO(d)$ only, so they live in the identity component; starting from $R=I$ and using a continuous parametrization you stay in $SO(d)$. If the (global) optimum for a finetuning problem truly required an orthogonal map in the $\det=-1$ component, a pure $SO(d)$ parametrization could not reach that exact point without first leaving the identity component — but that situation would be unusual in practice because OFT is initialized at identity and we are seeking small, structured adjustments near the pretrained weights. In other words, rotations already give the continuous degrees of freedom that are most useful when you want to preserve pretrained structure and only change neuron directions modestly.

If you do want the ability to realize $\det=-1$ transforms there are straightforward, cheap ways to add that capacity without changing the rest of the OFT machinery. A single learned Householder reflector per block (a vector $u$ with $H=I-2\frac{uu^\top}{u^\top u}$) multiplied with the Cayley rotation (e.g. $R=H\cdot\text{Cayley}(Q)$) gives access to the other component of $O(d)$ while keeping everything orthogonal; a product of an odd number of Householders also has $\det=-1$. This costs only one extra vector per block and is numerically stable. By contrast, $R=\exp(A-A^\top)$ does not add reflections (it still yields $\det=1$ because $\det(\exp(S))=\exp(\mathrm{tr}(S))=1$ for skew‑symmetric $S$). Other orthogonal parametrizations (Givens, product of Householders, Lezcano et al.’s “cheap” orthogonal parametrizations, etc.) are valid alternatives with different computational / stability trade‑offs; the paper cites Lezcano and Liu for other differentiable orthogonal strategies but does not include a systematic comparison.

Practical recommendation: if you see any pathology that suggests a discrete orientation flip would help (unlikely in the OFT setting described), the simplest experiment is to add a learned Householder per block or to initialize one block with a reflection and run the same Cayley‑based optimization. Otherwise, the paper’s experiments indicate Cayley ($\mathrm{SO}(d)$) with block‑diagonal structure gives stable, fast convergence and good final metrics, so restricting to $\det(R)=1$ did not hurt in practice.

### Are you effectively performing Riemannian optimization on \(\mathrm{SO}(d)\), or is Cayley + SGD sufficient?
Question:
Since \(\mathrm{SO}(d)\) is a Lie group, one could use Riemannian gradients/retractions. With Cayley, you instead optimize over \(\mathbf{Q}\) in Euclidean space and map to \(\mathbf{R}\). Does this introduce gradient bias or conditioning issues compared to explicit Riemannian methods? Any observed differences in practice on SD v1.5 when using exponential-map-based parametrizations vs. Cayley?
Answer:
Short answer: you are not doing textbook Riemannian gradient descent on $\mathrm{SO}(d)$; OFT/COFT parameterize the manifold by a skew‑symmetric matrix $Q$ and optimize $Q$ in Euclidean parameter space (backprop through the Cayley map). In principle that is a different algorithm than projecting ambient gradients to the tangent and re‑tracting along geodesics, so there is a metric/conditioning mismatch — but in the regime of finetuning studied in the paper (identity initialization, small deviations, block‑diagonal $Q$ and the COFT small‑epsilon constraint) the mismatch is small and Cayley+SGD is sufficient and empirically effective on Stable Diffusion v1.5.

Why they are different (technical intuition). OFT builds orthogonals by
$$
R(Q)= (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1},\qquad Q^\top=-Q,
$$
and optimizes the entries of $Q$ with standard optimizers. The loss gradient flows via the chain rule, i.e. $\partial L/\partial Q=(D_Q R)^\top(\partial L/\partial R)$. That is an extrinsic parameterization: you are optimizing in $Q$-space, not directly on tangent vectors of $\mathrm{SO}(d)$.

By contrast a Riemannian approach would take the ambient Euclidean gradient $G=\partial L/\partial R$, project it to the tangent $T_R\mathrm{SO}(d)$ (for the canonical metric)
$$
\operatorname{Proj}_R(G)=G-R\operatorname{sym}(R^\top G)=R\operatorname{skew}(R^\top G),
$$
and then move along that tangent direction and re‑tract (for example by the exponential map $R\mapsto R\exp(-\eta\Omega)$ with $\Omega$ skew‑symmetric). That projected direction is the intrinsic steepest descent on the manifold; the $Q$-parameterization produces a different direction in $R$-space because of the Jacobian $D_Q R$. So in theory you can get bias/conditioning differences.

Why the practical difference is small here. Two facts collapse the gap in the OFT setting. First, the Cayley map is locally linear around $Q=0$: using the Neumann series
$$
(I-Q)^{-1}=\sum_{k\ge0}Q^k\quad\Rightarrow\quad R(Q)\approx I+2Q+\mathcal{O}(Q^2),
$$
so for small $Q$ the Jacobian $D_Q R$ is approximately a constant scaling and the $Q$-updates correspond closely to small tangent steps. Second, OFT initializes $R=I$ (i.e. $Q=0$) and COFT explicitly keeps $\|R-I\|$ (equivalently small $\|Q\|$ via the Neumann approximation) bounded. Those two choices make the Cayley parametrization behave like a retraction near the identity, so SGD on $Q$ is nearly equivalent to a first‑order Riemannian update. The paper exploits exactly this regime (small, structured orthogonal updates) and reports fast, stable finetuning on SD v1.5.

Practical/implementation consequences called out in the paper. Cayley is inexpensive relative to exact exponential/retraction in the sense that you avoid a matrix exponential; the cost is a linear solve / inverse $(I-Q)^{-1}$ which the authors make tractable by block‑diagonalizing $R$. They also note the Cayley map (like the exponential of a skew matrix) lands in $\mathrm{SO}(d)$ (determinant $=1$) and that this restriction did not hurt empirical results. The paper also warns about the cost of the inverse and uses projected gradient steps on $Q$ to enforce the COFT epsilon constraint for stability.

When a Riemannian method might matter. If you plan to learn very large rotations ($Q$ not small), or you deliberately leave the optimization far from identity, the local linear approximation breaks down and the metric mismatch can produce different trajectories / worse conditioning. In that regime a true Riemannian optimizer (project ambient gradient to tangent, use a geodesic or high‑quality retraction such as expm on block blocks) can give better behaved steps and theoretically clean gradients. But that comes at much higher computational cost (matrix exponentials or SVD/QR based retractions at full scale).

Empirical comparison in the paper. The authors used Cayley (with block structure), identity initialization and COFT’s projection on $Q$ and report strong empirical performance and stability on Stable Diffusion v1.5. They do not report an ablation that replaces Cayley by an exponential‑map parameterization or an explicit Riemannian optimizer, so there is no direct empirical comparison in the paper to quote. Their practical claim is that Cayley+SGD (with the small‑deviation tricks) is sufficient and efficient for finetuning.

Recommendations
- For the OFT use case in the paper (finetuning pretrained SD with small, structured orthogonal changes): Cayley + standard optimizers (and COFT’s small‑epsilon constraint) is the pragmatic choice — cheaper and empirically robust.  
- If you expect large rotations or want the exact manifold steepest‑descent behavior, try a Riemannian optimizer (projected gradient → retraction, or parametrize by $A$ and $R=\exp(A)$) on block‑diagonal blocks so the cost is manageable, and compare convergence/conditioning. Monitor $\|Q\|$ (or $\|A\|$) and the condition number of $I-Q$ because Cayley breaks down if $I-Q$ becomes ill‑conditioned.

If you want to test the difference experimentally on SD v1.5, a minimal ablation is: (1) implement the same block structure and initialization, (2) replace $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ by $R=\exp(A)$ with $A=-A^\top$ (or compute the Riemannian gradient and re‑tract via exp), (3) keep identical optimizers/hyperparameters and compare convergence, sample quality and stability. My expectation (and what the paper’s design choices imply) is that with small updates you’ll see only minor practical differences but at non‑small updates Riemannian updates may be more stable at higher compute cost.

### How does OFT affect text alignment and classifier-free guidance (CFG) behavior?
Question:
Because OFT rotates attention weights, could it systematically change alignment to text embeddings and alter the optimal CFG scale? Did you observe shifts in CLIP-T scores, required guidance scales, or prompt sensitivity compared to LoRA or DreamBooth? Any recommendations for CFG tuning when using OFT/COFT?
Answer:
Short answer: OFT’s rotations do not, in the paper’s experiments, produce a systematic breakdown of text–image alignment nor force a different classifier‑free guidance (CFG) scale. Theoretically this is because OFT enforces orthogonality and therefore preserves the angular geometry of the layer (the paper’s preserved quantity is hyperspherical energy), and empirically the authors report CLIP‑T (prompt fidelity) that is “slightly better or comparable” to DreamBooth / LoRA. The paper does not report an explicit sweep of CFG scales, and for fairness they used the same sampling hyperparameters across methods, so no systematic change of the optimal guidance scale was needed in their evaluations.

Why that makes sense technically: OFT replaces a weight matrix by a left‑multiplication with an orthogonal matrix, i.e. the finetuned weight is
$$W = R W^0,\quad R^\top R = I,$$
so a layer output $z = W^\top x$ can be written as
$$z = (W^0)^\top (R^\top x).$$
An orthogonal matrix preserves inner products and angles, since for any vectors u,v we have $(Ru)\cdot(Rv) = u\cdot v$. The key design of OFT is to preserve pairwise neuron angles (hyperspherical energy), so the internal geometry that encodes pretrained semantic alignments is kept stable. Concretely, if the same orthogonal transform is applied consistently across the projections that participate jointly in attention (so that both sides of a dot product are effectively rotated together), relative dot products that underlie cross‑attention logits remain stable.

A few caveats and practical details that matter for CFG behavior. First, if a rotation is applied asymmetrically (for example only to a key or value matrix but not to the corresponding query or to the frozen text encoder outputs), in principle attention logits can shift; OFT’s orthogonality constraint and the COFT radius constraint limit how big that shift can be. Second, the re‑scaled OFT variant (OFT + a learnable diagonal $D$) explicitly changes neuron magnitudes; since classifier‑free guidance multiplies the conditional–unconditional difference, i.e. a guided denoiser step is commonly implemented as
$$\hat\epsilon = \epsilon_{\text{uncond}} + s\bigl(\epsilon_{\text{cond}}-\epsilon_{\text{uncond}}\bigr),$$
changing the magnitude of $\epsilon_{\text{cond}}-\epsilon_{\text{uncond}}$ can make the effective strength of a given $s$ different. The paper’s main results use the plain OFT/COFT (no large magnitude re‑scaling), so they did not find the need to retune guidance.

What the paper observed: in subject‑driven and controllable generation the authors report improved or comparable prompt fidelity (CLIP‑T) and substantially better stability / convergence than DreamBooth and LoRA. They did not report any systematic change in the guidance scale used for sampling; sampling hyperparameters were held the same across methods for fair comparison. Prompt fidelity did not degrade, so there was no evidence in the paper that OFT forces a different CFG regime.

Practical recommendations when you finetune with OFT/COFT. Start from the same CFG scale you used for the pretrained model (the common practice with Stable Diffusion, e.g. around 7–8 for many workflows). Run a small validation grid (for example $s\in\{5,7.5,10\}$) and measure CLIP‑T (or visual inspection / task‑specific metrics) to confirm the best trade‑off for your task. If you use COFT with a small $\epsilon$ you are even less likely to need retuning because COFT explicitly keeps $R$ near $I$. If you use the re‑scaled OFT variant, expect that you may need to re‑calibrate $s$ since the learned diagonal $D$ can change magnitudes of conditional signals; a quick diagnostic is to compare the average norm of $\epsilon_{\text{cond}}-\epsilon_{\text{uncond}}$ before and after finetuning and scale $s$ to restore a similar effective magnitude if desired. Finally, because OFT/COFT gave more stable and less iteration‑sensitive finetuning in the paper, you can afford to be less conservative about finetuning length; this stability also reduces the danger of accidentally creating a model that needs a very different CFG setting.

Summary: both on theoretical grounds (orthogonality preserves geometry) and on the paper’s empirical results (CLIP‑T comparable or slightly better; same sampling hyperparameters used), OFT/COFT do not force a systematic change in CFG scale. Still, for any production use, run a small guidance‑scale check (especially if you use re‑scaled OFT or a large COFT $\epsilon$) and prefer COFT with a modest $\epsilon$ if you want extra insurance that the pretrained text alignment is preserved.

### Compositionality: when can multiple task-specific OFT matrices be multiplied without interference?
Question:
The paper suggests a compositional potential: if tasks yield \(\mathbf{R}_1,\mathbf{R}_2,\dots\), one can form \(\mathbf{R}_{\text{combo}} = \prod_i \mathbf{R}_i\), which is still orthogonal. Under what conditions does this preserve performance across tasks (e.g., matrices commuting approximately, acting on disjoint subspaces, or small \(\|\mathbf{R}_i-\mathbf{I}\|\))? Any empirical results on composing two downstream finetunes (e.g., subject-driven + controllable) and measuring interference?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — the product of OFT matrices is still orthogonal (so it still preserves hyperspherical energy), but that algebraic fact alone doesn’t guarantee that the composed model will perform every downstream task well. In practice composition will work with low interference when the task-specific orthogonal transforms either (a) commute (or nearly commute), (b) act on (nearly) disjoint/invariant subspaces of the neuron space, or (c) are small rotations (the COFT / small‑$\epsilon$ regime). These are the key, intuitively distinct ways to avoid one task’s rotation “breaking” another’s. The paper points out this compositional potential and lists it as an open problem but does not report empirical results for composing two downstream finetunes.

Why those three conditions matter (intuition + a few formulas)
1) Commutation. If two orthogonal transforms $\mathbf R_1,\mathbf R_2$ commute ($\mathbf R_1\mathbf R_2=\mathbf R_2\mathbf R_1$) they can be simultaneously block‑diagonalized by an orthogonal change of basis into independent $1\times1$ signs and $2\times2$ planar rotations. In that common basis each plane is just rotated by the sum of the two angles, so the order doesn’t matter and the two task effects do not “interfere.” More formally, when $\mathbf R_i=\exp(Q_i)$ with skew‑symmetric $Q_i$, if $[Q_1,Q_2]=0$ then $\exp(Q_2)\exp(Q_1)=\exp(Q_1+Q_2)$ (no higher‑order interaction). The Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff identity shows the source of interference: nonzero commutators produce extra terms ($\frac12[Q_1,Q_2]+\cdots$) that change the combined effect.

2) Disjoint / invariant subspaces. If the neuron space splits as an orthogonal sum $U\oplus V$ and $\mathbf R_1=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf R_{1,U},\mathbf I_V)$ while $\mathbf R_2=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf I_U,\mathbf R_{2,V})$, then $\mathbf R_1$ and $\mathbf R_2$ commute and composition simply applies each rotation on its own subspace. Practically this corresponds to the two tasks changing different channels/blocks or different layers so their finite rotations do not overlap.

3) Small rotations (COFT). The Cayley parameterization used in the paper writes $\mathbf R=(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}$ with $\mathbf Q^\top=-\mathbf Q$. For small $\mathbf Q$ one has $\mathbf R\approx \mathbf I+2\mathbf Q+\mathcal O(\mathbf Q^2)$. If each task’s generator $\mathbf Q_i$ is small and the commutators $[\mathbf Q_i,\mathbf Q_j]$ are small, then products $\prod_i\mathbf R_i$ behave approximately like $\exp(\sum_i Q_i)$ and interference (higher‑order noncommutative terms) is controlled by those commutators and quadratic terms. This is why COFT (the explicit $\|\mathbf R-\mathbf I\|\le\epsilon$ constraint) is plausibly helpful for composing tasks.

Why orthogonality (and HE preservation) is necessary but not sufficient
OFT guarantees that each per‑task transform preserves hyperspherical energy because orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise angles; and a product of orthogonals remains orthogonal. This means the composed model still respects the paper’s angular invariants. However task performance depends on how those rotated neuron directions propagate through the nonlinearity and decoder. Even if HE is unchanged, a later orthogonal rotation can map task‑relevant directions into directions that break another task’s specialized mapping. So algebraic orthogonality is a helpful structural prior but doesn’t by itself guarantee function‑level compositionality.

Practical diagnostics and simple experiments (what I would run)
Because the paper does not include composition experiments (it flags this as an open problem), an empirical protocol is the most direct way to test compositionality. Train two OFT finetunes independently on the same base model to get per‑layer orthogonals $\{\mathbf R^{(A)}_\ell\}$ and $\{\mathbf R^{(B)}_\ell\}$ (A=subject, B=control). Build the composed model by multiplying the per‑layer matrices, e.g. $\mathbf R^{(\text{combo})}_\ell=\mathbf R^{(B)}_\ell\mathbf R^{(A)}_\ell$ (and try the reverse order as well). Evaluate: subject fidelity metrics (DINO, CLIP‑I) and control consistency metrics from the paper. Also measure algebraic indicators: per‑layer commutator norms $\|\mathbf R^{(A)}_\ell\mathbf R^{(B)}_\ell-\mathbf R^{(B)}_\ell\mathbf R^{(A)}_\ell\|_F$, spectral norms $\|\mathbf R^{(i)}_\ell-\mathbf I\|$, and overlap of their invariant subspaces (principal angles). Use ablations: (i) COFT small $\epsilon$ versus larger $\epsilon$, (ii) shared versus different block partitions, (iii) enforcing a commutator penalty $\|[\mathbf R^{(A)}_\ell,\mathbf R^{(B)}_\ell]\|_F$ during joint finetuning. These diagnostics will tell you whether failures correlate with non‑commutation, block misalignment, or large rotations.

Practical recommendations to reduce interference
If you want composed behavior to work reliably, try to make transforms commute or be localized. Constrain each $\mathbf R$ to be close to identity (use COFT with small $\epsilon$), align block structure across tasks (use the same block partition so products remain block‑diagonal), or train tasks to use different layers/blocks. If joint composition is a core requirement, consider joint multitask OFT (optimize $\mathbf R$’s with both losses and an optional decomposition penalty that yields near‑commuting per‑task factors) or explicitly regularize commutators during finetuning.

Bottom line
Mathematically composition is possible and attractive (products of orthogonals remain orthogonal and preserve hyperspherical energy), but successful, low‑interference task composition requires additional structure: exact/approximate commutation, support on disjoint invariant subspaces, or small generators (COFT regime). The paper raises this compositional potential as an open problem but does not report experiments; the simplest follow‑ups are the diagnostic experiments above (compose per‑layer $\mathbf R$’s, measure commutators and task metrics, and try COFT / block alignment to reduce interference).
