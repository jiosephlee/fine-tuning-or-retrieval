\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 6: Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)

# Adding a constraint on deviation from identity: $\|R - I\| \le \varepsilon$

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) augments Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) by explicitly limiting how far the learned orthogonal transform $R$ may move away from the identity matrix. The COFT forward pass for a pretrained layer with weights $W^0$ is written as
\[
\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top \bm{x} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x},
\qquad\text{subject to}\qquad
\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}, \quad \|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \le \varepsilon.
\]
The orthogonality constraint $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$ preserves pairwise angles among neurons and therefore preserves hyperspherical energy; the new deviation constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\varepsilon$ explicitly bounds how much the finetuned model can drift from the pretrained weights in an angular sense. Intuitively, this radius constraint makes the finetuning problem less prone to catastrophic drift: it restricts the feasible orthogonal transforms to a controlled neighborhood around the identity so the model cannot arbitrarily rotate neuron directions away from the pretrained configuration.

Putting the radius constraint directly into the model makes an otherwise implicit regularization (small learning rates or tiny additive updates) explicit. Practically, COFT therefore gives a clear knob, $\varepsilon$, to trade off preservation of pretrained generative behavior against the ability to adapt to a downstream task. When $\varepsilon$ is very small, COFT enforces only tiny orthogonal deviations and the finetuned model remains close to the pretrained generator; when $\varepsilon$ is large, the method relaxes to OFT and gains more flexibility.

# Relating the constraint to Cayley parameters via Neumann series

COFT uses a computationally convenient parameterization of orthogonal matrices via the Cayley transform. Let $\bm{Q}$ be a skew-symmetric matrix ($\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$). The Cayley parameterization builds an orthogonal matrix as
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q}) (\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]
This construction guarantees orthogonality (and in fact produces matrices in the special orthogonal group with determinant $+1$). Because $\bm{R}$ is expressed through $\bm{Q}$, the radius constraint on $\bm{R}$ can be converted to a constraint on $\bm{Q}$, which is easier to enforce during optimization.

To make that conversion explicit we use the Neumann series expansion of $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$. Under the standard convergence assumption (convergence in operator norm), if $\|\bm{Q}\| < 1$ then
\[
(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \bm{Q}^k.
\]
Plugging this into the Cayley form gives
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})\sum_{k=0}^\infty \bm{Q}^k
= \bm{I} + 2\sum_{k=1}^\infty \bm{Q}^k
= \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2).
\]
Hence for sufficiently small $\bm{Q}$ (so higher-order terms are negligible) we have the first-order approximation
\[
\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q},
\qquad\text{and therefore}\qquad
\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \approx 2\|\bm{Q}\|.
\]
This shows that bounding $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\varepsilon$ is approximately equivalent to bounding $\|\bm{Q}\|\le\varepsilon'$, where $\varepsilon'$ is on the order of $\varepsilon/2$ up to higher-order corrections. The paper therefore treats the radius constraint as an equivalent constraint on the Cayley parameter $\bm{Q}$:
\[
\|\bm{Q}\| \le \varepsilon',
\]
and uses $\varepsilon'$ as the practical hyperparameter during optimization. Because the Neumann-series argument depends on small $\bm{Q}$, this mapping is an approximation; in practice $\varepsilon'$ is treated as an independent tuning parameter that controls the allowed deviation in $\bm{Q}$ space.

# Projected gradient descent on $\bm{Q}$ to enforce $\|\bm{Q}\| \le \varepsilon'$

Once the orthogonal matrix is parameterized by a skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$, the radius constraint can be enforced by simple constrained optimization on $\bm{Q}$. The paper proposes projected gradient descent as an effective way to maintain $\|\bm{Q}\|\le\varepsilon'$ throughout learning. The high-level training loop for COFT is:

1. Parameterize $\bm{R}$ via Cayley: $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{Q}^\top=-\bm{Q}$.
2. Initialize $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ so that $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ and the finetuned model starts exactly at the pretrained weights.
3. On each optimization step compute gradients of the finetuning loss with respect to $\bm{Q}$, take a gradient-descent update, and then project the updated $\bm{Q}$ back into the feasible set $\{\bm{Q}:\|\bm{Q}\|\le\varepsilon'\}$.
4. Recompute $\bm{R}$ from the projected $\bm{Q}$ for the next forward/backward pass.

Projected gradient descent enforces the deviation constraint by clipping the parameter back into the allowed ball after each unconstrained gradient step. The projection operation is conceptually simple: after an update step you replace $\bm{Q}$ by the closest matrix (under the chosen matrix norm) that satisfies $\|\bm{Q}\|\le\varepsilon'$. Because $\bm{Q}$ is constrained to be skew-symmetric by construction, the projected updates are typically implemented so as to maintain that skew-symmetry (for example by parameterizing only the independent skew-symmetric degrees of freedom or re-symmetrizing as part of the projection).

The paper treats the projection step as straightforward to implement and computationally light compared to the overall finetuning. Enforcing the constraint directly on $\bm{Q}$ (rather than trying to project $\bm{R}$ itself) is convenient because $\bm{Q}$ lives in an unconstrained linear space (skew-symmetric matrices) and the Cayley transform maps it to the nonlinear orthogonal manifold.

# Effect of $\varepsilon$: flexibility–stability trade-off and practical tuning

The radius hyperparameter $\varepsilon$ (and its Cayley-space counterpart $\varepsilon'$) governs a clear flexibility–stability trade-off:

- When $\varepsilon$ is very small, COFT tightly restricts $\bm{R}$ to remain near the identity. In this regime the finetuned model changes little from the pretrained generator: hyperspherical structure is preserved and the pretrained generalization behavior is largely maintained. This setting is useful when the downstream task requires only modest, conservative adaptations and when one wants to avoid risking collapse or loss of pretrained capabilities.

- When $\varepsilon$ is large, the radius constraint becomes loose and COFT approaches OFT. This gives more representational freedom and can improve task adaptation but reduces the explicit protection against drifting too far from the pretrained solution.

The paper reports empirical behavior consistent with this intuition: "With larger $\varepsilon$, the COFT-finetuned model resembles the OFT-finetuned model. With smaller $\varepsilon$, the COFT-finetuned model behaves increasingly similar to the pretrained text-to-image diffusion model." In practice, COFT makes hyperparameter choices easier because an appropriate $\varepsilon$ reduces sensitivity to learning rate and to the number of finetuning iterations. The authors note that with a proper $\varepsilon$, both the learning rate and the iteration number become "effortless to set"—COFT allows one to pick a reasonably large iteration budget without risk of catastrophic model collapse that can afflict unconstrained finetuning.

As a practical tuning guideline (consistent with the paper's findings): treat $\varepsilon$ as the primary control for the allowable degree of model adaptation. Start with a conservative (small) value to ensure preservation of pretrained behavior; increase $\varepsilon$ only if the model is failing to adapt adequately to the downstream control signal. Because the Cayley-to-$R$ mapping is nonlinear, the mapping between $\varepsilon$ and task performance is not strictly linear; the paper therefore treats $\varepsilon'$ as an independent hyperparameter to be validated empirically.

# Initialization and stability benefits observed empirically

A key implementation detail and stability advantage of COFT is its identity initialization. Setting $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ at the start yields $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ and therefore recovers exactly the pretrained weights at initialization. This zero-start property gives two practical benefits. First, it guarantees that the finetuning trajectory begins from a known good generator; second, because the radius constraint restricts how far $\bm{R}$ can move, the training dynamics remain stable and predictable as the model adapts.

The empirical results in the paper demonstrate these stability benefits. In subject-driven generation experiments, COFT and OFT converge stably and preserve subject identity while baselines such as LoRA and standard finetuning can either fail to maintain identity or collapse after many iterations. The authors report that after a moderate number of iterations (e.g., a few hundred) OFT and COFT already achieve good control, while standard methods sometimes collapse if training is continued. In controllable generation tasks, COFT also shows faster and more stable convergence than adapter-based baselines: for example, OFT/COFT reach strong control at far fewer epochs than LoRA, and COFT's explicit radius constraint further reduces sensitivity to training schedule and hyperparameter settings.

Another practical advantage is that COFT introduces no additional inference overhead. Once training is finished, the learned orthogonal transform can be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix to produce an equivalent finetuned weight matrix $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$; the resulting model is identical in runtime cost to the original pretrained model. Finally, all parameter-efficiency strategies used for OFT (Cayley parameterization, block-diagonal orthogonal matrices, optional block sharing) are compatible with COFT, so the stability benefits of the radius constraint come at little or no extra parameter or inference cost.

Summary

COFT makes the OFT idea more robust by combining the angle-preserving power of orthogonal transforms with an explicit radius constraint around the identity. The Cayley parameterization gives a practical way to enforce orthogonality, the Neumann-series expansion links the radius on $\bm{R}$ to an approximate ball in Cayley-space for $\bm{Q}$, and projected gradient descent on $\bm{Q}$ enforces the constraint during optimization. Empirically, COFT stabilizes finetuning, reduces sensitivity to iteration count and learning rate, and preserves pretrained generative ability while still enabling effective adaptation for subject-driven and controllable image generation tasks.
