\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 2: Background: Diffusion Models, Conditioning, and Finetuning Strategies

# Denoising diffusion and latent diffusion basics

Diffusion-based generative models have become a dominant paradigm for high-quality image synthesis. At a high level, these models are trained to reverse a gradual corruption process: a clean image is progressively noised and the model learns to map noisy inputs back toward the data distribution. This denoising perspective is the conceptual backbone behind many recent text-to-image systems; the literature cited in the paper places diffusion approaches at the center of recent advances in photorealistic text-guided generation.

Two practical flavors of diffusion model training appear repeatedly in the literature and in the paper's comparisons: pixel-space diffusion and latent-space diffusion. Pixel-space models operate directly on image pixels; prominent examples mentioned in the paper include GLIDE and Imagen, which train the diffusion process in the pixel domain. Latent-space diffusion moves the denoising process into a learned, lower-dimensional representation of images. The advantage of the latent route is computational efficiency: an encoder (or codebook) compresses images to a latent code, the diffusion model operates in that compact latent space, and a decoder reconstructs pixels from the latent. Stable Diffusion and DALL·E 2 are examples of systems that train in such a latent space. Concretely, the paper notes that Stable Diffusion uses VQ-GAN to learn a visual codebook as its latent representation, while DALL·E 2 leverages CLIP to construct a joint embedding for images and text.

Conditioning is central to text-to-image applications: the denoising model must be guided by a textual instruction (and sometimes by extra signals such as edges or segmentation maps). The engineering choices for how to feed conditioning information—whether to jointly train a text encoder or to use a frozen pretrained encoder—vary across systems. The paper observes that GLIDE trains its text encoder jointly with a diffusion prior, whereas Imagen uses a frozen pretrained text encoder. These choices affect both training complexity and how tightly the text representations are aligned with the image generative path.

# Stable Diffusion architecture: UNet and cross-attention conditioning

State-of-the-art text-to-image diffusion systems typically organize the denoising computation in a modular backbone and attach conditioning channels that inject information from language or auxiliary signals into that backbone. In practice, many implementations use a denoising network with an image-processing backbone (frequently a U‑Net style denoiser in the literature) and conditioning mechanisms that let text and other inputs steer the denoising dynamics.

One widely used conditioning mechanism in modern text-to-image models is attention-based conditioning: a text encoder produces token-level representations that are used to modulate image features via attention operations inside the denoiser. From an engineering point of view this means that the denoiser contains components (often implemented as Transformer-style attention blocks or cross-attention layers) whose parameters connect textual representations to image features. Because these attention components are explicit places where text interacts with image generation, they are frequent targets for adaptation when people personalize or control pretrained diffusion models. The paper highlights this operational fact when discussing finetuning comparisons: for fair comparisons with adapter methods like LoRA, the experiments apply OFT to the same attention-related layers where LoRA would normally be attached.

Stable Diffusion, as a representative latent diffusion implementation, therefore combines (1) a learned latent image space (the VQ-GAN codebook referenced above), (2) a denoising backbone operating in that latent space, and (3) conditioning inputs (text encodings and, when used, extra control signals) that modulate the denoiser’s computations. Implementation choices—whether to freeze the text encoder, how to incorporate control signals, and which layers to adapt during finetuning—affect both effectiveness and practical costs such as parameter counts and inference speed.

# Subject-driven generation (e.g., DreamBooth) and its challenges

Subject-driven generation is the personalization task where the model is given a small set of images of a particular subject and the goal is to synthesize new images that faithfully depict that subject in new contexts specified by text prompts. DreamBooth is the canonical framework the paper uses as a baseline for this task. DreamBooth’s practical recipe is to introduce a custom token representing the subject, and then finetune the pretrained text-to-image diffusion model on the small subject-specific dataset using a combination of a reconstruction objective and a class-specific prior preservation loss. The latter is intended to prevent catastrophic drift: while the model should learn the new subject, it should not forget the prior that makes the model generally capable of producing diverse images.

Despite these ingredients, subject-driven finetuning poses several concrete challenges. First, subject fidelity is hard to preserve: finetuning on very few images risks overfitting or producing identity drift, where the synthesized images no longer look like the original subject. Second, finetuning must balance adaptation and preservation: we want the model to acquire the new subject-specific capability while maintaining its pretrained generalization and fidelity to text prompts. The paper abstracts these needs into two desiderata for a finetuning method: (1) training efficiency (few trainable parameters and few epochs) and (2) generalizability preservation (retaining high-fidelity, diverse, pretrained generative behavior).

Existing methods commonly take two approaches: small-weight updates across some parameters (e.g., updating the entire model or a subset with a tiny learning rate) or adding a small reparameterized component (adapter-style modules). DreamBooth itself finetunes the model directly, typically with conservative hyperparameters. However, the paper points out that simply minimizing Euclidean distance of weights to the pretrained checkpoint is an implicit regularization used by many methods, and that Euclidean closeness alone is not a structural measure for whether the model’s semantic generation ability has been preserved.

This motivates looking for measures that better capture the internal relational structure of neurons during finetuning. The paper introduces hyperspherical energy as such a structural measure: for a layer with neurons $ \{\mathbf{w}_i\} $, normalize each neuron to the unit sphere by defining $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i := \mathbf{w}_i / \|\mathbf{w}_i\|$, and then compute
\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
Hyperspherical energy captures pairwise relationships (the angles) among neurons on the unit hypersphere. The paper hypothesizes that preserving this pairwise angular structure is important for maintaining the pretrained model’s semantic capabilities when adapting to a new subject; this insight underpins the orthogonal finetuning approach introduced later.

Evaluating subject-driven generation requires measuring both subject fidelity and prompt fidelity. In the experiments reported in the paper, metrics such as DINO embeddings and CLIP image embeddings (for subject fidelity) and CLIP text-image similarity (for prompt fidelity), together with perceptual diversity metrics like LPIPS, are used to quantify different aspects of the finetuned models’ behavior.

# Controllable generation (e.g., ControlNet, T2I-Adapter) and overhead

Controllable generation refers to the family of tasks where the diffusion model is conditioned not only by text but also by an auxiliary control signal that prescribes structure or attributes the generated image should follow. Typical control signals considered in the paper include Canny edges, segmentation maps, and facial landmarks. The goal is for the model to respect both the text prompt and the image-structure constraint represented by the control input.

Recent works like ControlNet and T2I-Adapter take the pretrained diffusion model and augment or adapt it so that it accepts and respects such control signals. ControlNet, for example, finetunes the diffusion model to take additional control inputs and achieves impressive controllability in practice. Adapter-style approaches like T2I-Adapter insert small modules to inject control information. These methods successfully add controllability but often introduce additional parameters and architectural components. That extra capacity typically translates into added computation or memory during inference: the finetuned model now carries adapter modules or auxiliary networks that must be evaluated when generating images.

A notable practical distinction the paper emphasizes is that orthogonal finetuning (OFT) does not introduce inference-time overhead. OFT learns layer-shared orthogonal transformations that multiply into the pretrained weight matrices; at inference the finite set of learned orthogonal matrices can be multiplied into the original weights to produce an equivalent dense model. Thus, unlike some adapter-based solutions, OFT yields no extra modules to run at inference time.

Measuring the effectiveness of controllable generation requires task-appropriate metrics. The paper introduces a control consistency metric that estimates the control signal from the generated image and compares it to the original control input. Concretely, for Canny-edge-to-image (C2I) tasks the evaluation uses IoU and F1 between detected edges and the original; for segmentation-to-image (S2I) tasks the evaluation uses mean IoU and segmentation accuracy; for landmark-to-face (L2F) tasks the evaluation uses mean $\ell_2$ distance between target landmarks and landmarks predicted from generated images. These metrics quantify how well the generated image adheres to the control signal, complementing perceptual or semantic metrics used elsewhere.

Empirically, the paper reports that adapter-based approaches and ControlNet can be slower to converge and sometimes exhibit sudden or unstable training dynamics, whereas OFT and its constrained variant (COFT) tend to converge faster and more stably while achieving strong control adherence with fewer tunable parameters.

# Standard finetuning, adapter modules, and LoRA: strengths and limits

The landscape of finetuning large pretrained models includes several recurring strategies. The simplest approach is standard finetuning: adjust a subset (or all) of the pretrained weights using gradient descent with a small learning rate. The small learning rate is an implicit form of regularization that keeps the finetuned model near the pretrained checkpoint in Euclidean parameter space; in effect, standard finetuning often aims to keep $\|\mathbf{M} - \mathbf{M}^0\|$ small, where $\mathbf{M}^0$ denotes the pretrained weights. While effective in many applications, standard finetuning can be brittle in large generative models: small changes in parameter space do not always map to semantically small changes in the model’s generative behavior, and tuning hyperparameters such as number of iterations and learning rate becomes delicate.

Adapter-style methods were introduced to reduce the number of trainable parameters and to isolate task-specific changes. One influential adapter approach for transformer-style architectures is LoRA, which parameterizes the weight update as a low-rank additive term. Concretely, LoRA assumes the finetuning update has low rank and learns low-dimensional factors rather than updating the full weight matrix. The paper notes a simple parameter count formula for LoRA: with a low-rank parameter $r'$, the number of trainable parameters scales like $r'(d+n)$ for a weight matrix of size $d\times n$. The appeal of LoRA is parameter efficiency: by restricting updates to a low-rank subspace, it can adapt models with far fewer parameters than full finetuning, and it has been applied successfully to attention weights in Transformer-style modules.

Despite these strengths, adapter methods and low-rate standard finetuning do not offer structural guarantees about what internal geometric properties of the pretrained model they preserve. The paper argues that Euclidean proximity of weights to the pretrained checkpoint is an incomplete proxy for preserving the model’s semantic generation ability. To address this gap the authors introduce the idea of preserving hyperspherical energy, a measure of pairwise angular relations among neurons on the unit hypersphere. As formulated in the paper, the objective of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) can be interpreted as minimizing the difference in hyperspherical energy between the finetuned weights and the pretrained weights:
\[
\min_{\mathbf{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\mathbf{W})-\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)\right\|
\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad
\min_{\mathbf{W}} \bigg\| \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}
- \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_j\|^{-1}\bigg\|,
\]
where $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i := \mathbf{w}_i/\|\mathbf{w}_i\|$.

A mathematical observation underpins OFT: the pairwise angular relationships among vectors (neurons) are preserved if the whole set is transformed by a common orthogonal matrix. Therefore, OFT parametrizes the finetuned layer weights as a shared orthogonal transform applied to the pretrained weights. For a fully connected layer with pretrained weights $\mathbf{W}^0$, OFT replaces the usual forward computation
\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}
\]
with
\[
\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x},
\qquad\text{subject to}\quad \mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^\top=\mathbf{I},
\]
where $\mathbf{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix shared across the neurons in that layer. Initialized at the identity, learning $\mathbf{R}$ deforms the coordinate system of neurons while keeping pairwise angles invariant and thus preserves hyperspherical energy exactly.

Implementationally, maintaining orthogonality can be expensive if done naively. The paper adopts a Cayley parameterization to generate orthogonal matrices:
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\]
where $\mathbf{Q}$ is skew-symmetric ($\mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^\top$). Cayley parameterization produces orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$ (the special orthogonal group) and, in conjunction with a block-diagonal structure for $\mathbf{R}$, yields a practical balance between expressivity and parameter efficiency. Specifically, writing $\mathbf{R} = \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{R}_r)$ with each $\mathbf{R}_i\in \mathrm{O}\!\left(\tfrac{d}{r}\right)$ reduces the number of free parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ down to roughly $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$; sharing blocks further reduces cost to about $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$.

The paper compares parameter complexities qualitatively against LoRA. For a LoRA low-rank $r'$, the trainable parameter count is $r'(d+n)$. When both $r$ and $r'$ scale with $d$ (for example $r=r'=\alpha d$), the paper reports that LoRA’s complexity becomes $\mathcal{O}(d^2 + dn)$ while OFT’s becomes $\mathcal{O}(d)$ under block-sharing assumptions. As a concrete numerical illustration quoted in the paper, for a $128\times 128$ weight matrix with LoRA rank $r'=8$, LoRA has $2{,}048$ trainable parameters, whereas OFT with $r=8$ has $960$ trainable parameters.

Two practical extensions and variants are discussed in the paper. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) adds an explicit small-deviation constraint to keep the learned orthogonal transform close to the identity:
\[
\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\| \le \epsilon,
\]
which, via a first-order expansion of the Cayley transform ($\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\mathbf{Q}^2)$), can be enforced approximately by constraining $\|\mathbf{Q}\| \le \epsilon'$. COFT is intended to improve finetuning stability by making the trade-off between flexibility and preservation explicit. A second extension, re-scaled OFT, allows per-neuron magnitude scaling by including a learnable diagonal matrix $\mathbf{D} = \mathrm{diag}(s_1,\ldots,s_n)$ with positive entries, yielding the forward pass
\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0 \mathbf{D})^\top \mathbf{x}.
\]
Because hyperspherical energy depends only on normalized directions, multiplying by a diagonal magnitude matrix does not change the hyperspherical energy; the scaling increases expressive flexibility with little parameter overhead.

Strengths and limits. The OFT family combines two desirable properties: (1) it enforces a structural invariance (pairwise angles among neurons) that the authors argue is important for preserving pretrained semantic capabilities, and (2) it is parameter-efficient relative to many full-finetuning alternatives. Empirically in the paper, OFT and COFT converge faster and more stably than comparable adapter or fine-update methods and often achieve superior task metrics (both in subject-driven and controllable generation). Limitations noted by the authors include the computational cost of Cayley parameterization (it requires a matrix inverse; block-diagonalization mitigates but does not remove this cost), and the inductive bias introduced by block-diagonal structure (the parameter-efficiency gains come with modeling constraints that may limit flexibility in some regimes). The paper also points to open directions such as efficient scalable orthogonal parametrizations and investigating compositionality of orthogonal transforms across multiple downstream tasks.

# Concluding connections and practical takeaways

Putting the pieces together, a reader should take away a few practical lessons that the paper emphasizes. First, diffusion models provide a powerful generative backbone for text-to-image tasks, and engineering choices such as operating in latent space and how to inject conditioning information materially affect performance and cost. Second, personalization (subject-driven generation) and controllable generation are naturally framed as finetuning problems on top of a strong pretrained diffusion model; however, naive finetuning risks losing pretrained capabilities or being unstable. Third, adapter-style and low-rank update strategies like LoRA are popular because they reduce the number of trainable parameters, but they do not by themselves guarantee preservation of the internal geometric structure the pretrained model uses for semantic generation. Finally, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), together with COFT and re-scaled variants, offers a principled alternative that preserves pairwise neuronal angular relationships (hyperspherical energy) by construction, yields stable and often faster convergence in experiments reported in the paper, and—importantly—introduces no extra inference-time modules because the learned orthogonal transforms can be multiplied into the original weights.

For a student preparing to read the research literature in this area, it is useful to (1) recognize whether a paper trains diffusion in pixel or latent space, (2) note how conditioning is implemented and where adaptations are inserted (attention blocks are a common target), and (3) pay attention to both optimization/regularization choices and structural invariants (like hyperspherical energy) that methods claim to preserve. These perspectives will make it easier to compare methods such as DreamBooth, ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, LoRA, and the OFT family on a principled basis.
