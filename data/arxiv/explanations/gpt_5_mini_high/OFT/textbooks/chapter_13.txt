\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 13: Limitations, Ethics, and Future Directions

# Scalability of Cayley inverse and alternatives (matrix exponential, Householder, Givens)

A core implementation choice in the paper is the Cayley parameterization to guarantee orthogonality of the learned transform. The Cayley map is written in the paper as
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
\qquad \text{with}\quad
\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top,
\]
so that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal and $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$. The authors point out two useful properties of this choice: it is differentiable and it guarantees the orthogonality constraint, and initializing $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ yields $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, so the finetuning starts exactly at the pretrained weights. The Cayley parameterization, however, produces orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$, i.e. elements of the special orthogonal group; the paper notes that in practice this restriction did not harm performance.

The practical bottleneck highlighted in the paper is the matrix inverse $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ that is required by the Cayley transform. The inverse appears inside the forward/backward pass and therefore affects both memory and compute at finetuning time. To reduce this cost the paper adopts a block-diagonal parameterization of $\bm{R}$: rather than a single $d\times d$ orthogonal, $\bm{R}$ is formed as a diagonal of $r$ smaller orthogonal blocks. This reduces the number of free parameters and makes the matrix inverse operate on smaller blocks, which the paper empirically found to be an effective practical trade-off.

The paper quantifies parameter counts for these choices: a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix has $d(d-1)/2$ degrees of freedom; an $r$-block diagonal orthogonal matrix has approximately $d(d/r - 1)/2$ parameters and complexity on the order of $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. If block matrices are shared across blocks, parameter complexity can further drop toward $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. These formulae are used in the paper to argue that the block-diagonal Cayley construction can be made parameter-efficient while preserving the key invariance of hyperspherical energy.

Crucially, the paper frames the matrix-inverse cost as an explicit open problem: "Cayley parametrization which involves a matrix inverse slightly limits the scalability of OFT" and "how to speed up this matrix inverse in a differentiable way remains a challenge." The authors also point readers to other differentiable orthogonalization strategies cited in the paper. In short, the Cayley parameterization is practical and provably orthogonality-preserving, but its matrix inverse motivates further engineering research to make OFT scale to very large layer dimensions without relying heavily on block structure.

# Improving parameter efficiency without strong structural bias

One of the recurring themes in the paper is the trade-off between expressivity and regularization when choosing how to parameterize the orthogonal transform. Block-diagonalization is an attractive lever: it reduces the number of parameters and the size of the Cayley inverses, while still guaranteeing that hyperspherical energy is preserved. But the authors explicitly recognize that the block-diagonal structure introduces a structural bias: when blocks are small or when blocks are shared, the family of allowable rotations is restricted, and this restriction can both help optimization (by regularizing the finetuning) and hamper flexibility (by preventing some transforms that might better suit a downstream task).

The paper contrasts OFT with LoRA to make the point concrete. LoRA parameterizes additive low-rank updates with parameter count $r'(d+n)$ for a weight matrix of size $d\times n$, while OFT’s block-diagonal Cayley parameterization incurs asymptotic parameter complexity on the order of $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ (or $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ under certain sharing choices). The authors give a concrete example: for a $128\times 128$ weight matrix with LoRA rank $r'=8$, LoRA has about $2{,}048$ trainable parameters, whereas OFT with $r=8$ blocks has about $960$ trainable parameters. These comparisons show that OFT can be parameter-competitive, but the savings and their implications depend on the chosen structural choices.

Because parameter efficiency in OFT relies on structure (block sizes and sharing), the paper identifies the need for alternatives that reduce parameter count without imposing strong, potentially harmful biases on the transform family. This is left as an open direction: finding parameterizations or compression strategies that retain OFT’s hyperspherical-energy invariance while being less prescriptive than simple block-diagonalization. The paper also points out application-specific interpretations (for example, in convolutional layers, blocks can line up with channels and resemble depthwise transforms), which suggests that architecture-aware parameterization choices are promising but must be examined for induced biases.

# Compositional finetuning and interference across tasks

One of the most interesting observations in the paper is that the OFT parameterization naturally supports composition: orthogonal matrices are closed under multiplication, so orthogonal transforms learned for separate tasks can in principle be multiplied together to yield another orthogonal transform. Concretely, if task A learns $\bm{R}_A$ and task B learns $\bm{R}_B$, then applying $\bm{R}_B\bm{R}_A$ to the pretrained weights yields another orthogonal transform and thus preserves hyperspherical energy by construction.

The authors raise this compositionality as an open research question: does composing orthogonal transforms preserve the capabilities of all constituent finetuned tasks, or does interference accumulate? The paper does not resolve this question; rather, it points out the algebraic convenience of composition and leaves the empirical and theoretical study of multi-task composition to future work. Several important subtleties are implicit in the paper’s phrasing. For example, orthogonal matrices do not generally commute: $\bm{R}_A\bm{R}_B \neq \bm{R}_B\bm{R}_A$, so the order of composition matters and could affect the resulting behavior. Moreover, composing many task-specific rotations multiplies degrees of freedom into the final transform but does not increase the underlying capacity of the pretrained base model; as more tasks are composed, destructive interference is a plausible failure mode.

The paper frames the broad research questions to explore next: can multiple task-specific orthogonal transforms be combined so that the composite model retains the strengths of each finetuned instance? If not, what mechanisms (for example, structured orthogonal subspaces per task, gating between transforms, or meta-learning to merge transforms) can avoid interference? These are precisely the compositionality and interference questions the authors highlight as important open problems for OFT-style methods.

# Beyond hyperspherical energy: richer measures of semantic preservation

Hyperspherical energy (HE) is central to the paper’s conceptual argument: HE measures an aggregate of pairwise angular relationships among normalized neurons and is defined in the paper as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \big\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\big\|^{-1},
\qquad\text{with}\quad \hat{\bm{w}}_i := \frac{\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{w}_i\|}.
\]
The authors motivate HE as a structural statistic that captures neuron uniformity on the hypersphere and argue (and empirically observe) that preserving HE is important to preserve the pretrained semantic generation ability. Orthogonal transforms provably preserve HE, which is the mathematical justification for OFT’s choice of update family.

At the same time the paper acknowledges that designing a good finetuning strategy also requires practical measures of semantic preservation beyond a single aggregated statistic. The HE captures only pairwise angular relationships within a layer and does not, by itself, guarantee preservation of layer-to-layer interactions, activations produced on natural inputs, or task-level behavior. The authors supplement HE-based reasoning with empirical downstream metrics: for subject-driven generation they report DINO, CLIP-I, CLIP-T and LPIPS; for controllable generation they introduce a control-consistency metric that computes the control signal back from the generated image and compares it to the input control. These practical downstream measures are used in the paper to validate that HE-preserving OFT tends to keep the pretraining semantics while enabling task-specific control.

The paper therefore frames a research agenda: combine structural, parameter-level metrics such as HE with functional, task-level metrics (CLIP-based alignment, segmentation IoU, landmark errors, perceptual diversity) to build a richer and more reliable picture of how much of the pretrained generative ability is retained after finetuning. The authors leave open the problem of designing or identifying better intrinsic measures (possibly layer-wise, activation-distribution based, or function-oriented) that correlate more tightly with generation quality and controllability than HE alone.

# Ethical considerations in personalization and controllable synthesis

The paper focuses on technical advances (OFT and COFT) for two practical finetuning scenarios: subject-driven personalization given a few images, and controllable generation using additional conditional signals. Although the paper itself does not include a dedicated ethics section, the capabilities it develops naturally bring ethical considerations that practitioners and researchers should take seriously when deploying or extending this work.

Personalization via subject-driven finetuning involves training on images of a real individual to produce new images in different contexts. This raises questions of privacy, consent, and potential misuse. Systems that can reliably generate realistic images of a person from a few photos can be used for creative personalization but can also be used to impersonate or produce non-consensual content. Responsible deployment therefore requires attention to data provenance (who provided the images and under what consent), to retention policies (data minimization), and to access controls (who may run finetuning and generate content). The paper demonstrates that OFT can be sample-efficient and stable for few-shot personalization, which increases the need to think carefully about policies around collection and use of personal images.

Controllable synthesis—where a model is guided by edges, segmentation maps, or landmarks—amplifies the ability to create realistic or targeted imagery. This increases the risk that the system could be used to fabricate deceptive visual content or to violate privacy and safety norms. It also raises concerns about bias: pretrained generative models reflect the distributions they were trained on, and finetuning may further amplify or shift biases, potentially producing outputs that are stereotyped or unfair across demographic groups. Practitioners should therefore assess dataset representativeness and evaluate generated outputs across demographic axes where appropriate.

Because OFT improves finetuning stability and enables efficient personalization and control, it is particularly important to think about mitigation strategies. While the paper does not prescribe specific governance measures, possible mitigations consistent with responsible practice include explicit consent mechanisms for personalization data, watermarking or provenance markers for generated content, controlled release of models and checkpoints, and broad evaluation of failure modes and bias. The computational costs and relative ease of finetuning (an advantage of OFT) also compels attention to governance: easier finetuning lowers the barrier to misuse, so procedural and technical safeguards matter. The paper motivates these as important practical concerns for future work and deployment.

# Reproducibility and benchmarking recommendations

The paper contributes a number of experimental choices and metrics that can serve as reproducibility and benchmarking exemplars for future work on finetuning diffusion models. In terms of datasets and tasks, the authors evaluate OFT on standard subject-driven and controllable generation benchmarks: DreamBooth-style few-shot personalization, and control tasks on COCO (Canny edge \(\to\) image), ADE20K (segmentation-map \(\to\) image) and CelebA-HQ (landmark \(\to\) face). They compare against reasonable baselines (DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet, T2I-Adapter) and carefully report downstream metrics.

Concretely, the paper uses a mix of intrinsic and downstream evaluation metrics. For subject fidelity and semantic alignment it reports DINO and CLIP-based measures (CLIP-I and CLIP-T), and for diversity LPIPS. For controllable generation the paper introduces a control-consistency metric that reconstructs the control signal from generated images and computes task-specific scores: IoU and F1 for edge-to-image, mean IoU and accuracy for segmentation-to-image, and mean \(\ell_2\) landmark distance for landmark-to-face. These metric choices illustrate how to measure both fidelity to input controls and semantic quality of generations; the control-consistency idea is a concrete novel contribution of the paper that future benchmarks can adopt.

From the paper’s experimental design we can extract reproducibility recommendations that the authors themselves follow and that are sensible to adopt generally: release exact training recipes (datasets, data splits, learning rates, number of iterations/epochs), report the number of trainable parameters per method and the model initialization strategy, provide random-seed averages (the paper repeats DreamBooth experiments over 30 seeds), and measure both convergence speed and final quality. The authors also emphasize the importance of matching where finetuning is applied for fair comparisons (for example, applying OFT or LoRA to the same layer when comparing against LoRA).

The paper’s discussion also suggests benchmarking axes that future work should include: (1) convergence dynamics (how many iterations/epochs to reach usable control), (2) sensitivity to finetuning hyperparameters such as learning rate and iteration count, (3) sample efficiency (how many finetuning images are needed), (4) stability (tendency to collapse under long finetuning), and (5) computational cost of finetuning (wall-clock and hardware). Collecting and reporting these metrics will make it easier to compare OFT against other parameter-efficient finetuning methods and to evaluate trade-offs in practice.

# Concluding perspective

The paper presents Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and Constrained OFT (COFT) as a principled family of finetuning strategies that preserve hyperspherical energy and thereby tend to retain the semantic abilities of large text-to-image diffusion models while enabling strong controllability. At the same time, the paper is explicit about its limitations and about several directions that deserve further research: alleviating the computational burden of the Cayley inverse, finding more parameter-efficient and less biased orthogonal parameterizations, understanding if and how orthogonal transforms compose across multiple downstream tasks, and developing richer measures of semantic preservation than a single aggregate statistic.

For practitioners and researchers approaching this line of work, the paper provides practical starting points (implementation choices, parameter-count formulas, datasets, and evaluation metrics), but it also clearly signals that several engineering and scientific problems remain open. Addressing these will be necessary both to scale OFT-style approaches to larger models and to ensure their responsible use in personalization and controllable generation scenarios.
