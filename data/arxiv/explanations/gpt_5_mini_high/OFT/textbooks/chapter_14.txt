Chapter 14: Exercises and Mini-Projects

# Implement an OFT layer with Cayley parameterization and block-diagonal structure

The core of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is simple to state: for a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ (a fully connected layer, or a flattened convolutional layer), replace the forward pass
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}
\]
by the OFT forward pass
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{W})^\top\bm{x}=((\bm{R}\bm{W}^0))^\top\bm{x},
\qquad\text{with}\qquad
\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}.
\]
Because an orthogonal $\bm{R}$ preserves pairwise angles among the column vectors of $\bm{W}^0$, this construction is the operational realization of the paper's objective to preserve hyperspherical energy during finetuning.

A numerically convenient parameterization of orthogonal matrices is the Cayley transform. Let $\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ be skew-symmetric so that $\bm{Q}^\top=-\bm{Q}$. The Cayley parameterization builds $\bm{R}$ as
\[
\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]
This yields an orthogonal matrix with determinant $+1$ (special orthogonal group). In implementation you therefore need to (1) maintain a skew-symmetric parameter $\bm{Q}$, (2) form $\bm{R}$ by evaluating the Cayley formula, (3) compute $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ for the forward pass, and (4) let automatic differentiation compute gradients back to the parameters used to build $\bm{Q}$.

A common and practical way to represent a skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$ is to store an unconstrained matrix $\bm{A}$ and form $\bm{Q}=(\bm{A}-\bm{A}^\top)/2$ in each forward pass. This guarantees skew-symmetry by construction while keeping optimization straightforward: the actual optimizer updates the entries of $\bm{A}$ and the forward pass enforces the skew constraint.

Directly parameterizing a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix can be expensive when $d$ is large. OFT reduces parameter and compute cost by using a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix
\[
\bm{R}=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r),
\qquad \bm{R}_i\in\text{O}\!\left(\tfrac{d}{r}\right).
\]
Each block $\bm{R}_i$ is generated via an independent Cayley transform from its skew-symmetric block $\bm{Q}_i$. This block structure reduces parameter complexity from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$, and further sharing the same block across groups of channels reduces parameters even more (paper: complexity can drop to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ under block sharing). In practice, implement each block separately: reshape the parameter matrices so that each block computes its Cayley transform and its contribution to $\bm{R}$, then assemble $\bm{R}$ as a block-diagonal matrix and multiply it with $\bm{W}^0$.

Initialization is important: set the Cayley skew parameters such that $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ at the start. Then $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{0})(\bm{I}-\bm{0})^{-1}=\bm{I}$, and the finetuned model starts exactly from the pretrained weights. During training you update the free parameters that define $\bm{Q}$, which is equivalent to optimizing on the orthogonal manifold via the Cayley parameterization.

Practical tips. Compute each block inverse $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}_i)^{-1}$ per block; the block-diagonal design keeps the matrix inverse small and efficient. To make the Cayley transform stable, keep the norm of each $\bm{Q}_i$ reasonably small (initialization at zero helps). Implement the skew-symmetric formation $\bm{Q}=(\bm{A}-\bm{A}^\top)/2$ in a numerically stable way (store only the lower triangular elements of $\bm{A}$ if you want to save parameters). During training, compute gradients through the Cayley formula—automatic differentiation handles the required matrix-inverse derivative.

A short algorithmic sketch: at each forward step construct $\bm{Q}_i$ from unconstrained parameters, compute each $\bm{R}_i=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q}_i)(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}_i)^{-1}$, assemble $\bm{R}$, form $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, and use $\bm{W}$ for the model forward pass. Backpropagate to the parameters underlying $\bm{Q}_i$ and update them with your optimizer.

# Replicate the angle-versus-magnitude toy experiment

One of the motivating observations in the paper is that the angular information between kernels (neuron directions) and patches carries most of the semantic content; magnitude contributes little. The inner product decomposes as
\[
\bm{w}^\top\bm{x}=\|\bm{w}\|\,\|\bm{x}\|\,\cos\theta,
\]
so testing magnitude-only or angle-only feature maps is natural.

A minimal experiment to reproduce the paper's toy result is as follows. Train a standard convolutional autoencoder (encoder and decoder built from ordinary convolution layers) on a small collection of flower images as in the paper. Train with the usual inner-product-based convolution (no cosine normalization) and standard reconstruction loss. After training, perform three different reconstructions from the encoder feature maps:

1. The original inner-product feature map: for each convolutional filter $\bm{w}$ and local patch $\bm{x}$ compute $z(\bm{w},\bm{x})=\bm{w}^\top\bm{x}$ and decode using the trained decoder.

2. Magnitude-only feature map: for each pair use
\[
z_{\text{mag}}(\bm{w},\bm{x})=\|\bm{w}\|\,\|\bm{x}\|.
\]
This discards angle information and retains only magnitudes.

3. Angle-only (cosine) feature map: for each pair use
\[
z_{\text{angle}}(\bm{w},\bm{x})=\frac{\bm{w}^\top\bm{x}}{\|\bm{w}\|\,\|\bm{x}\|+\delta}
\]
where a small $\delta>0$ prevents division by zero in practice.

Feed each of these three variants of the feature map through the (fixed) decoder and visualize the resulting reconstructions. The paper's result is that the angle-only reconstruction preserves the semantic content and reconstructs images almost perfectly, whereas the magnitude-only reconstruction contains little useful information.

Implementation notes. When computing $\|\bm{x}\|$ at test time, compute the norm of the local patch corresponding to each sliding window (same receptive field as the convolution). Make sure the decoder expects the same channel and spatial dimensionality as the encoder output; if your decoder expects unnormalized activations, rescale appropriately so that the three variants are comparable. Add a tiny constant $\delta$ in divisions to avoid numerical issues. The goal is primarily qualitative: visualize the three reconstructed images side by side and confirm the angular dominance effect observed in the paper.

# Finetune a small diffusion UNet with OFT vs LoRA and compare convergence

The experiments in the paper compare OFT and its constrained variant (COFT) to baseline finetuning strategies such as DreamBooth finetuning and LoRA. Reproducing a scaled-down version of these comparisons is an excellent mini-project that makes the convergence and stability differences concrete.

Set up a small latent diffusion UNet (or use a publicly available small diffusion UNet checkpoint). For a fair comparison to LoRA, apply both OFT and LoRA to the same weight matrices: in the paper the authors applied OFT to the attention weights when comparing to LoRA, so do the same in your experiment. Implement LoRA's additive low-rank update to the chosen matrices and implement OFT's multiplicative orthogonal transform as described above; make sure the number of trainable parameters is comparable between LoRA and OFT runs (use block sizes $r$ in OFT to match parameter counts).

Choose one downstream finetuning task to begin with. If you mirror the paper, you may try a subject-driven finetuning task in the DreamBooth style (a few images for a subject with a prompt), or a controllable generation task (e.g., canny edge to image) similar to ControlNet. Use the same loss used in the corresponding baseline: the paper follows DreamBooth for subject-driven generation and ControlNet/T2I-Adapter for controllable generation.

Training and evaluation protocol. Train each method with the same optimizer, batch size, and (where applicable) number of iterations/epochs. In the paper, controllable generation finetuning was run for 20 epochs; subject-driven finetuning experiments in the paper observe behavior at iteration scales such as 400 and 2000 iterations. During training log the same evaluation metrics the paper uses: for subject-driven generation log DINO and CLIP-I for subject fidelity, CLIP-T for prompt fidelity, and LPIPS for diversity; for controllable generation compute the control consistency metrics described below. Monitor generated samples at regular checkpoints and plot metric curves versus iterations or epochs.

What to expect. According to the paper's empirical findings, OFT and COFT converge more quickly and more stably than LoRA and naive finetuning. OFT often reaches acceptable control at fewer iterations/epochs and preserves subject identity better. COFT, with an explicit radius constraint, further improves finetuning stability by keeping the learned orthogonal transform close to the identity.

Practical suggestions. Initialize OFT so that $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ and thus $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$. If you use COFT, implement the projection on $\bm{Q}$ (see the COFT section below). To keep comparisons fair, apply OFT and LoRA to the exact same subset of layers (for example, only attention projection matrices) and keep their trainable parameter counts close.

# Ablate r and \epsilon and measure hyperspherical energy preservation

One of the core claims of the paper is that OFT preserves hyperspherical energy (HE) and that controlling the deviation from pretrained weights provides a useful stability lever. Design an ablation study that varies the block count $r$ (the number of diagonal blocks in $\bm{R}$) and the COFT radius parameter $\epsilon$, and measure both a) how well hyperspherical energy is preserved and b) how downstream performance changes.

First, implement a routine to compute hyperspherical energy for a matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\ldots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. Normalize each column vector and compute
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1},
\qquad\text{where}\qquad
\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\frac{\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{w}_i\|}.
\]
During finetuning track layerwise HE and report the difference $\|\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W})-\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\|$ for each layer as training progresses.

Design a grid of $r$ values (the paper uses $r$ to trade parameter efficiency and flexibility; smaller $r$ means larger blocks and more parameters) and COFT radii $\epsilon$ (COFT constrains the deviation of $\bm{R}$ from $\bm{I}$). For each setting run finetuning and log: (1) HE differences per layer and aggregated, (2) downstream generation metrics (DINO, CLIP-I/T, LPIPS for subject-driven or control consistency for controllable tasks), and (3) training curves to examine stability and convergence speed.

Interpretation. Because any exact orthogonal $\bm{R}$ preserves pairwise angles, an ideal OFT implementation will show HE differences near zero (up to numerical precision). COFT adds an explicit constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le \epsilon$, which in practice is enforced by constraining the Cayley skew parameter $\bm{Q}$ (via the linearized approximation $\bm{R}\approx\bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$, the paper enforces $\|\bm{Q}\|\le\epsilon'$ and projects via projected gradient descent). The ablation should reveal the trade-off the paper describes: smaller blocks (larger $r$) reduce parameter count and can limit flexibility, while smaller $\epsilon$ keeps the finetuned weights close to pretrained ones (higher stability but lower task-specific flexibility). Visualize HE difference and downstream metrics as functions of $r$ and $\epsilon$ to make this trade-off explicit.

# Implement and apply control consistency metrics on C2I, S2I, L2F

The paper introduces a practical evaluation approach for controllable generation called control consistency: estimate the control signal from the generated image and compare it to the original control input. Implement and apply these metrics to evaluate how well finetuned models follow control inputs.

For Canny-edge-to-image (C2I) tasks, the control signal is an edge map. For each generated image run the same edge detector used to produce the input control (e.g., Canny with the same thresholds). Compute intersection-over-union (IoU) between the input edge map $E_{\text{in}}$ and the edge map recovered from the generated image $E_{\text{out}}$:
\[
\mathrm{IoU}=\frac{|E_{\text{in}}\cap E_{\text{out}}|}{|E_{\text{in}}\cup E_{\text{out}}|}.
\]
Also compute the F1 score where precision and recall are defined in the usual way in terms of the matching pixels between $E_{\text{in}}$ and $E_{\text{out}}$. These two metrics measure how faithfully the generator reproduced the input edge geometry.

For segmentation-to-image (S2I) tasks, the control is a segmentation map. For each generated image apply an off-the-shelf segmentation model to obtain a predicted segmentation map. Compute per-class IoUs and average them to get mean IoU. Compute per-class accuracy and average these to obtain mean accuracy, and compute overall pixelwise accuracy as
\[
\text{Overall Acc}=\frac{\sum_{p} \mathbb{1}\{ \hat{y}_p=y_p\}}{N_{\text{pixels}}}.
\]
Here $\hat{y}_p$ is the predicted class at pixel $p$ and $y_p$ is the input control class at pixel $p$. These are standard segmentation consistency metrics and capture whether the generated image contains the semantic regions implied by the input segmentation.

For landmark-to-face (L2F) tasks, the control is a set of facial landmark coordinates. Run a face landmark detector on the generated image to obtain predicted landmarks and compute the mean Euclidean distance between predicted and input control landmark coordinates, averaged over landmarks and images:
\[
\text{Mean L2}=\frac{1}{N_{\text{images}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{images}}}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\| \hat{\ell}_{i,k}-\ell_{i,k} \|_2.
\]
Lower values indicate better compliance with the control pose.

Implementation guidance. Use the same preprocessing, resolution, and detector thresholds for control extraction from generated images as used to produce the input control signals. For S2I and L2F tasks the paper used off-the-shelf models to estimate the recovered control signal; follow the same approach. Evaluate metrics over the full validation set for robust comparisons.

# Compose multiple OFT transforms and study interference

A theoretically attractive property is that the composition (matrix product) of orthogonal matrices is still orthogonal. This leads to an interesting empirical mini-project: finetune separate OFT transforms for two different tasks and study whether composing them retains each task's behavior, or whether the tasks interfere.

Concretely, start from the same pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$. Finetune with OFT on task A to obtain orthogonal transform $\bm{R}_A$ so that the finetuned weights are $\bm{W}_A=\bm{R}_A\bm{W}^0$. Independently finetune another copy on task B to obtain $\bm{R}_B$ and $\bm{W}_B$. Now consider the composed transform $\bm{R}_{\text{comp}}=\bm{R}_B\bm{R}_A$ and the composed weights
\[
\bm{W}_{\text{comp}}=\bm{R}_{\text{comp}}\bm{W}^0=(\bm{R}_B\bm{R}_A)\bm{W}^0.
\]
Because $\bm{R}_{\text{comp}}$ is orthogonal, hyperspherical energy is preserved relative to $\bm{W}^0$, but it is an open empirical question whether $\bm{W}_{\text{comp}}$ will simultaneously satisfy the requirements of both tasks.

Design experiments that compare three models on both tasks: (1) single-task $\bm{W}_A$ for task A and $\bm{W}_B$ for task B, (2) sequential finetuning where you finetune for task A and then finetune the result (starting from $\bm{W}_A$) for task B, and (3) composed transform $\bm{W}_{\text{comp}}$. Evaluate each model on the task-specific metrics (subject-driven metrics, control consistency metrics, etc.) and report how performance trades off. The paper mentions this compositionality as an open problem; your experiments will explore whether simple multiplication of orthogonal transforms yields a practical route to multi-tasking without catastrophic forgetting, or whether interference occurs.

Report both objective metrics and qualitative examples. Given the theoretical preservation of pairwise angles, analyze whether and how the two tasks are compatible in the same rotated coordinate system.

# Extend OFT to convolutional layers (depth-wise vs shared blocks)

Although OFT is described for fully connected layers, the paper highlights that OFT naturally extends to convolutional layers by interpreting the convolution kernel as a matrix whose columns are neurons. Concretely, a convolutional weight tensor shaped $(\text{out\_channels},\ \text{in\_channels},\ k, k)$ can be reshaped into a matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ with $d=\text{in\_channels}\cdot k\cdot k$ and $n=\text{out\_channels}$. Applying an orthogonal transform $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ to this matrix as $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ corresponds to rotating the input-kernel basis for all output channels.

Two interesting block strategies are particularly worth studying as mini-project variants. First, set the number of blocks $r$ equal to the number of input channels so that each block has size $k\cdot k$. Each block $\bm{R}_i$ then acts on the spatial kernel weights of one input channel only; this is analogous to an orthogonal depth-wise transform per input channel. Second, share the same orthogonal block among multiple channels so that the same spatial transform applies to several channels; this reduces the parameter count and imposes a different inductive bias.

Implement both variants: (a) reshape the convolutional kernels into a matrix, apply OFT with $r=\text{in\_channels}$ so that each block is small (depth-wise), (b) apply OFT with fewer blocks and shared blocks across input channels. Retrain or finetune on the same downstream tasks and compare both performance and parameter counts. The paper indicates that the depth-wise block setting maps naturally to depth-wise convolution interpretations and can be parameter-efficient.

Practical considerations. Reshaping and reassembling convolutional weights correctly is crucial. When converting back to convolutional weight shapes, ensure channel order and kernel layout match the original framework conventions (e.g., channel-first vs channel-last). Use block-diagonal Cayley transforms to avoid inverting a huge $(d\times d)$ matrix; operate on small per-block inverses. Measure both final downstream metrics and HE preservation to ensure orthogonality is maintained.

# Suggested project sequence, deliverables, and reporting

A pedagogically effective sequence is: first implement a correct OFT layer with Cayley and block-diagonal structure and verify that with $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ the model reproduces pretrained outputs. Second, reproduce the angle-vs-magnitude toy experiment to internalize why direction matters. Third, implement COFT projection and run small-scale finetuning comparisons between OFT, COFT, and LoRA on a toy diffusion UNet; plot metric curves and visualize samples across checkpoints. Fourth, conduct the $r$ and $\epsilon$ ablations and produce HE-preservation plots and downstream metric trade-off plots. Fifth, implement the control consistency metrics and evaluate on the C2I, S2I, and L2F settings. Sixth, explore composition of orthogonal transforms and extensions to convolutional layers.

For each mini-project deliver: (1) code for the implemented OFT/COFT layer(s), (2) a short experimental script that runs a chosen finetuning and evaluation protocol, (3) plots of training curves (HE difference and downstream metrics), and (4) representative qualitative samples illustrating success or failure modes. Where possible follow the paper's evaluation practices: for subject-driven generation the paper repeated finetuning across many random seeds (30) to reduce randomness; report mean and variance of metrics where feasible. For controllable generation, follow the paper's practice of reporting control consistency metrics such as IoU, F1, mean IoU, accuracy, and mean L2 landmark distance.

These exercises are intentionally open-ended so you can explore implementation variants and empirical trade-offs. The paper provides the guiding principles and the mathematical definitions you need: OFT's multiplicative orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$, Cayley parameterization $(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$, block-diagonal parameterization for efficiency, COFT's small-radius constraint implemented as a projection on $\bm{Q}$ using the approximation $\bm{R}\approx\bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$, and the hyperspherical energy
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
Following these steps will let you reproduce and extend the empirical findings in the paper, and will give hands-on experience with the trade-offs between flexibility and regularity that underlie OFT and COFT.
