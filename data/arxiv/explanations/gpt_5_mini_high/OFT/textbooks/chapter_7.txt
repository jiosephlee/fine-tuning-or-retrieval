\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 7: Re-scaled OFT and Method Variants

# Adding a positive diagonal scaling D on neuron outputs

Re-scaled OFT extends the basic Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) idea by giving each output neuron an independent magnitude (scale) in addition to the shared orthogonal rotation applied to neuron directions. Concretely, for a pretrained fully connected layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ (columns $\bm{w}^0_i$ are the pretrained neurons), re-scaled OFT replaces the usual OFT forward pass
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}
\]
by
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x},
\]
where $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is the layer-shared orthogonal matrix learned by OFT and $\bm{D}=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is a learnable diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries $s_i$ are strictly positive. Each $s_i$ rescales the $i$-th column of $\bm{W}^0$ (after rotation by $\bm{R}$), so re-scaled OFT modifies both angle (via $\bm{R}$) and magnitude (via $\bm{D}$) of neurons.

Two practical points are important to keep in mind. First, to preserve the exact pretrained behavior at the start of finetuning, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is initialized as the identity and the diagonal scaling is typically initialized as $\bm{D}=\bm{I}$ (so the finetuned model starts from $\bm{W}^0$). Second, the constraint $s_i>0$ is enforced conceptually so that scaling does not introduce sign flips per neuron; the paper explicitly assumes the diagonal entries are larger than zero.

# Why scaling does not affect hyperspherical energy

The central analytic observation behind OFT is that hyperspherical energy (HE) depends only on the directions of neurons, not their magnitudes. Re-scaled OFT preserves this key invariance: adding a positive diagonal scaling $\bm{D}$ does not change the hyperspherical energy of the layer. We show this formally.

Recall the hyperspherical energy of a weight matrix $\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ is defined using normalized columns $\hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ as
\[
\operatorname{HE}(\bm{W}) \;:=\; \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\]
Under re-scaled OFT we have $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$. Let $\bm{w}_i^0$ denote the $i$-th column of $\bm{W}^0$ and $s_i$ the corresponding diagonal element of $\bm{D}$. Then the $i$-th column of $\bm{W}$ is
\[
\bm{w}_i \;=\; \bm{R}\,\bm{w}_i^0\,s_i.
\]
Normalize this column:
\[
\hat{\bm{w}}_i \;=\; \frac{\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{w}_i\|} \;=\; \frac{\bm{R}\,\bm{w}_i^0\,s_i}{\|\bm{R}\,\bm{w}_i^0\,s_i\|}.
\]
Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal and $s_i>0$, the norm in the denominator factors as $\|\bm{R}\,\bm{w}_i^0\,s_i\| = s_i\|\bm{w}_i^0\|$, so
\[
\hat{\bm{w}}_i \;=\; \bm{R}\,\frac{\bm{w}_i^0}{\|\bm{w}_i^0\|} \;=\; \bm{R}\,\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0.
\]
Thus every normalized neuron after re-scaling is just the orthogonal transform of the corresponding normalized pretrained neuron. Using orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ (which preserves Euclidean norms), pairwise normalized distances satisfy
\[
\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|
= \|\bm{R}\,\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0 - \bm{R}\,\hat{\bm{w}}_j^0\|
= \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0 - \hat{\bm{w}}_j^0\|.
\]
Plugging this into the HE definition yields
\[
\operatorname{HE}(\bm{W}) \;=\; \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}
\;=\; \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0 - \hat{\bm{w}}_j^0\|^{-1}
\;=\; \operatorname{HE}(\bm{W}^0).
\]
Therefore the diagonal scaling $\bm{D}$ (so long as its entries are positive) does not change hyperspherical energy: per-neuron magnitudes are normalized away and the shared orthogonal rotation preserves the pairwise geometry on the unit hypersphere.

This invariance is the reason re-scaled OFT retains the same structural guarantee of OFT: re-scaled OFT can increase flexibility by adjusting magnitudes without violating the hyperspherical-energy preservation that the OFT framework enforces.

# Forward pass with both R and D, and parameter overhead

The re-scaled OFT forward pass for a layer is
\[
\bm{z} \;=\; (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D})^\top \bm{x},
\]
with learnable $\bm{R}$ (orthogonal, layer-shared) and learnable diagonal $\bm{D}$. In comparison to plain OFT, re-scaled OFT adds only the diagonal degrees of freedom in $\bm{D}$. From a parameter-count perspective this is very light: $\bm{D}$ contains $n$ scalar parameters (one per output neuron or output channel), which is typically negligible compared to the parameters used to represent an orthogonal $\bm{R}$ or the original dense matrix $\bm{W}^0$.

To recall the cost of representing $\bm{R}$: a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix can be parameterized through a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ of size $d\times d$ (Cayley parameterization), and the free parameters are on the order of $d(d-1)/2$, i.e. $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. To save parameters, the paper uses a block-diagonal orthogonal parameterization
\[
\bm{R} = \operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r),
\qquad \bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{(d/r)\times(d/r)},
\]
whose parameter count is
\[
r\cdot \frac{(d/r)((d/r)-1)}{2} \;=\; \frac{d(d/r - 1)}{2},
\]
i.e. $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. The block sharing trick (making all $\bm{R}_i$ identical) can further reduce parameter complexity toward $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. Against that background, the $n$ extra scalars in $\bm{D}$ are small: re-scaled OFT has essentially the OFT parameter cost plus $n$ for the diagonal.

To make this concrete with the example used in the paper: for a $128\times 128$ weight matrix with block parameter $r=8$, the OFT implementation given in the paper uses about $960$ trainable parameters; adding a diagonal $\bm{D}$ of length $n=128$ raises the total to $960+128=1088$ parameters. The paper calls this additional overhead negligible relative to other adapter-style methods (for instance LoRA in the example uses $2048$ trainable parameters with $r'=8$). Thus re-scaled OFT achieves extra modeling flexibility at very small parameter cost.

# When re-scaled OFT helps and interplay with COFT

Why add diagonal scaling at all if orthogonal transforms already preserve hyperspherical energy and capture directional change? The paper frames re-scaled OFT as a lightweight way to give the finetuning procedure slightly more expressive power while keeping the hyperspherical-energy invariance intact. Intuitively, $\bm{R}$ changes neuron directions (angles) — which, as argued in the main OFT discussion, are the primary carriers of semantic structure — while $\bm{D}$ lets the finetuner adjust per-neuron amplitudes to better match downstream targets, without disturbing the pairwise angular geometry that OFT preserves.

Empirically (as reported in the paper), the authors kept most main experiments on the simpler OFT to highlight the role of orthogonal transforms, but they observed that re-scaled OFT is generally better (see the paper's appendix). That matches the conceptual picture: adding a small number of per-neuron magnitudes typically helps optimization and fitting on downstream tasks while preserving the hyperspherical structure that the OFT principle enforces.

Regarding interaction with Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT): COFT augments OFT with an explicit deviation constraint that keeps the learned orthogonal transform near the identity (formally $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq\epsilon$, implemented via a constraint on the skew-symmetric Cayley parameter $\bm{Q}$). Because $\bm{D}$ does not alter hyperspherical energy, COFT's HE-preserving guarantee remains valid when $\bm{D}$ is added. However, COFT was introduced to improve finetuning stability by explicitly limiting deviation from the pretrained weights; re-scaled OFT does change the Euclidean relation between $\bm{W}$ and $\bm{W}^0$ (since the magnitudes are modified), so practitioners who want to control Euclidean closeness as well as angular structure may consider whether to (a) keep the COFT constraint on $\bm{R}$ alone or (b) also constrain $\bm{D}$ (for example, to keep $\bm{D}$ near $\bm{I}$). The paper reports COFT as a practical stability-improving variant; combining it with diagonal scaling is conceptually straightforward and leaves hyperspherical-energy invariance intact, but the main experimental comparisons in the paper focus on the original OFT/COFT choices (the paper notes the re-scaled variant is generally better and provides results in the appendix).

# Implementation note and errata clarification

Two practical implementation notes from the paper are worth calling out. First, the correct re-scaled OFT forward pass used in the authors' implementation and experiments is
\[
\bm{z} \;=\; (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D})^\top \bm{x},
\]
i.e. the diagonal scaling $\bm{D}$ multiplies the pretrained weights on the right, after which the orthogonal rotation $\bm{R}$ is applied on the left. The paper includes an explicit erratum: in the NeurIPS camera-ready version the forward pass for re-scaled OFT was mistakenly printed as $(\bm{D}\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$; the authors clarify that the original implementation used $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$ and the reported experimental results are based on that correct implementation, so the results and appendix are unaffected by the typo.

Second, and related, re-scaled OFT preserves the key OFT guarantee (hyperspherical-energy invariance) only when the diagonal entries are positive; the paper explicitly assumes $\bm{D}=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ with $s_i>0$. In practice one therefore initializes $\bm{D}$ so that it starts at the identity (no scaling) and ensures the diagonal entries remain positive during optimization (the paper states the positivity assumption but leaves exact parameterization details to the implementation).

In summary, re-scaled OFT is a minimal, principled extension of OFT: it keeps the hyperspherical-energy invariance that underlies OFT's semantic preservation while adding very inexpensive per-neuron magnitudes. This preserves the desirable stability and geometry of OFT and provides modest extra capacity that the authors observed to be generally beneficial in practice.
