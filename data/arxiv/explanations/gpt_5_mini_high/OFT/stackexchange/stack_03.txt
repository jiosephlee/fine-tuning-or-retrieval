\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### If OFT preserves all pairwise neuron angles, how can it still change the model’s behavior?
Question:
OFT preserves the hyperspherical energy by keeping all pairwise neuron angles the same within a layer via \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\). Intuitively that sounds like preserving most of the layer’s "geometry." Yet the method achieves different behavior (e.g., stronger controllability) after fine-tuning. What is changing if all inter-neuron angles are fixed? Is the key that angles between neurons and the layer inputs \(\mathbf{x}\) are not preserved (since \(\mathbf{x}\) is not rotated), so the alignment of features with the input distribution changes even though neuron-neuron geometry stays the same? Some intuition or a simple example would help.
Answer:
Short answer: your intuition is correct. OFT preserves pairwise neuron angles (the hyperspherical energy) because it replaces a pretrained weight matrix by a left-multiplication with an orthogonal matrix, $\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$. That guarantees the Gram matrix among neurons is unchanged, but it does not preserve the inner products between neurons and a fixed input $\mathbf{x}$. Those neuron–input alignments are exactly what determine the layer’s outputs, so rotating the whole neuron set can produce very different activations and thus different downstream behavior.

Here is the algebra that makes this immediate. With OFT the layer output is
$$\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top(\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{x}).$$
The neuron–neuron Gram matrix is preserved because
$$\mathbf{W}^\top\mathbf{W}=(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0=(\mathbf{W}^0)^\top\mathbf{W}^0,$$
so all pairwise neuron inner products (and therefore angles) stay the same. But the layer sees $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{x}$ instead of $\mathbf{x}$: unless the input distribution is rotationally symmetric, the set of dot products $\{\mathbf{w}_i^\top\mathbf{x}\}$ will change, and that changes activations after the nonlinearity and the whole downstream computation.

A tiny 2D example makes this concrete. Take $\mathbf{W}^0=[(1,0),(0,1)]$ (two orthonormal neurons) and rotate by $\theta=\pi/4$. The rotated neurons are $\mathbf{w}_1'=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$, $\mathbf{w}_2'=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$; their mutual inner product remains 0, so their angle is still $90^\circ$. For input $\mathbf{x}=(1,0)$ the original pre-activations were $(1,0)$; after rotation they are $\bigl(\cos\theta,\,-\sin\theta\bigr)\approx(0.707,\,-0.707)$. That sign flip and magnitude change will typically alter ReLU thresholds, normalization statistics, and every downstream layer that depends on these activations — so the network’s outputs change substantially even though neuron–neuron angles did not.

Two important nuances that the paper also alludes to are worth noting. First, if the input distribution were exactly rotation-invariant (e.g., isotropic Gaussian), then rotating neurons would not change the distribution of activations in expectation, so OFT would have little effect; real image distributions are not rotation-invariant, so OFT can change behavior by re-aligning neurons to the data. Second, because activations pass through nonlinearities and later layers, even a modest rotation of neuron directions can be amplified into a large functional change; COFT (the constrained variant) and the re-scaled OFT (adding per-neuron magnitudes) are precisely ways the paper uses to control how much alignment to the inputs is changed while still preserving hyperspherical structure.

In short: OFT preserves internal geometry among neurons (good for keeping pretrained semantics stable) but deliberately manipulates the neurons’ absolute orientation relative to the fixed input space (the degrees of freedom that actually change the mapping from $\mathbf{x}$ to $\mathbf{z}$). That is why OFT can both keep the model stable and still produce controlled behavioral changes.
