\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Does re-scaled OFT with a diagonal \(\mathbf{D}\) actually preserve \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})\)? Any side effects on normalization?
Question:
Re-scaled OFT uses \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0\,\mathbf{D}\) with \(\mathbf{D}=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n), s_i>0\), and claims that scaling does not change HE because columns are normalized when computing \(\text{HE}(\cdot)\). Can you provide a short proof that \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\mathbf{D}) = \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)\)? Also, since \(\mathbf{D}\) changes pre-activations, does it interact poorly with LayerNorm/GroupNorm or require additional stabilization (e.g., constraints on \(s_i\) or learning rate)?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — re‑scaled OFT with a positive diagonal $\mathbf{D}=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ preserves hyperspherical energy, and the proof is one line; however $\mathbf{D}$ does change pre‑activation magnitudes and so can interact with normalization layers (LayerNorm/GroupNorm/BatchNorm). In practice you should initialize and constrain the scales (or use a smaller LR / regularizer) to avoid destabilizing activations — the paper notes re‑scaled OFT helps empirically but does not claim any special normalization trick, so a little care is advised.

Proof that $\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\mathbf{D})=\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)$ (short and self‑contained). Let $\mathbf{W}^0=[\mathbf{w}^0_1,\dots,\mathbf{w}^0_n]$ and form $\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\mathbf{D}$. The $i$th column of $\mathbf{W}$ is
$\mathbf{w}_i=\mathbf{R}(s_i\mathbf{w}^0_i)=s_i\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^0_i$. The normalized column used in the hyperspherical energy is
$$
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i=\frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|}=\frac{s_i\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^0_i}{s_i\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^0_i\|}
=\frac{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^0_i}{\|\mathbf{w}^0_i\|}
=\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_i,
$$
where we used $\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u}\|=\|\mathbf{u}\|$ for orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$. Hence for any $i\neq j$
$$
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|=\|\mathbf{R}(\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_j)\|
=\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_j\|.
$$
Since $\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}$, the pairwise distances (and therefore the $\mathrm{HE}$ sum) are identical for $\mathbf{W}$ and $\mathbf{W}^0$. The $s_i>0$ assumption is important: a negative $s_i$ flips direction before normalization and can change angles between columns, so the paper restricts $s_i>0$.

About LayerNorm / GroupNorm / other normalizations. The preservation of $\mathrm{HE}$ is purely a geometric statement about normalized columns and does not imply that the network’s forward / backward numerical behavior is unchanged. Multiplying the $i$th column by $s_i$ multiplies that channel’s pre‑activation $z_i=\mathbf{w}_i^\top x$ by $s_i$, so activation statistics seen by any downstream normalization (and by nonlinearities and residual sums) do change. Whether this is a problem depends on where the normalization sits and whether it has trainable affine parameters. If the linear layer you re‑scale is immediately followed by a LayerNorm/GroupNorm with trainable per‑channel gains, those gains can partially absorb the effect of $D$; if the norm is upstream or frozen, per‑channel scaling will alter the normalized outputs and can affect training stability or require the optimizer to re‑calibrate other parameters.

Practical recommendations (simple, conservative rules consistent with the paper’s presentation). Initialize all $s_i=1$ (so you start exactly at the pretrained network), enforce positivity (e.g. parametrize $s_i=\exp(t_i)$ or $s_i=\text{softplus}(t_i)$ with $t_i\!=\!0$ init), and use milder updates for the scales than for the orthogonal transform and other weights: either a smaller learning rate for the $t_i$, a small weight decay / penalty on $\log s_i$, or a hard/soft clamp such as $s_i\in[1/\alpha,\alpha]$ (typical $\alpha$ might be 1.5–2). Alternatively, share scales across blocks (per‑group instead of per‑neuron) to reduce degrees of freedom. If you observe instability, freeze $D$ for a few warmup steps or add a small regularizer on $\log s_i$ (so the optimizer prefers staying near 1). These are standard stabilizations when you add per‑channel multiplicative freedom; the paper reports that re‑scaled OFT generally helps in their experiments but doesn’t report special normalization fixes, so the above are pragmatic safeguards rather than requirements.

In short: mathematically $\mathrm{HE}$ is preserved (proof above), but because $\mathbf{D}$ changes pre‑activation magnitudes it can interact with normalization layers and the optimizer; initialize $s_i=1$, keep positivity, and control the learning dynamics of $s_i$ (smaller LR, regularization or clipping, or coarser per‑block scales) to avoid instabilities.
