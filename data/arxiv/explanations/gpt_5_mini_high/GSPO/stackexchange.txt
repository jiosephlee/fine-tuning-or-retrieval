\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### What exactly makes GRPO "ill-posed"—bias, variance, or unit mismatch?
Question:
The paper says GRPO is ill-posed because it applies token-level importance weights $w_{i,t} = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$ while rewards are given at the sequence level. Can you make precise what "ill-posed" means here? Is the gradient estimator biased, does its variance explode with sequence length $|y|$, or is it a mismatch between the unit of reward (sequence) and the unit of optimization (token) that breaks an assumption? A small counterexample or formal statement would help.
Answer:
Short answer: the root cause is a unit mismatch — GRPO applies single-step (token-level) importance ratios to a sequence-level reward. That mismatch invalidates the usual importance-sampling correction and therefore produces a gradient estimator that is not the correct (sequence-level) importance-sampled gradient. Concretely this produces a biased estimator (relative to the sequence-level objective) in general, and it also exposes you to much larger, length-dependent variance and pathological behavior when token ratios are noisy (as in MoE). Clipping on token ratios makes the bias worse. Below I make this precise with equations and a tiny counterexample.

Why GRPO is wrong in one equation (no clipping)
- The true sequence objective is
  $$ J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal D,\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}[r(x,y)]
  =\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}\,r(x,y)\right]. $$
  Its importance-sampled REINFORCE-style gradient (expand $ \nabla\log\pi_\theta(y|x)=\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}) $) is
  $$ \nabla_\theta J(\theta)
  =\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\left[\Big(\prod_{s=1}^{|y|} w_s\Big)\,r(x,y)\,\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\right], $$
  where $ w_t=\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})} $ and $ \prod_s w_s=\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)} $.

- GRPO (without clipping) uses token-level ratios inside the sum:
  $$ \nabla_\theta J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)
  =\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\left[\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
  w_t\,\widehat A(y)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\right], $$
  with $ \widehat A(y) $ the (sequence) advantage shared across tokens.

- Subtracting gives the exact gap (no clipping):
  $$ \nabla_\theta J(\theta)-\nabla_\theta J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)
  =\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
  \Big(\prod_{s=1}^{|y|}w_s-\frac{1}{|y|}w_t\Big)\,r(x,y)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\right]. $$
  This difference is generally nonzero for $|y|>1$. So GRPO does not estimate the correct sequence-level importance-sampled gradient: it is biased with respect to the true sequence objective.

A minimal numeric counterexample
Take $|y|=2$, independent tokens under $ \pi_{\text{old}} $ with $ \pi_{\text{old}}(y_t=\text{``good''})=0.5 $. Suppose only $ y=(\text{good},\text{good}) $ has reward 1. Pick $ \pi_\theta(y_1=\text{good})=0.6 $ and $ \pi_\theta(y_2=\text{good})=0.8 $. Then for that rewarded sequence
- sequence ratio $= (0.6/0.5)\cdot(0.8/0.5)=1.2\cdot1.6=1.92$,
- GRPO token-average ratio $=(1.2+1.6)/2=1.4$.

If the two token gradients happen to be equal (say each contributes “1”), the correct IS-weighted contribution to the gradient is $ \approx 1.92\times 2 $ (because the sum over two tokens), while GRPO gives $ \approx 1.4\times 1 $ (because of the $1/|y|$ averaging). They differ substantially — so GRPO is optimizing a different objective.

Intuition about variance and why it gets worse with length
- Product vs single-step noise. The correct sequence IS weight is the product $ \prod_t w_t $. If the per-token log-ratios $ \delta_t=\log w_t $ are (roughly) independent with variance $ \sigma^2 $, then $ \log\prod_t w_t=\sum_t\delta_t $ has variance $ T\sigma^2 $. Thus the product has multiplicative spread $ \mathrm{Var}(\prod_t w_t)\approx e^{T\sigma^2}-1 $, which explodes with $ T $. This is the usual curse of importance sampling for long sequences.
- Why GRPO is still bad: GRPO does not form the product, but it uses single-step ratios $ w_t $ (which themselves can be very noisy in MoE because routing or small likelihood changes give huge per-token swings). Those per-token spikes act like high-frequency noise on the gradient and, because GRPO mixes differently across tokens (unequal weights within a sequence), these noisy contributions do not cancel in expectation and can accumulate into unstable updates. GSPO’s sequence-level weighting (and length-normalization) aggregates the per-token log-ratios first (the geometric mean) and therefore smooths out token-level extremes: $ s=\exp\big(\frac1T\sum_t\delta_t\big) $ has variance $ \approx e^{\sigma^2/T}-1 $, which shrinks with $ T $. That is why the paper argues GSPO is numerically far more stable.

Role of clipping and MoE routing volatility
Clipping is a nonlinear operation, so it destroys even more of the importance-sampling identity and introduces additional bias that depends on the sign/magnitude of advantages. With token-level clipping you can systematically under- or over-weight tokens depending on stochastic per-token ratios; small, frequent clipping-induced biases can compound through SGD and lead to catastrophic collapse. In MoE models the per-token ratio noise (from routing changes) is especially large, so token-level clipping is particularly harmful. The paper’s empirical observation that GRPO needed Routing Replay to stabilize MoE training fits exactly this diagnosis.

When would GRPO be valid?
GRPO would coincide with the correct sequence gradient in special cases: $|y|=1$ (trivial), or if the reward/advantage decomposes exactly per-token so that a token-level IS correction is the right correction, or if the conditional expectations of the product of the other token ratios equal 1 in the relevant weighted sense (a very strong and typically false condition). In practice for language-model RL (one sample per prefix, sequence rewards) those conditions do not hold.

Bottom line
“Ill-posed” in the paper means the algorithm violates the basic unit-consistency required by importance sampling: you must correct at the same unit that carries the reward. By applying single-token importance weights to whole-sequence rewards, GRPO is not performing valid sequence-level importance sampling. That produces a gradient estimator that is generally biased (it does not equal the sequence-level IS gradient), suffers from amplified instability/noise as responses get longer (and from MoE routing volatility), and is further corrupted by token-level clipping. GSPO fixes the unit mismatch by forming sequence-level likelihood ratios (with length normalization) and so aligns the importance weights with the reward unit, which reduces pathological variance and the clipping-induced damage observed under GRPO.

### Deriving GSPO from importance sampling: what target expectation is being estimated?
Question:
GSPO optimizes $\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)}[\min(s_i(\theta)\hat A_i, \operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\hat A_i)]$ with $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}$. If we start from the desired on-policy objective $\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal D, y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[A(x,y)]$ (with groupwise normalized advantages), what exact importance-sampling steps lead to the unclipped GSPO surrogate $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}[s_i(\theta) A(x,y)]$? Is the $1/|y|$ exponent merely a variance-reduction heuristic or part of a principled derivation?
Answer:
Short answer: GSPO’s unclipped surrogate comes from the usual importance-sampling rewrite of the on‑policy objective, but then the authors replace the correct sequence importance weight
$$
R(y)\;=\;\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}
$$
by its per‑token geometric mean
$$
s(y)\;=\;R(y)^{1/|y|} \;=\;\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid\cdot)}\Big).
$$
That replacement yields the unclipped GSPO surrogate
$$
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal D,\;y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}}\big[s(y)\,A(x,y)\big],
$$
but it is not an unbiased importance-sampling estimator of the original on‑policy objective (the unbiased IS estimator would use weight $R(y)$). The $1/|y|$ exponent is therefore a deliberate length‑normalization / variance‑reduction design choice, not a step forced by the basic IS identity.

Derivation and the gradient-level difference (short):
Start from the on‑policy objective
$$
J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal D,\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[A(x,y)].
$$
Standard importance sampling (rewriting the target expectation as an expectation under the behavior policy) gives the unbiased form
$$
J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\big[R(y)\,A(x,y)\big],\qquad R(y)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}.
$$
GSPO instead uses the surrogate weight $s(y)=R(y)^{1/|y|}$ and optimizes
$$
J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\big[s(y)\,A(x,y)\big].
$$
Taking gradients (unclipped) gives the form in the paper:
$$
\nabla_\theta J_{\text{GSPO}}=\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\text{old}}}\Big[s(y)A(x,y)\nabla_\theta\log s(y)\Big]
=\mathbb{E}\Big[s(y)A(x,y)\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\Big].
$$
Compare to the true IS gradient
$$
\nabla_\theta J=\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\text{old}}}\Big[R(y)A(x,y)\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\Big].
$$
Thus the GSPO update differs from the unbiased IS update by the multiplicative factor
$$
\frac{s(y)/( |y| )}{R(y)}=\frac{R(y)^{1/|y|-1}}{|y|},
$$
so GSPO is a biased surrogate (the bias is intentional: it changes how much different sampled sequences influence the gradient).

Why that particular exponent? Intuition and motivation
- Numerical stability and variance: $R(y)$ is a product of per‑token ratios and therefore can explode or vanish exponentially with sequence length. Taking the geometric mean (the $1/|y|$ root) converts a product into a per‑token scale (it is the exponentiation of the mean log‑ratio), keeping importance weights near a per‑token multiplicative change instead of an exponentially large/small number. This makes a single clipping range feasible across varying lengths and dramatically reduces variance in practice — exactly the motivation stated in the paper (“length normalization … to reduce the variance and to control $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range”).
- Bias–variance tradeoff: raising the weight to a power $<1$ is a standard stabilizing/tempering idea (it reduces spread of weights at the cost of bias). GSPO’s choice $\alpha=1/|y|$ makes the tempering adapt to sequence length (stronger shrinkage for longer sequences).
- Algorithmic consequence: because GSPO uses the sequence-level geometric‑mean weight and then averages token gradients (the $1/|y|$ factor inside the gradient), all tokens in a sequence are weighted equally by the same sequence scalar — this removes the high variance introduced by token‑level importance ratios used in GRPO, which is the explicit design goal of GSPO.

A few practical notes consistent with the paper
- Clipping and the token‑variant: the clipping step and the GSPO‑token construction further modify the surrogate and its gradients; clipping introduces additional bias (as in PPO), and the GSPO‑token trick is a way to keep the numerical weight equal across tokens while allowing per‑token advantages. When token advantages are equal, GSPO and GSPO‑token are numerically identical.
- If you want an unbiased estimator of the on‑policy objective you must keep the full sequence weight $R(y)$ (or use unbiased per‑decision IS variants). In large‑language‑model RL the variance of that unbiased estimator is typically prohibitive, which is why the paper adopts the biased but lower‑variance GSPO surrogate and empirically finds much more stable training (especially for long responses and MoE models).

Conclusion: the unclipped GSPO surrogate is obtained by first applying the usual IS identity and then replacing the exact sequence IS weight by its geometric mean (the $1/|y|$ root). That exponent is a pragmatic length‑normalization / variance‑reduction design choice (a tempered/normalized IS weight), not a consequence of the unbiased IS equality.

### Why raise the sequence importance ratio to the power $1/|y|$ (geometric mean of token ratios)?
Question:
GSPO defines $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|} = \exp\left(\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right)$. I see that this is the geometric mean of per-token ratios. Does using the geometric mean change the fixed point compared to using the raw ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}$? Can you quantify how this normalization affects bias/variance and why it improves numerical stability without altering the optimization target?
Answer:
Short answer: raising the sequence importance ratio to the power $1/|y|$ (i.e. using the geometric mean of token ratios) is a length-normalization step that dramatically reduces variance and keeps the importance weight on a unified scale across response lengths. It does not change the local (first‑order) learning signal at the current policy (so small, trust‑region updates behave the same), but it does change the global surrogate objective (so stationary points away from the current policy can differ). In practice this tradeoff—small bias for much lower variance and better numerical stability—is exactly what GSPO exploits.

Why the geometric mean is natural and numerically stable. The sequence likelihood ratio is
$$
r(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|} w_t(\theta),
\qquad w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}.
$$
GSPO uses
$$
s(\theta)=r(\theta)^{1/|y|}
=\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log w_t(\theta)\Big),
$$
so $\log s$ is the arithmetic mean of the per‑token log‑ratios. Because the sequence ratio multiplies token ratios, a few token changes can blow up $r$ exponentially with $|y|$; working in log‑space and averaging per token keeps the weight in a stable, length‑aware range.

Variance reduction (quantified). Let $Z_t=\log w_t(\theta)$ and (for intuition) suppose the $Z_t$ have finite variance $\sigma^2$ and are weakly dependent or roughly iid. Then
$$
\log r = \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} Z_t,\qquad \log s=\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} Z_t.
$$
Thus
$$
\mathrm{Var}(\log r)\approx |y|\,\sigma^2,\qquad
\mathrm{Var}(\log s)\approx \frac{\sigma^2}{|y|}.
$$
The standard deviation of $\log s$ is smaller than that of $\log r$ by a factor $|y|$. In other words, raw sequence log‑ratios spread out like $\sqrt{|y|}$, while the geometric mean concentrates like $1/\sqrt{|y|}$. This huge reduction in dispersion is why numerical stability and clipping behave sensibly across different lengths (you do not need exponentially different clipping ranges for different $|y|$).

Effect on bias and fixed points. Replacing $r$ by $s$ does change the surrogate objective: GSPO optimizes expectations weighted by $s$ rather than by the true importance weight $r$, so it is not an unbiased estimator of $ \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[f] $ in the strict importance‑sampling sense. However two important mitigating facts make this acceptable:

1. Monotonicity and ordering: $s=r^{1/|y|}$ is strictly increasing in $r$ (for fixed $|y|$), so high‑probability sequences under $\pi_\theta$ remain high under $s$ and vice versa. Thus sample ranking is preserved (within a given length), which helps clipping and selection behave sensibly.

2. Local (first‑order) equivalence at the current policy: evaluate the gradients given in the paper at $\theta=\theta_{\text{old}}$. For both the token‑level GRPO surrogate and the GSPO surrogate the importance multipliers become 1 at the old policy, so the two gradients coincide at that point:
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}\big|_{\theta=\theta_{\text{old}}}
=\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}\big|_{\theta=\theta_{\text{old}}}
= \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_i \frac{\widehat A_i}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\Big|_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\right].
$$
So in the small‑step / trust‑region regime (which PPO‑style clipping is designed to enforce) GSPO and a raw sequence‑ratio objective give the same local update direction; the bias from using $s$ is higher‑order in the step size and therefore small when updates are proximal.

Intuition about the tradeoff. Raw sequence ratio $r$ is the correct multiplicative IS weight if you wanted an unbiased reweighting of expectations under $\pi_\theta$, but it is extremely high‑variance for long responses (a single token with a tiny probability under the old policy can drive $r$ to near zero or huge). GSPO’s $s$ is a variance‑reducing, length‑normalized surrogate: it compresses outliers, makes clipping thresholds interpretable across lengths, and prevents a few token likelihood changes (or expert‑activation volatility in MoE) from dominating the gradient and causing catastrophic instability. The price is a bias in the surrogate; in practice that bias is small because PPO/GRPO already work with clipped, biased surrogates and because updates are kept small.

A simple numeric illustration: if every token ratio were $1.5$ and $|y|=50$, then $r=1.5^{50}\approx 8\times10^8$ (explosion!), while $s=1.5^{50/50}=1.5$. Clipping and gradient computation with $s$ are numerically sane; with $r$ they'd be catastrophic unless you used length‑dependent clipping ranges.

Conclusion. Using the geometric mean is primarily a variance‑reduction and normalization device that (i) keeps importance weights on a unified scale across lengths, (ii) concentrates the distribution of log‑weights (CLT/delta‑method intuition), and (iii) preserves the local first‑order learning signal at the current policy. It does change the global surrogate objective (so it’s not the exact IS correction), but that bias is small in the trust‑region regime and, as the paper shows empirically, the variance/stability benefits are decisive for large models and long responses.

### Does the $1/|y|$ factor in the GSPO gradient introduce a length bias?
Question:
The GSPO gradient is proportional to $\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})$, i.e., an average rather than a sum. Does this averaging reduce the learning signal for longer sequences and subtly favor shorter responses? How does this interact with rewards that naturally increase with reasoning depth, and what happens if we remove or change the $1/|y|$ factor?
Answer:
Short answer: No — the $\frac{1}{|y|}$ factor is a length *normalization* intended to control variance and keep the sequence importance ratio and clipping ranges comparable across lengths; it does not by itself create a systematic bias toward shorter responses. Whether longer responses get more or less learning signal is determined by how the reward/advantage $\widehat A$ scales with length and by clipping/importance-ratio behavior, not by the normalization alone.

Why (mathematically and intuitively).  The paper gives the per-sequence ( unclipped ) gradient contribution for one sampled response $y$ as
$$
\nabla_\theta J \propto s(\theta)\,\widehat A \cdot \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}),
$$
with
$$
s(\theta)=\Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y\mid x)}\Big)^{\!1/|y|}
=\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_t\mid\cdot)}\Big).
$$
So the $\frac{1}{|y|}$ appears for two related reasons: it (a) makes $s(\theta)$ the geometric-mean (per-token) importance ratio so that $s(\theta)$ stays in a comparable numerical range for different $|y|$, and (b) produces the averaged token-gradient $(1/|y|)\sum_t\nabla\log\pi$ in the derivative of $\log s(\theta)$.

Two simple regimes show there is no automatic “short-length” preference:

- If the advantage is roughly length-independent (e.g. $\widehat A\approx\text{const}$), the gradient from a long response reduces to that constant times the *mean* per-token log-gradient. Long sequences therefore do not produce larger updates just because they have more tokens; they contribute the per-token average signal, so longer outputs are not advantaged purely by length.

- If the reward/advantage grows with length (e.g. $\widehat A\propto |y|$ because deeper reasoning tends to earn higher reward), then the factor $|y|$ in $\widehat A$ cancels the $1/|y|$ and the gradient scales with the sum of per-token gradients. In that case longer responses indeed produce larger updates — but that is because the reward says they should, not because of the normalization.

Why the paper uses the normalization.  The authors explicitly motivate the $1/|y|$ exponent on the sequence ratio as a variance-control and range-unification trick: without it the plain sequence ratio $\pi_\theta(y)/\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y)$ is the product of many token ratios, its log is a sum that grows with length, and a small change in a few tokens can make that ratio astronomically small or large for long $y$. That explosion of variance would force length-dependent clipping or produce very unstable training (and is exactly the kind of variance/clipping pathology the paper aims to avoid).

What happens if you remove or change the factor.  Removing the $1/|y|$ (i.e. using raw sequence ratio) gives
$$
\nabla_\theta J \propto \tilde s(\theta)\,\widehat A \cdot \sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot),
$$
where $\tilde s(\theta)=\pi_\theta(y)/\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y)$. Two consequences follow: (1) the raw importance ratio $\tilde s(\theta)$ will have variance that typically grows exponentially with length (unstable, length-dependent clipping required), and (2) the gradient magnitude will scale with sequence length even when $\widehat A$ does not — so longer sequences get larger updates by virtue of length alone. Either effect can be undesirable: instability and extreme clipping can drown useful signal or require carefully tuned, length-dependent hyperparameters; giving uncalibrated extra weight to long sequences may push behavior you did not intend. Conversely, one could choose a different tempering exponent (e.g. $(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\rm old})^{1/|y|^\alpha}$) or other scalings, but those trade off variance/stability against how much you want length to magnify updates; the paper motivated $1/|y|$ as a practical, theoretically natural choice.

Practical guidance.  If you want the algorithm to favor longer reasoning because it genuinely improves reward, shape the reward (or advantage estimator) so that $\widehat A$ increases with desired reasoning depth. Keep the $1/|y|$ normalization (as GSPO does) to avoid exploding/squeezing importance ratios and to use uniform clipping ranges. If you need token-level credit assignment, use the GSPO-token variant (the paper shows it is numerically consistent with GSPO when per-token advantages are equal) or design per-token advantages explicitly rather than removing length normalization entirely.

Bottom line: the $1/|y|$ is a stabilizing normalization, not an arbitrary penalization of long outputs. Whether long responses are preferred is determined by the reward/advantage and clipping behavior; the normalization merely keeps those signals comparable across different lengths.

### How should we select the clipping range $\varepsilon$ in GSPO and why is it so small (e.g., $3\mathrm{e}{-4}$ to $4\mathrm{e}{-4}$)?
Question:
In the experiments, GSPO uses left/right clipping ranges around $3\times10^{-4}$ to $4\times10^{-4}$, while GRPO uses values like $0.2$ and $0.27$. Given $s_i(\theta)$ is a geometric mean of token ratios (thus closer to 1), how should we calibrate $\varepsilon$ as a function of sequence length and expected policy shift per update? Is there a rule of thumb tying $\varepsilon$ to a target per-token KL or to $\mathbb{E}[\log s_i]$?
Answer:
Short answer: $\varepsilon$ in GSPO is small because GSPO’s importance ratio $s_i$ is a length‑normalized (geometric mean) per‑token multiplier, so $\varepsilon$ is a bound on the allowed *per‑token* multiplicative/log change. You should pick $\varepsilon$ so that the implied *sequence‑level* change (which accumulates linearly in log space over $|y|$ tokens) equals the sequence‑level change or KL you are willing to tolerate. Concretely a very simple rule of thumb is

$$
\varepsilon \approx \frac{\log R_{\text{target}}}{|y|}\quad\text{or}\quad
\varepsilon \approx \frac{D_{\text{seq}}}{|y|}
$$

where $R_{\text{target}}$ is the allowed multiplicative change of whole‑sequence likelihood (e.g. $1.05$–$1.3$) and $D_{\text{seq}}$ is a target sequence KL (for small changes $\log R_{\text{target}} \approx D_{\text{seq}}$). This is why the paper’s values (around $3\times10^{-4}$–$4\times10^{-4}$) are sensible for typical response lengths: they correspond to modest whole‑sequence changes.

Why that formula: GSPO defines

$$
s_i(\theta)
=\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log w_{i,t}(\theta)\Big),
$$

and the sequence ratio is $R_i=\pi_\theta(y_i)/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i)=s_i^{|y_i|}$, so

$$
\log R_i = |y_i|\log s_i.
$$

If you clip $s_i\in[1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]$ then for small $\varepsilon$ we have $\log s_i\in[\log(1-\varepsilon),\log(1+\varepsilon)]\approx[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]$ and therefore $\log R_i$ is bounded about $\pm |y_i|\varepsilon$. If you want the whole‑sequence log change to stay below, say, $0.08$ ($\approx 8\%$ in multiplicative space), choose $\varepsilon\approx 0.08/|y|$. Equivalently, because the sequence KL satisfies (using $w_{\text{seq}}=R$)

$$
\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\text{old}}\|\pi_\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{old}}}[\log R] = -|y|\,\mathbb{E}[\log s_i],
$$

controlling $|\mathbb{E}[\log s_i]|\le\varepsilon$ yields a conservative bound $\mathrm{KL}_{\text{seq}}\lesssim |y|\varepsilon$, so $\varepsilon\approx\mathrm{KL}_{\text{seq}}/|y|$ is another natural calibration.

Numerical examples to build intuition: for $|y|=256$ and $\varepsilon=3\times10^{-4}$ the worst‑case $\log R \approx 256\times 3\times 10^{-4} = 0.0768$, so $R\lesssim e^{0.0768}\approx 1.08$ (about an $8\%$ change in full sequence likelihood). That is exactly why GSPO’s $\varepsilon$ looks tiny compared to GRPO’s token‑level clipping ($0.2$): GRPO’s $0.2$ bounds each token’s multiplier, which would multiply (and blow up) across many tokens; GSPO’s $\varepsilon$ bounds the per‑token *average* change so it must be much smaller numerically.

Practical recommendations consistent with the paper: pick a desired whole‑sequence tolerance (either a multiplicative factor $R_{\text{target}}$ or a sequence KL $D_{\text{seq}}$), divide by the typical response length to get $\varepsilon$, and then validate by monitoring the empirical distribution of $\log s_i$ and the fraction of responses clipped. Many teams pick modest sequence tolerances (e.g. $D_{\text{seq}}$ on the order of $0.05$–$0.3$), which for lengths in the few hundreds yields $\varepsilon$ in the $10^{-4}$–$10^{-3}$ range (explaining the paper’s $3\times10^{-4}$–$4\times10^{-4}$ choices). You can also tune slightly asymmetrically left/right (the paper uses $3\times10^{-4}/4\times10^{-4}$) as an empirical knob to give a little more room to up‑weighting or down‑weighting; this is a practical tuning detail rather than a conceptual change.

Finally, a useful operational check is to compute the empirical mean and standard deviation of $\log s_i$ after a few gradient steps. If the bulk of $\log s_i$ lies well within $[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]$ you are being conservative; if most mass is outside, either reduce the learning step size or increase $\varepsilon$ (guided by the sequence‑level target). This ties $\varepsilon$ to the actual policy shift per update and keeps GSPO’s sequence‑level clipping doing the exact job it is designed for.

### Why define $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ with a stop-gradient trick in GSPO-token instead of using $s_i$ directly?
Question:
GSPO-token sets $s_{i,t}(\theta) = \operatorname{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\operatorname{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})]}$, which is numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$, but yields a gradient proportional to $s_i(\theta)\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$. Could you show the autograd steps that motivate this construction, and explain why simply multiplying $s_i(\theta)$ by per-token advantages would not produce the desired token-level gradient?
Answer:
Short answer: the stop-gradient (detach) is needed so that the sequence-level importance score $s_i(\theta)$ acts purely as a multiplicative constant for each token's likelihood-gradient, rather than itself contributing gradients that mix all tokens together. With the detaches the autograd chain gives exactly the desired per-token term $s_i(\theta)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)$. Without detaching you get extra terms coming from $\nabla_\theta s_i(\theta)$ and so per-token advantages get averaged / leaked across tokens instead of weighting each token's own log-probability gradient.

Below are the simple chain-rule steps that make this precise.

1) Gradient produced by the GSPO-token construction (with stop-grad)
Write $\bar s_i \equiv \operatorname{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$ and $\bar\pi_{i,t} \equiv \operatorname{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})]$. Then
$$
s_{i,t}(\theta)=\bar s_i\cdot\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\bar\pi_{i,t}}.
$$
Because $\bar s_i$ and $\bar\pi_{i,t}$ are treated as constants by autograd,
$$
\nabla_\theta s_{i,t}(\theta)
= \bar s_i\cdot\frac{1}{\bar\pi_{i,t}}\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)
= \bar s_i\cdot\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\bar\pi_{i,t}}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot).
$$
Numerically $\bar\pi_{i,t}=\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)$ and $\bar s_i=s_i(\theta)$, so
$$
\nabla_\theta s_{i,t}(\theta)=s_i(\theta)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot).
$$
Multiplying by the per-token advantage $\widehat A_{i,t}$ and averaging over tokens gives exactly the gradient reported in the paper:
$$
\nabla_\theta\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO-token}}
=\mathbb E\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_i s_i(\theta)\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\widehat A_{i,t}\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\right].
$$
So the detach trick enforces that $s_i(\theta)$ is used as a multiplicative weight but does not itself contribute derivative terms.

2) What goes wrong if you use $s_i(\theta)$ without stop-grad
If you naively put the sequence weight $s_i(\theta)$ directly into a tokenized objective, e.g.
$$
\tilde J=\mathbb E\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_i\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_{i,t}\right],
$$
then the gradient flows through $s_i(\theta)$. Using $\nabla_\theta s_i(\theta)=s_i(\theta)\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t'}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t'}\mid\cdot)$ we get
$$
\nabla_\theta\tilde J
=\mathbb E\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_i
s_i(\theta)\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\widehat A_{i,t}\right)
\cdot\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t'}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t'}\mid\cdot)\right)\right].
$$
Two bad things happen: (i) each token's gradient is multiplied by the average advantage across the whole sequence $\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t\widehat A_{i,t}$, so individual token advantages $\widehat A_{i,t}$ are not applied to their own token gradients, and (ii) each token-gradient receives contributions coming from all other tokens through $\sum_{t'}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t'})$ (cross-token leakage). This is exactly the unwanted mixing the detach was designed to avoid.

3) Intuition and why the paper’s form is the right hack
We want the sequence-level importance $s_i(\theta)$ to reweight token-level likelihood gradients, but we do not want the dependence of $s_i(\theta)$ on those same token probabilities to create extra gradient paths that change the credit assignment. The stop-gradient construction yields a numerically identical importance factor (so clipping and objective values behave the same) while forcing autograd to produce the clean per-token term $s_i(\theta)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)$. In short, detach isolates the multiplicative role of $s_i$ from its derivative, preventing averaging/leakage of per-token advantages across the sequence.

This is a standard pattern when you need a quantity to act as a scalar multiplier in the forward pass but do not want gradients to flow back through it.

### Is GSPO unbiased in the on-policy limit, and what are the biases from clipping and groupwise normalization?
Question:
If $\pi_\theta = \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ (on-policy), is the GSPO estimator $\mathbb{E}[s_i(\theta)\hat A_i]$ an unbiased estimator of the policy gradient with a baseline? How do clipping and groupwise $z$-score normalization (subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group std) introduce bias, and under what conditions does that bias vanish?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — in the strict on‑policy limit ($\theta = \theta_\text{old}$), and ignoring clipping and finite‑sample effects from the group normalization, GSPO’s estimator recovers the usual score‑function (REINFORCE) style gradient up to the paper’s explicit length normalization. The two practical sources of bias are (1) clipping and (2) using a sample‑based group z‑score (subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group std). Subtracting the group mean that includes the current sample produces a small multiplicative bias of $(G-1)/G$ (vanishing as group size $G \to \infty$ or if you use a leave‑one‑out mean); dividing by the sample standard deviation introduces a non‑linear finite‑sample bias that also vanishes as $G \to \infty$. Clipping is a non‑linear truncation and therefore generically biases the estimate whenever it is active, but it has no effect exactly at $\theta = \theta_\text{old}$ (since $s_i(\theta_\text{old}) = 1$ and $1$ is inside any reasonable clipping interval).

Derivation and intuition (concise)

1) what GSPO’s gradient is and the on‑policy simplification. The paper shows (without clipping)
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GSPO}}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}
\Big[ \tfrac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i \,\nabla_\theta\log s_i(\theta)\Big],
$$
with
$$
s_i(\theta)=\Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)}\Big)^{1/|y_i|},\qquad
\nabla_\theta\log s_i(\theta)=\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}).
$$
At the on‑policy point $\theta = \theta_\text{old}$ we have $s_i(\theta)=1$, so the gradient simplifies to
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GSPO}}(\theta)\Big|_{\theta=\theta_\text{old}}
= \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_\theta}\Big[ \widehat A(y)\,\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)\Big].
$$
This is exactly the score‑function gradient of the GSPO objective (the paper intentionally uses the length normalization factor $1/|y|$). If you compare to the usual policy gradient form
$$
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y)\,(r(y)-b(x))\big]
$$
you see GSPO matches that form only when you accept the same length normalization (or equivalently rescale the advantage). So GSPO is unbiased for the gradient of its stated objective in the on‑policy limit (i.e., no clipping and treating the advantage as independent baseline).

2) bias from subtracting the group mean. Let $\hat\mu=(1/G)\sum_{j=1}^G r_j$ be the group mean used in $\widehat A_i$ (ignore std for now). Under iid sampling from the on‑policy distribution
$$
\mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_i)\,(r_i-\hat\mu)\big]
= \Big(1-\frac{1}{G}\Big)\,\mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y)\,r(y)\big].
$$
The derivation uses independence of different samples and $\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi]=0$. Thus including the current sample in the mean multiplies the true gradient by $(G-1)/G$. This is a small finite‑sample bias; it disappears as $G \to \infty$. If you instead use a leave‑one‑out group mean (exclude the i‑th sample when computing the mean) the bias is removed exactly.

3) bias from dividing by the group std (z‑scoring). Dividing by the sample standard deviation $\hat\sigma$ is nonlinear and couples numerator and denominator, so you no longer get a simple multiplicative factor. In finite $G$ this produces an additional bias because $\hat\sigma$ is random and correlated with $r_i$. By the law of large numbers, $\hat\sigma \to \sigma$ (population std) as $G \to \infty$, and the z‑score tends to the population standardized advantage $(r-\mu)/\sigma$, so the extra bias vanishes asymptotically. In practice for moderate/large $G$ the std‑division mainly rescales the signal (helps stability) and the bias is small.

4) bias from clipping. Clipping is a nonlinear truncation: replacing $s_i$ by $\mathrm{clip}(s_i,1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)$ changes the estimator whenever $s_i$ falls outside the clip interval, so it generally biases the gradient. However, in the exact on‑policy case $s_i(\theta_\text{old})=1$ and clipping does nothing, so there is no clipping bias exactly at $\theta = \theta_\text{old}$. For small off‑policy deviations only the samples whose sequence ratio crosses the clip thresholds are biased; the bias vanishes as the policy difference $\to 0$ (or as the clip width $\to \infty$).

Practical takeaways

- GSPO is unbiased for the gradient of the GSPO objective in the strict on‑policy, no‑clipping, infinite‑group limit. Because GSPO intentionally uses a length‑normalized log‑ratio, you should compare it to a length‑normalized policy gradient (the paper consistently uses that normalization).

- The dominant finite‑sample bias in practice is the inclusion of the current sample in the group mean (factor $(G-1)/G$). Use large $G$ or a leave‑one‑out mean to remove it. The z‑score denominator introduces smaller finite‑sample bias which vanishes as $G$ grows.

- Clipping trades unbiasedness for variance and stability; it introduces bias whenever active but is harmless exactly at the on‑policy point and is typically essential for off‑policy stability in practice.

These facts are all consistent with the GSPO design choices in the paper: sequence‑level importance weighting and groupwise normalization trade a tiny finite‑sample bias for large variance reduction and stability, and those biases vanish (or become negligible) with large group sizes or in the on‑policy limit.

### Why is a single per-token importance weight considered "invalid" here while PPO commonly uses it?
Question:
The paper argues that using $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}$ at each step is a misapplication of importance sampling because it is based on one sample per next-token distribution and accumulates high variance. But standard PPO in control problems also uses a single action sample per state. What is different in sequence modeling that makes the variance accumulation problematic here? Is the mismatch primarily due to assigning a sequence-level advantage to each token (unit mismatch), sequence length compounding variance, or MoE routing volatility?
Answer:
Short answer: the token-level ratio in GRPO is a misapplication of importance sampling because it tries to do sequence-level off‑policy correction with one noisy sample from each next‑token conditional. That mismatch (a sequence reward being “distributed” across tokens) produces large, compounding variance as length grows; MoE routing volatility then amplifies that instability. In contrast, PPO in control problems typically uses per-step ratios together with genuine per‑step advantages (from a value function / GAE) and shorter/less sparse action conditionals, so the per‑step treatment is a different, valid bookkeeping choice.

Why this matters mathematically and intuitively. Importance sampling for a sequence objective with reward $r(x,y)$ requires the sequence importance weight
$$
\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}
$$
because the identity
$$
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[f(y)] \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y)} f(y)\right]
$$
holds only when the whole sample $y$ is reweighted by the full sequence ratio. GSPO implements this idea (with length normalization), so its gradient (omitting clipping) has the form
$$
\nabla_\theta J_{\text{GSPO}} \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[s_i \,\widehat A_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\right],
$$
where $s_i=\big(\pi_\theta(y_i)/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i)\big)^{1/|y_i|}=\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t\log r_{t}\big)$ is a length‑normalized sequence ratio.

GRPO, by contrast, inserts the single‑sample token ratios $w_{i,t}=\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)$ inside the token sum:
$$
\nabla_\theta J_{\text{GRPO}} \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\widehat A_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} w_{i,t}\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\right].
$$
Those two estimators are fundamentally different. GRPO’s estimator uses one noisy sample from each conditional next‑token distribution to form $w_{i,t}$ and then weights individual token gradients unequally. This is not the same as weighting the whole trajectory by the (length‑normalized) sequence ratio, and it injects extra per‑token noise into every gradient term.

Why variance explodes with tokens/length. Because
$$
\log s_i=\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log r_t,
$$
the length‑normalized sequence log‑ratio is an average of per‑token log‑ratios; by averaging it concentrates as $|y_i|$ grows (roughly $\text{Var}(\log s_i)\propto\text{Var}(\log r_t)/|y_i|$ under mild independence). GSPO therefore benefits from variance reduction via averaging. GRPO, however, uses the raw per‑token ratios $w_{i,t}$ directly inside the sum: the noisy multipliers do not average down but instead add up (and can correlate with the token gradients), so variance (and bias introduced by clipping interactions) compounds with sequence length. The paper points out that this high‑variance token noise, amplified by clipping, empirically leads to catastrophic and often irreversible collapse.

Why PPO in control usually doesn’t fail the same way. In classic control settings PPO ordinarily attaches a per‑step advantage $A_t$ (estimated by a value function and GAE) to the per‑step ratio; the unit of correction (one ratio per action) matches the unit of the advantage (one advantage per time step). That alignment keeps the estimator coherent and low enough variance for practical updates—especially because action spaces are typically much smaller, episodes shorter, and the value model reduces variance. GRPO, in contrast, uses a sequence‑level relative advantage $\widehat A_i$ copied to every token: that is a unit mismatch (sequence reward vs token correction) which breaks the theoretical bookkeeping that importance sampling relies on.

How MoE routing volatility worsens things. In Mixture‑of‑Experts models the activated experts for a given token can change after a small parameter update, so $\pi_\theta(y_{t}\mid\cdot)$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{t}\mid\cdot)$ may come from very different networks; that makes individual token ratios $w_{i,t}$ violently unstable even when the model’s sequence‑level behavior hasn’t meaningfully changed. The paper shows Routing Replay was needed for GRPO to converge on MoE models; GSPO obviates that by depending on the (more stable) sequence likelihood instead of fragile per‑token likelihoods.

Bottom line: it’s not a single cause but a combination. The primary conceptual mistake is the unit mismatch (sequence reward being corrected by per‑token single‑sample weights), which leads to mathematically inappropriate weighting. That error becomes practically disastrous because per‑token noise doesn’t average down but compounds with sequence length; MoE routing volatility then amplifies the token‑level swings. GSPO fixes all three by using a principled sequence‑level importance ratio (with length normalization) and sequence‑level clipping so the importance weights and the reward share the same unit and the variance is much reduced.

### Can you quantify the variance reduction from token-wise ratios to sequence-wise ratios?
Question:
Is there an analysis comparing $\operatorname{Var}\left[\sum_t w_t \nabla\log\pi(y_t\mid\cdot)\right]$ versus $\operatorname{Var}\left[s\,\tfrac{1}{|y|}\sum_t \nabla\log\pi(y_t\mid\cdot)\right]$, where $w_t$ are per-token ratios and $s$ is the sequence-level geometric-mean ratio? Any bounds or empirical measurements showing how variance scales with $|y|$ in GRPO vs GSPO would clarify the claimed stabilization.
Answer:
Short answer: yes — you can quantify the variance reduction analytically under mild simplifying assumptions. Intuitively GSPO replaces a tokenwise arithmetic mixture of multiplicative importance weights by a single geometric-mean weight applied to the averaged token gradients; averaging in the log-domain (the geometric mean) compresses extremes and therefore reduces the second moment of the weight that multiplies the gradient. Below I give a short derivation (from the paper's gradient formulas), a clean toy closed form under Gaussian assumptions, the key takeaway inequality, and how you can check this empirically.

Start from the paper's gradient forms (clipping and the common advantage factor $A$ omitted for clarity). For a single response $y$ (drop the group index) define token gradients $g_t \equiv \nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)$ and token log-ratios $u_t\equiv\log w_t=\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)-\log\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\mid\cdot)$. GRPO uses
$$
G_{\text{GR}}=\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} w_t g_t
\quad\text{with}\quad w_t=e^{u_t},
$$
whereas GSPO uses
$$
G_{\text{GS}}=s\cdot\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} g_t
\quad\text{with}\quad s=\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t} u_t\Big)=e^{\bar u}.
$$

Assume (to get a clean baseline comparison) that the token gradients have zero mean and identical variance $\operatorname{Var}(g_t)=\sigma_g^2$, that token pairs are uncorrelated across positions, and that the $u_t$ are i.i.d. and independent of the $g_t$ (these are the usual simplifying assumptions for first-pass variance analysis; I state caveats below). Then
$$
\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GR}}]
=\frac{1}{|y|^2}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\operatorname{Var}(w_t g_t)
=\frac{\sigma_g^2}{|y|}\,\mathbb{E}[e^{2u}],
$$
while
$$
\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GS}}]
=\operatorname{Var}\!\Big(s\cdot\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_t g_t\Big)
= \mathbb{E}[s^2]\cdot\frac{\sigma_g^2}{|y|}
= \frac{\sigma_g^2}{|y|}\,\mathbb{E}\!\big[e^{2\bar u}\big].
$$
So both leading scalings contain the $1/|y|$ factor coming from averaging the $g_t$, but the multiplicative prefactors differ: GRPO has $\mathbb{E}[e^{2u}]$ while GSPO has $\mathbb{E}[e^{2\bar u}]$ where $\bar u=(1/|y|)\sum_t u_t$.

You can make the comparison explicit via moment-generating (cumulant) functions. Let $K(t)=\log\mathbb{E}[e^{t u}]$ be the log-MGF of $u$. Then
$$
\mathbb{E}[e^{2u}]=e^{K(2)},\qquad
\mathbb{E}[e^{2\bar u}]=e^{\,|y|K(2/|y|)}.
$$
Thus the variance ratio is
$$
\frac{\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GR}}]}{\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GS}}]}
=\frac{e^{K(2)}}{e^{|y|K(2/|y|)}}=e^{\,K(2)-|y|K(2/|y|)}.
$$
For small-to-moderate fluctuations of $u$ (expand $K$ to second order, assuming mean zero for simplicity), $K(t)\approx\frac{\sigma_u^2}{2}t^2$, so
$$
\frac{\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GR}}]}{\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GS}}]}
\approx\exp\!\big(2\sigma_u^2(1-\tfrac{1}{|y|})\big).
$$
For large sequence length $|y|$ this ratio approaches $\exp(2\sigma_u^2)$. In a Gaussian toy model ($u_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_u^2)$, independent of $g_t$) this approximation is exact for the second moments and therefore produces the closed forms
$$
\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GR}}]\approx\frac{\sigma_g^2}{|y|}e^{2\sigma_u^2},\qquad
\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GS}}]\approx\frac{\sigma_g^2}{|y|}e^{2\sigma_u^2/|y|}.
$$
Numerically this can be large: if $\sigma_u=1$ then $\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GR}}]/\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GS}}]\approx e^{2}\approx 7.4$; if $\sigma_u=1.5$ the ratio is $\approx e^{4.5}\approx 90$.

The intuition is simple and matches the paper's qualitative claim: GRPO effectively averages the exponentials $w_t=e^{u_t}$ (an arithmetic mean of exponentials), while GSPO exponentiates the mean log-ratio (the geometric mean). Because the exponential is convex, arithmetic averaging of exponentials has a larger second moment than exponentiating the arithmetic mean of logs; formally (under independence) H ölder / Jensen-type inequalities give $\mathbb{E}[e^{2\bar u}]\le \mathbb{E}[e^{2u}]$, so the GSPO prefactor is always no larger than the GRPO prefactor in this i.i.d. setting. Put another way: averaging in the log-domain compresses multiplicative noise.

A few important caveats and extensions relevant to practice. First, the derivation above ignores clipping, correlations across token positions, and dependence between $u_t$ and $g_t$. Positive correlations among the $u_t$ (e.g. MoE routing changes that shift many token likelihoods together) increase the covariance terms in $\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GR}}]$ and can make GRPO even worse than the i.i.d. calculation suggests; GSPO is usually more robust to such intra-sequence correlations because it collapses those correlated fluctuations into the single scalar $\bar u$. Second, heavy tails in the $u_t$ distribution blow up $\mathbb{E}[e^{2u}]$ and so penalize GRPO much more severely than GSPO. Third, clipping interacts with the variance/bias tradeoff: token-level clipping can remove wildly-weighted tokens but at the cost of inconsistent token-level bias, whereas sequence-level clipping (GSPO) excludes whole sequences and better aligns with a sequence reward; the paper reports empirically that GSPO actually clips a much larger fraction of individual tokens but still trains better (their Figure on clipping fractions), which is consistent with the above variance rationale.

If you want empirical confirmation on your model, a straightforward experiment is to fix $\theta$ and $\theta_{\text{old}}$, sample many responses $y$ from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ at different lengths, compute the two sequence-level gradient surrogates
$$
\hat G_{\text{GR}}(y)=\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_t w_t g_t,\qquad
\hat G_{\text{GS}}(y)=s\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_t g_t,
$$
project them onto a fixed random direction (or take squared norms) and estimate sample variances across the draws as a function of $|y|$. Also compute $\operatorname{Var}(\log w_t)$ across the same samples; plugging that empirical $\sigma_u^2$ into the Gaussian toy formula above gives a rough prediction for the variance ratio to compare with measurements. The paper shows stability and clipping-fraction empirical evidence (their training curves and clipping-figure) but does not publish raw gradient-variance numbers; the procedure above will give you direct confirmation and a quantitative match (or not) to the simple theory.

Takeaway: under the realistic condition that per-token log-importance ratios fluctuate (and especially if they have nontrivial variance or are correlated), GSPO’s sequence-level geometric-mean weighting substantially reduces the multiplicative second moment of the importance weight and therefore reduces gradient variance relative to GRPO. In simple i.i.d. models the reduction in variance is approximately multiplicative by $\exp(2\operatorname{Var}(\log w_t))$ (for large $|y|$), and in more realistic correlated/heavy-tailed regimes GSPO is typically even more robust — which is precisely the stabilization effect the paper documents empirically.

### What keeps the sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$ from becoming extreme on long sequences?
Question:
Even with the $1/|y|$ exponent, if a few tokens have large log-ratio deviations, could $s_i(\theta) = \exp\left(\tfrac{1}{|y|}\sum_t \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\right)$ still become very small or large? How do length normalization and sequence-level clipping interact to bound the effective weight, and is there a recommended maximum sequence length or per-update KL target to keep $s_i$ in a safe range?
Answer:
Short answer: two built‑in devices in GSPO keep the sequence weight from running away on long responses: (1) length normalization turns the sequence ratio into a per‑token geometric mean, so a small number of token outliers is averaged down by $1/|y|$; and (2) sequence‑level clipping literally bounds the multiplier that enters the objective so the *effective* weight used for the update always lies in a tiny interval around 1. Together these make extreme effective updates unlikely in practice; the paper’s experiments use very tight GSPO clipping (left/right = $3\!\times\!10^{-4}$ / $4\!\times\!10^{-4}$) as a concrete reference.

A bit more detail and intuition. GSPO defines
$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}\Big).
$$
If only $k$ tokens are large outliers with per‑token log‑ratio $L$ and the rest are near zero, the average inside the exponential is $kL/|y_i|$, so
$$
s_i\approx\exp\!\Big(\frac{kL}{|y_i|}\Big).
$$
Thus as $|y_i|$ grows a fixed small number $k$ of outliers is diluted and $s_i\to 1$; conversely, $s_i$ becomes large only if either (a) many tokens (a non‑negligible fraction) systematically change, or (b) per‑token log‑ratios themselves are huge. That latter case represents a genuine, large policy shift (not a mere artifact of sequence length) and is something we want to limit anyway.

Crucially, GSPO then clips $s_i$ in the objective:
$$
\min\big(s_i\widehat A_i,\ \operatorname{clip}(s_i,1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i\big).
$$
Because of the $\min(\cdot)$ structure, the *effective* multiplier that actually scales the advantage is always inside $[1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]$: for $\widehat A_i>0$ the term can never exceed $(1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i$, and for $\widehat A_i<0$ it can never go below $(1-\varepsilon)\widehat A_i$. In short, even if the raw $s_i$ computed from likelihoods is very small or very large, clipping forces the update to use a bounded weight. The paper emphasizes this: length normalization keeps raw $s_i$ in a unified numerical range, and sequence‑level clipping bounds the *used* weight.

Practical implications and recommendations consistent with the paper. There is no hard “maximum sequence length” or per‑update KL target given in the text. Instead the recipe is: (1) use the length‑normalized $s_i$ so clipping can be length‑agnostic, (2) choose a clipping $\varepsilon$ appropriate to the scale of $s_i$ (GSPO’s clip numbers are orders of magnitude smaller than typical token‑level PPO clips because $s_i$ is near 1), and (3) monitor the fraction of clipped responses and the sequence‑level KL or policy drift. If you see many responses being clipped or sudden rises in sequence‑level KL, reduce the step size / tighten clipping (or introduce KL regularization). The authors report stable training with GSPO while using clipping of $3\times10^{-4}$ / $4\times10^{-4}$ in their runs, and they explicitly note GSPO remains stable as they increase generation length — which supports the view that no fixed max length is needed so long as you use length normalization plus an appropriately tight clip and monitor clipping/KL during tuning.

Summary: a few extreme token ratios get averaged away by the $1/|y|$ exponent for long sequences, and the clipping operation guarantees the effective weight used in the objective is bounded to $[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]$. Only a systematic, large shift across a substantial fraction of a sequence can drive the raw $s_i$ far from 1, and that kind of change is exactly what clipping/step‑size control or an explicit KL trust‑region is meant to prevent.

### What exact objective does GSPO's clipped surrogate lower-bound or approximate?
Question:
PPO’s clipped surrogate is often motivated as a lower bound on a KL-constrained improvement. Does GSPO’s sequence-level clipping yield a similar monotonic improvement guarantee or a lower bound on an objective like $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[A(x,y)]$ under a trust-region constraint on $\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)$ at the sequence level? If so, could you outline the bound and assumptions; if not, how should we interpret the surrogate theoretically?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not derive a formal monotonic-improvement / lower-bound theorem for GSPO’s clipped surrogate analogous to TRPO/PPO for the full sequence objective. GSPO’s clipped objective is a practically motivated, length‑normalized, sequence‑level surrogate that trades unbiasedness for much lower variance and a per‑token trust‑region — it can be seen as an approximate, conservative proxy for a sequence-level PPO surrogate, and it recovers the usual PPO/TRPO picture only in limiting cases (e.g. $|y|=1$ or infinitesimal updates).

Why: write down the two quantities and compare them. The sequence objective you would like to improve is the expected sequence advantage under the current policy,
$$
J(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[A(x,y)]
\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\!\Big[\,w_{\text{seq}}(y;\theta)\,A(x,y)\Big],
$$
where the exact importance weight is the full sequence ratio
$$
w_{\text{seq}}(y;\theta)\;=\;\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}.
$$
GSPO instead uses the length‑normalized geometric mean
$$
s(y;\theta)\;=\;\big(w_{\text{seq}}(y;\theta)\big)^{1/|y|}
=\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\mid\cdot)}\Big)
$$
and optimizes the clipped surrogate (writing a single-sample form)
$$
L_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\Big[\min\!\big(s(y;\theta)\,\widehat A(y),\,
\mathrm{clip}(s(y;\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat A(y)\big)\Big].
$$

Two simple but decisive observations follow from the paper’s equations (they give the gradient in closed form). First, the gradient of the true sequence objective (pulling the importance ratio inside) is
$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\Big[w_{\text{seq}}(y;\theta)\,A(x,y)\!\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)\Big],
$$
whereas GSPO’s (unclipped) gradient is
$$
\nabla_\theta L_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\Big[s(y;\theta)\,A(x,y)\,\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)\Big],
$$
These are not the same: GSPO replaces the full sequence weight $w_{\text{seq}}$ by the length‑normalized $s$ and also inserts the $1/|y|$ averaging. Therefore GSPO is a biased surrogate for $J(\theta)$ (but purposely so, to reduce variance and stabilize updates).

When do PPO/TRPO‑style guarantees carry over? Two limiting cases give you familiar behavior. If every response has length $|y|=1$ (so sequences are single-step), then $s=w_{\text{seq}}$ and GSPO reduces exactly to a PPO‑style clipped sequence surrogate; the standard TRPO/PPO local improvement analyses then apply (subject to their assumptions: bounded advantage, small KL, etc.). More generally, when policy changes are infinitesimal (all per‑token log‑ratios small), one may Taylor‑expand
$$
w_{\text{seq}}\approx 1+\Delta,\qquad s\approx 1+\frac{\Delta}{|y|},
$$
so gradients are approximately aligned and the GSPO update behaves like a first‑order, local trust‑region step. In that small‑change regime GSPO is approximately optimizing the same local surrogate that TRPO analyzes, so you get approximate local improvement guarantees.

How to interpret the clipping/constraint intuitively. Clipping $s$ in $[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]$ enforces a bound on the average per‑token log‑ratio:
$$
\log s(y)=\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\mid\cdot)},
$$
so clipping limits the average per‑token log‑change to about $\log(1+\varepsilon)$. This is naturally a per‑token trust region: it controls typical per‑step deviations rather than the (potentially huge) full sequence ratio. The sequence ratio itself is then bounded by
$$
w_{\text{seq}}\in\big[(1-\varepsilon)^{|y|},(1+\varepsilon)^{|y|}\big],
$$
so the guarantee on full sequence change degrades with sequence length unless $\varepsilon$ is scaled accordingly. This explains why GSPO is designed to normalize by length: it trades some bias in the importance weight for vastly reduced variance and more stable updates on long responses and in MoE settings.

Bottom line and practical takeaway. GSPO’s clipped surrogate is best read as a conservative, variance‑reducing proxy that enforces a per‑token trust region and aligns optimization unit (sequence) with reward. The paper presents empirical and gradient‑level justification for this choice but does not prove a rigorous monotonic improvement theorem for the GSPO surrogate applied to the true sequence objective. If you need a formal lower bound like TRPO’s, you must either (a) work with the full sequence ratio $w_{\text{seq}}$ and apply sequence‑level clipping or an explicit sequence KL penalty (which recovers the standard TRPO/PPO analysis), or (b) make small‑change / short‑sequence assumptions under which the GSPO surrogate is a good local approximation and invoke the usual local improvement arguments. In practice the paper’s point is that the bias introduced by using $s$ and grouping/normalizing advantages is an acceptable tradeoff for the strong empirical stability gains on long sequences and large, sparse models.

### How does groupwise $z$-scoring of rewards interact with off-policy correction?
Question:
Each response’s advantage is $\hat A_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \operatorname{mean}(\{r\})}{\operatorname{std}(\{r\})}$ computed within the group sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. Since this baseline and scale depend on the sampled group, does this introduce bias in the off-policy estimator with importance weights $s_i(\theta)$? Are there conditions (e.g., large group size $G$) under which the bias becomes negligible?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — computing advantages by z‑scoring inside each sampled group (i.e. using the group mean and group std) is a sample‑dependent transformation and it does introduce a finite‑sample bias into an importance‑weighted off‑policy estimator. The bias is controlled by the group size and by how the importance weight correlates with rewards; for the common “subtract the group mean” part you can write the bias in closed form and see it vanishes as $G$ grows, and a simple leave‑one‑out baseline removes that mean‑induced bias exactly. Division by the sample standard deviation (z‑scoring) introduces extra, higher‑order coupling (nonlinear) bias, but it also shrinks as the group size grows.

Explanation and key formula. Fix a query $x$ and write $r_i = r(x,y_i)$ and $s_i = s_i(\theta)$ for the importance/weight associated to sample $i$. Let the group mean be $\bar r = (1/G)\sum_{j} r_j$ and denote expectations under the behaviour sample distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ by $E[\cdot]$. Define
$$
\mu := E[r], \quad m_s := E[s],\quad m_{sr} := E[s\,r].
$$
A straight expectation computation (using iid sampling so off‑diagonal factors factorize) gives the exact identity for the mean‑subtracted estimator
$$
E\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i\,(r_i-\bar r)\right]
=\frac{G-1}{G}\,\bigl(m_{sr}-m_s\,\mu\bigr).
$$
Two immediate special cases are illuminating. If $s_i$ is the true sequence importance weight $w_i=\pi_\theta(y_i)/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i)$ then $m_s=1$ and $m_{sr}=E_{\pi_\theta}[r]$, so the expectation becomes
$$
\frac{G-1}{G}\bigl(E_{\pi_\theta}[r]-E_{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}[r]\bigr),
$$
i.e. a multiplicative factor $(G-1)/G$ on the quantity you would get with a population baseline. If instead $s$ and $r$ are uncorrelated under the behaviour distribution ($m_{sr}=m_s\mu$) the expectation is zero. The important point is the $(G-1)/G$ factor: for finite $G$ you get a bias of order $1/G$, and it disappears as $G\to\infty$.

Leave‑one‑out fix for the mean. If you use the leave‑one‑out mean baseline
$$
\bar r_{-i} = \frac{1}{G-1}\sum_{j\ne i} r_j
$$
and form advantages $r_i-\bar r_{-i}$, the analogous expectation becomes exactly $m_{sr}-m_s\mu$ (no $(G-1)/G$ prefactor). In particular when $s$ is the true importance ratio this removes the finite‑$G$ bias from the mean subtraction step exactly. So a simple practical option to eliminate the mean‑induced bias is to use leave‑one‑out centering.

What about division by sample standard deviation (z‑scoring)? Dividing by the group std couples all samples nonlinearly. A short heuristic with Taylor (delta‑method) expansion shows the extra bias introduced by replacing the population std $\sigma$ by the sample std $\hat\sigma$ is higher order in $1/G$ (the sample std has expectation error $O(1/G)$ when variance is estimated with denominator $G$; fluctuations are $O(1/\sqrt{G})$ but expectations of the nonlinear terms yield $O(1/G)$ corrections). In plain words: the standardization step adds more bias than mean subtraction, but that bias also shrinks with increasing $G$. Using leave‑one‑out mean and leave‑one‑out std (compute $\bar r_{-i},\sigma_{-i}$ excluding sample $i$) reduces the coupling and therefore reduces bias further, though the std still introduces a small nonlinear residual bias (std is biased as an estimator of the population $\sigma$ and the reciprocal is nonlinear).

Practical implications for GSPO (and PPO‑style surrogates). Two points matter for practice. First, GSPO already uses a surrogate (clipped) objective and a length‑normalized sequence weight $s_i(\theta)$ (not the plain un‑normalized importance weight); both design choices already make the training objective an approximation (i.e. biased relative to exact IS). Second, group normalization (z‑scoring) is a strong variance reducer and stabilizer in practice — the paper’s empirical claim is exactly that groupwise advantages give much more stable RL for large LMs/MoE. So the tradeoff is bias vs variance/stability: a small, controllable bias (decaying like $1/G$) is often an acceptable price for much lower variance and far better training behaviour.

Concrete recommendations
use leave‑one‑out centering (and, if you want, leave‑one‑out std) if you care about removing the mean‑induced finite‑$G$ bias; add a small epsilon to the std to avoid blowups when rewards are nearly identical; increase group size $G$ when possible (bias scales like $O(1/G)$); remember that clipping and length‑normalization of $s_i$ are already sources of approximation/bias, so small group‑normalization bias is typically negligible compared with those effects; finally, if you need an unbiased IS estimator for diagnostics (not optimization), compute expectations with true importance weights and population statistics or use very large $G$ / multiple independent groups.

Summary. Groupwise z‑scoring is not free: it introduces a finite‑sample bias because the baseline and scale are themselves random and correlated with the importance weights. For mean subtraction the bias has the simple closed form above and decays like $O(1/G)$, and leave‑one‑out centering removes that piece exactly. The std‑division step adds further (nonlinear) bias but it too shrinks as $G$ grows. In practice GSPO accepts these small approximations in exchange for much lower variance and markedly better stability.

### Why can clipping a larger fraction of tokens still yield higher training efficiency in GSPO?
Question:
The paper reports that GSPO clips many more tokens (because entire responses are clipped) yet achieves better sample efficiency than GRPO. Intuitively, clipping discards gradient information. Can you reconcile this with PPO theory and explain why the token-level gradients in GRPO are so noisy that discarding more of them (via sequence-level clipping) can improve the signal-to-noise ratio and overall efficiency?
Answer:
Short answer: because GSPO trades quantity for quality. GRPO keeps many token-level gradient contributions but those per-token importance weights are intrinsically high‑variance (and often invalid as IS corrections), so the net gradient is noisy and can even destabilize learning. GSPO computes a single, length‑normalized sequence importance ratio (the geometric mean of per‑token ratios), clips whole sequences, and hence throws away many noisy sequences (and thus many tokens) but keeps a much lower‑variance, higher‑signal gradient from the sequences that remain. The resulting increase in signal‑to‑noise per used token yields better sample efficiency and stability.

Why this is true (intuition + math grounded in the paper). The paper gives the unclipped gradients (omitting expectation notation) for GSPO and GRPO:

GSPO:
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GSPO}}
= \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i\;\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
$$
where
$$
s_i(\theta)=\Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_i\mid x)}\Big)^{1/|y_i|}
= \exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log w_{i,t}\Big).
$$

GRPO:
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}
= \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \widehat A_i\;\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
$$
with token ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}$.

Two points follow directly.

1) Averaging in log-space reduces variance. GSPO’s $s_i$ is the geometric mean of the per‑token ratios (length‑normalized). If the random variables $\log w_{i,t}$ have variance $\sigma^2$, then by the usual averaging/CLT logic
$$
\operatorname{Var}\Big(\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t}\log w_{t}\Big) = \frac{\sigma^2}{|y|},
$$
so the variance of $\log s_i$ shrinks like $1/|y|$. By the delta method this makes the variance of $s_i$ much smaller than the variance of individual $w_{i,t}$. In short, sequence‑level weighting pools information across tokens and produces a far less volatile importance weight than per‑token ratios. Lower variance in the multiplier of the gradient directly improves signal‑to‑noise of the estimated policy gradient.

2) Token‑level ratios are single‑sample, high‑variance corrections and interact badly with clipping. Importance sampling is effective when the weight corrects an expectation by averaging over many samples; GRPO’s $w_{i,t}$ is based on one sample from each next‑token conditional, so it is a noisy estimator of any conditional correction. GRPO then multiplies each token’s gradient by its own noisy weight (and clips them separately). Because the advantage in GRPO is shared across a whole response, unequal, noisy per‑token weights create heteroskedastic, possibly sign‑conflicting contributions inside the same sequence. Clipping at token granularity can make this worse: different tokens inside a single sequence get clipped differently, skewing the net direction and amplifying variance. GSPO avoids this by applying one (smoothed) weight to the whole token sum, and by clipping whole responses: if a sequence’s averaged weight is too far off, discard that whole (high‑variance) sample rather than mixing many noisy partial contributions.

Why clipping more tokens can still be better. GSPO’s sequence clipping will reject entire responses whose sequence importance $s_i$ lies outside the clip window. That naturally removes many tokens at once, so the raw fraction of clipped tokens can be much larger than GRPO’s per‑token clipping fraction. But those discarded tokens would have been the biggest contributors to gradient variance (and potential instability) under GRPO. By throwing them away GSPO keeps fewer but much cleaner gradient terms; the per‑update variance drops and the remaining signal points in a more reliable direction. The bias introduced by clipping is the usual PPO bias/variance tradeoff, and here the variance reduction dominates — yielding better sample efficiency and stability (especially visible for long responses and for MoE models where per‑token likelihoods fluctuate due to routing volatility).

Finally, this explains the practical MoE gain the paper observes: routing/expert‑activation volatility makes token‑level likelihoods jump, invalidating $w_{i,t}$ or forcing expensive “Routing Replay.” Sequence‑level likelihoods (length‑normalized) are much less sensitive, so GSPO both avoids the noisy per‑token corrections and removes the need for routing replay, improving efficiency and stability.

In short: GSPO discards more tokens but the tokens it keeps produce a much higher signal‑to‑noise policy gradient because sequence‑level (geometric mean) importance weights dramatically reduce per‑sample variance and avoid the pathological effects of noisy, per‑token importance ratios.

### Mechanism of MoE expert-activation volatility causing unstable token ratios
Question:
You report that about $10\%$ of experts for the same sample change after each update in a 48-layer MoE model, which destabilizes token-level ratios $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\mid\cdot)}$. Could you provide a concrete example or simplified model showing how a change in top-2 gating for one token can make $w_t$ spike, and why aggregating to a sequence-level ratio $s_i$ (geometric mean across tokens) is robust to such routing changes?
Answer:
Short answer: a single token can see a huge multiplicative jump in its token-level importance ratio $w_t=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\mid\cdot)}$ whenever the discrete top-k routing swaps one expert for another that produces very different outputs; but the sequence-level ratio
$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log w_t\Big)
$$
is the geometric mean of per-token ratios, so a single (or small fraction of) spikes in $\log w_t$ are averaged down by the factor $1/|y_i|$. Below I give a tiny concrete toy that makes this precise and explain why the averaging gives robustness in practice.

Toy MoE + softmax example (concrete numbers). Consider a single next-token softmax with two outcomes (so $p=\sigma(z)$ using a scalar logit $z$), and let the logit be produced from an MoE layer followed by a linear head, i.e. $z=v^\top h$ and the MoE output is the (top-2) gated mixture of expert outputs. Suppose for one particular token the old routing produced a hidden value $h_\text{old}=-4$ (because the chosen mixture of experts gave a negative output) and after a small parameter update the top-2 routing changed and the new mixture gives $h_\text{new}=+4$. With $v=1$ we get
$$
p_\text{old}=\frac{e^{-4}}{1+e^{-4}}\approx 0.018,\qquad
p_\text{new}=\frac{e^{4}}{1+e^{4}}\approx 0.982,
$$
and therefore the token-level ratio is
$$
w_t=\frac{p_\text{new}}{p_\text{old}}\approx \frac{0.982}{0.018}\approx 54.6.
$$
A single discrete swap of the second expert (or a swap of a top-2 member) can easily create this sort of jump because experts implement very different transforms and top-k routing is a discrete decision: a tiny change in gating scores can change which expert appears in the top-2 and thereby replace one expert’s contribution by another’s.

Why the geometric mean / sequence-level ratio damps the spike. Take a long response of length $|y|=100$. If only one token experiences that extreme swap (all other tokens have $w_t\approx 1$), then
$$
\log s_i=\frac{1}{100}\sum_{t}\log w_t=\frac{1}{100}\log 54.6\approx \frac{4.0}{100}=0.04,
$$
so $s_i\approx e^{0.04}\approx 1.04$. Even though one token had a 55x change, the per-sequence, length-normalized ratio moved only a few percent. If instead 10 tokens ($10\%$) spiked with the same $w_t$, then $\log s_i\approx 10\cdot4/100=0.4$ and $s_i\approx e^{0.4}\approx1.49$ — still far smaller than a 50x jump on a single token. In other words, $\log s_i$ is the average of the token log-ratios, so its variance shrinks roughly by $|y|$ (under an independence-like intuition); length-normalization thus strongly compresses single-token or small-fraction spikes.

Intuition about why top-2 routing causes discontinuities. The gating network ranks experts and the final choice of the top-k experts is a combinatorial (discrete) selection: when two experts’ gate scores cross due to a parameter update, one expert is replaced by another for that token. Because each expert has different parameters (different linear maps / nonlinearities), swapping an expert can change the hidden state $h$ by an $O(1)$ amount and therefore shift logits by $O(1)$ (or larger) — this is exactly the cause of the $w_t$ spike. In deep MoE stacks these switches can occur at many layers and tokens; the paper reports roughly $10\%$ of activated experts change for the same sample after an update in a deep (48-layer) model, so per-token $w_t$ can be wildly variable across updates.

Why GSPO (sequence-level geometric mean) stabilizes training compared to token-level weights. GRPO multiplies token gradients by the (potentially huge and highly varying) per-token $w_{i,t}$, so a single routing-induced spike can overweight one token’s gradient, push parameters hard, and interact badly with clipping and off-policy noise. GSPO instead uses $s_i$ to scale the whole-response gradient uniformly (and $s_i$ is length-normalized), so an isolated routing change cannot concentrate the update on a single token: its contribution is diluted by the sequence length. Formally, because
$$
\log s_i=\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_t \log w_t,
$$
extreme individual $\log w_t$ values are averaged, which reduces variance and makes clipping ranges much tighter in practice (this is why the paper can use much smaller clipping windows for $s_i$ than GRPO’s token-level clipping). That averaging is what removes the need for heavy engineering workarounds like Routing Replay: you no longer need to force the new policy to use the old routing to get stable per-token ratios.

Takeaway. A discrete expert swap can easily make a token-level importance ratio $w_t$ explode (my toy showed a ~55x effect), but because GSPO defines the importance weight as the geometric mean across tokens (length-normalized sequence likelihood), those spikes are averaged down: a few bad tokens give only a small change to $s_i$. This averaging is the mechanism behind the empirical observation that GSPO is robust to the MoE “expert-activation volatility” the paper reports, and it explains why GSPO removes the need for Routing Replay while GRPO remains sensitive to per-token routing flips.

### Why is sequence likelihood more tolerant to precision differences between engines?
Question:
The paper suggests GSPO can directly use sequence likelihoods from the inference engine (potentially with different precision) without recomputing in the training engine. Since $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ is a product of per-token probabilities, wouldn’t small per-token numerical discrepancies compound multiplicatively? Why is the sequence-level use in GSPO still robust, and is this mainly because the ratio is exponentiated by $1/|y|$ (geometric mean) and then tightly clipped?
Answer:
Short answer: the multiplicative danger you worry about is real if you keep the importance weight as a raw product of per-token ratios, but GSPO avoids that danger by working in log space and using the geometric mean. Converting a product into an average of log-errors makes per-token rounding/precision noise add up and then get divided by the sequence length, so random small token-level errors largely cancel out (variance shrinks with sequence length). Tight sequence-level clipping then bounds the remaining deviations. In other words, the $1/|y|$ exponent (geometric mean) is the main stabilizer and clipping is the safety net.

Why, mathematically. GSPO uses
$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid \cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{i,t}\mid \cdot)}\right).
$$
If the inference engine injects small per-token log‑errors (write $ \tilde\ell_t(\theta)=\ell_t(\theta)+\delta_t^\theta$, where $\ell_t=\log\pi_\theta(y_{t}\mid\cdot)$ and $\delta_t$ is the precision error), then the error in the estimated sequence log‑ratio is
$$
\log\tilde s_i-\log s_i=\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\big(\delta_t^\theta-\delta_t^{\theta_{\rm old}}\big).
$$
So the log‑error on the sequence importance weight is the average of tokenwise errors, not their sum. If the token errors are roughly unbiased and not strongly correlated, the central limit theorem gives that the standard deviation of this average scales like $1/\sqrt{|y_i|}$. Concretely, small independent per-token rounding noise of size $\sigma$ produces a typical multiplicative error on $s_i$ of order $\exp(O(\sigma/\sqrt{|y_i|}))\approx 1+O(\sigma/\sqrt{|y_i|})$, which is tiny for modest lengths.

Contrast that with token-level weighting: GRPO uses token ratios $w_{t}(\theta)$ directly, so each per-token error survives without averaging and can produce high variance (and these per-token weights are applied unevenly across the sequence). That is the core reason sequence-level GSPO is much more tolerant to small precision differences.

Role of clipping and grouping. Length normalization (geometric mean) is the primary variance reducer; clipping is still crucial because it removes rare but large deviations (e.g., sequences where precision + model differences conspire to give a large bias). GSPO’s sequence clipping therefore can be set quite tight (the paper reports much smaller clipping ranges than GRPO) because $s_i$ already lives close to 1; the clipping then prevents the few sequences with large numerical or modeling discrepancies from corrupting the gradient. The group advantage normalization used by GSPO further reduces sensitivity to a common multiplicative bias across all sequences in a group.

Caveats. If the inference engine produces large, strongly correlated biases across tokens (for example very aggressive quantization or an architecture mismatch that systematically changes logits), the average may not shrink to zero and $s_i$ can be biased; in that case you should verify the distribution of $\log s_i$ between engines and, if necessary, recompute likelihoods on the training engine or relax clip settings. For short sequences the $1/\sqrt{|y|}$ benefit is weaker, so the tolerance is reduced; empirically GSPO’s behavior and the tiny clipping ranges reported in the paper indicate that for typical LLM rollouts the averaging + clipping strategy is enough to tolerate inference/training precision differences.

Takeaway: yes—the geometric mean (the $1/|y|$ exponent) is the main reason GSPO is tolerant to per-token precision differences, and tight sequence-level clipping (plus group normalization and operating in log space) completes the robustness story.

### How do you handle near-zero reward variance within a group (division by zero in $\hat A$)?
Question:
When rewards $\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G$ are nearly identical, $\operatorname{std}(\{r\})$ can be very small. What is the practical strategy in GSPO for this case (e.g., add $\epsilon$ to the denominator, skip the batch, or fall back to a different baseline), and how sensitive is training to this choice?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper defines the per‑group z‑score $\widehat A_i=(r_i-\bar r)/\mathrm{std}(\{r\})$ but does not spell out the numerical guard for near‑zero variance; in practice you must make the normalization numerically safe (or fall back to a no‑signal behavior). The usual, robust implementation is to replace the denominator by a clipped/guarded value and/or use an alternative normalization (batch/run‑level or rank normalization). With reasonable choices (a very small epsilon, clipping of advantages, or a running std) training is not very sensitive; with a too‑large epsilon or no clipping you can either destroy signal or generate huge, unstable gradients.

Why this matters: from the GSPO gradient (omitting clipping) we have roughly
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}\propto \mathbb{E}\big[ s_i\,\widehat A_i\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_i|x)\big],
$$
so any blow‑up in $\widehat A_i$ (division by a tiny std) multiplies the gradient magnitude directly and can destabilize updates even if the importance ratios $s_i$ are clipped.

Practical, safe recipes anchored to the paper’s design
In descending order of simplicity and robustness, implement one of these:

1) Numerically‑stable z‑score (recommended minimal change). Compute $\bar r$ and $\sigma=\mathrm{std}(\{r\})$ and use
$$
\widehat A_i = \frac{r_i-\bar r}{\max(\sigma,\epsilon)} .
$$
Because rewards in the paper are in $[0,1]$, a practical default is $\epsilon$ in the $10^{-4}$–$10^{-3}$ range; for very small groups you may use $\epsilon\!\approx\!10^{-3}$. Also clip advantages to a reasonable range, e.g. $|\widehat A_i|\leq A_{\max}$ with $A_{\max}\in[5,20]$, to prevent occasional huge updates.

2) Detect exact (or effectively) zero variance and treat as no signal. If $\sigma<\epsilon_{\text{zero}}$ (for example $\epsilon_{\text{zero}}=10^{-9}$ or simply check exact equality), set $\widehat A_i=0$ for that group. Mathematically if $\sigma=0$ then all rewards are identical and there is no within‑group ranking information, so setting the advantage to zero is principled (it yields no update from that group).

3) Use a larger statistical pool instead of the tiny group to get a stable denominator. If your rollout batch is partitioned into many groups, compute mean/std across the whole mini‑batch (or use a running exponential average of std across recent batches) and divide by $\max(\sigma_{\text{group}},\alpha\sigma_{\text{batch}},\epsilon)$. This preserves small signals while avoiding division by near‑zero numbers.

4) Rank (order) normalization as a robust alternative. Replace the z‑score by a centered rank inside the group:
$$
\widehat A_i^{\text{rank}}=\frac{\operatorname{rank}(r_i)-\frac{G+1}{2}}{G}
$$
(with average ties). This gives bounded, stable advantages in about $[-0.5,0.5]$ and is insensitive to tiny variance; the downside is loss of magnitude information (you only keep ordering).

Sensitivity guidance
Training is not hyper‑fragile to the exact small epsilon if you pick a value that is orders of magnitude below the typical group std. If $\epsilon$ is too small you can get rare huge advantages and instability; if $\epsilon$ is too large you dampen the learning signal and slow convergence. Clipping $\widehat A_i$ and monitoring the fraction of groups that hit the epsilon/fallback path are good safeguards. If you see many groups with near‑zero variance, consider increasing group size $G$, aggregating statistics across groups, or refining the reward function (quantization of the reward is often the root cause).

A compact recommended implementation for GSPO: compute per‑group mean and std, then
$$
\widehat A_i = \frac{r_i-\bar r}{\max(\sigma, \epsilon)},\qquad \widehat A_i\leftarrow\mathrm{clip}(\widehat A_i,-A_{\max},A_{\max}),
$$
with $\epsilon\sim 10^{-4}$ (for rewards in $[0,1]$) and $A_{\max}\sim 10$. If $\sigma$ is exactly zero, set $\widehat A_i=0$. Log how often you hit the fallback and tune (or switch to batch/run‑level std or rank normalization) if it happens frequently.

This approach is fully compatible with GSPO’s sequence‑level design and keeps gradients well‑behaved without introducing a heavy additional baseline (which GSPO was designed to avoid).

### Where does KL regularization fit into GSPO and how does it interact with clipping?
Question:
The paper omits the KL term for brevity. In practice, do you add a KL penalty to a reference policy or to $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$? How does a sequence-level KL penalty change the effective calibration of the sequence-level clipping range $\varepsilon$, and is there a recommended way to tune KL coefficients jointly with GSPO’s clipping?
Answer:
Short answer: KL regularization is orthogonal to GSPO’s sequence-level clipping and is usually added as an extra (sequence‑level, length‑normalized) penalty term. You can penalize divergence either to the previous iterate (soft trust region) or to a fixed reference (e.g., the SFT/pretrained policy). Conceptually the KL controls average drift (a mean‑norm constraint) while GSPO’s clipping controls worst‑case per‑sequence shifts (a max‑norm constraint); they therefore complement each other and should be tuned together by monitoring the per‑sequence log‑ratio and the fraction of clipped examples.

How to add it (natural, implementation‑friendly form)
Define the per‑sequence, length‑normalized average log‑ratio that GSPO already uses:
$$
\ell_i(\theta)
= \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
$$
so that for the special case $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}=\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ we have $\ell_i(\theta)=\log s_i(\theta)$. A convenient surrogate KL (per token) computed on rollouts from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ is
$$
\widehat{KL}_{\mathrm{token}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\left[\ell_i(\theta)\right].
$$
You can add this to the GSPO maximization as a penalty:
$$
\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GSPO}}(\theta)\;-\;\beta\;\widehat{KL}_{\mathrm{token}}(\theta),
$$
where $\beta>0$ is the KL coefficient. In practice we compute $\widehat{KL}_{\mathrm{token}}$ on the same batch of rollouts (so the expectation is taken over $y\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$), which is a biased but standard and practical surrogate.

Which reference to use
If you want a per‑update trust‑region (soft version of clipping), set $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}=\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. If you want to anchor the fine‑tuned policy to the pretrained / SFT distribution (to avoid long‑term drift or reward‑hacking), set $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ to that fixed reference. The former is redundant with clipping (it softens each update); the latter is complementary (it restrains accumulated drift across many updates).

Intuition for interaction with clipping
GSPO’s clip operates on the sequence importance ratio $s_i(\theta)=\exp(\ell_i(\theta))$ by forcing $s_i$ into $[1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]$. For small $\ell$ we have $\ell\approx s_i-1$, so clipping with $\varepsilon$ roughly bounds the per‑token average log‑ratio $\ell$ to magnitude $\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)$. The KL penalty, however, acts on the batch average of $\ell$. Therefore:

- Clipping = hard cap on extreme per‑sequence updates (controls outliers).  
- KL = soft control over the mean shift of the policy (controls systematic drift).

If you add a sequence‑level KL that keeps the batch mean $\mathbb{E}[\ell]$ small, fewer sequences will hit the clip and you can safely loosen $\varepsilon$ (allowing bigger average updates while still bounding outliers). Conversely, very tight clipping (small $\varepsilon$, as in the paper’s length‑normalized GSPO numbers) already strongly limits per‑sequence shifts and may make a KL to $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ unnecessary.

Practical tuning recipe
Start simple and measure. A practical order of operations that many teams use is:

1. Run GSPO with sequence clipping only (use the paper’s length‑normalized scale; note their example clamps were extremely small because $s_i$ is per‑token, e.g. left/right ~3e‑4 / 4e‑4). Record: mean $\ell$, standard deviation of $\ell$, fraction of sequences with $s_i$ outside the clip, and primary task metrics.

2. If you see unstable updates or reward‑hacking / quality regressions over many updates, add a KL to a fixed reference (SFT) with a small $\beta$. If the problem is only per‑update instability, add a KL to $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ (this is a soft trust‑region).

3. Use an adaptive scheme for $\beta$: pick a target batch mean $\widehat{KL}_{\mathrm{token}}^*$ (monitoring the units used in your system; because GSPO normalizes by length the target will be small). If measured $>$ target, increase $\beta$; if measured $\ll$ target, decrease $\beta$. This is the common practical strategy in PPO variants.

4. If you add a KL, you can loosen $\varepsilon$ somewhat (fewer sequences will hit clipping). If you keep clipping very tight (paper’s setting), keep $\beta$ small or zero because the clipping already enforces a strong bound.

Concrete diagnostics to look at
Report and monitor the batch statistics of $\ell_i(\theta)=\log s_i(\theta)$: mean, variance, and the fraction clipped. Those tell you whether the KL is controlling the mean drift and whether clipping is still frequently active. Because GSPO uses per‑token normalization, always compute KL in the same normalized way so scales line up.

Final practical tips tied to the paper
GSPO was designed around a sequence‑level, length‑normalized importance ratio $s_i$. That makes sequence‑level KL natural (rather than token‑wise KL), and—importantly—more tolerant to engine/recomputation precision mismatches (the paper emphasizes GSPO’s robustness here). If you are anchoring to a pretrained reference, compute reference log‑probs at rollout time (or cache them) so the KL term is inexpensive. If you only want per‑update conservatism, prefer $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ as the KL target; if you want to prevent long‑term drift or reward‑exploitation, prefer a fixed SFT reference.

Summary
Add KL at sequence level (length‑normalized) to either $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ (soft trust region) or a fixed reference (prevention of drift). Clipping bounds per‑sequence extremes while KL controls the average drift; tune them jointly by monitoring $\ell=\log s$, clipped fractions, and task metrics, and use an adaptive $\beta$ controller (targeting a small per‑token KL) if you want automatic balancing.

### Is GSPO connected to reward-weighted regression or preference-based objectives like DPO/IPO?
Question:
At a high level, GSPO scales gradients by a sequence-level weight and an advantage derived from rewards. Is there a formal connection to reward-weighted regression (e.g., maximizing $\mathbb{E}[r\,\log\pi_\theta]$) or to preference-based objectives such as DPO/IPO that also operate at the sequence level? Any equivalences or notable differences would help position GSPO among sequence-level training methods.
Answer:
Short answer: GSPO is related in spirit to reward‑weighted regression (RWR) and to sequence‑level preference methods (DPO/IPO) because all three scale likelihood updates by a sequence‑level scalar. However GSPO is not equivalent to either family: its objective is a PPO‑style, sequence‑level surrogate that (i) applies an explicit sequence importance correction, (ii) length‑normalizes that correction, (iii) standardizes rewards into group advantages, and (iv) applies clipping. These ingredients change both the bias and variance of the update compared with RWR or pairwise preference objectives, so GSPO should be viewed as a sequence‑level importance‑weighted policy surrogate rather than as RWR or DPO/IPO.

Mathematically the paper gives the unclipped GSPO objective (group and sequence sampling omitted for brevity) as
$$
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GSPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}\big[ s(\theta)\,\widehat A\big],
\qquad
s(\theta)=\Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y)}\Big)^{1/|y|},
$$
and its gradient (again unclipped) is
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GSPO}}
= \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}
\Big[ s(\theta)\,\widehat A \cdot \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)\Big].
$$
Compare that to common forms you asked about. Reward‑weighted regression (a weighted maximum‑likelihood objective) is typically written as
$$
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{RWR}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}\big[ r(y)\,\log\pi_\theta(y)\big],
$$
whose gradient is
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{RWR}}
= \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}\big[ r(y)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y)\big].
$$
The (on‑policy) policy gradient for expected reward is
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[r(y)]
= \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}\Big[\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y)}\,r(y)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y)\Big].
$$

From these formulas you can see the concrete relationships and differences. If the new policy is very close to the old one (so $s(\theta)\approx 1$), if you ignore the length normalization $1/|y|$, set $\widehat A$ equal to the raw reward $r$, and ignore clipping, then GSPO’s gradient has the same structural form as a reward‑weighted likelihood (RWR) / policy‑gradient update (both are expectations of a scalar times $\nabla\log\pi$). So locally (infinitesimal updates around $\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$) GSPO behaves like a weighted likelihood / policy‑gradient update. In practice GSPO deliberately departs from RWR / the exact policy gradient in three important ways:

1. GSPO inserts a tempered sequence importance ratio $s(\theta)=\big(\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}\big)^{1/|y|}$ (geometric mean of per‑token ratios) rather than the full sequence ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}$. This reduces variance and keeps ratios comparable across lengths, but it also biases the estimator relative to the exact importance‑sampling policy gradient.

2. GSPO uses a group standardized advantage $\widehat A=(r-\mathrm{mean})/\mathrm{std}$ (a zero‑mean, unit‑scale signal) instead of raw nonnegative reward weights that are typical in RWR. That changes the update direction and stabilizes training (it is analogous to a baseline/normalization in policy gradient).

3. GSPO applies PPO‑style clipping at the sequence level. Clipping produces a biased but lower‑variance and more stable update; it is crucial to GSPO’s empirical stability but makes the method different from plain RWR or unregularized pairwise objectives.

Preference‑based methods such as DPO/IPO are also sequence‑level in the sense that they operate on whole responses, but they are conceptually different: DPO/IPO turn preferences into supervised (usually pairwise) training signals. A generic pairwise preference objective has the form
$$
\mathbb{E}_{(y^+,y^-)}\big[ \log\sigma( s_\theta(y^+)-s_\theta(y^-) ) \big],
$$
with $s_\theta$ typically a scoring function (often related to $\log\pi_\theta$). The gradient of this objective is a difference of two score‑gradients weighted by a logistic factor, so the update explicitly pushes preferred responses up relative to dispreferred ones. GSPO does not operate on pairwise classification; instead it produces a scalar advantage per sequence (from a group), multiplies that by a sequence‑level importance correction, and uses a surrogate objective with clipping. In the special case $G=2$ and with simple standardization, the sign of GSPO’s advantage will push the higher‑reward item up and the lower down, so behaviorally the two families both prefer high‑reward sequences. But their weighting functions, regularization, and bias/variance tradeoffs are different: preference losses typically avoid importance sampling and clipping but solve a different supervised problem (pairwise ranking), while GSPO is explicitly constructed as a stable off‑policy surrogate for policy optimization with sequence‑level importance correction.

Conclusion: GSPO is best read as a sequence‑level, importance‑weighted PPO surrogate that shares superficial structure with RWR (both scale $\nabla\log\pi$ by a scalar per sequence) and with preference methods (both operate at sequence granularity), but it is not formally equivalent to RWR or to DPO/IPO except in limiting or informal senses. The key practical distinctions are the tempered sequence importance ratio, length normalization, group advantage standardization, and sequence‑level clipping — these are precisely the mechanisms the paper argues give GSPO its improved stability and empirical performance on large LLM / MoE RL problems.

### Why not simply average or cap token-level ratios in GRPO to mimic GSPO?
Question:
If GRPO’s instability comes from unequal token weights $w_{i,t}$, why not replace them with a per-sequence constant (e.g., the arithmetic or geometric mean of $\{w_{i,t}\}_t$) so that all tokens within a response share the same weight? Would that not recover GSPO’s key stability property, or is there a subtle difference due to how gradients and clipping are applied?
Answer:
Short answer: you can only recover GSPO’s stability by replacing token-level ratios with the exact sequence-level importance ratio (or equivalently the geometric mean of token ratios computed from the full joint likelihood) and by moving clipping to the sequence level. Naively averaging (especially the arithmetic mean) or capping token ratios is not equivalent and will not reliably fix GRPO’s instability because it changes both the importance-sampling semantics and the gradient / clipping geometry that causes the high variance.

Why, in detail. GRPO uses token-level ratios
$$
w_{i,t}=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
$$
and its gradient (omitting clipping) has the form (paper, §3.2)
$$
\nabla\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}
=\mathbb{E}\Big[ \tfrac{1}{G}\sum_i \widehat A_i \cdot \tfrac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} w_{i,t}\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\Big].
$$
That is, each token gradient is scaled by its own token-ratio. GSPO instead uses the sequence likelihood ratio (length-normalized)
$$
s_i(\theta)=\Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Big)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log w_{i,t}\Big),
$$
and its gradient is
$$
\nabla\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}
=\mathbb{E}\Big[ \tfrac{1}{G}\sum_i s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i\cdot \tfrac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\Big].
$$
Two crucial differences follow.

First, arithmetic mean vs geometric mean. The GSPO weight is the geometric mean of the token ratios (the exponentiated average of log ratios). The arithmetic mean
$$
\bar w^{\text{arith}}_i=\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t w_{i,t}
$$
is generally not equal to $s_i$ and can be dominated by outlier token ratios (toy example: $w_1=100,w_2=0.01$ gives geometric mean $=1$ but arithmetic mean $\approx 50$). Using the arithmetic mean therefore produces a different (and generally biased) importance correction that is not derived from the correct sequence-level likelihood ratio required by importance sampling.

Second, gradient and clipping placement matter. GRPO’s instability comes from the per-token multiplicative noise: token-wise $w_{i,t}$ multiply token gradients unevenly and these per-token noises accumulate across long sequences. Simply "capping" or clipping token ratios inside the token sum (as GRPO does) is a different operation from computing a single sequence-level weight and clipping that. If you replace each $w_{i,t}$ with a per-sequence constant but keep tokenwise clipping or keep the arithmetic-mean definition, you still change the gradient geometry and the bias introduced by clipping. By contrast, GSPO’s $s_i$ is the correct sequence IS ratio (length-normalized), and GSPO explicitly clips at sequence level; this both matches the unit of reward and yields the stable gradient form above (all token gradients share the same multiplicative factor $s_i$).

A final practical point that the paper emphasizes: MoE routing volatility and single-sample token noise make token-level ratios highly unstable. Aggregating the log-probabilities into the sequence ratio (as GSPO does) smooths out per-token fluctuations and eliminates the pathological per-token weighting that caused catastrophic collapse in GRPO; ad-hoc averaging or token capping does not reliably remove that source of instability.

Consequence / prescription: if you want GRPO to behave like GSPO you must (a) compute the sequence log-likelihoods under both policies and form
$$
s_i=\exp\!\Big(\frac{\log\pi_\theta(y_i)-\log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i)}{|y_i|}\Big),
$$
(b) apply clipping to $s_i$ at sequence level, and (c) use that sequence factor to scale the summed token gradients (or use the GSPO-token construction from the paper if you need token-wise advantages while keeping stability). Naive arithmetic averaging or per-token capping does not buy you these theoretical and practical benefits.
