# Why Token-Level RL Breaks at Scale—and the Simple Fix

Large language models are scored and rewarded for whole answers, but many reinforcement-learning recipes still tweak probabilities one token at a time. That mismatch is not just inelegant — it becomes actively harmful as models, response lengths, and batch sizes grow. In practice it injects high-variance noise into gradients, which accumulates over long sequences and can trigger catastrophic collapse. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) is a simple, principled fix: compute importance weights and do clipping at the sequence level, not the token level. The result is markedly more stable and efficient RL for large (including Mixture-of-Experts) models.

## The core problem in one sentence

Importance sampling corrections must operate on the same unit as the reward. If your reward is sequence-level, doing importance sampling per token — using a single sampled token to compute a token-level correction — does not properly correct the distribution mismatch and produces noisy, unstable updates.

## Quick refresher: what current token-level methods do

PPO-style methods apply importance-weighted, clipped updates to keep the new policy close to the old policy. In token-level PPO/GRPO this takes the form of a per-token importance ratio
$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
$$
and (in GRPO) a group-based advantage per response
$$
\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \text{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_j)}{\text{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_j)}.
$$

GRPO then plugs $w_{i,t}$ and $\widehat{A}_i$ into a clipped per-token objective and averages across tokens. This removes the need for a value model (you get a relative advantage per response from a group of samples), but it keeps token-level importance ratios in the updates.

## Why that token-level correction is invalid at scale

The basic importance-sampling identity is
$$
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_\text{beh}}\left[\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}\,f(z)\right].
$$
This correction works because the weight $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ re-weights many samples $z\sim\pi_\text{beh}$ to estimate an expectation under $\pi_\text{tar}$. Crucially, the correction is meaningful when we're re-weighting whole-sample likelihoods (and averaging across many samples).

Token-level corrections in GRPO try to apply the same idea to each next-token distribution, but they compute a ratio using exactly one sampled token from that next-token distribution. A single sample is not a good estimator of the distributional shift — the ratio is effectively a noisy scalar, not an averaged correction. When you do this for every token in a long sequence, the noise accumulates. The clipping mechanism, intended to stabilize updates, interacts with these noisy per-token ratios and can amplify the instability rather than tame it. Empirically, this pattern has been observed to cause irreversible collapse in large-scale RL runs: once the model collapses under token-level importance weighting, reverting checkpoints and re-tuning clip ranges often does not recover training.

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models make this worse. Sparse routing causes "expert-activation volatility": after a gradient update, the set of experts activated for the same response can change significantly, so token-level per-token likelihoods swing wildly between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. That invalidates token-level ratios even more. Previously, teams used heavy engineering (Routing Replay — replaying the old routing decisions during likelihood recomputation) to suppress this instability, at the cost of memory/communication overhead and constrained model capacity.

## The simple, principled fix: sequence-level importance and clipping (GSPO)

If rewards are given to whole responses, compute importance ratios and clip at the response (sequence) level. GSPO does exactly that.

Define a length-normalized sequence importance ratio
$$
s_i(\theta)
= \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
$$
The objective is then a clipped, sequence-level update averaged over the group:
$$
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\min\!\big(s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat{A}_i\big)\right],
$$
where $\widehat{A}_i$ is the same group-standardized reward as in GRPO.

Two implementation details matter:

- Length normalization (the $1/|y_i|$ exponent) reduces variance and keeps ratios comparable across different response lengths.
- Group-based advantage normalization (subtract mean and divide by std across the group) provides a stable relative signal without training a separate value head.

## Why GSPO reduces variance (a short gradient-level intuition)

Omitting clipping for clarity, the GSPO gradient looks like
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
\;=\;
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\right].
$$
By contrast, the GRPO gradient (token-level ratios inside the token sum) is
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
\;=\;
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G\widehat{A}_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
w_{i,t}(\theta)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\right].
$$

The critical difference is that GSPO multiplies the entire token-gradient sum by the same sequence-level scalar $s_i(\theta)$, whereas GRPO multiplies each token's score by a separate, noisy $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. Those per-token multipliers do not average out well (each is computed from a single sampled token) and they can vary a lot across positions and across updates. GSPO removes that source of variance by making the per-token contributions internally consistent across a response.

## A token-level compromise: GSPO-token

If you need token-wise advantages (for example in multi-turn settings where different tokens should carry different credit), GSPO offers a variant called GSPO-token. It keeps a sequence-level importance scalar but allows per-token advantages by detaching the scalar when combining it with the per-token probabilities. Numerically, GSPO-token is equivalent to GSPO when per-token advantages are equal, but it gives you the flexibility to apply different advantages to different tokens while preserving the low-variance sequence-level correction.

## Practical wins observed in experiments

The paper reports empirical comparisons where GSPO was applied to a cold-start model fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base and evaluated on benchmarks such as AIME'24, LiveCodeBench and CodeForces. Under the same training compute and queries, GSPO:

- Ran much more stably (no catastrophic collapses observed).
- Achieved better training efficiency and downstream benchmark performance than GRPO.
- Continued to improve as training compute, query freshness, and generation length were increased.
- Eliminated the need for Routing Replay in MoE training, removing memory/communication overhead and allowing the model to use its full capacity.
- Made infrastructure simpler: sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant to precision discrepancies between inference and training engines, enabling direct use of inference-engine likelihoods in some workflows.

A few concrete experimental notes from the paper: rollout batches were split into four minibatches for updates; GSPO clipping ranges were tuned to small values (left/right clipping set to $3\mathrm{e}{-4}$ and $4\mathrm{e}{-4}$ in the reported run) while GRPO required very different, larger clip ranges (e.g., $0.2$ and $0.27$ in that comparison) because the definitions of the importance ratios have different numerical scales.

One counter-intuitive empirical observation: GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens (at token-level inspection) than GRPO, yet still uses samples more effectively and trains faster. That supports the view that token-level gradient estimates in GRPO are noisy and inefficient.

## Takeaways and practical recommendations

- Align the unit of importance-sampling correction with the unit of reward. If your reward is per-response, compute importance weights per response.
- Length-normalize sequence ratios (raise the sequence likelihood ratio to the $1/|y|$ power) to reduce variance and make clip ranges meaningful across different response lengths.
- Use group-standardized advantages (subtract mean, divide by std within a group of responses to the same prompt) to avoid a value head while keeping stable credit assignment.
- For MoE/Routing-sensitive models, prefer sequence-level corrections — they avoid expensive routing-replay workarounds.
- If you need token-level credit in multi-turn setups, use GSPO-token to keep the low-variance sequence correction while allowing token-wise advantages.

## Conclusion

The instability observed in large-scale RL for language models is not an inevitable consequence of model size or sparsity. It can arise from a simple misalignment: applying importance sampling and clipping at the token level when the signal (reward) is at the sequence level. GSPO fixes this mismatch with a compact change — sequence-level importance ratios, length normalization, and sequence-level clipping — and the empirical gains are large: more stable training, better sample efficiency, and simpler MoE workflows. If you're running RL at scale, aligning your optimization unit with your reward unit is a cheap change that pays off in robustness.



# GSPO in Plain English: Reward the Whole Answer, Not Each Word

Reinforcement learning for language models often asks the model to improve from feedback on full responses (answers, essays, code, etc.). Yet many RL recipes correct for off-policy sampling at the token level — they re-weight each predicted word independently. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) makes a simple but powerful change: measure how likely the entire response is under the new policy versus the old one, normalize by length, and clip and optimize at the sequence level. Intuitively: grade the essay, not each word.

## The core problem: token-level importance weights are noisy

When you train with off-policy data (samples generated by the old policy but used to update the current policy), importance sampling is the standard way to correct for distribution mismatch:

$ \displaystyle \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[ \frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\, f(z)\right]. $

PPO-style methods typically use token-level importance ratios, e.g. for token $y_t$:

$w_t(\theta)=\dfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})},$

and apply clipping and per-token advantages. GRPO adapted this idea to grouped responses (multiple samples per query) by sharing a group-based advantage across tokens, but it still multiplies each token gradient by its own token-level ratio.

The problem is that a token-level ratio is computed from a single sample of the next-token distribution. Importance-sampling correction assumes you are reweighting whole-sample expectations — estimating token-level expectations from one sample is inherently high variance. That noise accumulates across long sequences, interacts badly with clipping, and in practice can lead to catastrophic and often irreversible model collapse when training large or long-response models.

## GSPO’s simple fix: sequence-level ratios and sequence-level clipping

GSPO defines importance weights for entire sequences, not individual tokens. For a response $y$ to query $x$ the sequence-level, length-normalized ratio is

$ \displaystyle s(\theta) \;=\; \left( \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y \mid x)} \right)^{\!1/|y|}
\;=\; \exp\!\left( \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})} \right). $

GSPO optimizes the clipped sequence objective across a group of $G$ sampled responses per query:

$ \displaystyle \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\min\big( s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\; \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat{A}_i \big)\right], $

where $\widehat{A}_i$ is the group-normalized advantage computed from the verifier rewards for the $G$ responses:
$\displaystyle \widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \text{mean}_j r(x,y_j)}{\text{std}_j r(x,y_j)}$.

Two small but important design choices: (1) use group-normalized rewards instead of a learned value model (avoids an expensive and fragile value network), and (2) length-normalize $s_i$ so ratios are comparable across responses of different lengths and so clipping ranges stay meaningful.

## Why this is more stable (gradient-level intuition)

Omitting clipping, the GSPO gradient has the form

$ \displaystyle \nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}
= \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \frac{1}{G}\sum_i s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdots) \right]. $

Compare to the GRPO/PPO-style gradient where each token is multiplied by its own token-level ratio:

$ \displaystyle \nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}
= \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \frac{1}{G}\sum_i \widehat{A}_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdots)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdots)}\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdots)
\right]. $

The crucial difference: GSPO multiplies the whole-token-sum for a response by one sequence-level factor $s_i(\theta)$, so all tokens in a response are weighted equally. GRPO multiplies each token gradient by its own noisy ratio. Those per-token multipliers vary and can amplify random fluctuations, especially over long sequences or when model internals (like Mixture-of-Experts routing) change. GSPO removes that instability source.

## Practical wins reported in the paper

- Stability and sample efficiency: GSPO produced steady training improvements and better benchmark performance than GRPO under comparable compute in the experiments described in the paper. It allowed continuous gains when increasing compute, updating queries, or extending generation length.

- Clipping behavior: GSPO clips at the sequence level. The authors observed a two-orders-of-magnitude difference in the fraction of clipped tokens between GSPO and GRPO (even after tuning clipping ranges), but GSPO still learned more efficiently. This suggests token-level gradients were noisy and less useful for learning.

- Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models: MoE training can change which experts activate for the same sample across updates (the paper reports roughly a 10% expert-activation change in a 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base after updates). That volatility makes token-level ratios fluctuate wildly. Previous practical mitigations required a Routing Replay strategy (cache and replay routing modes), which is memory- and communication-heavy and constrains model capacity. GSPO eliminates the dependence on Routing Replay because sequence likelihoods are robust to token-level routing fluctuations, simplifying and stabilizing MoE RL.

- Easier infrastructure: Sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of precision differences between inference engines and training engines, so GSPO can make it feasible to use inference-engine likelihoods directly (avoiding expensive recomputation) in some deployment/training setups.

## GSPO-token: token-wise advantages without the instability

If you need token-level advantages (for multi-turn setups, for example), GSPO offers a variant called GSPO-token. It keeps the same sequence-level importance signal but allows per-token advantages. The trick is to detach the sequence ratio when forming token-level weights so the gradient still uses the single stable sequence factor. Numerically the token weights equal the sequence weight, but this variant exposes per-token reward shaping while avoiding per-token ratio noise.

## How to think about GSPO in one sentence

Because your reward applies to whole responses, correct your off-policy sampling at the same level: compute a sequence-level likelihood ratio (length-normalized), normalize rewards across a small group of sampled responses, clip at the sequence level, and optimize. That alignment — unit of importance correction = unit of reward — is what reduces variance and stabilizes learning.

## Final notes

GSPO is a modest change in objective design with outsized practical benefits for large, long-response, and sparse (MoE) language models. It keeps the group-advantage idea (so you avoid a heavyweight value model), enforces a principled importance-sampling correction at the sequence scale, and stabilizes training that previously suffered from token-level noise. The paper reports that these properties contributed to improvements in the Qwen3 RL training runs where GSPO was applied.



# Clipping, Reimagined: Cutting Whole Responses Yields Cleaner Gradients

Token-by-token clipping is the default in many RL-for-LM pipelines: compute a per-token importance ratio, clip it, and multiply it into the gradient. It sounds reasonable — after all, generation is autoregressive — but it turns out to be brittle in practice. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) shows a different and surprisingly effective design: compute importance ratios at the sequence level, clip whole responses, and use those sequence-level ratios to scale gradients. The result is fewer-but-cleaner gradient contributions that produce more stable and efficient learning, even when GSPO clips *more aggressively* (measured in clipped tokens) than token-level methods.

This post explains why this works, how length normalization keeps the sequence ratios well-behaved across answers of different lengths, and what practical consequences this has for large models (including Mixture-of-Experts).

## Why token-level clipping breaks down

Importance sampling rests on a simple identity:
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)]
= \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)} f(z)\right].
\]
The importance weight \(\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\) corrects for the mismatch between the sampling distribution and the target distribution, but only when it is applied to samples that meaningfully represent the behavior distribution. Crucially, importance weights are statistical weights that get their power from averaging over many samples.

GRPO-style token-level clipping applies an importance ratio at each token position:
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]
and uses those per-token ratios to scale token gradients. The problem is two-fold:

- Each token-level ratio is computed from a *single* sampled token from the next-token distribution. That single sample is not a reliable estimator of the distributional mismatch; the ratio is noisy rather than corrective.
- When you sum or average many such noisy per-token terms across long responses, the noise accumulates. In large models and long-response tasks this accumulation can become catastrophic: training becomes unstable and can collapse irreversibly.

Clipping per-token can help, but it can also interact badly with this high-variance signal and amplify pathological updates rather than prevent them.

## GSPO in a nutshell: clip entire responses

GSPO aligns the unit of optimization with the unit of reward. If the reward is assigned to a whole response \(y\), GSPO computes importance ratios and clipping at the sequence level. For a response \(y_i\) to query \(x\), GSPO uses the length-normalized sequence ratio
\[
s_i(\theta)
= \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]
GSPO then clips and scales at the response level. Using groups of responses per query, the group advantage for response \(i\) is the standardized reward
\[
\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_j)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_j)},
\]
and the per-response term is clipped with \(s_i(\theta)\) rather than per-token ratios.

There is also a GSPO-token variant that allows per-token advantages while still using the same sequence-level scaling factor. Numerically, when token advantages in a response are identical, GSPO and GSPO-token are equivalent.

## The gradient-level difference — neat and revealing

Compare the two gradient forms (clipping omitted for clarity). GSPO's gradient term for response \(y_i\) looks like:
\[
s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}).
\]
GRPO's gradient instead weights each token gradient by a different per-token ratio:
\[
\widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot).
\]

Key implications:

- GSPO multiplies the *average* token gradient by a single per-sequence scalar \(s_i(\theta)\). All tokens in the response are treated equally except for the common scaling factor, which is a smoothed aggregate of per-token changes (via the average log).
- GRPO multiplies each token gradient by its own noisy per-token ratio. These unequal multipliers can vary up and down and do not average out reliably when each is estimated from a single sampled token.

The result is that GRPO amplifies per-token sampling noise in the gradient, especially as sequence length grows. GSPO washes out that noise by consolidating evidence at the sequence level, producing a cleaner learning signal.

## Length normalization: why the 1/|y| exponent matters

The per-sequence ratio is raised to the power \(1/|y|\). That is not a cosmetic choice — it stabilizes the numerical behavior across answers of different lengths. Without length normalization, a change in the likelihood of a few tokens can inflate or deflate a sequence ratio exponentially with length; responses of different lengths would require very different clipping ranges. The form
\[
s_i(\theta) = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t}\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\right)
\]
is an average log-ratio, which reduces variance and keeps \(s_i(\theta)\) in a unified numerical range. That makes it practical to pick clipping thresholds that work across lengths.

## The counterintuitive empirical observation: more clipping, faster learning

One striking observation from the experiments is that GSPO ends up clipping a much larger fraction of *tokens* (roughly two orders of magnitude more) than GRPO, yet GSPO learns faster and more stably. Why?

Because GSPO clips at the response level. When a response is clipped, all of its tokens are considered clipped, so the token-level fraction of clipped elements can look very large. But GSPO discards noisy, off-policy responses wholesale and keeps fewer, higher-quality gradient contributors. Those cleaner gradients have lower variance and are more informative, so the optimizer makes more reliable progress despite using fewer token-level terms.

In contrast, GRPO keeps many token-level contributions that individually are noisy and inconsistently weighted; the optimizer is exposed to a noisy, high-variance signal that slows or destabilizes learning. In practice this can even lead to catastrophic model collapse with GRPO on large, long-response tasks, whereas GSPO maintains stable improvement.

## Extra wins: MoE stability and simpler RL infra

Two practical wins emerge from the sequence-level view:

- Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models: GRPO required a Routing Replay trick — caching activated experts under the old policy and replaying those routing modes under the new policy when computing per-token ratios — to stabilize token-level ratios. GSPO removes the need for Routing Replay because sequence-level likelihoods are far less sensitive to per-token routing volatility. That reduces memory and communication overhead and lets the model use its full capacity naturally.

- Training/inference engine mismatches: GSPO only needs sequence-level likelihoods, which are more tolerant of numerical differences between inference and training engines. This makes it more feasible to use likelihoods produced by an inference service directly, avoiding expensive recomputation on the training engine in some deployment setups.

## Practical takeaways

- Align the unit of off-policy correction with the unit of reward. If rewards are assigned at the response level, apply importance sampling and clipping at the response level.
- Normalize sequence ratios by length: use \(s_i(\theta)=\big(\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)\big)^{1/|y_i|}\) (or equivalently the average log-ratio). This reduces variance and makes clipping thresholds stable across lengths.
- Expect larger token-level clipping *counts* when clipping whole responses — this is fine and can be beneficial because the retained gradients are cleaner.
- If you need token-level customization (e.g., per-token advantages in multi-turn setups), use a GSPO-token variant that keeps the same sequence-level scaling while allowing per-token advantage signals.
- For MoE models and large-scale setups, GSPO can simplify infrastructure (no Routing Replay) and make RL training more robust.

Small practical numbers from the reported experiments: GSPO's clipping thresholds were very tight (left/right set to 3e-4 / 4e-4 in the reported run) while GRPO needed much larger per-token thresholds (0.2 / 0.27) to be comparable. Those values highlight that the numerical scale of the two importance-ratio definitions is very different; you should tune clipping ranges in the context of how you compute importance weights.

## Conclusion

Clipping entire responses instead of clipping token-by-token is more than a cosmetic change. It fixes a fundamental mismatch between the unit of reward and the unit of importance correction, dramatically reduces variance in the gradient signal, and yields more stable, efficient RL training for large language models. GSPO shows that fewer, cleaner gradients beat many noisy ones — and that length normalization is the simple trick that keeps sequence ratios well behaved across answers of different sizes.



# MoE Without Meltdowns: How GSPO Tames Expert Routing Volatility

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures unlock much larger model capacity by routing tokens to different experts. That conditional routing is powerful — but it also creates a fragile interaction with common RL fixes that correct for off-policy data. After each gradient update, which experts are activated for the same sample can change, and token-level importance corrections built on those per-token likelihoods can oscillate wildly. The result: noisy gradients, amplified variance over long responses, and sometimes catastrophic, irreversible training collapse.

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) offers a simple, principled fix: stop trying to do off-policy correction token-by-token and do it at the sequence level instead. The authors show that this small change stabilizes RL for large MoE models, removes the need for costly routing replay workarounds, and yields more efficient training in practice — including improvements used in the Qwen3 family.

## Why token-level importance weights break for MoE

Most RL methods adapted to language models (PPO-style variants and GRPO) apply an importance ratio at each token,
$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}$,
and then clip or weight per-token contributions. That looks reasonable at first glance, but it fails on two counts:

- Importance sampling is meant to correct a distributional mismatch by reweighting many samples from the behavior distribution. Using a single sampled token from the next-token distribution to define the importance weight does not perform the intended correction and introduces high variance instead.
- In MoE models, the activated expert set itself can change after each parameter update. The paper reports that for the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, roughly 10% of experts activated for the same rollout sample differ after an RL gradient update. Those activation changes make token-level likelihoods and hence $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ fluctuate sharply, invalidating the token-level corrections.

When these fluctuating token-level weights are summed over long sequences and then subject to clipping, the noise can accumulate and destabilize training — in some cases causing irreversible collapse.

## GSPO: clip, reward, and optimize whole responses

GSPO replaces token-level importance ratios with a sequence-level ratio that matches the unit of the reward (responses are scored at sequence level). For each sampled response $y_i$ to query $x$, GSPO computes a length-normalized sequence importance ratio

$s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).$

The objective then applies clipping and group-based advantages at the response level (the group advantage is the standardized score of each response within the group of $G$ samples for the same query). Two practical design choices matter:

- Length normalization ($1/|y_i|$ in the exponent) keeps the ratio numerically well behaved across different response lengths.
- Clipping is applied per response (not per token), so overly off-policy whole responses are excluded instead of intermittently clipping tokens inside a response.

That change has a direct effect on gradients. GSPO ends up weighting every token in the same response by the same factor $s_i(\theta)$, instead of weighting each token by a different per-token $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. Equal weighting across tokens removes a major source of instability introduced by unequal, single-sample-derived weights.

The authors also describe a GSPO-token variant that allows token-level advantages while still using the sequence-level scaling factor; numerically it is equivalent to GSPO when token advantages are identical, but provides flexibility for multi-turn or token-specific reward shaping.

## Routing Replay: what it is and why GSPO makes it unnecessary

To stabilize GRPO on MoE models, the group previously relied on a trick called Routing Replay: cache the expert routing decisions from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and replay those exact routing modes when evaluating $\pi_{\theta}$ for importance weights. That forces the numerator and denominator of $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ to use the same activated subnetwork, taming volatility in token-level ratios.

Routing Replay works, but it has downsides documented in the paper: it increases memory and communication overhead, complicates infrastructure, and can constrain how the model uses its capacity (because you're effectively replaying old routing patterns instead of letting the new policy choose freely).

GSPO removes the need for Routing Replay. Because it reasons about whole-sequence likelihoods rather than per-token likelihoods, the sequence score is much less sensitive to local routing changes. The MoE model's overall language modeling capacity remains intact, so the sequence-level likelihood does not jump wildly even when a subset of experts changes activation. That stability lets GSPO compute importance ratios conventionally and converge without the extra routing bookkeeping.

## Empirical highlights (what the paper reports)

The paper evaluates GSPO starting from a cold-start fine-tune of Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base and compares against GRPO. Key experimental notes:

- Each rollout batch was split into four mini-batches for gradient updates (an off-policy setting).
- GSPO used very small clipping ranges (left/right clipping set to 3e-4 and 4e-4 in the experiments). GRPO used much larger token-level clipping (0.2 and 0.27), tuned for fair comparison. The magnitude difference reflects that sequence-level and token-level importance ratios live on very different numerical scales.
- Benchmarks used include AIME'24 (Pass@1 averaged over 32 samplings), LiveCodeBench (Pass@1 averaged over 8 samplings), and CodeForces (Elo rating).

Reported outcomes:

- GSPO training is stable across runs and improves steadily with more training compute, refreshed query sets, and longer generation lengths.
- Under the same compute and consumed queries, GSPO reaches higher training accuracy and better benchmark performance than GRPO.
- GSPO succeeded at MoE RL without Routing Replay; GRPO required Routing Replay to converge stably.
- Curiously, GSPO clipped a much larger fraction of tokens (roughly two orders of magnitude more clipped tokens than GRPO, even after adjusting clipping ranges) yet still achieved higher training efficiency. That counterintuitive result suggests that GRPO's token-level gradients are intrinsically noisy and inefficient to exploit.

Finally, the paper notes that GSPO has been adopted in the development of the latest Qwen3 models, indicating its practical value at scale.

## Practical infrastructure wins

A small but important point for real systems: many inference stacks (vLLM, SGLang) and training stacks (Megatron) compute likelihoods with different numeric precisions. Previously teams recomputed likelihoods in the training engine to ensure consistent token-level ratios. Because GSPO only needs sequence-level likelihoods, which are empirically much more tolerant to small precision differences, it becomes feasible to use likelihoods directly from the inference engine. That can save recomputation cost and simplify rollout pipelines, which matters most at the massive scales where MoE + RL are applied.

## Takeaways

- Token-level importance ratios estimated from a single sampled token are high-variance and can be invalidated by MoE routing changes; summed across long responses they can destabilize training.
- GSPO defines importance ratios at the sequence level, applies sequence-level clipping and group-based advantages, and length-normalizes the ratio to control variance.
- That change stabilizes RL for MoE models and removes the need for Routing Replay, lowering memory/communication overhead and allowing the model to use its full capacity.
- Empirically, GSPO is more stable and more sample-efficient than GRPO, even though it clips many more tokens — a sign that GSPO’s learning signal is cleaner and more usable in practice.

If you work on large-scale RL for language models, especially with MoE architectures, the practical lesson is clear: make your importance weights speak the same language as your rewards. When rewards are sequence-level, correct at the sequence level. GSPO is a simple, principled instantiation of that idea — and it appears to tame many of the fragilities that used to plague MoE RL.



# Less Plumbing, More Learning: GSPO Simplifies RL Infrastructure

Reinforcement learning at scale for large language models often means splitting work across specialized engines: inference services (vLLM, SGLang, etc.) generate rollouts, while a separate training engine (Megatron-style) recomputes likelihoods and applies updates. That recomputation — especially at the token level — is a lot of engineering plumbing: expensive, fragile, and awkward in sparse models like Mixture-of-Experts (MoE). Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) changes the math so the plumbing can be reduced.

## The plumbing problem in a nutshell

Most sequence RL pipelines use importance weights computed at the token level. For example, GRPO and similar algorithms rely on token-level ratios
$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} \mid x,y_{i,<t}) }$,
and then clip or weight gradients per token. In practice this creates two pain points.

First, training and inference engines can have precision and implementation differences. To get numerically consistent token-level likelihoods under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, practitioners typically recompute token likelihoods in the training engine rather than trusting the inference engine outputs. That recomputation couples rollout collection and training resources, increases communication and memory overhead, and complicates disaggregated deployments.

Second, MoE models introduce routing volatility: after an update, the set of experts activated for the same token can change, making token-level likelihoods unstable. To force stability in GRPO-style training, teams resort to tricks like Routing Replay (cache and reapply routing), which again adds memory/communication cost and constrains model capacity.

Both issues stem from optimizing at the token level while rewards and off-policy corrections are naturally sequence-level.

## GSPO flips the unit of correction to sequences

GSPO defines importance weights at the sequence level and performs sequence-level clipping/optimization. The sequence-level, length-normalized importance ratio is

$s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i \mid x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i \mid x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\!\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} \mid x,y_{i,<t})} \right).$

Two simple observations explain why this reduces plumbing:

- The reward is issued to a whole response, so matching the unit of importance sampling to the unit of reward makes statistical sense. Token-level importance weights computed from a single token sample are high-variance and can be misleading.

- Aggregating log-likelihoods across tokens (and normalizing by length) smooths per-token noise. A small numeric mismatch between engines or a minor change in expert routing will have much smaller impact on the aggregated sequence likelihood than on individual token probabilities.

Because $s_i(\theta)$ measures how far an entire sampled response under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ deviates from $\pi_\theta$, it serves both as a meaningful clipping criterion and as a robust importance weight that tolerates modest precision differences.

## Concrete infrastructure wins

Switching to sequence-level ratios unlocks several practical simplifications that reduce plumbing and speed iteration:

- Reduced recomputation: sequence-level likelihoods returned by an inference engine are typically robust enough that they can be used directly for optimization, avoiding the common practice of re-evaluating token-by-token likelihoods in the training engine.

- Simpler partial rollouts and multi-turn RL: partial outputs (or multi-turn responses) are easier to handle because you do not need exact token-by-token likelihood reconciliation between engines for every intermediate step; sequence-level aggregation smooths intermediate numerical noise.

- Looser coupling in disaggregated setups: you can separate rollout collection (inference service) from training without a heavy recomputation step or tight synchronization, simplifying deployment and scaling.

- Less MoE plumbing: GSPO naturally tolerates expert-activation volatility and therefore obviates the Routing Replay workaround. That removes memory/communication overhead and avoids artificially restricting the model’s capacity.

The paper reports that GSPO converged stably in settings where GRPO required Routing Replay to avoid divergence, and that GSPO was used successfully in training the latest Qwen3 models.

## A few practical notes

- GSPO uses length-normalization and sequence-level clipping. Because the importance ratio definitions differ, the numerical clipping ranges used for GSPO are typically orders of magnitude smaller than those used for token-level algorithms. In the experiments reported, GSPO’s left/right clipping ranges were tuned to very small values compared to GRPO’s (reflecting the different scale of $s_i(\theta)$ vs. per-token $w_{i,t}(\theta)$).

- GSPO often clips a higher fraction of tokens (because clipping is applied per response rather than per token), yet it still achieved higher training efficiency in experiments. This counterintuitive result underscores that token-level gradients can be noisy and that sequence-level filtering yields more reliable learning signals.

- If you need token-wise advantage shaping (for multi-turn tasks, for example), the GSPO-token variant provides per-token advantage flexibility while still anchoring importance correction to the sequence-level ratio. In practice this preserves most of GSPO’s infrastructure benefits because the heavy lifting — the importance weight that would normally require token-level recomputation — remains a sequence-level quantity.

## Takeaway

Token-level recomputation between inference and training engines is expensive and brittle, especially for MoE models and disaggregated deployments. By shifting off-policy correction and clipping to the sequence level, GSPO reduces sensitivity to per-token numeric discrepancies and routing volatility. That makes it practical to trust inference-engine likelihoods directly, removes the need for ad hoc routing replay, and untangles rollout and training infrastructure so teams can spend less time on plumbing and more on learning.



# From PPO to GRPO to GSPO: The Road to Stable RL for LLMs

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a key tool for pushing large language models (LLMs) further — from better reasoning to stronger code generation. But as models get bigger, sparser (Mixture-of-Experts, or MoE), and are asked to produce longer outputs, stability becomes the dominant engineering challenge. This post walks through the evolution from PPO to GRPO and then to Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), the algorithmic change that fixes a fundamental instability in prior approaches and makes large-scale RL for LLMs much more reliable.

## Quick recap: PPO and why it’s heavy for LLMs

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a widely-used policy-gradient method. In autoregressive language modeling it is usually applied token-by-token: given a sampled response $y=(y_1,\dots,y_{|y|})$ to query $x$, PPO builds a clipped objective over per-token importance ratios $w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}$, typically optimizing

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\left[\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
\min\big(w_t(\theta)\,\widehat A_t,\ \mathrm{clip}(w_t(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat A_t\big)\right],
\]

where $\widehat A_t$ are token advantages, usually estimated from a learned value model.

Two practical pain points with PPO for LLM RL:

- The value model is large (often comparable to the policy) and costly in memory and compute.
- Getting reliable per-token value estimates is hard, especially as sequences lengthen and tasks become complex.

These limitations motivated alternatives that avoid an explicit value model.

## GRPO: group-relative rewards — clever but fragile

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) sidesteps a learned value function by estimating advantages from a group of sampled responses to the same query. For a group $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ sampled from the old policy, GRPO defines each response’s advantage as the group-normalized reward

\[
\widehat A_i = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\text{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\})}{\text{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\})},
\]

then plugs those advantages into a PPO-style token-level objective:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\big(w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_{i},\ \mathrm{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i\big)\right],
\]

with the same per-token $w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$.

GRPO’s main advantage is removing the value model. But keeping token-level importance weighting creates a critical instability, especially for large models and long sequences — and this is where GSPO intervenes.

## Why token-level importance weights are a bad fit

The core statistical tool behind importance-weighted RL updates is importance sampling: to estimate an expectation under a target distribution you reweight samples drawn from a behavior distribution. A crucial property of such reweighting is that it operates at the same granularity as the samples and benefits from averaging across many samples from the behavior distribution for a stable correction.

GRPO applies importance weights at each token position based on a single sampled token from the respective next-token distribution. That is conceptually wrong for two reasons:

1. Per-token importance weights are estimated from a single sample of each next-token distribution, so they do not perform a reliable distribution correction and inject high variance.
2. That per-token noise accumulates along long sequences and interacts poorly with clipping, producing amplified, unpredictable gradient noise. Empirically this can cause catastrophic, often irreversible model collapse.

In short: the unit of importance-correction and clipping should match the unit that receives the reward. If the reward is sequence-level, the importance ratio and clipping should be sequence-level.

## GSPO: sequence-level importance and sequence-level clipping

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) fixes the mismatch by moving importance weighting and clipping to the sequence level. For each sampled response $y_i$ GSPO defines a length-normalized sequence importance ratio

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]

Length normalization (the $1/|y_i|$ exponent) keeps ratios numerically stable across different response lengths, so a single clipping range can be used.

The GSPO objective mirrors GRPO but applies sequence-level ratios and clipping:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\min\big(s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat A_i\big)\right].
\]

Intuition: reweight entire responses according to how probable the current policy thinks they are (relative to the old policy), and clip at that same sequence granularity. This aligns the unit of correction with the unit of reward and optimization.

## Gradient-level difference: equal token weighting vs. noisy per-token weights

A concise way to see what changed is to look at the gradients (ignoring clipping for clarity). GSPO’s gradient contains terms of the form

\[
\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_i|}\widehat A_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
\]

where every token in the response is weighted equally by the same sequence-level factor $s_i(\theta)$.

By contrast, GRPO’s gradient weights each token by its own per-token importance ratio inside the summation:

\[
\widehat A_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}).
\]

Those unequal per-token multiplicative weights vary across tokens, introduce variance, and can accumulate badly. GSPO removes that instability by applying a single, sequence-level weight to the whole gradient of the sequence log-likelihood.

## GSPO-token: token-wise advantage customization without noise

There are use-cases (multi-turn RL, token-level credit assignments) where you want per-token advantages. GSPO offers a variant, GSPO-token, that preserves the sequence-level scaling but allows per-token advantages. GSPO-token constructs per-token terms that are numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$ while stopping gradients through the per-token scaling factors (a detach operation). As a result, GSPO-token is flexible for token-level advantage signals while keeping the stable sequence-level weighting in the gradient.

## Empirical behavior: stability, efficiency, and MoE friendliness

The authors evaluated GSPO on a cold-start RL fine-tuning from a Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base checkpoint and measured training reward and downstream benchmarks (AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, CodeForces Elo). Key empirical findings reported:

- GSPO delivers stable training dynamics over long runs. Training continues to improve with more compute, updated query sets, and extended generation length.
- Under the same compute and query budget, GSPO achieves better training accuracy and downstream benchmark performance than GRPO.
- GSPO was applied successfully to the latest Qwen3 models and helped unlock notable performance gains.

Implementation details that illustrate differences in scale: in the experiments GSPO used clipping ranges (left/right) of approximately $3\mathrm{e}{-4}$ and $4\mathrm{e}{-4}$ respectively, while GRPO used much larger ranges around $0.2$ and $0.27$. This large numerical difference reflects the distinct definitions of importance ratios (sequence length–normalized vs. per-token).

A counter-intuitive but important empirical observation: GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens (orders of magnitude more) than GRPO under comparable settings, yet still achieves higher training efficiency. The interpretation is that token-level gradients in GRPO are noisy and inefficient to exploit, so using fewer but cleaner sequence-level examples is superior.

## Why GSPO matters for MoE models

MoE models introduce another source of instability: expert-activation volatility. After parameter updates, the set of activated experts for the same input can change; for example, in the studied Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model roughly 10% of experts activated for the same rollout sample can differ after a gradient update. This makes per-token likelihoods fluctuate wildly and breaks token-level importance ratios.

Previously, the team used a workaround called Routing Replay: cache the activated experts under the old policy and replay those routing modes under the new policy while computing per-token ratios. Routing Replay stabilizes GRPO training but adds memory, communication, and capacity overhead.

GSPO obviates Routing Replay because it relies on sequence-level likelihoods which are much less sensitive to per-token routing changes. Practically, GSPO stabilizes MoE RL training without routing replay, simplifying the training pipeline and letting MoE models use their full capacity.

## Practical infrastructure benefits

GSPO’s reliance on sequence-level likelihoods brings an infrastructure win. Training and inference engines often differ in numerical precision and implementation; GRPO’s per-token ratios usually require recomputing token likelihoods in the training engine to avoid precision drift. Sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of such discrepancies, opening the possibility of using likelihoods returned by inference engines directly and avoiding expensive recomputation. That is beneficial in scenarios like partial rollouts, multi-turn RL, and disaggregated training-inference systems.

## Takeaways

- Principle first: match the unit of importance sampling and clipping to the unit of reward. For sequence-level rewards, perform sequence-level importance correction and clipping.
- GRPO’s clever group-relative advantages remove the value model, but keeping token-level importance ratios injects high-variance noise that accumulates and can collapse training.
- GSPO replaces token-level ratios with a length-normalized sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$ and clips at the sequence level. This change stabilizes and improves RL for large LLMs, simplifies MoE training by removing the need for routing replay, and relaxes infrastructure constraints.
- Empirically, GSPO yields more stable training and better sample efficiency than GRPO and was instrumental in the RL-based improvements of the Qwen3 family.

If you are building large-scale RL for LLMs, GSPO is worth considering as the default: it keeps the value-model-free advantage of group-based methods while fixing a fundamental variance source that becomes crippling at scale.



# Fine-Grained When You Need It: GSPO-token for Multi-Turn Feedback

## TL;DR
GSPO-token is a small, principled variant of GSPO that gives you token-level advantages while keeping GSPO’s sequence-level importance ratio and clipping. It uses a stop-gradient trick so that the numeric importance weight is the same sequence-level scalar $s_i$, but gradients flow per token. If all per-token advantages are equal, GSPO-token is numerically identical to GSPO — so you get flexibility without losing the stability benefits of sequence-level correction.

## Why this exists (quick recap)
GRPO tried to avoid a value network by using group-relative, token-level importance ratios, but token-level ratios introduce high variance and instability: the importance weight at each token is computed from a single next-token sample, so it doesn't properly correct distributional mismatch and its noise accumulates over long sequences.

GSPO fixes that by defining the importance ratio at the sequence level:
$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}},
$$
and clipping/optimizing at the response (sequence) level. That stabilizes training, simplifies MoE handling, and matches the unit of reward (responses are scored at sequence level).

But sometimes you do need finer-grained advantages — for example, in multi-turn interactions where different turns (or tokens) should receive different credit. That’s where GSPO-token comes in.

## What GSPO-token does, in plain terms
GSPO-token keeps the same sequence-level importance ratio $s_i$ and the same clipping philosophy, but allows you to supply a per-token advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ instead of a single per-sequence advantage $\widehat{A}_i$. The objective is

$$
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO-token}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\!\left(s_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i,t},\ \mathrm{clip}(s_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_{i,t}\right)
\right],
$$

with the crucial definition

$$
s_{i,t}(\theta)
= \mathrm{sg}\big[s_i(\theta)\big]\cdot\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\mathrm{sg}\big[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\big]}.
$$

Here $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ is stop-gradient (the same as `.detach()` in PyTorch). Numerically $s_{i,t}(\theta)=s_i(\theta)$, so clipping is effectively sequence-level, but gradients are arranged so each token’s log-prob receives its own advantage.

## Why that retains GSPO’s stability
Because $s_{i,t}$ is numerically the sequence-level $s_i$, all the clipping and off-policy correction are controlled by a single, length-normalized sequence scalar. That avoids the highly variable token-level importance weights that caused trouble in GRPO.

At the gradient level (clipping omitted for clarity), GSPO-token yields

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO-token}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \widehat{A}_{i,t}\,\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
$$

Compare this to GRPO’s gradient (again omitting clipping and using the GRPO notation):

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \widehat{A}_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)
\right].
$$

Two differences matter:

- GRPO injects token-level importance ratios $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t})}$ inside the token sum. Those per-token scalars vary and can accumulate high variance across long sequences.
- GSPO-token uses the single sequence-level scalar $s_i(\theta)$ to weight the whole token-sum, but still lets each token have its own advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ multiplying its gradient. That keeps the variance under the same control as GSPO while giving you fine-grained credit assignment.

If you set $\widehat{A}_{i,t}=\widehat{A}_i$ for all tokens, the GSPO-token gradient reduces exactly to GSPO’s gradient, so the two are numerically identical in objective, clipping condition, and gradient.

## When to use GSPO-token
Use GSPO-token when you have reliable, meaningful per-token (or per-turn) signals that you want the model to act on. Typical example from the paper: multi-turn RL where different turns can carry different reward contributions. GSPO-token is designed for those situations because it:

- preserves GSPO’s sequence-level off-policy correction and clipping (stability),
- enables token-level advantage shaping (flexibility),
- reduces to GSPO when you don’t need fine-grained signals (no downside).

If your feedback is only at the sequence level (a single score per response), GSPO (or GSPO-token with equal token advantages) is the straightforward choice.

## Implementation notes (practical)
- Implement $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ as `.detach()` in PyTorch. That implements the stop-gradient behavior used in $s_{i,t}$.
- Keep GSPO’s length-normalization for the sequence ratio:
  $$
  s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|},
  $$
  which stabilizes the numeric range of importance ratios across different response lengths.
- Compute per-token advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ according to your task (e.g., per-turn rewards or token-level annotations). GSPO-token does not force a particular way to produce those advantages — it just provides the stable optimization wrapper to use them.
- Clipping still operates on the numeric $s_i$ (through $s_{i,t}$), so you get the same kind of trust-region behavior as GSPO.

## Bottom line
GSPO-token is a minimal, well-motivated extension to GSPO: it gives you token-wise credit assignment while preserving the theoretical and practical stability advantages of sequence-level importance ratios and clipping. When you need per-token or per-turn control (multi-turn RL being the canonical case), GSPO-token is a clean way to get it without reintroducing the variance problems that token-level importance ratios caused in prior approaches.



# What It Delivers: Efficiency, Stability, and Qwen3’s Gains

## TL;DR
Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) replaces token-level importance weighting with a sequence-level, length-normalized importance ratio and sequence-level clipping. The result is more stable RL fine-tuning for large language models (especially long generations and MoE models), better sample-to-progress efficiency than GRPO, and practical benefits for RL infrastructure. In experiments starting from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, GSPO trains stably across longer generations, scales with more compute, and improves on difficult math and coding benchmarks that powered gains in the latest Qwen3 models.

## The practical problem: token-level IS breaks at scale
When you run RL at the scale of modern LLMs you typically:
- generate multiple responses per query (a group),
- partition large rollout batches into mini-batches (creating off-policy updates),
- and push for long responses and sparse models (e.g., MoE).

PPO-style clipping is there to limit how “off-policy” a sample can be, but GRPO and similar methods apply importance sampling at the token level. Concretely, GRPO weights each token by
$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$,
while the reward is given to the whole sequence. The paper shows this mismatch is problematic:

- A token-level importance weight is computed from a single sampled next-token, so it does not provide a reliable correction for the distribution mismatch.
- These per-token weights are noisy; noise accumulates across long sequences and is amplified by clipping, which empirically can cause catastrophic and often irreversible collapse.
- In MoE models, activated experts can change across updates (the paper observed roughly a 10% change in activated experts for the same sample after updates), making token-level likelihoods especially volatile.

In short: unit-of-optimization should match unit-of-reward. For sequence rewards, sequence-level correction makes more sense.

## GSPO in one paragraph (what it does)
GSPO defines importance ratios at the sequence level and clips whole responses. Its objective (grouped per-query) is, in words: standardize rewards within a group of responses, compute a length-normalized sequence importance ratio for each response, clip that ratio, and use it to weight the standardized reward. Formally, for a response $y_i$ GSPO uses
$s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right)$,
and optimizes a clipped objective over the group average of $s_i(\theta)$ times the group-normalized advantage $\widehat{A}_i$. Length normalization stabilizes $s_i(\theta)$ across different response lengths and avoids explosion of likelihood ratios for long sequences.

There is also a GSPO-token variant if you need per-token advantages (e.g., in some multi-turn setups). GSPO-token is designed so that it is numerically equivalent to GSPO when all token advantages inside a response are the same, while allowing finer-grained advantage assignment when needed.

## Why GSPO stabilizes and uses samples better
Two complementary reasons explain the improved behavior:

1. Better importance-sampling semantics. Importance sampling estimates correct expectations only when the reweighting factor corresponds to the full sample. GSPO applies that principle at the sequence level (the natural unit of reward) instead of at each next-token distribution where a single token sample cannot recover the distributional mismatch.

2. Lower variance in gradient weighting. The gradient of GSPO effectively multiplies the sum of token log-gradients by one uniform sequence-level weight $s_i(\theta)$, while GRPO weights each token unevenly by its own $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. Unequal per-token weights are a source of high variance that accumulates across positions and training steps; GSPO removes that instability.

Empirically the paper reports an interesting paradox: GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens (two orders of magnitude more tokens clipped, for the chosen clipping settings) than GRPO, yet it achieves higher training efficiency and stronger downstream performance. That suggests GRPO's token-level gradients are noisy and consume samples inefficiently, while GSPO discards more but higher-quality training signals.

## MoE models: you can drop Routing Replay
A practical pain point in MoE RL with token-level weighting is expert-activation volatility: after parameter updates the exact experts used for the same input can change, which wildly perturbs token likelihoods. The prior workaround (Routing Replay) caches routing modes from the old policy and replays them under the new policy when computing token likelihoods. That stabilizes GRPO but costs memory, communication, and effectively limits capacity.

GSPO sidesteps this: focusing on sequence likelihood makes the method robust to per-token routing changes because the model’s overall sequence likelihoods remain much more stable than individual token likelihoods under routing shifts. The paper shows GSPO converges for MoE RL without Routing Replay, simplifying the training pipeline and enabling the model to use its full capacity.

## Infrastructure wins: inference-engine likelihoods and recomputation
In practice you often have precision or implementation differences between training engines and inference-serving engines; that’s why teams traditionally recompute rollout likelihoods on the training engine before optimizing. The paper argues that sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant to such discrepancies, so GSPO can often use inference-engine likelihoods directly without expensive recomputation, which is useful in disaggregated training-inference workflows, partial-rollout setups, and multi-turn RL.

## Empirical footprint (what the curves mean for you)
The main experimental signals in the paper come from fine-tuning a cold-start model based on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, with rollout batches partitioned into four mini-batches (an off-policy setting). Benchmarks included AIME'24 (math), LiveCodeBench (coding), and CodeForces (Elo rating). The empirical observations are:

- Training with GSPO remains stable across training and continues to improve with more compute, refreshed query sets, and longer generation lengths.
- For the same training compute and number of consumed queries, GSPO achieves better training accuracy and better benchmark performance than GRPO.
- GSPO eliminated the need for Routing Replay in MoE training and was used in RL for the latest Qwen3 models.
- The clipping hyperparameters differ greatly in scale between GSPO and GRPO: the paper used clipping bounds of approximately 3e-4 / 4e-4 (left/right) for GSPO and 0.2 / 0.27 for GRPO in those experiments, after careful tuning.

Translate that: if you’re fine-tuning at scale, GSPO is less likely to hit catastrophic collapses, will make more consistent use of expensive rollout samples, and lets you scale generation length and compute without destabilizing the model.

## Practical guidance and choices
- Unit matching: prefer sequence-level importance correction when rewards are sequence-level. This is especially important for long generations and sparse models.
- Grouping and advantage normalization: GSPO uses group-normalized advantages (standardize rewards across the $G$ sampled responses for the same query). Keep a reasonable group size to get a stable empirical advantage distribution.
- Clipping: GSPO’s importance ratios are length-normalized and numerically much nearer to 1 than per-token ratios; clipping ranges should be tuned and are typically much smaller than GRPO’s.
- MoE: GSPO removes the need for routing-replay workarounds; if you are training MoE models with GRPO you’ll likely simplify your pipeline by switching to GSPO.
- GSPO-token: use this if you need token-level advantage customization (multi-turn objectives), but recognize it’s designed to be numerically consistent with GSPO when token advantages are equal.

## Caveats and tuning
GSPO improves the core instability that arises from token-level IS, but it is not a plug-and-play magic wand. The paper shows that hyperparameters (clipping bounds, group size, how you partition batches for off-policy updates) still matter and were tuned carefully in experiments. Expect to validate clipping ranges and group normalization choices on your workloads.

## Bottom line
If you are doing large-scale RL fine-tuning of language models—especially with long responses or MoE architectures—GSPO gives you a principled, lower-variance way to apply importance sampling and clipping. That translates into robust training (less catastrophic collapse), better use of rollout samples, simplified MoE training (no Routing Replay), and concrete downstream gains on hard math and coding benchmarks. These practical benefits are what enabled the RL-driven gains reported for the latest Qwen3 models.
