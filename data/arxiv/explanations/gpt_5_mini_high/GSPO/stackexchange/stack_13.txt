\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why can clipping a larger fraction of tokens still yield higher training efficiency in GSPO?
Question:
The paper reports that GSPO clips many more tokens (because entire responses are clipped) yet achieves better sample efficiency than GRPO. Intuitively, clipping discards gradient information. Can you reconcile this with PPO theory and explain why the token-level gradients in GRPO are so noisy that discarding more of them (via sequence-level clipping) can improve the signal-to-noise ratio and overall efficiency?
Answer:
Short answer: because GSPO trades quantity for quality. GRPO keeps many token-level gradient contributions but those per-token importance weights are intrinsically high‑variance (and often invalid as IS corrections), so the net gradient is noisy and can even destabilize learning. GSPO computes a single, length‑normalized sequence importance ratio (the geometric mean of per‑token ratios), clips whole sequences, and hence throws away many noisy sequences (and thus many tokens) but keeps a much lower‑variance, higher‑signal gradient from the sequences that remain. The resulting increase in signal‑to‑noise per used token yields better sample efficiency and stability.

Why this is true (intuition + math grounded in the paper). The paper gives the unclipped gradients (omitting expectation notation) for GSPO and GRPO:

GSPO:
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GSPO}}
= \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i\;\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
$$
where
$$
s_i(\theta)=\Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_i\mid x)}\Big)^{1/|y_i|}
= \exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log w_{i,t}\Big).
$$

GRPO:
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}
= \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \widehat A_i\;\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
$$
with token ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}$.

Two points follow directly.

1) Averaging in log-space reduces variance. GSPO’s $s_i$ is the geometric mean of the per‑token ratios (length‑normalized). If the random variables $\log w_{i,t}$ have variance $\sigma^2$, then by the usual averaging/CLT logic
$$
\operatorname{Var}\Big(\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t}\log w_{t}\Big) = \frac{\sigma^2}{|y|},
$$
so the variance of $\log s_i$ shrinks like $1/|y|$. By the delta method this makes the variance of $s_i$ much smaller than the variance of individual $w_{i,t}$. In short, sequence‑level weighting pools information across tokens and produces a far less volatile importance weight than per‑token ratios. Lower variance in the multiplier of the gradient directly improves signal‑to‑noise of the estimated policy gradient.

2) Token‑level ratios are single‑sample, high‑variance corrections and interact badly with clipping. Importance sampling is effective when the weight corrects an expectation by averaging over many samples; GRPO’s $w_{i,t}$ is based on one sample from each next‑token conditional, so it is a noisy estimator of any conditional correction. GRPO then multiplies each token’s gradient by its own noisy weight (and clips them separately). Because the advantage in GRPO is shared across a whole response, unequal, noisy per‑token weights create heteroskedastic, possibly sign‑conflicting contributions inside the same sequence. Clipping at token granularity can make this worse: different tokens inside a single sequence get clipped differently, skewing the net direction and amplifying variance. GSPO avoids this by applying one (smoothed) weight to the whole token sum, and by clipping whole responses: if a sequence’s averaged weight is too far off, discard that whole (high‑variance) sample rather than mixing many noisy partial contributions.

Why clipping more tokens can still be better. GSPO’s sequence clipping will reject entire responses whose sequence importance $s_i$ lies outside the clip window. That naturally removes many tokens at once, so the raw fraction of clipped tokens can be much larger than GRPO’s per‑token clipping fraction. But those discarded tokens would have been the biggest contributors to gradient variance (and potential instability) under GRPO. By throwing them away GSPO keeps fewer but much cleaner gradient terms; the per‑update variance drops and the remaining signal points in a more reliable direction. The bias introduced by clipping is the usual PPO bias/variance tradeoff, and here the variance reduction dominates — yielding better sample efficiency and stability (especially visible for long responses and for MoE models where per‑token likelihoods fluctuate due to routing volatility).

Finally, this explains the practical MoE gain the paper observes: routing/expert‑activation volatility makes token‑level likelihoods jump, invalidating $w_{i,t}$ or forcing expensive “Routing Replay.” Sequence‑level likelihoods (length‑normalized) are much less sensitive, so GSPO both avoids the noisy per‑token corrections and removes the need for routing replay, improving efficiency and stability.

In short: GSPO discards more tokens but the tokens it keeps produce a much higher signal‑to‑noise policy gradient because sequence‑level (geometric mean) importance weights dramatically reduce per‑sample variance and avoid the pathological effects of noisy, per‑token importance ratios.
