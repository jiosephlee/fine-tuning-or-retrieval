\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### How does groupwise $z$-scoring of rewards interact with off-policy correction?
Question:
Each response’s advantage is $\hat A_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \operatorname{mean}(\{r\})}{\operatorname{std}(\{r\})}$ computed within the group sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. Since this baseline and scale depend on the sampled group, does this introduce bias in the off-policy estimator with importance weights $s_i(\theta)$? Are there conditions (e.g., large group size $G$) under which the bias becomes negligible?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — computing advantages by z‑scoring inside each sampled group (i.e. using the group mean and group std) is a sample‑dependent transformation and it does introduce a finite‑sample bias into an importance‑weighted off‑policy estimator. The bias is controlled by the group size and by how the importance weight correlates with rewards; for the common “subtract the group mean” part you can write the bias in closed form and see it vanishes as $G$ grows, and a simple leave‑one‑out baseline removes that mean‑induced bias exactly. Division by the sample standard deviation (z‑scoring) introduces extra, higher‑order coupling (nonlinear) bias, but it also shrinks as the group size grows.

Explanation and key formula. Fix a query $x$ and write $r_i = r(x,y_i)$ and $s_i = s_i(\theta)$ for the importance/weight associated to sample $i$. Let the group mean be $\bar r = (1/G)\sum_{j} r_j$ and denote expectations under the behaviour sample distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ by $E[\cdot]$. Define
$$
\mu := E[r], \quad m_s := E[s],\quad m_{sr} := E[s\,r].
$$
A straight expectation computation (using iid sampling so off‑diagonal factors factorize) gives the exact identity for the mean‑subtracted estimator
$$
E\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i\,(r_i-\bar r)\right]
=\frac{G-1}{G}\,\bigl(m_{sr}-m_s\,\mu\bigr).
$$
Two immediate special cases are illuminating. If $s_i$ is the true sequence importance weight $w_i=\pi_\theta(y_i)/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i)$ then $m_s=1$ and $m_{sr}=E_{\pi_\theta}[r]$, so the expectation becomes
$$
\frac{G-1}{G}\bigl(E_{\pi_\theta}[r]-E_{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}[r]\bigr),
$$
i.e. a multiplicative factor $(G-1)/G$ on the quantity you would get with a population baseline. If instead $s$ and $r$ are uncorrelated under the behaviour distribution ($m_{sr}=m_s\mu$) the expectation is zero. The important point is the $(G-1)/G$ factor: for finite $G$ you get a bias of order $1/G$, and it disappears as $G\to\infty$.

Leave‑one‑out fix for the mean. If you use the leave‑one‑out mean baseline
$$
\bar r_{-i} = \frac{1}{G-1}\sum_{j\ne i} r_j
$$
and form advantages $r_i-\bar r_{-i}$, the analogous expectation becomes exactly $m_{sr}-m_s\mu$ (no $(G-1)/G$ prefactor). In particular when $s$ is the true importance ratio this removes the finite‑$G$ bias from the mean subtraction step exactly. So a simple practical option to eliminate the mean‑induced bias is to use leave‑one‑out centering.

What about division by sample standard deviation (z‑scoring)? Dividing by the group std couples all samples nonlinearly. A short heuristic with Taylor (delta‑method) expansion shows the extra bias introduced by replacing the population std $\sigma$ by the sample std $\hat\sigma$ is higher order in $1/G$ (the sample std has expectation error $O(1/G)$ when variance is estimated with denominator $G$; fluctuations are $O(1/\sqrt{G})$ but expectations of the nonlinear terms yield $O(1/G)$ corrections). In plain words: the standardization step adds more bias than mean subtraction, but that bias also shrinks with increasing $G$. Using leave‑one‑out mean and leave‑one‑out std (compute $\bar r_{-i},\sigma_{-i}$ excluding sample $i$) reduces the coupling and therefore reduces bias further, though the std still introduces a small nonlinear residual bias (std is biased as an estimator of the population $\sigma$ and the reciprocal is nonlinear).

Practical implications for GSPO (and PPO‑style surrogates). Two points matter for practice. First, GSPO already uses a surrogate (clipped) objective and a length‑normalized sequence weight $s_i(\theta)$ (not the plain un‑normalized importance weight); both design choices already make the training objective an approximation (i.e. biased relative to exact IS). Second, group normalization (z‑scoring) is a strong variance reducer and stabilizer in practice — the paper’s empirical claim is exactly that groupwise advantages give much more stable RL for large LMs/MoE. So the tradeoff is bias vs variance/stability: a small, controllable bias (decaying like $1/G$) is often an acceptable price for much lower variance and far better training behaviour.

Concrete recommendations
use leave‑one‑out centering (and, if you want, leave‑one‑out std) if you care about removing the mean‑induced finite‑$G$ bias; add a small epsilon to the std to avoid blowups when rewards are nearly identical; increase group size $G$ when possible (bias scales like $O(1/G)$); remember that clipping and length‑normalization of $s_i$ are already sources of approximation/bias, so small group‑normalization bias is typically negligible compared with those effects; finally, if you need an unbiased IS estimator for diagnostics (not optimization), compute expectations with true importance weights and population statistics or use very large $G$ / multiple independent groups.

Summary. Groupwise z‑scoring is not free: it introduces a finite‑sample bias because the baseline and scale are themselves random and correlated with the importance weights. For mean subtraction the bias has the simple closed form above and decays like $O(1/G)$, and leave‑one‑out centering removes that piece exactly. The std‑division step adds further (nonlinear) bias but it too shrinks as $G$ grows. In practice GSPO accepts these small approximations in exchange for much lower variance and markedly better stability.
