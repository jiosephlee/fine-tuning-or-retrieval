\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why not simply average or cap token-level ratios in GRPO to mimic GSPO?
Question:
If GRPO’s instability comes from unequal token weights $w_{i,t}$, why not replace them with a per-sequence constant (e.g., the arithmetic or geometric mean of $\{w_{i,t}\}_t$) so that all tokens within a response share the same weight? Would that not recover GSPO’s key stability property, or is there a subtle difference due to how gradients and clipping are applied?
Answer:
Short answer: you can only recover GSPO’s stability by replacing token-level ratios with the exact sequence-level importance ratio (or equivalently the geometric mean of token ratios computed from the full joint likelihood) and by moving clipping to the sequence level. Naively averaging (especially the arithmetic mean) or capping token ratios is not equivalent and will not reliably fix GRPO’s instability because it changes both the importance-sampling semantics and the gradient / clipping geometry that causes the high variance.

Why, in detail. GRPO uses token-level ratios
$$
w_{i,t}=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
$$
and its gradient (omitting clipping) has the form (paper, §3.2)
$$
\nabla\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}
=\mathbb{E}\Big[ \tfrac{1}{G}\sum_i \widehat A_i \cdot \tfrac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} w_{i,t}\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\Big].
$$
That is, each token gradient is scaled by its own token-ratio. GSPO instead uses the sequence likelihood ratio (length-normalized)
$$
s_i(\theta)=\Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Big)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log w_{i,t}\Big),
$$
and its gradient is
$$
\nabla\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}
=\mathbb{E}\Big[ \tfrac{1}{G}\sum_i s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i\cdot \tfrac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\Big].
$$
Two crucial differences follow.

First, arithmetic mean vs geometric mean. The GSPO weight is the geometric mean of the token ratios (the exponentiated average of log ratios). The arithmetic mean
$$
\bar w^{\text{arith}}_i=\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t w_{i,t}
$$
is generally not equal to $s_i$ and can be dominated by outlier token ratios (toy example: $w_1=100,w_2=0.01$ gives geometric mean $=1$ but arithmetic mean $\approx 50$). Using the arithmetic mean therefore produces a different (and generally biased) importance correction that is not derived from the correct sequence-level likelihood ratio required by importance sampling.

Second, gradient and clipping placement matter. GRPO’s instability comes from the per-token multiplicative noise: token-wise $w_{i,t}$ multiply token gradients unevenly and these per-token noises accumulate across long sequences. Simply "capping" or clipping token ratios inside the token sum (as GRPO does) is a different operation from computing a single sequence-level weight and clipping that. If you replace each $w_{i,t}$ with a per-sequence constant but keep tokenwise clipping or keep the arithmetic-mean definition, you still change the gradient geometry and the bias introduced by clipping. By contrast, GSPO’s $s_i$ is the correct sequence IS ratio (length-normalized), and GSPO explicitly clips at sequence level; this both matches the unit of reward and yields the stable gradient form above (all token gradients share the same multiplicative factor $s_i$).

A final practical point that the paper emphasizes: MoE routing volatility and single-sample token noise make token-level ratios highly unstable. Aggregating the log-probabilities into the sequence ratio (as GSPO does) smooths out per-token fluctuations and eliminates the pathological per-token weighting that caused catastrophic collapse in GRPO; ad-hoc averaging or token capping does not reliably remove that source of instability.

Consequence / prescription: if you want GRPO to behave like GSPO you must (a) compute the sequence log-likelihoods under both policies and form
$$
s_i=\exp\!\Big(\frac{\log\pi_\theta(y_i)-\log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i)}{|y_i|}\Big),
$$
(b) apply clipping to $s_i$ at sequence level, and (c) use that sequence factor to scale the summed token gradients (or use the GSPO-token construction from the paper if you need token-wise advantages while keeping stability). Naive arithmetic averaging or per-token capping does not buy you these theoretical and practical benefits.
