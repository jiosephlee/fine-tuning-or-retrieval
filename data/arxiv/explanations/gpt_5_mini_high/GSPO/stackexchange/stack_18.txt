\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Is GSPO connected to reward-weighted regression or preference-based objectives like DPO/IPO?
Question:
At a high level, GSPO scales gradients by a sequence-level weight and an advantage derived from rewards. Is there a formal connection to reward-weighted regression (e.g., maximizing $\mathbb{E}[r\,\log\pi_\theta]$) or to preference-based objectives such as DPO/IPO that also operate at the sequence level? Any equivalences or notable differences would help position GSPO among sequence-level training methods.
Answer:
Short answer: GSPO is related in spirit to reward‑weighted regression (RWR) and to sequence‑level preference methods (DPO/IPO) because all three scale likelihood updates by a sequence‑level scalar. However GSPO is not equivalent to either family: its objective is a PPO‑style, sequence‑level surrogate that (i) applies an explicit sequence importance correction, (ii) length‑normalizes that correction, (iii) standardizes rewards into group advantages, and (iv) applies clipping. These ingredients change both the bias and variance of the update compared with RWR or pairwise preference objectives, so GSPO should be viewed as a sequence‑level importance‑weighted policy surrogate rather than as RWR or DPO/IPO.

Mathematically the paper gives the unclipped GSPO objective (group and sequence sampling omitted for brevity) as
$$
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GSPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}\big[ s(\theta)\,\widehat A\big],
\qquad
s(\theta)=\Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y)}\Big)^{1/|y|},
$$
and its gradient (again unclipped) is
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GSPO}}
= \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}
\Big[ s(\theta)\,\widehat A \cdot \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)\Big].
$$
Compare that to common forms you asked about. Reward‑weighted regression (a weighted maximum‑likelihood objective) is typically written as
$$
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{RWR}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}\big[ r(y)\,\log\pi_\theta(y)\big],
$$
whose gradient is
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{RWR}}
= \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}\big[ r(y)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y)\big].
$$
The (on‑policy) policy gradient for expected reward is
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[r(y)]
= \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}\Big[\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y)}\,r(y)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y)\Big].
$$

From these formulas you can see the concrete relationships and differences. If the new policy is very close to the old one (so $s(\theta)\approx 1$), if you ignore the length normalization $1/|y|$, set $\widehat A$ equal to the raw reward $r$, and ignore clipping, then GSPO’s gradient has the same structural form as a reward‑weighted likelihood (RWR) / policy‑gradient update (both are expectations of a scalar times $\nabla\log\pi$). So locally (infinitesimal updates around $\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$) GSPO behaves like a weighted likelihood / policy‑gradient update. In practice GSPO deliberately departs from RWR / the exact policy gradient in three important ways:

1. GSPO inserts a tempered sequence importance ratio $s(\theta)=\big(\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}\big)^{1/|y|}$ (geometric mean of per‑token ratios) rather than the full sequence ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}$. This reduces variance and keeps ratios comparable across lengths, but it also biases the estimator relative to the exact importance‑sampling policy gradient.

2. GSPO uses a group standardized advantage $\widehat A=(r-\mathrm{mean})/\mathrm{std}$ (a zero‑mean, unit‑scale signal) instead of raw nonnegative reward weights that are typical in RWR. That changes the update direction and stabilizes training (it is analogous to a baseline/normalization in policy gradient).

3. GSPO applies PPO‑style clipping at the sequence level. Clipping produces a biased but lower‑variance and more stable update; it is crucial to GSPO’s empirical stability but makes the method different from plain RWR or unregularized pairwise objectives.

Preference‑based methods such as DPO/IPO are also sequence‑level in the sense that they operate on whole responses, but they are conceptually different: DPO/IPO turn preferences into supervised (usually pairwise) training signals. A generic pairwise preference objective has the form
$$
\mathbb{E}_{(y^+,y^-)}\big[ \log\sigma( s_\theta(y^+)-s_\theta(y^-) ) \big],
$$
with $s_\theta$ typically a scoring function (often related to $\log\pi_\theta$). The gradient of this objective is a difference of two score‑gradients weighted by a logistic factor, so the update explicitly pushes preferred responses up relative to dispreferred ones. GSPO does not operate on pairwise classification; instead it produces a scalar advantage per sequence (from a group), multiplies that by a sequence‑level importance correction, and uses a surrogate objective with clipping. In the special case $G=2$ and with simple standardization, the sign of GSPO’s advantage will push the higher‑reward item up and the lower down, so behaviorally the two families both prefer high‑reward sequences. But their weighting functions, regularization, and bias/variance tradeoffs are different: preference losses typically avoid importance sampling and clipping but solve a different supervised problem (pairwise ranking), while GSPO is explicitly constructed as a stable off‑policy surrogate for policy optimization with sequence‑level importance correction.

Conclusion: GSPO is best read as a sequence‑level, importance‑weighted PPO surrogate that shares superficial structure with RWR (both scale $\nabla\log\pi$ by a scalar per sequence) and with preference methods (both operate at sequence granularity), but it is not formally equivalent to RWR or to DPO/IPO except in limiting or informal senses. The key practical distinctions are the tempered sequence importance ratio, length normalization, group advantage standardization, and sequence‑level clipping — these are precisely the mechanisms the paper argues give GSPO its improved stability and empirical performance on large LLM / MoE RL problems.
