\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why raise the sequence importance ratio to the power $1/|y|$ (geometric mean of token ratios)?
Question:
GSPO defines $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|} = \exp\left(\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right)$. I see that this is the geometric mean of per-token ratios. Does using the geometric mean change the fixed point compared to using the raw ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)}$? Can you quantify how this normalization affects bias/variance and why it improves numerical stability without altering the optimization target?
Answer:
Short answer: raising the sequence importance ratio to the power $1/|y|$ (i.e. using the geometric mean of token ratios) is a length-normalization step that dramatically reduces variance and keeps the importance weight on a unified scale across response lengths. It does not change the local (first‑order) learning signal at the current policy (so small, trust‑region updates behave the same), but it does change the global surrogate objective (so stationary points away from the current policy can differ). In practice this tradeoff—small bias for much lower variance and better numerical stability—is exactly what GSPO exploits.

Why the geometric mean is natural and numerically stable. The sequence likelihood ratio is
$$
r(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|} w_t(\theta),
\qquad w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}.
$$
GSPO uses
$$
s(\theta)=r(\theta)^{1/|y|}
=\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log w_t(\theta)\Big),
$$
so $\log s$ is the arithmetic mean of the per‑token log‑ratios. Because the sequence ratio multiplies token ratios, a few token changes can blow up $r$ exponentially with $|y|$; working in log‑space and averaging per token keeps the weight in a stable, length‑aware range.

Variance reduction (quantified). Let $Z_t=\log w_t(\theta)$ and (for intuition) suppose the $Z_t$ have finite variance $\sigma^2$ and are weakly dependent or roughly iid. Then
$$
\log r = \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} Z_t,\qquad \log s=\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} Z_t.
$$
Thus
$$
\mathrm{Var}(\log r)\approx |y|\,\sigma^2,\qquad
\mathrm{Var}(\log s)\approx \frac{\sigma^2}{|y|}.
$$
The standard deviation of $\log s$ is smaller than that of $\log r$ by a factor $|y|$. In other words, raw sequence log‑ratios spread out like $\sqrt{|y|}$, while the geometric mean concentrates like $1/\sqrt{|y|}$. This huge reduction in dispersion is why numerical stability and clipping behave sensibly across different lengths (you do not need exponentially different clipping ranges for different $|y|$).

Effect on bias and fixed points. Replacing $r$ by $s$ does change the surrogate objective: GSPO optimizes expectations weighted by $s$ rather than by the true importance weight $r$, so it is not an unbiased estimator of $ \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[f] $ in the strict importance‑sampling sense. However two important mitigating facts make this acceptable:

1. Monotonicity and ordering: $s=r^{1/|y|}$ is strictly increasing in $r$ (for fixed $|y|$), so high‑probability sequences under $\pi_\theta$ remain high under $s$ and vice versa. Thus sample ranking is preserved (within a given length), which helps clipping and selection behave sensibly.

2. Local (first‑order) equivalence at the current policy: evaluate the gradients given in the paper at $\theta=\theta_{\text{old}}$. For both the token‑level GRPO surrogate and the GSPO surrogate the importance multipliers become 1 at the old policy, so the two gradients coincide at that point:
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}\big|_{\theta=\theta_{\text{old}}}
=\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}\big|_{\theta=\theta_{\text{old}}}
= \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_i \frac{\widehat A_i}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\Big|_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\right].
$$
So in the small‑step / trust‑region regime (which PPO‑style clipping is designed to enforce) GSPO and a raw sequence‑ratio objective give the same local update direction; the bias from using $s$ is higher‑order in the step size and therefore small when updates are proximal.

Intuition about the tradeoff. Raw sequence ratio $r$ is the correct multiplicative IS weight if you wanted an unbiased reweighting of expectations under $\pi_\theta$, but it is extremely high‑variance for long responses (a single token with a tiny probability under the old policy can drive $r$ to near zero or huge). GSPO’s $s$ is a variance‑reducing, length‑normalized surrogate: it compresses outliers, makes clipping thresholds interpretable across lengths, and prevents a few token likelihood changes (or expert‑activation volatility in MoE) from dominating the gradient and causing catastrophic instability. The price is a bias in the surrogate; in practice that bias is small because PPO/GRPO already work with clipped, biased surrogates and because updates are kept small.

A simple numeric illustration: if every token ratio were $1.5$ and $|y|=50$, then $r=1.5^{50}\approx 8\times10^8$ (explosion!), while $s=1.5^{50/50}=1.5$. Clipping and gradient computation with $s$ are numerically sane; with $r$ they'd be catastrophic unless you used length‑dependent clipping ranges.

Conclusion. Using the geometric mean is primarily a variance‑reduction and normalization device that (i) keeps importance weights on a unified scale across lengths, (ii) concentrates the distribution of log‑weights (CLT/delta‑method intuition), and (iii) preserves the local first‑order learning signal at the current policy. It does change the global surrogate objective (so it’s not the exact IS correction), but that bias is small in the trust‑region regime and, as the paper shows empirically, the variance/stability benefits are decisive for large models and long responses.
