\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Deriving GSPO from importance sampling: what target expectation is being estimated?
Question:
GSPO optimizes $\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)}[\min(s_i(\theta)\hat A_i, \operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\hat A_i)]$ with $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}$. If we start from the desired on-policy objective $\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal D, y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[A(x,y)]$ (with groupwise normalized advantages), what exact importance-sampling steps lead to the unclipped GSPO surrogate $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}[s_i(\theta) A(x,y)]$? Is the $1/|y|$ exponent merely a variance-reduction heuristic or part of a principled derivation?
Answer:
Short answer: GSPO’s unclipped surrogate comes from the usual importance-sampling rewrite of the on‑policy objective, but then the authors replace the correct sequence importance weight
$$
R(y)\;=\;\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}
$$
by its per‑token geometric mean
$$
s(y)\;=\;R(y)^{1/|y|} \;=\;\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid\cdot)}\Big).
$$
That replacement yields the unclipped GSPO surrogate
$$
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal D,\;y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}}\big[s(y)\,A(x,y)\big],
$$
but it is not an unbiased importance-sampling estimator of the original on‑policy objective (the unbiased IS estimator would use weight $R(y)$). The $1/|y|$ exponent is therefore a deliberate length‑normalization / variance‑reduction design choice, not a step forced by the basic IS identity.

Derivation and the gradient-level difference (short):
Start from the on‑policy objective
$$
J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal D,\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[A(x,y)].
$$
Standard importance sampling (rewriting the target expectation as an expectation under the behavior policy) gives the unbiased form
$$
J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\big[R(y)\,A(x,y)\big],\qquad R(y)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}.
$$
GSPO instead uses the surrogate weight $s(y)=R(y)^{1/|y|}$ and optimizes
$$
J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\big[s(y)\,A(x,y)\big].
$$
Taking gradients (unclipped) gives the form in the paper:
$$
\nabla_\theta J_{\text{GSPO}}=\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\text{old}}}\Big[s(y)A(x,y)\nabla_\theta\log s(y)\Big]
=\mathbb{E}\Big[s(y)A(x,y)\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\Big].
$$
Compare to the true IS gradient
$$
\nabla_\theta J=\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\text{old}}}\Big[R(y)A(x,y)\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\Big].
$$
Thus the GSPO update differs from the unbiased IS update by the multiplicative factor
$$
\frac{s(y)/( |y| )}{R(y)}=\frac{R(y)^{1/|y|-1}}{|y|},
$$
so GSPO is a biased surrogate (the bias is intentional: it changes how much different sampled sequences influence the gradient).

Why that particular exponent? Intuition and motivation
- Numerical stability and variance: $R(y)$ is a product of per‑token ratios and therefore can explode or vanish exponentially with sequence length. Taking the geometric mean (the $1/|y|$ root) converts a product into a per‑token scale (it is the exponentiation of the mean log‑ratio), keeping importance weights near a per‑token multiplicative change instead of an exponentially large/small number. This makes a single clipping range feasible across varying lengths and dramatically reduces variance in practice — exactly the motivation stated in the paper (“length normalization … to reduce the variance and to control $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range”).
- Bias–variance tradeoff: raising the weight to a power $<1$ is a standard stabilizing/tempering idea (it reduces spread of weights at the cost of bias). GSPO’s choice $\alpha=1/|y|$ makes the tempering adapt to sequence length (stronger shrinkage for longer sequences).
- Algorithmic consequence: because GSPO uses the sequence-level geometric‑mean weight and then averages token gradients (the $1/|y|$ factor inside the gradient), all tokens in a sequence are weighted equally by the same sequence scalar — this removes the high variance introduced by token‑level importance ratios used in GRPO, which is the explicit design goal of GSPO.

A few practical notes consistent with the paper
- Clipping and the token‑variant: the clipping step and the GSPO‑token construction further modify the surrogate and its gradients; clipping introduces additional bias (as in PPO), and the GSPO‑token trick is a way to keep the numerical weight equal across tokens while allowing per‑token advantages. When token advantages are equal, GSPO and GSPO‑token are numerically identical.
- If you want an unbiased estimator of the on‑policy objective you must keep the full sequence weight $R(y)$ (or use unbiased per‑decision IS variants). In large‑language‑model RL the variance of that unbiased estimator is typically prohibitive, which is why the paper adopts the biased but lower‑variance GSPO surrogate and empirically finds much more stable training (especially for long responses and MoE models).

Conclusion: the unclipped GSPO surrogate is obtained by first applying the usual IS identity and then replacing the exact sequence IS weight by its geometric mean (the $1/|y|$ root). That exponent is a pragmatic length‑normalization / variance‑reduction design choice (a tempered/normalized IS weight), not a consequence of the unbiased IS equality.
