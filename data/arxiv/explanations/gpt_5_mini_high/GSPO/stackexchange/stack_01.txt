\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### What exactly makes GRPO "ill-posed"—bias, variance, or unit mismatch?
Question:
The paper says GRPO is ill-posed because it applies token-level importance weights $w_{i,t} = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$ while rewards are given at the sequence level. Can you make precise what "ill-posed" means here? Is the gradient estimator biased, does its variance explode with sequence length $|y|$, or is it a mismatch between the unit of reward (sequence) and the unit of optimization (token) that breaks an assumption? A small counterexample or formal statement would help.
Answer:
Short answer: the root cause is a unit mismatch — GRPO applies single-step (token-level) importance ratios to a sequence-level reward. That mismatch invalidates the usual importance-sampling correction and therefore produces a gradient estimator that is not the correct (sequence-level) importance-sampled gradient. Concretely this produces a biased estimator (relative to the sequence-level objective) in general, and it also exposes you to much larger, length-dependent variance and pathological behavior when token ratios are noisy (as in MoE). Clipping on token ratios makes the bias worse. Below I make this precise with equations and a tiny counterexample.

Why GRPO is wrong in one equation (no clipping)
- The true sequence objective is
  $$ J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal D,\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}[r(x,y)]
  =\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}\,r(x,y)\right]. $$
  Its importance-sampled REINFORCE-style gradient (expand $ \nabla\log\pi_\theta(y|x)=\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}) $) is
  $$ \nabla_\theta J(\theta)
  =\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\left[\Big(\prod_{s=1}^{|y|} w_s\Big)\,r(x,y)\,\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\right], $$
  where $ w_t=\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})} $ and $ \prod_s w_s=\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)} $.

- GRPO (without clipping) uses token-level ratios inside the sum:
  $$ \nabla_\theta J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)
  =\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\left[\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
  w_t\,\widehat A(y)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\right], $$
  with $ \widehat A(y) $ the (sequence) advantage shared across tokens.

- Subtracting gives the exact gap (no clipping):
  $$ \nabla_\theta J(\theta)-\nabla_\theta J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)
  =\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
  \Big(\prod_{s=1}^{|y|}w_s-\frac{1}{|y|}w_t\Big)\,r(x,y)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\right]. $$
  This difference is generally nonzero for $|y|>1$. So GRPO does not estimate the correct sequence-level importance-sampled gradient: it is biased with respect to the true sequence objective.

A minimal numeric counterexample
Take $|y|=2$, independent tokens under $ \pi_{\text{old}} $ with $ \pi_{\text{old}}(y_t=\text{``good''})=0.5 $. Suppose only $ y=(\text{good},\text{good}) $ has reward 1. Pick $ \pi_\theta(y_1=\text{good})=0.6 $ and $ \pi_\theta(y_2=\text{good})=0.8 $. Then for that rewarded sequence
- sequence ratio $= (0.6/0.5)\cdot(0.8/0.5)=1.2\cdot1.6=1.92$,
- GRPO token-average ratio $=(1.2+1.6)/2=1.4$.

If the two token gradients happen to be equal (say each contributes “1”), the correct IS-weighted contribution to the gradient is $ \approx 1.92\times 2 $ (because the sum over two tokens), while GRPO gives $ \approx 1.4\times 1 $ (because of the $1/|y|$ averaging). They differ substantially — so GRPO is optimizing a different objective.

Intuition about variance and why it gets worse with length
- Product vs single-step noise. The correct sequence IS weight is the product $ \prod_t w_t $. If the per-token log-ratios $ \delta_t=\log w_t $ are (roughly) independent with variance $ \sigma^2 $, then $ \log\prod_t w_t=\sum_t\delta_t $ has variance $ T\sigma^2 $. Thus the product has multiplicative spread $ \mathrm{Var}(\prod_t w_t)\approx e^{T\sigma^2}-1 $, which explodes with $ T $. This is the usual curse of importance sampling for long sequences.
- Why GRPO is still bad: GRPO does not form the product, but it uses single-step ratios $ w_t $ (which themselves can be very noisy in MoE because routing or small likelihood changes give huge per-token swings). Those per-token spikes act like high-frequency noise on the gradient and, because GRPO mixes differently across tokens (unequal weights within a sequence), these noisy contributions do not cancel in expectation and can accumulate into unstable updates. GSPO’s sequence-level weighting (and length-normalization) aggregates the per-token log-ratios first (the geometric mean) and therefore smooths out token-level extremes: $ s=\exp\big(\frac1T\sum_t\delta_t\big) $ has variance $ \approx e^{\sigma^2/T}-1 $, which shrinks with $ T $. That is why the paper argues GSPO is numerically far more stable.

Role of clipping and MoE routing volatility
Clipping is a nonlinear operation, so it destroys even more of the importance-sampling identity and introduces additional bias that depends on the sign/magnitude of advantages. With token-level clipping you can systematically under- or over-weight tokens depending on stochastic per-token ratios; small, frequent clipping-induced biases can compound through SGD and lead to catastrophic collapse. In MoE models the per-token ratio noise (from routing changes) is especially large, so token-level clipping is particularly harmful. The paper’s empirical observation that GRPO needed Routing Replay to stabilize MoE training fits exactly this diagnosis.

When would GRPO be valid?
GRPO would coincide with the correct sequence gradient in special cases: $|y|=1$ (trivial), or if the reward/advantage decomposes exactly per-token so that a token-level IS correction is the right correction, or if the conditional expectations of the product of the other token ratios equal 1 in the relevant weighted sense (a very strong and typically false condition). In practice for language-model RL (one sample per prefix, sequence rewards) those conditions do not hold.

Bottom line
“Ill-posed” in the paper means the algorithm violates the basic unit-consistency required by importance sampling: you must correct at the same unit that carries the reward. By applying single-token importance weights to whole-sequence rewards, GRPO is not performing valid sequence-level importance sampling. That produces a gradient estimator that is generally biased (it does not equal the sequence-level IS gradient), suffers from amplified instability/noise as responses get longer (and from MoE routing volatility), and is further corrupted by token-level clipping. GSPO fixes the unit mismatch by forming sequence-level likelihood ratios (with length normalization) and so aligns the importance weights with the reward unit, which reduces pathological variance and the clipping-induced damage observed under GRPO.
