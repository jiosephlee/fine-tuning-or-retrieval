\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Can you quantify the variance reduction from token-wise ratios to sequence-wise ratios?
Question:
Is there an analysis comparing $\operatorname{Var}\left[\sum_t w_t \nabla\log\pi(y_t\mid\cdot)\right]$ versus $\operatorname{Var}\left[s\,\tfrac{1}{|y|}\sum_t \nabla\log\pi(y_t\mid\cdot)\right]$, where $w_t$ are per-token ratios and $s$ is the sequence-level geometric-mean ratio? Any bounds or empirical measurements showing how variance scales with $|y|$ in GRPO vs GSPO would clarify the claimed stabilization.
Answer:
Short answer: yes — you can quantify the variance reduction analytically under mild simplifying assumptions. Intuitively GSPO replaces a tokenwise arithmetic mixture of multiplicative importance weights by a single geometric-mean weight applied to the averaged token gradients; averaging in the log-domain (the geometric mean) compresses extremes and therefore reduces the second moment of the weight that multiplies the gradient. Below I give a short derivation (from the paper's gradient formulas), a clean toy closed form under Gaussian assumptions, the key takeaway inequality, and how you can check this empirically.

Start from the paper's gradient forms (clipping and the common advantage factor $A$ omitted for clarity). For a single response $y$ (drop the group index) define token gradients $g_t \equiv \nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)$ and token log-ratios $u_t\equiv\log w_t=\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)-\log\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\mid\cdot)$. GRPO uses
$$
G_{\text{GR}}=\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} w_t g_t
\quad\text{with}\quad w_t=e^{u_t},
$$
whereas GSPO uses
$$
G_{\text{GS}}=s\cdot\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} g_t
\quad\text{with}\quad s=\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t} u_t\Big)=e^{\bar u}.
$$

Assume (to get a clean baseline comparison) that the token gradients have zero mean and identical variance $\operatorname{Var}(g_t)=\sigma_g^2$, that token pairs are uncorrelated across positions, and that the $u_t$ are i.i.d. and independent of the $g_t$ (these are the usual simplifying assumptions for first-pass variance analysis; I state caveats below). Then
$$
\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GR}}]
=\frac{1}{|y|^2}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\operatorname{Var}(w_t g_t)
=\frac{\sigma_g^2}{|y|}\,\mathbb{E}[e^{2u}],
$$
while
$$
\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GS}}]
=\operatorname{Var}\!\Big(s\cdot\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_t g_t\Big)
= \mathbb{E}[s^2]\cdot\frac{\sigma_g^2}{|y|}
= \frac{\sigma_g^2}{|y|}\,\mathbb{E}\!\big[e^{2\bar u}\big].
$$
So both leading scalings contain the $1/|y|$ factor coming from averaging the $g_t$, but the multiplicative prefactors differ: GRPO has $\mathbb{E}[e^{2u}]$ while GSPO has $\mathbb{E}[e^{2\bar u}]$ where $\bar u=(1/|y|)\sum_t u_t$.

You can make the comparison explicit via moment-generating (cumulant) functions. Let $K(t)=\log\mathbb{E}[e^{t u}]$ be the log-MGF of $u$. Then
$$
\mathbb{E}[e^{2u}]=e^{K(2)},\qquad
\mathbb{E}[e^{2\bar u}]=e^{\,|y|K(2/|y|)}.
$$
Thus the variance ratio is
$$
\frac{\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GR}}]}{\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GS}}]}
=\frac{e^{K(2)}}{e^{|y|K(2/|y|)}}=e^{\,K(2)-|y|K(2/|y|)}.
$$
For small-to-moderate fluctuations of $u$ (expand $K$ to second order, assuming mean zero for simplicity), $K(t)\approx\frac{\sigma_u^2}{2}t^2$, so
$$
\frac{\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GR}}]}{\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GS}}]}
\approx\exp\!\big(2\sigma_u^2(1-\tfrac{1}{|y|})\big).
$$
For large sequence length $|y|$ this ratio approaches $\exp(2\sigma_u^2)$. In a Gaussian toy model ($u_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_u^2)$, independent of $g_t$) this approximation is exact for the second moments and therefore produces the closed forms
$$
\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GR}}]\approx\frac{\sigma_g^2}{|y|}e^{2\sigma_u^2},\qquad
\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GS}}]\approx\frac{\sigma_g^2}{|y|}e^{2\sigma_u^2/|y|}.
$$
Numerically this can be large: if $\sigma_u=1$ then $\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GR}}]/\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GS}}]\approx e^{2}\approx 7.4$; if $\sigma_u=1.5$ the ratio is $\approx e^{4.5}\approx 90$.

The intuition is simple and matches the paper's qualitative claim: GRPO effectively averages the exponentials $w_t=e^{u_t}$ (an arithmetic mean of exponentials), while GSPO exponentiates the mean log-ratio (the geometric mean). Because the exponential is convex, arithmetic averaging of exponentials has a larger second moment than exponentiating the arithmetic mean of logs; formally (under independence) H ölder / Jensen-type inequalities give $\mathbb{E}[e^{2\bar u}]\le \mathbb{E}[e^{2u}]$, so the GSPO prefactor is always no larger than the GRPO prefactor in this i.i.d. setting. Put another way: averaging in the log-domain compresses multiplicative noise.

A few important caveats and extensions relevant to practice. First, the derivation above ignores clipping, correlations across token positions, and dependence between $u_t$ and $g_t$. Positive correlations among the $u_t$ (e.g. MoE routing changes that shift many token likelihoods together) increase the covariance terms in $\operatorname{Var}[G_{\text{GR}}]$ and can make GRPO even worse than the i.i.d. calculation suggests; GSPO is usually more robust to such intra-sequence correlations because it collapses those correlated fluctuations into the single scalar $\bar u$. Second, heavy tails in the $u_t$ distribution blow up $\mathbb{E}[e^{2u}]$ and so penalize GRPO much more severely than GSPO. Third, clipping interacts with the variance/bias tradeoff: token-level clipping can remove wildly-weighted tokens but at the cost of inconsistent token-level bias, whereas sequence-level clipping (GSPO) excludes whole sequences and better aligns with a sequence reward; the paper reports empirically that GSPO actually clips a much larger fraction of individual tokens but still trains better (their Figure on clipping fractions), which is consistent with the above variance rationale.

If you want empirical confirmation on your model, a straightforward experiment is to fix $\theta$ and $\theta_{\text{old}}$, sample many responses $y$ from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ at different lengths, compute the two sequence-level gradient surrogates
$$
\hat G_{\text{GR}}(y)=\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_t w_t g_t,\qquad
\hat G_{\text{GS}}(y)=s\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_t g_t,
$$
project them onto a fixed random direction (or take squared norms) and estimate sample variances across the draws as a function of $|y|$. Also compute $\operatorname{Var}(\log w_t)$ across the same samples; plugging that empirical $\sigma_u^2$ into the Gaussian toy formula above gives a rough prediction for the variance ratio to compare with measurements. The paper shows stability and clipping-fraction empirical evidence (their training curves and clipping-figure) but does not publish raw gradient-variance numbers; the procedure above will give you direct confirmation and a quantitative match (or not) to the simple theory.

Takeaway: under the realistic condition that per-token log-importance ratios fluctuate (and especially if they have nontrivial variance or are correlated), GSPO’s sequence-level geometric-mean weighting substantially reduces the multiplicative second moment of the importance weight and therefore reduces gradient variance relative to GRPO. In simple i.i.d. models the reduction in variance is approximately multiplicative by $\exp(2\operatorname{Var}(\log w_t))$ (for large $|y|$), and in more realistic correlated/heavy-tailed regimes GSPO is typically even more robust — which is precisely the stabilization effect the paper documents empirically.
