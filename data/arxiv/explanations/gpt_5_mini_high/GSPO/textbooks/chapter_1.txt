\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 1: Motivation and Overview: Why Stable RL for LLMs Needs GSPO

# RL as a driver of advanced capabilities in LLMs

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a central tool for advancing the capabilities of large language models. In the context of language generation, we treat an autoregressive language model parameterized by $\theta$ as a policy $\pi_\theta$, which maps a prompt or query $x$ to a distribution over response sequences $y$. A verifier or evaluator assigns a scalar reward $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$ to a query–response pair. By directly optimizing the model to increase such verifier-defined rewards, RL enables models to develop behaviors that go beyond what supervised learning on next-token prediction alone can achieve. Concretely, the paper describes how large-scale RL allows language models to tackle sophisticated problems — for example, competition-level mathematics and programming — by enabling deeper and longer reasoning processes than those learned from supervised corpora alone.

This role of RL is not just a conceptual advantage but an operational one: RL trains the policy using samples of full responses, scoring them by a task-specific verifier and then updating the policy to favor higher-reward responses. That paradigm naturally aligns training with downstream evaluation objectives (for instance, correctness on math problems or passing unit tests for code). The formulation used throughout this work treats sequence generation as a decision process where the unit of final evaluation (the sequence) is explicitly used to shape learning.

# Stability as the prerequisite for scaling RL compute

When we attempt to scale RL training — larger models, longer responses, sparser architectures such as Mixture-of-Experts (MoE), and much greater computational budgets — stability becomes the single most important engineering and algorithmic requirement. The paper emphasizes that to “successfully scale RL with greater computational investment, the foremost prerequisite is maintaining stable and robust training dynamics.” If training is fragile, more compute does not translate into better models: instabilities can lead to catastrophic, sometimes irreversible, model collapse that wastes compute and prevents further progress.

Two practical drivers of instability arise immediately as we scale. First, maximizing hardware utilization pushes practitioners to generate very large rollout batches and then partition those batches into multiple mini-batches for gradient updates. That partitioning creates an off-policy learning setting: many gradient updates are performed using samples drawn from an older policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ rather than the current policy $\pi_\theta$ being optimized. Second, commonly used algorithms must therefore include mechanisms that limit how “off-policy” a sample can be when used for updating. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a widely used approach that implements such a constraint via clipping. In the paper PPO is written (omitting KL regularization) as

$$
\mathcal{J}_\text{PPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\, y\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x)}
\left[\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
\min\bigl(w_t(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_t,\ \mathrm{clip}\bigl(w_t(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat{A}_t\bigr)
\right],
$$

where the token-level importance ratio is $w_t(\theta)=\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}$ and $\widehat{A}_t$ is a per-token advantage estimated by a value model. This formulation highlights two practical difficulties: PPO relies heavily on a value model (which is costly to train and scale, and whose errors can destabilize training), and it applies off-policy correction at the token level even when the reward is defined at the sequence level.

Because of those practical burdens, prior work (and the paper) considers alternatives that avoid a large value model. One such alternative is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which replaces the value model by computing a relative advantage among a group of $G$ responses sampled for the same query. GRPO therefore avoids per-token value estimation, but as we will explain next, it introduces a different stability problem when applied at scale.

# Empirical instability and collapse with GRPO for large/long outputs

GRPO defines a group-based objective in which, for each query $x$, a set of $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ is sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x)$. Each response $y_i$ inherits the same normalized group advantage

$$
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)},
$$

and GRPO applies PPO-style clipping at the token level to obtain

$$
\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\bigr)\right],
$$

with token-level importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)=\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$. Empirically, however, applying importance sampling at the token level like this produces severe instability when models, responses, or groups grow large.

The core theoretical intuition behind the failure is a misapplication of importance sampling. The basic importance sampling identity is

$$
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)]=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_\text{beh}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}\,f(z)\right],
$$

which relies on reweighting many samples from the behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$ to estimate an expectation under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$. Crucially, importance sampling corrects a distributional mismatch by averaging over multiple draws from the behavior distribution; the reweighting is meaningful because the same $z$ could have been drawn from many possibilities under the behavior policy.

GRPO, however, applies an importance ratio at each token position $t$ based on a single observed token $y_{i,t}$ sampled from the one-step next-token distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x,y_{i,<t})$. That single sample is insufficient for the importance ratio to play the intended role of distribution correction; instead, it becomes a multiplicative noise term in the gradient. When the gradient includes one such noisy multiplicative factor per token, the noise accumulates across token positions and across long responses, producing very high variance updates. Moreover, the clipping mechanism — intended to prevent extremely off-policy updates — interacts with these noisy token-level weights in complex ways that empirically amplify instability rather than tame it. The paper reports that this leads to catastrophic and often irreversible model collapse in large-scale training runs; once the model collapses under GRPO, resuming training from a checkpoint and carefully tuning hyperparameters frequently does not recover the original performance.

This empirical failure suggests a simple but powerful principle: the unit of off-policy correction and clipping should match the unit of reward. If rewards are assigned to complete responses (sequences), then importance correction and clipping should also be applied at the sequence level. Token-level correction can be invalid and harmful when the reward is sequence-scoped.

# GSPO’s core idea: sequence-level importance weighting and clipping

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) implements that principle. Instead of computing and clipping importance ratios at every token, GSPO defines a single sequence-level importance ratio for each sampled response $y_i$ and applies clipping and optimization at that sequence level. The GSPO objective is

$$
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\min\bigl(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\ \mathrm{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat{A}_i\bigr)\right],
$$

using the same group-normalized advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ as in GRPO, but with

$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
$$

There are three closely related motivations that make this choice sensible.

First, $s_i(\theta)$ has a clear importance-sampling interpretation at the level of whole sequences: it quantifies how much the sampled response $y_i$ (drawn from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$) is reweighted under $\pi_\theta$. Because the unit of reward is the sequence, applying importance correction at that same unit is theoretically coherent.

Second, GSPO applies length-normalization by raising the full-sequence likelihood ratio to the power $1/|y_i|$. Length normalization reduces variance across responses of different lengths and keeps the numerical range of importance ratios more consistent; without this normalization a small change in a few tokens could inflate or deflate the full-sequence ratio dramatically, and clipping thresholds would need to depend strongly on length.

Third, in its gradient effect GSPO avoids the unstable pattern that GRPO introduces. Dropping clipping for the purpose of analysis, the gradient of the GSPO objective can be written as

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G 
\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}\widehat{A}_i
\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
$$

By comparison, the GRPO gradient (again ignoring clipping) weights each token's score-gradient by the individual token importance ratio:

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \widehat{A}_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
$$

The practical difference is that GSPO multiplies the entire token-gradient sum by a single sequence-level factor $s_i(\theta)$, thereby treating the tokens inside a response uniformly with respect to the importance correction. GRPO, by contrast, multiplies each token gradient by a potentially different token-level ratio. Those unequal per-token multipliers can vary stochastically and accumulate adverse variance across time steps; GSPO removes that source of instability by using the sequence ratio as a coherent scalar multiplier.

GSPO also admits a variant called GSPO-token for cases where one does want to assign different advantages to different tokens (for example, in some multi-turn settings). GSPO-token keeps the sequence-level importance factor as a detached scalar that scales token-level objectives, allowing token-wise advantage customization while retaining the stable sequence-level correction. When all token advantages within a response are identical, GSPO-token is numerically equivalent to GSPO; the tokenized variant simply provides extra flexibility without reintroducing the variance issues of GRPO.

One striking empirical observation reported in the paper is that GSPO actually clips entire responses much more often (a larger fraction of tokens end up inside clipped responses) than GRPO clips tokens, yet GSPO still achieves better training efficiency. This counter-intuitive result supports the interpretation that GRPO’s token-level corrections give noisy gradients that are costly to exploit, while GSPO’s fewer-but-more-coherent gradient signals are more effective even if fewer tokens survive the clipping filter.

# Contributions: stability, efficiency, MoE robustness, infrastructure simplification

The paper makes several interlocking contributions grounded in the sequence-level view just described.

First and foremost, GSPO provides a stable and scalable RL algorithm for training large language models. By aligning the unit of off-policy correction with the unit of reward (the full response), GSPO removes a core source of high-variance noise present in GRPO and similar token-level schemes. This stability is essential to realize the benefits of continued RL compute and larger models.

Second, GSPO improves sample and training efficiency. In experiments reported in the paper, GSPO delivers superior training reward curves and downstream benchmark performance compared with a carefully tuned GRPO baseline under the same compute and consumed queries. The combination of sequence-level weighting, group-normalized advantages, and sequence clipping yields a more reliable optimization signal that translates into better learning per unit of compute.

Third, GSPO materially improves the robustness of RL training for sparse architectures such as Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. MoE training introduces an additional source of instability called expert-activation volatility: after a gradient update the subset of experts used to compute a particular token probability can change, which makes token-level likelihoods and thus token-level importance ratios fluctuate dramatically. Under GRPO this volatility can prevent convergence and forces engineering workarounds such as Routing Replay (caching routing decisions from the old policy and replaying them when computing importance ratios). By relying on sequence-level likelihoods, GSPO is far less sensitive to which experts are routed at individual token steps; empirically, GSPO removes the need for Routing Replay, thereby simplifying MoE RL training and allowing the model to use its full capacity without routing-mode constraints.

Finally, GSPO simplifies RL infrastructure in practice. Engineering pipelines often compute likelihoods using high-throughput inference engines (e.g., for large rollouts) and then recompute them with training engines to avoid numerical precision discrepancies. Because GSPO uses only full-sequence likelihoods and is empirically more tolerant of small numerical differences, it makes it possible in many settings to directly use inference-engine likelihoods for optimization, reducing recomputation overhead. This is particularly useful in partial-rollout, multi-turn RL, or disaggregated training–inference deployments.

Taken together, these contributions are not just incremental; they change the operational calculus of large-scale RL for language models. GSPO provides a principled, empirically validated alternative to token-level importance weighting that is better matched to sequence-scoped rewards and the practical realities of scaling models, response lengths, and sparse architectures. The paper reports that GSPO’s properties played a substantive role in the improvements observed in the Qwen3 family of models.

# Roadmap: how this chapter fits into the full presentation

This chapter explained why stability is necessary to scale RL for large language models and motivated the shift from token-level to sequence-level importance correction. The rest of the paper (and the textbook chapters that follow) unpack these ideas in detail:

- Preliminaries: formal notation for policies, sequence likelihoods, and reward functions; a brief review of PPO and the GRPO formulation used as the baseline.
- Motivation: a careful analysis of off-policy sampling, importance sampling, and why token-level importance ratios are invalid in the setting of sampled next-token observations; empirical observations that motivated the change.
- Algorithm: the GSPO objective and its sequence-level importance ratio, length normalization, clipping strategy, and the GSPO-token variant; gradient-level analysis contrasting GSPO and GRPO.
- Experiments and Discussion: empirical comparisons of GSPO and GRPO on large-model fine-tuning runs, observations about clipping fractions and efficiency, and the specific benefits of GSPO for MoE training and RL infrastructure.
- Conclusion: a concise statement of the practical and theoretical implications of GSPO for continuing to scale RL with language models.

Understanding the material in those sections will give you both the theoretical intuition (why sequence-level correction is principled) and the practical knowledge (how GSPO is implemented and why it succeeds empirically) needed to read and evaluate research on RL for large language models.
