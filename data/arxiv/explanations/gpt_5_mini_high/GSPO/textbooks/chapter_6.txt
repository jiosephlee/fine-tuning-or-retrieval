\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 6: Gradient Analysis and Comparison to GRPO

# GSPO gradient: response-level weight times sum of token log-likelihood gradients

We begin from the GSPO surrogate objective (clipping omitted for clarity when taking derivatives). For a single group of responses sampled from the old policy, GSPO optimizes the sequence-level surrogate

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\left[ \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i \right],
\]

where the sequence-level, length-normalized importance ratio is defined as

\[
s_i(\theta)
= \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right),
\]

and \(\widehat{A}_i\) is the group-based advantage for response \(y_i\). Differentiating the unclipped objective with respect to \(\theta\) yields (applying the chain rule and \(\nabla_\theta s_i = s_i \nabla_\theta \log s_i\)):

\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
&= \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \widehat{A}_i \,\nabla_\theta s_i(\theta)\right] \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i \,\nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)\right] \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\right].
\end{aligned}
\]

This closed form has two immediately useful interpretations:

- The multiplicative importance factor that re-weights the gradient of a whole response is \(s_i(\theta)\), a single scalar for the entire sequence. Practically, each token's contribution to the response gradient is multiplied by the same scalar \(s_i(\theta)\) and by the same advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\).

- The inner sum \(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t}\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\) is exactly the gradient of the log-likelihood of the sequence, averaged per-token. Thus GSPO applies a response-level reweighting and then distributes that weight uniformly to the token log-likelihood gradients inside the response.

Length normalization (the \(1/|y_i|\) exponent in \(s_i\) and the \(1/|y_i|\) averaging of token gradients) reduces the sensitivity of the importance factor to sequence length and keeps the numeric range of \(s_i\) consistent across varying response lengths.

# GRPO gradient: per-token importance weights multiply token gradients

GRPO forms its surrogate by attaching a token-level importance ratio to each token and sharing a single response advantage across tokens. Omitting clipping, the GRPO surrogate leads to the gradient

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\right],
\]

where the token-level importance ratio is

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

Compare term by term with the GSPO gradient: in GRPO the multiplier in front of each token's \(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta\) is the token-specific scalar \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\), which can differ from token to token inside the same response. In GSPO that multiplier is the common scalar \(s_i(\theta)\) for all tokens in \(y_i\).

# Consequences of unequal token weights when advantages are positive or negative

The fact that GRPO uses distinct per-token multipliers \(w_{i,t}\) inside the same response has several concrete consequences:

- Incoherent intra-response updates. Suppose a response \(y_i\) has advantage \(\widehat{A}_i>0\); the algorithm intends to increase the probability mass of that response under the new policy. If different tokens have very different \(w_{i,t}\) (some much greater than 1, others much less than 1), the per-token contributions \(\widehat{A}_i w_{i,t}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\) can point in different directions and have very different magnitudes. A single token with a large \(w_{i,t}\) can dominate the summed update, while other tokens are effectively ignored. The net effect may move parameters in a direction that does not consistently increase the whole-sequence likelihood that produced the high reward.

- Sign-dependent clipping asymmetry. PPO-style clipping uses \(\min(wA,\mathrm{clip}(w)A)\). Because clipping interacts with the sign of \(\widehat{A}_i\), tokens that would otherwise be given large multipliers when \(\widehat{A}_i\) is positive get capped at the upper clip; when \(\widehat{A}_i\) is negative the clipping prevents very small \(w\) from driving excessively large negative updates. Applied per-token, this produces a token-by-token nonlinear modification of the intended surrogate, so different tokens in the same sequence can be clipped differently. This token-level clipping can amplify inconsistencies: some tokens may be saturated at a clip bound while others are not, producing an uneven, hard-to-predict update for the response as a whole.

- Simple numeric illustration. Consider a two-token response with per-token ratios \(w_1=2\), \(w_2=0.5\) and a positive advantage \(\widehat{A}>0\). GRPO's per-response contribution (ignoring the \(1/|y|\) and group average) is proportional to \(2\,\widehat{A}\nabla\log\pi(y_1)+0.5\,\widehat{A}\nabla\log\pi(y_2)\). If \(\nabla\log\pi(y_1)\) and \(\nabla\log\pi(y_2)\) point in different or opposing directions, the large multiplier applied to the first token may dominate and push parameters toward optimizing that token, possibly at the expense of the remainder of the response. GSPO instead uses the geometric mean \(s=(w_1w_2)^{1/2}=1\) as a single multiplier, producing an update proportional to \(\widehat{A}(\nabla\log\pi(y_1)+\nabla\log\pi(y_2))/2\): the two token gradients are treated coherently as parts of one sequence-level gradient.

These mechanisms explain why per-token unequal weights can lead to unstable, unpredictable dynamics in practice, particularly when the per-token ratios \(w_{i,t}\) vary widely inside the same response.

# Variance accumulation in GRPO vs. uniform within-response weighting in GSPO

A useful way to contrast GSPO and GRPO is to inspect where the stochastic variability (noise) enters the gradient estimator.

Two simple mathematical observations follow directly from the definitions.

1. GSPO's sequence multiplier is a geometric mean of the token ratios. From the definition

\[
s_i(\theta)=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log w_{i,t}(\theta)\right),
\]

we have \(\log s_i = \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \log w_{i,t}\). Thus the randomness of \(\log s_i\) is the average of the random variables \(\log w_{i,t}\). Under the (standard) simplifying assumption that the per-token log-ratios \(\log w_{i,t}\) are (approximately) identically distributed and weakly dependent, the variance of the average satisfies

\[
\mathrm{Var}(\log s_i) \approx \frac{\mathrm{Var}(\log w_{i,t})}{|y_i|}.
\]

Because \(s_i=\exp(\log s_i)\), fluctuations of \(s_i\) induced by multiplicative token-level noise are damped by the factor \(1/|y_i|\) in log-space. Intuitively, geometric averaging compresses multiplicative extremes: a single extreme token ratio has reduced influence on the sequence-level multiplier.

2. GRPO keeps token-level multiplicative noise inside the sum. The GRPO contribution for one response is

\[
G_\text{GRPO} = \frac{\widehat{A}_i}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} w_{i,t} g_{i,t},
\qquad\text{where } g_{i,t}\equiv\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot).
\]

Here each term \(w_{i,t}g_{i,t}\) carries its own multiplicative randomness through \(w_{i,t}\). Unless the \(w_{i,t}\) fluctuations cancel perfectly across the many terms, the sum collects these independent or weakly correlated sources of noise. Even when averaging over tokens (the \(1/|y_i|\) factor), the nonlinearity and heavy tails of \(w_{i,t}\) can prevent efficient cancellation: extreme per-token \(w_{i,t}\) values contribute disproportionally to the sum and therefore to the variance of \(G_\text{GRPO}\).

Putting the two points together:

- For GSPO, the dominant stochastic factors entering the response gradient are the (i) averaged token gradients \(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t g_{i,t}\) and (ii) a relatively low-variance scalar \(s_i\). Averaging the token gradients commonly reduces variance (law of large numbers), and geometric averaging in \(s_i\) reduces the multiplicative noise captured by the importance ratio.

- For GRPO, multiplicative noise exists at every token position and is not compressed by a log-average. As a result, heavy-tailed or volatile per-token ratios can inject substantial variance into the gradient estimator; that variance is present at every training step and tends to compound over long sequences where many noisy multipliers are summed.

This difference is particularly meaningful in regimes where per-token ratios are volatile (e.g., long responses, off-policy sampling, or architectures where the per-token likelihood is sensitive to small parameter changes). GSPO's use of a single, log-averaged multiplier per response converts many independent multiplicative fluctuations into a single, much less noisy, multiplicative effect.

# Why GSPO mitigates unpredictable dynamics over training

The mathematical and intuitive contrasts above explain why GSPO empirically stabilizes RL training for large language models and long generations:

- Coherent updates within a response. GSPO treats a response as one atomic unit for importance weighting and clipping. This ensures that the optimization step for a high-reward response moves parameters to improve the response as a whole, rather than privileging a subset of tokens that happen to have large per-token ratios. Token gradients inside a response are combined coherently (uniform within-response weighting) before being scaled by the sequence-level multiplier.

- Compression of multiplicative noise. By operating on the average of token log-ratios (i.e., taking a geometric mean), GSPO reduces the effect of extreme, per-token likelihood fluctuations. In particular, \(\log s_i\) is an average of \(\log w_{i,t}\); averaging in log-space compresses multiplicative deviations and produces a multiplier whose variance shrinks (approximately) as \(1/|y_i|\) under mild assumptions. Less multiplicative noise entering the gradient reduces the chance of unpredictable parameter moves.

- More robust clipping. Clipping applied at the sequence level excludes whole responses that are excessively off-policy according to a sequence-level measure consistent with the sequence reward. This contrasts with token-level clipping, which may clip some tokens but not others inside the same response and thereby distort the surrogate objective in a token-incoherent way.

- Practical implications for volatile likelihoods (e.g., MoE). When token likelihoods are particularly sensitive to parameter changes — for example due to expert routing volatility in Mixture-of-Experts models — per-token ratios can swing dramatically between consecutive gradient steps. GRPO, which directly uses such per-token ratios inside the sum, thus inherits this volatility. GSPO’s dependence on the sequence-level geometric mean is empirically and intuitively far less sensitive to single-token routing changes, because the geometric averaging mitigates the influence of a few extreme token ratios. This reduction in sensitivity is what allows GSPO to avoid special workarounds (such as the Routing Replay strategy) required by GRPO in MoE training.

In summary, the analytic forms of the two gradients make the difference transparent: GRPO inserts token-specific, potentially large and noisy multiplicative factors inside the token-sum; GSPO inserts a single, length-normalized sequence factor outside the sum. The latter design produces more coherent, lower-variance updates for sequence-level rewards, which explains GSPO's improved stability and empirical robustness for long generations and large models.
