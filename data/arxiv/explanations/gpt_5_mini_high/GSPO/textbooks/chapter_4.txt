\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 4: Diagnosing GRPO Instability: Importance Sampling and the Unit-of-Reward Principle

# Importance sampling basics: correcting distribution mismatch via weights

When we optimize a policy using data collected from an older policy, we face a distribution mismatch: the samples we observe are drawn from the behavior policy $\pi_\text{beh}$ (the old policy), while the expectation we want to evaluate or optimize is relative to a target policy $\pi_\text{tar}$ (the current policy). Importance sampling is the standard, principled tool to correct this mismatch. If $z$ denotes the basic unit over which an expectation is defined (for example, a full response $y$), then for any function $f$,

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)]
\;=\;
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}}\!\left[ \frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}\, f(z) \right].
\]

This identity says: to compute the expectation under $\pi_\text{tar}$ using samples drawn from $\pi_\text{beh}$, re-weight each sample by the importance weight $\pi_\text{tar}(z)/\pi_\text{beh}(z)$. Two points are crucial here. First, the importance weight must be defined on the same unit $z$ as the expectation (if $f$ is a function of full sequences $y$, then the correct importance weight is a sequence-level density ratio). Second, importance sampling produces an unbiased correction in expectation, but the quality of the estimator—its variance and practical usefulness—depends on how the weights behave across the sampled population.

# Why multiple samples are needed for stable importance-weighted estimates

Although the algebraic identity above holds for any single sample $z$, practical importance-sampling estimates are formed by averaging over many samples. The reason is statistical: the importance weight is itself a random variable (it depends on the sampled $z$), and the weighted estimator inherits this randomness. When the importance weights vary widely, a few samples with large weights can dominate the estimate, producing high variance and unstable optimization. To obtain a stable, low-variance estimate of $\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)]$ we therefore need many independent samples from $\pi_\text{beh}$ so that the average over the random weights concentrates (heuristically, the law of large numbers and related concentration apply when $N \gg 1$).

The paper emphasizes this point: the importance-sampling correction "relies on averaging over multiple samples ($N \gg 1$) from the behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$ for the importance weight $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ to effectively correct for the distributional mismatch." If, instead, we attempt to apply importance sampling in settings where only one sample is available from a particular conditional distribution (for example, exactly one sampled next token from each next-token distribution during an autoregressive rollout), the intended averaging over that conditional distribution does not occur and the correction becomes noisy and unreliable.

# GRPO’s single-sample token-level weights and variance inflation

GRPO was designed to avoid a value model by forming an advantage per response (group-relative advantage) and applying a PPO-style clipped update at the token level. Its objective can be written (omitting KL regularization) as

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot|x)}
\left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_{i},\; \mathrm{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat{A}_{i}\bigr)
\right],
\]

with token importance ratios

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\qquad
\widehat{A}_{i} = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_j)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_j)}.
\]

Two linked issues make this design fragile.

First, each $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is a ratio of next-token probabilities evaluated at a single sampled token $y_{i,t}$. From the importance-sampling point of view, the conditional next-token distribution at time $t$ is a distribution over many possible tokens; to form a stable importance-weighted estimate of expectations under a new conditional distribution one would need multiple independent samples from that conditional. GRPO, however, has exactly one observed sample at each conditional (the single token produced in the rollout). Thus the token-level importance ratio is based on one sample and cannot play the intended distribution-correction role reliably. Instead of correcting a distributional mismatch by averaging across that conditional support, the algorithm injects a random multiplicative factor into the gradient that has high variance.

Second, there is a unit mismatch between the reward and the place where importance sampling is applied. The reward $r(x,y)$ is assigned to entire responses $y$, and GRPO's advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ is computed at the sequence (response) level and then duplicated across tokens in that response. But the off-policy correction and clipping are applied token-by-token using $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. In other words, GRPO attempts to correct sequence-level expectations by mixing sequence-level advantages with token-level importance ratios computed from single-token samples. This violates the basic importance-sampling principle that the importance weight should be defined on the same unit as the expectation and the reward. The paper calls this an "ill-posed" objective: token-level weights are being misapplied to a sequence-level reward signal.

# Accumulation of noise over long sequences

To see how the token-level noise accumulates, compare the two gradients derived in the paper (clipping is omitted here for clarity). GSPO’s gradient is

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right],
\]

where the sequence-level importance factor

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right)
\]

acts as a single scalar multiplier applied uniformly to the sum of token log-gradients. By contrast, GRPO’s gradient is

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]

The qualitative difference is clear: GSPO computes a single, (length-normalized) sequence-level weight $s_i(\theta)$ and then sums the token gradients; GRPO multiplies each token gradient by its own token-level weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ and then sums. If each token-level weight carries high variance (because it is based on a single sampled token), then the sum over $t$ in GRPO accumulates many independent noisy multipliers. As sequence length $|y_i|$ grows, the cumulative variance of the per-token weighted sum typically grows as well, making the gradient estimate increasingly noisy and unstable. GSPO mitigates this because it forms a single weight from the entire sequence likelihood (and then length-normalizes it), which is a more stable statistic for that response than the individual per-token weight realizations.

The paper emphasizes exactly this mechanism: "the token-level importance weight ... introduces high-variance noise into the training gradients, which accumulates over long sequences and is exacerbated by the clipping mechanism." Length normalization in $s_i(\theta)$ was introduced precisely to reduce variance and to place sequence likelihood changes on a common numerical scale so that clipping can be applied sensibly across different response lengths.

# Clipping interactions that exacerbate instability

Clipping is a central practical tool in PPO-style algorithms to prevent excessively large policy updates from a single off-policy sample. In token-level PPO/GRPO the min-with-clip

\[
\min\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i},\; \mathrm{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_{i}\bigr)
\]

is applied at every token. But when $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is itself a high-variance, single-sample estimate, the clipping operation does not simply bound a well-behaved corrective factor; it interacts with the noisy token-wise multipliers and the duplicated sequence-level advantage in complex ways.

Two harmful consequences follow. First, clipping can mask which tokens truly carry informative signal by truncating many token weights to the threshold; if most token weights fall near the clip boundaries because of sampling noise, the effective diversity of learning signals is reduced and updates become driven by the arbitrary boundaries rather than by the true importance weights. Second, and more perniciously, clipping in the presence of per-token noisy weights produces biased and inconsistent gradient directions across tokens: for a positive advantage, tokens with inflated $w_{i,t}$ are clipped to $1+\varepsilon$, but tokens with deflated $w_{i,t}$ remain small, so the relative weighting of token gradients becomes dominated by the clip thresholds rather than by faithful importance correction. The paper notes the practical effect: these clipped, noisy per-token contributions can accumulate and "ultimately precipitate model collapse." Empirically, GSPO and GRPO exhibit very different clipping behavior — GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens (because it clips at sequence level and uses very tight numeric ranges after length normalization) yet still trains better. This counter-intuitive result supports the diagnosis that token-level importance-weighted gradients are inherently noisy and inefficient: clipping fewer noisy token-level contributions (as in GRPO) does not help if the remaining contributions are themselves unstable.

# Unit-of-optimization should match unit-of-reward (sequence-level)

The diagnosis given above points to a simple but fundamental design principle: the unit on which off-policy correction, clipping, and optimization are applied should match the unit at which rewards are given. In the RL-for-language setting of the paper the reward $r(x,y)$ is assigned to entire responses $y$. Therefore the appropriate importance-sampling correction is the sequence-level density ratio $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)$ and the clipping and optimization should operate at that same sequence level.

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) implements this principle directly. Its objective is

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{ x, \{y_i\} \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} }\!
\left[ \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\bigl(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\; \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\bigr)
\right],
\]

with the length-normalized sequence ratio

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{|y_i|}}.
\]

This single scalar $s_i(\theta)$ has a direct and correct importance-sampling interpretation for sequence-level expectations: it expresses how the probability assigned to the entire response $y_i$ changes under the new policy relative to the old. Applying the clip and the advantage at the same sequence level removes the conceptual mismatch present in GRPO and prevents the accumulation of token-wise sampling noise. The length normalization stabilizes the numeric scale across different response lengths so that a single clipping range can be used consistently.

The paper further proposes a GSPO-token variant that allows token-wise advantages while still grounding the off-policy correction in the sequence-level likelihood: GSPO-token uses the numerical value of the sequence-level factor $s_i(\theta)$ at each token but detaches it from gradient propagation so that the gradient flows only through the token log-probabilities. In other words, GSPO-token preserves the unit-of-reward alignment (the sequence-level importance factor) while enabling finer-grained advantage structure when needed.

# Conclusion and practical takeaway

Putting these pieces together yields a clear diagnosis of why GRPO can be fragile when training large language models, especially on long responses or in architectures with volatile per-token likelihoods (for example, sparse Mixture-of-Experts models with changing routing). GRPO applies token-level importance ratios that are computed from single sampled tokens. Because importance sampling corrects distributions only in expectation and achieves low variance only after averaging many samples, the single-sample token ratios are noisy; those noisy multipliers accumulate across tokens in the gradient sum and interact poorly with the token-level clipping used in practice. The result can be high-variance, biased updates that destabilize learning and, empirically, can cause catastrophic model collapse.

The remedy advocated and validated in the paper is to make the unit of off-policy correction and clipping match the unit of reward: work with sequence-level importance ratios (with sensible length normalization) so that each response is corrected and clipped as a whole. GSPO embodies this remedy, and the paper shows that doing so reduces variance, aligns optimization with reward, and yields substantially more stable RL training for large language models.
