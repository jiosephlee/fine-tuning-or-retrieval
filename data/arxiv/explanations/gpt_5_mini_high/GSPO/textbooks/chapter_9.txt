\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 9: GSPO for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE): Stability Without Routing Replay

# MoE routing and expert-activation volatility across updates

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models are characterized in the paper by sparse activation: for a given input (a token or a response) only a subset of the model's expert subnets are "activated" to compute the output. This sparse activation is one of the main scaling strategies for increasing model capacity without linearly increasing computation at every position. The paper observes that this very property—sparsity of active experts—interacts with reinforcement learning updates in a way that can produce instability.

Concretely, the paper reports that, during RL fine-tuning with a token-level objective like GRPO, the set of experts that are activated for the same rollout sample can change substantially after a gradient update. For a deep MoE model (the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base example in the paper), roughly 10% of the experts activated for a given response under the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$ can differ from those activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ after a single gradient step. This phenomenon is referred to in the paper as expert-activation volatility, and it becomes more prominent in deeper MoE models.

This volatility is important because the model's computation graph for a given token or sequence is not fixed across updates: different networks (different activated expert subsets) are effectively used to evaluate the same sample under $\pi_{\theta}$ and under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. As we will see, that fact has direct consequences for any algorithm that relies on comparing token-level likelihoods computed under the two policies.

# Why GRPO’s token-level ratios become unstable in MoE training

GRPO relies on token-level importance ratios. For token $y_{i,t}$ in response $y_i$, GRPO defines the token-level importance ratio as
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} \mid x,y_{i,<t}) }.
\]
These per-token ratios are then used (with clipping) to weight token gradients inside the GRPO objective. The paper points out two linked problems with this design when applied to MoE RL fine-tuning.

First, from the general importance-sampling perspective, applying an importance weight based on a single sample from the behavior distribution is problematic: importance sampling is designed to reweight many samples from the behavior distribution so that they collectively approximate an expectation under the target distribution. Using a single next-token sample to form a corrective weight does not perform that averaging and therefore can be a high-variance estimator. The paper emphasizes this mismatch between the unit of reward (the whole response) and the unit of importance correction (a single token).

Second, and more specifically for MoE models, expert-activation volatility makes the numerator and denominator of $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ refer to different activated networks. Because $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)$ may be computed by different combinations of experts, $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ can fluctuate drastically even for small parameter updates. The paper expresses the GRPO gradient as
\[
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO} (\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \widehat{A}_{i} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} \mid x,y_{i,<t}) } \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})
\right],
\]
which shows that each token gradient is multiplied by its individual importance ratio. When those ratios are unstable and vary widely across tokens, the weighted sum of token gradients becomes noisy and unpredictable. The paper reports that this phenomenon—amplified by token-level clipping and accumulation over long sequences—can drive catastrophic and often irreversible model collapse during RL training of large MoE models.

# Routing Replay: stabilizing GRPO via cached routing (costs and limits)

To counter the instability just described, the paper previously used a practical workaround called Routing Replay. The core idea is simple: when computing token-level importance ratios during optimization, force $\pi_{\theta}$ to evaluate each token using the same routing configuration (the same set of activated experts) that was used when the sample was generated under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. In other words, cache the routing choices produced by $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ for each rollout sample, and "replay" those routing modes in $\pi_{\theta}$ when forming the numerator $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$.

Routing Replay stabilizes the token-level ratios because it makes the numerator and denominator computed over the same activated network; changes in the importance ratio therefore reflect parameter shifts rather than structural changes in which experts are used. The paper demonstrates that this trick is essential for obtaining normal convergence of GRPO when training MoE models: without replay, the expert-activation volatility prevents GRPO from converging properly.

However, Routing Replay comes with practical costs and intrinsic limits. The paper highlights two concrete costs: additional memory and communication overhead. Caching routing modes for many rollout samples can be memory intensive, and replaying those cached routings across devices during optimization adds communication. More fundamentally, the paper notes that replaying old routing modes artificially constrains the model during optimization: by forcing $\pi_{\theta}$ to reuse $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$'s routing, you are preventing the model from applying its evolving routing decisions at optimization time, which can limit the actual capacity and expressiveness of the MoE during training. In short, Routing Replay is a stabilization patch that reduces variance but also increases engineering complexity and prevents the model from fully exercising its adaptive routing.

# GSPO’s robustness: stability of sequence likelihood vs. token likelihoods

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) takes a different, principled route. Instead of forming importance ratios at the token level, GSPO forms a sequence-level importance ratio and performs clipping and optimization at the response level. For response $y_i$ the length-normalized sequence importance ratio is defined as
\[
s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i \mid x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i \mid x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} \mid x,y_{i,<t}) } \right).
\]
GSPO then optimizes the group-level objective that clips and weights whole responses by $s_i(\theta)$ and uses group-based advantages $\widehat{A}_i$ computed from the set of responses to the same query.

The paper derives the GSPO gradient (omitting clipping for clarity) as
\[
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
s_{i}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_{i}
\cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]
Two aspects of this expression give GSPO its robustness in the MoE setting. First, tokens in the same response are weighted equally inside the averaged log-gradient term; there is no per-token multiplicative importance weight that can vary arbitrarily from token to token. Second, the sequence weight $s_i(\theta)$ aggregates log-likelihood ratios across the whole response and length-normalizes them, which smooths out local token-level fluctuations and places the correction at the same granularity as the reward (the full response).

Because the MoE model "maintains its language modeling capability" even though which exact experts are selected may vary, the paper argues that the overall sequence likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)$ is far less volatile than individual token likelihoods computed under changing routing. In other words, while a small subset of tokens may see large per-token likelihood swings due to routing changes, the product (or average log probability) over the whole sequence is comparatively stable. Length-normalization in $s_i(\theta)$ further reduces variance and keeps importance ratios in a unified numeric range, avoiding the exploding or vanishing magnitudes that token-level products of ratios might produce for long responses.

Putting these pieces together, the paper shows that GSPO can compute sequence-level importance ratios conventionally (without replaying routing), apply clipping at the response level, and perform stable optimization. This design directly aligns the unit of optimization (the response) with the unit of reward and with the importance-correction, avoiding the mismatch and amplified variance that plague token-level GRPO in MoE training.

# Removal of Routing Replay and infrastructure simplification

One immediate implication demonstrated in the paper is that GSPO obviates the need for Routing Replay. Because GSPO never requires computing token-level importance ratios that compare per-token likelihoods under potentially different activated networks, there is no need to cache and replay routing configurations. The paper shows that GSPO converges normally when applied to MoE models without any routing replay mechanism: it can compute $s_i(\theta)$ with the usual forward pass under the current model and still obtain stable learning behavior.

Removing Routing Replay has several concrete infrastructure implications emphasized in the paper. First, it reduces memory and communication overhead: there is no need to store per-sample routing metadata or to transmit it across devices during optimization. Second, it lifts the artificial constraint placed on the model by routing replay, allowing the MoE to exercise its full routing capacity during optimization (the paper notes that replay can "limit the actual capacity of the MoE model"). Third, GSPO's reliance on sequence-level likelihoods is more tolerant to precision differences between inference and training engines. The paper points out that, in practice, teams often recompute token-level likelihoods on the training engine to avoid precision mismatch; because sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant, GSPO makes it possible to use the likelihoods returned by the inference engine directly, which can simplify the pipeline (for example, in partial rollout or multi-turn RL, and in disaggregated training–inference frameworks).

In short, by moving the importance correction to the sequence level, GSPO removes an engineering patch (Routing Replay) that was necessary under GRPO, and thereby simplifies the RL infrastructure while also restoring stable optimization behavior.

# Practical benefits for deep and large MoE models

The practical upshot reported in the paper is that GSPO makes RL fine-tuning of deep, large MoE models both simpler and more robust. Because GSPO eliminates the need for routing replay and stabilizes importance weighting via sequence-level aggregation and length normalization, it enables normal convergence of MoE RL without the memory and communication costs and capacity constraints introduced by replay.

Empirically, the paper finds that GSPO yields superior training stability and efficiency compared to GRPO: training proceeds stably across continued compute, updates to the query set, and increased generation length; GSPO attains better training accuracy and benchmark performance under the same training compute. The paper also reports a curious but important observation about clipping behavior: GSPO clips at the response level and consequently clips a much larger fraction of tokens (a difference of about two orders of magnitude in clipping fractions compared to GRPO), yet GSPO remains more sample-efficient. This counterintuitive result is interpreted in the paper as evidence that GRPO's token-level gradient estimates are inherently noisy and inefficient to exploit, while GSPO's sequence-level objective provides a more reliable learning signal even when many tokens are formally excluded by clipping.

Finally, the paper positions GSPO as having contributed materially to the improvements in the Qwen3 family of models: its inherent stability for MoE RL and its infrastructure benefits make it a scalable algorithmic foundation for continued RL scaling of very large language models. For practitioners and researchers training deep MoE models with reinforcement learning, the lesson is clear from the paper: align the importance-correction unit with the reward unit (the full response), use length-normalized sequence ratios as GSPO does, and you can avoid costly and capacity-limiting stabilizations such as Routing Replay while obtaining more stable and efficient RL training.
