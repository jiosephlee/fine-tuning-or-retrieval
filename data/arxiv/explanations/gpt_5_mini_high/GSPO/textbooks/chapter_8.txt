\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 8: Practical Training with GSPO: Implementation and Tuning

# Constructing groups: G responses per query and batch organization

Group construction is the foundation of GSPO. For each query $x$ you must sample a small group of $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ from the behavior policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x)$. GSPO uses these $G$ responses to form a relative, group-based advantage for each response. Concretely, the paper defines the (standardized) group advantage for response $y_i$ as
$\displaystyle \widehat{A}_{i} = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}\!\big(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\big)}{\mathrm{std}\!\big(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\big)}$,
where $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$ is the verifier reward. All tokens in the same response share that same $\widehat{A}_i$ in the basic GSPO objective, so the unit of credit is the whole sequence (the paper also presents GSPO-token as a variant that permits token-level advantages).

Practically, a rollout consists of many query groups. The implementation must preserve the $G$ responses for each query until you compute the group mean and standard deviation, because those statistics determine the advantage signals you will use for optimization. In the experiments reported in the paper a large rollout batch was partitioned into multiple mini-batches for optimization (the paper gives an example split into four mini-batches). That pattern—sample many query groups in rollouts, compute group advantages, then partition the resulting set of groups into mini-batches for gradient steps—matches the GSPO formulation and is the usual organization to maximize hardware utilization.

Finally, choose $G>1$ so that the sample standard deviation is meaningful; GSPO’s group standardization is the mechanism that replaces a learned value model (used in PPO), so the group must contain multiple responses for the same query to provide a relative signal.

# Mini-batch updates and off-policy considerations

Large models, sparse architectures, and long responses make it common to collect very large rollout batches and then split them into several mini-batches for gradient updates. The paper emphasizes that this split is inherently an off-policy setting: the samples in the rollout were generated by $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, while gradient steps change $\theta$ to obtain $\pi_{\theta}$. Off-policy samples can bias or destabilize updates if not corrected, which is why PPO-style clipping or KL penalties are standard safeguards.

GSPO enforces this safeguard at the sequence level. Its optimization objective (with clipping) is
\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\min\!\big( s_{i}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\; \mathrm{clip}\big(s_{i}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\big)\,\widehat{A}_i\big)
\right],
\]
where $s_i(\theta)$ is the length-normalized sequence importance ratio (see next section). Because the clipping is applied to a whole response via $s_i$, GSPO excludes overly “off-policy” responses from driving the gradient, and it does so in a way that is consistent with the sequence-level reward.

The paper also illustrates why token-level importance ratios (as used in GRPO) become problematic in this mini-batch/off-policy regime: token-level ratios are based on a single next-token sample from each token distribution and therefore do not properly estimate distributional mismatch, producing high-variance and accumulation of noise across long sequences. GSPO avoids that fundamental source of instability by operating at sequence granularity.

# Efficient computation of sequence likelihoods and normalized ratios

The core computational building block in GSPO is the sequence-level importance ratio. GSPO defines a length-normalized ratio
\[
s_i(\theta)
= \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]
This log-sum representation is the practical way to compute $s_i$ robustly: compute token log-probabilities, sum the per-token log differences, divide by the length $|y_i|$, and exponentiate. Working in log-space first avoids underflow and dramatically improves numerical stability compared to multiplying token probabilities.

Two implementation points from the paper are important here. First, the paper notes that practitioners often recompute likelihoods of sampled responses under the old policy using the training engine (because training- and inference-engine floating-point behavior may differ). Second, GSPO’s reliance on sequence-level likelihoods makes it much more tolerant of such numerical or precision discrepancies; the paper observes that GSPO “makes it possible to directly use the likelihoods returned by the inference engine for optimization, thereby avoiding the need for recomputation with the training engine.” Concretely, storing sequence log-likelihoods returned at generation time and then forming $\log\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)$ from those stored values is a valid and often cheaper option when using GSPO, because the aggregated, length-normalized ratio is robust to small per-token numeric differences.

Finally, length normalization itself serves two purposes emphasized by the paper: it reduces variance in the ratio across sequences of varying lengths, and it keeps $s_i(\theta)$ in a more unified numeric range so a single clipping range can be used across responses of different lengths.

# Choosing clipping ranges (different scale than token-level methods)

Clipping in GSPO is applied to sequence-level ratios $s_i(\theta)$, not token-level ratios. That difference implies a very different numeric scale for the clipping bounds. The paper calls attention to this by reporting the clipping ranges used in experiments: GSPO used very tight left and right clipping ranges (example values in the paper were $1-\varepsilon_\text{left}=1-3\times 10^{-4}$ and $1+\varepsilon_\text{right}=1+4\times 10^{-4}$), whereas the GRPO baseline used much larger asymmetrical bounds (example: $1-0.2$ and $1+0.27$). The order-of-magnitude gap reflects the fact that length-normalized sequence ratios are typically numerically much closer to one than per-token importance ratios.

Intuition: because $s_i$ aggregates many token-level effects and then normalizes by sequence length, single-token probability fluctuations translate into much smaller changes in $s_i$ than in raw sequence likelihood products. Consequently, effective clipping for GSPO is numerically much tighter. The paper also reports an empirical curiosity: GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens (or responses) under its very tight sequence-level bounds but nevertheless attains superior training efficiency. This suggests that tighter sequence-level clipping is an appropriate mechanism for removing overly off-policy sequences, even if that removes a larger nominal fraction of tokens from gradient influence.

The practical implication is simple: treat GSPO clipping ranges and token-level clipping ranges as entirely different tuning knobs. Monitor the distribution of $s_i$ (histogram) while you tune clipping; do not reuse token-level clipping defaults for GSPO.

# Reward normalization details (mean/std; stability floors for std)

GSPO uses group-standardized rewards to produce advantages. The paper’s definition is
\[
\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}\big(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\big)}{\mathrm{std}\big(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\big)},
\]
with $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$ coming from a verifier. This standardization centers the rewards within the group and rescales them by the group dispersion, producing comparable advantage magnitudes across queries and batches without requiring a learned value function. In GSPO the standardized advantage is the scalar credit for the entire response, which directly aligns the optimization unit to the unit of reward.

The paper does not delve into implementation minutiae such as numerical guards on the denominator. The core requirement from the formulation is that the group standard deviation is computed over the $G$ responses for the same query; consequently, you must ensure $G$ is large enough to produce meaningful statistics in your setting. If $G$ is extremely small or the rewards in the group are identical, the sample standard deviation can be degenerate; the paper’s experiments rely on non-degenerate groups so that the group standardization yields informative advantages.

Note also that GSPO-token is provided in the paper as a variant when you need per-token advantage customization (for example in multi-turn settings). In the basic GSPO objective all tokens in response $y_i$ share the same $\widehat{A}_i$, whereas GSPO-token allows a separate $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ at each token while still weighting those token gradients by the same sequence-level $s_i(\theta)$.

# Optional KL regularization and scheduling

The paper explicitly omits the KL regularization term from the objective for brevity (it states: “we omit the KL regularization term hereinafter for brevity, as it is not the focus of this paper”), but that omission does not mean KL control is incompatible with GSPO. In the broader family of PPO-style algorithms a KL penalty or a KL-tracking term is often used alongside clipping to bound policy drift. The paper focuses on demonstrating the benefits of sequence-level importance ratios and clipping; it does not provide a specific KL regularizer, coefficient, or scheduling recipe.

If you choose to include an explicit KL regularization term in your implementation, recall that it is an additional mechanism—besides clipping—that constrains policy movement. The GSPO sequence-level clipping already provides a strong, theoretically grounded way to exclude overly off-policy sequences. How to combine or schedule a KL term with GSPO is not addressed in the paper and therefore must be considered an implementation choice you would need to validate empirically for your training setup.

# Generation length schedules and query set refresh strategies

The paper reports an important experimental observation: GSPO “can deliver continuous performance improvement through increasing the training compute, regularly updating the query set, and extending the generation length.” This indicates that GSPO is robust to progressive increases in generation length and to periodically refreshing the set of queries used for rollouts.

Operationally this suggests a training curriculum where you can expand the generation horizon and cycle queries during longer training runs without running into the catastrophic instabilities that token-level methods experienced. The paper uses this capability to scale RL compute and to keep the training distribution from becoming stale. The paper does not prescribe a fixed schedule or refresh frequency; it simply documents that regular query updates and longer generation horizons are feasible and effective with GSPO.

# Monitoring: response-level clipping fraction, $s_i$ histograms, reward stats

Monitoring is essential when tuning GSPO. The paper highlights several informative signals that you should track during training.

First, monitor the clipping fraction: compute the fraction of responses whose sequence ratio $s_i$ lies outside the allowed clipping bounds $[1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]$ (or, if you prefer, the fraction of responses that are actually clipped by the min operation in the objective). The paper reports that GSPO’s tight sequence clipping leads to a much larger nominal fraction of clipped tokens/responses than GRPO’s token-level clipping, yet GSPO still achieves better sample efficiency—so use clipping fraction as a diagnostic rather than a raw verdict of failure.

Second, examine a histogram of $s_i(\theta)$ across the rollout batch. The distribution tells you whether your clipping bounds are too tight (most mass outside the range) or too lax (almost all mass near 1 and nothing clipped). Because GSPO’s $s_i$ is length-normalized, its histogram should typically concentrate near 1; dramatic skew or heavy tails are diagnostic of either large step sizes or mismatched likelihood computations.

Third, track reward statistics at the group level: group means and standard deviations used to form $\widehat{A}_i$, as well as the raw reward distributions. Sudden collapse in group std or shifts in mean are early warning signs. Finally, compare these signals versus a token-level baseline (if you run one): the paper reports two orders of magnitude difference in clipped-token fractions between GSPO and GRPO, which was a useful diagnostic of the different behaviors produced by the two methods.

# Handling precision discrepancies: using inference-engine likelihoods directly

A frequent practical complication in RL for large models is the mismatch between the engine used for generation (inference engines such as SGLang or vLLM) and the training engine (for example Megatron). The paper points out that many teams recompute sampled-response likelihoods under the training engine to avoid precision discrepancies when forming importance ratios. GSPO reduces that operational burden: because it uses only sequence-level (length-normalized) likelihood ratios, it is empirically far more tolerant of small per-token numeric differences introduced by different runtimes.

Concretely, the paper suggests that with GSPO you can often avoid a costly recomputation step and directly use the sequence log-likelihoods returned by the inference engine for each sampled response. That simplifies infrastructure (no repeated forward pass under the training engine just to get $\log\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)$) and is especially valuable in scenarios such as partial rollouts, multi-turn RL, and disaggregated training/inference workflows. The paper presents this as an infrastructural benefit of GSPO in practice.

# Compute and memory tips for large models and long outputs

GSPO brings several compute-and-memory advantages that are explicitly called out in the paper. First, like GRPO, GSPO avoids the heavy reliance on a learned value model (the paper notes that PPO’s dependence on a value model introduces considerable memory and compute cost). Second, GSPO eliminates the need for the Routing Replay strategy that was required to stabilize GRPO training of Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. Routing Replay cached activated experts and replayed routing in the new policy for stable token-level importance ratios; that caching and replaying introduced memory and communication overhead and limited the usable capacity of the MoE. GSPO’s focus on sequence likelihoods sidesteps token-level activation volatility in MoE models, removing the need for Routing Replay and thereby simplifying training infrastructure while reducing memory/communication overhead.

For throughput, the paper demonstrates the standard pattern: collect large rollout batches and split them into several mini-batches for gradient updates (the reported experiments used a four-way split). This pattern maximizes hardware utilization while remaining compatible with GSPO’s off-policy correction via sequence-level clipping.

Finally, for long outputs the paper’s choice of length normalization in $s_i(\theta)$ is a practical device that reduces variance of importance ratios and helps keep clipping ranges meaningful across different sequence lengths. In aggregate, GSPO’s algorithmic design reduces sources of instability that otherwise force expensive engineering workarounds; the result is a simpler and more memory-efficient RL training recipe for large and sparse language models.

# Summary

This chapter collected the practical implementation and tuning points for GSPO that follow directly from the paper. The central prescriptions are: form groups of $G$ responses per query and standardize rewards across the group; compute sequence-level ratios $s_i(\theta)$ via log-sum and length normalization; apply clipping at the sequence level (with much tighter numerical bounds than token-level clipping); partition rollouts into mini-batches for throughput while relying on sequence-level clipping to control off-policy drift; and monitor $s_i$ distributions, clipping fractions, and reward statistics. Two practical infrastructure benefits emphasized by the paper are that GSPO removes the need for Routing Replay in MoE training and tolerates inference/training engine precision differences so that inference-engine likelihoods can often be used directly. These ingredients together produce a stable, efficient, and scalable RL recipe for training large language models with GSPO.
