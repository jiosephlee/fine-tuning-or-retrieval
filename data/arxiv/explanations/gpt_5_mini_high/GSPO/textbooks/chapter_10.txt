\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 10: Experiments: Stability, Efficiency, and Scaling

# Setup: Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base cold start; rollout batches split into $4$ mini-batches

The empirical evaluation in the paper focuses on a controlled comparison between GSPO and the previous group-based baseline GRPO. The experiments start from a cold-start model that is fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base. All rollout data collected during RL is processed in the standard way used for large-scale language-model RL: a large batch of samples is partitioned into multiple mini-batches for gradient updates. Concretely, in these experiments each rollout batch is split into $4$ mini-batches for the downstream gradient steps. This batching practice is important to mention because partitioning a rollout into mini-batches creates an off-policy situation (samples are generated by the old policy but gradients are applied after additional updates), which is exactly the kind of setting where importance weighting and clipping become necessary.

Evaluation during training is carried out by tracking both training reward curves and external benchmark performance curves. The benchmarks and metrics used are described below; throughout training the authors monitor these curves to judge stability and sample efficiency. The experimental setup emphasises fairness: when comparing GSPO to GRPO the authors carefully tuned the baseline hyperparameters (notably clipping) so that both algorithms were given comparable, well-adjusted settings.

# GSPO vs. GRPO hyperparameters (distinct clipping ranges)

A salient experimental detail is that GSPO and GRPO use very different clipping magnitudes because they define importance ratios at different granularities. GSPO forms a sequence-level importance ratio
\[
s_{i}(\theta)
= \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}},
\]
which is length-normalized and therefore typically concentrated near $1$. For the experiments reported in the paper the left and right clipping offsets for GSPO were set to $3\times 10^{-4}$ and $4\times 10^{-4}$ respectively (i.e., clipping $s_i(\theta)$ to approximately $[1 - 3\times 10^{-4},\, 1 + 4\times 10^{-4}]$).

By contrast, GRPO applies clipping to token-level importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$; for the baseline runs the left and right clipping offsets were set to $0.2$ and $0.27$ respectively. These choices reflect a common practical point emphasized in the paper: because GSPO’s importance ratios are defined and length-normalized at the sequence level, their numeric scale is much closer to $1$ and therefore appropriate clipping tolerances are many orders of magnitude smaller than the typical token-level clipping used with GRPO. The authors also stress that they tuned GRPO’s clipping carefully to make the comparison fair.

# Benchmarks: AIME'24 (Pass@1 over $32$), LiveCodeBench 202410–202502 (Pass@1 over $8$), Codeforces (Elo)

The paper reports external evaluation on three benchmarks to measure task-level progress during RL: AIME'24, LiveCodeBench (time slice 202410–202502), and Codeforces. The metrics are presented exactly as in the experiments: AIME'24 performance is reported as average Pass@1 over $32$ samplings, LiveCodeBench performance is reported as average Pass@1 over $8$ samplings, and Codeforces performance is reported using the Elo rating produced by that benchmark suite. Training reward curves (internal verifier rewards) and these external benchmark curves are tracked in parallel so that stability and generalization can both be observed as training proceeds.

Using a mix of reward curves and external metrics is important: reward curves show whether optimization is behaving stably on the training objective, while the benchmark metrics show whether improvements translate to held-out, task-oriented gains. The reported experiments use all of these signals to compare GSPO and GRPO under the same consumed queries and training compute.

# Results: continuous improvements with more compute, query updates, and longer generations

A central empirical claim in the paper is that GSPO enables stable, sustained improvement when increasing training effort. The authors observe that GSPO training proceeds stably across the runs they report, and that performance improves continuously when they (a) increase total training compute, (b) regularly update the query set used for rollouts, and (c) extend generation length at evaluation time. In short, GSPO does not exhibit the catastrophic, often-irreversible collapses that have been observed for GRPO in large, long-response settings; instead, longer training and more compute continue to produce gains.

Intuitively, this behavior matches the algorithmic design: GSPO’s sequence-level importance ratio aggregates signal across a whole response and applies length-normalization, which prevents the per-token high-variance corrections that the paper identified as the root cause of instability in GRPO. Because the unit of optimization is the same as the unit of reward (the sequence), the updates remain coherent even as responses become longer or the query set evolves.

# Training efficiency gains relative to GRPO under equal compute

Under equal training compute and the same number of consumed queries, GSPO yields better training accuracy and stronger external benchmark performance than the carefully tuned GRPO baseline. The paper reports that GSPO achieves higher training-reward curves and superior scores on AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and Codeforces for the same computational budget.

There are several mechanisms responsible for this efficiency difference that the paper highlights. First, GSPO’s sequence-level weighting removes the per-token importance-weight noise that otherwise pollutes gradient estimates in GRPO; in practice this lowers gradient variance and improves the utility of each sampled response. Second, in the MoE setting GSPO obviates an expensive stabilization mechanism (Routing Replay, described below), so GSPO avoids the memory and communication overheads GRPO incurs when training sparse models. Both factors contribute to better use of the same compute and query budget.

# Clipping fractions: GSPO clips far more tokens yet learns faster

One of the most striking empirical observations reported is about clipping fractions. When comparing the fraction of tokens that are clipped during optimization, the authors observe a difference of roughly two orders of magnitude between GSPO and GRPO — GSPO clips many more tokens (or, put differently, GSPO’s clipping discards a much larger fraction of per-token contributions) than GRPO. This difference persists even when the clipping ranges are varied: adjusting clipping magnitudes does not erase the two-orders-of-magnitude gap.

At first glance this is counter-intuitive: clipping more tokens would naively appear to reduce the amount of data used by the optimizer and thus slow learning. The paper interprets the phenomenon differently. GSPO’s sequence-level clipping discards entire responses that are judged to be too off-policy under the current sequence-level importance ratio, thereby eliminating low-quality, high-variance training signals before token-level gradients are accumulated. GRPO’s token-level clipping, by contrast, keeps many tokens whose token-level importance ratios may still inject noisy, miscalibrated corrections into the gradient. Empirically, despite clipping far more tokens, GSPO produces superior learning because the retained signals are higher quality and much less noisy; in other words, GSPO prefers fewer but cleaner gradients over many noisy token-level corrections.

This clipping-fraction finding is evidence for the broader diagnostic in the paper: token-level importance ratios can be a poor off-policy correction for sequence rewards because they are computed from single-sample next-token draws and therefore carry high variance that accumulates along long sequences. Sequence-level importance ratios mitigate that accumulation and produce a more effective exploitation of samples.

# Role of GSPO in Qwen3 performance improvements

The paper emphasizes that GSPO is not only a stable algorithmic contribution in isolation but also a practical enabler for large-scale RL runs, and that its merits materially contributed to the improvements seen in the latest Qwen3 models. Two concrete practical advantages drive that claim.

First, GSPO stabilizes RL training of large Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models without the need for special-purpose mechanisms like Routing Replay. The paper documents that, under GRPO, MoE training suffers from expert-activation volatility (for example, in one 48-layer instance about $10\%$ of activated experts for the same response can change after a gradient update). Routing Replay was previously used to force the same routing decisions for the old and new policy when computing token-level likelihoods, restoring stability at the cost of extra memory and communication. GSPO, by relying on sequence-level likelihoods that are inherently less sensitive to token-level routing changes, removes this dependency and thus simplifies and speeds up MoE RL.

Second, GSPO relaxes stringent infrastructure requirements. Because GSPO uses only sequence-level likelihoods for importance weighting, it is more tolerant of the small numerical differences that can arise between different execution engines (for example between a training engine and an inference engine). This tolerance opens the possibility of directly using likelihoods produced by high-throughput inference engines during rollouts without recomputing them in the training engine, which simplifies rollout pipelines in practice (for example in partial-rollout, multi-turn RL, or disaggregated training-inference setups).

Taken together, these practical advantages — stable, low-variance updates that scale with response length, removal of expensive MoE stabilization workarounds, and relaxed infrastructure constraints — are the mechanisms by which GSPO supported the extended RL runs and model improvements reported for Qwen3.

# Summary

The experiments reported in the paper demonstrate three consistent empirical messages. First, GSPO yields stable optimization in regimes where GRPO can be fragile or even catastrophically collapse, particularly for long-response tasks and very large models. Second, GSPO is more sample- and compute-efficient than a carefully tuned GRPO baseline: for the same compute and consumed queries it attains higher training-reward curves and better benchmark performance (AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, Codeforces). Third, and unexpectedly, GSPO’s sequence-level clipping discards many more per-token contributions than GRPO but nevertheless produces faster, more reliable learning; this reveals that token-level importance weighting can be noisy and inefficient in practice. Finally, because GSPO resolves MoE-specific stability problems and is tolerant to cross-engine precision differences, it simplifies RL infrastructure and enabled the large-scale RL improvements reported for the Qwen3 models. Together these findings support the paper’s recommendation to adopt sequence-level importance weighting and clipping as a principled and practical approach for scaling RL with large language models.
