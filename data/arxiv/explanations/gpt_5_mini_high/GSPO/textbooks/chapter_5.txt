\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 5: The GSPO Algorithm: Sequence-Level Clipping and Optimization

# Sequence-level importance ratio based on response likelihood

At the core of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) is a redefinition of the importance sampling weight from a token-level quantity to a sequence-level one. For an autoregressive language model viewed as a policy $\pi_\theta$, a response $y$ to a query $x$ has sequence likelihood
\[
\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).
\]
When we perform off-policy correction (we sampled $y$ under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ but wish to estimate quantities under the current policy $\pi_\theta$), the correct importance weight for that response is the ratio of sequence probabilities,
\[
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y \mid x)}.
\]
GSPO adopts this principle directly and uses a length-normalized variant (explained next) as the per-response importance ratio. Concretely, for the $i$-th response in a group we define
\[
s_i(\theta)
=
\left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i \mid x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i \mid x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp\!\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} \mid x,y_{i,<t})} \right).
\]

GSPO then places these sequence-level ratios into a PPO-style clipped objective over a group of responses. With a group $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ drawn from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\mid x)$, the GSPO policy objective (omitting any KL regularization term) is
\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\min\!\big( s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\;\mathrm{clip}\big(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\big)\,\widehat{A}_i\big)
\right],
\]
where $\widehat{A}_i$ is the group-based advantage (next section). The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ has a clear theoretical meaning: it measures how much a whole response $y_i$ sampled from the behavior policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ differs from the current policy $\pi_\theta$, which is precisely the quantity that should reweight a sequence-level reward.

# Length normalization: exponent $1/|y|$ to control variance and scale

GSPO uses the exponent $1/|y|$ when converting the raw sequence-likelihood ratio into $s_i(\theta)$. This length normalization has two related goals: variance reduction and numeric unification across responses of different lengths.

Writing
\[
\log s_i(\theta) = \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]
we see that $\log s_i(\theta)$ is the average (per-token) log change in likelihood between the current and old policies. Averaging the per-token log ratios reduces variance relative to using the raw product of token ratios. Without this normalization, a large change in a few token probabilities could produce dramatic swings in the overall sequence importance weight, and responses of different lengths would naturally fall into very different numeric ranges. Length normalization keeps $s_i(\theta)$ on a more consistent scale: it behaves like a per-token geometric-mean ratio, making the distribution of sequence ratios less wildly spread and enabling a single, sensible clipping scale to be used across responses of varying lengths.

The paper emphasizes this practical benefit: without length normalization, likelihood changes of only a few tokens can dramatically swing the sequence ratio, and responses of different lengths would require different clipping ranges. The $1/|y|$ exponent is therefore a simple, effective mechanism to reduce the variance of the ratio and to control $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range.

# Group-based z-score advantage (mean/std over responses per query)

To avoid relying on a separately trained value model, GSPO adopts the group-based advantage normalization idea also used by GRPO: for each query $x$ we generate a group of $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ under the behavior policy, score each response with a verifier $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$, and form a z-score advantage for each response
\[
\widehat{A}_i
=
\frac{ r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}\big(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\big) }
{ \mathrm{std}\big(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\big) }.
\]
This group-normalized advantage has zero mean and unit variance within the group, and it directly expresses the relative quality of each sampled response to the same query. Using this group-based z-score bypasses the need for a value function and avoids the difficulties associated with training a value model of comparable size to the policy model.

In GSPO the group advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ is paired with the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ so that the normalized reward signal and the off-policy correction are both defined at the sequence (response) level. This alignment—advantages computed over whole responses and importance ratios defined on whole responses—ensures that the optimization target (the objective terms that enter gradients) corresponds to the same unit as the reward assignment.

# Sequence-level clipping of entire responses

GSPO applies PPO-style clipping to the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ rather than clipping token-level ratios. The clipped term inside the expectation is
\[
\min\!\big( s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\;\mathrm{clip}\big(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\big)\,\widehat{A}_i\big).
\]
The clipping serves the same high-level purpose as in PPO and GRPO: it prevents overly off-policy samples from contributing excessive gradient signal. The critical distinction is that the unit being clipped in GSPO is the entire response. Because the reward and advantage in GSPO are defined at the response level, excluding or limiting the contribution of whole responses is a principled way to control off-policy bias without introducing per-token artifacts.

Putting the clipping at the response level reduces the high-variance noise introduced when many per-token importance ratios (each computed on a single-token sample) are combined. The paper documents a striking empirical observation: GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens (or effectively excludes more token-gradient contributions through response clipping) than GRPO does under its token-level clipping, yet GSPO yields higher training efficiency. This counterintuitive result supports the argument that sequence-level clipping is better aligned with the true learning signal (the response reward) and that token-level clipping can leave noisy, inefficient gradients in place.

# Alignment of reward unit and optimization unit

A central conceptual point motivating GSPO is that the unit used for off-policy correction and clipping should match the unit to which rewards are assigned. Importance sampling in its basic form states that, for a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$ and behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$,
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_\text{tar}}\big[f(z)\big]
= \mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_\text{beh}}\!\left[ \frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}\, f(z) \right].
\]
This identity relies on using the probability ratio over the same domain $z$ on which $f$ is defined. In our RL-for-generation setting, the reward $r(x,y)$ (and hence the function $f$) is defined on whole responses $y$. Therefore the principled importance weight to correct for off-policy sampling is the ratio of sequence probabilities $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y\mid x)$, not a collection of per-token ratios each based on a single sample from a next-token distribution.

GRPO and PPO-style token-level formulations instead apply a per-token importance ratio
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)
=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\]
and average or clip these per-token weights. The paper highlights why that is problematic: each token-level ratio is based on exactly one sample from the corresponding next-token distribution, so the token-level ratio does not perform the intended distribution-correction role and instead injects high-variance noise. That noise accumulates over long sequences and is further amplified by clipping, producing unpredictable and sometimes catastrophic training instability.

The gradient-level distinction makes the difference concrete. Ignoring clipping, the GSPO gradient can be written as
\begin{align}
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
&=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\;\nabla_{\theta}\log s_i(\theta)
\right] \\
&=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\widehat{A}_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right]. \nonumber
\end{align}
Every token gradient within a response is multiplied by the same response-level scalar $s_i(\theta)$ (and averaged over tokens by $1/|y_i|$). By contrast, the GRPO gradient (again omitting clipping) is
\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \widehat{A}_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]
Thus in GRPO each token gradient is weighted by its own token-level ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, which can differ across tokens and fluctuate sharply. GSPO removes this source of token-wise weighting variability by using a single, sequence-level scalar to weight all token gradients from the same response.

This alignment—computing advantages over whole responses and correcting with sequence-level importance ratios—restores a principled off-policy correction that is coherent with the unit of reward and avoids the high-variance, accumulation-prone behavior observed in token-level schemes.

# Magnitude differences in clipping ranges versus GRPO and why they arise

Because GSPO and token-level methods define different importance quantities, the numerical ranges and suitable clipping magnitudes differ substantially. The paper notes this both analytically and empirically. Analytically, $s_i(\theta)$ is the length-normalized (geometric-mean) ratio of sequence likelihoods and tends to lie closer to 1 due to averaging of per-token log ratios; token-level ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ can exhibit much wider variability since they reflect single-step likelihood changes.

Empirically, the authors report that the clipping ranges used for GSPO are many orders of magnitude smaller than those used for GRPO in their experiments. For example, they set the GSPO left/right clipping values to approximately $3\times 10^{-4}$ and $4\times 10^{-4}$, respectively, while the tuned GRPO clipping range was roughly $0.2$ to $0.27$. This large difference is a direct consequence of the different definitions of importance ratios. Because $s_i(\theta)$ is a per-token-average ratio, small absolute deviations from 1 correspond to meaningful per-token average shifts and thus require very small $\varepsilon$ to control update magnitude. Token-level ratios, by contrast, are themselves token-probability ratios and naturally occupy a different numeric regime, necessitating larger clipping ranges.

An additional empirical observation helps to clarify what matters in practice: GSPO ends up clipping a much larger fraction of tokens (or equivalently excluding a larger fraction of token-gradients via response-level clipping) than GRPO does, by about two orders of magnitude in the reported experiments. Despite using many fewer token-gradient contributions in this clipped sense, GSPO achieves higher training efficiency and better final performance. This supports the interpretation that GRPO’s token-level gradients are noisy and inefficient; keeping more token-gradient terms does not produce better learning when those per-token importance weights are themselves unreliable. GSPO’s sequence-level formulation, with its different numeric scale and much smaller clipping window, yields more trustworthy gradient signals even while clipping more aggressively at the token level.

Concluding remark

GSPO replaces token-level importance ratios and token-wise clipping with sequence-level importance weights, length normalization, group-based z-score advantages, and response-level clipping. These design choices restore a principled form of importance sampling that matches the unit of reward, reduce variance via averaging over tokens, and yield practical benefits (stable training, better sample exploitation, and simpler handling of sparse-expert architectures) documented in the paper. The mathematical form of the GSPO objective and its gradient make explicit why sequence-level weighting removes a major source of token-wise instability present in token-level algorithms.
