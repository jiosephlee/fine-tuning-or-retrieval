\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 3: Background: PPO and GRPO

# PPO objective with token-level importance ratios and clipping

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a widely used policy-gradient style algorithm that constrains how far the updated policy may move from the policy that generated the training samples. In the language-modeling setting considered here, the policy is an autoregressive model $\pi_\theta$ that assigns a probability to each token $y_t$ conditioned on the query $x$ and previous tokens $y_{<t}$. PPO uses rollout samples generated by an older policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and re-weights those samples so they approximate an update under the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$. Concretely, the clipped PPO surrogate objective (omitting KL regularization) used in the paper can be written as

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{PPO}(\theta) =
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)}
\left[ \frac{1}{|y|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
\min\!\left( w_{t}(\theta) \,\widehat{A}_{t},\; \mathrm{clip}\!\big( w_{t}(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon \big) \,\widehat{A}_{t} \right)
\right],
\]

where the per-token importance ratio is

\[
w_{t}(\theta) \;=\; \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})},
\]

and $\widehat{A}_t$ is the (token-level) advantage estimate. The inner sum is averaged by $\frac{1}{|y|}$ so each response contributes a per-token average to the objective.

Intuitively, $w_t(\theta)$ measures how much the new policy changes the conditional distribution over the next token at time $t$, relative to the policy that produced the sample. Multiplying $w_t(\theta)$ by the advantage $\widehat{A}_t$ gives an importance-weighted correction intended to estimate the expected advantage under the new policy using samples from the old policy. The clipping term $\mathrm{clip}(w_t(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)$ is used to prevent individual importance ratios from becoming too large (or too small) and thus to keep each update within a “proximal” neighborhood of the old policy. This per-token application of importance ratios and clipping is the characteristic structure of PPO as applied to autoregressive language models in the paper.

# Role and burden of the value model in PPO

A crucial ingredient required by the PPO objective above is a reliable advantage estimate $\widehat{A}_t$ at each token position. In practice this advantage is typically obtained from a learned value function (a value model) that predicts expected returns (or scores) for partial trajectories, and the token advantages are computed from the value model’s estimates. The paper emphasizes two practical consequences of this design.

First, the value model itself is not cheap: it commonly has a size comparable to the policy model, so running and storing a separate value network imposes substantial memory and computational overhead during large-scale training. Second, and perhaps more fundamentally, PPO’s effectiveness depends on the reliability of the value estimates. Training an accurate value model is inherently challenging, and these challenges amplify as tasks become longer or more complex: producing trustworthy token-level advantage estimates across long responses or deep reasoning chains is difficult. The combination of heavy resource cost and the difficulty of obtaining trustworthy token-level value estimates motivates alternatives that avoid an explicit value model.

# GRPO objective: group-based (value-free) advantage estimation

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) proposes a simple and appealing value-free alternative to PPO. Rather than learning a value model to produce token-level advantages, GRPO generates a group of $G$ candidate responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ for the same query $x$ under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and computes a relative (group-normalized) advantage for each entire response based on the scores assigned by a verifier $r$. The GRPO surrogate objective used in the paper is

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta) =
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x)}
\left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\!\left( w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_{i,t},\; \mathrm{clip}\!\big( w_{i,t}(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon \big)\,\widehat{A}_{i,t} \right)
\right].
\]

GRPO defines the group-based (value-free) advantage for response $y_i$ as

\[
\widehat{A}_{i} \;=\; \frac{r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}\big(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\big)}
{\mathrm{std}\big(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\big)},
\]

and (in the common implementation used here) sets the token-level advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ equal to the response-level advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ for every token in $y_i$. Thus GRPO replaces the learned value function with a simple, empirically grounded group normalization: each response’s advantage is measured relative to the other responses sampled for the same query.

# Token-level ratios under GRPO and shared token advantages

Although GRPO avoids the value model by computing an advantage per response, it retains token-level importance ratios in exactly the same form as PPO. For token $y_{i,t}$ of response $y_i$ the importance ratio is

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta) \;=\; \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})},
\]

and every token in $y_i$ shares the same advantage $\widehat{A}_i$. Operationally, GRPO therefore distributes a single sequence-level advantage across all token positions of that sequence, while still applying per-token importance weights and per-token clipping when forming the surrogate. The objective still averages across tokens via the $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ factor, so each token contributes to the overall gradient, but the signal driving which tokens are encouraged or discouraged is the same across the whole sequence.

This hybrid design—response-level, value-free advantages combined with token-level importance corrections—gives GRPO its simplicity and practical attractiveness, since it sidesteps the need to train a separate value model yet keeps the familiar per-token clipping mechanics inherited from PPO.

# Practical benefits of GRPO and early successes

GRPO’s principal practical benefit is its value-free design. By replacing a learned token-level value function with a simple group-normalized reward, GRPO eliminates the memory and computational cost of running an additional value network, and removes an entire subsystem (value training, value-target computation, etc.) from the RL pipeline. This simplification can make large-scale RL on language models more tractable in settings where value models are expensive or difficult to train.

Because GRPO reuses the familiar token-wise surrogate structure of PPO while providing advantages from grouping and normalization, it gained practical traction as an attractive engineering trade-off: it preserves the per-token surrogate and clipping machinery that integrates well with autoregressive model training, while avoiding the burdens associated with value-function learning.

# Limitations: reliance on token-level correction and observed instability

Despite its practical appeal, the paper identifies a deep limitation in GRPO that can threaten stability at scale. The root of the problem is a misapplication of importance-sampling-style correction: importance sampling is intended to estimate expectations under a target distribution by re-weighting samples from a behavior distribution. Formally,

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)] \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}}\!\left[ \frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)} \, f(z) \right].
\]

A crucial condition for this identity to produce low-variance estimates is that the importance weights be estimated in a setting where the re-weighting averages over many samples from the behavior distribution so that the weights effectively correct the distributional mismatch.

GRPO, however, applies importance weights at each token conditional and computes each $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ from a single sampled token $y_{i,t}$ drawn from the next-token distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot \mid x,y_{i,<t})$. In other words, the token-level importance weight is computed on a single draw from the conditional next-token distribution, not by averaging over multiple draws from that conditional. Because of this, the token-level importance ratio in GRPO does not serve the intended role of reliably correcting an off-policy expectation; instead it acts like a high-variance multiplier on the per-token gradients. The paper emphasizes that this high-variance noise accumulates across token positions in a long sequence, and the situation is further worsened by the clipping mechanism: clipping changes the gradient dynamics nonlinearly and can amplify the adverse effect of noisy importance weights.

Empirically, this combination—token-level importance ratios estimated from single conditional samples, long response lengths, and per-token clipping—has been observed to lead to severe instabilities when training large language models. The authors report catastrophic and often irreversible model collapse in such settings: once a collapse occurs, simply reverting to a previous checkpoint or carefully re-tuning clipping hyperparameters does not reliably recover stable training. This practical failure mode motivates a reconsideration of the unit at which off-policy correction and clipping are applied: when rewards are given at the sequence level, applying importance correction at the sequence level (matching the unit of reward) avoids the single-sample token-level misapplication that underlies GRPO’s instability.

In short, GRPO’s combination of sequence-level, value-free advantages with token-level importance correction is practically attractive but can be ill-posed: the unit of importance-weight correction (token) does not match the unit of reward (sequence), and that mismatch—together with the single-sample token-weighting and clipping—can introduce high-variance noise that destabilizes large-scale RL training. This observation is a central motivation for the sequence-level approach developed later in the paper.
