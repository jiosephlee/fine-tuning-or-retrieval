\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 2: Preliminaries and Notation

# Policy as an autoregressive language model

In this work we treat an autoregressive language model as a policy in the reinforcement learning sense. Concretely, let $\theta$ denote the parameters of the model and write the policy as $\pi_{\theta}$. Given a query (or context) $x$ and a response (a sequence of tokens) $y = (y_1, y_2, \dots, y_{|y|})$, the autoregressive factorization defines the sequence likelihood under $\pi_{\theta}$ by the product of next-token conditionals:
\begin{align}
\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}),
\end{align}
where $y_{<t} = (y_1,\dots,y_{t-1})$ and $|y|$ denotes the number of tokens in $y$. This factorization is both a modeling assumption and an operational description: sampling a response sequentially means sampling each $y_t$ from the conditional $\pi_{\theta}(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t})$.

Working with the product form is most convenient after taking logarithms. The sequence log-likelihood and its gradient decompose additively over tokens:
\begin{align}
\log \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x) &= \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}),\\
\nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x) &= \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).
\end{align}
These identities are central when we write policy gradients or importance-sampling corrections: gradients that appear at the sequence level are sums of token-level gradients, and sequence likelihoods are products (or sums of log-probabilities). In practice we often evaluate both sampling and likelihood computation on the same parametrized model; when rollouts are produced under an older parameter vector $\theta_{\text{old}}$ but optimization proceeds with $\theta$, this distinction is the source of the off-policy phenomena discussed below.

# Queries, responses, and sequence likelihoods

We write $\mathcal{D}$ for the query set (a dataset or pool of contexts). A single training example is a query-response pair $(x,y)$ with $x \sim \mathcal{D}$ and $y$ produced by the policy. Because the policy is autoregressive, a response is a full sequence of discrete tokens and its probability is the sequence likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ defined above.

Two points about sequence likelihoods are worth emphasizing. First, many practical reward signals for language tasks are defined at the sequence level: the verifier assigns a score to the whole generated response $y$ given $x$, not to an isolated next-token decision. Second, sequence likelihoods can grow or shrink exponentially with sequence length when considered in raw product form; for numerical stability and for fair comparison across lengths it is common to work with the average log-probability per token,
\begin{align}
\frac{1}{|y|}\log \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)
= \frac{1}{|y|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}),
\end{align}
or to normalize sequence-level ratios by length. These normalized quantities are useful when forming importance ratios or when comparing responses of different lengths, because they keep numerical values on a similar scale and reduce variance caused by differing sequence lengths.

# Verifier and bounded reward in [0, 1]

The learning signal in policy optimization for language models typically comes from a verifier that scores query–response pairs. We denote the verifier by $r$, which maps each pair $(x,y)$ to a scalar reward
\begin{align}
r(x,y) \in [0,1].
\end{align}
The assumption that rewards lie in a bounded interval is convenient and is used in the algorithms discussed in the paper.

A bounded reward facilitates stable normalization and comparison between responses. For example, when multiple responses are generated for the same query (a common practice discussed next), their raw rewards can be standardized into advantages by subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation. Concretely, given a group of $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ to the same query $x$, a common group-based advantage estimator is
\begin{align}
\widehat{A}_i \;=\; \frac{r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}\!\big(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\big)}{\mathrm{std}\!\big(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\big)},
\end{align}
which produces advantages with zero mean and unit variance within the group. The boundedness of $r$ helps keep these statistics well behaved, which in turn stabilizes gradient updates that use these advantages.

# Query set and sampling procedures

Training proceeds by repeatedly sampling queries from the query set and generating model responses for those queries. A typical sampling step is
\begin{align}
x \sim \mathcal{D},\qquad \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\,\cdot\,\mid x),
\end{align}
where $G$ is the group size (the number of responses produced per query) and $\theta_{\text{old}}$ denotes the parameters of the policy used to generate the rollouts. Each response $y_i$ is produced by sequentially sampling tokens from the conditional distributions $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x, y_{<t})$.

Generating a group of responses for the same query serves two main purposes. First, a group provides a local baseline: by comparing rewards across responses to the same $x$, we can form relative advantages (the $\widehat{A}_i$ above) without training a separate value function. Second, sampling multiple responses captures some of the variance of the stochastic policy around a single context, which is useful for importance-sampling corrections and for stabilizing gradient estimates that would otherwise rely on a single sampled trajectory.

In practical pipelines the query set $\mathcal{D}$ may be fixed for a phase of training or periodically updated; the paper's experiments, for instance, discuss updating the query set during continued RL training. Regardless of whether $\mathcal{D}$ is static or changing, the sampling pattern always separates data collection (rollouts under some $\theta_{\text{old}}$) from policy updates (optimizing some objective with respect to $\theta$), and this separation is the source of off-policy effects described next.

# Large-batch rollouts, mini-batch gradient updates, and off-policy effects

To exploit hardware efficiently—especially when training large or sparse models and when responses are long—it is standard to collect rollouts in large batches. A large rollout batch is typically partitioned into multiple mini-batches for gradient updates: the same collected data are reused across several optimization steps or are split across workers. This practical engineering choice, however, creates an off-policy learning setting: the data were generated under the behavior policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, while the optimization updates are applied to the current parameter vector $\theta$ (which may differ from $\theta_{\text{old}}$).

Mathematically, when we want to evaluate or optimize an expectation under a target policy $\pi_{\text{tar}}$ but only have samples from a behavior policy $\pi_{\text{beh}}$, importance sampling gives the exact identity
\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}\big[ f(z) \big]
\;=\; \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[ \frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\, f(z) \right],
\end{align}
for any measurable function $f$. In our setting $z$ is a sampled response (or a trajectory of tokens) and $f$ is the learning signal derived from its reward and advantage. Importance weights $\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}$ therefore appear naturally to correct for the distributional mismatch introduced by off-policy sampling.

In practice, however, we only have finitely many samples and we usually perform multiple gradient steps using the same collected rollouts. Large or highly variable importance weights can cause high-variance gradient estimates and can make optimization unstable. Thus the combination of large-batch rollouts and multiple mini-batch updates is efficient for hardware but forces us to confront the off-policy correction problem carefully.

# Why clipping is necessary in off-policy training

A widely used practical approach to control the dangers of large importance weights is clipping. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) popularized a clipped surrogate objective that prevents each update from moving the policy too far away from the behavior policy used to collect samples. A standard form of the PPO surrogate objective (omitting additional regularization terms) is
\begin{align}
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)
&= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)}\!
\left[ \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} 
\min\!\Big( w_t(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_t,\; \mathrm{clip}\!\big(w_t(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\big)\,\widehat{A}_t\Big)
\right],
\end{align}
where the token-level importance ratio is
\begin{align}
w_t(\theta) \;=\; \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})},
\end{align}
and $\widehat{A}_t$ is an estimate of the (token-level) advantage. The clipping ensures that when an importance ratio departs far from $1$, its contribution to the surrogate objective is capped; this reduces variance and enforces a proximal constraint on the update.

Group-based methods like GRPO also use clipping but adopt group or sequence structure in the objective. For example, GRPO forms a per-response advantage by normalizing rewards within each group of $G$ responses and applies clipping at the token level across that group:
\begin{align}
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)
&= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)}
\left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\!\Big( w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\; \mathrm{clip}\!\big(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\big)\,\widehat{A}_i\Big)
\right],
\end{align}
where $w_{i,t}(\theta)=\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$ and $\widehat{A}_i$ is the group-normalized advantage for response $y_i$.

Clipping is necessary because it prevents a small number of high-weight (highly off-policy) samples from dominating the gradient estimates and making updates excessively large or unstable. However, clipping is a band-aid that assumes the importance ratios are meaningful and estimated at the right granularity. If the importance weights themselves are misapplied—e.g., computed at the token level from single next-token samples while the reward is defined at the sequence level—then clipping alone may not prevent high-variance noise from accumulating across a long sequence of tokens. In other words, clipping helps control variance arising from off-policy correction, but its effectiveness depends on using importance weights that correctly reflect the distributional mismatch at the same unit (token vs sequence) as the reward and optimization objective.

In summary, clipping is a practical and widely used mechanism that stabilizes off-policy policy optimization when large-batch rollouts and mini-batch updates produce distributional mismatch. Its correct use requires care about the unit of importance sampling and about how importance weights are estimated and applied.
