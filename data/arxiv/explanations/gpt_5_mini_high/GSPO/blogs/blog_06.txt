# From PPO to GRPO to GSPO: The Road to Stable RL for LLMs

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a key tool for pushing large language models (LLMs) further — from better reasoning to stronger code generation. But as models get bigger, sparser (Mixture-of-Experts, or MoE), and are asked to produce longer outputs, stability becomes the dominant engineering challenge. This post walks through the evolution from PPO to GRPO and then to Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), the algorithmic change that fixes a fundamental instability in prior approaches and makes large-scale RL for LLMs much more reliable.

## Quick recap: PPO and why it’s heavy for LLMs

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a widely-used policy-gradient method. In autoregressive language modeling it is usually applied token-by-token: given a sampled response $y=(y_1,\dots,y_{|y|})$ to query $x$, PPO builds a clipped objective over per-token importance ratios $w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}$, typically optimizing

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\left[\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
\min\big(w_t(\theta)\,\widehat A_t,\ \mathrm{clip}(w_t(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat A_t\big)\right],
\]

where $\widehat A_t$ are token advantages, usually estimated from a learned value model.

Two practical pain points with PPO for LLM RL:

- The value model is large (often comparable to the policy) and costly in memory and compute.
- Getting reliable per-token value estimates is hard, especially as sequences lengthen and tasks become complex.

These limitations motivated alternatives that avoid an explicit value model.

## GRPO: group-relative rewards — clever but fragile

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) sidesteps a learned value function by estimating advantages from a group of sampled responses to the same query. For a group $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ sampled from the old policy, GRPO defines each response’s advantage as the group-normalized reward

\[
\widehat A_i = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\text{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\})}{\text{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\})},
\]

then plugs those advantages into a PPO-style token-level objective:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\big(w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_{i},\ \mathrm{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i\big)\right],
\]

with the same per-token $w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$.

GRPO’s main advantage is removing the value model. But keeping token-level importance weighting creates a critical instability, especially for large models and long sequences — and this is where GSPO intervenes.

## Why token-level importance weights are a bad fit

The core statistical tool behind importance-weighted RL updates is importance sampling: to estimate an expectation under a target distribution you reweight samples drawn from a behavior distribution. A crucial property of such reweighting is that it operates at the same granularity as the samples and benefits from averaging across many samples from the behavior distribution for a stable correction.

GRPO applies importance weights at each token position based on a single sampled token from the respective next-token distribution. That is conceptually wrong for two reasons:

1. Per-token importance weights are estimated from a single sample of each next-token distribution, so they do not perform a reliable distribution correction and inject high variance.
2. That per-token noise accumulates along long sequences and interacts poorly with clipping, producing amplified, unpredictable gradient noise. Empirically this can cause catastrophic, often irreversible model collapse.

In short: the unit of importance-correction and clipping should match the unit that receives the reward. If the reward is sequence-level, the importance ratio and clipping should be sequence-level.

## GSPO: sequence-level importance and sequence-level clipping

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) fixes the mismatch by moving importance weighting and clipping to the sequence level. For each sampled response $y_i$ GSPO defines a length-normalized sequence importance ratio

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]

Length normalization (the $1/|y_i|$ exponent) keeps ratios numerically stable across different response lengths, so a single clipping range can be used.

The GSPO objective mirrors GRPO but applies sequence-level ratios and clipping:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\min\big(s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat A_i\big)\right].
\]

Intuition: reweight entire responses according to how probable the current policy thinks they are (relative to the old policy), and clip at that same sequence granularity. This aligns the unit of correction with the unit of reward and optimization.

## Gradient-level difference: equal token weighting vs. noisy per-token weights

A concise way to see what changed is to look at the gradients (ignoring clipping for clarity). GSPO’s gradient contains terms of the form

\[
\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_i|}\widehat A_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
\]

where every token in the response is weighted equally by the same sequence-level factor $s_i(\theta)$.

By contrast, GRPO’s gradient weights each token by its own per-token importance ratio inside the summation:

\[
\widehat A_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}).
\]

Those unequal per-token multiplicative weights vary across tokens, introduce variance, and can accumulate badly. GSPO removes that instability by applying a single, sequence-level weight to the whole gradient of the sequence log-likelihood.

## GSPO-token: token-wise advantage customization without noise

There are use-cases (multi-turn RL, token-level credit assignments) where you want per-token advantages. GSPO offers a variant, GSPO-token, that preserves the sequence-level scaling but allows per-token advantages. GSPO-token constructs per-token terms that are numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$ while stopping gradients through the per-token scaling factors (a detach operation). As a result, GSPO-token is flexible for token-level advantage signals while keeping the stable sequence-level weighting in the gradient.

## Empirical behavior: stability, efficiency, and MoE friendliness

The authors evaluated GSPO on a cold-start RL fine-tuning from a Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base checkpoint and measured training reward and downstream benchmarks (AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, CodeForces Elo). Key empirical findings reported:

- GSPO delivers stable training dynamics over long runs. Training continues to improve with more compute, updated query sets, and extended generation length.
- Under the same compute and query budget, GSPO achieves better training accuracy and downstream benchmark performance than GRPO.
- GSPO was applied successfully to the latest Qwen3 models and helped unlock notable performance gains.

Implementation details that illustrate differences in scale: in the experiments GSPO used clipping ranges (left/right) of approximately $3\mathrm{e}{-4}$ and $4\mathrm{e}{-4}$ respectively, while GRPO used much larger ranges around $0.2$ and $0.27$. This large numerical difference reflects the distinct definitions of importance ratios (sequence length–normalized vs. per-token).

A counter-intuitive but important empirical observation: GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens (orders of magnitude more) than GRPO under comparable settings, yet still achieves higher training efficiency. The interpretation is that token-level gradients in GRPO are noisy and inefficient to exploit, so using fewer but cleaner sequence-level examples is superior.

## Why GSPO matters for MoE models

MoE models introduce another source of instability: expert-activation volatility. After parameter updates, the set of activated experts for the same input can change; for example, in the studied Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model roughly 10% of experts activated for the same rollout sample can differ after a gradient update. This makes per-token likelihoods fluctuate wildly and breaks token-level importance ratios.

Previously, the team used a workaround called Routing Replay: cache the activated experts under the old policy and replay those routing modes under the new policy while computing per-token ratios. Routing Replay stabilizes GRPO training but adds memory, communication, and capacity overhead.

GSPO obviates Routing Replay because it relies on sequence-level likelihoods which are much less sensitive to per-token routing changes. Practically, GSPO stabilizes MoE RL training without routing replay, simplifying the training pipeline and letting MoE models use their full capacity.

## Practical infrastructure benefits

GSPO’s reliance on sequence-level likelihoods brings an infrastructure win. Training and inference engines often differ in numerical precision and implementation; GRPO’s per-token ratios usually require recomputing token likelihoods in the training engine to avoid precision drift. Sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of such discrepancies, opening the possibility of using likelihoods returned by inference engines directly and avoiding expensive recomputation. That is beneficial in scenarios like partial rollouts, multi-turn RL, and disaggregated training-inference systems.

## Takeaways

- Principle first: match the unit of importance sampling and clipping to the unit of reward. For sequence-level rewards, perform sequence-level importance correction and clipping.
- GRPO’s clever group-relative advantages remove the value model, but keeping token-level importance ratios injects high-variance noise that accumulates and can collapse training.
- GSPO replaces token-level ratios with a length-normalized sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$ and clips at the sequence level. This change stabilizes and improves RL for large LLMs, simplifies MoE training by removing the need for routing replay, and relaxes infrastructure constraints.
- Empirically, GSPO yields more stable training and better sample efficiency than GRPO and was instrumental in the RL-based improvements of the Qwen3 family.

If you are building large-scale RL for LLMs, GSPO is worth considering as the default: it keeps the value-model-free advantage of group-based methods while fixing a fundamental variance source that becomes crippling at scale.
