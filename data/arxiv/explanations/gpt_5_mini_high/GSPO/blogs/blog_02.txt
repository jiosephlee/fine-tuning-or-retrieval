# GSPO in Plain English: Reward the Whole Answer, Not Each Word

Reinforcement learning for language models often asks the model to improve from feedback on full responses (answers, essays, code, etc.). Yet many RL recipes correct for off-policy sampling at the token level — they re-weight each predicted word independently. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) makes a simple but powerful change: measure how likely the entire response is under the new policy versus the old one, normalize by length, and clip and optimize at the sequence level. Intuitively: grade the essay, not each word.

## The core problem: token-level importance weights are noisy

When you train with off-policy data (samples generated by the old policy but used to update the current policy), importance sampling is the standard way to correct for distribution mismatch:

$ \displaystyle \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[ \frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\, f(z)\right]. $

PPO-style methods typically use token-level importance ratios, e.g. for token $y_t$:

$w_t(\theta)=\dfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})},$

and apply clipping and per-token advantages. GRPO adapted this idea to grouped responses (multiple samples per query) by sharing a group-based advantage across tokens, but it still multiplies each token gradient by its own token-level ratio.

The problem is that a token-level ratio is computed from a single sample of the next-token distribution. Importance-sampling correction assumes you are reweighting whole-sample expectations — estimating token-level expectations from one sample is inherently high variance. That noise accumulates across long sequences, interacts badly with clipping, and in practice can lead to catastrophic and often irreversible model collapse when training large or long-response models.

## GSPO’s simple fix: sequence-level ratios and sequence-level clipping

GSPO defines importance weights for entire sequences, not individual tokens. For a response $y$ to query $x$ the sequence-level, length-normalized ratio is

$ \displaystyle s(\theta) \;=\; \left( \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y \mid x)} \right)^{\!1/|y|}
\;=\; \exp\!\left( \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})} \right). $

GSPO optimizes the clipped sequence objective across a group of $G$ sampled responses per query:

$ \displaystyle \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\min\big( s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\; \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat{A}_i \big)\right], $

where $\widehat{A}_i$ is the group-normalized advantage computed from the verifier rewards for the $G$ responses:
$\displaystyle \widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \text{mean}_j r(x,y_j)}{\text{std}_j r(x,y_j)}$.

Two small but important design choices: (1) use group-normalized rewards instead of a learned value model (avoids an expensive and fragile value network), and (2) length-normalize $s_i$ so ratios are comparable across responses of different lengths and so clipping ranges stay meaningful.

## Why this is more stable (gradient-level intuition)

Omitting clipping, the GSPO gradient has the form

$ \displaystyle \nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}
= \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \frac{1}{G}\sum_i s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdots) \right]. $

Compare to the GRPO/PPO-style gradient where each token is multiplied by its own token-level ratio:

$ \displaystyle \nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}
= \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \frac{1}{G}\sum_i \widehat{A}_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdots)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdots)}\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdots)
\right]. $

The crucial difference: GSPO multiplies the whole-token-sum for a response by one sequence-level factor $s_i(\theta)$, so all tokens in a response are weighted equally. GRPO multiplies each token gradient by its own noisy ratio. Those per-token multipliers vary and can amplify random fluctuations, especially over long sequences or when model internals (like Mixture-of-Experts routing) change. GSPO removes that instability source.

## Practical wins reported in the paper

- Stability and sample efficiency: GSPO produced steady training improvements and better benchmark performance than GRPO under comparable compute in the experiments described in the paper. It allowed continuous gains when increasing compute, updating queries, or extending generation length.

- Clipping behavior: GSPO clips at the sequence level. The authors observed a two-orders-of-magnitude difference in the fraction of clipped tokens between GSPO and GRPO (even after tuning clipping ranges), but GSPO still learned more efficiently. This suggests token-level gradients were noisy and less useful for learning.

- Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models: MoE training can change which experts activate for the same sample across updates (the paper reports roughly a 10% expert-activation change in a 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base after updates). That volatility makes token-level ratios fluctuate wildly. Previous practical mitigations required a Routing Replay strategy (cache and replay routing modes), which is memory- and communication-heavy and constrains model capacity. GSPO eliminates the dependence on Routing Replay because sequence likelihoods are robust to token-level routing fluctuations, simplifying and stabilizing MoE RL.

- Easier infrastructure: Sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of precision differences between inference engines and training engines, so GSPO can make it feasible to use inference-engine likelihoods directly (avoiding expensive recomputation) in some deployment/training setups.

## GSPO-token: token-wise advantages without the instability

If you need token-level advantages (for multi-turn setups, for example), GSPO offers a variant called GSPO-token. It keeps the same sequence-level importance signal but allows per-token advantages. The trick is to detach the sequence ratio when forming token-level weights so the gradient still uses the single stable sequence factor. Numerically the token weights equal the sequence weight, but this variant exposes per-token reward shaping while avoiding per-token ratio noise.

## How to think about GSPO in one sentence

Because your reward applies to whole responses, correct your off-policy sampling at the same level: compute a sequence-level likelihood ratio (length-normalized), normalize rewards across a small group of sampled responses, clip at the sequence level, and optimize. That alignment — unit of importance correction = unit of reward — is what reduces variance and stabilizes learning.

## Final notes

GSPO is a modest change in objective design with outsized practical benefits for large, long-response, and sparse (MoE) language models. It keeps the group-advantage idea (so you avoid a heavyweight value model), enforces a principled importance-sampling correction at the sequence scale, and stabilizes training that previously suffered from token-level noise. The paper reports that these properties contributed to improvements in the Qwen3 RL training runs where GSPO was applied.
