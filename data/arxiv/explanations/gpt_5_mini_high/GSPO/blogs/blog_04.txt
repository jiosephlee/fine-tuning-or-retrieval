\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

# MoE Without Meltdowns: How GSPO Tames Expert Routing Volatility

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures unlock much larger model capacity by routing tokens to different experts. That conditional routing is powerful — but it also creates a fragile interaction with common RL fixes that correct for off-policy data. After each gradient update, which experts are activated for the same sample can change, and token-level importance corrections built on those per-token likelihoods can oscillate wildly. The result: noisy gradients, amplified variance over long responses, and sometimes catastrophic, irreversible training collapse.

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) offers a simple, principled fix: stop trying to do off-policy correction token-by-token and do it at the sequence level instead. The authors show that this small change stabilizes RL for large MoE models, removes the need for costly routing replay workarounds, and yields more efficient training in practice — including improvements used in the Qwen3 family.

## Why token-level importance weights break for MoE

Most RL methods adapted to language models (PPO-style variants and GRPO) apply an importance ratio at each token,
$w_{i,t}(\theta) = \dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}$,
and then clip or weight per-token contributions. That looks reasonable at first glance, but it fails on two counts:

- Importance sampling is meant to correct a distributional mismatch by reweighting many samples from the behavior distribution. Using a single sampled token from the next-token distribution to define the importance weight does not perform the intended correction and introduces high variance instead.
- In MoE models, the activated expert set itself can change after each parameter update. The paper reports that for the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, roughly 10% of experts activated for the same rollout sample differ after an RL gradient update. Those activation changes make token-level likelihoods and hence $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ fluctuate sharply, invalidating the token-level corrections.

When these fluctuating token-level weights are summed over long sequences and then subject to clipping, the noise can accumulate and destabilize training — in some cases causing irreversible collapse.

## GSPO: clip, reward, and optimize whole responses

GSPO replaces token-level importance ratios with a sequence-level ratio that matches the unit of the reward (responses are scored at sequence level). For each sampled response $y_i$ to query $x$, GSPO computes a length-normalized sequence importance ratio

$s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).$

The objective then applies clipping and group-based advantages at the response level (the group advantage is the standardized score of each response within the group of $G$ samples for the same query). Two practical design choices matter:

- Length normalization ($1/|y_i|$ in the exponent) keeps the ratio numerically well behaved across different response lengths.
- Clipping is applied per response (not per token), so overly off-policy whole responses are excluded instead of intermittently clipping tokens inside a response.

That change has a direct effect on gradients. GSPO ends up weighting every token in the same response by the same factor $s_i(\theta)$, instead of weighting each token by a different per-token $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. Equal weighting across tokens removes a major source of instability introduced by unequal, single-sample-derived weights.

The authors also describe a GSPO-token variant that allows token-level advantages while still using the sequence-level scaling factor; numerically it is equivalent to GSPO when token advantages are identical, but provides flexibility for multi-turn or token-specific reward shaping.

## Routing Replay: what it is and why GSPO makes it unnecessary

To stabilize GRPO on MoE models, the group previously relied on a trick called Routing Replay: cache the expert routing decisions from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and replay those exact routing modes when evaluating $\pi_{\theta}$ for importance weights. That forces the numerator and denominator of $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ to use the same activated subnetwork, taming volatility in token-level ratios.

Routing Replay works, but it has downsides documented in the paper: it increases memory and communication overhead, complicates infrastructure, and can constrain how the model uses its capacity (because you're effectively replaying old routing patterns instead of letting the new policy choose freely).

GSPO removes the need for Routing Replay. Because it reasons about whole-sequence likelihoods rather than per-token likelihoods, the sequence score is much less sensitive to local routing changes. The MoE model's overall language modeling capacity remains intact, so the sequence-level likelihood does not jump wildly even when a subset of experts changes activation. That stability lets GSPO compute importance ratios conventionally and converge without the extra routing bookkeeping.

## Empirical highlights (what the paper reports)

The paper evaluates GSPO starting from a cold-start fine-tune of Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base and compares against GRPO. Key experimental notes:

- Each rollout batch was split into four mini-batches for gradient updates (an off-policy setting).
- GSPO used very small clipping ranges (left/right clipping set to 3e-4 and 4e-4 in the experiments). GRPO used much larger token-level clipping (0.2 and 0.27), tuned for fair comparison. The magnitude difference reflects that sequence-level and token-level importance ratios live on very different numerical scales.
- Benchmarks used include AIME'24 (Pass@1 averaged over 32 samplings), LiveCodeBench (Pass@1 averaged over 8 samplings), and CodeForces (Elo rating).

Reported outcomes:

- GSPO training is stable across runs and improves steadily with more training compute, refreshed query sets, and longer generation lengths.
- Under the same compute and consumed queries, GSPO reaches higher training accuracy and better benchmark performance than GRPO.
- GSPO succeeded at MoE RL without Routing Replay; GRPO required Routing Replay to converge stably.
- Curiously, GSPO clipped a much larger fraction of tokens (roughly two orders of magnitude more clipped tokens than GRPO, even after adjusting clipping ranges) yet still achieved higher training efficiency. That counterintuitive result suggests that GRPO's token-level gradients are intrinsically noisy and inefficient to exploit.

Finally, the paper notes that GSPO has been adopted in the development of the latest Qwen3 models, indicating its practical value at scale.

## Practical infrastructure wins

A small but important point for real systems: many inference stacks (vLLM, SGLang) and training stacks (Megatron) compute likelihoods with different numeric precisions. Previously teams recomputed likelihoods in the training engine to ensure consistent token-level ratios. Because GSPO only needs sequence-level likelihoods, which are empirically much more tolerant to small precision differences, it becomes feasible to use likelihoods directly from the inference engine. That can save recomputation cost and simplify rollout pipelines, which matters most at the massive scales where MoE + RL are applied.

## Takeaways

- Token-level importance ratios estimated from a single sampled token are high-variance and can be invalidated by MoE routing changes; summed across long responses they can destabilize training.
- GSPO defines importance ratios at the sequence level, applies sequence-level clipping and group-based advantages, and length-normalizes the ratio to control variance.
- That change stabilizes RL for MoE models and removes the need for Routing Replay, lowering memory/communication overhead and allowing the model to use its full capacity.
- Empirically, GSPO is more stable and more sample-efficient than GRPO, even though it clips many more tokens — a sign that GSPO’s learning signal is cleaner and more usable in practice.

If you work on large-scale RL for language models, especially with MoE architectures, the practical lesson is clear: make your importance weights speak the same language as your rewards. When rewards are sequence-level, correct at the sequence level. GSPO is a simple, principled instantiation of that idea — and it appears to tame many of the fragilities that used to plague MoE RL.
