\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

# Clipping, Reimagined: Cutting Whole Responses Yields Cleaner Gradients

Token-by-token clipping is the default in many RL-for-LM pipelines: compute a per-token importance ratio, clip it, and multiply it into the gradient. It sounds reasonable — after all, generation is autoregressive — but it turns out to be brittle in practice. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) shows a different and surprisingly effective design: compute importance ratios at the sequence level, clip whole responses, and use those sequence-level ratios to scale gradients. The result is fewer-but-cleaner gradient contributions that produce more stable and efficient learning, even when GSPO clips *more aggressively* (measured in clipped tokens) than token-level methods.

This post explains why this works, how length normalization keeps the sequence ratios well-behaved across answers of different lengths, and what practical consequences this has for large models (including Mixture-of-Experts).

## Why token-level clipping breaks down

Importance sampling rests on a simple identity:
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)]
= \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)} f(z)\right].
\]
The importance weight \(\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\) corrects for the mismatch between the sampling distribution and the target distribution, but only when it is applied to samples that meaningfully represent the behavior distribution. Crucially, importance weights are statistical weights that get their power from averaging over many samples.

GRPO-style token-level clipping applies an importance ratio at each token position:
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]
and uses those per-token ratios to scale token gradients. The problem is two-fold:

- Each token-level ratio is computed from a *single* sampled token from the next-token distribution. That single sample is not a reliable estimator of the distributional mismatch; the ratio is noisy rather than corrective.
- When you sum or average many such noisy per-token terms across long responses, the noise accumulates. In large models and long-response tasks this accumulation can become catastrophic: training becomes unstable and can collapse irreversibly.

Clipping per-token can help, but it can also interact badly with this high-variance signal and amplify pathological updates rather than prevent them.

## GSPO in a nutshell: clip entire responses

GSPO aligns the unit of optimization with the unit of reward. If the reward is assigned to a whole response \(y\), GSPO computes importance ratios and clipping at the sequence level. For a response \(y_i\) to query \(x\), GSPO uses the length-normalized sequence ratio
\[
s_i(\theta)
= \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]
GSPO then clips and scales at the response level. Using groups of responses per query, the group advantage for response \(i\) is the standardized reward
\[
\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_j)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_j)},
\]
and the per-response term is clipped with \(s_i(\theta)\) rather than per-token ratios.

There is also a GSPO-token variant that allows per-token advantages while still using the same sequence-level scaling factor. Numerically, when token advantages in a response are identical, GSPO and GSPO-token are equivalent.

## The gradient-level difference — neat and revealing

Compare the two gradient forms (clipping omitted for clarity). GSPO's gradient term for response \(y_i\) looks like:
\[
s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}).
\]
GRPO's gradient instead weights each token gradient by a different per-token ratio:
\[
\widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot).
\]

Key implications:

- GSPO multiplies the *average* token gradient by a single per-sequence scalar \(s_i(\theta)\). All tokens in the response are treated equally except for the common scaling factor, which is a smoothed aggregate of per-token changes (via the average log).
- GRPO multiplies each token gradient by its own noisy per-token ratio. These unequal multipliers can vary up and down and do not average out reliably when each is estimated from a single sampled token.

The result is that GRPO amplifies per-token sampling noise in the gradient, especially as sequence length grows. GSPO washes out that noise by consolidating evidence at the sequence level, producing a cleaner learning signal.

## Length normalization: why the 1/|y| exponent matters

The per-sequence ratio is raised to the power \(1/|y|\). That is not a cosmetic choice — it stabilizes the numerical behavior across answers of different lengths. Without length normalization, a change in the likelihood of a few tokens can inflate or deflate a sequence ratio exponentially with length; responses of different lengths would require very different clipping ranges. The form
\[
s_i(\theta) = \exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t}\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\right)
\]
is an average log-ratio, which reduces variance and keeps \(s_i(\theta)\) in a unified numerical range. That makes it practical to pick clipping thresholds that work across lengths.

## The counterintuitive empirical observation: more clipping, faster learning

One striking observation from the experiments is that GSPO ends up clipping a much larger fraction of *tokens* (roughly two orders of magnitude more) than GRPO, yet GSPO learns faster and more stably. Why?

Because GSPO clips at the response level. When a response is clipped, all of its tokens are considered clipped, so the token-level fraction of clipped elements can look very large. But GSPO discards noisy, off-policy responses wholesale and keeps fewer, higher-quality gradient contributors. Those cleaner gradients have lower variance and are more informative, so the optimizer makes more reliable progress despite using fewer token-level terms.

In contrast, GRPO keeps many token-level contributions that individually are noisy and inconsistently weighted; the optimizer is exposed to a noisy, high-variance signal that slows or destabilizes learning. In practice this can even lead to catastrophic model collapse with GRPO on large, long-response tasks, whereas GSPO maintains stable improvement.

## Extra wins: MoE stability and simpler RL infra

Two practical wins emerge from the sequence-level view:

- Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models: GRPO required a Routing Replay trick — caching activated experts under the old policy and replaying those routing modes under the new policy when computing per-token ratios — to stabilize token-level ratios. GSPO removes the need for Routing Replay because sequence-level likelihoods are far less sensitive to per-token routing volatility. That reduces memory and communication overhead and lets the model use its full capacity naturally.

- Training/inference engine mismatches: GSPO only needs sequence-level likelihoods, which are more tolerant of numerical differences between inference and training engines. This makes it more feasible to use likelihoods produced by an inference service directly, avoiding expensive recomputation on the training engine in some deployment setups.

## Practical takeaways

- Align the unit of off-policy correction with the unit of reward. If rewards are assigned at the response level, apply importance sampling and clipping at the response level.
- Normalize sequence ratios by length: use \(s_i(\theta)=\big(\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)\big)^{1/|y_i|}\) (or equivalently the average log-ratio). This reduces variance and makes clipping thresholds stable across lengths.
- Expect larger token-level clipping *counts* when clipping whole responses — this is fine and can be beneficial because the retained gradients are cleaner.
- If you need token-level customization (e.g., per-token advantages in multi-turn setups), use a GSPO-token variant that keeps the same sequence-level scaling while allowing per-token advantage signals.
- For MoE models and large-scale setups, GSPO can simplify infrastructure (no Routing Replay) and make RL training more robust.

Small practical numbers from the reported experiments: GSPO's clipping thresholds were very tight (left/right set to 3e-4 / 4e-4 in the reported run) while GRPO needed much larger per-token thresholds (0.2 / 0.27) to be comparable. Those values highlight that the numerical scale of the two importance-ratio definitions is very different; you should tune clipping ranges in the context of how you compute importance weights.

## Conclusion

Clipping entire responses instead of clipping token-by-token is more than a cosmetic change. It fixes a fundamental mismatch between the unit of reward and the unit of importance correction, dramatically reduces variance in the gradient signal, and yields more stable, efficient RL training for large language models. GSPO shows that fewer, cleaner gradients beat many noisy ones — and that length normalization is the simple trick that keeps sequence ratios well behaved across answers of different sizes.
