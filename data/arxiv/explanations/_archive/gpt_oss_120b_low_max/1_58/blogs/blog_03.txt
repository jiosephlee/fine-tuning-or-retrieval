# Scaling Laws for 1.58‑Bit Models: Why Bigger Isn’t Always Better… Until It Is

## Introduction  

Large language models (LLMs) have become the de‑facto workhorse for almost every natural‑language task. The usual recipe for better performance is simple: **make the model bigger**—more parameters, more training tokens, and more compute. But this scaling comes with a steep price tag in terms of memory, latency, and energy consumption.  

A recent paper, *The Era of 1‑bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits*, proposes a different approach. By quantizing every weight to a ternary set \(\{-1,0,+1\}\), the authors introduce **BitNet b1.58**, a model that effectively operates at 1.58 bits per weight while keeping activations at 8 bits. Remarkably, a 13 B‑parameter BitNet b1.58 can **outperform** a 3 B full‑precision (FP16) LLM on three critical deployment metrics: latency, memory footprint, and energy use.  

In this post we unpack the new scaling law that underlies this result, walk through the equivalence chart presented in the paper, and discuss how this changes the calculus for choosing model size in practice.

## The 1.58‑Bit Scaling Law in Plain English  

Traditional scaling laws for LLMs relate performance (e.g., perplexity or downstream accuracy) to two primary resources:

| Resource | Typical Trend |
|---|---|
| **Model size** (parameters) | Larger → lower perplexity |
| **Training tokens** | More → better generalization |

The trade‑off is that bigger models also demand *more* memory bandwidth, longer inference latency, and higher energy per token generated.

BitNet b1.58 reshapes this picture by **drastically reducing the cost of each parameter**. Because each weight is stored as a single ternary value (+1, 0, –1), the effective storage requirement drops from 16 bits (FP16) to roughly **1.58 bits**—the “0” bit adds a small overhead for representing sparsity but still yields an almost 10× compression factor.

The authors empirically discover that when you plot *inference cost* (latency, memory, energy) against *model size* for BitNet b1.58 versus FP16 LLaMA baselines, the curves intersect at surprising points:

- A **13 B** BitNet b1.58 is cheaper (in all three cost dimensions) than a **3 B** FP16 LLaMA.
- A **30 B** BitNet b1.58 beats a **7 B** FP16 model.
- A **70 B** BitNet b1.58 eclipses a **13 B** FP16 model.

In other words, **the compression factor is so large that you can afford to make the model *much* bigger without paying any of the usual inference penalties**. This is the new scaling law: *model performance now scales with effective bit‑width rather than raw parameter count*.

### Why Does “Bigger” Eventually Win Again?

While a 13 B BitNet b1.58 already overtakes a 3 B FP16 model on cost metrics, it still matches or exceeds its *accuracy* (perplexity and zero‑shot task scores). As you keep increasing the BitNet size, you reap two benefits simultaneously:

1. **Higher expressivity** – more parameters mean more capacity to capture linguistic patterns.
2. **Negligible extra cost** – each extra parameter adds only ~1.58 bits of storage and incurs integer addition instead of floating‑point multiplication.

Thus the classic “bigger is better but more expensive” rule is replaced by “bigger is better and still cheap,” up to the point where other bottlenecks (e.g., embedding layer size or communication overhead in distributed training) dominate.

## The Equivalence Chart Explained  

The paper presents a concise equivalence table that translates a BitNet size into an FP16 size with *comparable* inference cost:

| BitNet b1.58 Size | Equivalent FP16 Size (cost‑wise) |
|---|---|
| 13 B | 3 B |
| 30 B | 7 B |
| 70 B | 13 B |

Let’s break down how these numbers emerge.

### Memory Footprint  

A full‑precision weight occupies 2 bytes (FP16). A ternary weight needs only \(\lceil 1.58 \rceil = 2\) bits; however, in practice it can be packed into one byte with a small sparsity map. The memory ratio thus becomes:

\[
\frac{\text{FP16 memory}}{\text{BitNet b1.58 memory}} \approx \frac{16}{1.58} \approx 10.
\]

So a **13 B** BitNet consumes roughly the same memory as a **\(13/10 \approx 1.3\) B** FP16 model—but because we still need full‑precision embeddings (which dominate memory for larger models), the crossover point shifts up to around **3 B** FP16.

### Latency  

Inference latency is dominated by matrix multiplications performed by `nn.Linear`. In FP16 each multiply‑add costs both a floating‑point multiplication and an addition; BitNet replaces this with simple integer additions using the ternary weights plus an optional zero term (which effectively skips computation). Empirically the paper reports speed‑ups of:

- **2.71×** faster at 3 B,
- **4.1×** faster at 70 B,

compared to FP16 baselines.

Because latency scales roughly linearly with the number of FLOPs and BitNet cuts FLOPs by an order of magnitude, a larger BitNet can finish as fast as—or faster than—a much smaller FP16 model.

### Energy  

Energy consumption follows similar physics: integer addition consumes far less picojoules than floating-point multiplication on modern silicon (71× lower on a typical 7nm process according to cited energy models). Multiplying this savings by the number of operations yields an energy reduction that mirrors the memory and latency gains.

## Practical Implications for Model Sizing  

### Faster Prototyping & Deployment  

If you are building an API service or an on‑device assistant, you no longer have to choose between *accuracy* and *cost*. You can opt for a larger BitNet model that stays within your existing GPU memory budget while delivering lower latency—meaning higher throughput and better user experience.

### Re‑thinking Token Budgets  

Training cost is still tied to token count; however, because each parameter is cheap to store and compute with, you can allocate more parameters without exploding the overall training budget. The paper demonstrates this by training a 3‑B‑scale BitNet on **2 trillion tokens**, achieving superior zero‑shot scores over a similarly sized FP16 baseline trained on the same token budget.

### Hardware Design Opportunities  

Integer addition kernels already run efficiently on GPUs and even CPUs; but dedicated hardware—like Groq’s LPUs or future ASICs—could exploit the *near‑zero multiplication* nature of BitNet to push speed and energy gains even further. Designers might prioritize high-bandwidth SRAM for ternary weight storage rather than massive DRAM caches.

### Edge & Mobile Deployment  

Because each weight fits into less than two bits, you can store multi‑billion‑parameter models directly on edge devices without exhausting flash or RAM budgets. Combined with 8‑bit activations, inference becomes feasible on CPU‑only platforms—a huge step toward truly ubiquitous LLMs.

## When Bigger Isn’t Better (Yet)

The scaling law does not imply that infinite growth is always optimal:

- **Embedding Layers Remain Full Precision:** Even if weights are ternary, token embeddings are still stored in FP16/BF16 to preserve representational fidelity. As model size scales beyond ~30 B parameters, embeddings become a sizeable fraction of total memory.
- **Communication Overheads:** Distributed training or inference across multiple devices still incurs bandwidth costs proportional to activation size (8 bits here). At extreme scales these could become bottlenecks.
- **Algorithmic Limits:** Certain tasks may demand finer-grained numeric precision in intermediate activations that ternary weights alone cannot supply; specialized fine-tuning strategies may be needed.

Thus practitioners should still evaluate their specific workload before blindly upsizing.

## Takeaway Checklist  

- **If latency or memory is your primary constraint**, consider swapping an FP16 LLaMA baseline for a roughly tenfold larger BitNet b1.58 model.
- **For new training runs**, allocate token budgets as before but feel free to increase parameter count; you’ll likely see both better performance *and* lower inference cost.
- **Watch out for embedding size**, especially when pushing past 30–50 B parameters—this is where the equivalence curve may start deviating.
- **Explore hardware acceleration**, because integer addition kernels are already fast but could become even more efficient with purpose-built ASICs designed around ternary weights.

The era of “cheaper bigger models” has arrived with 1.58‑bit LLMs. By leveraging this new scaling law you can push model capacity without paying the traditional price tag—until other system components become dominant, at which point smarter engineering will take over again.
