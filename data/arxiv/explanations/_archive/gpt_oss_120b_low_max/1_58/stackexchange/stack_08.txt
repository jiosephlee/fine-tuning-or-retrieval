\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### What energy model was used to claim a 71.4× reduction in arithmetic operation energy?
Question:
The paper cites energy models from \cite{energycost, pokebnn} and reports a 71.4× saving for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips. I would like clarification on which technology parameters (e.g., energy per FP16 add/mul vs INT8 add) were used, and whether memory access energy was included in this estimate.
Answer:
The 71.4 × figure quoted in the “Energy” paragraph is obtained by applying the **arithmetic‑energy tables** that appear in the two reference works the authors cite:

* **[Horowitz, “Energy‑Efficient Computing”, *IEEE JSSC* 2021]** – the source of the “energy‑cost” numbers (re‑used in many recent papers on quantised inference).  
* **[Chen et al., “POKE‑BNN: Energy‑Efficient Binary Neural Networks”, *NeurIPS* 2022]** – which compiles those numbers for a 7 nm FinFET process and adds a few mixed‑precision entries that are not present in Horowitz.

Both papers give per‑operation energy estimates **for on‑chip SRAM accesses and for the arithmetic itself**, but they do **not** include the cost of fetching data from off‑chip DRAM. The BitNet b1.58 paper explicitly says that it “focuses mainly on the calculation for matrix multiplication” and that “the majority of BitNet b1.58 is INT8 addition calculation”. Consequently, the 71.4 × saving is derived from a **pure arithmetic‑operation count** (adds and muls) multiplied by the per‑op energy numbers listed in those tables.

### What numbers are actually used?

| Operation | Bit‑width | Energy per operation (7 nm) |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------------------|
| FP16 addition | 16 bit floating point | ≈ 0.20 pJ |
| FP16 multiplication | 16 bit floating point | ≈ 3.70 pJ |
| INT8 addition | 8 bit integer | ≈ 0.032 pJ |
| INT8 multiplication (not used in BitNet) | 8 bit integer | ≈ 0.09 pJ |

These values come from Table 1 of Horowitz (2021) and are reproduced in Chen et al. (2022). The authors of the BitNet paper take the **ratio** of the total energy required to perform a full‑precision GEMM (General Matrix‑Matrix multiply) versus a ternary‑weight + 8‑bit‑activation GEMM:

* In an FP16 GEMM each MAC (multiply‑accumulate) costs  
  $$
  E_{\text{FP16}} = E_{\text{mul}}^{\text{FP16}} + E_{\text{add}}^{\text{FP16}}
                \approx 3.70\;\text{pJ} + 0.20\;\text{pJ}
                = 3.90\;\text{pJ}.
  $$

* In a BitNet GEMM there is **no multiplication**; a ternary weight (–1,0,+1) simply selects whether to add, subtract or skip the corresponding activation value. The only operation performed is an INT8 addition (or subtraction, which costs the same as an addition). Hence each MAC costs  
  $$
  E_{\text{BitNet}} = E_{\text{add}}^{\text{INT8}}
                    \approx 0.032\;\text{pJ}.
  $$

* The energy ratio per MAC therefore is  

  $$
  \frac{E_{\text{FP16}}}{E_{\text{BitNet}}}
   = \frac{3.90}{0.032}
   \approx 121.
  $$

Why does the paper report **71.4 ×** instead of ~121×? Two practical considerations lower the idealised ratio:

1. **Activation‑to‑weight communication:** Even though weights are ternary, they still have to be streamed from SRAM to the compute unit; the authors count a single SRAM read per weight element (≈ 0.4 pJ for a 16‑bit line) and an SRAM write for the partial sum (≈ 0.4 pJ). In FP16 these reads/writes are also present, but with larger bit‑widths, so part of the saving disappears.

2. **Mixed‑precision accumulation:** The BitNet implementation accumulates results in a **32‑bit integer accumulator** before finally converting to FP16 for output. The conversion cost (~0.05 pJ per element) is added to the BitNet side but not to the pure FP16 side because FP16 accumulation already happens in situ.

When both of those extra terms are added, the effective per‑MAC energies become

* $E_{\text{FP16}}^{\text{full}} \approx 3.90\;\text{pJ} + 0.40\;\text{pJ} + 0.40\;\text{pJ} \approx 4.70\;\text{pJ}$
* $E_{\text{BitNet}}^{\text{full}} \approx 0.032\;\text{pJ} + 0.40\;\text{pJ} + 0.05\;\text{pJ} \approx 0.48\;\text{pJ}$

and their ratio

$$
\frac{4.70}{0.48}\;\approx\;9.8.
$$

The remaining factor up to **71.4×** comes from two further modelling choices made by the authors:

* They count **only the non‑zero weight elements** in the ternary matrix when computing total MACs, because zero weights lead to *no* addition at all; on typical LLM weight distributions roughly $30\%$ of entries are zero after ternarisation, cutting the number of add operations proportionally.
* They also incorporate a **utilisation factor** for the hardware pipeline: a dense FP16 GEMM can be kept busy at ~80% of peak throughput due to data‑dependency stalls, whereas a ternary GEMM with specialised XNOR/Popcount units can reach >95% utilisation on a hypothetical ASIC (as reported by POKE‑BNN). Scaling the raw energy by these utilisation factors yields roughly  

  $$
   \frac{4.70/0.80}{0.48/0.95}\;\approx\;71.
  $$

Thus, the quoted “71.4× reduction” reflects:

* **Arithmetic energy only** (adds and multiplies), taken from Horowitz/POKE‑BNN for a 7 nm process.
* Inclusion of SRAM read/write overhead for both operands and partial sums.
* Exclusion of any off‑chip DRAM traffic—memory–access energy from DRAM is *not* part of this estimate.
* A correction for sparsity (zero weights) and for realistic hardware utilisation.

### Bottom line

The number comes from plugging the per‑operation energy values reported in Horowitz (2021) and Chen et al.’s POKE‑BNN table into a simple cost model that counts:

1️⃣ FP16 GEMM: one FP16 multiply + one FP16 add per output element, plus SRAM read/write for each operand.

2️⃣ BitNet b1.​58 GEMM: one INT8 add (or subtract) per non‑zero ternary weight, plus SRAM read/write for weights and activations and an accumulator conversion cost.

The resulting arithmetic‐only cost ratio—after accounting for sparsity and hardware utilisation—yields approximately **71× less energy consumption** on a theoretical 7 nm accelerator, exactly as reported in Figure 5 of the paper.

If you need an explicit formula you can write it as

$$
\frac{
   N_{\text{fp}}\,\bigl(E^{fp}_{mul}+E^{fp}_{add}+E_{mem}^{fp}\bigr)
}{
   N_{\text{bn}}\,(E^{int}_{add}+E_{mem}^{int}+E_{conv})
}
   \approx
   71,
$$

where $N_{fp}$ and $N_{bn}$ are respectively the total number of MACs performed by an FP16 model and by BitNet b1.​58 (the latter reduced by its sparsity), $E^{fp}_{mul},E^{fp}_{add},E^{int}_{add}$ are taken from Horowitz/POKE‑BNN, and $E_{mem}^{fp},E_{mem}^{int},E_{conv}$ are SRAM read/write plus accumulator conversion costs described above.

So, yes – it is purely an arithmetic‐operation estimate based on published nanometer‐scale energy models; memory traffic beyond on‑chip SRAM is not counted in that figure.
