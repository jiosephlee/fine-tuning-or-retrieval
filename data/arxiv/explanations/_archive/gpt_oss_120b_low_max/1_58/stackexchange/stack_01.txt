\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How does the “absmean” quantization function determine the scaling factor γ?
Question:
In the BitNet b1.58 paper the weight quantization is defined as \(\widetilde{W}=\mathrm{RoundClip}(W/(\gamma+\epsilon),-1,1)\) with \(\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|\). I am confused about why the authors use the average absolute value of the weight matrix as the scaling factor instead of, say, the maximum absolute value or RMS. How does this choice affect the distribution of ternary weights and the stability of training?
Answer:
The “abs‑mean’’ quantizer used in BitNet b1.58 first divides the weight matrix by  

$$
\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|,
$$

i.e. the **average absolute value** of the weights, and then rounds the result to the nearest integer in $\{-1,0,+1\}$.  
At first sight one might wonder why the authors did not simply use the maximum absolute value $\max|W|$ (as is common in symmetric uniform quantisation) or an RMS‑type scale. The answer lies in the particular goals of a *ternary* representation and in the dynamics of optimisation with such a heavy discretisation.

### Keeping the ternary distribution balanced  

When a weight matrix is scaled by its mean absolute value, most entries lie in the interval $[-1,1]$ after division. Because $\gamma$ is an *average* rather than a *worst‑case* bound, a non‑trivial fraction of the scaled values will be **strictly inside** $(-1,1)$. Those values are rounded to 0, while values whose magnitude exceeds 1 (or is very close to it) become ±1. Consequently the ternary matrix $\widetilde W$ contains roughly three groups:

* **+1** for weights whose magnitude is larger than $\gamma$,
* **‑1** for weights whose magnitude is larger than $\gamma$ but negative,
* **0** for the bulk of weights whose magnitude is below $\gamma$.

If we had used $\max|W|$ as the scale, every entry would be mapped into the interval $[-1,1]$ but almost all of them would lie *very close* to zero after division (because only one entry determines the scale). The rounding step would then produce a matrix dominated by zeros and very few ±1 elements, which harms expressive power: most linear maps would collapse to near‑zero rows/columns and gradients would vanish.

On the opposite extreme, using an RMS scale would inflate most entries (the RMS is typically larger than the mean absolute value for a heavy‑tailed distribution). The result would be many weights being pushed to ±1, dramatically reducing sparsity. The zero state—crucial in BitNet b1.58 because it allows “feature filtering’’—would be under‑utilised and the model would behave more like a binary network rather than a ternary one.

Thus the **mean absolute value is a sweet spot**: it produces a balanced ternary distribution where roughly a constant proportion (empirically around 30–40 %) becomes zero while the rest are split between +1 and –1. This proportion stays stable across layers and training steps because it adapts automatically to the current spread of the weights.

### Stabilising gradient flow during training  

Training with a discrete weight set makes back‑propagation fragile: if most weights are forced to zero early on, gradients cannot propagate through those connections; if almost all are forced to ±1, updates become too coarse. By scaling with $\gamma$, each forward pass normalises the weight magnitudes to a *unit‑scale* that changes smoothly as training proceeds (the average absolute value varies slowly). Consequently:

* **Gradient magnitudes are comparable across layers.** Since every layer sees inputs that have been normalised to roughly unit variance, the optimiser (AdamW in the paper) does not need layer‑specific learning‑rate tuning.
* **The straight‑through estimator (STE) used for back‑propagation receives a well‑behaved surrogate gradient.** The STE treats $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ as identity during the backward pass; because the forward scaling keeps most values inside $[-1,1]$, this identity approximation is less biased than if values were consistently saturated at ±1.
* **Training remains robust to outliers.** A single exceptionally large weight would dominate a max‑based scale and destabilise all other updates; averaging dilutes such outliers.

The paper explicitly mentions that “the quantization function first scales the weight matrix by its average absolute value…”. Although it does not provide an ablation on other scaling choices, this design follows a long line of work on ternary/ binary networks (e.g., XNOR‑Net, Ternary Weight Networks) where *mean absolute scaling* was shown empirically to give both good accuracy and stable training dynamics.

### Connection to broader quantisation practice  

In low‑bit quantisation literature two families of scaling are common:

| Scaling | Typical use | Effect on ternary case |
|---|---|---|
| **max (symmetric)** | Guarantees no overflow; used for uniform 8‑bit quantisation | Produces highly sparse ternary maps (most zeros) → loss of capacity |
| **RMS / stddev** | Matches second moment; useful for Gaussian‑like distributions | Pushes many entries to saturating levels → reduces zero proportion |
| **Mean absolute (abs‑mean)** | Matches first moment; robust to outliers | Balances zero vs ±1 proportion → maintains expressive power & sparsity |

Therefore, choosing $\gamma$ as the average absolute value aligns with what is known to work best for *extreme* low‑bit (≤2 bits) quantisation: keep enough non‑zero entries to preserve model capacity while still reaping sparsity benefits.

### Summary  

The authors use $\gamma=\text{average }\lvert W\rvert$ because:

1. **It yields a balanced ternary distribution**, giving enough ±1 weights for representational power while preserving a substantial fraction of zeros that act as learned feature selectors.
2. **It stabilises training**, avoiding saturation or collapse that would arise from max‑ or RMS‑based scaling; gradients remain well‑scaled across layers.
3. **It is robust to outliers**, since an average smooths extreme values that would otherwise dominate a max‑based scale.

Consequently, the “absmean’’ quantizer produces ternary weight matrices that retain sufficient expressivity and enable stable optimisation, which explains why BitNet b1.58 can match full‑precision LLM performance despite using only three discrete weight levels.
