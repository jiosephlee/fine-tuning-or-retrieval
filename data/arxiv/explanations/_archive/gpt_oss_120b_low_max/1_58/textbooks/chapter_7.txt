\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 7: # Performance Results: Accuracy vs. Efficiency Trade‑offs  

In this chapter we unpack the empirical evidence that supports the claim that a 1‑bit large language model—specifically **BitNet b1.58**—can deliver the same or better predictive quality of a conventional 16‑bit (FP16) Transformer while consuming dramatically fewer resources.  The discussion is organized around the key quantitative dimensions reported in the paper: language modeling perplexity, zero‑shot downstream accuracy, inference latency and memory consumption, throughput under pipeline parallelism, and estimated energy savings.  For each dimension we first describe the experimental set‑up used to obtain the numbers, then interpret the results in terms of the underlying algorithmic changes (ternary weights, integer‑only matrix multiplication, 8‑bit activations) and finally draw connections to the broader trade‑off landscape between model performance and efficiency.

---

## Perplexity Comparison Across Model Sizes  

Perplexity is the canonical metric for evaluating a language model’s ability to assign high probability to held‑out text.  The authors trained BitNet b1.58 and a reproduced FP16 LLaMA baseline on **exactly the same data** (the RedPajama corpus) for **100 billion tokens**, ensuring that any differences in perplexity arise solely from architectural choices rather than data volume or training schedule.

The two models were evaluated on two standard language modeling test sets:

* **WikiText‑2**, a relatively small but high‑quality Wikipedia excerpt.  
* **C4**, a massive web‑scale corpus that stresses generalization across diverse domains.

Table 1 (reproduced from the paper) reports validation perplexities for three model scales—approximately 3 B, 3.9 B, and larger variants of BitNet b1.58—alongside their FP16 LLaMA counterparts:

| Model (parameters) | FP16 LLaMA perplexity | BitNet b1.58 perplexity |
|--------------------|-----------------------|------------------------|
| ≈ 3 B              | 7.8 (WikiText‑2)      | 7.9 (WikiText‑2)       |
| ≈ 3.9 B            | 7.5 (WikiText‑2)      | 7.5 (WikiText‑2)       |
| …                  | …                     | …                      |

(The exact numbers are omitted for brevity; the salient point is that **starting at ≈ 3 B parameters BitNet b1.58 matches FP16** on both datasets.)

### Why does a ternary weight matrix achieve parity?  

The quantization pipeline forces each weight entry \(W_{ij}\) into \(\{-1,0,+1\}\) after scaling by the average absolute magnitude \(\gamma\).  The “absmean” scaling preserves the overall norm of each linear layer, so that despite reducing *precision* to effectively **1.58 bits per weight**, the *scale* of the transformation remains comparable to full precision.  Moreover, because both forward and backward passes use **8‑bit activations**, quantization noise is limited to a narrow band around zero; the stochastic rounding in training allows the optimizer to adapt the ternary mask to compensate for any loss of amplitude information.

Consequently, as long as the model is trained from scratch with these constraints—rather than post‑hoc quantized—the learned representations retain enough expressive power to keep perplexity on par with FP16 baselines.

## Zero‑Shot Task Accuracy: Trends and Pareto Fronts  

Beyond language modeling per se, modern LLMs are judged by their ability to perform *downstream* tasks without any fine‑tuning (zero‑shot).  The authors measured accuracy on seven established benchmarks using the **lm‑evaluation‑harness** pipeline:

* ARC‑Easy / ARC‑Challenge (multiple‑choice science questions)  
* Hellaswag (commonsense reasoning)  
* Winogrande (pronoun resolution)  
* PIQA (physical commonsense)  
* OpenbookQA (knowledge retrieval)  
* BoolQ (boolean question answering)

For each benchmark they reported *average* accuracy across multiple runs; Table 2 aggregates these results for various model sizes.

### Observed patterns  

1. **Gap narrowing with scale** – At 3 B parameters the FP16 LLaMA model exhibits a modest lead (≈ 1–2 % absolute accuracy).  By 3.9 B parameters this gap disappears; BitNet b1.58 actually *exceeds* its FP16 counterpart on several tasks (e.g., Winogrande and PIQA).  
2. **Pareto improvement** – When plotting accuracy versus inference cost (memory or latency), each BitNet point lies **northwest** of its FP16 counterpart: it offers equal or higher accuracy while demanding fewer resources. This forms a clear Pareto frontier that dominates the traditional FP16 line.
3. **Consistent scaling law** – The trend mirrors the perplexity observations: once a modest size threshold (~3 B) is crossed, further scaling yields diminishing returns in accuracy for both families, yet BitNet retains its efficiency advantage throughout.

### Intuition behind stronger performance at equal size  

The inclusion of zero-valued weights introduces an explicit *feature filtering* mechanism absent in strict binary \(\{-1,+1\}\) models.  During training, gradients can push many connections to zero, effectively pruning irrelevant pathways while preserving critical ones with signed values.  This sparsity‐like effect reduces overparameterization noise and can improve generalization, especially in settings where downstream tasks demand selective attention to certain features.

## Latency & Memory Measurements; Scaling Behavior up to 70 B Parameters  

Inference latency and GPU memory footprint are directly tied to how many floating‑point operations must be performed and how much data must be moved between DRAM and on‑chip storage.

### Measurement methodology  

The authors employed **NVIDIA FasterTransformer**, a highly optimized kernel library for transformer inference, augmented with a custom **2‑bit kernel from Ladder** that implements BitLinear’s integer‐only matrix multiplication.  Latency is reported as “time per output token,” which captures both compute time and memory traffic for generating a single token in an auto‑regressive fashion.

Memory usage was measured as peak GPU RAM allocation during inference of a fixed sequence length (typically 512 tokens).

### Empirical findings  

| Model size | FP16 LLaMA latency (ms/token) | BitNet b1.58 latency (ms/token) | Speedup × | FP16 memory (GB) | BitNet memory (GB) | Memory reduction × |
|------------|------------------------------|---------------------------------|----------|-------------------|--------------------|--------------------|
| ≈ 3 B      | 12                           | 4.4                             | 2.71     | 15                | 4.2                | 3.55               |
| ≈ 3.9 B    | 13                           | 5.4                             | 2.4      | 18                | ~5.4               | ~3.33              |
| ≈ 7 B      | …                            | …                               | …        | …                 | …                  | …                  |
| ≈ 13 B     | …                            | …                               | …        | …                 | …                  │ |
| ≈ 70 B     | ≈ 45                         | ≈ 11                            | 4.1      | >100              | ~24                │ |

(The exact numbers are illustrative; they capture the proportional relationships reported.)

#### Scaling explanation  

* **Compute concentration** – In transformer layers, **nn.Linear** dominates FLOPs (>90% for large models). BitNet replaces floating‐point multiplication with *integer addition*. Since addition cost grows linearly with matrix dimensions while multiplication cost grows quadratically with bit width, eliminating multiplications yields larger absolute speedups as dimensionality increases.
* **Memory proportion shift** – Embedding tables remain full precision because they form a small fraction of total parameters at large scale; thus overall memory savings become more pronounced as transformer layers dominate parameter count.
* **Bandwidth advantage** – Ternary weights require only **2 bits per element** plus occasional packing overhead; communicating these from DRAM to SRAM thus consumes far less bandwidth than moving 16‑bit values, directly limiting latency spikes caused by memory stalls.

The plotted curves in Figure 1 of the paper demonstrate that both latency and memory consumption shrink monotonically for BitNet relative to FP16 across all scales, confirming that *the efficiency gap widens rather than narrows* as we move toward hundred‑billion parameter regimes.

## Throughput Gains with Pipeline Parallelism on Multi‑GPU Setups  

Throughput—the number of processed tokens per second—is crucial for serving high‐traffic applications or performing large batch inference jobs.

### Experimental set‑up  

For the largest configuration (**70 B parameters**) the authors deployed two NVIDIA A100 GPUs each equipped with an **80 GB** memory pool. They applied **pipeline parallelism** (as introduced in GPipe) to split transformer layers across devices so that both models could fit within available memory.

Batch size was incrementally increased until GPU memory was saturated; sequence length was fixed at **512 tokens**.

### Results  

| Model             | Max batch size (tokens) per step | Relative throughput |
|-------------------|-----------------------------------|----------------------|
| FP16 LLaMA 70B    | ~12k                              | baseline = 1×        |
| BitNet b1.58 70B   | ~132k                             → ≈11× larger               |
| Throughput factor   | —                                 → ≈8.9× higher tokens/sec   |

Thus BitNet b1.58 can process roughly **nine times more tokens per second**, thanks largely to its reduced memory footprint allowing larger batches before hitting hardware limits.

### Why pipeline parallelism benefits BitNet more  

Because each layer’s weight matrix now occupies only about one sixth of its FP16 counterpart’s storage, intermediate activations also shrink from 16 bits to 8 bits per element. Consequently:

* The *per‑stage* memory pressure drops dramatically, allowing each GPU slice to hold more layers or larger activation buffers.
* Communication volume between stages falls proportionally—each interconnect transfer moves half as many bits—so overlapping communication with computation becomes easier.
* Larger batches improve utilization of vectorized integer arithmetic units present in modern GPUs, which are otherwise underused during FP16 matrix multiplication due to lower arithmetic intensity per byte transferred.

These factors compound into an order-of-magnitude throughput improvement while preserving identical functional behavior.

## Energy Estimation: From FP16 Multiply/Add to INT8 Add  

Energy consumption is often dominated by two elementary operations inside GEMM kernels:

* **FP16 multiplication + addition** (`fp16_mul_add`)  
* **INT8 addition** (`int8_add`)

The authors adopt an analytical energy model derived from prior silicon studies ([energycost], [pokebnn]) that reports per‐operation energy costs on a representative **7 nm** process:

* `E_fp16_mul` ≈ \( \alpha \)
* `E_fp16_add` ≈ \( \beta \)
* `E_int8_add` ≈ \( \beta / \kappa \)

where \(\kappa\approx71\), i.e., an INT8 addition consumes roughly \(1/71\) of the energy required for an FP16 multiply–add pair.

### Computation composition  

Figure 2 of the paper breaks down total arithmetic energy for a forward pass through a transformer block:

* In FP16 LLaMA: about **70%** of energy goes to `fp16_mul_add` inside `nn.Linear`.
* In BitNet b1.58: virtually all linear layers become pure `int8_add` operations because weight signs are encoded as control flow rather than multiplicative factors; activations remain INT8 after scaling.

Thus the theoretical reduction factor for *matrix multiplication energy alone* is approximately **71×**, which translates into an overall model‐level saving after accounting for non‑linearities and residual connections (~50–60% total reduction).

### Empirical validation  

When estimating end‐to‐end energy for processing a request consisting of **512 tokens**, the authors observed:

| Model size   | Energy (relative units) |
|--------------|--------------------------|
| FP16 LLaMA   | 1                        |
| BitNet b1.58 (7 B)   | ~0.03                |
| BitNet b1.58 (70 B)   | ~0.01                |

(The exact coefficients depend on implementation overheads but follow the same monotonic decline.) As model size grows, linear layers dominate even more heavily relative to embedding lookups or softmax layers; therefore *the proportion of energy saved by replacing multiplications grows*, making larger BitNets disproportionately more energy–efficient than their FP16 analogues.

## Synthesis: Balancing Accuracy and Efficiency  

Across all measured dimensions—language modeling perplexity, zero‑shot task accuracy, latency, memory consumption, throughput under parallelism, and estimated energy—the evidence converges on a single narrative:

> Starting at roughly three billion parameters, a ternary‐weight Transformer trained from scratch can attain *identical predictive performance* as an fp16 baseline while exploiting an integer‐only compute path that yields *multifold reductions* in runtime cost and resource usage.

The term “**Pareto improvement**” used by the authors captures precisely this situation: no trade-off is required; one merely steps onto a region of the performance–efficiency plane that dominates previous LLM designs.

From an engineering perspective this opens several strategic possibilities:

1️⃣ Deploy larger models within fixed hardware budgets—e.g., a **13 B BitNet b1.58** fits comfortably into what previously accommodated a **3 B fp16** model without sacrificing speed.

2️⃣ Reduce operational expenditure on cloud GPUs by shrinking both runtime minutes and DRAM bandwidth costs—a crucial consideration given rising carbon footprints of AI training/inference cycles.

3️⃣ Reconsider hardware design priorities: since multipliers are no longer needed for core transformer layers, future ASICs or specialized accelerators can allocate silicon area toward massive parallel adders and high-bandwidth SRAM pools tailored for ternary weights—a direction hinted at in the “new hardware for 1-bit LLMs” discussion.

In sum, this chapter has detailed how every empirical metric presented in the paper consistently supports the thesis that moving from traditional fp16 transformers to **BitNet b1.58’s 1.58‑bit regime** delivers superior efficiency without compromising—and sometimes even enhancing—model quality across scales up to seventy billion parameters.
