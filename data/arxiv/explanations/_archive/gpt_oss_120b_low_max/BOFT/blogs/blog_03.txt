\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

# The Information Transmission View: How Graph Theory Helps Design Better Finetuning Layers

Finetuning massive foundation models—whether they are vision transformers, large language models, or text‑to‑image diffusion systems—has become a daily routine for researchers.  The challenge is not just *how* to adapt a pretrained model, but *how* to do it while touching as few parameters as possible.  

A recent line of work called **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** tackles this by multiplying each pretrained linear layer with a learned orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \).  Orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise neuron angles and keep the spectral norm of the original weights unchanged, which often translates into better stability and generalization.  The downside?  An orthogonal matrix of size \( d \times d \) normally needs \( \mathcal{O}(d^2) \) trainable entries—far too many for truly parameter‑efficient finetuning.

Enter the **Information Transmission View**.  By reinterpreting the factorisation of an orthogonal matrix as a problem of routing information on a grid‑structured graph, we obtain clean design criteria that guide us toward much more compact parameterizations.  This perspective also reveals why **butterfly graphs**, the backbone of the fast Fourier transform, are ideally suited for building dense orthogonal matrices with only \( \mathcal{O}(d\log d) \) trainable parameters.

Below we walk through this graph‑theoretic lens, explain the notions of “edges” and “paths,” and show how they culminate in the **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)** finetuning method.

---

## 1. From Orthogonal Matrices to Information Flow

Consider a single linear layer in a pretrained model with weight matrix \( \mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n} \).  OFT replaces the forward pass

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{W}^0)^{\!\top}\!\mathbf{x}
\]

by

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0)^{\!\top}\!\mathbf{x},
\qquad \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\; \mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\!\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}_d.
\]

If we write \( \mathbf{R} \) as a product of \( m \) square factors,

\[
\mathbf{R}= \underbrace{\mathbf{B}_m}_{\text{layer }m}
               \underbrace{\mathbf{B}_{m-1}}_{\text{layer }m-1}
               \dots
               \underbrace{\mathbf{B}_1}_{\text{layer }1},
\]

each factor can be thought of as a **step** that shuffles and mixes information across neurons.

### The Grid Graph Interpretation

Create a grid with \( d \) nodes on each level and \( m+1 \) levels total (one level for the input, one for each factor, and one for the output).  An entry \( (\mathbf{B}_i)_{jk}\neq0 \) corresponds to a directed edge from node \(k\) on level \(i-1\) to node \(j\) on level \(i\).  

Multiplying all factors together is then exactly the same as *traversing* from the first level to the last along **paths** composed of these edges.  The resulting dense matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) has a non‑zero entry \( R_{pq} \) if and only if there exists at least one path that connects input node \(q\) (level 0) to output node \(p\) (level \(m+1\)).

Thus, constructing a **dense orthogonal matrix** translates into designing a layered graph where **every source node can reach every sink node**, using as few edges as possible.

---

## 2. Two Design Desiderata

From the graph viewpoint two natural criteria emerge:

| Criterion | Graph‑theoretic meaning | Why it matters |
|-----------|------------------------|----------------|
| **Dense connectivity** | Every node on level 0 must have at least one directed path to every node on level \(m+1\). | Guarantees that the final matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) is dense (no zero entries), preserving full expressive power. |
| **Minimum free edges** | The total number of *trainable* edges (i.e., non‑zero entries that are not forced by orthogonality) should be as low as possible. | Directly reduces the number of learnable parameters, giving us parameter efficiency. |

A subtlety: orthogonal matrices impose extra constraints on edge patterns. For an orthogonal factor \( \mathbf{B}_i \), each row must be an orthonormal vector; consequently each level must contain exactly \( d \) edges forming a bijection between its nodes and those of the next level. Those “mandatory” edges are not learnable (they are essentially fixed signs), so we only count *free* edges that we actually get to optimise.

The goal becomes: **connect all sources to all sinks with the smallest possible set of free edges while respecting orthogonality**.

---

## 3. Why Block‑Diagonal Sparsity Falls Short

The original OFT paper used block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices:

\[
\mathbf{R}= \operatorname{diag}\big(\mathbf{R}_1,\dots,\mathbf{R}_r\big),
\qquad
\mathbf{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b},\; br=d.
\]

In graph terms this corresponds to splitting the node set into \( r \) independent islands—edges never cross between blocks. Consequently, many source–sink pairs have *no* path at all, violating dense connectivity unless we set \( b=d \), which defeats the purpose of parameter saving.

Thus, block‑diagonal sparsity trades expressivity for fewer parameters but creates an undesirable inductive bias: it forces neurons in different blocks never to interact during finetuning.

---

## 4. Butterfly Graphs: The Optimal Trade‑off

The **butterfly graph**—famously used in Cooley‑Tukey’s fast Fourier transform—provides precisely what we need:

* It consists of \( \log_2 d \) layers.
* Each layer connects every node to two nodes in the next layer via a fixed pattern reminiscent of “butterfly wings.”
* The total number of free edges is only \( \mathcal{O}(d\log d) \).

Visually, each step interleaves pairs of nodes, swaps halves, and repeats; after \( \log_2 d \) steps every source can reach every sink through a unique combination of those sparse connections.

### Parameter Count

Each butterfly factor is mostly diagonal blocks; each block contributes only a small constant number of trainable parameters (e.g., a single rotation angle for a \(2\times2\) orthogonal block). With \( m = \log_2 d \) factors we obtain

\[
\#\text{params} = O(d\,\log d),
\]

a dramatic reduction from \( O(d^{2}) \). Yet because paths exist between all source–sink pairs, the resulting product matrix is dense.

---

## 5. From Theory to Practice: Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)

Armed with butterfly graphs, we can now define a concrete finetuning layer:

1. **Factorisation** – Represent the learnable orthogonal matrix as  
   \[
   \mathbf{R}(m,b)=
   \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
   \]
   where each factor \( \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} \) is an orthogonal butterfly component with block size \(b\).  
   - If \(b=2\), each component is built from many independent \(2\times2\) rotations (the classic butterfly).  
   - Larger blocks allow more flexibility at modest extra cost.

2. **Orthogonality enforcement** – Each component is parametrised via a Cayley transform or directly as a small rotation matrix; thus every factor stays perfectly orthogonal throughout training.

3. **Zero‑initialisation** – Set all rotation angles to zero so that every component starts as an identity matrix; consequently the whole product starts as identity and the finetuned model initially coincides exactly with the pretrained one.

4. **Multiplicative Dropout** – Randomly replace selected butterfly components or interior blocks by identities during training, preventing over‑fitting without breaking multiplicative structure.

Because we multiply all components **before** applying them to the frozen weight matrix (\( (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^0 )^{\!\top}\)), BOFT incurs **no extra inference cost**: after training we simply fold the learned orthogonal matrix into the original weights.

---

## 6. Why Butterflies Shine: Expressivity vs Regularity

One might ask whether restricting ourselves to butterfly‑structured orthogonal matrices hurts expressivity compared to an unrestricted dense orthogonal matrix. Empirical studies show:

* With only a few dozen thousand parameters (for \(d=1024\)), BOFT can approximate random dense orthogonal matrices much better than block‑diagonal OFT at comparable parameter budgets.
* Theoretical analysis (Theorem 1 in the paper) proves that composing a modest number of butterfly matrices can generate *any* orthogonal matrix when we allow alternating transposes—so BOFT sits in a sweet spot between full freedom and strong structural priors.

The structured sparsity acts like an inductive bias similar to how low‑rank adapters bias solutions toward smoother changes; here the bias leans toward transforms familiar from signal processing (FFT, Hadamard), which appear to regularize finetuning and improve downstream generalization.

---

## 7. Takeaways

1. **Graph theory offers a clean abstraction for designing compact orthogonal transforms**—the problem becomes “connect every source to every sink using few edges while respecting orthogonality.”
2. **Butterfly graphs satisfy both desiderata**: dense connectivity with only \( O(d\log d)\) trainable edges.
3. **BOFT turns this insight into a practical finetuning recipe**, bringing down OFT’s parameter count from quadratic to near‑linear without sacrificing stability or performance.
4. **The approach is generic**—it works for vision transformers, large language models, and diffusion models alike, demonstrating that orthogonal finetuning is not limited to any single modality.

If you’re building PEFT pipelines and care about preserving pretrained knowledge while keeping your parameter budget tight, give BOFT a try.  Its elegant blend of information theory, graph design, and classic signal‑processing structures might just become your new go‑to tool for efficient model adaptation.
