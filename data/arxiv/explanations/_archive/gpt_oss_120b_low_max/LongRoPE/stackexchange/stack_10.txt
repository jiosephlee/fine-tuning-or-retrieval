\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### Can LongRoPE be applied to models that use absolute sinusoidal positional encodings instead of RoPE?
Question:
The paper repeatedly states that LongRoPE works for any LLM based on RoPE. If an LLM uses classic sinusoidal embeddings or learned absolute embeddings, could a similar evolutionary search be performed on those embeddings, or are there fundamental obstacles (e.g., lack of rotational invariance) that prevent direct transfer?
Answer:
LongRoPE relies on a property that is unique to rotary‑position embeddings (RoPE): the *rotational invariance* of the attention scores.  
In a transformer with RoPE, the query‑key inner‑product for two tokens at positions $n$ and $m$ can be written as  

$$
\mathbf{q}_n^{\!\top}\mathbf{k}_m
 = \sum_{i=0}^{d/2-1}
   \bigl[\cos ((n-m)\theta_i)\; \mathbf{q}^{(i)}_n\!\cdot\!\mathbf{k}^{(i)}_m
   +\sin ((n-m)\theta_i)\; \mathbf{q}^{(i)}_n\!\times\!\mathbf{k}^{(i)}_m\bigr],
$$

i.e. the relative distance $(n-m)$ appears **only** through the sinusoidal factor $\cos((n-m)\theta_i),\sin((n-m)\theta_i)$.  
If we change the set of rotation angles $\{\theta_i\}$ by a scalar factor $\lambda_i$ (the “rescale factor” that LongRoPE searches), the whole attention matrix is unchanged up to a harmless re‑scaling of the angles; the model therefore continues to interpret the same relative positions even after we have interpolated or extrapolated the angles to accommodate new token indices.  
This invariance makes it possible to keep the original pre‑trained weights while only modifying the RoPE table – exactly what LongRoPE does.

Absolute positional embeddings, whether sinusoidal (the classic $\sin (n/\beta^i),\cos (n/\beta^i)$ used in the original Transformer paper) or learned vectors, do **not** enjoy this invariance. Their contribution to a query or key is added element‑wise, and the attention score depends on the *absolute* embedding vectors:

$$
\mathbf{q}_n^{\!\top}\mathbf{k}_m
 = (\mathbf{w}_Q \mathbf{x}_n + \mathbf{p}_n)^{\!\top}(\mathbf{w}_K \mathbf{x}_m + \mathbf{p}_m),
$$

where $\mathbf{p}_n,\mathbf{p}_m$ are the absolute position vectors.  
If we alter the mapping from token index $n$ to $\mathbf{p}_n$ (e.g., by scaling frequencies or interpolating), we change **both** query and key vectors in a way that is not expressible as a simple relative‑distance function. Consequently, the attention pattern learned during pre‑training is destroyed, and the model typically experiences a huge spike in loss – exactly the “catastrophic values” described in the paper for naive extensions.

Because of this fundamental difference, two obstacles prevent a straight‑forward transfer of LongRoPE to absolute embeddings:

1. **Lack of rotational invariance** – there is no closed‑form relation between “new” and “old” absolute positions that preserves dot‑products. Any interpolation must be learned from scratch, which defeats LongRoPE’s goal of re‑using the pretrained weights with only a few fine‑tuning steps.

2. **Non‑linear interaction with token content** – absolute embeddings are added to token representations before projection, so scaling or reshaping them changes how each individual token interacts with every other token, not just their relative distance. An evolutionary search over per‑dimension scale factors would need to compensate for this interaction across all layers, which is far more complex than searching over scalar rescale factors for RoPE.

In principle you could run an evolutionary search on a model that uses absolute sinusoidal embeddings, but you would have to search over *full embedding vectors* (or at least large blocks of them) rather than simple scalar factors, and you would still need extensive fine‑tuning on extremely long sequences because the model has no built‑in notion of relative position that survives interpolation. The paper’s experiments show that even with RoPE, fine‑tuning becomes necessary once the extension ratio exceeds about $8\times$; without rotational invariance this need would be much more severe.

Therefore, LongRoPE is specifically designed for RoPE‑based LLMs. For models that use classic sinusoidal or learned absolute positional encodings, you would need a different strategy—typically one that either retrains the position embeddings from scratch on long texts or replaces them with a relative‑position mechanism (e.g., adding a separate learnable bias or switching to RoPE). The non‑uniform search ideas introduced in LongRoPE could inspire similar searches over absolute embeddings, but they would not enjoy the same theoretical guarantee of preserving attention patterns and would likely require far more data and compute.
