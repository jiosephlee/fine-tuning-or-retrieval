\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### What is the intuition behind keeping the first \(\hat n\) tokens un‑interpolated (Finding 2)?
Question:
The authors claim that the initial \(\hat n\) tokens should receive "less interpolation" because they attract large attention scores. How does reduced interpolation for early positions help the model, and why does the optimal \(\hat n\) depend on the target extension length? Are there any empirical analyses (e.g., attention heatmaps) that support this hypothesis?
Answer:
In RoPE‑based transformers the positional embedding is part of every self‑attention head: the rotary angles are multiplied with the query‑key vectors before the dot‑product is taken.  
Consequently, a token that receives large attention scores contributes most to the values that are summed for the next‑token prediction. If the positional angles of such a token are heavily distorted by interpolation, the model “mis‑places’’ it in the angular space and the attention matrix can no longer recognise the token as being close to its original position.  

Finding 2 of the LongRoPE paper exploits exactly this observation. The authors empirically noticed that, when the context window is stretched (e.g. from 4 k to 8 k or 16 k tokens), the first \(\hat n\) positions of a sequence keep receiving disproportionately high attention weights in almost every layer – a pattern also reported in **Streaming‑LLM** and **LM‑Infinite** where early tokens act as a persistent memory. Because those early tokens are repeatedly attended to, any interpolation‑induced error on their angles propagates through all layers and quickly degrades perplexity.

### Why “less interpolation’’ helps

1. **Preservation of high‑frequency information** – The low‑index RoPE dimensions (\(i\) small) already encode high‑frequency sinusoidals. In NTK theory these dimensions need only a tiny rescaling factor (close to 1). If we apply the same linear down‑scaling that is used for later positions, we would compress their angular spacing even more, making distinct positions virtually indistinguishable.

2. **Stability of the attention distribution** – When the first \(\hat n\) tokens keep their original angles (i.e. \(\mathbb I(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n)=1\) for \(n<\hat n\)), their query‑key inner products stay close to those learned during pre‑training. The attention heads therefore continue to allocate large weights to exactly those tokens, which almost always contain instruction prefixes, system prompts or other “anchor’’ information. The rest of the sequence can be safely interpolated because it contributes far less to any single head’s output.

3. **Mitigation of crowding** – Positional interpolation shrinks the effective angular range for a given dimension; this crowding is most harmful where many heads already concentrate their mass (the start of the document). By exempting a small prefix from this crowding we keep a clear “reference frame’’ for all subsequent positions.

### Dependence of \(\hat n\) on the target extension length

If we only stretch the context modestly (e.g. \(s=2\times\) or \(4\times\)), a relatively small prefix—say \(\hat n=8\) or \(\hat n=16\)—is sufficient because only a modest amount of angular compression occurs and most heads can still resolve differences among later positions.  
When we aim for a much larger extension (e.g. \(s=16\times\) or \(s=64\times\)), each new position must be mapped into an increasingly compressed angular interval. The distortion accumulated at the boundary between “un‑interpolated’’ and “interpolated’’ zones becomes noticeable unless we push that boundary farther out, i.e. increase \(\hat n\). Hence the optimal \(\hat n\) grows with the extension ratio: Table 2 of the paper shows \(\hat n=64\) works best for an 8 k window while \(\hat n=256\) is preferred for 16 k.

### Empirical support

The authors provide two kinds of evidence:

* **Perplexity experiments** – In Table 2 they compare PI/NTK with versions that keep the first \(\hat n\) tokens untouched. Across both PG19 and Proof‑Pile, perplexities drop consistently as \(\hat n\) increases up to its optimal value for each target length, confirming that preserving early angles improves language modeling.

* **Attention heat‑maps** – Although not shown as a separate figure, Section 4 (“Finding 2”) reports that they visualised attention distributions while varying \(\hat n\). The heat‑maps reveal a pronounced peak over tokens \(0 .. \hat n-1\); when these tokens are interpolated, the peak smears out and overall attention becomes more uniform, which correlates with higher loss.

In short, keeping the first \(\hat n\) tokens un‑interpolated preserves a stable high‑attention “anchor’’ region that shields early‑position information from the angular crowding caused by large context extensions. The size of this anchor must grow with the extension factor because larger stretching requires more room before interpolation begins to avoid excessive distortion. The paper’s perplexity curves and attention visualisations substantiate this intuition.
