# Harvesting Math from the Web: How DeepSeekMath Built a 120‑Billion‑Token Corpus  

When the DeepSeek team announced **DeepSeekMath‑7B**, the most capable open‑source model on the competition‑level MATH benchmark, the spotlight fell on its new reinforcement‑learning tricks and code‑first pre‑training. Yet a quieter but equally impressive achievement underlies those numbers: a **120 billion‑token math corpus** scraped from the public web. In this post we walk through the data pipeline that turned raw Common Crawl dumps into the multilingual **DeepSeekMath Corpus**, explain why a fastText classifier was chosen, describe the iterative domain‑harvesting loop, and show how de‑duplication and benchmark de‑contamination keep the data both clean and useful. Finally, we discuss why openly available web pages can outperform curated arXiv collections for mathematical reasoning.

---

## 1. Why Build a New Math Corpus?

Large language models learn to reason mathematically only if they see enough *high‑quality* math text during pre‑training. Existing corpora (MathPile, OpenWebMath, Proof‑Pile‑2) are either dominated by arXiv papers or limited in size. The DeepSeek team asked two simple questions:

1. **Is there untapped math content on the public web?**  
2. **Can we extract it at scale while keeping it relevant to diverse languages?**

The answer turned out to be “yes, and yes.” By engineering a focused data selection pipeline, they harvested **35 million web pages**—roughly 120 B tokens—without needing any proprietary data.

---

## 2. The FastText Classifier: A Fast, Light‑Weight Tagger

### 2.1 Choosing fastText

- **Speed & memory:** A full transformer classifier would be costly to run over billions of pages. fastText can train and score millions of documents in hours on a single GPU/CPU node.
- **Interpretability:** It uses bag‑of‑ngrams embeddings, making it easy to see which word patterns (e.g., “∫”, “proof”, “theorem”) drive the score.
- **Iterative fine‑tuning:** The model can be quickly re‑trained after adding more positive examples.

### 2.2 Training the First Model

1. **Positive set:** 500 k snippets sampled from **OpenWebMath**, a curated collection of high‑quality math web pages.  
2. **Negative set:** 500 k random pages from Common Crawl (CC).  
3. **Hyperparameters:**  
   - Vector dimension = 256  
   - Learning rate = 0.1  
   - n‑gram length = 3  
   - Minimum word count = 3  
   - Epochs = 3  

The result is a binary classifier that outputs a *math relevance score* for any HTML page.

---

## 3. Iterative Domain Harvesting – Scaling Up Precision

A single pass of the classifier would miss many math pages because the seed set is narrow. DeepSeekMath therefore runs an **iterative loop** that expands its notion of “math domain” after each round.

### Step 1 – Initial Recall
- Apply fastText to a deduplicated 40 B page slice of CC.
- Keep only the top‑scoring pages (≈40 B tokens). This forms *Iteration 1*.

### Step 2 – Domain Analysis
- Partition CC into **domains** (all URLs sharing a base domain).  
- Compute the fraction of pages already selected per domain.
- Domains with >10 % coverage are flagged as *math‐related* (e.g., `mathoverflow.net`, `aoPS.com`).

### Step 3 – Human Annotation
- For each flagged domain, annotators label sub‑paths that contain math content (e.g., `/questions/`, `/solutions/`).  
- Unselected but annotated URLs are added to the **positive seed pool**.

### Step 4 – Retrain & Rescore
- Train a new fastText model on the enlarged positive set.
- Re‑run recall on the remaining CC pages.

The loop repeats four times, each time recovering more obscure but valuable sources (e.g., university lecture notes in Chinese or Japanese). By **Iteration 4**, coverage plateaus at ~98 %, indicating diminishing returns and prompting termination.

---

## 4. De‑Duplication: Keeping Only One Copy of Each Page

Even after domain filtering, CC contains massive redundancy (mirrored slides, forum reposts). Duplicate content inflates token counts without adding new knowledge and can cause benchmark leakage.

The pipeline applies two layers:

1. **URL‐based deduplication:** Exact match on normalized URLs removes obvious copies.
2. **Near‑deduplication:** A MinHash signature is computed for each page’s text; signatures with Jaccard similarity >0.9 are collapsed.

After this step, only **35.5 M unique pages** remain—still enough for 120 B tokens but with far less noise.

---

## 5. Benchmark De‑Contamination: No Cheating!

Training on data that directly contains evaluation questions would give an illusory boost. To prevent this:

- Any *10‑gram* appearing verbatim in any benchmark (GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, AGIEval etc.) is stripped from the training corpus.
- For shorter strings (<10 grams but ≥3 grams), exact matching is also used.
- The process runs before tokenization, ensuring that even embedded problem statements are removed.

This strict filtering guarantees that DeepSeekMath’s performance truly reflects reasoning ability rather than memorization.

---

## 6. Multilingual Reach – English + Chinese + Others

Most prior math corpora are English‑centric; DeepSeekMath deliberately kept non‑English pages that passed the classifier:

- After early iterations, language detection revealed ~80 % English and ~15 % Chinese content; the rest spanned Korean, Japanese, Russian, etc.
- Including Chinese proved beneficial: models fine‑tuned on this corpus achieve state‑of‑the‑art scores on Chinese benchmarks like CMATH and Gaokao‐MathCloze—areas where English-only corpora actually hurt performance.

Thus, the corpus is not just larger; it is *more diverse*.

---

## 7. How Does This Corpus Compare to arXiv Collections?

A natural assumption is that arXiv papers—rich with formal mathematics—should be the gold standard for pre‑training math models. The DeepSeek experiments tell a different story:

| Corpus | Tokens | Source Mix | Benchmark Impact |
|--------|--------|------------|------------------|
| DeepSeekMath | 120 B | CC web + small code/arXiv mix | GSM8K ↑64→82% →88%; MATH ↑36→51% |
| MathPile (85% arXiv) | 8.9 B | Mostly arXiv | Modest gains; plateau early |
| Proof-Pile-2 (incl. arXiv) | ~28 B | Mixed but arXiv dominant | Improvements limited; sometimes harms Chinese tasks |

Key observations:

1. **Signal-to-noise ratio:** Web pages contain informal explanations, step-by-step solutions, and pedagogical commentary that resemble how LLMs generate answers (natural language + occasional LaTeX). ArXiv papers are dense formal text with fewer explanatory cues.
2. **Domain diversity:** Web sources cover K–12 textbooks, forum Q&A, lecture transcriptions—problem types present in benchmarks—while arXiv focuses on research articles.
3. **Multilingual advantage:** Public web data naturally includes non-English educational material; arXiv’s Chinese coverage is tiny.

Hence, *public web data can beat curated scientific collections for training mathematical reasoning*, at least for models in the 7–70B range.

---

## 8. Putting It All Together – From Tokens to a Model

After building the corpus:

1. **Base model initialization** – DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 (7B) was used because code pretraining already teaches symbolic manipulation.
2. **Continual pretraining** – 500 B total tokens (`56%` math corpus, `20%` code, `10%` general CC text). Learning rate peaked at $4.2\times10^{-4}$ with a batch size of 10M tokens.
3. **Instruction tuning & RL** – The high-quality math passages enable downstream chain-of-thought and program-of-thought tuning; later RL via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) lifts performance further.

The final outcome: **DeepSeekMath‑7B reaches 51.7 % on MATH without external toolkits**, matching many proprietary models and setting a new open-source record—all thanks to that massive web-derived corpus.

---

## 9. Takeaways for Researchers & Practitioners

- **FastText still shines** when you need billions of cheap binary classifications; start simple before scaling up transformers.
- **Iterative seed expansion** is crucial: use domain statistics to discover new positive examples rather than relying on one static seed set.
- **De‑duplication matters** both for token efficiency and for avoiding inadvertent benchmark leakage.
- **Don’t dismiss public web data**: with careful filtering it can exceed specialized scientific collections in both size and relevance.
- **Multilingual inclusion** pays dividends when you target diverse benchmarks; try not to filter out non-English math pages prematurely.

If you’re building your own domain-specific LLM—whether for chemistry, law, or finance—consider replicating this pipeline: start with a modest high-quality seed set, train a lightweight classifier, iterate over domains, aggressively dedupe, and always decontaminate against downstream eval sets.

---

*DeepSeekMath shows that massive reasoning ability doesn’t have to wait for trillion-token models or closed APIs; it can emerge from clever use of publicly available web data.*
