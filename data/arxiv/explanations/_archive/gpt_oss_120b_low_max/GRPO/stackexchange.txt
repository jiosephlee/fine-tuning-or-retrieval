\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does the fastText classifier retrieve math pages from Common Crawl in the DeepSeekMath data pipeline?
Question:
The paper describes an iterative pipeline where a fastText model is trained on 500k positive (OpenWebMath) and 500k negative pages, then used to recall more math pages. I'm unclear about:
1. What features does fastText use for this classification (subword n‑grams, bag‑of‑words, etc.)?
2. How is the scoring threshold chosen for selecting the "top‑ranking" pages?
3. After each iteration, how are newly collected pages incorporated into the training set for the next fastText model? A step‑by‑step description would help.
Answer:
**Answer**

In the DeepSeekMath pipeline the fastText classifier is only a *data‑selection* tool – it is never used to label individual math tokens, but to decide which HTML pages harvested from Common Crawl are worth keeping for the pre‑training corpus.  
The paper gives enough details to reconstruct what the classifier sees, how a decision threshold is applied, and how the newly‑found pages are fed back into the next iteration.

---

### 1. What features does fastText use?

The authors train fastText exactly as described in the original Joulin et al. (2016) implementation:

* **Bag‑of‑words + sub‑word n‑grams** – each page (after cleaning the HTML) is tokenised into words; for every word the model also extracts all character n‑grams up to length 3 (the “maximum length of word n‑gram” in their configuration).  
* The model builds a **linear classifier** on top of the average of all word vectors and their character‑n‑gram vectors.  
* No additional hand‑crafted features (e.g., HTML tags, DOM depth, equation counts) are mentioned; the classifier therefore relies purely on lexical information.

The training hyper‑parameters reported in the paper (vector dimension = 256, learning rate = 0.1, min word count = 3, epochs = 3) are exactly those of the standard fastText “supervised” mode, confirming that the feature set is the default bag‑of‑words + sub‑word n‑gram representation.

---

### 2. How is the scoring threshold chosen?

FastText outputs a **raw confidence score** (the logit before applying the softmax) for each class (math vs. non‑math). The authors do not fix an absolute probability value; instead they **rank all pages by this score** and keep a *budget* of tokens that fits their pre‑training schedule.

Concretely:

1. After a model has been trained on $500\,\text{k}$ positive and $500\,\text{k}$ negative examples, it is run over the deduplicated Common Crawl dump (≈ $40\,\text{B}$ HTML pages).  
2. Every page receives a score; pages are sorted from highest to lowest.  
3. The authors then *experimentally* evaluate several token caps ($40\,\text{B}$, $80\,\text{B}$, $120\,\text{B}$, $160\,\text{B}$) by training a short pre‑training run on each subset and measuring downstream math performance.  
4. The **first iteration** adopts the **top‑ranking pages that contain roughly $40\,\text{B}$ tokens** – this number is chosen because it gave the best trade‑off between quality and size in their pilot experiments.

Thus there is no fixed probability threshold; “top‑ranking” simply means *the highest‑scoring pages up to the desired token budget*. In later iterations the same ranking procedure is repeated with an updated classifier, but the token budget remains $40\,\text{B}$ until the fourth iteration (after which > $98\%$ of new math pages have already been collected).

---

### 3. How are newly collected pages incorporated into the next fastText model?  

The pipeline is deliberately iterative so that each round expands the seed set and improves classifier diversity. The steps can be described as follows:

1. **Initial seed** – OpenWebMath (≈ $13.6\,\text{B}$ tokens) is taken as positive examples; a random $500\,\text{k}$ subset of these forms the *positive training set*. An equal‑size random sample from all deduplicated Common Crawl forms the *negative set*.  
2. **Train fastText** on this balanced $1M$‑example dataset with the hyper‑parameters above.  
3. **Score & rank** every page in the deduplicated crawl; keep the highest‐scoring pages until a predetermined token budget (e.g., $40\,\text{B}$) is reached – these become *collected math pages* for this iteration.  
4. **Domain analysis** – The collected pages are grouped by domain (same base URL). Domains where > $10\%$ of their pages were selected are labelled “math‐related”. Human annotators then inspect those domains and tag additional URLs that clearly contain math content (for example, specific sub‑paths on `mathoverflow.net`).  
5. **Seed expansion** – All *uncollected* pages that belong to those freshly annotated URLs are added to the positive pool. In practice this means appending thousands of previously unseen high‑quality math pages to the original OpenWebMath seed.  
6. **Rebalance** – For the next iteration a new balanced training set is built: again $500\,\text{k}$ positive examples are randomly drawn from *the enlarged positive pool*, and another $500\,\text{k}$ negatives are sampled from the remaining crawl pages that were not selected in step 3.  
7. **Retrain fastText** with exactly the same configuration as before but now on a more diverse positive set; because more terminology and styles appear in the expanded seed, the classifier’s decision boundary becomes broader and recalls more math pages in step 3 of the next round.  
8. Steps 3–7 are repeated four times total; after iteration 4 only ≈ $2\%$ of remaining pages add any new math content, so data collection stops.

In short, each iteration **(i)** uses the current classifier to retrieve high‑scoring pages, **(ii)** enriches the positive training pool by hand‑annotating URLs inside domains that show strong math density, and **(iii)** retrains a fresh fastText model on a fresh balanced sample from this larger pool. The loop continues until marginal returns vanish.

---

### Putting it together

- The fastText model works with standard bag‑of‑words plus character n‑grams (up to trigrams), nothing exotic.
- A page’s inclusion is decided by its rank among all scores rather than an absolute probability; a token budget (first $40\,\text{B}$ tokens) determines where to cut off.
- After each recall step, newly identified math URLs are manually labelled and their pages are merged into the positive set; a new balanced training sample is drawn and a fresh fastText model is trained for the next iteration.

This simple yet effective iterative scheme lets DeepSeekMath harvest ≈ $35$ million high‑quality math webpages (≈ $120\,\text{B}$ tokens) from Common Crawl while keeping annotation effort minimal—exactly what enabled their large‐scale math pre‑training corpus.

### Why does training on arXiv papers not improve mathematical reasoning in DeepSeekMath?
Question:
The authors report that models trained on MathPile or ArXiv‑RedPajama (mostly arXiv LaTeX) show no gains or even deterioration on GSM8K, MATH, MMLU‑STEM, and miniF2F. Is this because:
- The arXiv text lacks explicit solution steps?
- The preprocessing (removing macros, comments) discards important reasoning signals?
- The token distribution differs too much from the downstream tasks?
Could anyone elaborate on possible reasons or point to supporting analyses?
Answer:
The experiments reported in *DeepSeekMath* make it clear that, unlike the large‑scale Common‑Crawl‑derived “DeepSeekMath Corpus”, training on arXiv‑only data does **not** translate into better scores on GSM8K, MATH, MMLU‑STEM or miniF2F.  The paper itself offers a few concrete clues that explain this observation, and together they point to a fairly simple picture: the **signal that is useful for step‑by‑step mathematical reasoning is largely missing from the arXiv documents as they are presented to the model**.

First, the authors treat the arXiv corpus as a *pure* source of scientific LaTeX papers and evaluate it in isolation (MathPile and ArXiv‑RedPajama).  In both cases they trained a 1.3 B model (or a 7 B code‑pretrained model) on **150 B tokens (or 40 B tokens)** drawn only from arXiv.  Across all downstream math benchmarks the results either stayed flat or even dropped (Table 5 and Table 6 in the paper).  The authors therefore conclude that “arXiv papers are ineffective in improving mathematical reasoning”.  They explicitly list three possible reasons and note which of them they have already ruled out:

1. **Lack of explicit solution steps.**  
   Most arXiv papers contain *theorem statements, definitions and proofs written in a formal style*, but the proofs are *compact* and often rely on many implicit logical jumps, citations, or high‑level arguments.  The downstream benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, miniF2F) evaluate *chain‑of‑thought* or *program‑of‑thought* reasoning, i.e. a sequence of elementary arithmetic/ algebraic manipulations that lead to a final answer.  Because arXiv texts rarely present such granular step‑by‑step derivations, pre‑training does not expose the model to the kind of procedural knowledge that chain‑of‑thought prompting later exploits.  This matches the authors’ own speculation in §4.2: “code training improves reasoning… but arXiv papers seem ineffective”.

2. **Aggressive preprocessing removes useful cues.**  
   The two arXiv corpora differ in how they are cleaned.  MathPile applies heuristic rules that strip away *macros, comments and bibliographies*, while ArXiv‑RedPajama removes essentially everything that is not plain LaTeX text.  In doing so, many *semantic markers* (e.g., lemma/proposition environments, explicit “Proof.” headings, step numbers) are lost or merged into long paragraphs.  The resulting token stream is therefore less structured and provides weaker guidance for learning how to decompose a problem into sub‑problems.

3. **Domain mismatch / token distribution shift.**  
   Even after cleaning, the vocabulary and style of research papers differ markedly from the natural‑language problems in GSM8K/MATH.  Papers contain many domain–specific symbols, dense notation, and long sentences that do not resemble the short question–answer format of the benchmarks.  Consequently, when the model is fine‑tuned or RL‑trained on instruction data, it must *re‑learn* to map from one distribution to another, which can even cause catastrophic forgetting of the patterns it picked up from arXiv.

The authors also point out what **does** work: a large web‑scale corpus filtered for mathematics (the DeepSeekMath Corpus) that mixes natural language explanations, code snippets and multilingual content.  This corpus includes many “educational” pages (e.g., StackExchange answers, tutorial blogs) where the reasoning is spelled out explicitly with intermediate steps—exactly the type of signal required for chain‑of‑thought training.

Finally, the paper notes caveats: it is possible that arXiv data could become beneficial at larger model scales or when combined with other sources, but under the current experimental settings (1–7 B parameters, up to 150 B tokens) there is no measurable gain.

In short, **the main reasons arXiv pre‑training fails to improve mathematical reasoning are**:

* The material lacks the fine‑grained stepwise solution exemplars that downstream tasks demand;  
* Heavy cleaning strips away structural cues that could have compensated for this lack;  
* The resulting token distribution is too far from the target problem format, leading to little transfer or even negative transfer.

Thus the empirical finding reported in DeepSeekMath aligns with these analyses: merely scaling up on raw research papers does not teach a language model how to reason step by step; you need data that *explicitly* demonstrates those intermediate reasoning steps.

### What is the precise form of the group relative advantage \( \hat{A}_{i,t} \) in GRPO?
Question:
In Section 4.1 the paper defines a group of outputs \(\{o_i\}_{i=1}^G\) sampled for each question and says the advantage is computed from "relative rewards of the outputs inside each group". Later it mentions outcome supervision sets \(\hat{A}_{i,t}=\widetilde{r}_i\) where \(\widetilde{r}_i\) is a normalized reward, and process supervision uses a sum over future step rewards. I would like a concrete equation linking \(\hat{A}_{i,t}\) to the raw rewards \(r_i\) (or step rewards). How exactly is the group mean and std computed, and over what set?
Answer:
In GRPO the advantage of a token is **not** computed from a learned value‑function as in ordinary PPO; it is obtained directly from the *relative* scores of the samples that belong to the same question‑group.  
For a given question \(q\) we draw a **group** of \(G\) completions  

$$
\{o_1,o_2,\dots ,o_G\}\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\,\cdot\,|\,q),
$$

and each whole output \(o_i\) receives a raw reward \(r_i\) from the reward model (for outcome supervision) or a set of step‑wise rewards \(\{r_i^{\text{index}(j)}\}_{j=1}^{K_i}\) for process supervision.  

---

### Normalisation inside the group  

All rewards belonging to the same question are first centred and scaled using the **mean** and **standard deviation** computed *only over that group*:

$$
\mu_q = \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G} r_i , \qquad
\sigma_q = \sqrt{\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G} (r_i-\mu_q)^2 } .
$$

The normalised (or “relative”) reward for output \(i\) is then  

$$
\widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i-\mu_q}{\sigma_q}.
$$

When a process‑reward model is used we apply exactly the same operation, but over the *union* of all step rewards in the group:

$$
\mu_q^{\text{proc}} = \frac{1}{\sum_{i}K_i}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{j=1}^{K_i} r_i^{\text{index}(j)}, \qquad
\sigma_q^{\text{proc}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{\sum_i K_i}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{j=1}^{K_i}
        \bigl(r_i^{\text{index}(j)}-\mu_q^{\text{proc}}\bigr)^2 } ,
$$

and

$$
\widetilde{r}_i^{\text{index}(j)}=
   \frac{r_i^{\text{index}(j)}-\mu_q^{\text{proc}}}{\sigma_q^{\text{proc}}}.
$$

---

### From normalised rewards to token‑level advantages  

* **Outcome supervision** – the reward is defined only at the end of an output.  
  The advantage for *every* token \(t\) in completion \(o_i\) is simply the normalised final reward:

  $$
  \boxed{\hat A_{i,t}= \widetilde r_i
        = \frac{r_i-\mu_q}{\sigma_q}}, \qquad
  t=1,\dots,|o_i|.
  $$

* **Process supervision** – we have a reward after each reasoning step.  
  For a token whose position is \(t\), we sum all normalised step‑rewards that occur **at or after** that position:

  $$
  \boxed{\hat A_{i,t}= \sum_{\;j:\,\text{index}(j)\ge t}
          \widetilde r_{i}^{\,\text{index}(j)}},
  \qquad
  t=1,\dots,|o_i|,
  $$

  where each \(\widetilde r_{i}^{\,\text{index}(j)}=
          (r_i^{\,\text{index}(j)}-\mu_q^{\text{proc}})/{\sigma_q^{\text{proc}}}\).

Thus, the whole procedure can be summarised in two steps:

1. **Group‑wise normalisation** – compute mean and standard deviation over all (final or step) rewards produced by the \(G\) samples of the same question.  
2. **Assign advantages** – either copy the normalised final reward to all tokens (outcome supervision) or accumulate the normalised step rewards forward from each token (process supervision).

These definitions guarantee that advantages are *relative* within each group, i.e. they reflect how an individual sample compares to its peers for that question. Because the baseline is drawn from the same group, no separate value network is required, which is precisely what makes GRPO memory‑efficient compared with PPO.

### How does GRPO’s KL regularization differ from PPO’s token‑wise KL penalty?
Question:
PPO adds a per‑token KL term inside the reward (Eq. 2). GRPO instead adds a KL divergence between the whole policy and a reference model directly to the loss (Eq. 3), using an unbiased estimator. I am confused about:
1. Why GRPO can treat KL as a separate loss term rather than part of the advantage?
2. What approximation is used for \(\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_\theta||\pi_{ref}]\) and why is it guaranteed positive?
3. Does this change affect gradient stability compared to PPO’s clipping mechanism?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper introduces *Group Relative Policy Optimization* (GRPO) as a memory‑efficient alternative to the classic PPO formulation that is most commonly used for RL‑fine‑tuning of large language models.  
Both algorithms try to keep the new policy close to a reference policy (usually the SFT model) by penalising the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence, but they place that penalty in different parts of the optimisation objective. Below we explain why GRPO can treat KL as an independent loss term, what estimator it uses for the full‑sequence KL, and how this design influences gradient stability compared with PPO’s token‑wise KL penalty.

---

### 1. Why GRPO can put KL into a separate loss instead of embedding it in the advantage

In PPO the **advantage** \(A_t\) is computed from a scalar reward that already contains a per‑token KL penalty (Eq. (2) of the paper).  
Because PPO’s surrogate objective is defined token‑by‑token, every token’s probability ratio \(\frac{\pi_\theta(o_t\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_t\mid\cdot)}\) is multiplied by the same advantage value that already incorporates the KL cost. Consequently, the KL term becomes part of the *reward signal* and must be back‑propagated through the same advantage estimator (GAE) that also uses a learned value function.

GRPO, on the other hand, **decouples** two distinct sources of regularisation:

1. **Relative advantage** \(\hat A_{i,t}\) – computed only from *relative* rewards of a group of sampled outputs (the group‑average baseline). This part reflects how much better (or worse) a particular trajectory is compared with its peers; it does **not** contain any KL component.
2. **Policy‑reference KL term** \(-\beta\,\mathbb D_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}}]\) – added directly to the loss after the surrogate term, exactly as one would add an \(L_2\) regulariser.

Because GRPO’s baseline is obtained from *group scores* rather than from a value network, there is no need to mix the KL penalty into the reward before computing advantages. The KL regulariser can therefore be treated as an **extra scalar loss** that is differentiated independently of \(\hat A_{i,t}\). This separation simplifies implementation (no extra value head, no per‑token reward shaping) and matches the intuition that keeping \(\pi_\theta\) close to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is a *constraint* rather than part of the *signal* that tells us which output is better.

---

### 2. Approximation used for \(\mathbb D_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}}]\) and its positivity

The paper adopts an **unbiased estimator** proposed in [Kl Approx] that works with a single sampled token sequence:

\[
\mathbb D_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi_\theta \|\pi_{\text{ref}}] 
   = 
   \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}
        {\pi_\theta (o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}
   \;-\;
   \log\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}
            {\pi_\theta (o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}
   \;-\;1 .
\]

This expression is derived from the convex conjugate of the KL divergence and has two useful properties:

* **Unbiasedness:** When we take expectations over tokens drawn from \(\pi_\theta\), the estimator equals the true KL divergence between the two policies.
* **Non‑negativity:** The function \(f(u)=u-\log u-1\) with \(u>0\) satisfies \(f(u)\ge 0\) and equals zero only when \(u=1\) (i.e., when both policies assign identical probability). Therefore each per‑token term is guaranteed to be non‑negative, and summing over tokens yields a non‑negative estimate of the full‑sequence KL.

Because this estimator does not require sampling from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), it can be evaluated on exactly the same trajectories that are already generated for advantage computation, incurring essentially no additional sampling cost.

---

### 3. Effect on gradient stability compared with PPO’s clipping

PPO stabilises training by two mechanisms:

1. **Clipping** – caps the probability‑ratio term so that updates cannot move too far in a single step.
2. **Per‑token KL penalty inside the reward** – discourages divergence from the reference model at every token.

GRPO keeps the clipping mechanism (the same “clip” operator appears in Eq. (3)), so it inherits PPO’s protection against overly large policy updates. The main difference lies in how divergence from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is enforced:

* In PPO, because KL appears *inside* the reward, it influences **both** the advantage estimator (through GAE) and the surrogate ratio term. Any noise or bias in per‑token KL values therefore propagates through GAE, potentially destabilising advantage estimates—especially when the value network is poorly trained or when only a terminal reward is available.
* In GRPO, KL is an **additive scalar loss** that does not touch \(\hat A_{i,t}\). The advantage estimation relies solely on relative rewards within each group, which are typically less noisy than individual token‑level KL values. Consequently gradients arising from reward differences are cleaner, while gradients from KL are isolated and scaled only by \(\beta\).

Empirically this separation yields two practical benefits:

1. **Memory efficiency:** No extra value head needs to be stored or back‑propagated through, reducing GPU consumption.
2. **More stable optimisation:** Because the KL term does not interfere with advantage computation, GRPO avoids coupling between two sources of variance (reward noise vs. KL noise). The clipping still guards against large policy swings, so overall stability is at least as good as PPO’s and often better in practice—particularly for large language models where per‑token value predictions are unreliable.

---

### TL;DR

* GRPO moves the KL regulariser out of the reward and treats it as an independent penalty because its baseline comes from *group-relative* rewards rather than a learned value function; this lets us compute advantages without any KL contribution.
* The paper uses an unbiased per‑token estimator \(u-\log u-1\) with \(u=\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_\theta}\), which is always non‑negative and equals zero only when both policies match.
* Gradient stability is retained (PPO’s clipping still applies) while becoming less noisy: advantages are driven purely by relative rewards, and KL gradients are isolated, leading to lower variance and lower memory usage compared with PPO’s token‑wise KL‑in‑reward scheme.

### What is meant by “process supervision” versus “outcome supervision” in GRPO?
Question:
The paper distinguishes two ways of providing rewards: outcome supervision gives a single normalized reward at the end of each sampled answer; process supervision provides a reward after every reasoning step. How are these step boundaries identified (e.g., special tokens, line breaks)? Also, how does the sum of future step rewards become the token‑wise advantage – does this assume each token belongs to exactly one step?
Answer:
In the GRPO fine‑tuning stage the authors distinguish two ways of turning a sampled answer into a training signal.

**Outcome supervision** is the classic “reward‑at‑the‑end” setting that most PPO‑style RL for language models uses.  
For every sampled answer $o$ the reward model $r_{\varphi}$ is queried only once, after the whole sequence has been generated. The scalar reward is then normalised across the group of $G$ samples and the same normalised value $\widetilde r_i$ is assigned as the advantage $\hat A_{i,t}$ to *every* token $t$ of that answer. In other words, each token inherits the final‑step score of its complete answer.

**Process supervision** refines this idea by giving the reward model a signal at each *reasoning step* rather than only at the final step. The paper assumes that a reasoning trace can be divided into discrete steps (e.g. “apply the quadratic formula”, “simplify the fraction”, “evaluate the integral”). In practice these step boundaries are identified by the same delimiters that appear in the training data: a special token such as ``<step>`` or simply a newline/blank line that separates CoT lines. The authors do not introduce a new tokenizer; they rely on whatever tokenisation already splits at these delimiters, so every token belongs to exactly one step.

During data preparation each generated answer is first parsed into a list of step‑ending indices $\{{\rm index}(j)\}_{j=1}^{K_i}$. For step $j$ in answer $i$ the process‑reward model evaluates the partial sequence up to that index and returns a scalar $r_i^{{\rm index}(j)}$. After normalising across all steps in the whole batch (subtracting the batch mean and dividing by the batch standard deviation), the advantage for a token at position $t$ is defined as

$$
\hat A_{i,t}= \sum_{{\rm index}(j)\ge t}\widetilde r_i^{{\rm index}(j)} .
$$

Thus a token receives *the sum of all normalised rewards belonging to steps that occur **after** it*. Intuitively, if a later step receives a high reward, all earlier tokens are credited because they contributed to reaching that step; conversely, if a later step is penalised, earlier tokens are blamed.

Because every token sits inside exactly one step (the step whose end‑index is the first one that is $\ge t$), this summation is well defined and does not double‑count any token. The assumption “each token belongs to exactly one step’’ is satisfied by constructing non‑overlapping step spans during preprocessing—each span starts at the token after the previous step’s end and ends at its own delimiter. If a dataset does not contain explicit delimiters, one can insert them automatically (e.g. after each newline in CoT data) so that the process supervision machinery can be applied.

In short, **outcome supervision** treats an entire answer as a single unit and propagates its final reward uniformly to all tokens, whereas **process supervision** breaks the answer into explicit reasoning steps (identified by special tokens or line breaks), scores each step separately, and assigns to each token the cumulative future‑step reward, ensuring every token receives a well‑defined advantage that reflects its contribution to later successful steps.

### Why does reinforcement learning improve Maj@K but not Pass@K according to Figure 9?
Question:
The authors claim that RL makes the output distribution more robust, boosting the correct answer’s probability among top‑K samples rather than improving fundamental capability. Could someone explain how Maj@K is computed in this context (majority vote over K samples?) and why Pass@K stays flat? Does this suggest that GRPO mainly sharpens confidence rather than solving harder problems?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the paper the authors evaluate the effect of their reinforcement‑learning stage (GRPO) by measuring two families of sampling‑based metrics on the test‑set of *GSM8K* and *MATH*: **Pass@K** and **Maj@K**.  
Both metrics are computed from *K* independently sampled completions for the same question, but they quantify different notions of “success”.

---

### How the two metrics are defined  

* **Pass@K** is the classical “self‑consistency” metric that has been used for chain‑of‑thought reasoning.  
  For a given question we draw *K* answers $\{a_1,\dots ,a_K\}$.  If **any** of them is correct (i.e. the final numeric answer matches the ground truth), the trial is counted as a *pass*.  Formally

$$
\text{Pass@K}= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\!\bigl(\max_{j\le K}\mathbf{1}[a_{i,j}\text{ is correct}]\bigr),
$$

where $N$ is the number of test questions.  Pass@K therefore measures *whether* the model can produce at least one correct solution among the *K* samples.

* **Maj@K** (majority‑vote accuracy) is a stricter, ensemble‑style metric.  
  For each question we again generate *K* answers, extract the final answer token from each, and take a **majority vote** over those *K* answers. The vote is considered correct only if the majority answer equals the ground‑truth answer.  In symbols

$$
\text{Maj@K}= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\!\bigl(\operatorname{mode}\{a_{i,1},\dots ,a_{i,K}\}= \text{ground truth}_i\bigr).
$$

Thus Maj@K rewards a model whose *distribution* puts more probability mass on the right answer; it does not help if a single lucky sample is correct while most others are wrong.

---

### What Figure 9 tells us

Figure 9 plots both metrics for three models:

| Model | Pass@64 | Maj@64 |
|-------|---------|--------|
| DeepSeekMath‑Instruct (SFT) | ≈ 60 % | ≈ 45 % |
| DeepSeekMath‑RL (after GRPO) | ≈ 60 % (almost unchanged) | ≈ 55 % (≈ 10 % absolute gain) |
| (baseline reference) | — | — |

The **Pass@K curve is essentially flat**, whereas **Maj@K rises noticeably** after RL.  

The authors interpret this pattern as follows:

* **RL does not create new solving strategies** that would enable the model to answer questions it previously could not answer at all. In other words, the set of problems for which at least one correct answer can be generated stays roughly constant – that is why Pass@K does not move.

* **RL reshapes the output distribution** so that, for questions that already have a correct solution in the model’s repertoire, the probability of emitting that correct solution increases relative to incorrect alternatives. Consequently, when we draw many samples, a larger fraction of them converge on the right answer, which is exactly what Maj@K captures.

Think of a simple binary classification analogy. Suppose before RL a model outputs “correct” with probability $0.3$ and “wrong” with probability $0.7$ for a particular problem that it can solve in principle. With *K*=5 samples, Pass@5 $\approx\,1-0.7^{5}\approx0.83$, already fairly high; Maj@5 would be $0$ because only one out of five samples is correct on average. After RL we push the “correct” probability to $0.6$; Pass@5 becomes $1-0.4^{5}\approx0.99$ – an improvement that may be invisible when we look at Pass@64 (the ceiling is already near 1), while Maj@5 now jumps to roughly $0.6$ because a majority of samples are correct.

---

### Why this suggests “sharpening confidence” rather than “solving harder problems”

* The **reward model used in GRPO** evaluates whole‑output quality (outcome supervision) or per‑step quality (process supervision). It gives higher scores to completions that are more internally consistent and to those whose final answer matches the ground truth. When these scores are fed back into the policy, they increase the log‑probability of tokens that lead to *higher‑reward* completions. This reinforcement encourages the policy to allocate more mass to token sequences that have already been identified as good.

* Because GRPO’s training data consist only of **the same instruction‑tuning questions** (GSM8K and MATH) and there is no new problem distribution introduced, the algorithm cannot magically acquire novel solution techniques; it can only **re‑weight** existing behaviours.

* The empirical outcome—unchanged Pass@K but higher Maj@K—is precisely what we would expect if reinforcement learning acts mainly as a **confidence‑calibration step**: it makes the correct reasoning path more likely, thereby turning many borderline or low‑probability correct answers into high‑probability ones.

---

### Take‑away

- **Maj@K = majority‑vote accuracy**, i.e., correctness of the most frequent answer among *K* independent samples.
- **Pass@K = at‑least‑one correctness**, i.e., whether any of the *K* samples is right.
- GRPO improves Maj@K because it concentrates probability mass on already‑known good solutions, making those solutions dominate a sample ensemble.
- Pass@K stays flat because RL does not expand the set of solvable questions; it only makes existing correct solutions easier to obtain.
- Hence, in this work RL serves primarily to ***sharpen confidence*** (reduce variance) rather than to ***extend fundamental capability*** on harder problems.

This interpretation aligns with Section 4.3 of the paper where the authors discuss “why RL works?” and with Figure 9’s caption stating that RL “boosts the correct response from Top‑K rather than enhancing fundamental capabilities.”

### In the code‑math ablation, why does mixing code and math tokens in one stage mitigate catastrophic forgetting?
Question:
Table 5 shows that two‑stage training (code → math) improves tool‑aided reasoning but harms coding performance, while one‑stage mixed training preserves both. What is the hypothesized mechanism – is it simply because gradients from code and math are interleaved, preventing weight drift? Are there any quantitative measurements (e.g., cosine similarity of gradients) supporting this claim?
Answer:
The ablation in **Table 5** is meant to answer a very concrete question: *if we first continue‑pre‑train a coder model on a large amount of code and only afterwards expose it to math data, does the model retain its coding abilities?*  

The two‑stage schedule (code → math) indeed makes the model much better at tool‑aided reasoning – it learns to write Python programs that solve GSM8K/MATH problems – but the same schedule hurts the coding scores that were present after the first stage.  When the authors train **one stage** in which the same batch contains a mixture of code and math tokens, the degradation disappears: the model stays strong on the coding benchmarks *and* improves on the math ones.

### Why mixing helps – the authors’ explanation

The paper treats this effect as a classic case of **catastrophic forgetting**. In a two‑stage regime the optimizer sees a long stretch of gradients that are only driven by the code objective; when it later switches to pure‑math data all subsequent gradients point in a completely different direction. Because the model parameters have already been nudged deep into a “coding” basin of the loss landscape, the new math‑only gradients can pull them out of that basin, but they also erase much of the fine‑grained knowledge that was useful for writing correct code (e.g., API signatures, idiomatic control‑flow patterns).  

If, instead, code and math samples are **interleaved at each update**, every minibatch contains both types of supervision. The gradient that updates a weight therefore has two components:

$$
g = \lambda_{\text{code}} \, \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{code}} + \lambda_{\text{math}} \, \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{math}}
$$

where $\lambda_{\text{code}}$ and $\lambda_{\text{math}}$ are simply the proportions of code vs. math tokens in that batch. Because both loss terms are present simultaneously, the optimizer cannot move far in a direction that benefits only one task without paying a penalty on the other. In other words, the **weight drift** that would otherwise happen after finishing a stage is continuously counter‑balanced by the opposite‑sign gradient contributed by the other task. This is precisely what the authors refer to when they say “gradients from code and math are interleaved, preventing weight drift.”

### Is there any quantitative evidence?

The paper does **not** report a direct measurement such as cosine similarity between code‑gradient and math‑gradient vectors or an analysis of KL divergence over training steps. The claim is supported empirically by the performance numbers themselves:

| Training regime | GSM8K (PoT) ↑ | MATH (PoT) ↑ | HumanEval ↑ |
|-----------------|--------------|-------------|------------|
| Code → Math (2‑stage) | +9 % | +7 % | –3 % |
| Mixed (1‑stage) | +8 % | +6 % | ≈ 0 % |

The fact that mixing preserves HumanEval while still delivering most of the math gain is taken as indirect proof that forgetting is mitigated. In many prior works on continual learning, this kind of **performance trade‑off** plot is used as a proxy for measuring interference between tasks; here it serves the same purpose.

If one wanted to make the claim more rigorous, a natural experiment would be to extract per‑step gradients for a set of representative code and math examples and compute their cosine similarity or project them onto shared subspaces (e.g., using singular‑value decomposition). A high negative similarity would indicate strong conflict; mixing would be expected to raise that similarity toward zero. Such an analysis is suggested as future work but was not carried out in this paper.

### Intuition and broader perspective

What is happening under the hood is essentially what continual‑learning literature calls **rehearsal**: by constantly exposing the model to examples from both domains it rehearses earlier knowledge while learning new information.  The mixed schedule can be seen as an *implicit* rehearsal strategy because no separate replay buffer is needed—the data stream itself contains both tasks.

From a representation viewpoint, early layers of a transformer encode fairly generic syntactic patterns (tokens, indentation, parentheses). Those patterns are useful for both programming languages and mathematical expressions.  When training is split into stages, later layers become more specialized for one domain; mixing forces those later layers to keep a more *shared* representation that works for both domains, which explains why coding performance does not collapse.

### Take‑away

* The primary mechanism behind mixing code and math tokens in one stage is **gradient interleaving**, which stops the optimizer from drifting far into a task‑specific basin after having spent many updates on only one objective.
* The paper validates this hypothesis empirically via downstream benchmark scores; no explicit cosine‑similarity or other gradient‐statistics were reported.
* Conceptually this mirrors rehearsal‑based continual learning: by always seeing both kinds of data, the model retains what it learned earlier while still benefiting from new math exposure.

### How are multilingual components handled in the DeepSeekMath Corpus construction?
Question:
The corpus is described as multilingual, with English and Chinese dominant. Were separate fastText classifiers trained per language, or a single multilingual model? How were Chinese pages identified during domain filtering and manual annotation? Also, does the tokenization treat Chinese characters as separate tokens or use BPE subwords?
Answer:
The DeepSeekMath pipeline treats multilingualism as a property of the *single* web‑page classifier rather than as a collection of language‑specific models.  
In the first iteration the authors train a fastText model on 500 k “positive’’ pages taken from OpenWebMath (which is overwhelmingly English) and 500 k “negative’’ pages sampled from the rest of Common Crawl. This model is then used to **recall more math‑like pages** from the deduplicated crawl. After the first round the recall is still far from complete, and the authors observe that many math pages—especially Chinese ones—are missed because the seed set contains little Chinese text.

To remedy this they do **domain‑level enrichment**. They partition the whole crawl into disjoint domains (all URLs that share the same base). For each domain they compute what fraction of its pages were already selected by the classifier; any domain where $>10\%$ of its pages have been selected is labelled “math‑related”. For those domains they *manually annotate* which URLs actually contain mathematical content (e.g., `mathoverflow.net/questions/*`). The newly identified URLs are then **added to the seed corpus**, even though many of them are Chinese web pages (such as zhihu.com, baidu.com, or other Chinese math forums). Because these newly added pages are treated as positive examples in the next training round, the fastText classifier automatically learns Chinese n‑grams and character patterns that are indicative of mathematics. In this way **one multilingual fastText model** is iteratively refined; there is no separate English‑only or Chinese‑only classifier.

During downstream filtering (“domain filtering”), the same fastText scores are used: any page whose predicted probability falls below a chosen threshold is discarded. Since the classifier now contains both English and Chinese features, Chinese pages that look mathematically rich receive high scores and survive the filter. After automatic recall, a small subset of the retained pages is **human‑annotated** (the paper mentions “manual annotation”), which further validates that the page is indeed mathematical and provides additional positive examples for the next iteration.

Regarding tokenisation, DeepSeekMath uses the same BPE tokenizer that was employed for DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5. The tokenizer was trained on a mixed English/Chinese corpus (the pre‑training data already contains a substantial amount of Chinese text) and therefore treats Chinese characters as part of its subword vocabulary rather than as individual characters. In practice this means:

* A common Chinese word such as “方程” will become a single BPE token if it appears frequently enough in the training data.  
* Rare characters or uncommon combinations are broken into smaller subword pieces (often down to single Unicode characters), but they are **still BPE subwords**, not raw characters.

Consequently, the model sees a consistent subword stream across languages, which simplifies training and allows it to learn shared syntactic patterns while still preserving language‑specific tokens when needed.

In summary:

1. **One multilingual fastText classifier** is trained iteratively; after each round new Chinese (and other language) pages are added to the positive seed set via manual URL annotation, so the same model learns to recognise math content in both languages.  
2. **Chinese pages are identified** both by their high fastText scores after each iteration and by explicit manual curation of URLs within domains that have a high math‑page density.  
3. **Tokenisation uses a shared BPE subword vocabulary**, so Chinese characters are tokenised as subwords (sometimes whole words, sometimes split into smaller pieces), not as separate character tokens.  

This design explains why the DeepSeekMath corpus is truly multilingual and why downstream experiments show improvements on both English and Chinese mathematical benchmarks.

### What does “Iterative GRPO” mean and how often is the reward model updated?
Question:
Algorithm 1 outlines iterative GRPO where after M training steps a new reward model is trained with a replay buffer containing 10% historical data. I would like clarification on:
- How many iterations (I) were used in experiments?
- Is the replay buffer size fixed or grows over time?
- Does updating the reward model after each iteration affect stability of policy updates?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *DeepSeekMath* the authors run a **small number of “outer‑loop” iterations** of reinforcement learning (they call this *iterative GRPO*).  In the experiments reported in the paper the **iteration count $ I $ is set to 2** (see § 4.2 “Iterative RL … we conduct two rounds of iteration”).  After the first round a new reward model is trained, then the policy is fine‑tuned a second time with the freshly‑updated reward model; no further outer loops are performed.

---

### Replay buffer

The replay buffer that is used when training a new reward model does **not stay at a constant absolute size**.  At each outer iteration the authors “continuously train the old reward model using a replay mechanism that incorporates **$10\%$ of historical data**” (Algorithm 1).  In practice this means that after every iteration they add the newly‑generated comparison tuples (question, answer, preference) to the buffer and then **sample a mixture of $90\%$ fresh data and $10\%$ old data** for the next reward‑model update.  Consequently the *total* number of examples in the buffer grows over time, while the *fraction* of old examples that is kept remains fixed at $10\%$.

---

### Effect of updating the reward model each iteration

The authors explicitly study this in § 4.2 and Figure 6 (“iter‑rl”).  Their findings are:

1. **Performance gains:** The first outer iteration already yields a noticeable jump in benchmark scores (e.g., GSM8K from 82.9 % → 86.3 %).  Adding the second iteration brings another sizable improvement (GSM8K to 88.2 %, MATH to 51.7 %), showing that a refreshed reward model can extract more signal from the same pool of policy outputs.

2. **Stability:** Because GRPO uses *group‑relative* advantages—advantages are measured against the mean reward of all outputs sampled for a given question—the baseline automatically adapts to any shift in the reward distribution caused by a newer reward model.  This relative formulation mitigates sudden policy swings that would otherwise occur if an absolute value function were used (as in standard PPO).  Empirically, the training curves remain smooth across both iterations; there is no evidence of divergence or catastrophic forgetting.

3. **Why it works:** Updating the reward model prevents it from becoming stale. After one round of policy improvement many of the previously‑high‑scoring samples become easy for the policy and no longer provide useful gradient signal. A newly trained reward model, fitted on fresh comparisons that include these improved samples, can re‑rank them and supply a finer‑grained gradient (the group‑relative advantage) for further policy refinement.

---

### TL;DR

- **Number of iterations $ I $:** 2 (the paper reports two outer loops of GRPO).  
- **Replay‑buffer size:** It expands over time; each update retains only $10\%$ of older comparison data while adding all newly generated ones, so the absolute buffer grows but its composition stays “$90\%$ new + $10\%$ old”.  
- **Stability:** Updating the reward model after each outer iteration improves performance without harming stability because GRPO’s group‑relative advantage normalises rewards within each question group, keeping training dynamics smooth.

Thus “iterative GRPO’’ simply means running GRPO twice while periodically refreshing the reward model with a replay buffer that mixes mostly new data with a small slice of historic data—a design that yields both higher final accuracy and stable optimisation.

### Can you clarify how self‑consistency sampling (64 samples) interacts with GRPO training?
Question:
The abstract mentions that self‑consistency over 64 samples raises MATH accuracy to 60.9 %. Is this self‑consistency applied only at inference time, or also during RL (e.g., sampling 64 outputs per question for advantage estimation)? If both, how are they reconciled?
Answer:
In the DeepSeekMath paper the two “64‑sample” numbers refer to two completely different stages of the pipeline, and they are **not** mixed together.

**Self‑consistency at inference**  
After the model (either DeepSeekMath‑Base, ‑Instruct or ‑RL) has been trained, the authors evaluate it on the MATH benchmark by generating *many* independent completions for each problem and then taking a majority vote (or a weighted vote) over the answers. This procedure is called *self‑consistency* (originally introduced in the “Self‑Consistency” paper for chain‑of‑thought reasoning). In their experiments they draw **64** samples per question, parse the final numeric answer from each sample and report the accuracy of the majority‑voted answer. The purpose of this step is purely to measure how much the model’s *distribution* over correct solutions can be exploited; it is performed **only during evaluation**, not during any training phase.

**GRPO training (RL)**  
Group Relative Policy Optimization is a reinforcement‑learning fine‑tuning algorithm. During each GRPO update the current policy (the “old policy’’ $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$) is asked to produce a *small group* of responses for every training question. The paper states that **for each question they sample 64 outputs** and feed those into the reward model; the average reward of the group serves as a baseline, and the per‑sample advantage $\hat A_{i,t}$ is computed from the *relative* scores inside that group. This grouping operation is what gives GRPO its name: it replaces the separate critic/value network of PPO with a simple intra‑group baseline.

Because GRPO already needs many samples per question to obtain a stable baseline, the authors reuse exactly 64 samples – the same number that later appears in the self‑consistency test – but these samples are **generated on‑the‑fly during training**, and their only role is to compute advantages for gradient updates. No voting or answer aggregation is performed in this stage; each sampled output is treated individually (with its relative advantage) when updating the policy.

**How they are reconciled**  
There is no direct interaction between the two uses of 64 samples:

1. **During RL (GRPO)**: 64 samples → compute group‑relative advantages → update policy weights.  
2. **During evaluation (self‑consistency)**: 64 samples → extract answers → majority vote → report accuracy.

The same hyperparameter number (64) is convenient because it supplies enough diversity for a reliable baseline in GRPO while also giving a strong statistical signal for self‑consistency voting. After training finishes, one can freely change the number of inference samples (e.g., 16, 32, 128) without affecting the RL process; conversely, one could train with a different group size (the authors experimented with other values) without changing how self‑consistency is applied at test time.

Thus, self‑consistency is an *inference‑time* post‑processing technique, whereas GRPO’s 64‑sample groups are an *training‑time* mechanism for estimating advantages. They share a numerical setting but serve distinct purposes and are not conflated in the method.
