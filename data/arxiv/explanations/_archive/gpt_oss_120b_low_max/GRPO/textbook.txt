\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Introduction to Mathematical Reasoning with Large Language Models  

Mathematical reasoning is a distinctive form of intelligence that demands precise manipulation of symbols, strict logical flow, and often the construction of multi‑step arguments.  When we consider large language models (LLMs) – neural networks trained on massive text corpora to predict the next token – it is natural to ask how well such models can emulate the disciplined thought processes that underlie mathematics.  This chapter explains why mathematical reasoning poses a special challenge for LLMs, surveys the historical trajectory of research in this area, and then presents **DeepSeekMath**, a recent open‑source system that pushes the limits of what can be achieved with a 7 billion‑parameter model.  Throughout we draw exclusively on the findings reported in the DeepSeekMath paper, emphasizing intuition and concrete experimental evidence.

---

## Why Mathematical Reasoning Is Hard for Language Models  

### Structured Symbolic Knowledge vs. Statistical Prediction  

At their core, LLMs are probabilistic sequence models: given a context of tokens they assign a probability distribution over the next token.  This works remarkably well for natural‑language tasks where meaning is often inferred from statistical regularities (e.g., “the cat sat on …” → “the”).  Mathematics, however, relies on **exact** relationships among symbols.  An arithmetic expression such as  

\[
\frac{(3+5)\times2}{4}=4
\]

cannot be tolerated to an off‑by‑one error; any deviation yields a completely invalid result.  The model must therefore learn to treat certain token sequences (operators, numbers, variable names) as **syntactic entities** with rigid semantics, rather than as loosely associated words.

### Long‑Range Dependencies and Multi‑Step Planning  

A typical competition‑level problem from the MATH benchmark contains a stem, several intermediate lemmas, and a final answer.  Solving it often requires **chain‑of‑thought** reasoning: generating an explicit sequence of intermediate statements before reaching the conclusion.  Each intermediate step may depend on calculations performed many tokens earlier, creating long‑range dependencies that exceed the usual context windows (4 K tokens in DeepSeekMath).  A naïve language model may lose track of earlier deductions, leading to contradictory or incomplete solutions.

### Requirement for External Knowledge and Tools  

Many mathematical problems become tractable only when auxiliary tools—symbolic algebra systems (e.g., *sympy*) or numerical libraries—are invoked.  A purely autoregressive model must either **internalize** these tool behaviours or explicitly generate code that calls them (the **program‑of‑thought** paradigm).  This hybrid reasoning—combining natural language explanation with executable code—adds another layer of difficulty: the model must learn both natural linguistic patterns and programming conventions.

### Sparse Supervision in Benchmarks  

Unlike next‑token prediction where every token provides a learning signal, most mathematical benchmarks provide only a **single correctness label** per problem (right or wrong answer).  Consequently, standard supervised training yields weak gradients for reasoning ability; the model rarely receives explicit feedback on whether each intermediate step was valid.

These obstacles collectively explain why early LLMs performed modestly on mathematics despite their impressive language abilities.

---

## Historical Progress: From Early LLMs to GPT‑4, Gemini‑Ultra, and Open‑Source Efforts  

### Early Attempts and Quantitative Reasoning Benchmarks  

The first systematic evaluations of LLMs on mathematics appeared with datasets such as **GSM8K** (grade‑school math) and later **MATH**, which contains competition‑level problems requiring multi‑step solutions.  Baseline transformer models trained solely on general text achieved accuracies in the low twenties on GSM8K and struggled dramatically on MATH (< 10 %).  

Researchers quickly discovered that **domain‑specific pre‑training** could raise performance substantially.  By feeding models large amounts of math–rich web text (e.g., StackExchange posts), papers like *Minerva* demonstrated that a 540 B parameter model pretrained on heavily curated mathematics data could reach roughly 45 % accuracy on MATH—still far below closed‑source giants but dramatically better than generic pretrained models.

### The Rise of Chain‑of‑Thought and Program‑of‑Thought Prompting  

A breakthrough came with **Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT)** prompting: simply asking a powerful model to “think step by step” caused it to generate explicit intermediate reasoning, boosting GSM8K scores by ~10 % points across many architectures.  Extending this idea, **Program‑of‑Thought (PoT)** prompted models to write executable Python snippets using libraries such as *math* or *sympy*; execution then produced a final answer.  PoT improved performance on problems where precise arithmetic or symbolic manipulation is essential.

### Closed‑Source Leaders: GPT‑4 and Gemini‑Ultra  

The most recent closed models—**GPT‑4** and **Gemini‑Ultra**—combine massive parameter counts (hundreds of billions), extensive instruction fine‑tuning, tool integration (code interpreter), and aggressive reinforcement learning from human feedback.  Their reported MATH scores exceed 60 % (GPT‑4) and approach 70 % (Gemini Ultra), establishing a new performance ceiling that open research strives to approach.

### Open‐Source Momentum  

Open‐source communities have responded with several notable projects:

* **Mistral 7B**, **Llemma 34B**, and **InternLM2-Math 20B**, which augment base language models with math datasets but remain considerably behind closed models.
* **WizardMath**, **MetaMath**, and **ToRA**, which incorporate CoT/PoT instruction tuning and sometimes PPO‐style reinforcement learning.
* Larger multilingual efforts such as **OpenWebMath** (13.6 B tokens) and **MathPile** (8.9 B tokens) that collect web pages containing mathematical content.

Despite these advances, none of the publicly available models have yet achieved > 50 % accuracy on the competition-level MATH benchmark—a gap that motivates the work presented in DeepSeekMath.

---

## Overview of DeepSeekMath: Goals and High-Level Architecture  

DeepSeekMath is designed as an end‑to‑end pipeline that systematically addresses each barrier identified above:

1. **Massive High-Quality Math Pretraining Corpus** – The authors construct the *DeepSeekMath Corpus* comprising ~120 B tokens drawn from Common Crawl webpages filtered for mathematical relevance using an iterative fastText classifier pipeline.  
2. **Foundation Model Initialized from Code-Optimized Pretraining** – The base model begins from *DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5* (7 B parameters).  Starting from a code‐oriented checkpoint gives the model strong familiarity with programming constructs, which later benefits PoT reasoning.
3. **Continual Pretraining Across Diverse Domains** – After initializing from the coder base, DeepSeekMath continues training on a mixture of math web text (56 %), algebraic code repositories (4 %), arXiv papers (10 %), GitHub code (20 %), and natural language data (10 %).  The total exposure is roughly 500 B tokens.
4. **Mathematical Instruction Tuning** – Using a curated dataset of ~776 K instruction examples covering both English and Chinese problems, each paired with CoT, PoT, or tool‐integrated solutions.
5. **Reinforcement Learning via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** – A lightweight variant of PPO that eliminates the need for an explicit value network by estimating baselines from groups of sampled completions; this reduces memory usage while preserving performance gains.
6. **Evaluation Across Multilingual Benchmarks** – Performance is measured not only on English datasets (GSM8K, MATH) but also Chinese benchmarks (CMATH, Gaokao variants) and formal theorem proving tasks (miniF2F).

The overall architecture can be visualized as three stages:

```
[Common Crawl → FastText Selector] → DeepSeekMath Corpus
          │
          ▼
[DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5] --(continual pretraining)→ DeepSeekMath-Base
          │
          ▼
[Math Instruction Data] --(supervised finetuning)→ DeepSeekMath-Instruct
          │
          ▼
[GRPO RL] ------------------------------------→ DeepSeekMath-RL
```

Each stage builds upon the previous one: higher-quality data yields richer representations; instruction tuning teaches explicit reasoning patterns; RL refines policy towards higher reward according to a learned comparator.

---

## Key Contributions of the DeepSeekMath Paper  

The authors delineate two major thematic contributions: (**I**) scalable mathematical pretraining; (**II**) an analysis and novel algorithm for reinforcement learning.

### I. Scalable Math Pretraining  

1. **Demonstration of Public Web Data Value** – By carefully filtering Common Crawl using an iterative fastText classifier trained first on OpenWebMath seeds then enriched through domain analysis, they assemble a corpus that is *seven times* larger than Minerva’s math webpages and *nine times* larger than OpenWebMath.  
2. **Empirical Validation Against Existing Corpora** – Training a 1.3 B parameter model for 150 B tokens on each candidate corpus shows that the DeepSeekMath Corpus yields superior downstream performance across eight benchmarks, especially in multilingual settings where English-only corpora fail to improve Chinese tasks.  
3. **Evidence That Parameter Count Is Not Sole Determinant** – The resulting *DeepSeekMath-Base* (7 B) attains performance comparable to Minerva’s 540 B model on GSM8K (64.2 %) and MATH (36.2 %). This indicates that high-quality domain data can compensate for smaller model capacity in mathematical reasoning contexts.
4. **Insights into Code Pretraining Benefits** – Starting from a code-specialized checkpoint markedly improves both program-aided reasoning (PoT) and pure CoT abilities compared with initializing from generic language models—a partial answer to whether “code training improves reasoning”.  
5. **Negative Result Regarding ArXiv Papers** – Contrary to prevailing practice, adding large volumes of arXiv LaTeX text does not yield measurable gains on any evaluated benchmark; in some cases it slightly harms performance.

### II. Exploration and Analysis of Reinforcement Learning  

1. **Introduction of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** – GRPO removes the traditional critic/value network required by PPO by computing baselines from *group-relative* reward averages across multiple sampled completions per question. The objective retains PPO’s clipped ratio term but adds an explicit KL penalty between current policy and reference model rather than embedding it into per-token rewards.
2. **Empirical Gains Using Only Instruction Data** – Applying GRPO solely on English instruction data boosts GSM8K accuracy from 82.9 % → 88.2 % and MATH from 46.8 % → 51.7 %, surpassing all open-source counterparts in the same parameter range.
3. **Unified Paradigm for SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, GRPO** – The paper formalizes all these methods under a single gradient equation involving three components: data source, reward function, algorithmic gradient coefficient. This abstraction enables systematic comparisons such as online versus offline sampling or outcome versus process supervision.
4. **Extensive Ablation Studies** – Experiments dissecting online vs offline data generation, outcome vs process reward signals, single-turn vs iterative RL illuminate why GRPO outperforms alternatives: group-relative advantage estimation provides finer-grained reinforcement while avoiding noisy value function training.
5. **Analysis of Why RL Helps** – Results show RL improves “Maj@K” accuracy (the proportion of correct answers among top-K generations) without substantially increasing “Pass@K”. This suggests RL reshapes the output distribution so that correct solutions become more likely among sampled candidates rather than fundamentally increasing per-sample competence—a subtle but valuable effect for downstream sampling strategies.

Collectively these contributions establish DeepSeekMath as both a high-performance open-source math LLM and a methodological reference point for future work in domain-specific pretraining and efficient reinforcement learning for large language models.

--- 

In summary, mathematical reasoning remains one of the most demanding tasks for language models due to its reliance on exact symbol manipulation, long-range logical dependencies, sparse supervision, and occasional need for external computational tools.  Over time researchers have progressed from naïve pretraining to sophisticated prompting techniques, massive closed-source systems now achieve near-human performance on competitive benchmarks, while open-source efforts continue catching up through better data pipelines and algorithmic innovations such as GRPO.  

DeepSeekMath exemplifies how targeted collection of high-quality web mathematics data—combined with code-aware initialization, instruction tuning spanning multiple languages, and memory-efficient reinforcement learning—allows a modestly sized 7 B model to surpass all existing open-source baselines and approach closed-source capabilities on challenging benchmarks like MATH.   The next chapters will delve deeper into each component described here: the fastText‐based data selection pipeline, the specifics of continual pretraining schedules, chain-of-thought versus program-of-thought instruction formats, and finally the mathematical derivation and practical implementation details of Group Relative Policy Optimization.*

**Building a Large‑Scale Math Pre‑Training Corpus**

The mathematical abilities of a language model are fundamentally tied to the quality and breadth of the data on which it is trained.  In the DeepSeekMath project, a dedicated corpus—**the DeepSeekMath Corpus**—was created by mining 120 billion tokens of mathematics‑related text from the public Common Crawl (CC) archive.  This chapter explains, in detail, how that corpus was assembled, emphasizing the intuition behind each design choice and the concrete engineering steps that turned raw web pages into a high‑quality, multilingual resource suitable for large‑scale pre‑training.

---

### Seed Data: OpenWebMath and Its Role

The construction process began with a **seed corpus** that would guide all subsequent data mining.  The authors selected **OpenWebMath**, a curated collection of math‑related web texts, for two reasons. First, OpenWebMath already contains verified examples of high‑quality mathematical content drawn from the Common Crawl, providing a reliable “positive” signal. Second, its size is modest enough to permit efficient training of a classifier while still being representative of the diverse styles (explanatory articles, problem statements, proofs) that appear on the web.

Using OpenWebMath as the seed serves two purposes: (1) it supplies concrete examples for a supervised classifier to learn what constitutes “math‑related” text, and (2) it anchors the iterative pipeline so that each new round of collection can be evaluated against an established benchmark of relevance.

---

### FastText Classifier Design and Training Procedure

To sift through billions of web pages, the authors employed **fastText**, a lightweight linear model that learns word‑level n‑gram embeddings. The simplicity of fastText makes it feasible to train on millions of examples and to evaluate tens of billions of documents quickly.

The training set was constructed as follows:

| Category | Source | Number of Examples |
|----------|--------|---------------------|
| Positive | Randomly sampled from OpenWebMath | 500 000 |
| Negative | Randomly sampled from Common Crawl (non‑math pages) | 500 000 |

Key hyper‑parameters were:

- Embedding dimension = 256  
- Learning rate = 0.1  
- Maximum n‑gram length = 3 (captures short phrases such as “solve for x”)  
- Minimum word occurrence = 3 (filters extremely rare tokens)  
- Training epochs = 3  

The resulting classifier outputs a **score** for each web page indicating its likelihood of containing mathematical content.  By ranking pages according to this score, the pipeline can retain only those pages that are most reminiscent of the seed data.

---

### Iterative Data‑Collection Pipeline (Four Iterations)

A single pass of the classifier does not retrieve all relevant material because the initial seed lacks diversity.  Consequently, the authors designed an **iterative refinement loop**:

1. **First Pass** – Apply the classifier to a deduplicated subset of Common Crawl (≈40 B HTML pages after URL deduplication). Keep only the highest‑scoring pages until a pre‑chosen token budget is reached (initially 40 B tokens).  
2. **Analysis & Expansion** – Examine which domains contributed many retrieved pages and which still had low coverage.  
3. **Domain Enrichment** – Identify domains where *≥10 %* of their pages were already classified as math‑related; manually label additional URLs within those domains that likely contain math (e.g., `mathoverflow.net/questions`). These newly labeled pages are added to the seed set.  
4. **Retraining** – Re‑train fastText on the enlarged positive set and repeat steps 1–3.

After four such iterations, the process converged: the third iteration had already captured ~98 % of what would later be obtained in the fourth, indicating diminishing returns.  The final output consisted of **35.5 million** distinct web pages amounting to **120 billion tokens**.

---

### Domain‑Level Heuristics and Manual URL Annotation

To accelerate coverage beyond what automatic classification alone could achieve, the authors introduced a **domain‑level heuristic**:

- The entire Common Crawl corpus was partitioned into *domains* defined by a shared base URL (e.g., all pages under `math.stackexchange.com`).
- For each domain, they computed the proportion of pages flagged as mathematical in the first iteration.
- Domains with a proportion >10 % were flagged as *potentially math‑rich*.
- Within these domains, human annotators examined URL patterns (such as `/questions/` or `/problems/`) to pinpoint sections most likely to host math content.
- URLs thus identified were crawled for additional pages that had not been retrieved automatically; these new pages became part of the positive training set for subsequent classifier retraining.

This combination of coarse statistical filtering followed by fine‑grained manual labeling ensured both breadth (capturing whole sites) and depth (collecting lower‑level pages that may have been missed).

---

### Deduplication, Near‑Deduplication, and Token Budgeting

Processing hundreds of billions of raw HTML documents would be infeasible without aggressive duplication removal.  The pipeline applied two complementary strategies:

1. **URL‑Based Deduplication** – Exact duplicate URLs were discarded immediately.
2. **Near‑Deduplication** – Documents with highly similar textual content (e.g., mirrored tutorials across different subdomains) were identified using hashing techniques and removed, preserving only one representative copy.

These steps reduced the raw Common Crawl dump to **40 B unique HTML pages**, dramatically lowering both storage requirements and downstream computational cost.

Because pre‑training resources are finite, the authors experimented with several token budgets—40 B, 80 B, 120 B, and 160 B—to determine diminishing returns. Empirically, models trained on up to 120 B tokens continued to improve on downstream benchmarks, while additional tokens yielded marginal gains; thus 120 B was adopted as the final corpus size.

---

### Benchmark De‑Contamination Strategy

A crucial concern is **benchmark leakage**: if any training document contains verbatim excerpts from evaluation datasets (e.g., GSM8K or MATH), model performance could be artificially inflated.

To guard against this, DeepSeekMath applied an exact‑match filter inspired by prior work:

- Any 10‑gram sequence appearing in any benchmark test set was searched across the collected corpus.
- If a page contained such a sequence, that segment was excised.
- For shorter benchmark excerpts (3–9 grams), exact matching was also performed.
- This procedure was applied both to English benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH) and Chinese benchmarks (CMATH, AGIEval).

By removing any overlapping n‑grams before pre‑training, the authors ensured that improvements measured on those benchmarks stem from genuine reasoning ability rather than memorization.

---

### Multilingual Coverage and Its Impact

Unlike many existing math corpora that are overwhelmingly English (\>85 % arXiv papers), the DeepSeekMath Corpus deliberately retained **multilingual content**, with English and Chinese comprising the majority of tokens. This multilinguality emerged naturally because:

- The domain heuristics captured entire sites that host bilingual educational material (e.g., Chinese university lecture notes alongside English translations).
- Manual URL annotation did not restrict collection to English domains.

Empirical evaluation showed that models pre‑trained on this corpus achieved superior scores on both English and Chinese mathematical benchmarks compared with models trained on monolingual corpora such as MathPile or OpenWebMath. The multilingual exposure also yielded modest improvements on general language understanding tasks (MMLU) and coding benchmarks, suggesting cross-lingual transfer benefits.

---

### Summary

The DeepSeekMath Corpus demonstrates how systematic mining of publicly available web data can produce a massive, high-quality mathematical dataset without relying on proprietary sources.  Starting from a modest seed (OpenWebMath), training an inexpensive fastText classifier, iteratively expanding positive examples via domain statistics and human annotation, rigorously deduplicating content, enforcing strict de‑contamination against benchmark leaks, and preserving multilingual material together resulted in **120 billion tokens** spanning **35.5 million** web pages.  

These engineering decisions directly translated into measurable gains: when used for continued pre‑training of a 7B parameter model, this corpus enabled performance comparable to much larger closed‑source systems on challenging reasoning benchmarks such as MATH and GSM8K. The methodology outlined here provides a reproducible template for building domain-specific corpora at scale from open web archives.

# Evaluating Corpus Quality  

The capability of a large language model (LLM) to perform mathematical reasoning is heavily dependent on the data it has seen during pre‑training.  In the DeepSeekMath project the authors therefore devoted a substantial effort to constructing a new, large‑scale math‑focused corpus – the **DeepSeekMath Corpus** – and then empirically validated its superiority over previously available mathematical corpora.  This chapter walks through those validation experiments in detail.  It explains which corpora were compared, how a controlled training protocol was set up for a 1.3‑billion‑parameter model, which benchmarks were used for evaluation, and what the observed learning curves and final performance numbers tell us about data quality, scale, and multilingual coverage.

---

## Competing Corpora: MathPile, OpenWebMath, Proof‑Pile‑2  

To assess whether the DeepSeekMath Corpus truly provides an advantage, the authors selected three publicly released math‑related datasets that have been used in prior work:

1. **MathPile** – An 8.9 B‑token collection assembled from textbooks, Wikipedia, ProofWiki, StackExchange, and a dominant proportion (≈ 85 %) of arXiv papers.  The corpus is deliberately diverse but heavily weighted toward scientific literature.

2. **OpenWebMath** – A 13.6 B‑token slice of Common Crawl that has been filtered using heuristics for mathematical content.  It is smaller than MathPile but originates entirely from web pages rather than academic PDFs.

3. **Proof‑Pile‑2** – A composite corpus consisting of three components:  
   * OpenWebMath (≈ 13 B tokens),  
   * **AlgebraicStack**, a 10.3 B‑token dataset of mathematical code (e.g., SymPy scripts), and  
   * arXiv papers (≈ 28 B tokens).  
   The authors followed the preprocessing recipe of Llemma and adopted a token‐ratio of 2 : 4 : 1 (web : code : arXiv) when training on this mix.

All three baselines are substantially smaller than the **DeepSeekMath Corpus**, which contains **120 B** tokens of mathematics harvested from Common Crawl via a fastText classifier and subsequent human verification.  The size difference alone suggests potential gains, but the experiments were designed to isolate *quality* from sheer quantity.

---

## Training Protocol for a 1.3 B Model on Each Corpus  

A common experimental platform is essential for a fair comparison.  The authors used an internally built 1.3 B‑parameter LLM (named **DeepSeek‑LLM 1.3B**) whose architecture mirrors that of the larger DeepSeek series (decoder‑only transformer with rotary positional embeddings).  The training configuration was identical across all corpora:

| Hyperparameter | Value |
|----------------|-------|
| Optimizer | AdamW (β₁=0.9, β₂=0.95) |
| Weight decay | 0.1 |
| Peak learning rate | 5.3×10⁻⁴ |
| Warm‑up steps | 2 000 |
| Learning‑rate schedule | Multi‑step: decay to 31.6 % at 80 % of steps, then to 10 % at 90 % |
| Batch size | 4 M tokens |
| Context length | 4 K tokens |
| Total tokens seen | **150 B** per corpus (≈ 115 epochs on Proof‑Pile‑2, fewer epochs on larger corpora) |
| Training framework | HAI‑LLM (lightweight distributed engine) |

By fixing **exactly** the same number of token updates (150 B) across all runs, any performance differences can be attributed to the *content* of the corpora rather than differences in training duration or optimizer settings.

---

## Evaluation Benchmarks  

The authors assessed each trained model using few‑shot **chain‑of‑thought (CoT)** prompting on eight well‑established mathematics benchmarks that span elementary to graduate level problems and include both English and Chinese data:

| Benchmark | Language | Task type |
|-----------|----------|-----------|
| **GSM8K** | English | Grade‑school quantitative reasoning (≈ 8 K problems) |
| **MATH** | English | Competition‑level problems from high school olympiads |
| **CMATH** | Chinese | Analogous to MATH but sourced from Chinese contest sets |
| **MGSM‑zh** | Chinese | Chinese translation of GSM8K style questions |
| **MMLU‑STEM** | English | Multiple‑choice STEM questions from the Massive Multitask Language Understanding suite |
| **Gaokao‑MathCloze** | Chinese | Cloze style high school exam items |
| **Gaokao‑MathQA** | Chinese | Full question‐answer format from Chinese national exams |
| **SAT** (optional) | English | Standardized test quantitative reasoning |

All evaluations were performed under a *zero‑shot* regime for most tasks; when few‐shot examples were required by the benchmark protocol, the same prompt template was used for every model so that variations in performance arise only from model knowledge.

---

## Analysis of Learning Curves and Performance Gaps  

### Overall Trend  

When plotted as accuracy versus training step (or equivalently versus tokens processed), the model trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus consistently outperformed its peers across every benchmark.  Two complementary phenomena emerged:

1. **Higher Asymptotic Accuracy** – Even after many training steps the DeepSeekMath model retained an advantage of several percentage points on most benchmarks.
2. **Steeper Early Learning Curve** – In the first few hundred million steps the DeepSeekMath model’s accuracy rose more quickly than those trained on MathPile or Proof‑Pile‑2, indicating that high‐quality data yields more informative gradient signals early on.

Figure 4 in the original paper (reproduced conceptually here) shows that at around **50 B tokens**, DeepSeekMath attains ≈ 55 % on MATH while Proof‑Pile‑2 lags near 44 %. By the time all models have consumed their full 150 B tokens, DeepSeekMath climbs to **51.7 %**, whereas Proof‑Pile‑2 reaches only ≈ 38 % and MathPile hovers near 35 %.

### Multilingual Gains  

Because the DeepSeekMath Corpus intentionally incorporated non-English web pages—most prominently Chinese—its benefit is especially evident on Chinese benchmarks:

* On **CMATH**, DeepSeekMath achieved a gain of roughly +8 % absolute over Proof‑Pile‑2.
* On **MGSM-zh**, performance improved by +6 % absolute relative to both MathPile and OpenWebMath.

In contrast, MathPile and Proof-Pile-2 are overwhelmingly English; training on them actually degraded performance on Chinese tasks compared with a baseline that received no math pretraining at all.

### Scale Versus Quality  

One might argue that simply adding more tokens would close the gap. The authors examined this by extending training on smaller corpora to larger token counts (e.g., 120 B tokens of Proof-Pile-2). The curves exhibited clear *plateaus*: after roughly 50–60 B tokens further exposure produced diminishing returns, whereas DeepSeekMath continued to improve up to its full 120 B token budget. This observation supports two conclusions:

* The DeepSeekMath Corpus provides higher *average* per-token information density for mathematical reasoning.
* Merely scaling up token count without improving data selection yields limited extra benefit.

### Effect of Domain Diversity  

Proof-Pile-2 mixes code (AlgebraicStack) with text; however, its code component does not directly translate into better mathematical problem solving when evaluated purely on natural language reasoning tasks such as GSM8K or MATH. The experiments suggest that while code exposure can help *program-aided* reasoning (e.g., writing Python scripts), it does not substitute for high-quality textual explanations of problem statements and solution strategies—precisely what the DeepSeekMath pipeline curated through fastText classification and human annotation.

---

## Interpretation of Results  

The empirical evidence leads to several interconnected insights about corpus design for mathematical LLMs:

1. **Targeted Web Mining Yields High-Quality Tokens**  
   The fastText classifier trained on OpenWebMath seed data succeeded in retrieving web pages rich in step-by-step explanations, proofs, and worked examples—content that directly teaches a model how to reason rather than merely presenting static formulas.

2. **Multilingual Coverage Is Not Optional**  
   Inclusion of Chinese web pages lifted performance not only on Chinese benchmarks but also provided a modest cross-lingual benefit on English tasks, possibly because multilingual exposure encourages more robust token representations for mathematical symbols and terminology.

3. **Quantity Amplifies Quality When Data Are Clean**  
   Scaling from 40 B to 120 B tokens continued to push accuracy upward without encountering saturation points seen in smaller corpora; thus an *efficient data selection pipeline* can be safely combined with massive token budgets.

4. **Code Pretraining Complementary but Not Sufficient**  
   Adding large blocks of code improves program-of-thought capabilities but does not replace textual reasoning data; this aligns with later findings in the paper that pretraining on code before math yields better overall reasoning abilities.

5. **Benchmark Contamination Controls Matter**  
   By rigorously excising any n-gram overlap with official test sets (10-gram exact matches or shorter exact matches when possible), the authors ensured that gains cannot be explained by memorization—a crucial step when evaluating open-domain web data.

Overall, these results validate the central claim of the DeepSeekMath paper: *a carefully engineered, multilingual web-derived corpus containing hundreds of billions of math tokens can dramatically boost mathematical reasoning in modestly sized LLMs*, achieving performance comparable to much larger proprietary systems.

# Pre‑Training DeepSeekMath‑Base 7B

In this chapter we describe how the DeepSeekMath‑Base 7B model is built from a code‑oriented founder, how a massive mixture of mathematical, programming, and natural‑language data is combined, and which training recipes are employed to obtain a strong reasoning engine.  After the training details we examine the model’s performance on a broad spectrum of tasks: step‑by‑step mathematical problem solving, tool‑augmented reasoning, formal theorem proving, and generic language understanding, reasoning, and coding benchmarks.  Throughout the discussion we remain faithful to the experimental observations reported in the DeepSeekMath paper.

---

## Model Initialization from DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5

DeepSeekMath‑Base 7B does not start from a generic language model.  Instead it inherits the parameters of **DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5 7B**, a model that was originally trained on a large corpus of source code together with natural language.  This choice is motivated by two empirical findings reported in the paper:

1. **Code pre‑training endows the model with strong execution‐oriented capabilities** (e.g., handling Python libraries such as `math` and `sympy`).  
2. **A code foundation provides a better starting point for subsequent mathematical pre‑training** than a purely general LLM.  

Consequently, all subsequent learning stages begin with the weights of DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5, after which only the newly introduced mathematics‑focused data are presented to the model.

---

## Token Distribution (Math, Code, Natural Language)

During continual pre‑training the authors mix several data streams in fixed proportions:

| Data source                              | Approximate share of total tokens |
|------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **DeepSeekMath Corpus** (120 B math tokens collected from Common Crawl) | **56 %** |
| **AlgebraicStack** (mathematical code)   | 4 % |
| **arXiv** papers                         | 10 % |
| **GitHub** source code                    | 20 % |
| **General Natural Language** (English & Chinese web text) | 10 % |

The dominant portion (more than half) consists of web pages that have been filtered for high mathematical content using a fastText classifier trained on OpenWebMath.  The remaining streams serve complementary purposes: AlgebraicStack supplies well‑structured symbolic code; arXiv offers scientific discourse (although later experiments show its limited impact on reasoning); GitHub reinforces the model’s ability to read and generate executable code; and natural language data maintain fluency across English and Chinese.

Because the training corpus is multilingual—English dominates but Chinese appears in substantial quantity—the resulting model exhibits improved performance on Chinese math benchmarks compared with prior English‑only pipelines.

---

## Training Hyperparameters

All stages of the pre‑training follow the same optimization framework that was already successful for DeepSeek LLMs:

* **Optimizer:** AdamW with β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.95, weight decay = 0.1.  
* **Learning rate schedule:** A multi‑step schedule that linearly ramps up to a peak after 2 000 warm‑up steps. The learning rate then decays to 31.6 % of its peak at 80 % of total steps and finally to 10 % of the peak at 90 % of steps.  
* **Peak learning rate:** For DeepSeekMath‑Base the peak is set to **4.2 × 10⁻⁴** (slightly lower than in earlier quality‑validation experiments).  
* **Batch size:** Tokens are accumulated into batches of **10 M tokens** per update, substantially larger than the 4 M token batches used for smaller experiments, which improves throughput on modern clusters.  
* **Context length:** Sequences are truncated or padded to a maximum length of **4 K tokens**, ensuring that long mathematical passages can be processed while keeping memory consumption tractable.  
* **Total training budget:** The model is exposed to **500 B tokens** drawn from the mixture described above.

The same hyperparameters that proved effective for quality validation experiments (see Section “Validating the Quality of the DeepSeekMath Corpus”) are retained here, underscoring their robustness across scales.

---

## Mathematical Problem Solving with Chain‑of‑Thought Prompting

To assess pure reasoning ability—i.e., solving problems without invoking external tools—the authors evaluate DeepSeekMath‑Base using *few‑shot chain‑of‑thought* (CoT) prompting on eight benchmarks spanning English and Chinese:

* **Quantitative reasoning:** GSM8K, MATH (competition level), CMATH (Chinese).  
* **Multiple‑choice style:** MMLU–STEM, Gaokao–MathQA (Chinese).  

The CoT protocol presents a small number of exemplars that explicitly show intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at a final answer.  Results (Table `base_math_cot` in the paper) demonstrate that DeepSeekMath‑Base:

* Outperforms all open‑source base models—including Mistral 7B and Llemma 34B—by large margins across all eight datasets.
* Beats Minerva 540B—a closed‐source base model that is more than seventy times larger—on the competition‐level MATH benchmark by more than ten absolute percentage points.

These findings confirm that *high‐quality math pre‐training can compensate for much larger parameter counts*, provided the training data are suitably curated.

---

## Program‑of‑Thought (Tool‐Use) Evaluation

Beyond textual reasoning, modern benchmarks often allow models to generate executable Python programs whose outputs constitute the answer—a paradigm called *program‐of‐thought* (PoT) or *tool‐integrated reasoning*.  

The authors test DeepSeekMath‑Base on GSM8K and MATH using few‑shot PoT prompts where libraries such as `math` and `sympy` may be called for exact symbolic manipulation or numerical evaluation. The pipeline is:

1. The model receives a problem statement and is asked to write a Python script that solves it.
2. The generated script is executed in a sandbox; its printed result is compared with the ground truth answer.

DeepSeekMath‑Base attains higher accuracy than Llama‐based baselines such as Llemma 34B, confirming that its *code foundation plus extensive math token exposure* enables robust program synthesis for mathematical tasks.

---

## Formal Mathematics: MiniF2F Autoformalization

Formal theorem proving provides an even stricter test: given an informal statement together with an informal proof sketch, the model must output a formally correct proof in a proof assistant (here Isabelle).  

The authors evaluate DeepSeekMath‑Base on *miniF2F*, a benchmark derived from Olympiad‐level problems curated for autoformalization research. The evaluation protocol follows prior work:

1. Provide few examples of informal statements, their formal counterparts, and informal proof outlines.
2. Ask the model to generate a “proof sketch” in Isabelle syntax.
3. Feed this sketch to *Sledgehammer*, an automated prover that attempts to fill missing details and produce a complete formal proof.

Despite being only 7 B parameters, DeepSeekMath‑Base shows “strong few‐shot autoformalization performance,” indicating that large‐scale math pre‐training yields representations useful for bridging informal mathematics and rigorous formal systems.

---

## General Language Understanding (MMLU), Reasoning (BBH), and Coding (HumanEval/MBPP)

Mathematical pre‐training should not degrade other abilities; instead it can enhance them because many reasoning patterns are shared across domains.

* **MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding):** Evaluates 57 multiple‐choice subjects including STEM topics; DeepSeekMath‑Base scores substantially higher than its predecessor DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base for STEM categories, confirming transfer from math to broader knowledge tasks.
* **BBH (BIG Bench Hard):** Consists of challenging multi-step reasoning problems; DeepSeekMath‑Base again outperforms both its code precursor and general LLM baselines.
* **Coding Benchmarks (HumanEval & MBPP):** These require generating correct Python functions given docstrings or problem descriptions. Since DeepSeekMath retains its original code training data (~20 % of tokens), it preserves coding competence comparable to DeepSeek‑Coder 7B while gaining additional mathematical insight.

Overall, Table `understanding_reasoning_code` in the paper shows unified gains across all three dimensions: language understanding, reasoning, and coding.

## Ablation Insights: Code Pre‑Training vs. Pure Math Pre‑Training

A central research question addressed by the authors is whether *code pre-training contributes to mathematical reasoning*. To answer this they conduct controlled ablations:

1. **Two–stage pipeline:**  
   *Stage A:* Train on 400 B code tokens → *Stage B:* Fine tune on 150 B math tokens.  
   *Baseline:* Train on 400 B generic text → fine tune on math tokens.

   Results reveal that models receiving explicit code exposure before math training achieve significantly higher performance on both GSM8K/MATH *with* tool use *and* on pure CoT tasks compared with those trained only on generic text first.

2. **One–stage mixture:**  
   Combine code tokens and math tokens into a single training corpus (400 B code + 150 B math). This mitigates catastrophic forgetting observed when code pre-training is followed by pure math training; it also synergistically improves both coding ability and program-assisted math solving.

3. **Effect on non-tool mathematics:**  
   Even without tool use, code pre-training yields modest gains early in training; however when mixed with math tokens in one stage, very small models (1.3 B) may still struggle to fully absorb both modalities simultaneously.

These ablations support the claim made in Section “Contributions → Math Pre-Training at Scale” that *code training indeed improves mathematical reasoning*, especially when followed by or interleaved with extensive math token exposure.

---

### Summary

DeepSeekMath‑Base 7B demonstrates that a modestly sized model can achieve near–state‐of‐the‐art quantitative reasoning performance when:

* It starts from a strong code foundation (DeepSeek‑Coder).
* It is continually exposed to an unprecedented volume of high-quality webderived mathematics (≈120 B tokens), carefully filtered by fastText classifiers.
* It receives balanced exposure to programming material, scholarly articles, and bilingual natural language text.
* It follows robust optimizer settings—AdamW with aggressive warmup/decay schedules—and processes large token batches over half a trillion tokens total.

Evaluations confirm improvements across pure chain-of-thought solving, program-aided reasoning, formal proof generation, as well as broader linguistic and coding competencies. Ablation studies underscore that *code pre-training plays an essential role* in catalyzing these gains, validating longstanding hypotheses about cross-domain transfer between programming and mathematical reasoning.
