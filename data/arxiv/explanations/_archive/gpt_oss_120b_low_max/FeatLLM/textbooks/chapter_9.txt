\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 9: **Experimental Evaluation – Datasets and Baselines**

The empirical section of the study is built upon a careful selection of thirteen tabular benchmark problems and a diverse suite of comparative methods.  This chapter presents an in‑depth description of the data resources, explains the motivations for choosing each dataset, and details the implementation choices that make the comparison both fair and reproducible.  Throughout the discussion we adopt the notation introduced in the Methods chapter: each dataset consists of a collection  
\[
\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}^{i},\mathbf{y}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]
where \(\mathbf{x}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) denotes a vector of \(d\) features whose natural‑language names are gathered in the set \(F=\{f_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d}\), and \(\mathbf{y}^{i}\) denotes a class label drawn from a finite set of \(c\) categories.  In every experiment only a small subset of \(k\) examples (\(k\in \{2,4,8,16,32\}\)) is retained for training, thereby placing the methods under a strict few‑shot regime.

---

### Dataset Characteristics

The thirteen data sets span traditional UCI repositories, recent contributions that post‑date the pre‑training cut‑off of contemporary large language models, and fully synthetic problems designed to rule out memorization effects.  All of them are classification tasks—either binary or multi‑class—and they vary widely in size, dimensionality, and domain.

1. **Adult** (UCI Census Income) supplies demographic attributes such as age, education, and occupation for the purpose of predicting whether an individual earns more than \$50 000 per year.  The data set contains roughly \(N\approx 48\,842\) records and \(d=14\) features, most of which are categorical.

2. **Bank** (Portuguese Banking Marketing) records information about clients contacted during a telemarketing campaign.  The goal is to predict subscription to a term deposit.  It includes \(N\approx 45\,211\) rows and \(d=16\) attributes, combining numeric balances with categorical contact details.

3. **Blood** (Blood Donation) tracks donor histories (e.g., number of donations, interval since last donation) to predict whether a donor will return.  The set comprises \(N\approx 748\) instances with \(d=5\) numeric variables.

4. **Car** (Automobile Evaluation) encodes characteristics of cars—such as buying price and safety—into a multi‑class quality label (unacceptable, acceptable, good, very good).  This benchmark contains \(N=1\,728\) samples and \(d=6\) features that are largely categorical.

5. **Communities** (Crime Data) aggregates socio‑economic statistics for neighborhoods across the United States to predict high versus low crime rates.  It is one of the most demanding benchmarks in this study: \(N\approx 1\,993\) instances but more than \(d=128\) attributes, many of which are continuous.

6. **Credit‑g** (German Credit Data) asks whether an applicant presents a good or bad credit risk based on financial and personal variables.  The data set consists of \(N=1\,000\) rows with \(d=20\) mixed‑type features.

7. **Diabetes** (Pima Indians Diabetes) records physiological measurements (e.g., glucose level, BMI) to predict diabetes onset.  The collection holds \(N=768\) samples with exactly \(d=8\) numeric attributes.

8. **Heart** (Heart Failure Clinical Records) contains clinical measurements such as cholesterol level and ejection fraction; the task is to forecast coronary artery disease presence.  It includes \(N=303\) rows and \(d=13\) attributes spanning both numeric and categorical types.

9. **Myocardial** (Myocardial Infarction Complications) addresses chronic heart failure prediction from medical test results.  This data set has over one hundred features (\(d>100\)) and poses a substantial challenge for models that rely on fixed prompt lengths.

10. **Cultivars** (Soybean Grain Yield) predicts whether a soybean cultivar will achieve high grain yield based on agronomic traits.  It is a recent addition to the UCI repository (released after September 2023), ensuring that it could not have been encountered during LLM pre‑training; it holds approximately \(N=5\,560\) instances with \(d≈50\).

11. **NHANES** (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey) requires predicting an individual’s age group from health examination variables.  Like Cultivars, this dataset was released after the LLM’s training cut‑off; it comprises around \(N=10\,000\) cases with roughly \(d≈30\).

12. **Sequence‑type** is a synthetic collection where each example is a numeric sequence generated from one of four families: arithmetic progression, geometric progression, Fibonacci numbers, or Collatz orbit.  The task is to assign the correct family label; each instance is represented by its first ten terms (\(d=10\)), making it fully deterministic yet unseen by any language model.

13. **Solution‑mix** is another synthetic benchmark in which four solution concentrations are mixed; the label indicates whether the resulting mixture exceeds a threshold concentration of 0.5 .  Each record contains four continuous values (\(d=4\)), guaranteeing that no memorization can aid performance.

The inclusion of recent real‑world data (Cultivars and NHANES) together with artificial problems safeguards against inadvertent leakage of training knowledge into the language model side‑information; consequently any observed gains can be confidently attributed to the reasoning capabilities of FeatLLM rather than memorized facts.

### Baseline Methods

To situate FeatLLM’s performance within the broader landscape of few‑shot tabular learning, nine representative baselines were selected: three classical supervised learners that ignore prior knowledge, two semi‑supervised approaches that exploit large unlabeled corpora, one pre‑training based Bayesian network method, and three recent LLM‑centric techniques.

1. **Logistic Regression (LogReg)** proceeds by fitting a linear decision function directly on the raw feature matrix \(\mathbf{X}\).  The model optimizes cross‑entropy loss with \(\ell_{2}\) regularization; hyper‑parameters are selected via grid search combined with cross‑validation on the few available shots.

2. **XGBoost** implements gradient‑boosted decision trees that iteratively improve upon residuals from previous trees.  It is highly effective for tabular problems due to its ability to capture non‑linear interactions without extensive feature engineering.

3. **Random Forest** aggregates an ensemble of decision trees trained on bootstrap samples of \(\mathcal{D}\).  Like XGBoost it leverages tree structures but without boosting; depth and number of estimators are tuned using cross‑validation.

All three classic baselines rely purely on the labeled subset; they provide a lower bound for performance when no external knowledge is available.

4. **SCARF** adopts semi‑supervised contrastive learning on an extensive pool of unlabeled tables; it learns representations that are subsequently fine‑tuned on the few labeled examples.

5. **STUNT** extends semi‑supervised learning by constructing pseudo‑labels for unlabeled instances through self‐training; its design specifically targets tabular data with mixed feature types.

Both SCARF and STUNT assume access to large unlabeled repositories that may be unrealistic in many industrial contexts; nevertheless they serve as strong comparators for methods that exploit auxiliary information beyond few labeled points.

6. **TabPFN** pre‑trains a Bayesian neural network on synthetically generated tables sampled from diverse distributions; at inference time it receives both training and test rows simultaneously and produces posterior predictive probabilities without further gradient updates.

7-9 encompass approaches that explicitly enlist large language models:

* **In‑context Learning** places few-shot examples and task description directly into an LLM prompt (here GPT‑3.5), expecting the model to output predictions for each test sample end‑to‑end.
* **TABLET** augments plain in‑context prompting with an external classifier that supplies rule prototypes; this hybrid design aims to improve reliability while still relying on LLM inference per sample.
* **TabLLM** fine‑tunes an LLM using parameter‑efficient adapters such as IA3 or LoRA on the few labeled rows before employing it for prediction; it thus incurs additional training cost but seeks higher accuracy by adapting model weights.

These three baselines share a common limitation: every test instance requires at least one forward pass through the LLM, which translates into substantial computational overhead and often runs into prompt length restrictions when many features are present.

### Implementation Specifics

All experiments were conducted using Python 3.10 on machines equipped with Nvidia A100 GPUs unless otherwise noted.  

*The language model backbone.*  
FeatLLM employs OpenAI’s GPT‑3.5 Turbo (model identifier `gpt-3.5-turbo-0613`) as its reasoning engine for rule extraction and code generation.  Although FeatLLM’s architecture is agnostic to the particular LLM—appendix results demonstrate similar behavior with Llama 2—GPT‑3.5 was chosen because it offers stable API access without requiring local weights or fine‑tuning infrastructure.

*Prompt parameters.*  
When calling the LLM for rule generation we set temperature \(=0.5\), allowing moderate stochasticity needed for ensemble diversity while keeping responses coherent enough for reliable parsing.  Top‑p was left at its default value \(1\).  

*Ensemble configuration.*  
Each run spans \(T=20\) independent trials; within each trial FeatLLM extracts exactly \(R=10\) binary rules per class (a value chosen after ablation studies indicated diminishing returns beyond ten).  Random seed variation influences both demonstration ordering and feature bagging—subsets of columns or rows are selected per trial so that prompt length stays within model limits even for high–dimensional data such as Communities or Myocardial.

*Linear downstream model.*  
The binary rule matrix \(\mathbf{Z}_{k}^{i}\in \{0,1\}^{R}\), defined for class \(k\), feeds into a biasless linear classifier whose parameters \(\mathbf{w}_{k}\in \mathbb{R}^{R}_{+}\)are constrained to be nonnegative by applying \(\max(\mathbf{w}_{k},0)\).  Optimization uses Adam with learning rate \(0 .01\); training proceeds for up to \(200\) epochs while monitoring cross‑entropy loss via $k$-fold cross validation on the few-shot training split to select an early stopping point.

*Baseline reproduction.*  
For fair comparison we reproduced each baseline under identical few-shot splits:

- In‐context Learning used GPT‑3.5 with identical temperature settings but no chain‐of‐thought instructions.
- TABLET added rule prototypes generated by an auxiliary decision tree trained on available shots.
- TabLLM was implemented using T0 as specified in its original paper because GPT‐based checkpoints were not publicly available for adapter tuning.
- Classical models received raw numeric encodings: categorical variables were one‐hot encoded after consulting their textual definitions supplied in $F$, matching common practice across tabular pipelines.
- SCARF and STUNT accessed external unlabeled repositories provided by their authors; their hyperparameters followed recommended defaults (e.g., number of pretraining epochs \(50\), contrastive temperature \(0 .07\)).

All baselines were evaluated using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), averaged over three random repetitions of train/test splits per dataset; standard deviations capture variability induced by different few-shot selections.

### Summary

The experimental design deliberately spans a wide spectrum—from modestly sized health records to high‐dimensional crime statistics—and juxtaposes FeatLLM against both traditional machine learners and state-of-the-art LLM‐based systems under strict few-shot constraints.  By standardizing implementation details—such as optimizer settings for downstream linear models, prompt temperature for LLM calls, and ensemble size—we ensure that performance differences can be attributed primarily to methodological innovations rather than engineering disparities.  The subsequent results chapter will reveal how FeatLLM consistently leverages its rule extraction capability to achieve superior AUROC scores while incurring only marginal inference cost compared with full LLM end-to-end baselines.
