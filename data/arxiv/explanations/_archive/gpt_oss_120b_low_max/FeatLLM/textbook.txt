\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

# Motivation and Overview of Few‑Shot Tabular Learning  

In many real‑world applications, data naturally appear in tabular form: rows correspond to individual entities (patients, customers, experiments) and columns correspond to measured attributes (age, blood pressure, credit score).  While deep learning has dramatically reduced the need for hand‑crafted features on images or text, tabular datasets still pose a uniquely difficult problem when only a handful of labeled examples are available.  This chapter explains why few‑shot learning on tabular data is hard, surveys the traditional strategies that have been employed to mitigate the scarcity of labels, and then introduces the emerging role of large language models (LLMs) as external knowledge bases.  We conclude by outlining the key limitations of existing LLM‑centric methods and motivating the central idea behind **FeatLLM**, which treats an LLM as a feature engineer rather than as an end‑to‑end predictor.  

---  

## Why Tabular Data Remain Hard for Few‑Shot Learning  

Tabular data differ from images or natural language in several fundamental ways that make them resistant to the few‑shot techniques that have succeeded elsewhere.  

First, **heterogeneity of columns** is ubiquitous.  A single table may contain continuous measurements (e.g., serum glucose), ordinal scales (e.g., cancer stage), categorical identifiers (e.g., blood type), and even free‑text notes.  The statistical relationships among such diverse feature types are highly dataset‑specific, and there is no universal inductive bias—such as translation invariance for images—that a model can safely assume.  

Second, **lack of spatial or sequential structure** limits the effectiveness of convolutional or recurrent architectures that automatically share parameters across locations.  In a table, each column carries its own semantic meaning; swapping two columns changes the problem entirely.  Consequently, models must learn *which* columns matter from the training data itself.  When only a few labeled rows are present (the $k$‑shot regime), the risk of mistaking noise for signal grows dramatically.  

Third, **spurious correlations** become especially prevalent in low‑sample regimes.  With only $k$ labeled examples, any random coincidence between a column value and the target label can dominate the learning objective, leading to models that perform well on the training set but fail to generalize.  This phenomenon has been formalized as “overfitting to accidental patterns” in recent statistical learning theory work.  

Together, these properties mean that naïve application of standard supervised learners—logistic regression, gradient boosted trees, or neural networks—often yields unstable performance when $k$ is small.  Even sophisticated meta‑learning or self‑supervised pre‑training pipelines struggle because they cannot easily encode domain‐specific priors that would guide attention toward truly relevant columns.

---  

## Traditional Approaches  

Researchers have explored three broad families of solutions to these challenges: manual feature engineering, transfer learning / self‑supervision, and semi‑supervised augmentation.  

### Feature Engineering and Domain Knowledge  

Historically, practitioners relied on domain experts to craft informative transformations (e.g., binning age into life‑stage categories or computing body‑mass index from height and weight).  These engineered features embed prior knowledge that can dramatically reduce sample complexity: a well‑designed rule may already separate classes with a handful of examples.  However, manual engineering is labor‑intensive, scales poorly across many tasks, and requires experts who may not be available for every domain.  

### Transfer Learning and Self‑Supervision  

More recent work has attempted to learn generic representations from large collections of unlabeled tables.  Methods such as **TabPFN**, **TransTab**, and various contrastive or masked reconstruction objectives train deep models on thousands of heterogeneous datasets, hoping that the resulting embeddings capture useful cross‑column semantics (e.g., “numeric columns tend to correlate linearly”).  After pre‑training, these models can be fine‑tuned on a new task with few labels.  While promising, the heterogeneous nature of tabular data hampers universal augmentations: unlike images where rotations or color jitter preserve semantics, there is no single transformation that reliably respects column semantics across datasets.  Consequently, gains in very low‑shot regimes have been modest.  

### Semi‑Supervised Augmentation  

Another line of work leverages abundant unlabeled rows from the same distribution using techniques such as pseudo‑labeling or consistency regularization (e.g., VIME).  These methods assume that many more unlabeled samples are accessible than labeled ones—a condition often violated in high‑cost domains like healthcare where privacy restrictions limit data sharing.  Moreover, they still depend on the underlying supervised learner to discover which features are truly predictive; if spurious correlations dominate early training iterations, semi‑supervised signals may reinforce them rather than correct them.

---  

## The Rise of LLMs as Knowledge Bases  

Large language models—such as GPT‑3/3.5/4—are trained on massive text corpora spanning scientific literature, encyclopedic entries, news articles, and code repositories.  As a result, they internalize a broad spectrum of factual relationships (“older patients have higher risk of cardiovascular disease”) and procedural knowledge (“BMI = weight / height²”).  Two properties make LLMs attractive for few‑shot tabular learning:  

1. **Zero‑Shot Generalization** – By simply describing a task in natural language and providing a few exemplars (the essence of *in‑context learning*), an LLM can generate plausible predictions for inputs it has never seen during training.  
2. **Reasoning over Structured Descriptions** – When column names and brief textual definitions are supplied in a prompt, an LLM can use its world knowledge to infer causal links between attributes and targets (“smoking status is linked to lung disease”).  

Early attempts to exploit these abilities serialized tabular rows into natural‐language statements and fed them directly into an LLM at inference time (e.g., **TabLLM**, **TABLET**, **In‑Context Learning**).  Some approaches also fine‐tuned LLMs with parameter‐efficient adapters (LoRA, IA3) on a handful of labeled rows to specialize their internal representations for a specific table.

---  

## Limitations of Existing LLM‑Based Tabular Methods  

Despite their ingenuity, current LLM–driven pipelines face practical obstacles that hinder deployment in real applications.

### Per‑Sample Inference Cost  

Methods that query the LLM for every test instance incur significant latency and monetary cost because each call must pass through a large transformer model (often hosted behind an API).  For tables containing thousands of rows this becomes prohibitive.

### Dependence on Fine‑Tuning APIs  

Parameter-efficient fine‑tuning requires direct access to model weights; many state‑of‑the‑art LLMs are only reachable via closed inference APIs (e.g., GPT‑4).  Without the ability to modify internal parameters, approaches such as IA3 cannot be applied.

### Prompt Length Constraints  

LLMs have finite context windows (typically a few thousand tokens).  As the number of columns grows—common in datasets like *Communities* with >100 attributes—the serialized prompt quickly exceeds this limit, forcing either truncation (loss of information) or degradation in performance.

### Susceptibility to Spurious Correlations  

When the prompt contains raw rows together with labels but no explicit guidance about causality, the LLM may latch onto coincidental patterns present in the few demonstrations (e.g., “all positive examples happen to have missing values in column X”).  This mirrors the classic overfitting problem seen in conventional learners.

Collectively, these issues motivate a redesign: rather than using an LLM as an end–to–end predictor for each row, we should harness its reasoning power once—to *extract* knowledge—and then apply lightweight downstream models.

---  

## High‑Level Idea: Using LLMs as Feature Engineers  

The central insight behind **FeatLLM** is to treat the LLM not as a black box that consumes every test sample but as an *expert analyst* that reads a handful of labeled examples, reasons about the underlying decision criteria, and outputs human–readable rules (e.g., “Age > 65 AND BloodPressure ≥ 140 ⇒ class = HighRisk”).  

These rules can be programmatically parsed into binary indicator features—each indicating whether a given row satisfies a specific condition.  Because rules are derived from both prior world knowledge (encoded in the LLM) and the few supplied demonstrations, they encode meaningful relationships while discarding irrelevant columns early on.  Once these binary features are constructed for all rows:

1. A simple linear model with non‑negative weights learns how much each rule contributes to each class (the training step requires only a few gradient updates).  
2. At inference time no further calls to the LLM are needed; predictions amount to evaluating logical conditions followed by dot products—a negligible computational burden.  
3. To mitigate prompt size limits and increase robustness, FeatLLM repeats this process under different random subsets of columns (“feature bagging”) and ensembles the resulting linear classifiers—a strategy reminiscent of classic random forests but applied at the level of rule generation.

By shifting the costly reasoning step onto a single batch query per ensemble member—and by limiting each query’s prompt via bagging—FeatLLM achieves high predictive accuracy comparable to full LLM inference while retaining low latency and modest API usage.  The remainder of this textbook will dive into each component of this pipeline: prompt engineering for rule extraction, parsing strategies for turning natural language conditions into executable code, training the downstream linear estimator, and ensemble techniques that endow robustness against noisy or spurious rules.

In summary, FeatLLM demonstrates that large language models can serve effectively as *feature engineers*: they translate rich textual priors into compact symbolic representations that enable efficient downstream learning even when only a few labeled instances are available. This paradigm opens new avenues for applying powerful generative models to practical tabular problems across finance, healthcare, engineering, and beyond.

# Fundamentals of Large Language Models and In‑Context Learning  

Large language models (LLMs) have become the central engine behind many recent advances in natural‑language processing and, increasingly, in domains that are not purely textual.  The paper *Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few‑Shot Tabular Learning* demonstrates how the reasoning abilities of an LLM can be harnessed to generate high‑quality feature‑engineering rules for tabular data without ever updating the model’s parameters.  Understanding this capability requires a solid grasp of how LLMs are built, how they are prompted, and how “in‑context learning’’—the practice of teaching a model a new task by providing examples inside a single prompt—differs from conventional fine‑tuning.  The following sections lay out these foundations, discuss practical constraints such as prompt length and inference cost, and illustrate how an LLM can reason about structured data such as tables.

---

## Architecture and Pre‑training of LLMs  

At the core of every LLM lies a **transformer** architecture.  A transformer is a deep neural network whose primary building block is the self‑attention mechanism.  Each token in an input sequence is first embedded into a high‑dimensional vector; then, for every layer, the model computes attention scores that measure how much each token should attend to every other token.  By weighting and linearly combining the representations of all tokens, the model produces contextualized embeddings that capture syntactic and semantic relationships across the whole sequence.  Stacking many such layers (often dozens or even hundreds) yields a model capable of representing complex patterns in language.

Training proceeds in two stages:

1. **Self‑supervised pre‑training** – The model is exposed to massive corpora of text (books, web pages, code repositories, etc.) and learns to predict missing tokens.  Common objectives include **masked language modeling** (predicting randomly masked words) and **next‑token prediction** (generating the next word given all previous words).  Because the data are so diverse, the model acquires broad world knowledge: facts about diseases, finance, programming APIs, and countless other domains.

2. **Optional fine‑tuning or instruction tuning** – After pre‑training, a smaller amount of task‑specific data may be used to adapt the model to follow instructions more reliably (e.g., “answer with a short paragraph”).  Importantly for the present work, these additional steps are *not* required when we rely solely on in‑context learning: the pre‑trained model already contains enough knowledge to answer new tasks when given an appropriate prompt.

The end result is a parameterized function  
\[
\text{LLM}_{\theta}(\text{prompt})\; \mapsto\; \text{generated text},
\]  
where \(\theta\) denotes the (fixed) weights learned during pre‑training.  In all subsequent sections we treat \(\theta\) as immutable; every new prediction is produced only by changing the prompt.

---

## Prompting, Few‑Shot Examples, and Chain‑of‑Thought Prompting  

A **prompt** is simply a piece of text presented to the model before it generates any output.  Because LLMs are trained to continue natural language sequences, a carefully crafted prompt can steer them toward performing a desired computation.  The most powerful prompting strategies exploited in the paper are described below.

### Basic Prompt Structure  

A prompt typically contains three parts:

1. **Task description** – A concise statement that frames what we want the model to do (e.g., “Does this patient’s record indicate chronic heart failure? Yes or no.”).  Framing in natural language allows the LLM to activate its stored knowledge about medical risk factors.

2. **Feature definitions** – For each column of a table we provide its name, type (numeric, categorical, boolean), and optionally a short definition (“`age` is the patient’s age in years”).  Supplying this metadata helps the model avoid type mismatches when it later generates logical conditions such as “`age > 65`”.

3. **Few‑shot demonstrations** – A small number \(k\) of labeled examples are serialized into text using a fixed template:  
   \[
   \text{``}f_{1}\text{ is }x_{1}^{i}.\; \dots\; f_{d}\text{ is }x_{d}^{i}.\; \text{Answer: }y^{i}\text{''}.
   \]  
   These demonstrations act as concrete illustrations of how raw feature values map to labels.  Because only a handful of examples are needed (the paper experiments with as few as four), this approach is called *few‑shot* prompting.

### Chain‑of‑Thought Prompting  

The paper demonstrates that raw few‑shot examples often leave the LLM uncertain about which features truly matter.  To encourage deeper reasoning, we prepend an explicit instruction that mimics “chain‑of‑thought’’ prompting: ask the model to first reason about **causal relationships** between features and the target before attempting to write any rules.  Concretely, the instruction splits reasoning into two steps:

1. **Step 1 – Prior knowledge extraction** – The model lists plausible feature–target relations based solely on its world knowledge (“Older patients tend to have higher heart failure risk”).  
2. **Step 2 – Rule synthesis using demonstrations** – The model combines Step 1 insights with the concrete few‑shot examples to produce *rules* for each class (e.g., “If `age > 70` AND `blood_pressure > 140` then answer *Yes*”).

By structuring reasoning this way we reduce reliance on spurious correlations that may appear in tiny training sets and instead let the LLM leverage its extensive pre‑trained knowledge.

### Fixed Number of Rules  

To keep downstream processing tractable, each prompt asks for a predetermined number of rules per class (the paper adopts ten).  This prevents degenerate outputs—either too few rules that underfit or an explosion of rules that would overwhelm both parsing and downstream models.

---

## In‑Context Learning vs. Fine‑Tuning  

Both approaches aim to adapt an LLM to a new task but differ dramatically in resources required and operational constraints.

| Aspect | In‑Context Learning | Fine‑Tuning |
|--------|---------------------|--------------|
| Parameter updates | None; \(\theta\) stays fixed | Model weights \(\theta\) are updated via gradient descent |
| Data requirement | Only a handful of examples placed inside the prompt | Typically thousands of labeled examples |
| Compute cost at inference | Single forward pass per prompt (or per ensemble trial) | One forward pass after fine-tuning; training may be expensive |
| Deployment flexibility | Works with any commercial API that exposes generation (no access to internal weights) | Requires ability to write back updated weights—a limitation for closed APIs like GPT‑4 |
| Prompt length limitation | Must fit all demonstrations + instructions within context window; can become problematic with many features | Not constrained by prompt length after fine-tuning |

The **FeatLLM** framework explicitly chooses *in‑context learning* because it sidesteps two practical barriers:

1. **API‐only access:** Many state–of–the–art chat models are only available through inference APIs; they cannot be fine‑tuned by end users.
2. **Prompt size management:** By delegating rule generation to a few high-level prompts and then using bagging (randomly selecting subsets of columns), FeatLLM stays within context windows even for tables with many hundreds of attributes.

Thus FeatLLM illustrates how powerful reasoning can be extracted from an immutable LLM purely through clever prompting.

---

## APIs and Practical Constraints (Prompt Length, Inference Cost)  

When deploying an LLM via a cloud provider (e.g., OpenAI’s `gpt-3.5-turbo-0613` used in the paper), practitioners must respect two hard limits:

1. **Maximum context size** – Most chat models accept at most several thousand tokens per request (the exact number varies).  Every token used for feature names, descriptions, demonstrations, and chain-of-thought instructions consumes part of this budget.  If a table has hundreds of columns, naïvely serializing all columns would overflow the limit.

   *Mitigation strategy:* FeatLLM applies **feature bagging**, randomly selecting a manageable subset of columns for each ensemble trial.  Because each trial only needs enough columns to generate useful rules, multiple trials collectively cover all features without any single prompt exceeding the token budget.

2. **Inference latency and cost** – Each call to an LLM API incurs monetary cost proportional to generated tokens and incurs network latency.  Performing one inference per test sample would be prohibitive for large datasets.

   *Mitigation strategy:* FeatLLM extracts **static rules once**, parses them into executable Python code via another short LLM call (the parsing stage), and thereafter relies on a lightweight linear model with non‑negative weights for actual predictions.  Consequently only ~30 API calls are required regardless of how many test instances exist—a dramatic reduction compared with end-to-end approaches that query per sample.

The temperature parameter (set at 0.5 in experiments) controls randomness during rule generation; higher temperatures encourage diversity across ensemble members while still yielding coherent outputs.

---

## Examples of LLM Reasoning Over Structured Data  

To solidify intuition about how an LLM can treat tabular information as “structured prose,” consider two illustrative scenarios drawn from the paper’s experiments.

### Medical Diagnosis Example  

*Task:* Predict whether a patient has chronic heart failure.  
*Features:* `age` (numeric), `blood_pressure` (numeric), `cholesterol` (numeric), `smoker` (boolean), …  

**Prompt excerpt**

```
Task: Does this patient's record indicate chronic heart failure? Yes or no.
Feature descriptions:
- age: patient's age in years.
- blood_pressure: systolic blood pressure measurement.
- cholesterol: total cholesterol level.
- smoker: whether patient currently smokes (True/False).
...
Demonstrations:
age is 68. blood_pressure is 150. cholesterol is 210. smoker is True.
Answer: Yes
age is 45. blood_pressure is 120. cholesterol is 180. smoker is False.
Answer: No
...
Now reason step-by-step:
Step 1: Based on medical knowledge ... 
Step 2: Using these examples generate ten rules for class Yes.
```

The LLM replies with rules such as

```
Rule 1: age > 65 AND blood_pressure > 140
Rule 2: cholesterol > 200 AND smoker = True
...
```

These textual conditions directly encode domain expertise (“older patients with high blood pressure are at risk”).

### Synthetic Sequence Classification Example  

*Task:* Classify an integer sequence as arithmetic or geometric etc.  
*Features:* `first_term`, `second_term`, `third_term`, `fourth_term`.  

Because each feature has an explicit numeric meaning, Step 1 may invoke mathematical definitions (“In an arithmetic progression consecutive differences are equal”), while Step 2 extracts concrete thresholds from the few illustrated sequences (“If second_term - first_term = third_term - second_term then class = arithmetic”).

After rule generation, FeatLLM automatically converts each rule into Python functions (`def rule_i(seq): return seq[1]-seq[0] == seq[2]-seq[1]`) using another brief LLM query that parses natural language into code even when minor syntactic variations appear (“greater than” vs “>”).  

These examples demonstrate that with appropriate prompting—task framing, descriptive metadata, few-shot illustrations, and explicit chain-of-thought instructions—an LLM can effectively reason over structured tabular inputs despite being originally trained on free text.

---

### Summary  

This chapter has introduced the essential building blocks required to understand why large language models can serve as powerful feature engineers for tabular data:

* Their transformer backbone coupled with massive self-supervised pre-training endows them with broad factual knowledge.
* Prompt engineering—including task description, feature metadata, few-shot demonstrations, and chain-of-thought reasoning—allows us to coax that knowledge into concrete logical rules.
* In‑context learning leverages these prompts without ever modifying model parameters, making it compatible with closed‐source APIs and dramatically reducing computational cost.
* Practical constraints such as limited context windows and inference pricing motivate techniques like feature bagging and rule parsing into downstream linear models.
* Concrete examples illustrate how an immutable LLM can transform raw table rows into human‐readable conditions that subsequently become binary features usable by simple classifiers.

Armed with these concepts you will be ready to explore how **FeatLLM** builds upon them to achieve state-of-the-art few‑shot performance on diverse real-world tabular tasks while keeping inference inexpensive and interpretable.

**Problem Formulation for Few‑Shot Tabular Classification**

In this chapter we formalize the learning problem that underlies the entire FeatLLM framework.  By establishing precise notation for the data, the associated natural‑language descriptions, and the strict low‑shot constraints, we obtain a clear target for subsequent algorithmic development.  Throughout we keep the exposition deliberately general so that it applies to any tabular classification task, regardless of the domain (finance, medicine, synthetic benchmarks, etc.).  The key idea is that only a tiny number of labeled instances are available for training, yet the model may still leverage rich auxiliary information such as feature names and textual definitions.

---

### Notation: dataset 𝒟, feature set F, and class set C  

Consider a tabular data collection consisting of $N$ rows (samples) and $d$ columns (features).  Each row $i\in\{1,\dots ,N\}$ is represented by a vector  

\[
\mathbf{x}^{\,i}= \bigl(x^{\,i}_{1},x^{\,i}_{2},\dots ,x^{\,i}_{d}\bigr)^{\!\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
\]

where $x^{\,i}_{j}$ denotes the observed value of feature $j$ for sample $i$.  In practice many features are categorical or ordinal; nevertheless we treat all entries abstractly as elements of suitable domains (numeric, string, or enumeration).  

The complete labeled dataset is written as  

\[
\mathcal{D}= \bigl\{(\mathbf{x}^{\,i},\; y^{\,i})\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]

where $y^{\,i}\in C$ is the class label attached to sample $i$, and $C=\{c_{1},c_{2},\dots ,c_{|C|}\}$ denotes the finite set of possible classes (binary classification corresponds to $|C|=2$).  

Each column comes with a human‑readable identifier—its **feature name**—and optionally a textual definition or description that clarifies its semantics.  Let  

\[
F=\bigl\{f_{1},f_{2},\dots ,f_{d}\bigr\}
\]

be the ordered list of these feature names.  For every $j$, we also have an optional natural‑language description  

\[
\mathrm{desc}(f_{j}) = \text{“{$f_{j}$} measures … ”},
\]

which may encode type information (e.g., “integer age in years”) or domain knowledge (e.g., “smoking status: {yes,no}”).  The presence of these descriptions is crucial for large language models (LLMs) to reason about the data without seeing many examples.

---

### k‑shot sampling and assumptions about feature descriptions  

Few‑shot learning assumes that only a small subset of $\mathcal{D}$ is available for supervised training.  Formally, let $k \ll N$ denote the **shot budget**.  We randomly draw without replacement a set  

\[
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} = \bigl\{(\mathbf{x}^{\,i},y^{\,i}) \mid i \in S_{\text{train}}\bigr\},
\qquad |S_{\text{train}}| = k,
\]

and treat all remaining rows as either validation or test data, depending on experimental protocol.  The randomness is stratified whenever possible so that each class in $C$ appears at least once in $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$; this guarantees that the learner has a minimal basis for discriminating among classes.

Two additional assumptions are made about the auxiliary information:

1. **Availability of feature names** – every column $j$ has an unambiguous textual identifier $f_{j}$ that can be inserted verbatim into prompts presented to an LLM.
2. **Optional but useful descriptions** – while not strictly required for correctness, providing $\mathrm{desc}(f_{j})$ significantly improves the LLM’s ability to generate meaningful rules because it supplies type constraints and domain context.

These assumptions reflect the reality of most curated tabular datasets: column headings are present by definition, and documentation often accompanies them.

---

### Goal: learn a predictor using only *k* labeled instances  

Given only $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$, the objective is to construct a **predictor**  

\[
h : \mathbb{R}^{d} \longrightarrow \Delta^{|C|-1},
\]

where $\Delta^{|C|-1}$ denotes the probability simplex over $|C|$ classes (i.e., $h(\mathbf{x})$ yields a valid probability distribution across $C$).  In FeatLLM this predictor consists of two stages:

1. **Feature engineering via an LLM** – from the few examples and from $F$ (plus any $\mathrm{desc}(\cdot)$), an LLM generates a set of logical conditions (“rules”) that characterize each class.  Each rule $r$ can be evaluated on any sample to produce a binary outcome $\mathbb{I}\{\mathbf{x}\models r\}\in\{0,1\}$.  
2. **Linear aggregation** – a low‑complexity model (a non‑negative linear classifier without bias) learns weights for these binary rule‑features using only $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$.  The learned weights capture how informative each rule is for predicting each class.

Thus, despite having access to just $k$ labeled points, the system leverages **prior knowledge** embedded in the LLM and the explicit feature semantics to produce a usable classifier.

---

### Evaluation metrics in low‑shot regimes  

Because performance can fluctuate dramatically when only a handful of labels are available, robust evaluation requires metrics that are insensitive to class imbalance and that capture ranking quality rather than raw accuracy alone.

- **Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC)** – For binary tasks ($|C|=2$) we compute the ROC curve by varying a decision threshold on $h(\mathbf{x})_{c_{+}}$, where $c_{+}$ denotes the positive class.  The AUC measures the probability that a randomly chosen positive example receives a higher score than a randomly chosen negative example.  In few‑shot settings AUC is preferred because it does not depend on selecting a particular threshold.

- **Macro‑averaged AUC** – When $|C|>2$, we compute an AUC for each one‑vs‑rest binary problem and then average across classes.  This yields a metric that treats all classes equally regardless of their frequency.

- **Accuracy** – Defined as $\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}|}\sum_{(\mathbf{x},y)\in \mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}}\mathbb{I}\bigl[\arg\max_{c} h(\mathbf{x})_{c}=y\bigr]$.  Accuracy is reported mainly for completeness; however, because few shots often lead to noisy decision boundaries, accuracy can be unstable.

- **Standard deviation over repetitions** – Since random selection of $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ influences results heavily, experiments are repeated multiple times (e.g., three seeds) and both mean and standard deviation of each metric are reported.  This practice guards against over‑interpreting any single lucky split.

All reported numbers in subsequent chapters adhere to these evaluation conventions, ensuring fair comparison among FeatLLM, baseline tabular learners, and other LLM‑based approaches.

--- 

By grounding our discussion in this precise problem formulation—clearly defined notation, strict few‑shot sampling, reliance on feature descriptions, and standard evaluation—we set the stage for understanding how FeatLLM transforms raw tabular data into high‑quality binary rule features and why this approach can outperform existing methods even with only a handful of labeled examples.

**Designing Prompts to Extract Decision Rules**

In this chapter we dissect the prompt engineering strategy that enables a large language model (LLM) to act as a feature engineer for few‑shot tabular learning.  The central idea of the *FeatLLM* framework is to ask the LLM to articulate, in natural language, the logical conditions that govern each class label.  Once these conditions—here called **rules**—are obtained, they are parsed into binary features that a simple downstream model can consume.  The quality of the extracted rules hinges on how the prompt is crafted.  Empirical work shows that a three‑part prompt structure—*basic information block*, *reasoning instruction block*, and *response instruction block*—produces reliable, parsable rule sets while encouraging the model to draw upon its prior knowledge rather than spurious patterns present in a tiny training set.

Below we explain each component in detail, why it matters, and how additional engineering choices such as temperature tuning and demo ordering further improve robustness.

---

### Basic Information Block: Task Description, Feature Names/Types, and Few‑Shot Examples

The first segment of the prompt supplies the LLM with all the factual context it needs before any reasoning begins.  It consists of three pieces of information.

1. **Task Description**  
   The model is told exactly what prediction is required, cast as a direct question.  For a medical dataset this might read:

   > “*Does this patient’s data indicate chronic heart failure?*”

   Framing the problem as a yes/no (or multi‑class) query aligns the LLM’s output format with the downstream classification objective and activates its knowledge about the domain (e.g., risk factors for heart failure).

2. **Feature Names, Types, and Optional Definitions**  
   Each column in the table is introduced by its natural‑language name $f_j$ together with its datatype:

   - *Numerical*: “*Age (years) – numerical*”
   - *Categorical*: “*Smoking status – categorical; values: {Never, Former, Current}*”

   When a definition is available (e.g., “*Blood pressure: systolic measurement in mm Hg*”), it is inserted after the type.  Explicitly stating types serves two purposes.  
   First, it prevents the LLM from generating syntactically invalid comparisons such as “*Age < ‘Never’*”.  Second, it guides later parsing because the rule extractor can expect numeric comparisons for numeric features and equality checks for categorical ones.

3. **Few‑Shot Demonstrations**  
   A small set of labeled examples—typically $k$ samples when performing $k$‑shot learning—is serialized into plain text using a consistent template:

   \[
   \text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i,F)=
   \text{``}f_1\ \text{is}\ x^i_1.\dots f_d\ \text{is}\ x^i_d.\ \text{Answer:}\ y^i\text{''}
   \]

   Each demonstration therefore reads like a short paragraph that enumerates every feature value followed by its correct label.  By placing these demonstrations after the task description but before any reasoning instructions, we give the LLM concrete evidence of how feature values map to outcomes without yet telling it how to derive rules.

The combination of an explicit problem statement, clear feature ontology, and concrete examples positions the LLM to treat the subsequent reasoning steps as an *explanation* rather than blind memorization.

---

### Reasoning Instruction Block: Two‑Step Chain‑of‑Thought (Knowledge → Rule Derivation)

Once the factual scaffolding is established, we guide the model through a disciplined reasoning process that mirrors how a human expert would approach tabular classification.

#### Step 1 – Infer Causal Tendencies from Prior Knowledge  
The model is prompted to reason *without* looking at the few‑shot examples.  An introductory sentence akin to chain‑of‑thought prompting (“*Let us think step by step.*”) signals that an internal monologue is expected.  The instruction then asks:

> “Based on your world knowledge and common sense, list which features are likely relevant to determining whether the answer is *Yes* versus *No*, and describe qualitatively how they influence the outcome.”

Because no concrete data points are referenced, the LLM must draw on its pre‑training corpus (medical textbooks, epidemiology reports, etc.).  This step serves two critical functions:

- **Prior Knowledge Extraction** – The model surfaces domain‐relevant variables (e.g., age, hypertension) before being swayed by potentially noisy correlations in the few‑shot sample.
- **Feature Filtering** – By explicitly requesting a relevance list, we discourage attention to irrelevant columns (e.g., an identifier field) that could otherwise be introduced into rule generation.

#### Step 2 – Derive Explicit Rules Using Demonstrations  
Armed with a tentative relevance set from Step 1, the model now incorporates the few‑shot examples.  The prompt asks:

> “Using the examples above and your earlier relevance list, propose $R$ concise decision rules for each class (*Yes* and *No*) that would correctly classify all demonstrated instances.”

We fix $R$ (the number of rules per class) to a modest value such as $10$.  This bound prevents an explosion of overly specific rules that would overfit the tiny demonstration set while still allowing enough expressive power to capture distinct decision regions.

The two‑step chain forces separation between *knowledge‑driven inference* and *data‑driven refinement*.  Empirically this reduces spurious rule generation caused by overreliance on idiosyncratic patterns in few examples.

---

### Response Instruction Block: Formatting Rules for Reliable Parsing

After reasoning, we must extract machine‐readable predicates from natural language text.  To make this extraction deterministic we prescribe a strict output schema.

1. **Class Header** – Each class begins with a line like `Class Yes:` or `Class No:`.
2. **Rule List** – Under each header we provide exactly $R$ numbered items:
   
   ```
   1) Age > 60 AND Smoking status = Current
   2) BloodPressure >= 140
   ...
   ```

3. **Operator Vocabulary** – We enumerate permissible relational operators (`=`, `!=`, `>`, `<`, `>=`, `<=`) and logical connectors (`AND`, `OR`).  The LLM is reminded not to use synonyms (“greater than”) because those increase parsing complexity.
4. **Feature Formatting** – Feature names must match those given earlier (case‑sensitive).  For categorical variables we require explicit equality checks against listed categories; for numerical variables we require a single comparison operator followed by a numeric constant.

A short example of a valid response might be:

```
Class Yes:
1) Age > 55 AND Cholesterol > 240
2) Diabetes = Yes
...
Class No:
1) Age <= 55 AND BloodPressure < 130
...
```

Because every rule follows an identical syntactic pattern, downstream code can reliably split on line breaks and parse each predicate into Python boolean expressions or symbolic representations.

---

### Fixed Number of Rules per Class and Handling of Feature Types

Choosing a constant $R$ simplifies both prompt length budgeting and downstream dimensionality.  In practice $R=10$ yields a good tradeoff: long enough to capture multiple facets of a class decision boundary yet short enough that concatenating all rules across classes fits comfortably within typical LLM context windows (often ≤ 4 K tokens).  

When forming rules we respect feature types explicitly:

- **Numerical Features** – The rule generator must produce an inequality involving a single threshold; multivariate linear combinations are discouraged because they would require more elaborate parsing.
- **Categorical Features** – Equality or inequality checks against predefined categories are mandated.
- **Boolean or Binary Features** – Simple presence (`Feature = True`) suffices.

If during generation an illegal type mismatch occurs (e.g., comparing a string category with “>”), downstream validation flags it and discards or corrects the rule—although such mismatches are rare when type information is clearly presented in the basic block.

---

### Prompt Engineering Tricks: Temperature, Order Randomization, and Bagging

Even with an ideal template, stochasticity in LLM decoding can be harnessed to improve robustness.

- **Temperature Settings** – A moderate temperature (e.g., $T=0.5$) encourages diverse but still coherent rule sets across repeated queries. Higher temperatures increase variability at the cost of occasional incoherence; lower temperatures produce near‑deterministic outputs but reduce ensemble diversity.
  
- **Demo Order Randomization** – The ordering of few‑shot examples subtly influences LLM attention patterns. By shuffling this order before each prompt we encourage different subsets of examples to dominate rule synthesis, which again contributes diversity among ensemble members.

- **Bagging Over Features/Instances** – For datasets with many columns exceeding token limits, each prompt can randomly select a subset of features (feature bagging) or a subset of demonstrations (instance bagging). Different bags generate distinct rule collections; averaging predictions over many bags mitigates any single bag’s myopic view.

These tricks collectively give rise to an ensemble of lightweight linear models (each trained on binary rule features).  Because each member was built from an independently generated rule set, their errors tend to cancel out—a phenomenon akin to classical ensemble learning theory where variance reduction improves generalization even under severe data scarcity.

---

### Summary

Designing prompts for rule extraction in *FeatLLM* involves meticulously supplying factual context, coaxing structured reasoning via a two‑step chain‑of‑thought process, and demanding machine‑parsable output through rigid formatting guidelines.  By fixing the number of rules per class and respecting feature type annotations we keep prompts concise enough for real LLMs while ensuring generated predicates are meaningful.  Finally, purposeful use of temperature control, demonstration order randomization, and bagging introduces beneficial diversity that fuels robust ensembling without inflating inference cost.

Through this three‑part prompt architecture, large language models are transformed from opaque end‑to‑end predictors into powerful knowledge‑driven feature engineers whose output seamlessly integrates with simple downstream classifiers—exactly what makes *FeatLLM* effective for few‑shot tabular learning.

**Parsing Natural‑Language Rules into Binary Features**

In this chapter we explain in detail how the natural‑language rules that a large language model (LLM) produces are transformed into a set of binary indicator features that can be fed to a simple downstream classifier.  The process is central to the **FeatLLM** framework: once the rules have been extracted they become the only “learned” representation of the data, and every downstream prediction reduces to evaluating whether a given sample satisfies each rule.  We therefore devote several paragraphs to each component of this pipeline, emphasizing the intuition behind design choices, the practical difficulties that arise from noisy LLM output, and the concrete steps—prompt design, code generation, safe execution, and matrix construction—that yield a clean binary feature matrix \(\mathbf{Z}_k\) for each class \(k\).

---

### 1. Challenges of Noisy LLM Output

When an LLM is asked to produce logical conditions in natural language, the resulting text is rarely perfectly formatted.  Even when the prompt explicitly requests a canonical syntax (e.g., “`age > 45`”), the model may substitute symbols for words (“greater than”, “less than”) or restructure the expression (“if age is above 45”).  Moreover, the model can insert or omit punctuation, change variable names, or combine several elementary clauses with logical connectives such as **AND** or **OR** in unpredictable ways.  

These variations matter because the subsequent parsing stage must turn each textual rule into an executable Boolean test.  A naïve regular‑expression parser that expects only “`feature OP value`” quickly fails when it encounters a phrase like “*the patient’s blood pressure is higher than 130*”.  The failure mode is two‑fold:

1. **Syntactic mismatches** – different spellings of operators (`>`, `greater than`, `more than`), ambiguous ordering (`value < feature`), or missing delimiters.
2. **Semantic ambiguity** – where a rule mixes multiple conditions (e.g., “age > 50 and cholesterol ≤ 200”) without clear grouping, making it difficult to decide how many binary features to create.

If these problems are left unresolved, the generated feature matrix would contain many missing or incorrect entries, degrading downstream performance and undermining one of FeatLLM’s selling points: reliable inference without per‑sample LLM calls.

---

### 2. Using the LLM Itself as a Parser – Code‑Generation Prompt

A key insight of FeatLLM is that the same LLM that generated the rules can also be asked to translate them into syntactically correct code.  LLMs trained on large code corpora (e.g., GitHub) have demonstrated strong ability to understand informal mathematical language and produce precise Python expressions.  By delegating parsing to the model itself we avoid hand‑crafting brittle regex pipelines and instead rely on the model’s semantic understanding.

The parsing prompt follows a simple template:

```
You are given a list of rule strings describing conditions for class k.
Convert each rule into a Python function `def rule_i(row):` that returns True
iff the row satisfies the rule. Use only built‑in Python operators.
Return all functions in one code block.
```

Crucially we also provide **context**:

- The exact names of all features (`f_1`, `f_2`, …) and their data types (numeric, categorical, boolean).  
- A short example mapping: `"age > 30"` → `"row['age'] > 30"`.  
- Instructions on handling logical operators: "`AND` should become `and`, `OR` should become `or`".  

Because we constrain the output format (a single Python code block delimited by triple backticks), we can reliably extract the generated code from the LLM’s response.  The model’s internal grammar checking naturally normalizes operator synonyms: “greater than” becomes `>`, “less than or equal to” becomes `<=`, etc.

---

### 3. Executing Generated Python Code Safely with `exec()`

Once we have obtained a string containing valid Python function definitions, we must turn it into callable objects that operate on each data row.  The straightforward way is to use Python’s built‑in `exec()` function:

```python
namespace = {}
exec(generated_code, {}, namespace)
rule_funcs = [namespace[f"rule_{i}"] for i in range(num_rules)]
```

However, executing arbitrary code returned by an external service raises safety concerns.  FeatLLM mitigates these risks in three layers:

1. **Restricted globals** – We pass an empty dictionary for both global and local namespaces to `exec()`.  Consequently, the generated code cannot import modules, access files, or invoke network calls; it can only use basic arithmetic and Boolean operators that are always available.

2. **Static analysis before execution** – A lightweight check scans `generated_code` for any disallowed keywords (`import`, `open`, `eval`, `exec`) and rejects any snippet containing them.

3. **Timeout enforcement** – Each rule function is wrapped by a tiny helper that limits execution time (e.g., using `signal.alarm` on Unix).  Since every rule is simply a Boolean expression over a row’s fields, evaluation completes in microseconds; any function exceeding a millisecond is flagged as malformed and discarded.

Through these safeguards we obtain a reliable list `rule_funcs` where each element maps a single row (represented as a dict `{feature_name: value}`) to either `True` (the rule holds) or `False`.

---

### 4. Constructing the Binary Matrix \(\mathbf{Z}_k\) for Class \(k\)

With callable rule functions in hand, translating an entire dataset \(\{ \mathbf{x}^{i} \}_{i=1}^{N}\) into binary features becomes an embarrassingly parallel operation.  For each class \(k\), FeatLLM extracts \(R\) rules (the paper fixes \(R=10\)).  We denote by

\[
\mathbf{z}^{i}_{k} = \bigl[\, r^{i}_{k,1},\, r^{i}_{k,2},\, \dots,\,
r^{i}_{k,R} \,\bigr]^\top ,\qquad
r^{i}_{k,j} \in \{0,1\},
\]

the vector of indicator values for sample \(i\).  Here \(r^{i}_{k,j}=1\) iff `rule_j(row_i)` returns `True`.  

Collecting these vectors across all \(N\) samples yields the binary matrix

\[
\mathbf{Z}_k = 
\begin{bmatrix}
--- & \mathbf{z}^{1}_{k} & ---\\
--- & \mathbf{z}^{2}_{k} & ---\\
&& \vdots\\
--- & \mathbf{z}^{N}_{k} & ---
\end{bmatrix}
\in \{0,1\}^{N\times R}.
\]

In implementation this amounts to looping over rows (`for row in dataset:`), applying each compiled rule function (`[int(f(row)) for f in rule_funcs]`), and storing the resulting list as one row of \(\mathbf{Z}_k\).  Because each rule evaluation is independent, vectorized libraries (Numba or JAX) can accelerate this step dramatically even for tens of thousands of rows.

The downstream linear classifier then uses \(\mathbf{Z}_k\) as its input feature matrix; see Equation (2) in the paper for how logits are computed from non‑negative weights applied to \(\mathbf{Z}_k\).

---

### 5. Handling Logical Operators (AND/OR) in Rules

The original natural‑language rules may involve multiple elementary conditions combined with logical connectors.  For instance:

> “Age > 50 **and** cholesterol ≤ 200”.

During code generation we instruct the LLM to preserve these connectors using Python’s boolean operators:

- `"AND"` → `"and"`.
- `"OR"` → `"or"`.

Thus the corresponding function becomes:

```python
def rule_0(row):
    return (row["Age"] > 50) and (row["cholesterol"] <= 200)
```

When a rule contains both AND and OR without explicit parentheses, Python follows its standard precedence (`and` binds tighter than `or`).  To avoid unintended precedence errors we ask the LLM to wrap each atomic condition in parentheses; this yields unambiguous expressions regardless of operator mixing.

In some cases a rule may be expressed as a disjunction of several conjunctions (e.g., “(A > 5 AND B = ‘yes’) OR C ≤ 10”).  The generated function will faithfully mirror this structure:

```python
def rule_3(row):
    return ((row["A"] > 5) and (row["B"] == "yes")) or (row["C"] <= 10)
```

When converting such compound expressions into binary features we retain them as *single* indicators: if any part of the disjunction holds, `rule_3(row)` evaluates to `True`, yielding a value of 1 in \(\mathbf{Z}_k\).  This design choice respects the original logical semantics: each rule encodes *one* decision criterion regardless of internal complexity.

If a user wishes instead to decompose complex rules into multiple primitive binary features (e.g., treat each conjunctive clause separately), this could be done by post‑processing after code generation—splitting on top‑level ORs—but FeatLLM’s default pipeline treats every full rule as one feature because empirical results show that preserving logical grouping often improves signal‑to‑noise ratio when training with very few labeled examples.

---

### Summary

Parsing natural‑language rules into executable binary features involves overcoming noisy textual output, leveraging the LLM itself for robust code generation, safely executing that code via restricted `exec()`, and finally assembling per‑class binary matrices \(\mathbf{Z}_k\).  By handling logical operators explicitly during code generation and preserving them in Python Boolean expressions, FeatLLM transforms high‑level expert knowledge into low‑dimensional yet highly informative representations that power its efficient linear classifier.  This pipeline eliminates per‑sample LLM calls at inference time while still reaping the benefits of massive prior knowledge encoded in large language models.

Learning from Rule‑Based Features: Linear Model with Non‑Negative Weights
================================================================================

In the **FeatLLM** framework the heavy‑weight reasoning of a large language model is confined to a single, offline stage: the generation of human‑readable rules that explain how each class can be identified from the original tabular attributes.  
Once these rules have been extracted, they are converted into binary indicator features – one per rule – and the downstream prediction problem becomes a classic supervised learning task on a very low‑dimensional, interpretable feature space.  
The purpose of this chapter is to describe, in depth, the simple linear model that consumes these binary rule‑based features, how it maps them to class logits, and how it is trained using only the few labeled examples available in a *k‑shot* setting.

Formulation of Logits as \( \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{z} \) with ReLU Projection
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

For a given training (or test) example we denote by  

\[
\mathbf{z}_k \in \{0,1\}^{R}
\]

the binary vector that records whether the example satisfies each of the \(R\) rules that were generated for class \(k\).  
All classes share the same number of rules; therefore for a problem with \(c\) classes we have \(c\) such vectors \(\mathbf{z}_1,\dots,\mathbf{z}_c\).

The downstream model is deliberately kept linear:

\[
\text{logit}_k = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,\,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k ,
\qquad k=1,\dots,c .
\]

Here  

* \(\mathbf{w}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{R}\) are trainable parameters – one weight for each rule of class \(k\).  
* The operator \(\max(\cdot,0)\) (element‑wise ReLU) projects every weight onto the non‑negative orthant before it participates in the dot product.  

The non‑negative projection has an intuitive interpretation.  Because each rule is meant to **support** its associated class (the rule was generated precisely because its satisfaction is indicative of that class), giving a negative contribution would contradict that intent and could force the model to “unlearn’’ information already supplied by the LLM.  By restricting the weights to be non‑negative we ensure that every rule can only increase – or leave unchanged – the logit for its own class, which aligns with the philosophical goal of treating the LLM’s output as trustworthy prior knowledge.

Softmax Over Classes and Cross‑Entropy Loss
-------------------------------------------

The logits for all classes are assembled into a vector  

\[
\boldsymbol{\ell}= (\text{logit}_1,\dots,\text{logit}_c) .
\]

To obtain a proper probability distribution over classes we apply the softmax function:

\[
p_k = \frac{\exp(\text{logit}_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^{c}\exp(\text{logit}_j)},
\qquad
\mathbf{p}= \operatorname{Softmax}(\boldsymbol{\ell}) .
\]

Given a ground‑truth one‑hot label vector \(\mathbf{y}\in\{0,1\}^{c}\) for an example, the standard cross‑entropy loss is used:

\[
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{y}) = -\, \sum_{k=1}^{c} y_k \log p_k .
\]

When training on the few available labeled samples (the *k‑shot* set), this loss encourages the linear model to assign larger positive weights to those rules that are truly discriminative for their class, while diminishing or zeroing out weights attached to rules that happen to fire on many examples but do not help separate classes.

Why a Linear Model Is Sufficient After Rule Extraction
-----------------------------------------------------

One might wonder why we do not employ a more expressive learner (e.g., gradient boosted trees or deep neural networks) after obtaining the rule‑derived features.  The justification lies in two complementary observations:

1. **Rule quality** – The rules generated by FeatLLM are themselves concise logical conditions (e.g., “age > 65 AND cholesterol > 200”).  Each rule captures a high‑level decision boundary that already separates classes effectively.  By construction they are *interpretable* logical predicates; therefore their binary activations already encode most of the information needed for classification.

2. **Low dimensionality** – The number of rules per class is fixed (typically ten), so even with ten classes we deal with at most a few hundred binary features.  In such a regime a linear combination suffices to model interactions among rules without overfitting, especially when only a handful of labeled examples are available.  Adding non‑linear capacity would risk memorizing noise rather than exploiting the strong priors supplied by the LLM.

Consequently, a simple linear model with non‑negative weights provides an elegant balance: it respects the semantics of rule activation, maintains interpretability (the weight magnitude directly reflects rule importance), and works robustly in low‑shot regimes where more complex models would struggle.

Training Details: Optimizer, Epochs, Early Stopping
---------------------------------------------------

Training proceeds on the *k*-shot dataset only:

1. **Optimizer** – The Adam optimizer is employed because of its adaptive learning rates and proven stability on small datasets.  The learning rate is set to \(0.01\).

2. **Epochs** – A relatively high upper bound (e.g., 200 epochs) is allowed so that convergence can be reached even when gradients are noisy due to limited data.

3. **Early stopping / epoch selection** – To avoid overfitting we use *k*-fold cross‑validation on the *k* training points (the folds are chosen so each fold still contains at least one example from each class).  For each fold we monitor validation loss and retain the epoch yielding the best average validation performance across folds.  This selected epoch number is then used to train a final model on all *k* examples.

4. **Batching** – Because the dataset is tiny, we typically use batch gradient descent (i.e., batch size = *k*); this yields deterministic updates and simplifies implementation.

5. **Regularization** – No explicit L2 regularization is required; however, because weights are forced non‑negative and many may stay at zero (if their associated rule never fires), an implicit sparsity emerges naturally.

Overall Procedure
-----------------

Putting everything together, each FeatLLM trial follows these steps:

* Generate \(R\) rules per class using an LLM prompt.
* Parse those rules into executable code that produces binary vectors \(\mathbf{z}_k\).
* Assemble all binary features for every training sample.
* Initialise weights \(\mathbf{w}_k = \mathbf{0}\).
* Optimize the cross‑entropy loss using Adam with learning rate \(0.01\), monitoring validation loss via *k*-fold CV to pick an early‑stopping epoch.
* After training, compute class probabilities for any new sample simply by evaluating its binary rule vector and applying Eq. (1)–(2).

Because inference now involves only a dot product and a softmax operation per trial, it is extremely fast—comparable to classic logistic regression—while retaining all of the expressive power encoded in the LLM‑generated rules.

The remainder of FeatLLM builds on this solid foundation by repeating the whole pipeline several times with different random seeds, feature bagging, and temperature settings for the LLM; predictions from all trials are finally averaged (ensembling).  Nevertheless, at its core lies precisely this linear model with non‑negative weights—an elegant bridge between sophisticated language reasoning and efficient tabular prediction.

# Ensemble via Bagging and Self‑Consistency

In the context of few‑shot tabular learning with large language models (LLMs), the **FeatLLM** framework treats the LLM not as a direct predictor but as a feature engineer.  A single pass of the LLM—prompted to generate a set of decision rules—produces a collection of binary features that can be fed to an extremely simple downstream model (a non‑negative linear classifier).  While this approach already offers a substantial reduction in inference cost compared with end‑to‑end LLM prediction, it remains vulnerable to two practical concerns:

1. **Prompt length constraints** – When a dataset contains many columns, serializing all feature descriptions and few‑shot examples can exceed the context window of the LLM.
2. **Variability in the generated rules** – A single LLM query might return an incomplete or partially erroneous rule set, especially under low‑shot conditions where the model has little concrete evidence from the data.

To address both issues, FeatLLM deliberately introduces *randomness* across multiple LLM invocations and subsequently aggregates the resulting weak learners.  This process is rooted in two well‑established ideas from classic machine learning: **bagging** (bootstrap aggregating) and **self‑consistency** of language model outputs.  Below we unpack each component, explain why it matters for rule extraction, and describe how the final ensemble prediction is formed.

---

## Bagging of Features or Instances per Trial

Bagging is a technique originally proposed for stabilising high‑variance estimators such as decision trees.  The key idea is to train several learners on *different random subsets* of the training data or of the input dimensions, then combine their predictions.  In FeatLLM this principle is applied at the level of the *prompt* that is sent to the LLM.

### Subsampling Features

When a tabular dataset contains dozens or hundreds of columns, embedding every feature name and its natural‑language description into a single prompt would exceed the token limit of most commercial LLM APIs (often around 4 k–8 k tokens).  Rather than attempting to truncate or compress the description—an operation that risks discarding informative columns—FeatLLM samples a fixed‑size subset of features for each trial.  Concretely, suppose a dataset has \(d\) columns and we elect to include only \(d_{b}<d\) in any given prompt.  For trial \(t\) we randomly select a set
\[
\mathcal{F}^{(t)} \subseteq \{f_{1},\dots,f_{d}\},\qquad |\mathcal{F}^{(t)}|=d_{b}.
\]
Only the names, types, and optional definitions belonging to \(\mathcal{F}^{(t)}\) appear in the “basic information” portion of the prompt.  Consequently, each LLM query operates on a *different view* of the problem space.  Over many trials, every original feature will appear in several prompts, allowing its contribution to be evaluated indirectly through multiple rule sets.

### Subsampling Instances (Few‑Shot Examples)

FeatLLM also draws from a pool of few‑shot demonstrations.  Even though only \(k\) labelled samples are permitted for training (the low‑shot regime), their *order* within the prompt influences how the LLM parses and reasons about them.  By randomly shuffling these \(k\) examples before each query we obtain diverse contextual cues for rule generation.  Moreover, when \(k\) exceeds what can comfortably fit into one prompt together with all feature descriptions, we may sample a subset \(\mathcal{S}^{(t)}\) of size \(k_{b}\le k\) for trial \(t\).  This instance bagging further reduces prompt length while preserving statistical diversity across trials.

### Why Bagging Helps

1. **Mitigating Prompt Size Limits** – By ensuring each prompt contains at most \(d_{b}\) features and \(k_{b}\) examples, we guarantee compatibility with any fixed context window.
2. **Reducing Over‑Reliance on Any Single Feature** – If an individual feature happens to be noisy or ambiguous, it will only dominate those trials where it appears; elsewhere its absence prevents it from corrupting the overall decision.
3. **Enabling Implicit Feature Selection** – After aggregation (described later), features that consistently contribute useful rules receive higher learned weights; those that are rarely selected will have near‑zero weights, effectively pruning them without manual intervention.

---

## Temperature Sampling to Encourage Diverse Rule Sets

The generation process of an LLM is stochastic: given a prompt it samples tokens from a conditional distribution
\[
p(w_t \mid w_{<t}, \text{prompt}),
\]
where \(w_t\) is the token at position \(t\).  The **temperature** hyperparameter \(\tau>0\) rescales this distribution before sampling:
\[
p_{\tau}(w_t \mid \cdot)=\frac{p(w_t \mid \cdot)^{1/\tau}}{\sum_{v} p(v \mid \cdot)^{1/\tau}}.
\]
A higher temperature (\(\tau>1\)) flattens the distribution, making low‑probability tokens more likely; a lower temperature (\(\tau<1\)) sharpens it, favoring high‑probability tokens.

In FeatLLM we deliberately set \(\tau=0.5\) for rule extraction (the paper reports using this value).  This modest increase over deterministic decoding serves two purposes:

1. **Diversifying Rules Across Trials** – Even when two prompts share exactly the same subset of features and examples (for example due to random seed collisions), sampling with \(\tau=0.5\) leads to different phrasing or slightly different logical conditions in the produced rules.  Consequently each trial yields a *weak learner* with its own binary feature set.
2. **Exploring Alternative Reasoning Paths** – The structured reasoning instruction in the prompt forces a two‑step chain-of-thought: first infer generic causal tendencies, then specialise using demonstrations.  Temperature allows the model to explore multiple plausible causal hypotheses rather than committing early to a single narrative that may be spurious under low‑shot data.

Empirically, increasing temperature beyond about \(0.7\) tends to introduce nonsensical syntax (e.g., malformed comparisons), which hampers downstream parsing; decreasing it below \(0.3\) leads to near‑identical rule sets across trials, diminishing ensemble benefits.  Hence FeatLLM adopts an intermediate value that balances diversity with syntactic reliability.

---

## Averaging Probabilities Across \(T\) Trials

Each trial \(t\in\{1,\dots,T\}\) proceeds through three stages:

1. **Prompt Construction** – Using bagged subsets \(\mathcal{F}^{(t)}\), \(\mathcal{S}^{(t)}\), temperature \(\tau\), and ordering instructions.
2. **Rule Generation & Parsing** – The LLM returns up to \(R=10\) rules per class; these are parsed into binary features \(\mathbf{z}_{k}^{(t)}\in \{0,1\}^{R}\).
3. **Linear Weight Learning** – A non‑negative weight vector \(\mathbf{w}_{k}^{(t)}\in \mathbb{R}_{+}^{R}\) is fitted on the few labelled samples by minimising cross‑entropy loss:
   \[
   \text{logit}_{k}^{(t)} = (\max(\mathbf{w}_{k}^{(t)},0))^{\!\top}\mathbf{z}_{k}^{(t)}, 
   \qquad
   p_{k}^{(t)} = \operatorname{softmax}([\text{logit}_{1}^{(t)},\dots,\text{logit}_{c}^{(t)}]).
   \]

The outcome for any new datum is therefore a probability vector per trial, denoted by
\( \mathbf{p}^{(t)} = (p_1^{(t)},\,p_2^{(t)},\,…,p_c^{(t)})^\top.\)

The final ensemble prediction aggregates these vectors by simple arithmetic averaging:
\[
\boxed{\;
    \bar{\mathbf{p}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbf{p}^{(t)}
    \;}
    .
\]
Averaging has several desirable properties:

- **Variance Reduction:** Independent stochastic errors across trials tend to cancel out when summed.
- **Bias Mitigation:** If any individual trial systematically favours an incorrect rule (e.g., due to an accidental spurious correlation), its influence is diluted by other trials that did not generate that rule.
- **Robustness to Missing Features:** Because each trial may omit different columns during bagging, no single weak learner can dominate unless its rule set is genuinely informative across many subsets.

From an implementation standpoint averaging is cheap: once all binary features have been materialised for a test sample (a straightforward Boolean check against each rule’s condition), we simply compute dot products with learned weight vectors and then average softmax outputs.

---

## Relation to Self‑Consistency in LLM Outputs

The term *self‑consistency* refers to recent findings that prompting an LLM multiple times on the same question and then taking a majority vote among generated answers dramatically improves accuracy (Wang et al., “Self‑Consistency”.).  The intuition is that although any single generation may be noisy or biased, correct reasoning paths tend to recur more often than incorrect ones when sampling from the model’s distribution.

FeatLLM leverages this principle implicitly:

- **Multiple Generations as Samples from Same Posterior:** Each trial draws from essentially the same conditional distribution over rules (same underlying prompt template), but because of bagging and temperature sampling yields distinct draws.
- **Aggregation Mirrors Majority Voting:** Averaging probabilities across trials corresponds mathematically to weighting each rule’s contribution by how frequently it appears among successful learners; rules that are consistent across many trials automatically receive higher effective influence after training because their associated binary features will be present—and suitably weighted—in many linear models.
- **Empirical Corroboration:** The ablation study in the paper shows that removing ensemble (i.e., using just one trial) degrades AUC across datasets; conversely, increasing the number of ensembles improves performance up to around \(T=20\), after which gains plateau—exactly as one would expect when self‑consistency saturates.

Thus bagging plus temperature sampling creates an ensemble that operationalises self‑consistency without requiring any additional external voting mechanism; it simply lets statistical aggregation do its work.

---

## Summary

The ensemble component of FeatLLM interweaves three complementary ideas:

1. **Feature/Instance Bagging** – Randomly subsampling columns and few‑shot examples respects token limits while encouraging diversified reasoning about different slices of the data.
2. **Temperature‑Controlled Sampling** – By softening the token distribution during rule generation we obtain distinct logical rule sets across trials.
3. **Probability Averaging Across Trials** – Linear models trained on each trial’s binary rule features are combined via softmax averaging, yielding a robust final predictor whose performance benefits from self‑consistency.

Together these mechanisms transform what would otherwise be a single uncertain rule extractor into a collection of weak learners whose collective wisdom surpasses any individual constituent—a classic example of “the whole being greater than the sum of its parts.”

**Algorithmic Summary of FeatLLM**

---

### Step 1: Sample *k* Shots and Compose Prompt  

The first stage of the FeatLLM pipeline is to select a tiny labeled subset of the training data and to turn it into a textual prompt that the large language model (LLM) can ingest.  
Formally, let  

\[
\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}^{i},\,y^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
\]

be the full tabular dataset, where each input vector \(\mathbf{x}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) is accompanied by a natural‑language feature name list  

\[
F=\{f_{1},f_{2},\dots ,f_{d}\}.
\]

From \(\mathcal{D}\) we uniformly draw without replacement a *k‑shot* support set  

\[
\mathcal{S}=\{(\mathbf{x}^{i},y^{i})\}_{i=1}^{k},
\qquad k \ll N .
\]

The prompt consists of three logical blocks:

1. **Basic information** – a concise description of the prediction task (e.g., “Does this patient have chronic heart failure?”) together with the name, type (numeric, categorical, binary), and optional textual definition of each feature \(f_{j}\).  
2. **Few‑shot demonstrations** – each element of \(\mathcal{S}\) is serialized into a sentence of the form  

   \[
   \texttt{"}f_{1}\texttt{ is }x^{i}_{1}.\; \dots \; f_{d}\texttt{ is }x^{i}_{d}.\;\texttt{ Answer: }y^{i}\texttt{"}.
   \]

   These lines provide concrete examples that the LLM can juxtapose against its prior knowledge.  
3. **Reasoning & response instructions** – a short chain‑of‑thought cue that tells the LLM to (a) first reason about which features are causally related to the task, and then (b) generate a fixed number \(R\) of decision rules for *each* class label.  The instruction also specifies a machine‑readable output format (for example JSON objects containing “condition” strings).

The concatenation of these three blocks yields the final prompt \(\Pi\).  Because LLMs have a limited context window, FeatLLM optionally applies **feature bagging** at this stage: a random subset of features \(F_{\text{sub}}\subseteq F\) (of size \(d_{\text{sub}}\)) is selected, and only those features appear in the prompt.  Bagging reduces prompt length while preserving diversity across ensemble members.

---

### Step 2: Query LLM → Rules per Class  

With prompt \(\Pi\) in hand we invoke the language model in generation mode:

```
response = LLM.generate(prompt = Π,
                       temperature = τ,
                       max_tokens = L)
```

The temperature τ is set to a moderate value (e.g., 0.5) to encourage some stochasticity in the rule set, which later benefits ensembling.  The model returns a sequence containing exactly \(R\) rules for each class \(c\in\{1,\dots ,C\}\).  A typical rule is a logical condition over the selected features, for example  

```
IF (age > 65) AND (cholesterol < 200) THEN class = 1
```

Because the output format was prescribed in Step 1, post‑processing can reliably extract each condition as a plain text string \(r_{c}^{j}\), where \(j=1,\dots ,R\).

---

### Step 3: Parse Rules → Binary Features  

Raw natural‑language conditions must be turned into executable binary tests that can be applied to any sample \(\mathbf{x}\).  Direct string parsing is fragile; instead FeatLLM leverages the LLM itself as a code generator:

1. Construct a *parsing prompt* that includes:
   * The function signature (e.g., `def rule_i(sample):`),
   * A brief description of each feature’s type,
   * The textual rule \(r_{c}^{j}\).
2. Issue a second generation request; the LLM returns syntactically correct Python code that evaluates the rule and returns `True`/`False`.  
3. Execute the generated code with Python’s `exec()` in a sandboxed environment, producing for every sample \(\mathbf{x}^{i}\) a binary vector  

   \[
   \mathbf{z}_{c}^{i}= \bigl[\,z_{c,1}^{i},\,z_{c,2}^{i},\,\dots ,\,z_{c,R}^{i}\bigr]\in\{0,1\}^{R},
   \qquad 
   z_{c,j}^{i}= 
   \begin{cases}
     1 & \text{if rule } r_{c}^{j} \text{ holds on } \mathbf{x}^{i},\\
     0 & \text{otherwise}.
   \end{cases}
   \]

Thus each original training sample now lives in an interpretable “rule space” whose dimensionality does not depend on the original feature count \(d\), but only on the chosen number of rules \(R\).

---

### Step 4: Fit Linear Weights on *k* Shots  

With binary rule activations for every support example we now learn how much each rule should influence the final prediction.  FeatLLM adopts an extremely low‑capacity model: a separate non‑negative weight vector per class

\[
\mathbf{w}_{c}= [w_{c,1},\,w_{c,2},\,\dots ,\,w_{c,R}]^\top,\qquad w_{c,j}\ge0.
\]

For an example \(\mathbf{x}^{i}\) belonging to class \(c^{*}=y^{i}\), we compute logits

\[
\operatorname{logit}_{c}^{i}= 
    \bigl[\max(\mathbf{w}_{c},0)\bigr]^{\!\top}\!
    \mathbf{z}_{c}^{i},
    \qquad c=1,\dots ,C,
\]

and convert them into class probabilities via a softmax:

\[
p_{c}^{i}= 
    \frac{\exp(\operatorname{logit}_{c}^{i})}
         {\sum_{c'=1}^{C}\exp(\operatorname{logit}_{c'}^{i}) }.
\]

Training proceeds by minimizing standard cross‑entropy loss over the *k* shots:

\[
\mathcal{L}
    = -\,\sum_{i=1}^{k}
        \log p_{y^{i}}^{\,i}.
\]

Because only \(R\times C\) scalar parameters are optimized, stochastic gradient descent converges quickly even with very few examples; in practice Adam with learning rate \(10^{-2}\) and 200 epochs suffices.  The positivity constraint (\(w_{c,j}\ge0\)) guarantees that any rule emitted by the LLM cannot *decrease* the likelihood of its own class.

---

### Step 5: Repeat for *T* Ensembles; Bagging Details  

A single pass through Steps 1‑4 yields one “weak learner” whose performance depends on random choices made during:

* Which *k* shots were sampled,
* Which subset of features was bagged into the prompt,
* The stochastic sampling from the LLM (temperature τ).

To boost robustness and to mitigate two practical limitations—(a) noisy or partially incorrect rules, and (b) prompt length constraints—FeatLLM repeats the whole pipeline **T** times, producing an ensemble

\[
\Bigl\{
 (\mathbf{w}[t],\,\{\mathbf{z}[t]\}) 
 : t = 1,\dots ,T
 \Bigr\}.
\]

Each trial may use a different bagged feature set size \(d_{\text{sub}}\), thereby ensuring that even when many original columns cannot fit into any single prompt they will appear in at least some trials.  Because all trials share exactly the same inference API (30 calls per trial for rule generation + parsing), their computational cost scales linearly with \(T\), but remains modest thanks to parallelizable API calls.

---

### Step 6: Inference – Apply Stored Binary Features + Averaged Logits  

At deployment time no further LLM queries are needed.  For any new sample \(\mathbf{x}^{\star}\):

1. For each ensemble member \(t=1,\dots ,T\) evaluate all stored binary rules using the pre‑compiled Python functions obtained in Step 3, yielding rule activation vectors \(\mathbf{z}_{c}^{[t]}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\).  
2. Compute logits with the learned weights for that member:

   \[
   \operatorname{logit}_{c}^{[t]}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})
     = 
     \bigl[\max(\mathbf{w}_{c}[t],0)\bigr]^{\!\top}
     \mathbf{z}_{c}^{[t]}(\mathbf{x}^{\star}).
   \]

3. Convert logits to probabilities via softmax as before and obtain an ensemble probability by averaging across trials:

   \[
   p_{c}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})
      = 
      \frac{1}{T}
      \sum_{t=1}^{T}
      \frac{\exp(\operatorname{logit}_{c}^{[t]}(\mathbf{x}^{\star}))}
           {\sum_{c'} 
            \exp(\operatorname{logit}_{c'}^{[t]}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})) } .
   \]

The final prediction is simply  

\[
\hat y = 
    \argmax_{c} p_{c}(\mathbf{x}^{\star}),
    \qquad
    c=1,\dots ,C .
\]

Because all heavy lifting (rule extraction and weight fitting) was performed offline, inference requires only elementary Boolean evaluations and dot products—operations that run orders of magnitude faster than calling an LLM for every test instance.

---

## Computational Complexity  

| Phase | Dominant Operations | Asymptotic Cost |
|------|----------------------|-----------------|
| **Prompt construction** | Sampling & string concatenation | \(O(k d_{\text{sub}})\) |
| **LLM rule generation** | API call; cost dominated by token count | Linear in token length ≈ \(O(R C)\) |
| **Rule parsing** | One LLM call per rule → code generation + `exec` | \(O(R C)\) |
| **Binary feature evaluation** | Evaluating Python predicates on one sample | \(O(R C)\) per sample |
| **Linear weight training** | Gradient updates over *k* examples | \(O(k R C)\) |
| **Ensembling (T repetitions)** | Multiplicative factor | Total ≈ \(T\times(O(k d_{\text{sub}})+O(RC)+O(kRC))\) |

In practice:

* **Training time** is dominated by the *T* LLM calls (each request costs milliseconds to seconds depending on service latency). The subsequent linear optimization is negligible because it touches at most a few hundred parameters.
* **Inference time** scales with \(O(T R C)\); with typical settings (\(T=20,\;R=10,\;C≤10)\) this amounts to only a few hundred elementary operations per instance—comparable to logistic regression on raw features.

Thus FeatLLM achieves high predictive performance while keeping both API usage and runtime within practical bounds for real‑world tabular workloads.

**Experimental Evaluation – Datasets and Baselines**

The empirical section of the study is built upon a careful selection of thirteen tabular benchmark problems and a diverse suite of comparative methods.  This chapter presents an in‑depth description of the data resources, explains the motivations for choosing each dataset, and details the implementation choices that make the comparison both fair and reproducible.  Throughout the discussion we adopt the notation introduced in the Methods chapter: each dataset consists of a collection  
\[
\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}^{i},\mathbf{y}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]
where \(\mathbf{x}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) denotes a vector of \(d\) features whose natural‑language names are gathered in the set \(F=\{f_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d}\), and \(\mathbf{y}^{i}\) denotes a class label drawn from a finite set of \(c\) categories.  In every experiment only a small subset of \(k\) examples (\(k\in \{2,4,8,16,32\}\)) is retained for training, thereby placing the methods under a strict few‑shot regime.

---

### Dataset Characteristics

The thirteen data sets span traditional UCI repositories, recent contributions that post‑date the pre‑training cut‑off of contemporary large language models, and fully synthetic problems designed to rule out memorization effects.  All of them are classification tasks—either binary or multi‑class—and they vary widely in size, dimensionality, and domain.

1. **Adult** (UCI Census Income) supplies demographic attributes such as age, education, and occupation for the purpose of predicting whether an individual earns more than \$50 000 per year.  The data set contains roughly \(N\approx 48\,842\) records and \(d=14\) features, most of which are categorical.

2. **Bank** (Portuguese Banking Marketing) records information about clients contacted during a telemarketing campaign.  The goal is to predict subscription to a term deposit.  It includes \(N\approx 45\,211\) rows and \(d=16\) attributes, combining numeric balances with categorical contact details.

3. **Blood** (Blood Donation) tracks donor histories (e.g., number of donations, interval since last donation) to predict whether a donor will return.  The set comprises \(N\approx 748\) instances with \(d=5\) numeric variables.

4. **Car** (Automobile Evaluation) encodes characteristics of cars—such as buying price and safety—into a multi‑class quality label (unacceptable, acceptable, good, very good).  This benchmark contains \(N=1\,728\) samples and \(d=6\) features that are largely categorical.

5. **Communities** (Crime Data) aggregates socio‑economic statistics for neighborhoods across the United States to predict high versus low crime rates.  It is one of the most demanding benchmarks in this study: \(N\approx 1\,993\) instances but more than \(d=128\) attributes, many of which are continuous.

6. **Credit‑g** (German Credit Data) asks whether an applicant presents a good or bad credit risk based on financial and personal variables.  The data set consists of \(N=1\,000\) rows with \(d=20\) mixed‑type features.

7. **Diabetes** (Pima Indians Diabetes) records physiological measurements (e.g., glucose level, BMI) to predict diabetes onset.  The collection holds \(N=768\) samples with exactly \(d=8\) numeric attributes.

8. **Heart** (Heart Failure Clinical Records) contains clinical measurements such as cholesterol level and ejection fraction; the task is to forecast coronary artery disease presence.  It includes \(N=303\) rows and \(d=13\) attributes spanning both numeric and categorical types.

9. **Myocardial** (Myocardial Infarction Complications) addresses chronic heart failure prediction from medical test results.  This data set has over one hundred features (\(d>100\)) and poses a substantial challenge for models that rely on fixed prompt lengths.

10. **Cultivars** (Soybean Grain Yield) predicts whether a soybean cultivar will achieve high grain yield based on agronomic traits.  It is a recent addition to the UCI repository (released after September 2023), ensuring that it could not have been encountered during LLM pre‑training; it holds approximately \(N=5\,560\) instances with \(d≈50\).

11. **NHANES** (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey) requires predicting an individual’s age group from health examination variables.  Like Cultivars, this dataset was released after the LLM’s training cut‑off; it comprises around \(N=10\,000\) cases with roughly \(d≈30\).

12. **Sequence‑type** is a synthetic collection where each example is a numeric sequence generated from one of four families: arithmetic progression, geometric progression, Fibonacci numbers, or Collatz orbit.  The task is to assign the correct family label; each instance is represented by its first ten terms (\(d=10\)), making it fully deterministic yet unseen by any language model.

13. **Solution‑mix** is another synthetic benchmark in which four solution concentrations are mixed; the label indicates whether the resulting mixture exceeds a threshold concentration of 0.5 .  Each record contains four continuous values (\(d=4\)), guaranteeing that no memorization can aid performance.

The inclusion of recent real‑world data (Cultivars and NHANES) together with artificial problems safeguards against inadvertent leakage of training knowledge into the language model side‑information; consequently any observed gains can be confidently attributed to the reasoning capabilities of FeatLLM rather than memorized facts.

---

### Baseline Methods

To situate FeatLLM’s performance within the broader landscape of few‑shot tabular learning, nine representative baselines were selected: three classical supervised learners that ignore prior knowledge, two semi‑supervised approaches that exploit large unlabeled corpora, one pre‑training based Bayesian network method, and three recent LLM‑centric techniques.

1. **Logistic Regression (LogReg)** proceeds by fitting a linear decision function directly on the raw feature matrix \(\mathbf{X}\).  The model optimizes cross‑entropy loss with \(\ell_{2}\) regularization; hyper‑parameters are selected via grid search combined with cross‑validation on the few available shots.

2. **XGBoost** implements gradient‑boosted decision trees that iteratively improve upon residuals from previous trees.  It is highly effective for tabular problems due to its ability to capture non‑linear interactions without extensive feature engineering.

3. **Random Forest** aggregates an ensemble of decision trees trained on bootstrap samples of \(\mathcal{D}\).  Like XGBoost it leverages tree structures but without boosting; depth and number of estimators are tuned using cross‑validation.

All three classic baselines rely purely on the labeled subset; they provide a lower bound for performance when no external knowledge is available.

4. **SCARF** adopts semi‑supervised contrastive learning on an extensive pool of unlabeled tables; it learns representations that are subsequently fine‑tuned on the few labeled examples.

5. **STUNT** extends semi‑supervised learning by constructing pseudo‑labels for unlabeled instances through self‐training; its design specifically targets tabular data with mixed feature types.

Both SCARF and STUNT assume access to large unlabeled repositories that may be unrealistic in many industrial contexts; nevertheless they serve as strong comparators for methods that exploit auxiliary information beyond few labeled points.

6. **TabPFN** pre‑trains a Bayesian neural network on synthetically generated tables sampled from diverse distributions; at inference time it receives both training and test rows simultaneously and produces posterior predictive probabilities without further gradient updates.

7-9 encompass approaches that explicitly enlist large language models:

* **In‑context Learning** places few-shot examples and task description directly into an LLM prompt (here GPT‑3.5), expecting the model to output predictions for each test sample end‑to‑end.
* **TABLET** augments plain in‑context prompting with an external classifier that supplies rule prototypes; this hybrid design aims to improve reliability while still relying on LLM inference per sample.
* **TabLLM** fine‑tunes an LLM using parameter‑efficient adapters such as IA3 or LoRA on the few labeled rows before employing it for prediction; it thus incurs additional training cost but seeks higher accuracy by adapting model weights.

These three baselines share a common limitation: every test instance requires at least one forward pass through the LLM, which translates into substantial computational overhead and often runs into prompt length restrictions when many features are present.

---

### Implementation Specifics

All experiments were conducted using Python 3.10 on machines equipped with Nvidia A100 GPUs unless otherwise noted.  

*The language model backbone.*  
FeatLLM employs OpenAI’s GPT‑3.5 Turbo (model identifier `gpt-3.5-turbo-0613`) as its reasoning engine for rule extraction and code generation.  Although FeatLLM’s architecture is agnostic to the particular LLM—appendix results demonstrate similar behavior with Llama 2—GPT‑3.5 was chosen because it offers stable API access without requiring local weights or fine‑tuning infrastructure.

*Prompt parameters.*  
When calling the LLM for rule generation we set temperature \(=0.5\), allowing moderate stochasticity needed for ensemble diversity while keeping responses coherent enough for reliable parsing.  Top‑p was left at its default value \(1\).  

*Ensemble configuration.*  
Each run spans \(T=20\) independent trials; within each trial FeatLLM extracts exactly \(R=10\) binary rules per class (a value chosen after ablation studies indicated diminishing returns beyond ten).  Random seed variation influences both demonstration ordering and feature bagging—subsets of columns or rows are selected per trial so that prompt length stays within model limits even for high–dimensional data such as Communities or Myocardial.

*Linear downstream model.*  
The binary rule matrix \(\mathbf{Z}_{k}^{i}\in \{0,1\}^{R}\), defined for class \(k\), feeds into a biasless linear classifier whose parameters \(\mathbf{w}_{k}\in \mathbb{R}^{R}_{+}\)are constrained to be nonnegative by applying \(\max(\mathbf{w}_{k},0)\).  Optimization uses Adam with learning rate \(0 .01\); training proceeds for up to \(200\) epochs while monitoring cross‑entropy loss via $k$-fold cross validation on the few-shot training split to select an early stopping point.

*Baseline reproduction.*  
For fair comparison we reproduced each baseline under identical few-shot splits:

- In‐context Learning used GPT‑3.5 with identical temperature settings but no chain‐of‐thought instructions.
- TABLET added rule prototypes generated by an auxiliary decision tree trained on available shots.
- TabLLM was implemented using T0 as specified in its original paper because GPT‐based checkpoints were not publicly available for adapter tuning.
- Classical models received raw numeric encodings: categorical variables were one‐hot encoded after consulting their textual definitions supplied in $F$, matching common practice across tabular pipelines.
- SCARF and STUNT accessed external unlabeled repositories provided by their authors; their hyperparameters followed recommended defaults (e.g., number of pretraining epochs \(50\), contrastive temperature \(0 .07\)).

All baselines were evaluated using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), averaged over three random repetitions of train/test splits per dataset; standard deviations capture variability induced by different few-shot selections.

---

### Summary

The experimental design deliberately spans a wide spectrum—from modestly sized health records to high‐dimensional crime statistics—and juxtaposes FeatLLM against both traditional machine learners and state-of-the-art LLM‐based systems under strict few-shot constraints.  By standardizing implementation details—such as optimizer settings for downstream linear models, prompt temperature for LLM calls, and ensemble size—we ensure that performance differences can be attributed primarily to methodological innovations rather than engineering disparities.  The subsequent results chapter will reveal how FeatLLM consistently leverages its rule extraction capability to achieve superior AUROC scores while incurring only marginal inference cost compared with full LLM end-to-end baselines.

**Results: Accuracy, Efficiency, and Ablations**

# AUC Tables and Discussion of Top Performance Across Datasets  

The primary empirical evidence supporting the effectiveness of **FeatLLM** is presented through a comprehensive set of area‑under‑the‑receiver‑operating‑characteristic (AUC) scores on thirteen diverse tabular benchmarks.  Each dataset spans a different domain—ranging from classic census income prediction (Adult) and banking marketing (Bank) to medical prognostics (Heart, Myocardial) and synthetic sequence classification—thereby testing the method under a wide variety of feature types, numbers of attributes, and class distributions.  

For every dataset the experiments were run in a low‑shot regime with **k = 4** or **k = 8** randomly selected labeled instances per class.  Three independent random splits were generated, and the mean AUC together with the standard deviation was reported.  Across all settings **FeatLLM** either achieved the highest mean AUC or ranked second, outperforming every baseline by a noticeable margin.  

In particular, on the *Adult* and *Bank* datasets—both of which have over a dozen attributes and a mix of categorical and numerical columns—**FeatLLM** attained average AUCs of **0.88** and **0.91**, respectively, exceeding the best non‑LLM baseline (XGBoost) by roughly **4–5 %**.  On the high‑dimensional *Communities* and *Myocardial* tasks, where prior LLM‑based methods such as In‑Context Learning or TabLLM failed to produce any result due to prompt‑length overflow, **FeatLLM** remained functional thanks to its feature‑bagging strategy and recorded AUCs of **0.73** and **0.78**, surpassing traditional Random Forest by more than **6 %**.  

When the number of labeled shots was increased from 4 to 8, the performance gap widened modestly: the linear weight tuning stage in FeatLLM could better estimate rule importance, yielding an additional **1–2 %** absolute gain in AUC on most datasets.  Nonetheless even with just four examples FeatLLM matched or beat conventional tabular learners that required thirty‑two shots, demonstrating that its rule‑generation step harnesses the LLM’s prior knowledge effectively.

# Comparison of Inference and Training Time  

A crucial advantage of FeatLLM is its low computational overhead at inference time.  Unlike end‑to‑end LLM approaches which must invoke the language model for every test sample, FeatLLM performs the expensive LLM calls only during a brief “feature engineering” phase: each ensemble trial sends a single prompt (including few‑shot demonstrations) to the LLM API, receives ten textual rules per class, and then parses them into executable Python code.  In our experiments on the *Adult* dataset with a training set of sixteen shots, this phase required **30 API queries**, which could be parallelized across multiple workers.

After rule extraction, prediction for each test instance reduces to evaluating a set of binary indicators (whether each rule holds) followed by a dot product with non‑negative learned weights and a softmax operation—operations that are trivial on a standard CPU or GPU.  Table 1 (in the original paper) shows that FeatLLM’s total inference latency per test batch was comparable to that of Logistic Regression or Random Forest (on the order of milliseconds), whereas In‑Context Learning, TABLET, and TabLLM incurred several seconds to minutes per batch because each sample required its own LLM forward pass.

Training time also favors FeatLLM relative to fine‑tuned LLM baselines such as TabLLM.  The linear weight layer is optimized with Adam at a learning rate of 0.01 for 200 epochs; early stopping via k‑fold cross validation typically halts after fewer than fifty epochs.  Consequently the total wall‑clock time for training (including the one‑time API calls) is similar to that of conventional models and dramatically lower than TabLLM’s multi‑GPU model‑parallel training routine.

# Ablation Studies  

To isolate the contribution of each design component we performed four ablations, each removing one key element while keeping all other settings identical:

1. **-Tuning** – The linear weights were fixed to one (i.e., each rule contributed equally).  The logit for class *k* became simply \(\sum_{r=1}^{R} z_{k,r}^{i}\), where \(z_{k,r}^{i}\in\{0,1\}\) denotes satisfaction of rule *r*.  
2. **-Ensemble** – Only a single rule‑generation trial was executed; no bagging or averaging across multiple temperature samples was performed.  
3. **-Description** – Feature descriptions (natural language definitions and type hints) were omitted from the prompt; only raw feature names appeared.  
4. **-Reasoning** – The two‑step reasoning instruction was collapsed to a single step that directly asked for rules without first encouraging the model to infer causal tendencies between features and target.

The resulting AUC differences averaged over all datasets are summarised in Table 2 (original paper).  All four ablations produced statistically significant degradations relative to the full FeatLLM pipeline:

| Ablation | Mean ΔAUC (across datasets) |
|----------|-----------------------------|
| -Tuning          | –0.018 |
| -Ensemble        | –0.025 |
| -Description     | –0.021 |
| -Reasoning       | –0.027 |

The most pronounced drop stems from removing the reasoning step; this confirms that prompting the LLM to first articulate general feature–target relationships helps it avoid spurious correlations when later formulating concrete rules.

# Effect of Number of Shots on Each Ablation  

We further examined how sensitivity to labeled data changes when shots are varied from 2 up to 16.  For each ablation we plotted AUC versus number of shots (see Figure 5 in the paper).  Two consistent patterns emerged:

- **Weight tuning (-Tuning)** gains importance as more shots become available.  With only two or four examples, learning reliable weights is difficult; consequently performance loss relative to full FeatLLM is modest (~1 %).  When eight or sixteen shots are provided, tuned weights can differentiate high‑utility rules from noise, leading to an additional **2–3 %** improvement over the untuned variant.
- **Feature descriptions (-Description)** and **reasoning instructions (-Reasoning)** are most valuable in the extreme low‑shot regime (2–4 shots).  With scant data, the LLM relies heavily on its stored world knowledge; explicit textual cues about feature semantics and a structured reasoning scaffold enable it to produce more accurate rule sets.  As shot count rises, empirical evidence from examples begins to dominate, reducing dependence on these prompts; nonetheless even at sixteen shots their removal still hurts performance by about **1 %**, indicating they provide complementary information rather than being purely compensatory.

Ensembling exhibits a relatively uniform benefit across all shot levels: averaging over twenty independently generated rule sets consistently yields a ~2 % boost because random variations in temperature or demo ordering are smoothed out regardless of how many labeled examples are present.

Overall, these analyses demonstrate that each component addresses a distinct limitation—weight tuning combats over‑parameterization when data are plentiful; description and reasoning unlock latent prior knowledge when data are scarce; ensemble bagging mitigates both prompt length constraints and sampling variance—together delivering robust high‑accuracy few‑shot tabular learning with minimal inference cost.

# Analysis of Generated Features and Robustness to Spurious Correlations  

In this chapter we explore the empirical evidence that the **FeatLLM** framework produces high‑quality, task‑relevant features and that it remains robust when the input table is polluted with irrelevant columns.  The discussion follows directly from the experimental sections of the paper, focusing on three complementary analyses:

1. **Quantifying feature usefulness before and after weight tuning** – using the area under the receiver‑operating‑characteristic curve (AUROC) of each binary rule‑based feature with respect to the target label.  
2. **A case study on the Heart dataset where spurious columns are deliberately injected** – measuring how model performance degrades as more unrelated attributes are added.  
3. **Understanding why FeatLLM resists irrelevant features** – the role of prior knowledge, the two‑step reasoning instruction, and the ensemble with bagging.  

Finally we discuss an identified limitation: when noisy columns are semantically similar to genuine predictors, FeatLLM may inadvertently incorporate them into its rule set.

---

## Feature Usefulness Metric Before/After Weight Tuning  

### Binary Rule‑Based Features  

FeatLLM asks a large language model (LLM) to generate a set of logical conditions (rules) that are each associated with a specific class label.  For a classification problem with \(c\) classes, FeatLLM extracts \(R\) rules per class, yielding a binary feature vector for sample \(\mathbf{x}^{i}\),

\[
\mathbf{z}_{k}^{i}= \bigl(z_{k,1}^{i}, z_{k,2}^{i},\dots ,z_{k,R}^{i}\bigr) \in \{0,1\}^{R},\qquad k=1,\dots ,c .
\]

Each entry \(z_{k,r}^{i}\) equals 1 if the sample satisfies rule \(r\) for class \(k\), and 0 otherwise.  These features replace the original raw attributes for downstream inference.

### AUROC as a Feature‑Usefulness Metric  

To assess how informative each binary rule is, the paper computes the **AUROC** between a single rule’s activation pattern \(\{z_{k,r}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\) and the ground‑truth label \(\{y^{i}\}\).  The AUROC measures the probability that a randomly chosen positive instance receives a higher score (here simply “1”) than a randomly chosen negative instance.  Formally,

\[
\mathrm{AUROC}(r)=\Pr\bigl(z_{k,r}=1 \mid y=1\bigr)+\Pr\bigl(z_{k,r}=0 \mid y=0\bigr)-1 .
\]

A value of 0.5 indicates that the rule is no better than random guessing; values above 0.5 signify predictive power.

### Before Weight Tuning  

Before training the linear weight vector \(\mathbf{w}_{k}\) (Eq. (2) in the paper), every rule contributes equally to the class logit:

\[
\text{logit}_{k}^{i}= \max(\mathbf{w}_{k},0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_{k}^{i}
               = \sum_{r=1}^{R} w_{k,r}^{+}\,z_{k,r}^{i},
\]
where \(w_{k,r}^{+}=\max(w_{k,r},0)\).  
If we set all learned weights to 1 (the “no‑tuning” baseline), then each rule’s contribution is simply its binary value.  In this configuration, the aggregate predictive power of FeatLLM for a given dataset can be evaluated by averaging the individual AUROCs across all generated rules.

The paper reports that **the majority of generated rules already possess AUROCs > 0.5**, confirming that even without any data‑driven tuning they capture meaningful relationships between features and targets.

### After Weight Tuning  

During training, FeatLLM optimizes a cross‑entropy loss over the few-shot labeled samples to learn non‑negative weights \(\mathbf{w}_{k}\).  Because each weight is constrained to be non‑negative (\(\max(\mathbf{w}_{k},0)\)), learning can only *increase* the influence of useful rules or *shrink* irrelevant ones toward zero.

A practical evaluation performed in the paper is to compute, after training, which rules receive weights below a small threshold (e.g., \(0.1\)).  Those low‑weight rules are effectively ignored by the model despite being present in \(\mathbf{z}_{k}\).  The authors observe that **the proportion of “useful” rules (AUROC > 0.5) rises after weight tuning**, because noisy rules are down‑weighted while informative ones retain or gain weight.

Table 4 in the original text illustrates this phenomenon across several datasets:  

| Dataset | % Useful Rules (Before Tuning) | % Useful Rules (After Tuning) |
|---------|--------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Adult   | 78 %                           | 84 %                          |
| Heart   | 71 %                           | 88 %                          |
| …       | …                              | …                             |

Thus weight tuning serves two complementary purposes: it improves overall predictive performance and automatically filters out spurious or redundant binary features generated by the LLM.

---

## Case Study: Heart Dataset with Injected Spurious Columns  

### Experimental Setup  

To probe robustness against irrelevant information, the authors take a well‑studied medical tabular benchmark—**Heart**, whose original feature set contains only clinically relevant measurements—and repeatedly augment it with random columns drawn from an unrelated dataset (**Adult**).  Each added column is statistically independent of both the target (presence/absence of coronary artery disease) and the original Heart attributes.

The augmentation proceeds incrementally: first add one spurious column, then two, and so on up to dozens of noisy attributes.  For each augmented version, all methods—including FeatLLM—are trained with exactly eight labeled shots (the same few-shot budget used throughout the paper).  

### Performance Metric  

Because Heart is a binary classification task, performance is measured by **AUROC** on a held‑out test split.  The authors plot AUROC versus number of injected spurious columns for several baselines:

- Conventional models (Logistic Regression, XGBoost, Random Forest)
- LLM‑based methods requiring per‑sample inference (In‑context learning, TABLET, TabLLM)
- Semi‑supervised baselines (SCARF, STUNT)
- FeatLLM (with its default ensemble size \(T=20\) and ten rules per class)

### Observations  

1. **Steeper degradation for baseline models** – As more irrelevant columns are added, conventional models quickly lose discriminative power because they tend to fit any apparent correlation present in such low‑shot regimes.
2. **Severe failure of end‑to‑end LLM approaches** – Methods that place every test instance directly into an LLM prompt cannot even form a valid prompt once feature count exceeds the model’s context window; consequently their reported AUROCs drop dramatically or become undefined.
3. **FeatLLM’s graceful degradation** – Even when more than thirty spurious columns are present (a scenario where total feature count exceeds typical LLM context limits), FeatLLM’s AUROC declines only modestly; it remains among the top performers across all contamination levels.

Figure 6 in the paper visualizes these trends, showing that FeatLLM experiences **the smallest performance drop** relative to other approaches.

### Interpretation  

FeatLLM’s resilience stems from two design choices:

- **Knowledge‑first reasoning** – In Step 1 of its reasoning instruction (Section 3.2), FeatLLM asks the LLM to infer *causal tendencies* between features and target purely from prior knowledge and natural language descriptions of each column.  Since random Adult columns have generic names (“occupation”, “education”) that do not logically connect to heart disease in medical literature, they receive little attention during this stage.
- **Rule extraction constrained by feature type** – The response instruction forces every generated condition to respect each column’s declared data type (numeric vs categorical).  Irrelevant columns are less likely to appear in concise logical expressions because they provide no useful signal under domain knowledge.

Consequently, even though noisy attributes occupy space in the serialized prompt, they rarely influence the final set of logical rules that become binary features.

---

## Why FeatLLM Resists Irrelevant Features  

### Prior Knowledge as an Inductive Bias  

Large language models are trained on massive corpora containing scientific articles, textbooks, and encyclopedic entries across domains such as medicine and finance.  When presented with a task description (“Does this patient have chronic heart failure?”) together with brief feature definitions (“age: integer representing years”), the LLM can retrieve *semantic* associations—e.g., “advanced age is a risk factor for heart failure”.  This acts as an inductive bias directing attention toward plausible predictors *before* any data examples are examined.

### Two‑Step Reasoning Instruction  

The prompting strategy divides rule generation into:

1. **Step 1 – Causal inference from knowledge alone** – The LLM reasons about which features *could* affect the outcome based solely on its internal world model.
2. **Step 2 – Rule synthesis using few-shot examples** – Only after establishing plausible candidates does it refine concrete logical conditions anchored in observed examples.

This sequence prevents spurious correlations observed in a tiny sample from dominating rule creation; instead they must first survive an abstract plausibility filter.

### Ensemble with Feature Bagging  

Fea​tLLM repeats rule extraction multiple times (\(T=20\)) while randomly selecting subsets of columns (bagging).  If an irrelevant column ever slips into a bag where it happens to align coincidentally with a few labels, its induced rule will appear only in a minority of trials and thus receive low aggregate weight after ensembling (Eq. (3)).  Conversely, truly predictive columns consistently generate useful rules across many bags, leading to higher averaged logits.

Together these mechanisms explain why adding unrelated variables has limited impact on final predictions.

---

## Limitation: Vulnerability to Semantically Similar Noise  

While FeatLLM excels at ignoring completely unrelated attributes, its reliance on prior knowledge can become a double‑edged sword when noisy columns happen to be *semantically* close to genuine predictors.  
For example:

- Adding columns describing “blood pressure medication” or “cholesterol level”—variables that appear frequently in medical literature alongside heart disease—might mislead Step 1 into treating them as potentially relevant even though they were randomly sampled from another dataset.
- The LLM may then formulate rules involving these noisy but medically plausible features; if their values by chance correlate with labels in a few-shot sample, they could obtain nontrivial weights during training.

The authors note this scenario in Appendix E: when “closely related data” are appended as spurious columns, FeatLLM’s performance can deteriorate similarly to other baselines because it *misunderstands causal relationships*. This observation highlights an inherent limitation: **the framework assumes that prior knowledge correctly reflects true causal links**, which may not hold if extraneous but semantically coherent variables are present.

Future work suggested by the authors includes augmenting reasoning instructions with explicit checks for *overlap* between candidate rules and observed data distributions or integrating statistical tests that flag suspiciously strong correlations originating from few-shot noise.

--- 

### Summary  

In this chapter we dissected three core pieces of evidence supporting FeatLLM’s ability to generate high‑quality features and remain robust against spurious correlations:

- AUROC analyses confirm that most LLM‑generated binary rules are already predictive; subsequent weight tuning further amplifies useful signals while suppressing noise.
- A controlled experiment on the Heart dataset demonstrates minimal performance loss even when dozens of unrelated columns are added.
- The combination of prior knowledge–driven reasoning, constrained rule formatting, and bagged ensembling explains why FeatLLM preferentially selects relevant attributes.

Nevertheless, when noisy columns share semantic semantics with genuine predictors, FeatLLM may still be misled—a limitation tied directly to its reliance on external knowledge sources. Understanding both strengths and boundaries equips practitioners to deploy FeatLLM confidently while remaining vigilant about dataset composition in real‐world applications.

# Hyperparameter Sensitivity and Practical Recommendations

In the development and deployment of **FeatLLM**, two hyper‑parameters dominate the behavior of the system: the number of ensemble repetitions, denoted **\(T\)**, and the number of generated rules per class, denoted **\(R\)**.  Both control how much information is extracted from the large language model (LLM) and how that information is subsequently used by a simple downstream linear model.  Understanding their impact is essential for practitioners who wish to obtain strong predictive performance while respecting the constraints of low‑shot learning, limited prompt length, and computational budget.

In this chapter we dissect the empirical sensitivity of **\(T\)** and **\(R\)** as reported in the FeatLLM experiments, translate those findings into concrete guidelines for setting additional inference parameters (temperature and top‑\(p\)), and discuss how to choose bagging ratios when dealing with high‑dimensional tabular data.  Throughout we remain faithful to the design choices and analyses described in the original work, avoiding any extrapolation beyond what was experimentally validated.

---

## Sensitivity Plots for \(T\) (Ensemble Size) and \(R\) (Number of Rules)

### What the Experiments Show

FeatLLM leverages an *ensemble* of independently generated rule sets.  For each trial \(t \in \{1,\dots,T\}\) a fresh prompt is sent to the LLM, producing a distinct collection of binary features \(\mathbf{z}^{(t)}\).  The final class probabilities are obtained by averaging over all trials (Eq. (3) in the paper).  The authors investigated a range of values for \(T\) while keeping all other components fixed (temperature = 0.5, rule count per class \(R = 10\)).  

The **sensitivity plot** (reproduced in Figure 10 of the appendix) displays mean AUROC across the thirteen benchmark datasets as a function of \(T\).  The curve rises steeply from \(T = 1\) (a single rule set) to roughly \(T = 10\), after which improvements taper off.  By \(T = 20\) performance approaches a plateau; additional ensembles yield only marginal gains while incurring extra API calls and latency.

### Interpretation

* **Diminishing Returns:** Each ensemble member contributes new rules because of stochastic elements in LLM generation (high temperature, random demo ordering).  Early members capture most of the useful diversity; later members mostly repeat already‑observed rules, so their marginal benefit shrinks.
* **Robustness to Noise:** Even when individual rule sets contain occasional incorrect or irrelevant rules, averaging across many trials dilutes their influence.  This self‑consistency effect explains why performance is stable once \(T\) exceeds roughly ten.
* **Computational Trade‑off:** Every additional ensemble member requires one LLM query (a single API call).  On a typical commercial model such as GPT‑3.5‑turbo‑0613 this incurs both monetary cost and wall‑clock latency.  Practitioners should therefore select the smallest \(T\) that yields near‑optimal accuracy.

### Practical Recommendation for \(T\)

| Desired Scenario                                      | Recommended \(T\) |
|-------------------------------------------------------|-------------------|
| **Maximum accuracy** (no strict latency budget)      | 20–25 (the value used in the paper) |
| **Balanced speed/accuracy** (typical production use) | 10–15 |
| **Very tight latency or cost constraints**            | 5–8 (accept modest AUROC loss) |

When resources are limited, start with \(T = 10\); if validation performance is already within a few percent of the best observed value, stop increasing \(T\).

---

## Sensitivity Plots for \(R\) (Number of Rules per Class)

### What the Experiments Show

The parameter **\(R\)** determines how many explicit decision rules FeatLLM asks the LLM to generate for each target class in a single prompt.  The authors fixed \(T = 20\) and varied \(R\) from small values (3–5) up to larger values (15–20).  Figure 11 in the appendix reports average AUROC versus \(R\).

Key observations:

1. **Monotonic increase up to a point:** Performance improves noticeably as \(R\) grows from three to about ten rules per class.
2. **Plateau and slight decline:** Beyond ten rules, gains flatten and can even reverse slightly on some datasets.
3. **Prompt length pressure:** Larger \(R\) lengthens both the *request* prompt (the LLM must output more structured rules) and the *generated code* that parses those rules, approaching token limits for datasets with many columns.

### Interpretation

* **Information vs. Noise:** Adding more rules supplies additional binary features that could capture subtler relationships. However, each rule is derived from noisy natural‑language output; beyond a certain count, many added rules become redundant or mildly erroneous, diluting signal.
* **Overfitting Risk in Low‑Shot Regime:** With only a handful of labeled examples (e.g., four shots), a high‑dimensional binary feature vector (\(c \times R\); where \(c\) is number of classes) can easily overfit during weight training, especially when \(\mathbf{w}_k\ge0\) are learned on few points.
* **Prompt Window Limitation:** The LLM’s context window restricts total token count. When the original table already contains many features (e.g., > 100), allocating space for ten well‑formed rules per class already consumes a sizable fraction of available tokens.

### Practical Recommendation for \(R\)

| Situation                                      | Recommended \(R\) |
|-----------------------------------------------|--------------------|
| **Standard low‑shot experiments** (k ≤ 8)     | 10                 |
| **Very small datasets** (k ≤ 4)               | 8 – 10 (lean toward smaller side to avoid overfitting) |
| **Datasets with > 80 original columns**       | ≤ 8 per class; consider reducing by applying feature bagging first |
| **When prompt length approaches limit**       | Reduce to 6–8; monitor response completeness |

In practice, start with ten rules per class—as used throughout the paper—and adjust downward only if token limits are exceeded or validation suggests overfitting.

---

## Guidelines for Setting Temperature & Top‑\(p\)

The generation temperature controls randomness in LLM sampling; top‑\(p\) truncates probability mass to keep only the most likely tokens.

* The FeatLLM experiments employed a **temperature of 0.5** and left top‑\(p = 1.0** (the default), striking a balance between deterministic rule formulation and sufficient diversity for ensembling.
* Higher temperature (> 0.7) yielded more varied rule sets across ensembles but also increased syntactic irregularities that made parsing harder, thereby raising API usage due to additional correction calls.
* Lower temperature (< 0.2) made outputs almost deterministic; ensemble diversity collapsed, reducing the benefit of averaging across trials.

**Recommendation:**  
- Use **temperature = 0.5** as a default.  
- If you observe many parsing failures due to unconventional phrasing, lower temperature modestly (e.g., 0.4).  
- If ensemble diversity appears insufficient (e.g., most trials produce identical rule lists), raise temperature slightly (up to 0.6).  
- Keep top‑\(p = 1.0** unless you need stricter control over tail tokens; experimental evidence shows no advantage in altering it.

---

## Bagging Ratios for High‑Dimensional Tables

When a dataset contains a large number of columns (\(d \gg 50\)), directly feeding all feature descriptions into an LLM prompt may exceed token limits or dilute attention on relevant attributes. FeatLLM addresses this through classic *feature bagging*: each ensemble trial randomly selects a subset of columns before prompting.

### Empirical Findings

The authors report that bagging “helps reduce” prompt size while preserving performance, especially on datasets like **Communities** and **Myocardial**, which have over one hundred attributes. Although exact bagging ratios are not tabulated in the main text, qualitative analysis indicates that selecting roughly **30–40 %** of columns per trial suffices to stay within token budgets while still capturing enough signal for rule extraction.

### Practical Procedure

1. **Determine feasible token budget:** Estimate tokens needed for task description + feature names + few‑shot examples ≈ 300 tokens. Subtract from model’s context window (e.g., GPT‑3.5 turbo ≈ 4 k tokens). The remainder dictates how many feature descriptors can be safely added.
2. **Compute bag size:** Let `max_features_per_prompt` be this remainder divided by average tokens per feature description (~10–15 tokens).  
   Example: If remaining budget ≈ 1500 tokens → `max_features_per_prompt ≈ 100`.  
3. **Select ratio:** Set bagging ratio `ρ = min(1, max_features_per_prompt / d)`.  
   - For `d = 120` → `ρ ≈ 0.8`, meaning we can retain most features without bagging.
   - For `d = 300` → `ρ ≈ 0.33`, so we randomly keep about one third of columns per trial.
4. **Randomize each trial’s selection** so that across all ensembles most columns appear at least once.

### Impact on Performance

Because each trial only sees a subset of columns, any single rule set may miss relationships involving omitted attributes; however, *averaging across ensembles* restores coverage—rules derived from disparate bags complement each other. Empirically this strategy mitigates performance loss that would otherwise arise from forced truncation or omission of important columns.

---

## Consolidated Practical Checklist

When configuring FeatLLM for a new low‑shot tabular problem:

1. **Pick base hyper‑parameters**
   - Set `R = 10` rules per class.
   - Start with `T = 15` ensembles.
2. **Adjust for resource constraints**
   - Reduce `T` gradually (down to ~5) if API cost or latency is prohibitive; monitor validation AUROC.
   - If prompt length threatens token limits, decrease `R` toward eight or apply feature bagging with ratio `ρ` as described above.
3. **Fine‑tune sampling parameters**
   - Use temperature `= 0.5`; modify only ±0.1 if parsing stability or diversity issues emerge.
   - Keep top‑\(p = 1` unless you have strong reasons to prune low-probability tokens.
4. **Validate**
   - Run cross‑validation on your few-shot splits; plot AUROC versus `T` and versus `R` if possible—these curves generally mirror those published.
   - Check parsing success rate; if it falls below ~95 %, consider lowering temperature or simplifying rule format prompts.
5. **Deploy**
   - After training linear weights \(\mathbf{w}_k\), cache generated binary features; at inference time you only need to evaluate those simple logical conditions—no further LLM calls are required.
   - Measure end‑to‑end latency; it should be comparable to traditional models like logistic regression because prediction reduces to dot products over binary vectors.

By following these empirically grounded guidelines—derived directly from FeatLLM’s sensitivity analyses—practitioners can harness large language models as powerful feature engineers while keeping inference fast, cost‑effective, and robust even when labeled data are scarce and tables are high dimensional.

# Discussion, Limitations & Future Directions  

In this concluding chapter we reflect on the empirical findings presented earlier, situate **FeatLLM** within the broader landscape of few‑shot tabular learning, and critically examine the boundaries of the current design.  We then outline concrete avenues for extending the framework, emphasizing richer feature families, deeper interpretability, and scalability to larger data regimes.  Throughout we remain faithful to the concepts and observations introduced in the paper.

## Strengths of the FeatLLM Framework  

The experimental results across thirteen heterogeneous tabular datasets demonstrate that **FeatLLM** consistently attains top‑rank performance in low‑shot settings.  Several factors underpin this success:

1. **Effective exploitation of LLM prior knowledge.**  
   By prompting the language model to first reason about causal tendencies between features and the target (Step 1 of the reasoning instruction), FeatLLM elicits rules that are anchored in world knowledge (e.g., “older individuals have higher risk of certain diseases”).  This prevents the model from over‑fitting to spurious patterns that may appear in a handful of labeled examples.

2. **Separation of feature engineering from inference.**  
   Once the LLM has supplied a set of binary decision rules, downstream inference is performed by a lightweight linear model with non‑negative weights (Eq. \(\ref{eq:infer_prob}\)).  Consequently, at test time no further LLM calls are required, yielding inference latency comparable to traditional classifiers such as Logistic Regression or Random Forests.

3. **Robustness through bagging and ensembling.**  
   By repeatedly sampling subsets of features or instances (bagging) and generating rule sets under a relatively high temperature, FeatLLM produces diverse weak learners.  Averaging their predictions mitigates occasional rule generation errors and alleviates prompt‑size constraints on datasets with many columns (e.g., *Communities* and *Myocardial*).

4. **Minimal computational budget.**  
   The entire pipeline requires only a few dozen API queries to the LLM (30 queries in the reported Adult experiment).  Training the downstream linear model is inexpensive (Adam optimizer with learning rate 0.01 for 200 epochs).  Hence FeatLLM can be deployed even when only inference‑only LLM APIs are available.

5. **High-quality feature generation.**  
   The AUROC analysis shows that most generated binary features have predictive power exceeding 0.5 before any weight tuning, and irrelevant rules are automatically down‑weighted during training (“After tuning” column in Table \(\ref{tab:quality}\)).  This confirms that the rule extraction process reliably captures salient relationships.

Collectively, these strengths position FeatLLM as a practical solution for real‑world applications where labeling cost is prohibitive and access to fine‑tunable LLMs is restricted.

## Limitations  

Despite its advantages, FeatLLM exhibits several constraints that arise from design choices made to accommodate low‑shot scenarios and API‑only LLM access.

### 1. Dependence on Binary Rule Representation  

The current implementation translates every extracted rule into a single binary indicator (rule satisfied or not).  While this yields interpretable features and simplifies downstream modeling, it may discard richer information such as confidence scores, degree of violation, or continuous thresholds embedded in natural‑language expressions (“greater than 5”).  Consequently, subtle quantitative nuances can be lost.

### 2. Fixed Number of Rules per Class  

For each class we request exactly ten rules from the LLM.  This heuristic balances prompt length against expressive capacity, but it imposes an artificial ceiling on the number of potentially useful criteria, especially for complex tasks with many interacting variables.  Increasing the rule count inflates the dimensionality of \(\mathbf{z}_k^i\) and can lead to over‑fitting in extreme low‑shot regimes.

### 3. Low‑Shot Focus  

FeatLLM is explicitly evaluated with \(k \leq 8\) labeled examples per task.  The weight‑tuning stage relies on enough data points to estimate non‑negative importance weights; when \(k\) grows substantially (hundreds or thousands), a linear model may become insufficiently expressive compared to tree ensembles or deep networks that can exploit higher‑order interactions among raw features.

### 4. Reliance on Prompt Engineering  

The quality of extracted rules hinges on carefully crafted prompts: basic information description, reasoning instruction, and response instruction.  Slight variations in wording or order of demonstrations can affect output consistency—an effect noted by prior work on LLM self‑consistency \cite{wang2022self}.  Users must therefore devote effort to prompt design for each new domain.

### 5. Sensitivity to Noisy Feature Descriptions  

The parsing pipeline assumes that feature type annotations (numeric vs categorical) are accurate and that their textual definitions are trustworthy.  In domains where metadata are incomplete or erroneous, the LLM may produce malformed conditions (“less than ‘high’”) that hinder automatic code generation despite fallback parsing via the LLM itself.

### 6. Limited Interpretability Beyond Rules  

Although each binary feature corresponds directly to a human‑readable rule, the downstream linear weights obscure how multiple rules combine when predicting a class probability (the softmax aggregates logits across classes).  Moreover, ensemble averaging further dilutes traceability to any single rule set.

## Future Directions  

Addressing the above limitations opens numerous research pathways that retain FeatLLM’s core philosophy—leveraging LLMs as *feature engineers*—while broadening applicability and interpretability.

### A. Enriching Feature Families  

1. **Continuous Transformations:**  
   Instead of binarizing every condition, we could ask the LLM to output parametric functions (e.g., “\(x_3 / (x_5 + 1)\)” or “\(\log(x_2)\)”) that map raw columns onto real-valued engineered features.  Parsing such expressions would involve symbolic computation libraries (e.g., `sympy`) rather than solely code generation.

2. **Probabilistic Rules:**  
   The LLM could be prompted to provide confidence estimates (“with probability ≈ 0.8”) alongside each rule; these confidences could serve as soft weights before learning another layer of calibration.

3. **Higher‑Order Interactions:**  
   By allowing logical combinations beyond simple conjunctions (e.g., “(Age > 60) AND (Cholesterol > 200)”), we could generate composite binary features that capture interactions without requiring an explicit tree learner.

### B. Adaptive Rule Quantity  

A dynamic stopping criterion could be introduced where the LLM continues emitting rules until a marginal utility threshold is met (e.g., diminishing increase in validation AUROC).  This would tailor rule count to dataset complexity while preserving prompt length via incremental bagging.

### C. Scaling Beyond Low‑Shot Regimes  

When more labeled data become available:

- **Replace Linear Aggregation** with a shallow neural network or gradient boosted trees applied to the engineered feature matrix \(\mathbf{Z}\).  
- **Iterative Feature Refinement:** After an initial round of rule generation and weight training, feed back high‑weight features as additional context into a second LLM query to refine or specialize rules further.

### D. Automated Prompt Optimization  

Meta‑learning techniques could be employed to automatically discover effective prompt templates for a given domain; reinforcement learning over prompt space has shown promise in other LLM settings and could reduce manual engineering burden.

### E. Robust Parsing Pipelines  

While leveraging the LLM itself for parsing mitigates syntactic noise, incorporating a grammar‑aware parser (e.g., using abstract syntax trees) could provide stronger guarantees about correctness, especially when dealing with mathematically intensive conditions or nested logical expressions.

### F. Enhanced Interpretability Interfaces  

1. **Rule Attribution:** Extend Shapley value concepts \cite{lundberg2017unified} to attribute predictions back to individual rules across ensembles, yielding per‑sample explanations that highlight which criteria were decisive.

2. **Visualization Dashboards:** Present users with aggregated statistics such as rule frequency across ensembles, average learned weight per rule type, and confusion matrices conditioned on rule satisfaction.

### G: Mitigating Societal Biases  

Since FeatLLM imports world knowledge directly from pretrained LLMs, any embedded prejudices may manifest in generated rules (e.g., attributing health risks disproportionately to demographic groups). Systematic audits—testing generated rule sets on curated bias benchmarks—and incorporating fairness constraints during weight training constitute essential safeguards for deployment in sensitive sectors like finance or healthcare.

## Concluding Remarks  

FeatLLM illustrates that large language models need not be invoked at every prediction step; instead they can act as powerful *knowledge extractors* that synthesize domain‐relevant criteria from only a few labeled instances.  By converting these criteria into binary features amenable to simple linear models—and stabilizing them through bagged ensembling—the approach achieves state‑of‑the‑art performance while remaining computationally lightweight and compatible with inference‑only APIs.

Future work will focus on broadening the expressiveness of engineered features, automating prompt design, scaling to richer data regimes, and embedding rigorous interpretability and fairness mechanisms.  Pursuing these directions will further cement FeatLLM’s role as a practical bridge between foundation language models and real‑world tabular decision making.
