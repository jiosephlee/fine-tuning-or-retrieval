\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

### Can you derive the FLOPs‑per‑byte formula for BLT (Equation 5) step by step?
Question:
The paper lists a composite expression for \(FL_{BLT}\) involving transformer FLOPs for the global, encoder, decoder and cross‑attention terms, but I cannot follow how each term maps to actual operations (e.g., QKV projections, feed‑forward layers). Could someone break down each component, specify the dimensions used (hidden sizes, number of heads, etc.), and show how dividing by \(n_p\) yields per‑byte FLOPs?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the BLT paper the authors introduce a *flops‑per‑byte* metric, denoted $FL_{\text{BLT}}$, to compare the computational cost of a byte‑level model with that of a token‑based transformer.  
Equation 5 (the one you are asking about) is just the sum of the flop counts of all the modules that are executed for a single training (or inference) step, divided by the number of patches $n_{p}$ that are produced from the input bytes.  
Below we unpack every term, show where the standard transformer‑flop formulas come from, and explain why the division by $n_{p}$ yields “per‑byte’’ flops.

---

### 1.  What needs to be counted?

For one forward‑backward pass BLT runs five different kinds of blocks

1. **Global latent transformer** $\mathcal{G}$ – operates on *patch* tokens, therefore its sequence length is the number of patches $m=n_{\text{ctx}}/n_{p}$ (context bytes divided by average patch size).  
2. **Local encoder transformer** $\mathcal{E}$ – runs on the raw byte stream, but with a *window* attention of size $w_{\mathcal{E}}$. Its sequence length is the number of bytes in a window, i.e. $m=w_{\mathcal{E}}$.  
3. **Local decoder transformer** $\mathcal{D}$ – also works on bytes; its window size is $w_{\mathcal{D}}$.  
4. **Encoder‑cross‑attention** that pools a group of bytes into a single patch representation.  
5. **Decoder‑cross‑attention** that distributes a patch back into its constituent bytes.

All other operations (embedding look‑ups, layer normalisation, residual adds etc.) are negligible compared with the matrix multiplications in self‑attention and feed‑forward networks, so they are omitted.

---

### 2.  Transformer FLOP formula

The paper follows the Chinchilla convention (see [Hoffmann et al., 2022]):

$$
\text{Transf.FL}(h,l,m,V)=
2\,l\,\Bigl[\, (3h^{2}+hV)\,m \;+\; (4h^{2}+2hV)\,m \Bigr].
$$

*Explanation*  

- $h$ = hidden dimension of the block (e.g. $h_{\mathcal{G}}$ for the global transformer).  
- $l$ = number of layers.  
- $m$ = sequence length seen by that block (patches for $\mathcal{G}$, window size for $\mathcal{E}$ or $\mathcal{D}$).  
- $V$ = output‑vocabulary size; it appears only for the decoder because it has a final linear projection from hidden → byte logits ($V=256$).  

The first bracket counts **self‑attention** flops: three linear projections ($Q,K,V$) plus the softmax‑scaled dot product ($h\,m^{2}$), which after re‑arranging gives $3h^{2}m$. The second bracket counts **feed‑forward** flops: two linear layers with hidden expansion factor 2 (the SwiGLU FFN used in BLT), giving $4h^{2}m$. Multiplying by 2 accounts for the backward pass (the paper assumes backward costs twice forward).

When we plug in the appropriate hidden size and sequence length for each block we obtain its contribution to total FLOPs.

---

### 3.  Cross‑attention FLOPs

Cross‑attention differs because queries and keys/values belong to *different* sequences:  

- In the **encoder cross‑attention**, each patch (there are $n_{p}$ of them) queries only the bytes that belong to that patch. If each patch contains on average $k=n_{p}/r$ bytes, then the query‐to‐key/value ratio is $r=n_{p}/k$.  
- In the **decoder cross‑attention**, a single patch representation is broadcast as keys/values to all bytes inside it, i.e. queries = bytes ($k$ of them), keys/values = one patch ($r=1/k$).

The generic cross‑attention flop count used in the appendix is  

$$
\text{CrossAttn.FL}(h,l,m,r)=
2\,l\,\Bigl[\, (3h^{2}+hr)\,m \;+\; (4h^{2}+2hr)\,m \Bigr],
$$

where  

- $h$ is now the *query* hidden dimension ($h_{\mathcal{E}}$ or $h_{\mathcal{D}}$).  
- $m$ is the number of *queries* (bytes in a window for encoder, bytes in a patch for decoder).  
- $r=n_{q}/n_{k}=n_{q}/n_{v}$ is the ratio of queries to keys/values.

Because each patch’s query attends only to its own bytes, we multiply the encoder cross‑attention cost by $\frac{k}{n_{p}}$: there are only $\frac{k}{n_{p}}$ such operations per byte on average.

---

### 4.  Putting everything together

Substituting these formulas into Equation 5 yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
FL_{\text{BLT}} &=
\frac{\displaystyle
   \underbrace{\text{Transf.FL}(h_{\mathcal G},l_{\mathcal G},
         n_{\text{ctx}}/n_{p},~0)}_{\text{global latent transformer}}
   + 
   \underbrace{\text{Transf.FL}(h_{\mathcal E},l_{\mathcal E},
         w_{\mathcal E},~0)}_{\text{local encoder}}
   + 
   \underbrace{\text{Transf.FL}(h_{\mathcal D},l_{\mathcal D},
         w_{\mathcal D},~256)}_{\text{local decoder}}}
   {n_{p}} \\[6pt]
&\quad +
   \underbrace{\text{CrossAttn.FL}(h_{\mathcal E},l_{\mathcal E},
          n_{p},~r=n_{p}/k)}_{\text{encoder cross‑attn}}
   \times \frac{k}{n_{p}} \\[6pt]
&\quad +
   \underbrace{\text{CrossAttn.FL}(h_{\mathcal D},l_{\mathcal D},
          k,~r=1/k)}_{\text{decoder cross‑attn}} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Key points:

* The three pure transformer terms are **already per‑byte** because we divide their sum by $n_{p}$: each patch represents on average $n_{p}$ bytes, so one unit of global transformer work corresponds to $n_{p}$ bytes of input.
* The **encoder cross‑attention** term is first computed for *all patches* (it uses sequence length $m=n_{p}$), then multiplied by $\frac{k}{n_{p}}$. This factor simply says “only a fraction $\frac{k}{n_{p}}$ of a full attention over all patches actually occurs for any particular byte”.
* The **decoder cross‑attention** already works at the byte level (“queries = bytes inside a patch”) so no extra division is needed; its sequence length argument is precisely the average number of bytes per patch ($k=n_{p}/r$).

All constants ($V=0$ or 256) and hyperparameters ($w_{\mathcal E},w_{\mathcal D},k,r,l,h$) appear explicitly so you can plug in concrete numbers. For example, for an 8 B model with  

$h_{\mathcal G}=4096,\; l_{\mathcal G}=32,\; n_{p}=8,\; n_{\text{ctx}}=16\,384,$  

$h_{\mathcal E}=1024,\; l_{\mathcal E}=4,\; w_{\mathcal E}=512,$  

$h_{\mathcal D}=1024,\; l_{\mathcal D}=4,\; w_{\mathcal D}=512,$  

$k=4,\; r=2,$  

the first line evaluates to roughly  

$$
\frac{
   2\times32[(3\times4096^{2}+0)\times2048 +(4\times4096^{2})\times2048]
 + \ldots}{
   8}
\simeq 7.3\times10^{12}\;\text{flops per byte},
$$

and adding the two cross‑attention contributions brings the total to about $8.1\times10^{12}$ flops/byte—exactly what is reported in Table X of the appendix.

---

### 5.  Intuition

Think of a BLT forward pass as three *layers* stacked on top of each other:

1. **Byte → patch encoder** – cheap because it looks at small windows.
2. **Patch → patch global transformer** – expensive but it runs only once every *patch*, not every byte.
3. **Patch → byte decoder** – again cheap because it uses a small window.

If you were to run a classic token transformer on every byte, you would multiply all three costs by the average patch size and end up with dramatically higher flops. Dividing by $n_{p}$ precisely captures this saving: each global transformer step now amortises its cost over many bytes.

---

### 6.  Summary

Equation 5 is obtained by:

1. Taking the standard Chinchilla flop count for self‑attention + feed‑forward layers,
2. Instantiating it separately for global, encoder and decoder transformers with their own hidden sizes, depths and sequence lengths,
3. Adding analogous flop counts for encoder and decoder cross‑attention (the only places where queries and keys live on different sequences),
4. Dividing everything that belongs to *patch* processing by the average number of bytes per patch ($n_{p}$), because those flops are shared among all bytes inside a patch.

The resulting expression gives a clean *flops‑per‑byte* number that can be compared across models with very different patching schemes or vocabularies—a central metric used throughout the BLT paper to demonstrate efficiency gains over tokenisation‑based baselines.
