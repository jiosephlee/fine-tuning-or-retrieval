\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

### How are hash‑based n‑gram embeddings integrated with the byte embedding, and why is RollPolyHash used?
Question:
Section 3.1.1 defines \(e_i = x_i + \sum_{n=3}^{8} E_n^{hash}(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}))\). I am confused about three points: (1) Are the hash embeddings learned jointly with the rest of the model? (2) Since multiple n‑grams overlap, does each byte receive many hash contributions? (3) What are the properties of RollPolyHash that make it suitable for this purpose compared to a simpler modulo hash?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the Byte Latent Transformer the representation that is fed to the local encoder at position *i* is  

$$
e_i \;=\; x_i \;+\;\sum_{n=3}^{8}\!E^{\text{hash}}_{n}\!\bigl(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n})\bigr),
\qquad 
\text{where }\;g_{i,n}= \{b_{i-n+1},\dots ,b_i\}.
$$

Below we unpack what this formula means and why the authors chose the particular hash function **RollPolyHash**.

---

### 1. Are the hash‑embeddings learned together with the rest of the model?

Yes.  For each *n*‑gram length *n* (3 … 8) a separate embedding table  

$$
E^{\text{hash}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\,E^{\text{hash}}_{n}\,|\times h_{\mathcal E}}
$$

is instantiated (the paper uses a size of 500 000 entries for each table).  
During the forward pass the *n*‑gram at position *i* is hashed to an integer in  
$[0,|E^{\text{hash}}_{n}|-1]$ and the corresponding row of $E^{\text{hash}}_{n}$ is looked up.  
All rows of all six tables are parameters that receive gradients from the language‑model loss, exactly like the ordinary byte embedding matrix $x_i$.  Hence the hash embeddings are trained **jointly** with the encoder, decoder and global transformer; no pre‑training or fixed vectors are used.

---

### 2. Does a byte receive many hash contributions because of overlapping *n*‑grams?

Exactly.  For a given position *i$ the model forms six overlapping *n*‑grams:

* a 3‑gram ending at *i*: $b_{i-2}b_{i-1}b_i$  
* a 4‑gram ending at *i*: $b_{i-3}b_{i-2}b_{i-1}b_i$  
⋯  
* an 8‑gram ending at *i*: $b_{i-7}\dots b_i$.

Each of those six *n*‑grams contributes one vector from its own hash table, and all six vectors are summed and added to the plain byte embedding $x_i$.  Thus a single byte participates in up to six “contextual’’ embeddings, providing a cheap way to inject local substring information without running a separate convolution or RNN.  The sum is then normalised by dividing by the number of terms (seven, i.e. six hashes + the byte embedding) before being passed to the encoder layers.

---

### 3. Why RollPolyHash instead of a naïve modulo hash?

RollPolyHash is a rolling polynomial hash defined (Appendix C) as

$$
H(g)=\Bigl(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} c_k\,a^{k}\Bigr)\bmod P,
$$

where $c_k$ are the integer values of the bytes in the *n*‑gram, $a$ is a small odd base (e.g. 257) and $P$ is a large prime (the size of the embedding table).  Its desirable properties for BLT are:

| Property | Why it matters for BLT |
|----------|------------------------|
| **Deterministic but order‑sensitive** | A different ordering of bytes changes the hash value, so “abc’’ and “cba’’ map to distinct rows – crucial because byte order encodes syntax. |
| **Rolling (constant‑time update)** | When moving from position *i* to *i+1* we can compute $\text{Hash}(g_{i+1,n})$ from $\text{Hash}(g_{i,n})$ by removing the oldest byte and adding the new one, avoiding recomputation of a full polynomial for every overlap. This gives an **$O(1)$** per‑byte cost despite six overlapping n‑grams. |
| **Good distribution** | The polynomial modulo a prime yields a uniform spread over $[0,P)$, reducing collisions even with a modest table size (500 k). Fewer collisions mean that semantically different n‑grams are less likely to share an embedding, preserving discriminative power. |
| **Simple arithmetic** | It uses only integer multiplications and additions, which are fast on CPUs/GPUs and fit easily into existing tensor kernels without extra memory lookups beyond a single table index. |

A simple “take the last few bytes and mod’’ hash would be cheaper but would collapse many distinct n‑grams together (high collision rate) and would not support rolling updates, leading either to poorer representation quality or higher per‑byte overhead.

---

### Putting it together

During training each byte’s representation $e_i$ already carries information about its recent history through the summed hash embeddings.  Because these embeddings are learned end‑to‑end, the model can decide—for example—what patterns are useful for predicting the next byte and adjust their vectors accordingly.  The rolling polynomial hash makes this feasible computationally: despite six overlapping n‑grams per position, computing all six indices costs only a handful of integer operations per byte, keeping the encoder lightweight while still enriching it with sub‑byte context.

Thus:

1. **Joint learning** – hash tables are optimized together with all other parameters.  
2. **Multiple contributions** – every byte receives up to six contextual vectors (plus its own).  
3. **RollPolyHash** – provides order sensitivity, uniform collision behaviour, and an efficient rolling update, which a naive modulo hash cannot match.

These design choices enable BLT’s encoder to capture rich local n‑gram information without blowing up memory or compute, contributing to the strong scaling results reported in the paper.
