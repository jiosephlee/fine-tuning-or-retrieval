\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Does DPO guarantee convergence to the same optimal policy as RLHF with PPO?
Question:
Both DPO and PPO optimise Eq. (3) under the same KL constraint, yet DPO replaces RL with a cross‑entropy loss. Under what assumptions (e.g., expressive policy class, exact gradient estimation) can we guarantee that DPO will converge to the same optimum as PPO? Are there known cases where DPO could get stuck in a suboptimal stationary point while PPO would not?
Answer:
**Answer**

Both Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) and the standard PPO‑based RLHF solve the same *KL‑constrained* reward‑maximisation problem

\[
\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal D,\;y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}[\,r_{\phi}(x,y)\,]
-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl[\pi(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr] ,
\tag{1}
\]

but they take very different optimisation routes.  PPO treats (1) as a reinforcement‑learning objective and estimates the gradient by sampling trajectories, a value‑function baseline, and clipping.  DPO observes that (1) has a *closed‑form* optimal policy

\[
\pi^{*}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\;
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r_{\phi}(x,y)\Bigr),
\tag{2}
\]

and then rewrites the Bradley–Terry preference model in terms of this policy.  Substituting (2) into the likelihood of the human preference data eliminates the unknown partition function \(Z(x)\) and yields the binary‑cross‑entropy loss (Eq. 9 in the paper)

\[
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)
=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal D}
 \log\sigma\Bigl(
   \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
 - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
 \Bigr).
\tag{3}
\]

Optimising (3) therefore *directly* searches for a policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) that satisfies the preference model, i.e. a policy that is a solution of (1).  Under this re‑parameterisation the optimisation problem is exactly the same as PPO’s, only the gradient estimator is different.

---

### When do the two methods converge to the same optimum?

The paper shows that convergence to the same optimum is guaranteed under three fairly standard assumptions:

1. **Expressive policy class** – The parametrised policy family \(\{\pi_{\theta}\}\) must be rich enough to contain (or approximate arbitrarily well) the optimal policy (2).  In practice this means using a transformer whose capacity is not a bottleneck, just as with PPO.

2. **Exact maximum‑likelihood estimation of preferences** – If we could compute the expectation in (3) with respect to the true data distribution (or an infinitely large i.i.d. sample), then (3) is exactly the negative log‑likelihood of the Bradley–Terry model with reward \(r(x,y)=\beta \log (\pi(y|x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x))\).  Lemma 1 in the paper states that any reward in the *equivalence class* \(r'(x,y)=r(x,y)+f(x)\) induces exactly the same preference probabilities, and Theorem 1 proves that every reward class can be expressed by some policy through (2).  Consequently, maximizing (3) is *identical* to maximizing (1).

3. **Unbiased gradient estimation** – If gradients of (3) are computed without bias—e.g. by using all preference pairs in a minibatch or by taking expectations analytically—then stochastic gradient descent will converge to a stationary point of the true loss, which coincides with a stationary point of (1).  This mirrors the usual requirement for PPO convergence: unbiased policy‑gradient estimates plus diminishing step sizes.

When these conditions hold, DPO’s optimisation trajectory may be different from PPO’s because DPO never samples from its current policy; it only evaluates log‑probabilities of already sampled pairs.  Nevertheless, both algorithms are minimising the same convex (in each pairwise log‑odds) loss surface, so any global optimum of one is a global optimum of the other.

---

### Can DPO get stuck in a sub‑optimal stationary point while PPO would not?

Yes – the theoretical equivalence does **not** guarantee that every local optimum of DPO is also reachable by PPO, nor that PPO cannot escape a local optimum that traps DPO.  The divergence can arise from two practical sources:

* **Finite‑sample bias** – DPO treats each preference pair as a deterministic training example.  With a limited dataset, certain regions of the output space may be under‑represented, causing the cross‑entropy loss to have spurious local minima.  PPO, by contrast, samples from its own evolving policy and therefore sees *off‑policy* examples that can provide additional gradient signals and sometimes push it out of such minima.

* **Non‑convexity due to model parametrisation** – Although Eq. (3) is convex in the *log‑odds* \(\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y_w)-\log \pi_{\theta}(y_l))\), it becomes non‑convex once these odds are expressed through a deep neural network.  Both methods inherit this non‑convexity, but their optimisation dynamics differ: PPO uses clipped surrogate objectives and an explicit KL penalty that can smooth the landscape, whereas DPO’s loss lacks an explicit KL term because it is already baked into the importance weight \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))\).  In pathological cases this can lead DPO to over‑emphasise easy pairs and ignore harder ones, settling in a suboptimal basin.

Empirically, the authors report that DPO’s frontier dominates PPO’s on several benchmarks (sentiment control, summarisation, dialogue).  However they also note that if one removes the weighting factor \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))\) (the “naïve probability‑ratio” variant), DPO collapses to an unlikelihood update and fails to improve—illustrating how delicate gradient weighting influences whether DPO finds the global optimum.

---

### Summary

* Under an expressive policy class, exact maximum‑likelihood training on an arbitrarily large preference set, and unbiased gradient estimates, DPO **exactly** solves the same KL‑constrained reward maximisation problem as PPO; therefore both converge to the same optimal policy.
* In realistic settings with finite data and stochastic optimisation, DPO can end up in sub‑optimal stationary points because its loss is evaluated only on observed pairs and because non‑convexity may create extra local minima not present in PPO’s surrogate objective.
* Nevertheless, because DPO eliminates sampling noise and KL‐penalty clipping required by PPO, it often reaches higher reward for a given KL budget with far less hyperparameter tuning—a fact confirmed by the experimental results in the paper.
