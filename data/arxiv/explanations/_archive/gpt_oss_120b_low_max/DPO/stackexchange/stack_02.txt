\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why is $eta$ interpreted as a KL‑constraint strength rather than a temperature?
Question:
The paper introduces $eta$ in Eq. (3) as the coefficient of the KL divergence regularizer and later uses it in the DPO objective (Eq. 12) inside a logistic. In many RL‑HF works $eta$ is called a temperature controlling stochasticity of the policy. How should I think about $eta$ in DPO – is it a temperature, a KL penalty weight, or both? How does changing $eta$ affect the trade‑off between reward maximisation and staying close to the reference policy?
Answer:
In the DPO paper the scalar that appears in front of every KL‑term is denoted by $\beta$ (the manuscript sometimes writes it as “$\eta$”).  
It is introduced in the **constrained‑RL objective**  

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\;
\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \pi_\theta}[\,r(x,y)\,]
 \;-\;\beta \, \mathrm{D}_{\text{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr) .
\tag{1}
\]

Equation (1) tells us exactly what $\beta$ does: it balances two competing forces

* **Reward maximisation** – the first term pushes the policy toward actions that obtain high reward.
* **Staying close to the reference policy** – the second term penalises divergence from the SFT (or any chosen) reference distribution.

Because the KL term appears *linearly* with coefficient $\beta$, this coefficient is a **penalty weight**, not a temperature in the usual sense of “softening” a distribution.  

---

### Why it looks like a temperature inside the DPO loss

When we solve (1) analytically for the optimal policy we obtain  

\[
\pi^{*}(y|x)=
\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\bigr),
\tag{2}
\]

with $Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp(\tfrac1\beta r(x,y))$.  
If we take logs of (2) and rearrange, we get  

\[
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}+\beta \log Z(x).
\tag{3}
\]

In (3) the factor $\beta$ multiplies a log‑ratio of probabilities.  Because a log‑ratio looks like an *energy* and a scalar multiplying it looks like an inverse temperature, one can be tempted to interpret $\beta^{-1}$ as a temperature.  The paper exploits exactly this algebraic form to eliminate the unknown reward function and write the human‑preference likelihood directly in terms of the policy:

\[
p^{*}(y_w \succ y_l|x)
= \sigma\!\Bigl(\beta
   \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
  -\beta
   \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\Bigr).
\tag{4}
\]

Notice that **the same $\beta$ that weighed the KL penalty in (1) now appears inside the logistic**.  This does **not** turn it into a temperature that controls stochasticity at generation time; rather it simply rescales the *log‑odds* that DPO uses to fit the preference data.  Larger $\beta$ makes the logistic more “sharp”: a given difference in log‑probability ratios will produce a larger probability of matching the human preference. Conversely, smaller $\beta$ smooths the logistic, giving less weight to each individual comparison.

---

### Practical effect of changing $\beta$

* **Increasing $\beta$**  
  * The KL term in (1) becomes *more costly*, so during optimisation the policy is forced to stay nearer to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  
  * In (4) the same increase makes the loss react more strongly to any mismatch between preferred and dispreferred log‑ratios, which effectively *amplifies* each training example’s gradient.  Empirically this yields a higher reward for a given KL cost, i.e. you move further up the reward–KL frontier.

* **Decreasing $\beta$**  
  * The KL penalty is relaxed; the optimiser can move farther away from the reference model.  
  * The logistic in (4) becomes flatter, so each example contributes a milder gradient signal.  The learned policy will typically achieve higher KL divergence while gaining only modest additional reward.

Thus $\beta$ controls **the trade‑off** exactly as a KL‑constraint weight does: you pick a value according to how conservative you want to be with respect to the SFT model.  The “temperature” intuition is helpful for seeing why it appears inside a sigmoid, but it should not be confused with sampling temperature at generation time—those temperatures are separate hyper‑parameters (often denoted $T_{\text{gen}}$) that affect how stochasticly you draw tokens from a *fixed* policy.

---

### Summary

* In DPO, $\eta (= \beta)$ is fundamentally **the KL‑constraint coefficient** from the original RLHF objective.  
* Through algebraic re‑parameterisation it shows up inside the logistic that defines the preference likelihood, which can give the impression of a temperature but is merely rescaling the log‑odds used for learning.  
* Raising $\eta$ enforces stronger adherence to the reference policy while also making each preference example exert stronger influence on learning; lowering it relaxes that adherence and yields more exploratory policies.  

So treat $\eta$ as **the weight on the KL regulariser**, aware that its appearance inside Eq. (12) is a mathematical consequence rather than an additional temperature hyper‑parameter governing generation stochasticity.
