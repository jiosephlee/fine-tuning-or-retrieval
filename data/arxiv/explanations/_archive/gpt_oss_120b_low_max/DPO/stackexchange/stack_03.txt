\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What exactly is meant by “reference policy” $
abla_{ref}$ and how is it obtained when no SFT model exists?
Question:
The DPO algorithm requires a reference policy $
abla_{ref}$ for computing importance weights (Eq. 12). The paper says to set $
abla_{ref}=\pi^{SFT}$ if available, otherwise to train it by maximum likelihood on preferred completions $y_w$. Could you clarify why training on only $y_w$ yields a good approximation of the original SFT distribution, and what practical differences arise if we instead use a randomly initialized model as $
abla_{ref}$?
Answer:
In the DPO paper the *reference policy* $ \pi_{\text{ref}} $ plays exactly the same rôle that an SFT model plays in conventional RL‑HF: it defines the distribution that the fine‑tuned policy is not allowed to drift too far from.  
Mathematically the RL‑HF objective is  

$$
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal D,\;y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}[\,r_{\phi}(x,y)\,]
 \;-\;\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\;\|\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr),
$$

so any change of the policy is penalised by the KL term with respect to $ \pi_{\text{ref}} $.  When an SFT model $ \pi^{\text{SFT}} $ is available the natural choice is simply  

$$
\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi^{\text{SFT}},
$$

because that model already represents the “base” behaviour of the LM (the distribution that generated the preference pairs in the dataset).  In this case the importance weights that appear in Eq. 12 are exact – they are ratios of probabilities under the current policy and under the SFT model.

### When no SFT model exists

Often we only have a set of preference triples $ \mathcal D=\{(x,y_w,y_l)\} $ but we never trained a supervised‑fine‑tuned LM on the same domain.  The paper therefore proposes to **learn a proxy reference policy** by maximum‑likelihood on the *preferred* completions only:

$$
\pi_{\text{ref}} = \arg\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w)\sim\mathcal D}\bigl[\,\log \pi(y_w|x)\,\bigr].
\tag{1}
$$

Why does training on $y_w$ alone give a reasonable approximation of the original SFT distribution?

1. **The preference data were generated by sampling from an SFT model.**  
   In standard RL‑HF pipelines each prompt $x$ is given to a pre‑existing SFT model, which produces *two* continuations that are later labelled as “winner’’ ($y_w$) and “loser’’ ($y_l$). Consequently, *both* $y_w$ and $y_l$ are draws from the same underlying distribution $ \pi^{\text{SFT}}(\cdot|x) $. The winners are simply a subset of those draws that received higher human scores.

2. **Maximum‑likelihood on a subset of samples does not change the underlying distribution.**  
   If we had access to *all* samples from $ \pi^{\text{SFT}} $ then Eq. (1) would recover exactly that distribution. Using only the winners introduces a bias toward higher‑probability (or higher‑quality) regions, but because every winner is still **a sample** from $ \pi^{\text{SFT}} $, fitting a language model by MLE to them aligns its parameters with the part of the SFT space where good responses live while preserving roughly the same conditional token probabilities elsewhere. Empirically this yields a reference that is close enough for two reasons:
   * The KL penalty in Eq. (4) discourages large deviations, so even a slightly shifted reference still pulls the fine‑tuned policy toward its own mode.
   * The importance weight in DPO,
     $$
       w(x,y)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)},
     $$
     only needs *relative* probabilities between preferred and dispreferred completions; as long as both are evaluated under the same reference, any systematic shift cancels out.

3. **Practical convenience.**  
   Training a full SFT model on large amounts of demonstration data can be costly or impossible when only preference data exist (e.g. synthetic preferences generated by another LM). Fitting a reference with Eq. (1) is cheap—it uses exactly the same data that DPO will later consume and requires no extra annotation.

### What happens if we use a randomly initialised model as $ \pi_{\text{ref}} $?

If we set $ \pi_{\text{ref}} $ to an untrained language model, its token distribution bears no relation to how the preference pairs were generated. This has two concrete consequences:

* **Incorrect importance weights.**  
  The ratio $ \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - 
    \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)} $ becomes dominated by
  fluctuations of the random reference rather than by genuine differences between
  $y_w$ and $y_l$. The sigmoid term in Eq. (12) will therefore assign high weight
  to examples where the random reference erroneously gives low probability to
  either completion, leading to noisy or even harmful updates.

* **Loss of KL regularisation.**  
  The KL term in Eq. (4) would now measure divergence from a near‑uniform policy,
  essentially forcing the fine‑tuned model to stay close to randomness instead of
  staying close to its original knowledge‑rich behaviour. The resulting policy
  often collapses to generating very short or repetitive text because those have
  high probability under an untrained model’s shallow unigram distribution.

Empirically, papers that ablate this component (see Appendix B of DPO) report severe degradation: reward scores drop dramatically while KL values become meaningless because they no longer reflect distance from an informative base model.

### Summary

- The reference policy $ \pi_{\text{ref}} $ is meant to approximate the *base* LM that originally produced the preference data.
- When an SFT model exists we can reuse it directly.
- When no SFT exists we train a proxy by maximum likelihood on only the preferred completions; this works because those completions are themselves samples from the unknown base distribution, so fitting them recovers a good‑enough approximation.
- Using a random initialisation instead would corrupt importance weights and destroy the KL regularisation, leading to unstable training and poor final performance.

Thus, even a modestly trained “reference’’ obtained from Eq. (1) provides precisely what DPO needs: a stationary baseline against which preferred vs. dispreferred completions can be compared, without requiring any additional supervised data beyond what is already present in the preference set.
