\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How would one extend DPO to multi‑turn dialogue where preferences depend on entire conversation histories?
Question:
The current formulation assumes independent $(x, y)$ pairs and binary preferences between two completions. For multi‑turn dialogue, preferences may involve sequences of turns. How can the DPO loss be adapted to handle such sequential preference data? Would we need a different preference model (e.g., Plackett‑Luce over trajectories) or can we still use Eq. (12) with minor modifications?
Answer:
**Answer**

The DPO loss (Eq.\ 12) was derived for a single‑turn setting in which every data point is a triple \((x,y_w,y_l)\) and the human preference model is a Bradley–Terry (or, more generally, a Plackett‑Luce) distribution over *pairs* of completions.  
When we move to multi‑turn dialogue the basic objects change: the context is no longer a single prompt \(x\) but a **conversation history** \(h=\{(u_1,a_1),\dots,(u_{t},a_{t})\}\) and a preference may be expressed over **entire trajectories** of future turns, e.g. “the continuation \((a_{t+1},a_{t+2},\dots ,a_{t+K})\) is better than the alternative continuation”.  The question is whether we can keep the same loss by simply replacing each token‑level probability \(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\) with a trajectory‑level probability \(\pi_\theta(\tau\mid h)\), or whether we must adopt a different preference model such as a Plackett‑Luce over rankings of trajectories.

Below I explain why only *minor* modifications to Eq.\ 12 are required, how the loss looks for whole‑trajectory preferences, and what practical considerations arise when applying DPO to multi‑turn dialogue.

---

### 1. Preference model for trajectories

The Bradley–Terry model used in the paper only needs **differences of rewards**:

\[
p^*(\tau_1\succ\tau_2\mid h)=\sigma\!\big(r^*(h,\tau_1)-r^*(h,\tau_2)\big).
\]

If we interpret a “reward” as the sum of per‑turn rewards,
\(r^*(h,\tau)=\sum_{k=1}^{K} r^*(h_{k},a_{k})\), the same functional form holds for any length‑\(K\) continuation.  The same reasoning that led from Eq.\ (5) to Eq.\ (9) in the paper therefore works unchanged: we replace the token‑level reward \(r(x,y)\) by a trajectory‑level reward \(r(h,\tau)\).

In practice we never need to materialise an explicit scalar reward; the DPO derivation shows that any reward in the same equivalence class can be written as

\[
r(h,\tau)=\beta\,\log\frac{\pi_\theta(\tau\mid h)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(\tau\mid h)}+c(h),
\]

where \(c(h)\) is a history‑dependent constant that cancels when we form differences.  Consequently the **preference probability depends only on the log‑probability ratios of the two trajectories under the current policy and the reference policy**, exactly as in Eq.\ (9).

---

### 2. From pairs to sequences of turns

Suppose we have a dataset of *trajectory* preferences

\[
\mathcal{D}^{\text{traj}}=\bigl\{\,h^{(i)},\,\tau_w^{(i)},\,\tau_l^{(i)}\,\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]

where each \(\tau_w^{(i)}=\{a_{t+1}^{w},\dots,a_{t+K}^{w}\}\) is judged better than \(\tau_l^{(i)}\).  
Because language models factorise over tokens,

\[
\pi_\theta(\tau\mid h)=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\pi_\theta(a_{t+k}\mid h,a_{t+1},\dots,a_{t+k-1}),
\]

the log‑probability ratio appearing in DPO is simply

```
Δ_i = β [ Σ_k log π_θ(a_k^w | context_k^w )
        – Σ_k log π_ref(a_k^w | context_k^w )
        – Σ_k log π_θ(a_k^l | context_k^l )
        + Σ_k log π_ref(a_k^l | context_k^l ) ] .
```

Putting this into the binary‑cross‑entropy form yields the **trajectory‑level DPO loss**

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}^{\,\text{traj}}(\theta)=
-\mathbb{E}_{(h,\tau_w,\tau_l)\sim \mathcal{D}^{\text{traj}}}
   \Bigl[ \log\sigma
   \bigl(
      \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(\tau_w|h)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(\tau_w|h)}
      - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(\tau_l|h)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(\tau_l|h)}
   \bigr)
   \Bigr].
}
\]

This is *exactly* Eq.\ 12 with \(x\) replaced by the history \(h\) and \(y_w,y_l\) replaced by entire continuations.  No new normalization term appears because, as in the single‑turn case, the history‑dependent constant \(c(h)\) cancels when taking differences.

If one wishes to train from **rankings of more than two continuations** (e.g. “rank three possible replies”), the same derivation extends to the Plackett‑Luce likelihood:

\[
p^*(\sigma(1)\succ\sigma(2)\succ\dots|\;h)
=
\prod_{j=1}^{M}
   \frac{\exp(r(h,\tau_{\sigma(j)}))}
        {\sum_{k=j}^{M}\exp(r(h,\tau_{\sigma(k)}))}.
\]

Substituting the reparameterisation \(r=\beta\, \log[\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}}]\) again removes all partition functions and yields a **cross‑entropy loss over rankings** (see Appendix C of the paper).  In practice it is often sufficient to decompose an \(M\)-rank observation into \({M \choose 2}\) pairwise comparisons and reuse Eq.\ 12; this is exactly what many preference datasets do.

---

### 3. Practical considerations

| Issue | How it appears in multi‑turn DPO | Recommended fix |
|------|----------------------------------|-----------------|
| **Long trajectories → vanishing probabilities** | The product of many token probs can become extremely small, making log ratios numerically unstable. | Compute Δ at token level (as shown above) and accumulate it incrementally; most implementations already do this because they back‑prop through each token’s log‑probability separately. |
| **Reference policy drift** | The reference model should still represent “the SFT policy before any alignment”. In dialogue we usually start from an SFT fine‑tuned on human demonstrations; that model becomes \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). | If no SFT exists, first train a *preferred‑FT* model on all human‑chosen turns (as done in Sec.\ 4), then treat it as \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). |
| **Credit assignment across turns** | The gradient weights each token by how much it contributes to misranking (the σ term in Eq.\ 10). When continuations differ early on, later tokens receive very little weight. | This is desirable: early missteps dominate overall preference. If you want more uniform credit, you can weight each token equally by adding a small temperature inside σ, but experiments in the paper show that keeping the σ weighting works best. |
| **Batching variable‑length trajectories** | Different examples may have different numbers of future turns. | Pad trajectories to the maximum length in the minibatch and mask out padded tokens when summing log probabilities; this follows standard language‑model training practice. |
| **Exploration vs exploitation (\(\beta\))** | Larger \(\beta\) reduces the KL penalty, allowing bigger shifts from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\); smaller \(\beta\) keeps you close to SFT but may underfit preferences that span several turns. | As for single turn, sweep \(\beta\) (e.g., 0.05–5). Empirically values around \(\beta≈0.5–1\) work well for dialogue with 2–3 future turns; reduce \(\beta\) if you observe catastrophic forgetting of style or factuality. |

---

### 4. Summary

* The core DPO objective does **not** require a fundamentally new preference model for multi‑turn dialogue.
* We simply replace prompts \(x\) with conversation histories \(h\) and completions \(y_w,y_l\) with full continuations (trajectories) \(\tau_w,\tau_l\).
* The Bradley–Terry/Plackett‑Luce formulation still applies because it depends only on differences of rewards, and those differences can be expressed as log‑probability ratios of entire trajectories under the current and reference policies.
* Practically one implements DPO exactly as in Eq.\ 12 but computes the ratios token‑wise over each turn; all other components—reference policy, \(\beta\) hyperparameter, weighting by σ—remain unchanged.
* If your dataset contains rankings of more than two continuations you may either decompose them into pairwise comparisons or use the Plackett‑Luce likelihood derived in Appendix C; both reduce to cross‑entropy losses without additional normalisation terms.

Thus, extending DPO to multi‑turn dialogue is conceptually straightforward: keep Eq.\ 12, treat histories as conditioning contexts, and treat whole continuations as atomic “answers”. The resulting trajectory‑level DPO loss retains all theoretical guarantees of the original method while handling exactly the kind of sequential preferences that arise in realistic dialogue datasets.
