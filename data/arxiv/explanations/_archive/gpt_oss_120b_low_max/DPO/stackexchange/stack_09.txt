\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why does DPO appear more robust to sampling temperature than PPO in the TL;DR experiments?
Question:
Figure 2 shows DPO’s win rate remains high across temperatures while PPO’s degrades sharply at higher temperatures. Is this robustness an intrinsic property of the DPO objective, or could it be due to differences in how temperature interacts with KL regularisation during training? What mechanisms could explain this behaviour?
Answer:
In the TL;DR experiments the **win‑rate** of a model is measured by sampling several completions at a given temperature and asking an evaluator (GPT‑4) which of two models’ outputs is better.  Figure 2 shows that, as the sampling temperature is increased, the win‑rate of the PPO‑trained policy drops dramatically, whereas the win‑rate of a DPO‑trained policy stays roughly constant.  
The cause of this difference can be understood by looking at what each algorithm actually **optimises** and how the sampling temperature interacts with that optimisation.

---

### What the temperature does

When we generate a text with temperature $\tau$ we sample from the model’s categorical distribution
$$
p_\tau(y\mid x)\;\propto\;\pi(y\mid x)^{1/\tau},
$$
so larger $\tau$ flattens the distribution and makes low‑probability tokens more likely to appear.  Consequently, high‑temperature samples are farther from the **mode** of the policy and contain more noise.

If a training objective has only a loose connection to the model’s *log‑probabilities* (as in PPO), increasing $\tau$ can push the generation into regions of the policy space where the learned reward estimate is unreliable, which in turn reduces win‑rate.  If, on the other hand, the objective directly ties the **relative log‑probabilities** of the preferred and dispreferred strings to a *deterministic* preference model, then changing $\tau$ does not disturb that relationship.

---

### PPO: reward + separate KL penalty

PPO optimises

$$
\max_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}
        \Bigl[r_\phi(x,y)-\beta\,\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\Bigr],
$$

where $r_\phi$ is a **learned reward model**.  
Two facts make PPO sensitive to temperature:

1. **Reward‑model variance:**  The reward model is trained on a finite set of preference pairs.  Its value is only well‑behaved near the points it has seen – typically close to the reference policy’s mode (the SFT model).  When $\tau$ is large, sampled completions wander far from those points, so $r_\phi(x,y)$ becomes noisy and often contradicts human preferences.  

2. **Separate KL term:**  The KL regulariser penalises deviation from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, but it does not *scale* with $\tau$.  In practice we tune $\beta$ to hit a target average KL (e.g., 3–12).  When $\tau$ grows, the effective KL between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ increases much faster than what $\beta$ was set for, so optimisation pushes the policy toward overly conservative behaviour (often reverting to the SFT mode), which manifests as a drop in win‑rate.

Thus PPO’s objective depends on an *external* estimate of reward that is unreliable off‑policy, and on a KL term that does not automatically adapt to temperature.

---

### DPO: directly parameterising preferences with log‑probability ratios

DPO replaces the reward model by the **implicit reward**
$$
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} .
$$

The loss is

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}=
 -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal D}
   \log\sigma\!\Bigl(
        \hat r_\theta(x,y_w)- \hat r_\theta(x,y_l)
      \Bigr).
$$

Two properties follow immediately:

1. **Exact preference modelling:**  Because $\hat r_\theta$ is defined *solely* by log‑probability ratios, any change in $\pi_\theta$ instantly changes its implicit reward in exactly the same way as it changes the probability of preferring $y_w$ over $y_l$.  There is no separate, noisy estimator that can become mismatched when we move off‑policy.

2. **KL regularisation baked into the loss:**  The term $\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$ already penalises large deviations from the reference policy for *every* token; this penalty scales linearly with any change in log‑probabilities caused by temperature.  Consequently, when we increase $\tau$, samples become more diverse, but their log‑probability ratio under DPO automatically reflects that increased uncertainty, keeping the effective KL budget roughly constant without any extra hyper‑parameter tuning.

Because DPO’s gradient (Eq. 10 in the paper)

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}
 = -\beta\, 
   \sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))
   \bigl[\nabla_\theta \log \pi(y_w|x)-\nabla_\theta \log \pi(y_l|x)\bigr]
$$

weights each pair by how badly its current log‑probability ordering conflicts with the human label, it focuses learning on precisely those regions where the model’s *relative* probabilities are wrong.  Changing $\tau$ does not create new “wrong” regions—those are already encoded in the ratio of logits—so performance does not deteriorate.

---

### Intuitive picture

Think of PPO as learning two separate maps:

* a **reward map** that tells you how good each sentence looks,
* a **policy map** that tries to follow that reward while staying close to SFT.

If you start looking at sentences that are far from what SFT ever generated (high $\tau$), your reward map becomes blurry and your KL budget is exceeded; you end up stumbling.

DPO collapses both maps into *one*: it says “the only thing that matters is how much more likely I am to produce a preferred sentence than an unpreferred one compared with SFT”.  That relationship holds no matter how you sample; raising $\tau$ merely rescales probabilities but does not break the underlying ratio.

---

### Why this appears as “robustness”

In Figure 2 we evaluate win‑rate across temperatures while keeping $\beta$ fixed (the same value used for training).  
Because DPO’s loss already enforces a *per‑example* KL penalty through log‑ratio terms, its effective KL remains near target for all $\tau$ values; therefore its preference accuracy—and consequently its win‑rate—remains stable.  
PPO needs an explicit KL coefficient tuned for each $\tau$; using a single $\beta$ tuned for low temperatures leaves it either under‑penalising (high $\tau$ → policy drifts away → reward misestimation) or over‑penalising (low $\tau$ → overly conservative outputs). The result is a sharp decline in win‑rate as $\tau$ grows.

---

### Summary

The robustness of DPO to sampling temperature is **intrinsic to its objective**:

* The DPO loss ties human preferences directly to *log‑probability ratios* between the current policy and a fixed reference.
* This ratio simultaneously acts as both an implicit reward and a KL regulariser, so any change in temperature automatically updates both quantities in lockstep.
* PPO separates reward estimation from KL regularisation; when sampling away from the region where the reward model was trained, its estimates become noisy and its fixed KL coefficient no longer enforces an appropriate constraint, leading to severe degradation at high temperatures.

Hence the observed behaviour in Figure 2 stems from a fundamental difference in how each method incorporates KL regularisation rather than from an accidental interaction between temperature and hyper‑parameters.
