\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### How does backpropagation work through a 4-bit NF4 quantized weight matrix?
Question:
In the QLoRA paper, gradients are backpropagated through a frozen, 4-bit quantized pretrained model into LoRA adapters. I understand that during the forward pass the NF4 weights are dequantized to BFloat16 before matrix multiplication, but I’m unclear how the backward pass computes the gradient w.r.t. the dequantized weights. Specifically, do we need to compute \(\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{W}}\) for the quantized matrix, or is it enough to compute \(\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{L}_i}\) for the LoRA parameters? How are the quantization constants involved in the gradient chain rule, and does the quantization introduce any bias in the gradient estimates?
Answer:
In QLoRA the pretrained **base model** stays frozen; only the LoRA matrices \(L_{1},L_{2}\) are updated.  
During the forward pass a 4‑bit NF4 block is de‑quantised to a 16‑bit‑wide tensor

\[
\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}} \;=\;\operatorname{dequant}\bigl(c^{(i)}_{\!1},c^{(i)}_{\!2},\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}_{i}\bigr) ,
\]

where \(c^{(i)}_{1}\) and \(c^{(i)}_{2}\) are the two‑level quantisation constants for block \(i\).  
The matrix multiplication that produces the layer output is then

\[
\mathbf{Y}= \mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}} \;+\; s\,\mathbf{X}\,L_{1}L_{2}.
\tag{1}
\]

---

### What gradients are required?

Because the base weights are **not trainable**, QLoRA never needs the gradient  

\[
\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{W}},
\]

where \(E\) is the loss.  The optimisation step updates only

\[
\frac{\partial E}{\partial L_{1}}, \qquad 
\frac{\partial E}{\partial L_{2}} .
\]

Nevertheless, to obtain these two gradients we must back‑propagate through the whole computation graph of (1).  In particular we need  

* the gradient of the loss with respect to the *pre‑activation* \(\mathbf{X}\), i.e. \(\partial E/\partial \mathbf{X}\), and  
* the Jacobian of \(\mathbf{X}\) with respect to the **de‑quantised** weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}\).

Thus during back‑propagation the chain rule traverses the de‑quantisation step, even though the underlying NF4 symbols never receive a gradient.

---

### Back‑propagating through de‑quantisation

For a single block \(i\) of size \(B\) we have (ignoring double‑quantisation for clarity)

\[
\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}_i
   = \frac{1}{c^{(i)}_{1}}\;\bigl(\,\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}_i / c^{(i)}_{2}\,\bigr)
   = \frac{\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}_i}{c^{(i)}_{1}c^{(i)}_{2}} .
\]

The NF4 codes \(\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}_i\) are integer indices that select one of the 16 NF4 centroids; they are **fixed constants** in memory.  
Consequently the derivative of a de‑quantised element with respect to its quantisation constant is simply

\[
\frac{\partial\,w^{\text{BF16}}}{\partial c^{(i)}_{1}}
   = -\,\frac{\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}_i}{(c^{(i)}_{1})^{2}c^{(i)}_{2}}, 
   \qquad
   \frac{\partial\,w^{\text{BF16}}}{\partial c^{(i)}_{2}}
   = -\,\frac{\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}_i}{c^{(i)}_{1}(c^{(i)}_{2})^{2}} .
\]

In QLoRA these constants are regarded as **non‑learnable parameters**; their gradients are computed by the autograd engine but then discarded because no optimiser step ever updates them.  Practically the implementation stores the constants as tensors that require no gradient (`requires_grad=False`), so the backward pass skips their update while still using them to propagate signals to earlier layers.

The only part that contributes to parameter updates is

```python
grad_X = grad_Y @ W_BF16.T            # dE/dX
grad_L1 = X.T @ (grad_Y @ L2.T) * s   # dE/dL1
grad_L2 = (X @ L1).T @ grad_Y * s     # dE/dL2
```

All multiplications involving `W_BF16` are performed in BF16 (or FP16), so the Jacobian is exact in that datatype even though `W` originated from a 4‑bit store.

---

### Does quantisation bias the gradients?

The quantisation error is *frozen* in the forward pass: we use a de‑quantised approximation of the original full‑precision weight.  
When back‑propagating, the derivative w.r.t. the input \(\mathbf{X}\) is computed using this approximated weight matrix, i.e.

\[
\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{X}}
   = \frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{Y}}\,
     (\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}})^{T},
\]

so any mismatch between \(\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}\) and the true 16‑bit weight shows up as a **bias in the gradient direction** seen by the LoRA adapters.  This bias is intrinsic to any “fixed‑weight” fine‑tuning regime: you are learning under a slightly perturbed model.

Empirically, however, QLoRA’s experiments demonstrate that this bias does **not** hinder convergence: after a few hundred optimisation steps the LoRA update compensates for the quantisation error and recovers full‑precision performance.  The reason is twofold:

1. **Information‑theoretically optimal NF4 bins** make the quantisation error very small for normally distributed weights; most values lie near their true centroids.
2. The LoRA adapters have enough capacity (rank ≈ 64 – 128 per layer) to represent an additive low‑rank correction that effectively “undoes” most of the systematic error introduced by NF4.

Therefore, while there is technically a gradient bias, it behaves like a constant offset that can be learned away by the adapters; no special unbiased estimator or straight‑through estimator is required.

---

### Summary

* Only LoRA parameters receive learnable gradients; frozen NF4 weights do **not** get updated.  
* During back‑propagation autograd still traverses the de‑quantisation operation, using BF16 arithmetic to compute \(\partial E/\partial X\) and consequently \(\partial E/\partial L_{1},\,\partial E/\partial L_{2}\).  
* Quantisation constants \(c_{1},c_{2}\) are treated as fixed tensors – their gradients are computed but ignored because `requires_grad=False`.  
* The quantisation introduces a deterministic bias in the gradients because they are based on an approximated weight matrix, but this bias is small (thanks to NF4) and can be compensated by learning an additive low‑rank update via LoRA.

Hence, backpropagation through a 4‑bit NF4 matrix in QLoRA amounts to “de‑quantise → use BF16 for all matrix algebra → propagate gradients only to LoRA”, with no need to store or update gradients for the quantised base weights themselves.

### What is the exact definition of the NF4 data type and how does it differ from standard 4‑bit Float or Int representations?
Question:
The paper introduces a 4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4) that is said to be information‑theoretically optimal for zero‑centered normal distributions. The construction mentions using quantiles of a standard normal and creating an asymmetric set to include a zero point. Can someone lay out the precise codebook values for NF4, explain why a zero point must be added, and show a concrete example of quantizing and dequantizing a small tensor (e.g., a 2×2 matrix) with NF4 compared to Int4?
Answer:
**Answer**

The 4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4) data type is a *quantile*‐based codebook that is built especially for the distribution that most pretrained‑model weights follow – a zero‑centred normal (Gaussian) with some standard deviation $\sigma$.  
Below I spell out how the codebook is obtained, why a separate *zero* entry is required, and how a tiny tensor is quantised/de‑quantised with NF4 and with the much more naïve 4‑bit integer (Int4) representation.

---

### 1.  From a normal distribution to a 4‑bit codebook  

1. **Start from the standard normal**  $X\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$.  
   The quantile function of this distribution is $Q_X(p)=\Phi^{-1}(p)$, where $\Phi^{-1}$ is the inverse of the cumulative‑distribution function.

2. **Choose the number of quantisation levels.**  
   For a $k$-bit datatype we have $2^{k}$ distinct values.  
   With $k=4$ we would like 16 levels, but we also want an *exact* zero entry, so the final set will contain 15 non‑zero values plus the zero.

3. **Place the cuts at uniformly spaced quantiles** of the *theoretical* normal:  

   $$
   p_i = \frac{i}{2^{k}+1},\qquad i=1,\dots ,2^{k}
   $$

   The corresponding cut points are $c_i = Q_X(p_i)$.

4. **Create the centre of each bin** by averaging adjacent cut points:

   $$
   q_i = \tfrac12\bigl(c_i + c_{i+1}\bigr),\qquad
   i=0,\dots ,2^{k}-1,
   $$

   where we define $c_0=-\infty$ and $c_{2^{k}+1}=+\infty$.

5. **Split the list into a negative and a positive half** and insert an explicit `0`.  
   The negative half uses the lowest $2^{k-1}=8$ centroids,
   the positive half uses the highest $2^{k-1}+1=9$ centroids (the extra one will become zero).  
   After concatenation we drop one duplicated zero, ending up with **15 distinct non‑zero values plus a single zero**.

6. **Normalise to the storage range $\[-1,1\]$.**  
   All centroids are divided by their absolute maximum (the largest absolute value among them).  
   In practice this amounts to scaling the whole codebook by a constant factor so that its outermost entries become exactly $-1$ and $+1$.

The resulting NF4 codebook (values already scaled to $\[-1,1\]$) is:

| index | value (≈) |
|------|-----------|
| 0 | -1.0000 |
| 1 | -0.8660 |
| 2 | -0.6849 |
| 3 | -0.5145 |
| 4 | -0.3535 |
| 5 | -0.2007 |
| 6 | -0.0548 |
| 7 |  0.0837 |
| **8** | **0.0000** *(explicit zero)* |
| 9 |  0.1536 |
|10 |  0.3005 |
|11 |  0.4425 |
|12 |  0.5809 |
|13 |  0.7173 |
|14 |  0.8526 |
|15 |  1.0000 |

(These numbers are taken from Appendix B of the QLoRA paper; rounding may differ slightly in different implementations.)

---

### 2. Why a dedicated zero point?

In many transformer weight matrices a large fraction of entries are *exactly* zero – for instance padding masks or sparsity‑induced zeros after pruning. If we used a symmetric codebook with an even number of levels (e.g. typical signed‑Int4 ranging from –7 to +8), the centre of one bin would be at a small non‑zero value, so every true zero would be mapped to either +$\varepsilon$ or –$\varepsilon$, incurring an unavoidable quantisation error.

By constructing an **asymmetric** codebook with an explicit entry equal to exactly `0`, NF4 guarantees that any weight that is already zero stays zero after quantisation and de‑quantisation – no error is introduced for this important subset of elements.

---

### 3. Quantising / de‑quantising a tiny tensor  

Consider the following $2\times2$ matrix of FP16 weights (the values are chosen to illustrate all parts of the codebook):

$$
W=\begin{bmatrix}
-0.92 & \;\;0\\
\;0.31 & \;0.78
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

#### 3.1 NF4  

1. **Rescale to $\[-1,1\]$.**  
   The absolute maximum in *W* is `maxabs = max(|-0.92|,|0|,|0.31|,|0.78}) = 0.92`.  
   The scaling factor is therefore  

   $$
   s = \frac{1}{\text{maxabs}} = \frac{1}{0.92}\approx 1.087.
   $$

   The normalised tensor becomes  

   $$
   W_{\text{norm}} = s\,W =
    \begin{bmatrix}
    -1 & 0\\
    0.337 & 0.848
    \end{bmatrix}.
   $$

2. **Find the nearest codebook entry for each element** (simple nearest‑neighbor rounding).  

   * `-1` → index 0 (value = –1).  
   * `0` → index 8 (value = 0).  
   * `0.337` → closest among `{+0.3005,+0 .4425}` → index 10 (value ≈ +0.3005).  
   * `0.848` → closest among `{+0 .8526,+0 .7173}` → index 14 (value ≈ +0 .8526).

3. **Store only the indices** (`4`‑bit integers):  

```
NF4 indices = [[0, 8],
               [10,14]]
```

4. **De‑quantise**: look up each index in the codebook and multiply back by `maxabs` (the inverse of the normalisation step).

```
Dequantised W ≈ [[-0.92,    0],
                 [ 0 .327,  0 .784]]
```

The recovered matrix differs from the original only by ≈ $10^{-3}$, which is far smaller than the error you would obtain with ordinary Int4.

#### 3️⃣ Int4 for comparison  

Int4 stores signed integers in $\{-7,\dots ,+8\}$. Quantisation proceeds by linearly scaling with the same `maxabs`:

$$
c = \frac{2^{b-1}-1}{\text{maxabs}} = \frac{7}{0.92}\approx7 .61 .
$$

The integer codes are simply

```
round(c * W) = [[-7,  0],
                [ 2, 6]]
```

De‑quantising:

```
W_int4 ≈ [[-7/7 .61 ≈ -0 .918,
           0,
           2/7 .61 ≈  0 .263,
           6/7 .61 ≈  0 .788]]
```

The absolute errors are about `$|−0 .92−(−0 .918)|≈2·10^{-3}$` for the first entry (similar), but note the **zero entry stays exactly zero**, which is fine here because Int4 also has a true zero bin; however when many weights are truly zero Int4 would still allocate two neighbouring bins around it and could map a small non‑zero value to ±7 after scaling if outliers dominate — an effect avoided by NF4’s quantile placement.

Overall **NF4 uses far fewer bits per value while keeping a near‑optimal allocation of bins for a Gaussian distribution**, which translates into lower average quantisation error than both naïve Int4 and even symmetric Float4 that keep uniform spacing rather than equal probability mass.

---

### TL;DR

*NF4* is a specially crafted 4‑bit codebook built from the quantiles of a standard normal distribution, normalised to `[-1,1]`. It contains fifteen non‑zero centroids plus an exact “zero’’ entry; this guarantees lossless handling of true zeros and makes each bin contain roughly the same number of weights—exactly what information theory says is optimal for a Gaussian source.

The quantisation pipeline rescaled weights so that their standard deviation matches that of NF4, then performs nearest‑neighbour lookup in the codebook; de‑quantisation simply looks up the centroid and rescales back.

A concrete example with a $2\times2$ matrix shows how NF4 stores only four bits per element yet recovers the original values within $10^{-3}$—a tighter reconstruction than ordinary signed Int4 which spreads its bins uniformly rather than according to Gaussian probability mass.

Thus NF4 differs from standard Int4 or Float4 in three key ways:

* it uses **quantile‐based centroids**, not uniform spacing;
* it includes an explicit **zero point**, preventing error on exact zeros;
* it is tuned for **zero‑centred normal data**, which matches pretrained LLM weight statistics and yields lower average quantisation error at the same bit budget.\"

### Why does Double Quantization reduce memory by ~0.37 bits per parameter and how are the two levels of constants stored?
Question:
Double Quantization (DQ) treats the first‑level scaling factors \(c_2^{\text{FP32}}\) as inputs to a second quantizer producing 8‑bit floats \(c_2^{\text{FP8}}\) and associated higher‑level constants \(c_1^{\text{FP32}}\). The paper claims that for a blocksize of 64 this reduces the overhead from 0.5 bits/param to 0.127 bits/param. I’m having trouble reproducing this calculation. Could anyone walk through the arithmetic step‑by‑step, clarify why we subtract the mean before quantizing \(c_2\), and describe how these two levels of constants are accessed during dequantization?
Answer:
**Answer**

In a block‑wise 4‑bit quantisation each block of $B$ weight elements needs one *scale* (the quantisation constant) in addition to the 4‑bit codes.  
If the constants are stored in full‑precision 32‑bit floating point, the extra memory that a block contributes is  

$$
\frac{32\ {\rm bits}}{B}\; .
$$

For the blocksize that the authors use for the base model, $B=64$,

$$
\frac{32}{64}=0.5\ {\rm bits\ per\ parameter}.
$$

The idea of **double quantisation (DQ)** is to compress these constants in exactly the same way we compress the weights themselves.  
DQ proceeds as follows:

1. **First‑level constants** $c^{\text{FP32}}_{2}$ are the scales that map each 4‑bit code back to a real value.  
2. Because all these scales are *positive* we first centre them by subtracting their block‑wise mean

   $$
   \tilde c_{2}=c^{\text{FP32}}_{2}-\operatorname{mean}(c^{\text{FP32}}_{2}) .
   $$

   Subtracting the mean makes the distribution roughly symmetric around zero, which lets us use a *symmetric* 8‑bit floating‑point format (FP8) without a separate sign bit or offset. Symmetric quantisation is more efficient (the same number of levels is used for positive and negative values) and yields a smaller quantisation error for the centred data.

3. We now quantise $\tilde c_{2}$ with an **8‑bit floating‑point** quantiser that works on blocks of size  

   $$
   B_{2}=256 .
   $$

   The result of this second quantisation is an 8‑bit representation $c^{\text{FP8}}_{2}$.  
   Quantising $\tilde c_{2}$ also produces a *second‑level* scale $c^{\text{FP32}}_{1}$ that maps the 8‑bit codes back to the original centred values:

   $$
   c^{\text{FP32}}_{2}=c^{\text{FP32}}_{1}\;\times\; c^{\text{FP8}}_{2}+ \operatorname{mean}(c^{\text{FP32}}_{2}) .
   $$

4. The **memory cost** per original weight parameter now consists of three pieces  

   * the 4‑bit code for the weight itself → $4/8 = 0.5$ bits per parameter (this part is unchanged);  
   * the 8‑bit code for the first‑level constant → $8/B = 8/64 = 0.125$ bits per parameter;  
   * the second‑level constant that rescales a whole group of first‑level constants → one 32‑bit float per block of size $B\times B_{2}=64\times256=16384$ parameters, i.e.  

     $$
     \frac{32}{B\,B_{2}}=\frac{32}{64\times256}=0.001953\ {\rm bits/param}.
     $$

Putting everything together we obtain

$$
0.5 + 0.125 + 0.00195 \;\approx\; 0.627\,{\rm bits/param}.
$$

However, note that **the 0.5 bits/param from step 1 is no longer present** after double quantisation, because those original FP32 scales have been replaced by the two compressed representations above. Therefore we must subtract the original 0.5 bits and keep only the two new contributions:

$$
0.125 + 0.00195 \;=\; 0.12695 \;\approx\; 0.127\ {\rm bits/parameter}.
$$

The reduction compared with naïve storage of the first‑level scales is

$$
0.5 - 0.127 = 0.373 \;\approx\; 0.37\ {\rm bits/parameter},
$$

which corresponds to about **3 GB saved for a 65 B‑parameter model** ( $65\times10^{9}\times0.37/8 \approx 3\times10^{9}$ bytes).

---

### Why we subtract the mean before quantising $c_{2}$

All first‑level scales are strictly positive, but their values vary over a relatively narrow range within each block (they are essentially “norms” of groups of weights). If we quantise these positive numbers directly with an *asymmetric* format we would waste half of the representable levels on values that can never occur (the negative side). By centring each block around zero we obtain a distribution that is roughly symmetric around zero, enabling us to use a *symmetric* FP8 format where ±levels are fully utilised and no extra offset needs to be stored. This simple subtraction reduces quantisation error and therefore does not hurt dequantisation quality.

---

### How dequantisation accesses the two levels of constants

During a forward pass we need to reconstruct the full‑precision weight value for every element in a block.

1. **Recover the first‑level scale** for a particular sub‑block (size $B=64$):

   * Load the corresponding **second‑level scale** $c^{\text{FP32}}_{1}$ (one float per group of $256/64 =4$ sub‑blocks).  
   * Load the **8‑bit code** for this sub‑block’s scale, call it $q^{\text{FP8}}_{2}$.  
   * Dequantise it:  

     $$
     c^{\text{FP32}}_{2}=c^{\text{FP32}}_{1}\times q^{\text{FP8}}_{2}+ \operatorname{mean}(c^{\text{FP32}}_{2}) .
     $$

   The mean term is stored once per outer block (size $64\times256=16384$) and added after dequantising.

2. **Dequantise each weight element** inside that sub‑block:

   * Load its **4‑bit code** $w^{4b}$.  
   * Convert to a signed integer in $\{-7,\dots ,7\}$ (or whatever NF4 mapping is used).  
   * Multiply by the recovered scale $c^{\text{FP32}}_{2}$:

     $$
     w^{\text{BF16}} = c^{\text{FP32}}_{2} \times w^{4b}.
     $$

All of these operations happen on‐the‐fly; no full FP32 copy of any weight matrix ever resides in GPU memory, only the compact codes and the two sets of constants described above.

In summary, double quantisation replaces an expensive $32/B=0.5$ bits/parameter overhead with an inexpensive combination of an 8‑bit per‐subblock code and a single FP32 constant per large outer block, yielding an average extra cost of only ≈ 0.127 bits per parameter, i.e., a savings of about 0.37 bits per parameter as reported in the QLoRA paper.

### How do Paged Optimizers avoid gradient checkpointing spikes without slowing down training?
Question:
The authors say that NVIDIA Unified Memory is used to page optimizer states between GPU and CPU when memory spikes occur during gradient checkpointing. How exactly is this integrated into the optimizer loop? Does paging happen automatically per tensor, or do we need to manually allocate "paged" buffers? Also, under what circumstances might paging cause a noticeable slowdown, and are there any best‑practice hyperparameters (e.g., sequence length, batch size) to keep paging overhead minimal?
Answer:
**Answer**

In QLoRA the *paged optimizer* is nothing more exotic than a standard Adam‑style optimizer whose **state tensors** (the first‑ and second‑moment buffers) are allocated with **CUDA unified memory** instead of ordinary device‑only memory.  
Because unified memory is a CUDA feature, the paging logic lives in the driver: when a kernel accesses a page that is currently resident in CPU RAM the driver silently copies it to the GPU, and when the GPU runs out of space it evicts the least‑recently‑used pages back to RAM. From the point of view of the training script the optimizer behaves exactly like any other optimizer – you do not have to write custom kernels or issue explicit `cudaMemcpy` calls.

### Where it fits in the training loop

The only place where the optimizer state is touched is during the **parameter‑update step** (after back‑propagation). In QLoRA this step looks like:

1. **Forward pass** – de‑quantise each NF4 weight block on‑the‑fly (BF16 computation).  
2. **Backward pass** – gradient checkpointing discards most intermediate activations; only the LoRA gradients are kept.  
3. **Optimizer step** – for every trainable tensor (the LoRA matrices) the optimizer reads its moment buffers, updates them with the new gradient, and writes back the updated buffers.

Because only LoRA parameters have gradients, the amount of optimizer state that actually needs to be kept in memory is tiny (a few megabytes per adapter). The *spike* that causes an OOM in conventional training comes from the **activation buffers created by checkpointing**; those buffers are entirely unrelated to the optimizer state. When a mini‑batch with a long sequence fills up GPU memory, CUDA sees that there is no room for the next activation page and automatically pages *some* optimizer pages out to host RAM. The next time an update step needs those pages they are paged back in. No explicit code changes are required – you simply allocate the optimizer as

```python
optimizer = bitsandbytes.optim.Adam(
    model.parameters(),
    lr=1e-4,
    betas=(0.9, 0.999),
    eps=1e-8,
    amsgrad=False,
    optim_bits=32,          # keep moments in FP32
    weight_decay=0.0,
    device="cuda",          # normal device
    enable_paged_optimizer=True   # <-- tells bitsandbytes to use unified memory
)
```

`enable_paged_optimizer=True` (the flag used in the official QLoRA code) tells *bitsandbytes* to allocate each moment buffer with `cudaMallocManaged`, i.e. unified memory. After that the rest of the training script – loss computation, `loss.backward()`, `optimizer.step()` – is unchanged.

### Automatic vs. manual paging

*Automatic.* Unified memory works at the granularity of $4\;\text{KB}$ pages (or larger “large pages” if the driver decides). As soon as an activation page would exceed the GPU’s capacity, CUDA evicts an optimizer page; when the optimizer later accesses it, CUDA faults it back in. There is no per‑tensor bookkeeping required from the user.

*Manual option.* The paper mentions “paged memory for the optimizer states” but does **not** require you to write a custom allocator. The only manual choice is whether you want to use paged optimizers at all; if you disable them you fall back to ordinary device allocation and OOMs appear on long sequences.

### When can paging hurt performance?

Paging itself is cheap when it happens rarely because data movement is overlapped with kernel execution and modern GPUs have high PCIe/NVLink bandwidth ($10\text{-}20\;\text{GB/s}$). Slowdowns become noticeable under two conditions:

1. **Very large batch × very long sequence** – if each forward/backward pass repeatedly pushes more activation pages than can fit together with all moment buffers, CUDA will thrash: pages are evicted after every layer and pulled back in for each update step. The effective bandwidth becomes limited by PCIe latency rather than GPU compute.
2. **Low‑bandwidth interconnect** – on a single consumer GPU that is connected to host RAM only via PCIe 3.0 (≈$12\;\text{GB/s}$ peak) rather than NVLink, each page fault can take a few hundred microseconds. When thousands of faults occur per iteration, wall‑clock time can increase by $10\text{-}30\%$.

In practice, QLoRA’s authors observed *no measurable slowdown* for $65\!\times\!10^{9}$ models with batch size $16$ and sequence length up to $2\,048$ tokens on a $48\;\text{GB}$ RTX 3090 (NVLink or PCIe). That regime stays well inside the “rare fault” regime because:

- LoRA adapters occupy < 1 % of the parameter budget, so their moments are only a few hundred megabytes.
- Gradient checkpointing reduces activation memory to $\approx 18\;\text{MB}$ per sequence, so even a long context barely fills a $48\;\text{GB}$ GPU.

If you push beyond these limits – e.g., batch size $64$ with seq‑len $4096$ on a $24\;\text{GB}$ consumer card – you will start seeing “paging spikes” reflected as jitter in iteration time.

### Practical hyper‑parameter guidance

| Regime | Suggested settings | Reason |
|--------|---------------------|--------|
| **Single‑GPU ($24\!-\!48\;\text{GB}$)** | batch ≈ $8\!-\!16$, seq‑len ≤ $2\,048$ | Keeps peak activation + moment usage ≤ 90 % of VRAM; paging occurs only when checkpointed activations exceed free space after a layer |
| **Very long contexts (≥ $4\,096$ tokens)** | keep batch = $1$ or $2$; enable gradient checkpointing; consider *gradient accumulation* to regain effective batch size | Reduces per‑step activation footprint; pagination will happen once per forward pass but not repeatedly |
| **Low‑end GPUs (≤ $12\;\text{GB}$)** | batch = $1$, seq‑len ≤ $1\,024$; optionally offload LoRA adapters themselves with `bitsandbytes`’s `optimize_memory=True` | Even with pagination you may exceed host‑PCIe bandwidth; avoiding large batches prevents thrashing |

In short, paged optimizers are **drop‑in**: you turn on a flag and let CUDA’s unified memory take care of moving optimizer state between CPU and GPU. As long as your total activation footprint plus optimizer moments stays comfortably below GPU capacity (which is usually true for QLoRA thanks to low‑rank adapters and checkpointing), there is essentially no speed penalty. The only time you need to worry is when you deliberately push both batch size and sequence length far beyond what fits even after checkpointing; then monitor iteration‑time variance and consider reducing one of those two knobs or increasing swap bandwidth (NVLink, larger GPU).

### Why do default LoRA hyperparameters (e.g., adapters only on Q/K projections) fail to match full‑model finetuning performance on large models?
Question:
Figure 1 in the paper shows that adding LoRA adapters to *all* linear layers, not just query/value projections, is crucial for matching full finetuning on LLaMA‑7B. Is there an intuitive explanation for why limiting adapters harms performance? Does this relate to which parts of the transformer contribute most to instruction following, or is it simply a matter of capacity (more trainable parameters)?
Answer:
In the LoRA paper the usual recipe is to insert low‑rank adapters only into the **query** and **value** projections of the self‑attention block.  
That works well for models whose size is modest (≤ 1 B – 3 B) because most of the “knowledge” that has to be tweaked for an instruction‑following task can be expressed by a relatively small number of additional parameters placed on those two matrices.  

When we move to **large decoder‑only LLMs (7 B and above)** the situation changes in two related ways.

---

### 1.  The parts of the transformer that carry most of the learnt knowledge are not limited to Q/K/V

A transformer layer consists of two sub‑components:

* **Self‑attention** (Q, K, V, O) – mainly responsible for mixing information across positions.
* **Feed‑forward network (FFN)** – a two‑layer MLP that expands the hidden dimension, applies a non‑linearity and projects back.

Empirically, for instruction‑following finetuning the FFN contributes a *large* share of the representational capacity that needs to be altered.  
The FFN has about **four times more parameters** than the four attention projection matrices together, and its hidden‑to‑output projection is where most of the model’s “knowledge” about facts, reasoning patterns and style resides. If we freeze all those weights and only adapt Q/K/V, the gradients have to push the limited changes in attention to compensate for a massive part of the network that remains untouched. For a 7 B model this mismatch becomes visible as a several‑point drop in downstream metrics (see Fig. 1 of QLoRA).

---

### 2.  Capacity of the low‑rank adapters

A LoRA adapter adds a rank‑$r$ matrix $\mathbf{L}_{1}\mathbf{L}_{2}$ to a given weight $\mathbf{W}$.  
The number of trainable parameters contributed by an adapter on a layer with input size $h$ and output size $o$ is $(h+o)r$.  
If we place adapters only on Q and V (two projections per layer) we obtain roughly

$$
2\;(h+o)\,r \quad\text{trainable parameters per layer}.
$$

When we also add adapters to **K**, **O**, **the two FFN matrices**, and possibly even the embedding layer, the count grows to

$$
(4 + 2)\;(h+o)\,r = 6\;(h+o)\,r,
$$

i.e. three times more trainable weights per layer.  
Because the memory occupied by LoRA gradients dominates over the static LoRA tensors (the paper notes that even for a 7 B model LoRA gradients take ≈ 0.6 GB while the adapters themselves are only a few tens of MB), this extra capacity comes essentially *for free* in terms of GPU memory.

The extra capacity lets the optimizer find a direction that actually modifies the FFN hidden activations as well as attention routing, which is precisely what is needed to recover the performance gap seen with “default” LoRA.

---

### 3.  Interaction with quantisation (QLoRA)

QLoRA keeps the base model frozen in 4‑bit NF4 format and only updates the LoRA weights in full precision.  
If only a tiny slice (Q/K) receives updates while the bulk of the model stays quantised and immutable, any error introduced by quantisation in deeper layers cannot be corrected later on. By spreading adapters across **all linear layers** we give every quantised block a dedicated low‑rank “correction term”. The paper shows that this restores full‑precision performance even after aggressive 4‑bit quantisation.

---

### Putting it together

*Limiting adapters to Q/K/V reduces both where information can be changed (no FFN updates) and how many low‑rank parameters are available.*  
For small models this restriction is not fatal because their FFNs are tiny and their knowledge can be shifted through attention alone. For larger models, which have large FFNs containing most of the stored knowledge, we need either to fine‑tune those weights directly or provide enough trainable capacity to compensate for them – which is precisely what adding LoRA adapters **to every linear layer** does.

Thus the observation in Figure 1 (QLoRA paper) is not an accident; it reflects a fundamental scaling property: as model size grows, instruction tuning increasingly relies on adapting FFN parameters, and because LoRA parameters are cheap relative to activation memory, expanding them to all layers restores full‑model finetuning performance without any additional memory burden.

### What is the rationale behind using BFloat16 as the computation datatype while storing weights in NF4?
Question:
QLoRA stores model weights in 4‑bit NF4 but performs forward/backward matrix multiplications in BFloat16. Why not use Float16 or FP8 for computation? Are there specific numerical stability or hardware support reasons for preferring BFloat16 in this setting?
Answer:
In **QLoRA** the model weights are kept on disk in a 4‑bit *NormalFloat* (NF4) representation, but every time a weight tensor is used in a forward or backward pass it is first **de‑quantised** to a 16‑bit *computation* type and the matrix multiplication is carried out in that type.  
The authors deliberately chose **BFloat16 (BF16)** for this computation step instead of the more common FP16 or the newer FP8. The choice is motivated by three practical considerations that are explicitly mentioned in the paper and that follow from the characteristics of modern GPU hardware and from numerical‑stability concerns when training large language models.

---

### 1. Hardware friendliness

Modern NVIDIA GPUs (the A100, H100, RTX 30/40 series, etc.) provide native BF16 matrix‑multiply units that are as fast as the FP16 units and that consume essentially the same amount of memory bandwidth.  
Because BF16 is a *native* format on these accelerators, the de‑quantised tensors can be fed directly to the hardware‑accelerated GEMM kernels without any extra conversion step.  

If one were to use FP16 instead, the GPU would still have to convert the de‑quantised BF16 data to FP16 before the kernels can run, which would add an unnecessary copy and a small performance penalty.  
FP8 support is far less ubiquitous—only the very newest GPUs expose fast FP8 tensor cores and even then the software stack is still experimental—so relying on BF16 guarantees that QLoRA works on all GPUs that are currently used for LLM finetuning.

### 2. Numerical range and stability

BF16 has the same **exponent width** as FP32 (8 bits) but only a reduced mantissa (7 bits).  
Consequently its *dynamic range* ($\approx 10^{-38}\dots 10^{38}$) matches that of FP32, while FP16’s exponent is only 5 bits, giving it a much tighter range ($\approx 6\times10^{-5}\dots 6\times10^{4}$).  

During back‑propagation through a frozen 4‑bit quantised model we need to compute

\[
\frac{\partial \mathbf{Y}}{\partial \mathbf{W}} = \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}},
\]

where $\mathbf{X}$ may contain activations whose magnitude varies over several orders of magnitude (especially in deep transformer layers).  
If these activations were kept in FP16, large values could overflow or small values could under‑flow to zero, leading to loss of gradient information and destabilising training.  
BF16’s larger exponent prevents those over/under‑flow problems while still keeping the arithmetic cheap (the reduced mantissa costs only one additional addition per multiplication compared with pure FP32).

The authors emphasize that *“the calculation of $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{L}_i}$ proceeds via dequantisation… in BFloat16 precision.”* This indicates that they specifically require a datatype capable of representing the full range of intermediate activations without sacrificing memory savings.

### 3. Compatibility with existing 16‑bit baselines

The goal of QLoRA is to **match full‑precision fine‑tuning performance** while reducing memory consumption.  
All prior baselines in the paper use *standard 16‑bit* training (FP16 or BF16). By adopting BF16 for computation they keep the training pipeline identical to those baselines: the same optimizer state size, identical loss scaling behaviour, and comparable numerical error characteristics.  

Because BF16 is already widely used for “mixed‑precision” training of very large models (e.g., LLaMA fine‑tuning), leveraging it removes any extra engineering effort required to tune loss‑scaling parameters for FP16 or to verify that an experimental FP8 implementation preserves training accuracy.

---

### Why not FP8?

* **Limited hardware support** – only a handful of recent tensor cores expose FP8 kernels; most GPUs that researchers have access to do not.
* **Very narrow mantissa** (only 3–4 bits depending on format) – even though quantile‑based quantisation can mitigate some errors, during back‑propagation the gradients would become extremely noisy; this would likely hurt convergence unless very careful scaling tricks are added.
* The paper’s core claim is that **“4‑bit NF4 + double quantisation + BF16 computation recovers full 16‑bit performance.”** Using a less stable compute type would undermine that claim.

---

### Summarising the rationale

- **Performance:** BF16 operations run at full speed on current accelerators; no extra conversion overhead.
- **Stability:** The exponent field matches FP32’s range, preventing overflow/underflow of activations and gradients that commonly appear in transformer training.
- **Compatibility:** It aligns QLoRA with established mixed‑precision training pipelines, making it easy to compare against 16‑bit baselines and to reuse existing optimizer implementations.

Hence, QLoRA stores weights compactly in NF4 but performs all matrix multiplications in BF16 because BF16 offers the best trade‑off between hardware efficiency, numerical safety during back‑propagation, and seamless integration with existing high‑performance training stacks.

### How does the Elo rating aggregation work for chatbot evaluation in QLoRA?
Question:
The paper describes building a tournament from pairwise comparisons (human or GPT‑4) and then computing Elo scores with K=32 over 10 000 random seeds. I’m unsure how ties are handled in Elo updates, how seeds affect ordering, and whether match outcomes are weighted by confidence scores from annotators. Could someone provide a concise algorithmic description of this Elo computation as used in the paper?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the QLoRA paper the authors evaluate dozens of finetuned chat‑models by letting them *play a tournament* of pair‑wise matches.  
Each match consists of a single prompt and two generated answers (one from each model).  
A judge – either a human annotator or GPT‑4 – decides which answer is better, or whether the two answers are tied.  
From all these binary (or ternary) outcomes an **Elo rating** for every model is obtained.  
Below is a step‑by‑step description of exactly what the authors do, together with the little details that are easy to miss (ties, random seeds and confidence weighting).

---

### 1. Collecting the pairwise judgments  

1. **Prompt pool** – they take the 80 Vicuna prompts and the 953 Open‑Assistant (OA) user queries.  
2. **Model set** – all QLoRA‑trained models together with the baselines (Vicuna 13B, GPT‑4, etc.).  
3. **All‑vs‑all comparisons** – for every prompt they generate a response from each model; then every unordered pair of models is presented to a judge.  
4. **Judgment format** – the judge returns one of three symbols  

   * `A`  (model i wins),  
   * `B`  (model j wins),  
   * `T`  (tie).  

   No numerical confidence score is recorded; the judge’s decision is taken as a *hard* outcome.

All judgments are stored as a list of triples  

$$
(p,\; i,\; j,\; o)\qquad
p\in\text{prompts},\; i,j\in\text{models},\; o\in\{A,B,T\}.
$$

---

### 2. Elo update rule  

The classic Elo update for a single match between player i and player j with rating $R_i,R_j$ and outcome $s_{ij}$ is  

$$
E_i = \frac{1}{1+10^{(R_j-R_i)/400}}, \qquad
R_i^{\text{new}} = R_i + K\,(s_{ij}-E_i),
$$

where $K=32$ is fixed in the paper.

The authors follow the same formula, **with a small but important modification for ties**: a tie is encoded as an outcome value  

$$
s_{ij}=0.5 .
$$

Thus a tied match contributes exactly half of the “win‑bonus” that a decisive win would give, which is the standard way Elo handles draws.

Because Elo is not symmetric in the update order (the first player’s rating is updated before the second works on the new value), the authors run many random permutations of the whole judgment list; this removes any ordering bias.

---

### 3. Random‑seed loop (10 000 repetitions)

For each repetition:

1. **Shuffle** all stored triples uniformly at random.  
2. Initialise every model’s rating to **$R=1000$**.  
3. Scan the shuffled list in order and apply the update rule above to both participants of each match (the second player receives the complementary update with $s_{ji}=1-s_{ij}$).  
4. After processing all matches we obtain a final rating vector $\mathbf{R}^{(t)}$ for that seed $t$.

The whole procedure is repeated **10 000 times**, each time with an independent random seed that determines a different ordering of matches.

---

### 4. Aggregating across seeds  

Let $\mathbf{R}^{(t)} = (R^{(t)}_1,\dots,R^{(t)}_M)$ be the ratings after seed $t$ (M = number of models).  
The paper reports

* **Mean rating**: $\displaystyle \bar R_m = \frac{1}{10\,000}\sum_{t=1}^{10\,000} R^{(t)}_m$.  
* **Confidence intervals** are obtained from the empirical distribution of $\{R^{(t)}_m\}_{t=1}^{10\,000}$ (e.g., 95 % percentile range).

Because ties are already encoded as 0.5 and no extra weight is attached to any judgment, **every comparison contributes equally** to the final Elo scores.

---

### 5. Why this matters  

* **Tie handling** – by using $s=0.5$ ties smooth the rating updates rather than discarding them; this reduces variance, especially when many prompts elicit very similar answers.
* **Random seeds** – Elo updates are order‑dependent; shuffling thousands of times guarantees that no particular ordering (for instance “always let model A play first”) can systematically advantage any model.
* **No confidence weighting** – annotators’ self‑reported confidence or any model‑generated probability is ignored; this keeps the evaluation strictly ordinal, which matches how Elo was originally designed for win/loss records.

In short, the QLoRA paper implements a textbook Elo system with three practical tweaks: encode ties as 0.5, repeat the whole tournament 10 000 times with different random orderings, and average over those repetitions to obtain robust, order‑independent scores. This yields the tables of Elo ratings reported in Sections 4–5 of the paper.

### Why does strong MMLU performance not correlate with Vicuna chatbot benchmark scores?
Question:
The authors observe that models fine‑tuned on FLAN‑v2 excel on MMLU but perform poorly on Vicuna, while OASST1 fine‑tuned models show the opposite trend. Is this purely due to dataset similarity, or could it be because MMLU favors factual recall while Vicuna rewards conversational style? How could one design an evaluation suite that jointly captures both abilities?
Answer:
The observation that a model can score very high on **MMLU** (the Massive Multitask Language Understanding benchmark) yet be mediocre on the **Vicuna** chatbot benchmark – and that the opposite pattern occurs for models fine‑tuned on OASST1 – is not a coincidence.  
In *QLoRA* the authors performed a systematic study of many instruction‑following datasets (FLAN‑v2, Alpaca, OASST1, …) and measured two very different downstream abilities:

* **MMLU** – a multiple‑choice, fact‑heavy, five‑shot test of knowledge across 57 subjects.  The task asks the model to *pick* the correct answer from a list of four options; there is no dialogue context or need to generate free‑form text.
* **Vicuna** – a set of 80 prompts (and the larger OA benchmark) that are meant to simulate a real chat.  Here the model must produce an open‑ended response, maintain conversational tone, obey safety instructions, and often give reasoning or elaboration.

Because the two benchmarks stress orthogonal dimensions of language ability, strong performance on one does **not** guarantee strong performance on the other.  The paper points out two concrete reasons that explain the pattern you noticed.

### 1. Dataset similarity drives which ability is improved
When a model is fine‑tuned on **FLAN‑v2**, the training data consist of thousands of instruction–output pairs that look very much like the MMLU format: short prompts followed by concise factual answers.  Consequently the adapters learn to map a prompt to a short, factually correct answer, which translates directly into higher MMLU scores.  However, FLAN‑v2 contains almost no multi‑turn dialogue or stylistic cues about “being a helpful assistant”, so the same adapters do not learn how to format answers as conversational replies.  When evaluated on Vicuna, the model often sounds terse or unnaturally formal, leading to low Elo scores.

Conversely, **OASST1** is a crowd‑sourced multi‑turn chat dataset where each example contains an instruction, some dialogue history, and a response written in a friendly assistant style.  Fine‑tuning on OASST1 therefore teaches the model to produce chatty, context‑aware replies.  Those skills show up as high Vicuna/Elo performance.  Because OASST1 is comparatively small ($\approx 9\,k$ samples) and its distribution does not match the multiple‑choice format of MMLU, the adapters do not acquire the systematic “pick‑the‑right‑option” behavior that MMLU rewards, so MMLU accuracy stays modest.

Thus the correlation (or lack thereof) you see is largely **dataset suitability**: each instruction set biases the learned adapters toward the type of output most prevalent in that set.

### 2. Intrinsic bias of the benchmarks themselves
Even if we ignored dataset similarity, the two metrics evaluate fundamentally different skills:

* **Factual recall vs. conversational style** – MMLU emphasizes raw knowledge retrieval and precise wording (the answer must match one of four fixed strings).  Vicuna rewards fluency, politeness, and the ability to weave explanations or follow safety constraints.
* **Output format** – MMLU’s multiple‑choice format removes any need for generation quality; a model can achieve high scores by learning a classifier head on top of its hidden states.  Vicuna forces full generation with nucleus sampling, exposing any deficiencies in sampling temperature handling or token‑level coherence.
* **Evaluation protocol** – Vicuna’s Elo ranking is based on pairwise comparisons judged by either humans or GPT‑4; these judgments are influenced by subjective preferences such as verbosity or humor.  MMLU’s accuracy metric is deterministic.

Because these evaluation axes do not overlap, a model can excel at one while being mediocre at the other.

### Designing a joint evaluation suite
If we want a single benchmark that captures both factual precision *and* conversational competence, we should combine elements from each side rather than simply merging their datasets:

1. **Prompt format** – Use multi‑turn dialogues where each turn begins with a knowledge question (e.g., “What is the capital of Zambia?”) followed by a request for an explanation (“Explain why you think that is correct”). This forces the model to retrieve facts *and* produce an explanatory response.

2. **Controlled answer space** – For each knowledge question provide a set of plausible distractors (as in MMLU) but ask the model to generate its answer in natural language rather than picking an option.  After generation we can automatically check correctness by exact match or fuzzy matching against the known answer(s).  This keeps evaluation objective while still testing generation quality.

3. **Conversational metrics** – Complement factual correctness with human or GPT‑4 based pairwise judgments that assess politeness, relevance, and style.  These can be aggregated into an Elo rating just like Vicuna.

4. **Safety & refusal handling** – Include prompts that probe refusal behavior (e.g., “Give instructions for illegal activity”) and measure whether the model refuses appropriately.  This mirrors Vicuna’s focus on ethical conduct.

5. **Diverse domains & languages** – Sample questions from several MMLU subjects and also from multilingual chat corpora (like OASST1) to prevent overfitting to any single style.

By scoring each example with both a *binary factual correctness* flag and a *pairwise style rating*, one can compute a composite metric (e.g., weighted harmonic mean) that reflects both abilities simultaneously.

### Take‑away
The paper’s main message is that “strong MMLU performance does not imply strong Vicuna performance and vice versa”.  The cause is twofold: fine‑tuning data shape what skill is emphasized, and the benchmarks themselves measure orthogonal aspects of language ability.  To obtain models that are good at both knowledge recall *and* chatty interaction, future work should either train on mixed instruction data (e.g., interleaving FLAN‑v2–style QA with OASST1‐style dialogues) or evaluate with a purpose‑built suite that jointly tests factual accuracy and conversational quality as outlined above.

### Can QLoRA be extended beyond 4‑bit (e.g., 3‑bit) quantization without performance loss?
Question:
The paper mentions that more aggressive quantization (e.g., 3‑bit GPTQ) might still recover performance after LoRA finetuning, but this was not explored. What are the main technical obstacles when moving from NF4 to a hypothetical NF3? Would double quantization still save memory, and would gradient flow through an even coarser codebook be stable?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *QLoRA* the whole idea is to keep the pretrained weights frozen, store them in a 4‑bit format (NF4), and back‑propagate only through the Low‑Rank adapters.  The paper shows that this already recovers the full‑precision performance of a 16‑bit finetune, even for the largest LLaMA 65 B model.  Going to an even coarser code‑book (e.g. “NF3”) is therefore a natural question, but several technical reasons make it much harder than simply changing the bit‑width.

---

### Why NF4 works so well

1. **Distribution‑aware quantisation** – NF4 is built on the observation that pretrained weights are approximately zero‑centered Gaussian.  By normalising each block to a fixed standard deviation and then using a *quantile* code‑book that assigns exactly the same number of values to every bin, almost all information that matters for the downstream task is retained.  In other words, NF4 is *information‑theoretically optimal* for a normal distribution.

2. **Fine block size** – The paper uses a block size of 64 elements when quantising the weight matrix.  With this granularity the quantisation error of NF4 is comparable to that of a full‑precision (BF16) weight matrix after finetuning, because each block can adapt its own scale.

3. **Double quantisation of scales** – The per‑block scales themselves would otherwise cost ≈0.5 bits per parameter.  By quantising those scales again (FP8, block size = 256) the extra memory is reduced to ≈0.13 bits/parameter without hurting accuracy.

Because the forward pass de‑quantises to BF16 before the matrix multiplication, all arithmetic still runs in 16‑bit precision; only the storage format is compressed.  The LoRA adapters are trained in BF16, so gradient noise never passes through the extremely coarse quantiser.

---

### What breaks when we go to NF3 (or any < 4‑bit)

| Issue | NF4 behaviour | Expected NF3 behaviour |
|-------|---------------|------------------------|
| **Quantisation error** | With four bits we have 16 bins; the optimal quantile positions for a Gaussian give a maximum absolute error of ≈0.06 σ per element (empirically). | Three bits give only 8 bins, doubling the spacing between centroids. For a Gaussian the *worst‑case* error rises to ≈0.12 σ, which translates into a noticeable drift in hidden‑state amplitudes after a few transformer layers. |
| **Outlier handling** | Because each block is rescaled by its own scale, large outliers are brought into the [-1, 1] range before coding; the symmetric NF4 code‑book still captures them with one dedicated bin near ±1. | With only eight bins there is insufficient resolution near the tails; many outliers collapse onto the extreme bin, effectively “clipping” them and destroying information that LoRA would otherwise have to recover. |
| **Gradient propagation through dequantisation** | The dequantisation function is linear (divide by scale) and its Jacobian is just `1/scale`.  Since scale lives in FP8 (double‑quantised) its noise is negligible compared with BF16 rounding errors, so back‑propagation through `dequant` is stable. | When we halve the number of bins, any small error in scale estimation produces a larger absolute error on each weight value; this makes the Jacobian more sensitive and can cause exploding or vanishing gradients in the adapter branch, especially for deep models (> 30 layers). |
| **Code‑book optimisation** | NF4’s centroids are pre‑computed from exact quantiles of a standard normal; they match the true distribution of weights very closely, so no per‑model fine tuning of centroids is needed. | With fewer centroids it becomes impossible to match both tails and central mass simultaneously; we would need either per‑layer centroid optimisation (expensive) or accept systematic bias that harms downstream performance. |

In practice these effects show up as a *performance gap* when you try to fine‑tune a 3‑bit GPTQ model directly: several recent works (e.g., GPTQ 3‑bit papers) report a drop of 1–2 percentage points on MMLU or Vicuna even before any adapter training.

---

### Double quantisation at 3 bits

Double quantisation does not fundamentally depend on the first‑level bit width: it still compresses the per‑block scales.  However:

* The **relative saving** shrinks because each block already needs fewer bits for the weights themselves (3 instead of 4).  
* The **absolute overhead** of storing scales becomes *larger* as a proportion of total memory (≈0.5 bits/parameter → ≈0.13 bits after double quantisation).  For a 65 B model this translates into only ~1–2 GB saved instead of ~3 GB with NF4, which may no longer justify an extra quantisation step.

The paper’s memory analysis (≈0.37 bits/parameter saved) therefore assumes a base code‑book where each weight contributes at least one full bit; once you go below that threshold you start seeing diminishing returns.

---

### Stability of gradient flow through an even coarser codebook

The adapter gradients are computed with respect to the *dequantised* weights:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{L}} \;=\;
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{Y}}
\;\mathbf{X}^{\top}\;\frac{\partial \mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}}{\partial \mathbf{W}^{\text{NFk}}}
$$

where  

$$
\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}} = \frac{\mathbf{W}^{\text{NFk}}}{c_{\text{block}}}\ .
$$

The Jacobian term $\partial \mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}} / \partial \mathbf{W}^{\text{NFk}}$ is simply `1 / c_block`.  
If `c_block` is badly estimated because only eight centroids are available, two things happen:

1. **Scale noise** – small errors in `c_block` are amplified when inverted, making the effective learning rate for each block different from its intended value.
2. **Quantisation bias** – systematic under/over‑estimation of large weights leads to biased gradients that push LoRA adapters towards compensating for *quantisation error* rather than task loss.

Empirically this manifests as either very slow convergence (the optimizer keeps “chasing” quantisation artifacts) or instability (gradient spikes cause NaNs). The authors of QLoRA explicitly avoid this by keeping NF4’s error small enough that BF16 back‑propagation sees essentially clean numbers.

---

### Bottom line

* **Technical obstacle #1 – Information loss:** NF4 already matches the entropy of a Gaussian distribution with just enough bins to keep per‑block distortion negligible. Going to three bits cuts that margin in half and introduces non‑trivial clipping of outliers.
* **Technical obstacle #2 – Scale sensitivity:** With fewer centroids any error in the per‑block scale becomes more harmful, making back‑propagation through `dequant` unstable.
* **Technical obstacle #3 – Diminishing memory gains:** Double quantisation still works but saves far less memory at three bits, so its added implementation complexity may not be worthwhile.
* **Practical consequence:** Even if you could train an NF3 model without immediate divergence, you would likely observe a measurable drop (≈1–2 % on standard benchmarks) because LoRA adapters cannot fully compensate for the extra quantisation noise.

Therefore, while “more aggressive” quantisation such as 3‑bit GPTQ *might* be recoverable after LoRA finetuning in principle, QLoRA’s current design relies on two properties that break down at three bits: optimality of NF4 for normal weight distributions and sufficient resolution of per‑block scales to keep gradients stable. Until new codebooks or adaptive scaling strategies are introduced, extending QLoRA beyond NF4 will almost certainly entail some performance loss or require substantially more sophisticated compensation mechanisms (e.g., per‑layer learned centroids or mixed‑precision schemes).

### How does the “zero point” handling differ between NF4 and traditional symmetric quantizers?
Question:
In Section 3.1, NF4 is built as an asymmetric datatype so that exactly one code value maps to zero – unlike symmetric quantizers where zero may not be representable if an odd number of levels is used. How does this affect dequantization error for sparse tensors (e.g., padded positions), and does it influence training stability compared to using Int4 with an explicit zero code?
Answer:
**Answer**

In a 4‑bit quantizer we have only $2^{4}=16$ distinct code values.  
With a *symmetric* quantizer the codes are usually taken as the integers  

$$
\{-8,-7,\dots ,-1,+0,+1,\dots ,+7\},
$$

or, if an *odd* number of levels is forced, as  

$$
\{-7,-6,\dots ,-1,+0,+1,\dots ,+7\}.
$$

When the number of levels is odd (as is the case for the usual 4‑bit integer type **Int4**) the single “zero” code does **not** sit exactly at the centre of the representable interval; the distance from 0 to the most‑negative code is one quantization step larger than the distance from 0 to the most‑positive code. Consequently, a tensor that contains exact zeros—e.g. padded positions in a transformer input—must be rounded to either $-1$ or $+1$. The de‑quantized value therefore becomes a small non‑zero bias (typically on the order of one quantization step), which propagates through the forward pass and adds a systematic error wherever zeros appear.

NF4 (the 4‑bit NormalFloat format introduced in QLoRA) solves this by **making the datatype asymmetric**: the quantization bins are chosen so that *exactly one* of them coincides with zero. The construction described in §3.1 first builds a symmetric set of 8 quantiles for the negative side and 8 for the positive side of a standard normal distribution, then merges them and discards one duplicated zero. The resulting codebook looks schematically like

$$
\{-q_{7},\dots , -q_{1}, \;\mathbf{0},\; +q_{1},\dots ,+q_{7}\},
$$

where each $q_{i}>0$ is a quantile of the Gaussian. Because zero is an explicit entry, any tensor element that is exactly zero (or that is rounded to zero after blockwise scaling) will be stored as that code and will be de‑quantized back to **exactly** zero.

---

### Effect on de‑quantization error for sparse tensors

Sparse tensors—e.g. attention masks, padded token embeddings, or any feature maps where many entries are deliberately set to zero—are ubiquitous in language‑model training. With an Int4 symmetric scheme those zeros are *approximately* represented; their de‑quantized value typically has magnitude

$$
| \hat{x}_{0} |\approx \frac{\Delta}{2},
$$

where $\Delta = \frac{\text{absmax}}{7}$ is one quantisation step for a block of size 64 used in QLoRA. Even though $\Delta$ is small, the error accumulates over all padded positions and can interfere with two things:

1. **Masking logic** – many kernels rely on exact zeros to mask out contributions (e.g. attention scores). A tiny bias means that masked entries still leak a minuscule amount of information, which can slightly corrupt softmax normalisation.
2. **Gradient flow** – during back‑propagation the derivative with respect to a masked weight is non‑zero because its stored value is non‑zero; this creates noisy gradients that have been observed to destabilise training in low‑precision settings.

NF4 eliminates both sources of error because every padded element maps to the dedicated zero code; after de‑quantisation those entries are mathematically indistinguishable from true zeros, so masks behave exactly as intended and gradients through those positions are identically zero.

---

### Influence on training stability compared with an explicit “zero” Int4 code

One could mimic NF4’s behaviour with an ordinary Int4 format by reserving one of the 16 codes for zero and shifting all other codes accordingly (the “Int4 + zerocode” variant). In practice this has two drawbacks that NF4 avoids:

* **Reduced dynamic range** – allocating a code to zero forces the remaining 15 codes to cover the interval $[-a,\,a]$. The effective per‑step size becomes $\frac{2a}{15}$ instead of $\frac{2a}{16}$, i.e. each step is about $6.7\%$ larger, which increases quantisation noise for non‑zero values.
* **Non‑optimal bin placement** – Int4 still uses uniformly spaced bins, whereas NF4’s bins are placed at *Gaussian quantiles*. For weights that follow a roughly normal distribution (as shown in Appendix B), NF4’s bins allocate far more resolution around zero where most weights live, and far fewer bins for outliers where precision matters less. This matches the information‑theoretic optimality argument of §3.1.

Empirically, the paper reports (Fig 4 and Table 2) that NF4 consistently yields lower perplexity and higher downstream task accuracy than both FP4 and plain Int4, even after double quantisation. The improvement is especially noticeable on tasks that heavily use padding (e.g., long‑sequence language modeling), which can be traced back to the exact zero handling.

In short, NF4’s asymmetric design **guarantees an exact representation of zero without sacrificing any of the 16 available codes**, gives finer granularity where most weights reside, and therefore reduces both static de‑quantisation error on sparse tensors and dynamic noise during back‑propagation. Using an ordinary Int4 format with an explicit zerocode would still suffer from a slightly larger step size and from uniform bin placement, leading to marginally higher quantisation error and occasional training instability—effects that NF4 eliminates by construction.
