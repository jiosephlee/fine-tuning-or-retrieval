\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### What is incoherent processing and why does multiplying by a random orthogonal matrix reduce FP8 quantization error?
Question:
In Section 3.3 the paper introduces "incoherent processing" where \(Q\) and \(K\) are multiplied by a random orthogonal matrix \(M\) before FP8 quantization. I would like to know:
1) How does this operation preserve the exact attention output mathematically?
2) Why does spreading out values via \(M\) lead to lower quantization error in practice? Is there any theoretical bound or empirical evidence supporting this claim?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the FP‑8 version of FlashAttention‑3 the authors apply a random orthogonal matrix  

\[
\mathbf M\in\mathbb R^{d\times d},\qquad \mathbf M^{\!\top}\mathbf M =\mathbf I ,
\]

to the query and key matrices **just before** they are quantised to FP8:

\[
\widetilde{\mathbf Q}= \operatorname{Quant}_8(\mathbf Q\mathbf M),\qquad 
\widetilde{\mathbf K}= \operatorname{Quant}_8(\mathbf K\mathbf M).
\]

The term “incoherent processing’’ in the paper refers to exactly this step.  
Below we explain why it does not change the attention result and why it makes the subsequent FP8 quantisation more accurate.

---

### 1.  Exact preservation of the attention output  

The attention scores are defined as  

\[
\mathbf S = \alpha\,\mathbf Q \mathbf K^{\!\top},
\qquad \alpha = 1/\sqrt d .
\]

If we replace \(\mathbf Q,\mathbf K\) by their rotated versions we obtain  

\[
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{\mathbf S} 
&= \alpha\,(\mathbf Q\mathbf M)(\mathbf K\mathbf M)^{\!\top}               \\
&= \alpha\,(\mathbf Q\mathbf M)(\mathbf M^{\!\top}\mathbf K^{\!\top})       \\
&= \alpha\,\mathbf Q (\underbrace{\mathbf M \mathbf M^{\!\top}}_{=\mathbf I}) 
   \mathbf K^{\!\top} \\[2mm]
&= \alpha\,\mathbf Q \mathbf K^{\!\top}
   = \mathbf S .
\end{aligned}
\]

Because **\(M\) is orthogonal**, \(M M^{\!\top}=I_d\); the two rotations cancel out when the matrix product is formed. Consequently the soft‑max matrix  

\( \operatorname{softmax}(\widehat{\bf S}) = \operatorname{softmax}({\bf S}) \)

and the final output  

\( {\bf O}= {\bf P}{\bf V} \)

are unchanged.  The only place where \(M\) appears is inside the two GEMMs that compute the scores; after those GEMMs finish, \(M\) never shows up again.  Hence the algorithm is mathematically identical to the original attention, irrespective of how we later quantise the intermediate tensors.

---

### 2.  Why rotating (spreading) the values reduces FP8 quantisation error  

#### 2.1  The problem with per‑tensor scaling  

FP8 (e4m3) keeps only three mantissa bits and four exponent bits.  When a whole tensor (e.g. all of \(Q\)) is scaled by a single scalar \(s_Q^{-1}\) before conversion, the optimal scale must accommodate **the largest absolute entry** in that tensor.  If a few outlier entries are much larger than the bulk of the data (which is typical for LLM activations), the optimal scale becomes large, and most entries are forced into a narrow region of the FP8 dynamic range.  This yields a large *relative* quantisation error for the majority of elements.

#### 2.2  Block‑wise scaling mitigates but does not eliminate outliers  

FlashAttention‑3 therefore already uses *block quantisation*: each \(B_r\times d\) (or \(B_c\times d\)) block gets its own scale factor \(s_{Q}^{(b)} , s_{K}^{(b)} , s_{V}^{(b)}.\)  
This limits an outlier to affect only its own block, but if an outlier sits in a block that also contains many small numbers, those small numbers still suffer a relatively coarse quantisation step.

#### 2.3  Orthogonal rotation spreads out energy  

Multiplying a vector \(x^\top \in \mathbb R^{d}\) by a random orthogonal matrix produces  

\( y^\top = x^\top M . \)

Each component of \(y^\top = \sum_{i=1}^{d} x_i M_{i,j}\) is a sum of many signed contributions from all coordinates of \(x.\)  

*If an outlier lives in coordinate \(i_0,\)* its influence is divided among **all** output components because every column of \(M\) contains roughly equal numbers of +1/–1 (for the Hadamard‑diagonal construction used in the paper).  
Consequently, no single entry of \(QM\) or \(KM\) becomes dramatically larger than the others; instead all entries have roughly comparable magnitudes, i.e. their variance is homogenised.

From a statistical point of view this operation is akin to applying a random orthonormal basis change – it preserves Euclidean norms (\(\|xM\|_2=\|x\|_2)\) but *decorrelates* large spikes from any particular coordinate. When we subsequently compute per‑block scales, each block sees a distribution whose maximum is much closer to its typical magnitude, so the chosen scale can be tighter.

#### 2.4  Empirical and theoretical support  

*Empirically* the paper reports in §6 that FP8 FlashAttention‑3 with both block quantisation **and** incoherent processing reduces root‑mean‑square error by a factor of 2.6 compared with a baseline that uses only per‑tensor scaling (Table 3). The ablation in §6 shows that removing incoherent processing (i.e., keeping original \(Q,K,V)\) raises the error close to the baseline level.

*Theoretically* one can bound the expected quantisation error under random orthogonal mixing. Let an element of a block before rotation be distributed with variance \(\sigma^2_{\text{in}}\) and let its maximum be \(M_{\text{in}}\). After multiplication by an orthogonal matrix drawn uniformly from the set of signed Hadamard · diagonal matrices, each output entry has variance still equal to \(\sigma^2_{\text{in}}\) but its maximum satisfies  

\[
\E [\,M_{\text{out}}\,] \leq C\,(\sigma_{\text{in}}\sqrt{\log d}),
\]

where \(C<1\) is a constant that depends on the distribution of signs. In contrast, without rotation we have \(M_{\text{in}}\approx O(\sigma_{\text{in}}\sqrt{\log (Bd)})$ because a single outlier can dominate; for typical head dimensions (\(d=64{-}256)\), this reduction can be several ×. Since FP8 quantisation error is roughly proportional to
\(M / 2^{p}\) where \(p=3$ mantissa bits, reducing \(M_{\text{out}}\) directly reduces expected error.

Thus incoherent processing does not change any mathematically exact quantity in attention; it merely reshapes each block’s value distribution so that low‑precision FP8 representation captures it more faithfully.

---

### TL;DR

1. Multiplying both queries and keys by any orthogonal matrix leaves
   \((Q\,M)(K\,M)^\top = QK^\top,\)
   so every downstream operation (soft‑max, weighting of V) stays exactly the same.
2. The orthogonal matrix spreads large outliers evenly across all components,
   making each block’s dynamic range much tighter.
   With tighter per‑block ranges, FP8’s limited mantissa can represent most values more accurately,
   which experimentally reduces RMSE by about 2.6×.
   Simple probabilistic arguments about variance preservation and maximal‑value contraction give a theoretical intuition for this improvement.

Hence “incoherent processing’’ is an elegant way to keep attention mathematically unchanged while dramatically improving FP8 quantisation fidelity on hardware such as Hopper’s Tensor Cores.
