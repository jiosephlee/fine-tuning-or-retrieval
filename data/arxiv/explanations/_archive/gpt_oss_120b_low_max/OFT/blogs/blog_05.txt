# Future Directions: Scaling Orthogonal Finetuning and Compositional Control  

Controlling large text‑to‑image diffusion models has become a hot topic, and the recent **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** framework shows that a simple, mathematically‑grounded transformation can preserve the semantic “knowledge” of a pretrained model while letting us inject new abilities (subject‑driven generation, edge‑to‑image, etc.). The paper does a great job demonstrating OFT’s speed, stability, and parameter efficiency, but it also leaves several open questions that are ripe for experimentation. In this post we unpack those challenges, translate them into concrete research or hobby projects, and suggest practical ways you can start playing with them today.

---

## 1. Speeding Up the Cayley Transform  

### Why it matters  
OFT enforces orthogonality by **Cayley parameterization**:  

\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\qquad \mathbf{Q}^{\top}=-\mathbf{Q}.
\]

The inverse \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) guarantees that \(\mathbf{R}\) stays on the orthogonal group, but matrix inversion is an \(O(d^3)\) operation for a layer with hidden dimension \(d\). When you have dozens of such layers (as in Stable Diffusion’s UNet), the overhead can become noticeable, especially on commodity GPUs.

### Possible acceleration routes  

| Idea | Sketch of implementation | Pros | Cons |
|------|--------------------------|------|------|
| **Iterative approximation** (Neumann series) | Replace the exact inverse with a few terms: \(\mathbf{R}\approx \mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q}+2\mathbf{Q}^2+\dots\). Stop after 2–3 terms. | Simple to code; eliminates explicit inversion. | Approximation error may break strict orthogonality; need to monitor convergence. |
| **Low‑rank skew matrix** | Force \(\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{\top} - \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\) where \(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\) with \(k\ll d\). The inverse can be expressed via Woodbury identity. | Reduces computation to \(O(dk^2)\). | Introduces an extra design choice (rank \(k\)) and may limit expressivity. |
| **GPU‑native orthogonal layers** | Use libraries such as *torch.nn.utils.parametrize* with custom autograd that leverages `torch.linalg.qr` or `torch.linalg.svd` (both have efficient GPU kernels). | Leverages highly optimized linear algebra; retains exact orthogonality. | QR/SVD are still cubic in worst case, but the GPU kernels are heavily tuned; memory overhead can be higher. |
| **Cached Cayley updates** | Pre‑compute \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) once per training step and reuse for all forward passes of that step. Combine with checkpointing to limit memory use. | No algorithmic change; just better bookkeeping. | Still incurs the same per‑step cost; not a long‑term solution for very deep models. |

**Starter experiment:**  
Implement the Neumann series with two terms (`I + 2Q`) and compare training wall‑time against the exact Cayley version on a single UNet block (e.g., the middle attention layer). Plot loss curves to see if the approximation harms convergence.

## 2. Compositionality: Stacking Multiple OFT Adapters  

One intriguing property of orthogonal matrices is that their product is also orthogonal:

\[
\mathbf{R}_\text{combined}= \mathbf{R}_2\,\mathbf{R}_1 \quad \text{s.t.}\quad 
\mathbf{R}_1,\mathbf{R}_2 \in O(d) \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{R}_\text{combined} \in O(d).
\]

In practice this means you could fine‑tune a diffusion model *once* for “dog style” (subject‑driven) and *again* for “canny edge control”, then multiply the two learned \(\mathbf{R}\)’s to obtain a *single* adapter that handles both tasks simultaneously.

### Open research questions  

1. **Interference vs. synergy** – Does stacking adapters ever cancel each other’s effect? Empirically measuring the cosine similarity between the combined transformation and each individual one can give a first hint.
2. **Ordering matters?** – Matrix multiplication is not commutative; does applying “subject” first then “control” give different visual results than the opposite order?
3. **Scalability** – If you train ten distinct adapters (e.g., style transfer, pose control, color palette), the product may become numerically unstable. Regularizing the combined matrix (e.g., re‑orthogonalizing every few steps) could be necessary.

### How you can try it today  

1. **Train two independent OFT adapters** on a small dataset:  
   - Adapter A: DreamBooth on a single cat image (subject adaptation).  
   - Adapter B: ControlNet‑style Canny edge finetuning on 100 edge/real pairs.
2. **Extract their orthogonal matrices** (`R_A`, `R_B`). Most implementations expose them as learnable parameters; simply `.detach().cpu().numpy()` will do.
3. **Compose**: `R_combined = R_B @ R_A`.
4. **Inject** `R_combined` into the base model once (multiply each weight matrix by its corresponding `R_combined`).  
5. **Generate** using prompts that require both capabilities (e.g., “a cat sitting on a wooden bench, sketch style”). Compare against sequential finetuning (first A then B) to check for drift.

If you see degradation, try *re‑orthogonalizing* after multiplication:

```python
def reorthogonalize(R):
    # QR decomposition gives an orthogonal factor Q
    Q, _ = torch.linalg.qr(R)
    return Q
```

## 3. Improving Block‑Diagonal Efficiency  

The paper’s block‑diagonal parameterization reduces trainable parameters from \(O(d^2)\) to roughly \(O(d^2/r)\), where \(r\) is the number of blocks. While this works well, it introduces two practical concerns:

1. **Bias toward intra‑block interactions only** – Neurons in different blocks never mix directly during finetuning.
2. **Hyperparameter search over block count** – Choosing an optimal \(r\) often requires trial–and–error.

### Potential refinements  

| Approach | Core idea | Expected benefit |
|----------|-----------|------------------|
| **Overlapping blocks** | Instead of strict partitions, let blocks share some rows/columns (sliding windows). Each neuron belongs to multiple blocks, enabling cross‑block mixing while keeping parameter count low. | Better expressivity without blowing up parameters. |
| **Low‑rank plus block structure** | Decompose each block as `R_i = I + U_i V_iᵀ`, where `U_i,V_i` have rank `< d/r`. This brings back the low‑rank flavor of LoRA inside each block. | Further reduction in trainable weights while preserving orthogonality via Woodbury identity tricks. |
| **Learnable block assignment** | Use a small gating network that decides which neurons belong to which block at each training step (similar to mixture‑of‑experts). The gating weights are lightweight and can be trained jointly with the orthogonal matrices. | Allows dynamic allocation of capacity where it matters most (e.g., early layers vs later layers). |
| **Sparse orthogonal matrices** | Enforce sparsity patterns beyond block diagonal (e.g., random sparsity or structured sparsity like butterfly matrices). Efficient algorithms exist for multiplying sparse orthogonal transforms (FFT‑like patterns). | Drastically cut memory/computation while keeping full-rank transformations mathematically possible. |

### Quick hands‑on test  

Implement overlapping blocks with stride = half block size:

```python
def build_overlap_R(d, block_sz, stride):
    R = torch.eye(d)                     # start from identity
    for start in range(0, d - block_sz + 1, stride):
        sub = torch.randn(block_sz, block_sz)
        # enforce skew-symmetry
        Q = sub - sub.T
        # Cayley transform on sub-block
        I = torch.eye(block_sz)
        R_block = (I + Q) @ torch.inverse(I - Q)
        R[start:start+block_sz, start:start+block_sz] = R_block
    return R
```

Compare training speed and final FID scores against pure block-diagonal OFT on a Canny edge task with only 10% of ADE20K data.

## 4. From Research Questions to Developer Experiments  

Below is a concise checklist you can follow right now:

1️⃣ **Benchmark inversion speed:**  
   - Profile `torch.inverse` vs QR/SVD vs Neumann series on your GPU for dimensions typical of UNet attention heads (e.g., 1280).  
   - Log wall time per training step for each method.

2️⃣ **Prototype compositional adapters:**  
   - Train two small OFT adapters on publicly available subsets of DreamBooth & ControlNet data.  
   - Compose their matrices, re-orthogonalize if needed, and generate multi‑task samples.

3️⃣ **Test alternative block structures:**  
   - Implement overlapping blocks or low‑rank sub-blocks; keep track of trainable parameter count (`torch.numel`).  
   - Run a few epochs on a cheap task (e.g., MNIST → colored MNIST) to verify stability before moving back to diffusion.

4️⃣ **Automate hyperparameter search:**  
   - Use Optuna or Ray Tune to sweep `r` (number of blocks), overlap stride, and low‑rank dimension simultaneously; objective = validation CLIP‑I + control consistency metric.

5️⃣ **Share your findings!**  
   - Open-source your modified OFT layer as a plug‑and‑play PyTorch module (`torch.nn.Module`).  
   - Include a small Colab notebook demonstrating compositional control so that hobbyists can reproduce results without massive compute.

## Closing Thoughts  

Orthogonal Finetuning gave us a principled way to respect the geometry of pretrained diffusion models while still adding new capabilities—a rare win in the world of massive generative networks where finetuning often leads to catastrophic forgetting or unstable training.

The three avenues we highlighted—fast Cayley transforms, compositional adapter multiplication, and smarter block designs—are not just academic curiosities; they are practical levers you can pull today with modest resources. By experimenting with these ideas you’ll not only push forward what OFT can achieve but also build reusable tools that help anyone who wants fine-grained control over text-to-image models without paying an inference cost.

Happy hacking, and may your next diffusion run be both *orthogonal* and *optimal*!
