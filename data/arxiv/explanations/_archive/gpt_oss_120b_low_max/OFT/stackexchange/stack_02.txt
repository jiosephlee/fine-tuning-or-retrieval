\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### What is the intuition behind using the Cayley transform for orthogonal parameterization, and how does it enforce \(\det(\mathbf{R}) = 1\)?
Question:
Section 3.3 mentions that the orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) is constructed as \((\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) with a skew‑symmetric \(\mathbf{Q}\). I understand that this yields an orthogonal matrix, but I am confused about:
1. Why does the Cayley transform guarantee orthogonality?
2. Why does it restrict the resulting matrix to the special orthogonal group (determinant +1) and not allow reflections?
3. In practice, does this limitation affect performance, and could we use an alternative parameterization to obtain full \(O(d)\)?
Answer:
The Cayley transform is a classic way of turning a **skew‑symmetric matrix** into an orthogonal matrix.  
If  

\[
\mathbf Q^{\top}=-\mathbf Q ,
\]

then the matrix  

\[
\mathbf R = (\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}
\tag{1}
\]

satisfies  

\[
\mathbf R^{\top}\mathbf R = \mathbf I .
\]

---

### Why (1) is always orthogonal  

Start from the definition of \(\mathbf R\). Right‑multiply (1) by \((\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)\) and left‑multiply by \((\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{\top}\):

\[
(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{\top}\,\mathbf R\,(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)= (\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{\top}(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q).
\]

Because \(\mathbf Q^{\top}=-\mathbf Q\),

\[
(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{\top}= \mathbf I+\mathbf Q .
\]

Hence the right‑hand side becomes  

\[
(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)= (\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)^2 .
\]

The left‑hand side is  

\[
(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)\,\bigl[(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}\bigr]\,
(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)= (\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)^2 .
\]

Since both sides are identical, we obtain  

\[
(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{\top}\,\mathbf R\,(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)= (\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{\top}(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q).
\]

Multiplying on the left by \((\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-\top}\) and on the right by \((\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}\) yields  

\[
\boxed{\;\; \mathbf R^{\top}\,\mathbf R = \mathbf I \;\;}
\]

which is exactly the definition of orthogonality. In other words, the Cayley map sends every skew‑symmetric \(\mathbf Q\) to a matrix on the orthogonal group \(O(d)\).

---

### Why the determinant is forced to be + 1  

The orthogonal group splits into two connected components:

* \(SO(d)=\{\mathbf R: \det \bigl(\!\mathbf R\bigr)=+1\}\) (rotations);
* \(O(d)\setminus SO(d)=\{\det(\!\mathbf R)= -1\}\) (reflections).

The Cayley map is **smooth and defined on a single connected component of** \(O(d)\).  
Indeed, for any skew‑symmetric \(\mathbf Q\) the matrix \((\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)\) is invertible (its eigenvalues are \(1-\lambda_i i,\; 1+\lambda_i i,\; 1,\dotsc\), none of which are zero), so the map never jumps across the “reflection” component. Moreover, when \(\|Q\|_2<1\) we can expand

```
R = (I+Q)(I–Q)^{-1}
    = (I+Q)(I+Q+Q^2+…)
    = I + 2Q + O(Q^2).
```

Thus for \(\|Q\\|\to 0\) we have \(R \to I\), whose determinant is \(+1\). Because the determinant varies continuously with the entries of a matrix, every matrix reachable by (1) must stay in the same connected component as the identity; consequently all such matrices satisfy

\[
\det (\!R)=+1 .
\]

In other words, the Cayley transform parametrises **only** the special orthogonal group \(SO(d)\); it cannot produce a reflection because a reflection would require crossing a point where \(\det=0$, which never occurs in (1).

---

### Does restricting to \(SO(d)\) matter for finetuning?  

In principle OFT could use any orthogonal matrix, including those with determinant − 1. In practice two facts make the restriction harmless:

1. **Rotations already give full expressivity for preserving pair‑wise angles.**  
   The hyperspherical energy depends only on inner products between normalized neurons. Multiplying all neurons in a layer by a rotation or a reflection changes every vector in exactly the same way, so both operations preserve all pair‑wise cosines. Hence any solution that minimizes the energy difference can be represented with a rotation; there is no need to invoke reflections.

2. **Empirical results in the paper show no degradation.**  
   Section 3.3 states “such a limitation does not affect the performance in practice”. The authors compare OFT/COFT (using Cayley‑parameterised \(SO(d)\)) with baselines that have no orthogonality constraint and observe faster convergence and higher image fidelity. The experiments on subject‑driven and controllable generation never exhibit a situation where a reflection would be advantageous.

If one really wants to explore the full orthogonal group, any convenient parametrisation can be swapped in place of the Cayley map—e.g. an exponential map \(\exp(\widehat{\Omega})\) with a skew‑symmetric generator \(\widehat{\Omega}\), or Householder reflections—a simple product of reflectors also yields arbitrary orthogonal matrices. These alternatives involve either an extra matrix exponential (costly but differentiable) or sequential reflectors (cheap but may need more parameters). The authors chose Cayley because it avoids costly eigen‑decompositions while still providing a differentiable closed‑form inverse.

---

### Summary  

* The Cayley transform turns a skew‑symmetric matrix into an orthogonal one because \((I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\) satisfies \(R^{T}R=I.\)
* Since it is continuous and passes through the identity, its image stays in the connected component containing \(I,\) i.e. \(SO(d)\); therefore every matrix produced has determinant + 1.
* For the purpose of Orthogonal Finetuning, restricting to rotations does not limit performance: rotations already preserve all pairwise neuron angles that define hyperspherical energy, and experiments confirm that reflections are unnecessary.
* If one wishes to allow reflections, any alternative parametrisation of \(O(d)\)—for example an exponential map or a product of Householder reflectors—can be used at the cost of additional computation or parameters.

Thus, the Cayley transform gives an efficient, differentiable way to enforce orthogonality (and automatically det = + 1), which is sufficient for OFT’s goal of preserving hyperspherical energy while providing enough flexibility to achieve strong finetuning performance.
