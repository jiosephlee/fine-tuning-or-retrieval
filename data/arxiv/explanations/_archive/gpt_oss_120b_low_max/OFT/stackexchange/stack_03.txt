\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### How does Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) enforce the \(\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\| \leq \epsilon\) constraint during training?
Question:
Equation (7) adds an explicit deviation bound on the orthogonal matrix. The paper says we can move this bound onto the skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) via a Neumann series approximation: \(\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q}\). I would like to know:
- How is the bound on \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\) derived from the original bound on \(\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|?\)
- What specific norm is used (spectral, Frobenius?)
- How is projected gradient descent applied to keep \(\|\mathbf{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'"?""
Answer:
**Answer**

In COFT the orthogonal matrix that is applied to a pretrained weight block,  

\[
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\qquad 
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I},
\]

is required to stay close to the identity,
\[
\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\le \varepsilon .
\tag{7}
\]

Because the Cayley transform is used to generate $\mathbf{R}$,

\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\qquad \mathbf{Q}^{\top}=-\mathbf{Q},
\tag{8}
\]

the authors replace the explicit constraint (7) by a simpler one on the underlying skew‑symmetric parameter $\mathbf{Q}$.  
Below is a step‑by‑step derivation and description of how the constraint is enforced during training.

---

### From $\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\le \varepsilon$ to a bound on $\|\mathbf{Q}\|$

The Cayley transform can be expanded as a Neumann series provided that $\|\mathbf{Q}\|<1$ in the *operator norm* (i.e. the spectral norm).  
Writing  

\[
(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{Q}^{k},
\]

we obtain

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{R}
&= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q}) \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{Q}^{k}
   = \bigl(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q}\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{I}+ \mathbf{Q}+ \mathbf{Q}^{2}+ \cdots\bigr)\\
&= \mathbf{I} + 2\,\mathbf{Q} + 2\,\mathbf{Q}^{2}+ 2\,\mathbf{Q}^{3}+ \cdots .
\end{aligned}
\]

If we truncate after the linear term (which is justified when $\|\mathbf{Q}\|$ is small), we get the first‑order approximation

\[
\boxed{\;\mathbf{R}\approx \mathbf{I}+2\,\mathbf{Q}\;}
\tag{9}
\]

with an error of order $\mathcal O(\|\mathbf Q\|^{2})$.

Now insert (9) into the original deviation bound:

\[
\|\mathbf R-\mathbf I\|
   = \|(\mathbf I+2\,\mathbf Q)-\mathbf I + \mathcal O(\|\mathbf Q\|^{2}) \|
   = 2\,\|\mathbf Q\| + \mathcal O(\|\mathbf Q\|^{2}) .
\]

Consequently, if we enforce  

\[
2\,\|\mathbf Q\| \le \varepsilon',
\qquad 
\text{i.e. }\;\|\mathbf Q\| \le \frac{\varepsilon'}2,
\tag{10}
\]

then for sufficiently small $\varepsilon'$ the original constraint (7) is satisfied up to higher‑order terms. The paper simply absorbs the factor 2 into a new hyper‑parameter and writes (10) as  

```
‖Q‖ ≤ ε′
```

which corresponds to $\|\mathbf Q\| \le \varepsilon'$.

Thus the bound on the skew‑symmetric matrix follows directly from the first‑order Taylor expansion of the Cayley map.

---

### Which norm is used?

The discussion of convergence of the Neumann series refers explicitly to the *operator norm*. In matrix analysis the operator norm coincides with the **spectral norm** (the largest singular value). Therefore, throughout the derivation and in the implementation the authors use  

* $\;\|\cdot\|\;$ = spectral norm.

The spectral norm is a natural choice because it controls how much a matrix can stretch any unit vector, exactly what is needed to guarantee that $(I−Q)$ remains invertible and that $R$ stays close to $I$. Using Frobenius norm would not give a sharp condition for invertibility, and it would lead to a looser bound.

---

### Enforcing the bound with projected gradient descent

During training we maintain a parameter matrix `Q` that is:

1. **Skew‑symmetric** – enforced by construction: after each gradient step we replace `Q` by  

   \[
   \widehat{\mathbf Q}= \tfrac12(\mathbf Q - \mathbf Q^{\top}),
   \]

   which projects any square matrix onto the space of skew‑symmetric matrices.

2. **Within the radius** – after enforcing skew‑symmetry we apply a projection onto the spectral‑norm ball of radius `ε′`.  
   The projection onto an ℓ₂ (spectral) norm ball has a closed form: compute an SVD  

   \[
   \widehat{\mathbf Q}=U\,{\rm diag}(\sigma_{1},..., \sigma_{d})\,V^{\top},
   \]

   clamp each singular value  

   \[
   \sigma_{i}^{\,{\rm proj}} = 
      \min(\sigma_{i},\,\varepsilon'),
   \]

   and reconstruct  

   \[
   {\bf Q}_{t+1}=U\,{\rm diag}(\sigma^{\,{\rm proj}})\,V^{\\top}.
   \]

   Because `Q` is already skew‑symmetric, its SVD simplifies to an eigendecomposition with purely imaginary eigenvalues; many implementations instead use eigenvalue clipping, which is computationally cheaper.

Putting it together, one training iteration looks like:

```
# forward pass: compute R = (I+Q)(I−Q)^−1
# loss L(R)
L.backward()                     # obtain dL/dQ
optimizer.step()                # ordinary SGD/Adam update
# projection step:
Q = 0.5 * (Q - Q.T)             # enforce skew symmetry
U, S, V = svd(Q, full_matrices=False)
S = torch.clamp(S, max=eps_prime)
Q = U @ torch.diag(S) @ V       # spectral‑norm projection
```

The projection step does not interfere with gradient flow because it occurs **after** the optimizer step; only the next forward pass uses the projected `Q`. In practice `ε′` is chosen as a small constant (e.g. $10^{-2}$–$10^{-1}$) so that $\|\!R-\!I\!\|$ stays within a tight neighbourhood of identity while still allowing enough freedom for meaningful finetuning.

---

### Summary

* The deviation bound on `R` ($‖R−I‖≤ε$) is transferred to a bound on `Q` by expanding the Cayley transform with a Neumann series and keeping only the linear term, yielding $‖Q‖≤ε′/2$.  
* The norm involved is **the spectral (operator) norm**, because it guarantees convergence of the Neumann series and bounds how far an orthogonal matrix can stray from identity.  
* In training COFT we keep `Q` skew‑symmetric by explicit symmetrisation and enforce $‖Q‖≤ε′$ using projected gradient descent: after each optimizer update we project onto the spectral‑norm ball and onto the space of skew‑symmetric matrices. This projection guarantees that every subsequent Cayley‑generated `R` satisfies the original radius constraint.

This procedure yields an orthogonal transformation that stays inside an ε‑ball around the pretrained weights, giving COFT its improved stability while still allowing enough flexibility for downstream task adaptation.
