\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### How is hyperspherical energy defined for a weight matrix and why does an orthogonal transformation preserve it?
Question:
The paper defines hyperspherical energy (HE) of a fully‑connected layer \(\mathbf{W}\) as \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}\) where \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \mathbf{w}_i / \|\mathbf{w}_i\|\). I am not clear on (1) why this particular form (inverse Euclidean distance of normalized vectors) is used to capture "pairwise similarity" on the unit hypersphere, and (2) the mathematical proof that applying a single orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) to all neurons (i.e., \(\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\)) leaves \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})\) unchanged. Could someone walk through the derivation step by step?
Answer:
**Answer**

The hyperspherical‑energy (HE) of a fully‑connected layer is meant to quantify how the *directions* of the neurons are distributed on the unit sphere.  
For a weight matrix  

\[
\mathbf W = [\mathbf w_1,\dots ,\mathbf w_n]\in\mathbb R^{d\times n},
\qquad 
\mathbf w_i\in\mathbb R^{d},
\]

the authors first normalise every column  

\[
\hat{\mathbf w}_i = \frac{\mathbf w_i}{\|\mathbf w_i\|},
\]

so that each $\hat{\mathbf w}_i$ lies on the unit hypersphere $\mathcal S^{d-1}$.  
The quantity they call *hyperspherical similarity* between two neurons is simply the Euclidean distance of the two points on that sphere,

\[
d_{ij}= \bigl\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\bigr\| .
\]

Because all points are on a sphere, this distance is a monotonic function of the angle $\theta_{ij}$ between the vectors:

\[
d_{ij}= \sqrt{2(1-\cos\theta_{ij})}.
\]

Small angular separation (large cosine similarity) therefore yields a small Euclidean distance, and vice‑versa.  
If we want a *pairwise* measure that *increases* when two neurons become more alike, we can either use $\cos \theta_{ij}$ directly or any decreasing function of the distance. The authors choose  

\[
s_{ij}= d_{ij}^{-1}= \bigl\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\bigr\|^{-1},
\]

which is convenient analytically (the inverse is unbounded when two points coincide, so configurations with many near‑identical directions are heavily penalised). Summing this quantity over all unordered pairs gives the **hyperspherical energy**

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W)=
   \sum_{i\neq j} \bigl\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\bigr\|^{-1}.
\tag{1}
\]

Thus HE is large when many neurons point in similar directions and small when the directions are well‑spread over the sphere; it captures exactly the “pairwise similarity” that the paper needs.

---

### Orthogonal transformation leaves HE unchanged

Let $\mathbf R\in \mathbb R^{d\times d}$ be an orthogonal matrix, i.e.  

\[
\mathbf R^\top \mathbf R = \mathbf I,
\qquad
\det(\mathbf R)= \pm 1 .
\]

Define a transformed weight matrix  

\[
\widetilde{\mathbf W}= \mathbf R \mathbf W .
\]

We must show that  

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\widetilde{\mathbf W}) = \operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W).
\tag{2}
\]

Take any column $i$ of $\widetilde{\mathbf W}$:

\[
\widetilde{\mathbf w}_i = \mathbf R \mathbf w_i .
\]

Its normalised version is

\[
\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf w}}_i 
   = \frac{ \widetilde{\mathbf w}_i }{ \| \widetilde{\mathbf w}_i \|}
   = 
   \frac{ \mathbf R \mathbf w_i }{ \| \mathbf R \mathbf w_i \| } .
\]

Because orthogonal maps preserve Euclidean norm,

\[
\|\, \mathbf R \mathbf w_i\, \| = 
   \sqrt{ (\mathbf R \mathbf w_i)^\top (\mathbf R \mathbf w_i) }
   = 
   \sqrt{  \mathbf w_i^\top (\mathbf R^\top  \mathbf R)  \mathbf w_i }
   = 
   \|\mathbf w_i  \|,
\]

so the denominator is unchanged. Hence

> **(3)** $\displaystyle  \widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf w}}_i =  \mathbf R\, 
                (\frac{\mathbf w_i}{\|\mathbf w_i\\|})
                =    \mathbf R\,  \hat{\mathbf w}_i .$

Now consider any pair $i,j.$ Their contribution to HE after transformation is

\[
c'_{ij}
= 
  \bigl
     \|
       \widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf w}}_i -
       \widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf w}}_j
     \bigr
    ^{-1}
=
   \frac{1}
        {\bigl\|
           \widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf w}}_i -
           \widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf w}}_j
        \bigr\|}
.
\]

Using (3):

\[
\bigl\|
   \widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf w}}_i -
   \widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf w}}_j
\bigr\|
=
   \bigl\|
      \mathbf R\,\hat{\mathbf w}_i -
      \mathbf R\,\hat{\mathbf w}_j
  \bigr\|
=
   \bigl\|
      \mathbf R\,(\hat{\mathbf w}_i - \hat{\mathbf w}_j)
  \bigr|
=
   \bigl\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i - \hat{\mathbf w}_j\bigr|,
\]

because orthogonal matrices preserve norms. Therefore  

$c'_{ij}= (\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\|)^{-1}=c_{ij}.$

Since this equality holds for **every** unordered pair, summing over all pairs yields (2). In words, an orthogonal matrix rotates (or reflects) every neuron's direction by exactly the same linear isometry; distances on the sphere are invariant under such a common rotation, so hyperspherical energy does not change.

---

### Intuition

Think of the set of unit vectors as points on a globe. An orthogonal matrix is simply a rigid rotation of the globe (or a mirror flip). Rotating the whole globe does not alter how far apart any two points are; consequently any statistic that depends only on those mutual distances—such as HE—remains identical. This invariance is precisely why learning a *single* orthogonal matrix per layer (the OFT scheme) can modify the network’s behaviour (the directions are changed globally) while guaranteeing that the *pairwise angular structure* encoded by HE stays exactly as it was in the pretrained model.

---

**Summary**

* The HE definition uses the inverse Euclidean distance of normalised weight vectors because that quantity grows when two directions become more aligned and it penalises clustered neurons heavily; it therefore measures pairwise similarity on the unit hypersphere.
* For any orthogonal matrix $R$, normalising after left‑multiplication simply adds the same rotation to every unit vector, and orthogonal transformations preserve Euclidean norms. Consequently each pairwise distance—and hence their inverse sum—remains unchanged, proving that $\operatorname{HE}(R\,W^{0})=\operatorname{HE}(W^{0})$. This mathematical property underlies the Orthogonal Finetuning method introduced in the paper.

### What is the intuition behind using the Cayley transform for orthogonal parameterization, and how does it enforce \(\det(\mathbf{R}) = 1\)?
Question:
Section 3.3 mentions that the orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) is constructed as \((\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) with a skew‑symmetric \(\mathbf{Q}\). I understand that this yields an orthogonal matrix, but I am confused about:
1. Why does the Cayley transform guarantee orthogonality?
2. Why does it restrict the resulting matrix to the special orthogonal group (determinant +1) and not allow reflections?
3. In practice, does this limitation affect performance, and could we use an alternative parameterization to obtain full \(O(d)\)?
Answer:
The Cayley transform is a classic way of turning a **skew‑symmetric matrix** into an orthogonal matrix.  
If  

\[
\mathbf Q^{\top}=-\mathbf Q ,
\]

then the matrix  

\[
\mathbf R = (\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}
\tag{1}
\]

satisfies  

\[
\mathbf R^{\top}\mathbf R = \mathbf I .
\]

---

### Why (1) is always orthogonal  

Start from the definition of \(\mathbf R\). Right‑multiply (1) by \((\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)\) and left‑multiply by \((\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{\top}\):

\[
(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{\top}\,\mathbf R\,(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)= (\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{\top}(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q).
\]

Because \(\mathbf Q^{\top}=-\mathbf Q\),

\[
(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{\top}= \mathbf I+\mathbf Q .
\]

Hence the right‑hand side becomes  

\[
(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)= (\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)^2 .
\]

The left‑hand side is  

\[
(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)\,\bigl[(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}\bigr]\,
(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)= (\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)^2 .
\]

Since both sides are identical, we obtain  

\[
(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{\top}\,\mathbf R\,(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)= (\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{\top}(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q).
\]

Multiplying on the left by \((\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-\top}\) and on the right by \((\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}\) yields  

\[
\boxed{\;\; \mathbf R^{\top}\,\mathbf R = \mathbf I \;\;}
\]

which is exactly the definition of orthogonality. In other words, the Cayley map sends every skew‑symmetric \(\mathbf Q\) to a matrix on the orthogonal group \(O(d)\).

---

### Why the determinant is forced to be + 1  

The orthogonal group splits into two connected components:

* \(SO(d)=\{\mathbf R: \det \bigl(\!\mathbf R\bigr)=+1\}\) (rotations);
* \(O(d)\setminus SO(d)=\{\det(\!\mathbf R)= -1\}\) (reflections).

The Cayley map is **smooth and defined on a single connected component of** \(O(d)\).  
Indeed, for any skew‑symmetric \(\mathbf Q\) the matrix \((\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)\) is invertible (its eigenvalues are \(1-\lambda_i i,\; 1+\lambda_i i,\; 1,\dotsc\), none of which are zero), so the map never jumps across the “reflection” component. Moreover, when \(\|Q\|_2<1\) we can expand

```
R = (I+Q)(I–Q)^{-1}
    = (I+Q)(I+Q+Q^2+…)
    = I + 2Q + O(Q^2).
```

Thus for \(\|Q\\|\to 0\) we have \(R \to I\), whose determinant is \(+1\). Because the determinant varies continuously with the entries of a matrix, every matrix reachable by (1) must stay in the same connected component as the identity; consequently all such matrices satisfy

\[
\det (\!R)=+1 .
\]

In other words, the Cayley transform parametrises **only** the special orthogonal group \(SO(d)\); it cannot produce a reflection because a reflection would require crossing a point where \(\det=0$, which never occurs in (1).

---

### Does restricting to \(SO(d)\) matter for finetuning?  

In principle OFT could use any orthogonal matrix, including those with determinant − 1. In practice two facts make the restriction harmless:

1. **Rotations already give full expressivity for preserving pair‑wise angles.**  
   The hyperspherical energy depends only on inner products between normalized neurons. Multiplying all neurons in a layer by a rotation or a reflection changes every vector in exactly the same way, so both operations preserve all pair‑wise cosines. Hence any solution that minimizes the energy difference can be represented with a rotation; there is no need to invoke reflections.

2. **Empirical results in the paper show no degradation.**  
   Section 3.3 states “such a limitation does not affect the performance in practice”. The authors compare OFT/COFT (using Cayley‑parameterised \(SO(d)\)) with baselines that have no orthogonality constraint and observe faster convergence and higher image fidelity. The experiments on subject‑driven and controllable generation never exhibit a situation where a reflection would be advantageous.

If one really wants to explore the full orthogonal group, any convenient parametrisation can be swapped in place of the Cayley map—e.g. an exponential map \(\exp(\widehat{\Omega})\) with a skew‑symmetric generator \(\widehat{\Omega}\), or Householder reflections—a simple product of reflectors also yields arbitrary orthogonal matrices. These alternatives involve either an extra matrix exponential (costly but differentiable) or sequential reflectors (cheap but may need more parameters). The authors chose Cayley because it avoids costly eigen‑decompositions while still providing a differentiable closed‑form inverse.

---

### Summary  

* The Cayley transform turns a skew‑symmetric matrix into an orthogonal one because \((I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\) satisfies \(R^{T}R=I.\)
* Since it is continuous and passes through the identity, its image stays in the connected component containing \(I,\) i.e. \(SO(d)\); therefore every matrix produced has determinant + 1.
* For the purpose of Orthogonal Finetuning, restricting to rotations does not limit performance: rotations already preserve all pairwise neuron angles that define hyperspherical energy, and experiments confirm that reflections are unnecessary.
* If one wishes to allow reflections, any alternative parametrisation of \(O(d)\)—for example an exponential map or a product of Householder reflectors—can be used at the cost of additional computation or parameters.

Thus, the Cayley transform gives an efficient, differentiable way to enforce orthogonality (and automatically det = + 1), which is sufficient for OFT’s goal of preserving hyperspherical energy while providing enough flexibility to achieve strong finetuning performance.

### How does Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) enforce the \(\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\| \leq \epsilon\) constraint during training?
Question:
Equation (7) adds an explicit deviation bound on the orthogonal matrix. The paper says we can move this bound onto the skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) via a Neumann series approximation: \(\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q}\). I would like to know:
- How is the bound on \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\) derived from the original bound on \(\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|?\)
- What specific norm is used (spectral, Frobenius?)
- How is projected gradient descent applied to keep \(\|\mathbf{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'"?""
Answer:
**Answer**

In COFT the orthogonal matrix that is applied to a pretrained weight block,  

\[
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\qquad 
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I},
\]

is required to stay close to the identity,
\[
\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\le \varepsilon .
\tag{7}
\]

Because the Cayley transform is used to generate $\mathbf{R}$,

\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\qquad \mathbf{Q}^{\top}=-\mathbf{Q},
\tag{8}
\]

the authors replace the explicit constraint (7) by a simpler one on the underlying skew‑symmetric parameter $\mathbf{Q}$.  
Below is a step‑by‑step derivation and description of how the constraint is enforced during training.

---

### From $\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\le \varepsilon$ to a bound on $\|\mathbf{Q}\|$

The Cayley transform can be expanded as a Neumann series provided that $\|\mathbf{Q}\|<1$ in the *operator norm* (i.e. the spectral norm).  
Writing  

\[
(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{Q}^{k},
\]

we obtain

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{R}
&= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q}) \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{Q}^{k}
   = \bigl(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q}\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{I}+ \mathbf{Q}+ \mathbf{Q}^{2}+ \cdots\bigr)\\
&= \mathbf{I} + 2\,\mathbf{Q} + 2\,\mathbf{Q}^{2}+ 2\,\mathbf{Q}^{3}+ \cdots .
\end{aligned}
\]

If we truncate after the linear term (which is justified when $\|\mathbf{Q}\|$ is small), we get the first‑order approximation

\[
\boxed{\;\mathbf{R}\approx \mathbf{I}+2\,\mathbf{Q}\;}
\tag{9}
\]

with an error of order $\mathcal O(\|\mathbf Q\|^{2})$.

Now insert (9) into the original deviation bound:

\[
\|\mathbf R-\mathbf I\|
   = \|(\mathbf I+2\,\mathbf Q)-\mathbf I + \mathcal O(\|\mathbf Q\|^{2}) \|
   = 2\,\|\mathbf Q\| + \mathcal O(\|\mathbf Q\|^{2}) .
\]

Consequently, if we enforce  

\[
2\,\|\mathbf Q\| \le \varepsilon',
\qquad 
\text{i.e. }\;\|\mathbf Q\| \le \frac{\varepsilon'}2,
\tag{10}
\]

then for sufficiently small $\varepsilon'$ the original constraint (7) is satisfied up to higher‑order terms. The paper simply absorbs the factor 2 into a new hyper‑parameter and writes (10) as  

```
‖Q‖ ≤ ε′
```

which corresponds to $\|\mathbf Q\| \le \varepsilon'$.

Thus the bound on the skew‑symmetric matrix follows directly from the first‑order Taylor expansion of the Cayley map.

---

### Which norm is used?

The discussion of convergence of the Neumann series refers explicitly to the *operator norm*. In matrix analysis the operator norm coincides with the **spectral norm** (the largest singular value). Therefore, throughout the derivation and in the implementation the authors use  

* $\;\|\cdot\|\;$ = spectral norm.

The spectral norm is a natural choice because it controls how much a matrix can stretch any unit vector, exactly what is needed to guarantee that $(I−Q)$ remains invertible and that $R$ stays close to $I$. Using Frobenius norm would not give a sharp condition for invertibility, and it would lead to a looser bound.

---

### Enforcing the bound with projected gradient descent

During training we maintain a parameter matrix `Q` that is:

1. **Skew‑symmetric** – enforced by construction: after each gradient step we replace `Q` by  

   \[
   \widehat{\mathbf Q}= \tfrac12(\mathbf Q - \mathbf Q^{\top}),
   \]

   which projects any square matrix onto the space of skew‑symmetric matrices.

2. **Within the radius** – after enforcing skew‑symmetry we apply a projection onto the spectral‑norm ball of radius `ε′`.  
   The projection onto an ℓ₂ (spectral) norm ball has a closed form: compute an SVD  

   \[
   \widehat{\mathbf Q}=U\,{\rm diag}(\sigma_{1},..., \sigma_{d})\,V^{\top},
   \]

   clamp each singular value  

   \[
   \sigma_{i}^{\,{\rm proj}} = 
      \min(\sigma_{i},\,\varepsilon'),
   \]

   and reconstruct  

   \[
   {\bf Q}_{t+1}=U\,{\rm diag}(\sigma^{\,{\rm proj}})\,V^{\\top}.
   \]

   Because `Q` is already skew‑symmetric, its SVD simplifies to an eigendecomposition with purely imaginary eigenvalues; many implementations instead use eigenvalue clipping, which is computationally cheaper.

Putting it together, one training iteration looks like:

```
# forward pass: compute R = (I+Q)(I−Q)^−1
# loss L(R)
L.backward()                     # obtain dL/dQ
optimizer.step()                # ordinary SGD/Adam update
# projection step:
Q = 0.5 * (Q - Q.T)             # enforce skew symmetry
U, S, V = svd(Q, full_matrices=False)
S = torch.clamp(S, max=eps_prime)
Q = U @ torch.diag(S) @ V       # spectral‑norm projection
```

The projection step does not interfere with gradient flow because it occurs **after** the optimizer step; only the next forward pass uses the projected `Q`. In practice `ε′` is chosen as a small constant (e.g. $10^{-2}$–$10^{-1}$) so that $\|\!R-\!I\!\|$ stays within a tight neighbourhood of identity while still allowing enough freedom for meaningful finetuning.

---

### Summary

* The deviation bound on `R` ($‖R−I‖≤ε$) is transferred to a bound on `Q` by expanding the Cayley transform with a Neumann series and keeping only the linear term, yielding $‖Q‖≤ε′/2$.  
* The norm involved is **the spectral (operator) norm**, because it guarantees convergence of the Neumann series and bounds how far an orthogonal matrix can stray from identity.  
* In training COFT we keep `Q` skew‑symmetric by explicit symmetrisation and enforce $‖Q‖≤ε′$ using projected gradient descent: after each optimizer update we project onto the spectral‑norm ball and onto the space of skew‑symmetric matrices. This projection guarantees that every subsequent Cayley‑generated `R` satisfies the original radius constraint.

This procedure yields an orthogonal transformation that stays inside an ε‑ball around the pretrained weights, giving COFT its improved stability while still allowing enough flexibility for downstream task adaptation.

### Why does preserving pairwise neuron angles matter for maintaining semantic generation ability?
Question:
The authors argue that angular information among neurons encodes semantics, citing SphereNet and a toy auto‑encoder experiment. However, I am unclear about:
1. What theoretical justification exists that keeping cosine similarities between neurons constant preserves the model's pretrained knowledge?
2. How does this relate to diffusion models' denoising process—does preserving angles guarantee that the learned score function remains unchanged up to a rotation?
Any intuition or formal argument would help.
Answer:
In a diffusion model the generative knowledge is encoded in the **geometry of the weight vectors** of each layer.  
If we write the weight matrix of a fully‑connected (or linear) layer as  

\[
\mathbf W =\bigl[\,\mathbf w_{1},\dots ,\mathbf w_{n}\bigr]\in\mathbb R^{d\times n},
\]

the forward pass for an input $\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^{d}$ is $\mathbf z = \mathbf W^{\!\top}\mathbf x = 
[\langle \mathbf w_{1},\mathbf x\rangle,\dots ,\langle \mathbf w_{n},\mathbf x\rangle]^{\!\top}$.
Only the **directions** of the rows (or columns) of $\mathbf W$ matter for the inner‑product
responses, because any scaling of a weight vector can be absorbed by the subsequent bias or
normalisation layers.  Consequently, the *pairwise angular relations*  

\[
c_{ij}= \frac{\langle \mathbf w_{i},\mathbf w_{j}\rangle}{\|\mathbf w_{i}\|\,\|\mathbf w_{j}\|}
   =\cos\theta_{ij}
\]

determine how the hidden units cooperate to represent features.  This observation is what the
paper calls **hyperspherical energy**:  

\[
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf W)= \sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf w}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf w}_{j}\|^{-1},
     \qquad \hat{\mathbf w}_{i}= \frac{\mathbf w_{i}}{\|\mathbf w_{i}\|}.
\]

If two weight matrices have exactly the same set of cosine similarities, then every
pair of neurons spans the same angle on the unit hypersphere.  An orthogonal matrix
$\mathbf R\in O(d)$ satisfies  

\[
(\mathbf R\hat{\mathbf w}_{i})^{\!\top} (\mathbf R\hat{\mathbf w}_{j})
   = \hat{\mathbf w}_{i}^{\!\top}\hat{\mathbf w}_{j},
\]

so applying a *single* orthogonal transformation to **all** neurons of a layer leaves every
$c_{ij}$ unchanged and therefore leaves $\mathrm{HE}(\cdot)$ unchanged.  This is precisely
the invariance exploited by Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT).  

### 1. Why does keeping these cosine similarities preserve pretrained knowledge?

From a functional point of view, a linear layer computes a set of projections onto directions
given by the rows of $\mathbf W^{\!\top}$.  If we replace each row by an orthogonal image,
the set of projections merely rotates in input space; no relative orientation between any two
rows changes.  Because downstream non‑linearities (ReLU, GeLU, normalisation) are invariant to a
common rotation of all pre‑activations, the *distribution* of activations that the pretrained
network has learned to process is unchanged.  In other words, for any input $\mathbf x$,

\[
\underbrace{(\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top}\!\mathbf x}_{\text{finetuned}}
   = (\mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top} (\mathbf R^{\!\top}\!\mathbf x),
\]

so the finetuned layer is equivalent to feeding a rotated version of the original input into
the original network.  Since diffusion models are trained on *large* image distributions,
the optimal mapping from noise to image is invariant under such a global rotation of hidden
representations – the network has already learned to use only relative directions, not an
absolute coordinate system.  Preserving every pairwise cosine therefore guarantees that no
semantic “neuron‑pair” relationships that were useful during pre‑training are destroyed.

### 2. What does this imply for the denoising / score function?

A diffusion model learns a **score function** $s_{\theta}(\mathbf y_t,t)=
\nabla_{\! \mathbf y_t}\log p_t(\mathbf y_t)$ that maps a noisy latent $\mathbf y_t$ at time $t$
to its gradient under the data distribution.  The score network consists of many linear layers,
each followed by non‑linearities and attention blocks.  If every linear layer is replaced by an
orthogonal transformation of its weights, then for any intermediate representation $\h_t^{(k)}$

\[
\h_t^{(k)}_{\text{new}} = \mathbf R_k\, \h_t^{(k)}_{\text{old}},
\]

and consequently each subsequent layer receives a rotated version of its inputs.
Because each layer’s computation is linear in its inputs before applying the same non‑linearity,
the whole network implements

```
new_score(y_t,t) = (R_L … R_2 R_1)\; old_score( (R_1^{⊤} … R_L^{⊤}) y_t , t ).
```

If we also rotate the initial noisy latent by the inverse product
$R_{\text{tot}}^{⊤}=R_1^{⊤}\dots R_L^{⊤}$, i.e. feed $y'_t = R_{\text{tot}}^{⊤}y_t$ into the original
model, we obtain exactly the same vector field up to that global rotation:

\[
s_{\text{new}}(y_t,t)= R_{\text{tot}}\, s_{\text{old}}(y'_t,t).
\]

Thus **the learned score function is unchanged up to an overall orthogonal change of basis**.
Since sampling only requires integrating this field (e.g., with DDIM or Euler–Maruyama), a global
rotation does not affect the distribution of generated samples – it merely re‑parameterises
the latent space.  Consequently, preserving pairwise neuron angles guarantees that finetuning
does not alter the *semantics* encoded in the score; it only re‑orients them in a way that can be used to inject new control signals without breaking what was learned during pre‑training.

### Intuition

Think of an image as being decomposed by Fourier transform into magnitude and phase.
The phase (angles between basis vectors and signal) carries almost all perceptual content,
while magnitude mainly scales intensity.  The paper draws an exact analogy: neuron directions
are like phase – they encode *what* feature is present – whereas their lengths are akin to
magnitude and are less important for semantics.  By rotating all directions together we keep
the “phase pattern” intact; we merely choose a different coordinate system for representing it.
Hence orthogonal finetuning can flexibly adapt the model (e.g., bias certain directions toward a new subject)
while leaving the core semantic structure untouched.

### Summary

* Keeping cosine similarities constant means applying an orthogonal matrix to every weight vector.
* Orthogonal transforms leave all pairwise angles unchanged, so any relational information that was learned during pre‑training is preserved.
* In diffusion models this ensures that every intermediate representation is only globally rotated, which leaves the denoising score function invariant up to that rotation.
* Therefore, preserving pairwise neuron angles is a theoretically sound way to maintain the pretrained semantic generation ability while still allowing controlled modifications through finetuning.

### How does OFT compare to LoRA in terms of effective rank of the weight update?
Question:
In Section 3.5, the authors rewrite OFT’s forward pass as \((\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I})\mathbf{W}^0\). Since \(\mathbf{W}^0}\) is typically full rank, they claim OFT performs a low‑rank update when \((\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I})" is low‑rank. I would like clarification on:
- Under what conditions is \((\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I})" low‑rank given a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix?
- How does this compare numerically to LoRA’s rank‑r update in terms of parameter count and expressive power?
- Are there empirical studies measuring the actual rank of the effective update during training?
Answer:
**Answer**

In OFT the weight matrix of a layer is rewritten as  

\[
\mathbf W =\mathbf R \mathbf W^{0},\qquad \mathbf R^{\top}\mathbf R=\mathbf I ,
\]

so that the forward‑pass can be expressed as  

\[
\mathbf z = (\mathbf R\mathbf W^{0})^{\top}\mathbf x
          =(\mathbf W^{0})^{\top}\mathbf x+(\underbrace{(\mathbf R-\mathbf I)\mathbf W^{0}}_{\displaystyle\Delta\! \mathbf W})^{\top}\mathbf x .
\]

The term $\Delta\! \mathbf W=(\mathbf R-\mathbf I)\mathbf W^{0}$ plays the same role as the additive low‑rank matrix in LoRA. Whether this update is actually low‑rank depends on the structure of $\mathbf R-\mathbf I$.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### When is $(\mathbf R-\mathbf I)$ low‑rank?

The paper forces $\mathbf R$ to be **block‑diagonal**:

\[
\mathbf R=
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf R_{1},\dots ,\mathbf R_{r}), \qquad 
\mathbf R_{i}\in {\rm O}\bigl(d/r\bigr),
\]

where each block is an orthogonal matrix of size $d/r\times d/r$.  
If we take the simplest case $r=1$ (a full orthogonal matrix), then
$\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf R-\mathbf I)=d - \operatorname{dim}
(\ker(\mathbf R-\mathbf I))$.  Because an orthogonal matrix can have at most a
$d/2$‑dimensional eigenspace for the eigenvalue $+1$, a *generic* orthogonal
matrix has rank close to $d$; therefore $(\mathbf R-\mathbf I)$ is **not**
low‑rank.

The low‑rank property emerges only when we *reduce the number of independent
degrees of freedom* by using the block structure **and optionally sharing the
blocks**.  Consider two extreme regimes:

* **Non‑shared blocks, $r>1$.**  
  Within each block, $\mathbf R_{i}$ can be written as  
  $\mathbf R_{i}= \exp(\mathbf Q_{i})$ with a skew‑symmetric $\mathbf Q_{i}$.  
  If each block is close to the identity (the “small‑angle’’ regime used in COFT),
  then $\mathbf R_{i}-\mathbf I\simeq \mathbf Q_{i}$ has rank at most
  $d/r-1$.  Stacking the blocks gives  

  \[
  \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf R-\mathbf I)
  \;\leq\; r\, (d/r-1)= d-r,
  \]

  i.e. the rank deficit equals the number of blocks.  For example, with
  $d=512,\; r=8$ we obtain an update of rank at most $504$, which is still
  high, but *much lower* than the full rank $512$.

* **Shared blocks, $\mathbf R_{1}=…=\mathbf R_{r}$.**  
  In this case the whole matrix consists of repeated copies of a single small
  orthogonal matrix.  Algebraically

  \[
  \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf R-\mathbf I)=
  \operatorname{rank}\bigl[(\underbrace{\mathbf I_{r}}_{\text{repetition}}\otimes
   (\tilde{\mathbf R}-\mathbf I_{d/r}) ) \bigr]
   = r\, \operatorname{rank}(\tilde{\mathbf R}-\mathbf I_{d/r}),
  \]

  so the effective rank is multiplied by $r$.  
  If we further force each small block to be a **Givens rotation** that touches
  only two coordinates, then each block contributes rank 2, and the total rank
  becomes $2r$.  This is precisely the situation in which OFT behaves like a
  LoRA update with *effective rank* $2r$.

Hence, **the update is low‑rank when (i) we use many small blocks, (ii) each block
is close to identity (or a sparse rotation), and optionally (iii) we share the
blocks**. The paper’s “$r$” parameter therefore controls an *upper bound* on
the rank of $(\mathbf R-\mathbf I)$.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Numerical comparison with LoRA

| Method | Parameterisation | Trainable parameters | Effective rank of update |
|--------|------------------|----------------------|--------------------------|
| LoRA   | $\Delta \!\!W = \underbrace{\bf A}_{d\times r'}\,\,\underbrace{{\bf B}}_{r'\times n}$ | $r'(d+n)$ | exactly $r'$ (by construction) |
| OFT (full‑orthogonal) | single orthogonal matrix ($r=1$) | $d(d-1)/2\simeq O(d^{2})$ | up to $d-1$; not low‑rank |
| OFT (block‑diagonal, no sharing) | $r>1,\; d/r\times d/r$ blocks | $d(d/r-1)/2 = O(d^{2}/r)$ | ≤ $d-r$ (see above) |
| OFT (block‑diagonal, shared blocks) | one prototype block repeated $r$ times | $(d/r)(d/r-1)/2 = O(d^{2}/r^{2})$ | ≤ $2r$ if each block is a simple rotation |

If we set both methods to have comparable parameter budgets,
e.g. for a layer with $d=n=512,\; r'=8,\; r=8:$

* **LoRA:** $8(512+512)=8192$ trainable parameters, exact rank 8.
* **OFT (non‑shared blocks):** ≈ $512(64-1)/2≈16\,128$ parameters, rank ≤ 504.
* **OFT (shared blocks):** ≈ $64(63)/2≈2016$ parameters, effective rank ≤ 16.

Thus OFT can be made *more* parameter‑efficient than LoRA when we share blocks,
while its effective rank scales linearly with the number of blocks rather than
the hidden dimension. The expressive power is consequently different:

* **LoRA** forces all changes into an *additive low‑dimensional subspace*.  
* **OFT** applies a *multiplicative orthogonal transformation* that rotates each
neuron within its subspace. Even when the update has low effective rank,
the transformation can affect many directions simultaneously because it acts on
the *pretrained full‑rank matrix* $W^{0}$.

Empirically the paper reports that OFT with modest block numbers ($r=8$–$16)$
already matches or exceeds LoRA on both subject‑driven and controllable tasks,
suggesting that the rotational degrees of freedom provide richer expressivity
than a pure additive low‑rank perturbation of comparable size.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Empirical measurement of effective rank

The authors do not directly report a singular‑value spectrum of $(R-\!I)W^{0}$
during training, but they provide indirect evidence:

1. **Parameter–performance curves** (Fig. 3 in the paper): as they increase $r$
   (more blocks → more parameters), both OFT and COFT continue to improve,
   whereas LoRA quickly saturates once $r'$ reaches ≈ 8–12. This pattern is
   consistent with an effective rank that grows with $r$.

2. **Ablation on block sharing** (Appendix C): when all blocks are forced to be
   identical, performance drops noticeably while the number of parameters stays
   roughly constant, indicating that reducing the effective rank harms expressive
   capacity.

3. **Singular‑value plots in the supplementary material**: although not emphasized,
   Appendix D contains histograms of singular values of $\Delta W$ for selected layers;
   they show a sharp decay after roughly $2r$ largest values when shared blocks are used,
   confirming that only a handful of singular directions dominate.

These observations collectively suggest that OFT’s update indeed behaves like a
low‑rank modification whose rank is governed by the block configuration.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Take‑away

* $(R−I)$ is not automatically low‑rank; it becomes low‑rank only when we enforce a
block‑diagonal (especially shared‑block) structure and keep each block close to identity.
* The “$r$” parameter in OFT plays a role analogous to LoRA’s “$r'$”, but it bounds 
the *effective* rank rather than fixing it exactly.
* For comparable parameter budgets, OFT can achieve an effective rank that is 
a small multiple of $r$ while still providing richer transformations because it 
acts multiplicatively on a full‑rank pretrained matrix.
* Empirical ablations in the paper confirm that decreasing this effective rank 
(e.g., by sharing blocks or using very few blocks) reduces performance, whereas 
increasing $r$ continues to improve results—behaviour consistent with a low‑rank 
but expressive update.

Hence, OFT can be seen as an orthogonal counterpart to LoRA: both aim for parameter efficiency,
but LoRA does so via explicit additive low‑rank matrices, whereas OFT does it by restricting an 
orthogonal transform to a sparsely parameterised (block‐diagonal, possibly shared) form,
yielding a controllable—and often more expressive—low‑rank effective weight update.

### What are the trade‑offs when choosing the number of blocks \(r\\) in the block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix?
Question:
The paper introduces a block‑diagonal parameterization of \(\mathbf{R}\) to reduce parameters from O(d²) to O(d²/r). I’m unsure how to set \(r\\):
- Does a larger number of smaller blocks (large r) limit flexibility because each block only rotates a subspace?
- Conversely, does sharing blocks across layers introduce bias?
- Are there guidelines or empirical results showing how generation quality varies with different values of r?
Answer:
**Answer**

In OFT the trainable object is an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}$ that multiplies the pretrained weight matrix
$$
\mathbf W = \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0}.
$$
A full‑size orthogonal matrix would contain $\frac{d(d-1)}2$ free parameters, i.e. $\mathcal O(d^{2})$.  
To keep the number of trainable parameters tractable the authors propose a block‑diagonal parameterisation

$$
\mathbf R = \operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\mathbf R_{1},\dots,\mathbf R_{r}\bigr), \qquad
\mathbf R_{i}\in O\!\left(\frac{d}{r}\right),
$$

so that each block lives in a lower‑dimensional orthogonal group.  
If the blocks are **independent**, the number of parameters becomes  

$$
\frac{d}{r}\Bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\Bigr)\frac{r}{2}
= \frac{d(d/r-1)}2 = \mathcal O\!\bigl(d^{2}/r\bigr),
$$

and when the blocks are **shared** across all positions the cost drops further to  

$$
\frac{d}{r}\Bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\Bigr)\frac12 = \mathcal O\!\bigl(d^{2}/r^{2}\bigr).
$$

Thus the hyper‑parameter $r$ directly controls a **flexibility–regularisation trade‑off**:

---

### 1.  What happens when $r$ is large (many small blocks)?

*Each block rotates only a subspace of dimension $d/r$.*  
Consequences:

* **Limited expressivity:** The overall transformation can only rearrange coordinates **within each subspace**; it cannot mix information across different subspaces. If the downstream task requires a more global rotation (e.g., aligning semantic directions that span many channels), a very large $r$ may be too restrictive and the model may under‑fit, producing lower quality images or slower convergence.

* **Strong regularisation:** Because each block touches only a fraction of the dimensions, the optimisation landscape is smoother and gradients are less likely to push the model into a region that destroys the pretrained hyperspherical energy. Empirically the paper shows that even with $r=8$ for a $128\times128$ layer (960 parameters) the method already beats LoRA, suggesting that moderate block sizes already capture sufficient angular flexibility for subject‑driven and controllable generation.

* **Parameter efficiency:** When $r$ grows, the number of trainable parameters shrinks roughly as $1/r$ (or $1/r^{2}$ with sharing). This is useful when memory or bandwidth is limiting, e.g. fine‑tuning many layers simultaneously on a single GPU.

---

### 2.  What happens when $r$ is small (few large blocks)?

*With $r=1$ we revert to a full orthogonal matrix.*  

* **Maximum flexibility:** Any rotation or reflection in the whole $d$-dimensional space is admissible, so the model can adapt more freely to the downstream distribution. In tasks where large‑scale directional changes are needed (for example, completely new styles or drastic domain shifts), a smaller $r$ can yield higher fidelity.

* **More parameters / higher risk of over‑fitting:** The parameter count rises to $\mathcal O(d^{2})$; training becomes slower and may require stronger regularisers (learning‑rate schedules, weight decay). Moreover, because nothing constrains how much each element of $\mathbf R$ deviates from identity, there is a larger chance of destroying subtle pairwise angles that encode semantic knowledge, potentially harming generation quality if training data are scarce.

* **Empirical observation:** The authors report that even with modest values such as $r=4$–$8$ they achieve *both* fast convergence and high image quality, and increasing $r$ beyond that yields diminishing returns while further reducing parameters.

---

### 3.  Sharing blocks across layers

When every layer re‑uses the same block matrix (i.e. $\mathbf R_{i}=\mathbf R_{j}$ for all $i,j$), the parameter cost drops to $\mathcal O(d^{2}/r^{2})$.  

* **Bias introduced:** All layers apply exactly the same sub‑space rotation; this forces a *global* angular alignment that may not be optimal for every layer’s feature distribution. In practice it works surprisingly well because many layers in diffusion models learn broadly similar representations (especially in the UNet encoder/decoder where channels correspond to similar latent features).

* **Stability benefit:** Fewer independent degrees of freedom mean gradient noise is averaged out across layers, which can improve training stability—especially under small‑sample regimes (few subject images).

* The paper’s ablations indicate that *non‑shared* blocks give slightly higher final metrics but at a modest additional cost; shared blocks are a reasonable default when memory is tight.

---

### 4.  Practical guidelines from the experiments

| Setting | Block size ($d/r$) | Parameters per 128 × 128 weight | Observed effect |
|--------|----------------------|----------------------------------|-----------------|
| Full orthogonal ($r=1$) | 128 | 8 128 ≈ 8 k | Highest flexibility, comparable performance but slower convergence and higher GPU memory usage |
| Moderate ($r=8$) | 16 | 960 | Fast convergence, best trade‑off; OFT outperforms LoRA on all metrics |
| Very large ($r=32$) | 4 | ~240 | Slight drop in CLIP/DINO scores; training remains stable but images lose some fine‑grained control |
| Shared blocks ($r=8,$ shared) | 16 | ~120 | Slightly lower scores than independent blocks but still superior to LoRA and DreamBooth; saves memory |

The paper’s Fig. 7 (ε‑dependence for COFT) indirectly shows that decreasing flexibility (either by shrinking ε or by increasing r) moves the finetuned model toward the pretrained baseline, while larger ε or smaller r lets it deviate more aggressively. The sweet spot reported for both subject‑driven and controllable generation lies around **$r = 4$–$8** with *independent* blocks; sharing can be used when one wishes to push toward $\mathcal O(d^{2}/r^{2})$.

---

### 5.  How to choose $r$ for your own task

1. **Start with $r = \lfloor d / 16 \rfloor** (i.e., block dimension ≈ 16). For most UNet layers in Stable Diffusion ($d = 256,\,512,\ldots$) this corresponds to $r = 16$–$32.$

2. **If you have very few finetuning examples** (e.g., < 5 images for DreamBooth), increase $r$ (smaller blocks) or share blocks to keep regularisation strong.

3. **If you need maximal style change** (e.g., domain adaptation from photography to paintings), try reducing $r$ down to 4 or even 1, monitoring for signs of instability.

4. **Validate** on a small held‑out set using the same metrics as in Table 1 (DINO/CLIP‑I for subject fidelity, control consistency for controllable tasks). If performance plateaus while parameter count keeps growing, you have reached sufficient flexibility.

---

### Take‑away

The number of blocks $r$ is a knob that trades *parameter efficiency & regularisation* against *global rotational freedom*. Large $r$ → many small independent rotations → fewer parameters, stronger bias toward preserving pretrained geometry, possibly insufficient flexibility for very large semantic shifts. Small $r$ → fewer but larger rotations → more expressive but higher risk of over‑fitting and higher memory cost. Empirically, values around $r = 4$–$8$ give an excellent compromise for both subject‑driven and controllable diffusion finetuning; sharing blocks can be employed when memory constraints dominate at only a modest loss in quality.

### How is the new control‑consistency metric for controllable generation computed for each task?
Question:
In Section 5.2, a "control consistency metric" is introduced: IoU/F1 for Canny edges, mean IoU/accuracy for segmentation maps, and ℓ₂ distance for facial landmarks. Could someone detail:
1. Which off‑the‑shelf detector or parser is used to extract the control signal from generated images?
2. How are thresholding or post‑processing steps handled before computing IoU/F1?
3. Is any alignment (e.g., scaling or cropping) required between input control and extracted control before measuring error?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the controllable‑generation experiments the authors evaluate how faithfully the finetuned diffusion model follows the auxiliary control signal (Canny edges, semantic segmentation maps, or facial landmarks).  
The “control‑consistency” metric is simply the similarity between the **input control** that is fed to the model and a **re‑extracted control** that is computed from the generated image with an off‑the‑shelf parser.  
Below I summarise what the paper does for each of the three tasks and how the raw outputs are post‑processed before the similarity scores are computed.

---

### 1.  Which detector / parser is used?

| Task | Control signal | Parser applied to the generated image |
|------|----------------|---------------------------------------|
| Canny edge → image (C2I) | Binary edge map produced by the Canny detector (OpenCV `cv2.Canny`) on the source image | The same OpenCV Canny edge detector (same thresholds) is run on the generated image |
| Segmentation map → image (S2I) | Pixel‑wise semantic label map (ADE20K style) | A pretrained semantic‑segmentation network – **UPerNet** with a ResNet‑101 backbone trained on ADE20K – is used to predict a label map for each generated image |
| Landmark → face (L2F) | 68‑point facial landmark set (standard 2‑D facial keypoints) | A pretrained facial‑landmark extractor – **dlib’s 68‑point shape predictor** (or equivalently MediaPipe Face Mesh) – is run on the generated face to obtain a predicted landmark set |

All three parsers are *off‑the‑shelf*; they are not fine‑tuned on any of the diffusion outputs. The purpose of using standard tools is precisely to obtain an *external* estimate of how well the generated image respects the original control.

---

### 2.  Thresholding / post‑processing before computing IoU / F1

* **Canny edges** – The Canny operator already yields a binary map (pixel value 0 or 255). After applying it to the generated image, the authors simply binarise it again (`>0 → 1`) so that both input and output maps have identical datatype. No additional morphological cleaning is performed; IoU and F1 are computed directly on these binary masks.

* **Semantic segmentation** – The UPerNet model outputs a per‑pixel softmax over $150$ ADE20K classes. The argmax over classes gives a hard label map. Since some classes appear only rarely in a cropped patch, the authors ignore “void/ignore” pixels (label = 255 in ADE20K) when computing metrics. Then:
  * **Mean IoU** – For each class present in either map, $\text{IoU} = \frac{\text{intersection}}{\text{union}}$. The final score is the average over all present classes.
  * **Mean accuracy** – For each class, pixel accuracy $= \frac{\text{correct}}{\text{total pixels of class}}$; averaged over classes.
  * **Overall accuracy** – Ratio of all correctly labelled pixels to total valid pixels.

* **Facial landmarks** – The dlib predictor returns 68 points in image coordinates. Because diffusion generations are in latent space and then decoded back to pixel space, there may be a small shift between the input control landmarks and those extracted from the output. Before measuring distance, both sets are first **aligned by an affine transform** that removes global translation/scale: a similarity transform ($s\mathbf{R} + \mathbf{t}$) is estimated using Procrustes analysis between the two point sets and applied to the predicted points. After alignment, the Euclidean distance for each corresponding point is computed and then averaged, giving a single $\ell_2$ error per image.

---

### 3.  Alignment between input control and extracted control

*For edges and segmentation:*  
Both sides are already raster images defined on exactly the same spatial grid because during training/inference the diffusion model receives a **condition tensor of size $H\times W$** that matches the target resolution (e.g., $512 \times 512$). Consequently no explicit geometric alignment (scaling or cropping) is required; they compare pixel‑wise masks directly.

*For facial landmarks:*  
Because landmarks are defined in continuous coordinate space, a small global misalignment can arise from:  
1. Different cropping when decoding latent images;  
2. Minor rounding errors when mapping latent space back to pixel space.

To neutralise such global differences, the authors perform a **Procrustes alignment** as described above before computing $\ell_2$ distance. This step ensures that only *relative* pose differences affect the metric; pure translation or uniform scaling does not penalise the model.

---

### Putting it together

For each test sample:

1. Generate an image conditioned on a given control signal.
2. Run the appropriate off‑the‑shelf parser on that image.
3. Apply any necessary post‑processing (binary conversion for edges, argmax + ignore mask for segmentation, Procrustes alignment for landmarks).
4. Compute:
   * **Canny:** Intersection‑over‑Union and F1 between binary edge maps.
   * **Segmentation:** Mean IoU, mean per‑class accuracy, and overall pixel accuracy.
   * **Landmarks:** Average $\ell_2$ distance after alignment.

The resulting numbers are reported as “control consistency”. Because they rely solely on external detectors, they give an objective indication of how well OFT/COFT preserve the intended control while still benefiting from diffusion’s generative power.

### Why does OFT converge faster than standard fine‑tuning or LoRA?
Question:
The authors claim that updating only neuron directions on a smooth hypersphere manifold yields a better optimization landscape, citing faster convergence in experiments. I would like a deeper explanation:
- Is there any theoretical result (e.g., smoother gradients, lower curvature) for optimization on Stiefel manifolds that applies here?
- How does preserving hyperspherical energy affect gradient magnitudes compared to unconstrained fine‑tuning?
- Could this be simply because fewer parameters are updated (orthogonal matrices vs full weight updates), or is there something specific about orthogonality?
Answer:
**Answer**

The speed‑up you observe comes from three tightly linked reasons that are all spelled out in the paper:  

1. **The optimisation problem is restricted to a smooth, low‑dimensional manifold.**  
   In ordinary fine‑tuning each weight entry can move independently, so the optimiser has to explore a very high‑dimensional Euclidean space whose curvature can be highly irregular. OFT forces every layer’s weight matrix $\mathbf W$ to stay on the *hypersphere manifold* defined by  
   $$
   \mathbf W = \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0},\qquad 
   \mathbf R^{\top}\!\mathbf R=\mathbf I .
   $$  
   All admissible updates are therefore orthogonal rotations (or reflections) of the pretrained weight matrix. The set of orthogonal matrices $\mathrm{O}(d)$ is a compact Lie group; its geometry is well‑behaved (the metric is smooth, the curvature is bounded) and the tangent space at any point consists of skew‑symmetric matrices $\mathbf Q$ with $\mathbf Q^{\top}=-\mathbf Q$. Optimising over this tangent space (via the Cayley or exponential map) yields a *smooth optimisation landscape* that, as shown in [liu 2021 “Orthogonal over‑parameterized training”], leads to lower gradient variance and more stable curvature than unconstrained Euclidean optimisation. Because the search space is dramatically smaller—only $d(d-1)/2$ degrees of freedom instead of $d\times n$—the optimiser can make progress with larger effective step sizes, which manifests as faster convergence.

2. **Preserving hyperspherical energy keeps the “semantic geometry’’ of the network intact.**  
   The *hyperspherical energy*  
   $$
   \mathrm{HE}(\mathbf W)=\sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\widehat{\mathbf w}_i-\widehat{\mathbf w}_j\bigr\|^{-1},
   \qquad 
   \widehat{\mathbf w}_i=\frac{\mathbf w_i}{\|\mathbf w_i\|}
   $$  
   measures how the directions of neurons are distributed on the unit sphere. An orthogonal transformation multiplies every neuron by the same rotation, so pairwise angles (and thus HE) are **exactly invariant**. Consequently, each update does not disturb the relational structure that encodes semantic knowledge in the pretrained model. In contrast, unconstrained fine‑tuning or even LoRA’s low‑rank additive updates can rotate individual neurons arbitrarily, which often increases or decreases HE sharply and forces the network to relearn part of its representation from scratch. By keeping HE constant, OFT eliminates a large component of the loss surface that would otherwise need to be “flattened’’ during training; gradients therefore point more directly toward the downstream objective, which again accelerates convergence.

3. **Fewer effective parameters ⇒ larger signal‑to‑noise ratio in the gradient.**  
   Although OFT does not merely “reduce the number of trainable parameters’’—the orthogonal matrix still has full rank—it *compresses* them into a highly structured form. A block‑diagonal Cayley parametrisation with $r$ blocks reduces the number of free variables from $d^{2}$ to roughly $d(d/r-1)/2$. This structure acts like an implicit regulariser: each stochastic gradient step updates many correlated weight entries at once (because they are tied together through a single rotation matrix). The resulting gradient has lower variance than a naïve element‑wise update, which means that for a given learning‑rate schedule the optimiser can take larger steps without overshooting. Empirically this appears as a markedly steeper loss curve (see Fig. 5 and Fig. 8 in the paper).

Putting these three points together explains why OFT converges faster than both standard fine‑tuning and LoRA:

* The **manifold constraint** (orthogonal group) supplies a smoother geometry and reduces curvature pathologies.
* The **energy preservation** guarantees that semantic relationships among neurons are untouched, so the optimizer does not waste iterations re‑establishing them.
* The **parameter sharing** inherent in an orthogonal matrix yields gradients with higher signal‑to‑noise ratio, letting larger effective step sizes be taken.

Hence the speed gain is not simply a by‑product of updating fewer scalar values; it is fundamentally tied to *orthogonality*—the combination of a well‑conditioned search space and an invariance that protects the pretrained knowledge. This is precisely what the authors refer to when they say “OFT can be viewed as finetuning neurons on a smooth hypersphere manifold, which yields better optimisation landscape’’ (Section 4).

### Can multiple OFT finetuned tasks be composed by multiplying their orthogonal matrices?
Question:
In the conclusion, the authors mention compositionality: if we fine‑tune a model for task A obtaining orthogonal matrix \(R_A\\) and later fine‑tune for task B obtaining \(R_B\\), then applying both should correspond to \(R_B R_A\\). My questions are:
1. Does multiplying two learned orthogonal matrices preserve both tasks’ knowledge in practice?
2. Are there any empirical results or failure cases showing interference when composing OFT adapters?
3. Would ordering matter (i.e., is it non‑commutative), and how might one address potential conflicts?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper shows that an OFT adapter for a layer is a *single* orthogonal matrix $R$ that multiplies the pretrained weight matrix $W^{0}$ (Eq. (4)).  
Because the set of orthogonal matrices forms a group under matrix multiplication, the product of two adapters is again orthogonal:

$$
W_{\text{A}\!\to\!\text{B}} \;=\; R_{B}\,R_{A}\,W^{0}, \qquad
R_{B}R_{A}\in\mathcal O(d).
$$

In principle this means that you can **compose** several fine‑tuned tasks by simply stacking their adapters.

---

### 1. Does the product preserve the knowledge of both tasks?

Mathematically the product certainly retains the *orthogonality* of the transformation, so the hyperspherical‑energy invariant that OFT is designed to protect is still satisfied.  
However, preserving the invariant does **not guarantee** that the semantic behaviour learned for each task is retained.  

Each task‑specific adapter was trained to rotate the neuron directions in just the right way to satisfy its own objective (e.g. subject‑preserving DreamBooth vs. edge‑to‑image control).  
When you multiply two rotations you obtain a new rotation whose axis and angle are a combination of the two originals.  If those two rotations happen to interfere—for instance if they try to move the same set of neurons in opposite directions—the resulting transformation will typically satisfy neither objective as well as the individual adapters did.

The authors therefore list compositionality as an *open problem* (Section 7).  No experiment in the paper evaluates $R_{B}R_{A}$ on both tasks simultaneously, so we do not have empirical evidence that knowledge is preserved in practice.

---

### 2. Empirical evidence of interference or success?

The paper reports only single‑task fine‑tuning results (subject‑driven generation and controllable generation).  
All ablation studies compare OFT/COFT with DreamBooth, LoRA and ControlNet on each task separately; there is **no** quantitative or qualitative analysis of using two adapters together.  
Consequently we cannot point to a concrete failure case or a success story from this work; any observation would have to come from follow‑up experiments outside the scope of the paper.

---

### 3. Does order matter? (Non‑commutativity)

Yes.  Orthogonal matrices do **not** commute in general:

$$
R_{B}R_{A}\neq R_{A}R_{B}\;\;\text{unless }R_{A}\text{ and }R_{B}\text{ share a common set of eigenvectors}.
$$

Because composition is non‑commutative, swapping the order yields a different overall rotation and therefore a different transformed model.  In practice this means that whichever adapter is applied first biases the downstream rotation space for the second one.

#### How to mitigate conflicts

1. **Sequential fine‑tuning with regularisation** – after training $R_{A}$ you can fine‑tune again on task B *starting from* $R_{B}=I$ but with an additional regulariser that penalises deviation from $R_{A}$.  This forces the second rotation to stay close to the first, reducing destructive interference.

2. **Learn a shared “composition” matrix** – instead of multiplying two independently trained adapters, train a small adaptor $C$ such that  
   $$
   W_{\text{comp}} = C\,W^{0},\qquad C\approx R_{B}R_{A},
   $$  
   where $C$ is learned on a multi‑task objective (e.g., weighted sum of task losses).  The optimisation can exploit the orthogonal constraint directly (Cayley/skew‑symmetric parameterisation) and thus produce a single orthogonal matrix that jointly satisfies both tasks.

3. **Block‑diagonal isolation** – if each task only needs to rotate a subset of dimensions, you can allocate disjoint blocks of $R$ to each task (the block‑diagonal parameterisation described in §3.2).  In that case the product reduces to block‑wise multiplication where blocks are independent, eliminating most cross‑task interference.

4. **Task‑specific gating** – keep both adapters stored and at inference time select which one (or which combination) to apply depending on the desired behaviour, rather than permanently merging them.  This avoids any unwanted blending when you only need one task at a time.

---

### Bottom line

*Multiplying two OFT adapters yields another orthogonal matrix and therefore preserves the hyperspherical‑energy invariant, but it does not automatically preserve the functional behaviour learned for each individual task.*  
The paper does not provide empirical validation of compositionality; it explicitly calls it an open research direction.  
Since orthogonal matrices are non‑commutative, order matters, and conflicts can be mitigated by regularising subsequent fine‑tuning, learning a joint composition matrix, using block‑diagonal structures to isolate task subspaces, or simply gating adapters at inference time.
