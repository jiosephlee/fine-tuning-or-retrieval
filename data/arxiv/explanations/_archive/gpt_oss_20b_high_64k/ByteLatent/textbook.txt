# Background on tokenizer‑based LLMs  
Large language models (LLMs) have been trained in an almost completely end‑to‑end fashion since their inception, except for an essential preprocessing step—tokenisation. Tokenisation groups contiguous byte sequences into a *finite* set of symbols drawn from a predetermined dictionary known as a vocabulary.\footnote{Tokenisation methods such as byte‑pair encoding (BPE)~\citep{sennrich-etal-2016-neural,gage1994new} have become de facto standards because they compact input efficiently while allowing transformers short enough sequences that fit budgets imposed by quadratic self‑attention costs.\label{sec:bpe}}  
Once tokenised, the resulting sequence is projected through an embedding table and fed into a standard autoregressive transformer consisting mainly of block‑causal self‐attention layers coupled with FFN towers~(\S Architecture). Tokens therefore act both as *compression*, reducing input length from possibly millions of raw bytes down to several thousands of symbols, *bias*, restricting how information can be represented during learning~(\S Robustness), and *computational unit*, determining how much computation the transformer performs per step.  

However this design introduces constraints tightly coupled with the choice of vocabulary:
the size and composition of the dictionary constrain which sequences can appear together without splitting words or mixing languages.
Intrinsic compression heuristics—bisecting larger units into smaller ones when frequency thresholds are met—cause distortions that differ sharply between domains.
More importantly every token obliges one evaluation pass through both reference projection matrices (for input embeddings) and weight matrices within each transformer's layer,
introducing *fixed* computation overhead unrelated **a priori** to how complex the next prediction actually is.
Consequently many productive steps devoted naturally occur when predicting trivial continuations such as trailing whitespaces
(and thus consume unnecessary flops), while few steps are afforded when encountering hard initial characters before a new sentence pivots heavily future possibilities.’This rigid–volume assignment constrains both *efficiency* and *robustness*’~(\S Limitations).  

# Limitations imposed by fixed vocabularies  
The practical consequences listed above surface directly across architectures:

* **Domain / Modality Sensitivity** – The BPE heuristics dictate how end‑to‑end compression hovers around particular statistics captured during static training.
When encountering new textual domains these heuristics falter visibly~(\citep{dagangetting}).  
For instance,
improved entity types or unfamiliar scripts allocate poorly sized units whilst traditional strings remain well balanced—leading directly
to stronger overfitting or underfitting patterns in unseen data.
* **Noise Sensitivity** – A typographical mistake changes some trailing symbols often leaving other parts untouched,
although transcripts arriving at successive data samples crowd an entire pretrained embedding vector range.
Because embeddings are shared completely across all possible occurrences,
a single missing character alters downstream predictors drastically.
Further evidence surfaces experimentally wherein pre-trained BPE systems stumble heavily against character‐level noise injections (~24–37 points worse than Alt Bier). 
Because gradients propagate through a fixed symbol pathway once chosen during segmentation—a consequence traced back to off‑chain semantics—noise propagates amplified through causality chains~(\S Robustness). 
* **Orthographic Knowledge Deficit** – Tokenised systems inherently ignore deterministic operation over glyphs beyond source segmentation,
hence losing awareness needed for subtle sub-word transformations demonstrated embarrassingly well on character manipulation benchmarks such as CUTE or mbpp codify~(\S Byte-Level Tasks). 
This hampers learning transliteration or phoneme extraction despite large parameter counts because templates do not provide deterministic symbols aligned per character anymore.
* **Limited Flexibility Across Diverse Patch Sizes** – Standard settings let practitioners switch average byte occupancy per token only marginally,
because larger vocabularies necessitate proportionally larger embedding tables merging both increased sequence length handling capacity
and memory usage spike ~Llama 3’s growth from an average size \(3\.7 \rightarrow4\.4\) bytes increasing its embedding dimension four times~(Figure xx).
Thus attempt shifts tandemly suffering linear increases even if stored batch length shrinks proportionally yielding little room for sparse reallocation mechanisms normalizing variance on average complexity metrics X_{complexity}.  
Mathematically every policy defines a surrogate vector \(z_i = e^{(t)}(x_{<i}) \in \mathbb R^{d}\)—the same size irrespective 
how predictive remaining context is—thereby failing adaptivity required for distinguishing simple versus varied predictions over diverse cultural settings.~(Page 9).

These constitutes clear pressure points motivating creation of genuinely flexible replacements which are permitted nonbounded grouping units while maintaining control over overall computational load.



  
# Introduction to single-byte inputs  
Transformers trained directly on writing systems represented **as individual bytes** avoid all compression ambiguities incited above:
every possible sequence produces exactly one deterministic symbolic representation—not dependent upon any external dictionary choice.
By bypassing magic segmentation schemes it never predisposes input with pre-selected biases.
Empirical studies illustrate this property brings winning accuracy gaps even vis-à-vis mature symbolic pipelines on code completion tests
(increasing upstream sample distribution diversity).

There remains an essential optica challenge:
the prime bottleneck shifts from *self-attention*—the most familiar quadratic cost—to purely linear costs incurred mostly at immense numbers
of successive feed-forward network (FFN) layers evaluated over each raw byte entry.
Hence direct byte-level transformer instantiations blow up when faced with strings several billion long—it becomes volumetrically prohibitive neither memory requisites nor cycles reach obviously past billions (~).

**a)** designing *efficient attention variants* reducing thread‐wise operations its kernels focus around sliding windows or linear approximation techniques (`Mamba`/`MambaByte`, `CharacterBERT`).
**b)** deploying *attention‐free* architectures mitigating backlog partly relegating attention outside structural building blocks entirely (`Mamba`, pointers).  

Yet even these optimization webs prove more effective predominantly at modest scales (<200 M parameters),

Conceptual Foundations of Patching  

Large language models routinely rely on a pre‑tokenised representation of text before any neural computation takes place.  The existence of a fixed vocabulary imposes two intertwined constraints—each element ingested by the Transformer carries an identical cost regardless of its complexity, and the final projection layer must accommodate every symbol that ever appears during training.  When one wishes instead to learn directly from raw Unicode bytes—the “exact” material reality that modern software ultimately manipulates—these constraints become glaringly inefficient.  The core insight behind Byte‑Latent Transformers is that relevance–aware computation can hide itself simply within how sequences are partitioned into higher‑level units called **patches** rather than one‑size‑fits‑all tokens​.  Patches begin life as arbitrary contiguous groups of bytes whose lengths adapt locally according to signal predictability while remaining transparent enough for sequential processing during both training and inference​¹​&nbsp;&nbsp;(Section 2 introduces several concrete strategies for this end‑to‑end partitioning).

# Formal Definition of a Patch‑Patching Function  

Let \(\mathbf{x}=\{x_{i}\}\) denote an input sequence where \(x_{i}\in \mathcal V=\{0,\dots ,255\}\) is an individual byte and \(i=1,\dots ,n\) indexes position.  
A **patch‑function**  
\(f_{p}\colon \mathcal V^{n}\rightarrow {\mathbb N}\times {\mathbb N}^{*}\)
produces a sequence \(\mathbf p=\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m},\, m<n,\)
with each \(p_{j}=\{x_{s},\,x_{s+1},\dots ,x_{t}\}\subseteq \mathbf x.\)
Formally this corresponds to assigning every byte a binary flag
\(b_{i}\in\{0,1\}\), where \(b_{i}=1\) signals the inception
of a fresh patch beginning at position \(i.\)  
Thus
\(b_{i}=f^{b}((x_1,\dots ,x_n),\, i)\)
for some deterministic function \(f^{b},\)
and 
\(m=\sum_{i=1}^{n} b_i.\)  
The average patch size,
\[
\nu_\text{avg}= \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}|p_j|}{m},
\]
directly dictates how many “outer” Transformer steps must run
for any length‑\(n'\) portion \( \tilde{\mathbf x}\subseteq \mathbf x.\)  
Since computation grows linearithmically w.r.t additions conducted per *patch*, longer patches directly reduce Compute FLOPs while simultaneously demanding richer representations provided by the local encoder pathway discussed later.​²​

# K‑Byte Static Patches  

The most elementary incarnation sketched early‐in Megabyte​¹⁵ places a filler cut every fixed number \(k>0.\)
In practice, one sets stride length so that
the Transformer sees precisely \(k\) bytes per latent state.
Its chief virtue lies in implementation simplicity:
the slicing procedure requires no lookups beyond a modular counter,
and varying \(k\) offers a straightforward knob harmonising effective batch size against memory persistence​¹⁷.
Absolute drawbacks surface once real textual signals enter play ‒ an equal amount biography today carries vastly incongruent expectations;
the fact that every symbol receives identical attention even when surrounding context affords trivial predictions,
or similarly when longness invites high‑order regularities.
Consequently many semantic subtleties remain uncapitalised while high‑entropy glitches consume unnecessary resources​
—an inherited inefficiency made stark when anecdotal benchmark results expose Megabyte’s inferior test loss compared against more elaborated grouping strategies​
(see Section 4‐2′).

# SpaceBá Grouping  

Automated heuristics emerged yet again twenty years ago
via *SpaceBytes*​¹¹⁶,
which triggers split points whenever an incident **space‑like** bearer surfaces—
concretely any raw byte outside Latin letters, digits,
or UTF8 continuation codes,
provided at least one preceding non‐space symbol stays tethered within its current block.
Operationally it amounts merely to scanning forward until encountering such
masked symbols.
The underlying hypothesis aligns neatly with linguistic structure;
whitespace is statistically dense demarcator between words and usually heralds higher uncertainty—
hence placing anew global transformer state right after every word surface inexorably magnifies capacity precisely when prediction becomes most difficult.
Unlike strict strides however SpaceBytes flexibly modulate lengths cheaply:
frequent short tokens coalesce into long policy formations whilst long morphemes get compressed through prior redundancy—an attractive middle ground between uniformity and heuristical freedom​¹⁸.
Yet this approach still depends heavily on surface characterisation;
languages lacking clear whitespace delimiters or reconstructive regimes tempt it toward excessive fragmentation,

For language generation terms remain truncated when deciding whether attachment boundaries should trigger another *global* latent step before emitting further raw symbols.
When software wishes stable decoding order subsequent bits influence high-level representation creation ad infinitum,
every amble must rely purely upon what has already materialised;
preliminary cleverness could break system determinism evading replayability stochasticities mask indistinguishably well especially across multiple passes over same corpora waves;  

*Incrementality*
requires satisfying
\[                 f_p({\bf x}_{<i})=f_p({{\bf x}})\!\!{}_{\!< i}                \\                 .            \tag{Inc}
                             \\]
Simply put the public portion baked into previous prefix determines its own fate alone –
so standard neural forecasters weigh memory exclusively against past information,
no hidden lookahead dust remains overhead.

### # Byte Preprocessing Pipeline  

At the very beginning every language‑model instance first receives an unstructured stream of raw UTF‑8 bytes $\pmb{x}=\{x_1,x_2,\dots ,x_n\}$ that represents a document or a prompt plus its target continuation. BLT eschews the conventional fixed‑vocabulary tokenizer that would collapse this stream into subword tokens prior to learning any language signal. Instead it records every individual byte verbatim while postponing any grouping decision until an *entropy‑aware* segmentation module processes the incoming sequence during data loading or during generation itself.\

The goal of this segmentation step is twofold:</li>
1) **Dynamic consolidation** – contiguous runs whose next‑byte statistics are highly predictable (low Shannon entropy) are aggregated into one *patch*, while runs where predicting the immediate next byte requires significant uncertainty are split so that every ensuing model step focuses computation where it matters most.\</li>
2) **Uniform processing budget** – regardless of how blurry or crisp a particular segment looks, every capitalized character still consumes at least one *global* computation step once that segment has been mapped into its compact representation.\  

The practical realization employs one lightweight auto‑regressive byte language model trained **separately** on almost identical data mixtures (§5). Its per‑byte entropy estimate predicts whether a new byte starts a fresh conceptual context:
$$
H(x_t)=\sum _{v\in{\cal V}}p_e(x_t=v\,|\,{\bf x}_{<t})\,\log p_e(x_t=v\,|\,{\bf x}_{<t})
$$
where $p_e$ denotes the probability mass function learned by this auxiliary network over the entire 256‑byte vocabulary ${\cal V}$.
Depending on a chosen global threshold $\theta_g$ and an optionally monotonic relative rise criterion $\theta_r$, every position satisfying  
$$
H(x_t) > \theta_g    \qquad{\rm OR}\qquad H(x_t)-H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r 
$$
is declared a boundary between two adjacent patches.
The resulting list $\pmb p=\{p_1,p_2,\dots ,p_m\}$ captures arbitrary lengths ($m<n$), enabling later work units — namely *local encoders*, *latent transformers* and *local decoders* — to operate directly on $\pmb p$ without any fixed vocabulary lookup tables.\

Once established during pre‑training timeouts (where the whole batch and its entropies are available), these boundaries determine all subsequent inputs fed into BLT modules upon both training backward passes and real‑time inference generation rounds.

Local Encoder Architecture  
# Unified Byte Embedding Matrix  

The local encoder receives a raw sequence of bytes \(\mathbf{x}= \{x_1,\dots ,x_n\}\), where each \(x_i\in [0,255]\) represents one of the possible 256 byte values found in UTF‑8 encoded text. These bytes are first mapped into continuous vectors via a single lookup table shared across all model sizes:
\[
\mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{E}_b(x_i) \in \mathbb{R}^{h_\mathcal E},
\]
where \(\mathbf{E}_b \in \mathbb R^{256\times h_\mathcal E}\) is the **unified byte embedding matrix** and \(h_\mathcal E\) denotes the hidden dimensionality of the encoder (typically on the order of several hundred). Because every possible byte value can appear at any position in any context, this shared embedding achieves remarkable efficiency and memory reuse – only \(256\times h_\mathcal E\) parameters are required regardless of batch size or sequence length.

This base representation is subsequently enriched by hash‑based n‑gram embeddings (see next subsection). The resulting vectors form the initial token‑like state that flows through alternating transformer blocks and cross‑attention layers to produce latent patch embeddings used by the global latent transformer.



# Hash n‑gram Embeddings Mechanism  

To encode **contextual** information about each byte directly at its input head, BLT augments singleton-byte embeddings with hashed n‑gram features spanning up to eight previous bytes. For every position \(i\) we collect all contiguous substrings ending at \(i\):
\[
g_{i,n}=\bigl(b_{i-n+1},\,b_{i-n+2},\,\dots ,b_i\bigr),\qquad
n=3,\dots ,8.
\]
Each gram is passed through a deterministic rolling polynomial hash:
\[
\mathrm{Hash}\bigl(g_{i,n}\bigr)=
\bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\!a^{\,k-1}\cdot g_{i,n}[k]\bigr)\;\bmod |\!{\bf E}_{n}^{hash}\!|,
\]
where \(a=257\) is a small prime chosen for hashing robustness and \({\bf E}_{n}^{hash}\in\mathbb R^{|\!{\bf E}_{n}^{hash}| \times h_{\!proj}}\) denotes an auxiliary embedding table per n‑gram length \(n\). The embedded gram vector is added element‑wise to the raw byte embedding:
\[
e_i \;=\;\frac{1}{1+6}\,\Bigl(
x_i+\!\!\sum_{n=3}^{8}
{\bf E}_n^{hash}(\mathrm{Hash}(g_{i,n}))
{\Bigr)}.
\]
A normalisation factor guarantees that longer n‑grams do not dominate short ones when summing up (\({1+6}=7\) because eight values would yield 8 embeddings plus one base embedding). This hashed n‑gram mechanism provides *fixed‑size*, *trainable* contextual cues without exploding width or storing grammars explicitly – crucial when training billions of parameters on colossal text corpora.



# Cross–Attention Module Linking Bytes→Patches  

The core conversion from raw bytes to tractable patches arises through iterative *cross‑attention* embedded between standard token‑level Transformer layers inside the encoder.  For a given dynamic partition \(p_j=\bigl(b_{\ell}, b_{\ell+1},..., b_k\bigr)\), each boundary definition specifies a set of source tokens known as *patch‐bytes*.  Each per‑patch query vector \(Q_j^{(t)}\in \mathbb R^{h_\mathcal G}\) is derived from an initial proposal:
```latex
P^{(0)}_j=\Bigl(\sum_{\text{bytes } r\,:\,\ell(r)\le j}
    W_q^0\,h^{(t)}_r\bigr),
```
where \(h^{(t)}_r\) are hidden states after the \(t^{th}\) local Transformer layer and \(W_q^0:\mathbb R^{h_\mathcal E}->{h_\mathcal G}\) projects them toward the global latent space dimension (\(h_\mathcal G>h_\mathcal E,\)\ typically a multiple such as \(k=2\,.\))  

The standard multi‑head mechanism then refines these queries by attending over **only** those key/value pairs belonging to their own patch:
```latex
P^{(t+1)}_j 
   = P^{(t)}_j
     + W_o\, 
       \underbrace{\mathrm{softmax}\!\Bigl(
       \frac{{Q_j}^{(t)} {K_j}^{T}}{\sqrt d }
      \Bigr){V_j}}_{\substack{\text{byte}-\\
      {\text {attn}}}.
```
\(K_j,V_j,\dots~\(heads are projected onto `byte`–domain hidden states from preceding Transformer layers with
\(W_k,W_v:\mathbb R^{h_\mathcal E}->{d_h}\), while $W_o$ projects back to patch dimension.
A **residual connection** ensures gradient flow across successive updates – effectively turning each cross‑attention “hop” into another refinement onto latent representation.



# Masking Strategy (Local Block Causal Windows)  

All internal processes in every local encoder Transformer output must respect relative temporal ordering **within** documents; nevertheless it is desirable that bits belonging to distinct patches can inform one another when jointly processed by subsequent cross‑attention steps. Accordingly BLT employs a *local block causal* mask on every ordinary self‑attention operation:  
each token sees exactly `w_E` provide predecessors 
that may span across multiple patches but cannot jump beyond document boundaries.
Mathematically,
```latex
M[i,j] =
   \begin{cases}
   0,& i-j < w_E ~~\vee~~ i=j\\
   -\,\infty ,& otherwise~,
   ````  
where \(M[i,j]\in[-\,∞,0]\).
Because `w_E` defaults to values such as 512 or 2048 depending on model scale, this mask keeps computation modest while still preserving subtle intra‐patch dependencies purely within nested context windows.



# Layer Structures (‘Small’ Transformers)  

The entire encoder stack consists of only a handful of “small” Transformer blocks (\(l_{\cal E}=1$–$4< l_\G)\).
Each block contains:
* A single multi‐head self‐attention layer bounded by block causal mask `w_E`;
* A small feed–forward network employing SwiGLU activation once more at hidden dimensionality `2×h_E`;  
* Pre‐LayerNorm applied before both attentions and feed forward computations – allows faster convergence while keeping the depth shallow;
* An optional smaller number of key/output heads so that memory overhead remains below what would otherwise occur had we built full Self-Attention with d_k=d_q=d_v;
* After every module an explicit residual connection keeps representations stable even when stacking many small blocks together—especially vital because downstream cross‑attention already aggregates low‐level details into more abstract vectors before handing them off.\(

These “lightweight” designs keep most FLOPs concentrated in large feeding forward networks executed once per recent chunk of consecutive patches rather than repeatedly probing entire sequences independently.



# Residuals & LayerNorm Variants  

Across both Transformer layers and cross–attention schemes BLT retains two dominant design principles introduced in modern large language models:

| Component | Normalisation | Residual | Purpose |
|-----------|---------------|----------|---------|
| Self‐Attention | **RMSNorm** (root–mean‐square norm with learned scaling param.) | Yes | Keep scale uniform while removing mean bias |
| Feed Forward | Same RMSNorm upstream | Yes | Stabilise un­trending gradients |
| Cross Atten.| Pre-LayerNorm applied only on queries/keys/values | Yes | Allow non‑masked juxtaposition between heterogeneous spaces |
| Initial Patch Query |\ -- | Feature aggregation residual (from BOOST)? Not ; indices functional | Bypass many extra linear transfers |

In practice the same RMSNorm hyperparameters propagate through entire small contrastive encoder modules allowing rapid training at scale without separate tuning for each depth position type.




Collectively these building blocks – light weighted encoders using rich per‑byte summarization via hashed grams, adaptive locality via masking, modern normalisation plus reliable residual paths – collectively populate BLT's “local encoder” as an efficient gateway that transforms pixel-sized pre-token data into usable compact latent efforts that are fed straight into its larger global autoregressive latent transformer.

# Global Latent Transformer Mechanics  

Large language models traditionally process tokens through an autoregressive transformer that operates on a fixed‐size vocabulary. In a **Byte Latent Transformer** (\textsc BLT}, however, those “tokens” are replaced by **latent patches** obtained from raw bytes. To maintain performance while reducing compute overhead, deep design choices are taken at three levels—architecture width/height, masking strategy, positional encoding reuse and activation/normalisation methods—each deliberately borrowed from mainstream token‑based transformers such as LLaMA 3 yet tuned around patch semantics. The following sections unpack how these decisions shape \textsc BLT}’s global latent transformer.

---

## Layer Count & Dimensionality Decisions  

The **global latent transformer** \(\mathcal{G}\) is parameter‑equivalent to token‑based models trained on tens of billions of tokens – it maps each latent patch representation \(p_j \in \mathbb{R}^{h_{\mathcal{G}}}\) into a corresponding output \(o_j\). Because \(\mathcal{G}\) encapsulates almost all floating‑point operations during pre‑training and inference, its depth \(l_{\mathcal{G}}\) and width \(h_{\mathcal{G}}\) ultimately dominate total compute cost and learning capacity.

In practice, \(\mathcal{G}\) adopts the same dimensional hierarchy proposed for token‑based transformers:

\[
h_{\mathcal{G}} = \frac{4}{3}\,\texttt{model\_size},\qquad
d_{\text{ff}} = 4\,h_{\mathcal{G}},
\]

where \(\texttt{model\_size}\) denotes metaphoric “effective token size” – essentially one patched representation vector equal in anatomy to a standard token embedding length. Because byte patches replace tokens without changing their inferred semantic burden per instruction step, translating *patch‑width* directly into *token‐width* seems reasonable.

Regarding **layer count** \(l_{\mathcal{G}}\), the study experimented on multiple model scales – \(400\mathrm{M}\), \(1\mathrm{B}\), \(2\mathrm{B}\), \(4\mathrm{B}\), up to \(8\mathrm{B}\). Each step pursued a **compute‑optimal point** where training data quantity matched model capacity according to existing scaling laws derived for BPE transformers (\citealt{dubey2024llama}). Concretely:

| Scale | Layers \(l_{\mathcal G}\) | Hidden size \(h_{\mathcal G}\) |
|------|--------------------------|------------------------------|
| 400 M | ~10 | ~8 k |
| 1 B   | ~12 | ~12 k |
| …     | …   | … |
| 8 B   | ~36 | ~24 k |

(Values illustrate typical growth patterns rather than exact counts.) Because every increase in layer depth incurs linear head expansion (\(q,k,v,o\)), aligning numbers with pretrained cameras ensures rapid convergence even when using archival byte patches rather than static vocabulary words.

A salient effect emerges when **patch size varies** (§Patching). Increasing mean patch length reduces total sequence steps per document so fewer \(\mathcal{G}\) layers are applied during inference (i.e., lower FLOPs). Consequently researchers may scale ***both*** the global depth *and* average patch length while holding overall compute constant—an advantage absent when using static vocabularies because tokens cannot grow arbitarily large without collapsing data representation fidelity.

## *Block–Causal* Mask Per Document Boundary  

The core challenge of multi‑document training is controlling what one document can attend to relative another’s embeddings when feeding sequences via padding‐loaned batches. A naive soft‑max over all positions would inadvertently allow gradients from one document drift into another’s state history, corrupting autoregression on both fronts.

To halt this cross‑document leakage, \textsc BLT} adopts a *block‑causal* mask—identical to that employed by LLaMA 3—where every patch mask respects its own **document boundary**:

```
Mask(i,j)=1 ⇔ {
                 i≥j   AND
                 doc(i)=doc(j)
               }
```

In practice this means all trainable logits at a position \(i\) are evaluated against *intra‑document* preceding patches while future patches—even those belonging to distinct documents—are relegated to minus infinity scores and effectively ignored during softmax normalization. Consequently generated sequences obey strict left‑to‑right order within each document while remaining independent across documents even when padded together into one batched sequence matrix.\footnote{
Some tokenisers add special ``<DOC>'' separators before quantisation; \textsc BLT} does this implicitly because patches are derived on full byte streams maintaining exact document segmentation.\@}

The block‑causal rule becomes especially important because patches sometimes cross natural lexical boundaries—however long their dynamic segmentation will stretch—they stay rooted within their originating strings.\@ This avoids convoluting one prompt’s prediction probability distribution with unrelated context elsewhere,\@ guaranteeing reliable perplexity estimates (or Bits‐Per‑Byte measurement).

## Rotary Positional Embedding Reuse  

Relative position information remains essential even when input items lack explicit lexical identity—as tiny adjective formality cannot be zeroed out otherwise. For this purpose \textsc BLT} reuses **Rotary Position Encodings (RoPe)** directly from its parent design (LLaMA 3). RoPe injects sinusoidal rotational patterns into query/key vectors:

\[
q'_i = [\, q_i^{(0)} + R_i h_i,\ q_i^{(1)} + R_i h_i\,],
\]

where \(R_i=\sin(i/\theta)\) and \(\theta=5\times10^5\); this encourages relative distance learning without any learned positional table overhead.\@ Crucially RoPe works seamlessly on both *any* hidden dimension depth—the encoder’s lightweight local transformers omit them altogether because those layers attend within short fixed windows where absolute offset matters little.\@ Only self–attention inside \(\mathcal G\,\) requires it since longer buffer spans warrant delicate relative location management across many patches per batch.

Reusing RoPe confers two key benefits:\begin{enumerate}
		*1.* No additional parameters or memory are required—saving hold space normally reserved for learned positional tables.\item
		*2.* It yields a smooth continual coordinate system across variable patch lengths because each pass receives exactly one contiguous receptive window regardless of whether raw bytes were granted separate or collapsed into longer tokens.\@ 
\end{enumerate}

The straightforward adaptation backfires little on Bytes versus Token inputs because RoPe depends purely on positional indices—identified unchanged whether bytes or patches occupy those slots.\@

## SwiGLU / RMSNorm Activations  

Typical large trans­formers employ either GELU/GELU variants or heavier mixtures such as SwiGLU (\(f(x)=x\sigma(Wx)\)) along with either LayerNorm or RMSNorm normalisation nodes. Given Boök-lab scale constraints were respected meticulously in these experiments—a deeper network compounded even tiny constant overheads—the chosen combination was exactly the one proven best by recent high‑ranking architectures such as LLaMA ​and Mamba:

*(1) SwiGLU*: A gated linear unit built atop elementwise sigmoid functions furnishes both high expressive power and efficient back‑propagation compared to traditional GELU since it requires fewer independent evaluation operations.\@
*(2) RMSNorm*: Rather than caring about variance offsets globally per token distribution—a bookkeeping effort costly at logical tensor macros—we apply Root‐Mean–Square normalisation—that normalises vector magnitude without parameters—in every residual stage which simplifies gradient flow dramatically at massive depth.\@

Procedure inside each multiheaded self‐attention layer:
1\. Query/key/value projections take place;
2\. Multiheaded dot product yields attention scores masked appropriately;
3\. Subsequent `softmax` produces weighted sums;
4\. Result then concatenated back into `attn_out`;
5\. `attn_out` is integrated into prenorm residual using `RMSNorm`;
6\. A `SwiGLU` feedforward stage processes elementwise gated expression;
7\. Another residual connects back into normalized representation before passing downstream.

### Cross‑Attention Decoding Orientation

In standard causal language models every query attends “from left‑to‑right” toward its past keys/values (“self‑attention”).  
BLT introduces two distinct attention families:

* **Encoder Cross‑Attention** (`Encoder → Decoder`) – gathers *byte* events into a *latent* set.
* **Decoder Cross‑Attention** (`Decoder → Encoder`) – performs the reverse operation needed during generation/transduction (bytes → latent).

In contrast with its encoding partner where *queries* look toward *latent values*, here **queries are byte representations** while *keys* and *values* come from latents:

    W_v\!\big( \mathcal D_C(o_j)\big),\\

* \(h^{\text{enc}}_{l_\mathcal E}\) consists of per‑byte hidden vectors produced by the *local encoder*.
