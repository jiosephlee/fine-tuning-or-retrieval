# Designing Chips That Speak One Bill – Why AI Drivers Are Turning Toward Ultra Tiny Arithmetic  

Large‑language models have grown far beyond what a single commodity chip could ever run cheaply or sustainably today. When you strip down an inference pipeline you find two dominant constants worth disrupting: **floating‑point multiplications** that burn power and **gigabytes‑worth of parameters** that flood off‑chip memories every minute you wait for a response.



-----------------------------------------------------------------------

## The Grand Scaling Problem  

Predictive performance scales logarithmically with both dataset size and model depth while inferencing costs grow nearly cubically once you turn on an automatic‐mixed precision GEMM loop across dozens of thousands of voxels in every forward pass.
The industry’s standard workaround has been post‑training low‑bit quantisation (\< 8 bits) applied via techniques such as SmoothQuant or GPTQ.
However,
post‑training compression inevitably loses granularity that is vital when you start training larger meshes or raise token counts into trillions.
Moreover, even state‑of‑the‐art QAT pipelines still deploy floating‐point multipliers during training due to rule‑based extensions rather than fundamental change.



-----------------------------------------------------------------------

## From Floating Point Paradise To One‑bit Language Models  

Enter **BitNet b\(^{\,{\scriptscriptstyle​}}_{\,^{—}}\)**—an elegant project extending its predecessor BitNet into a fully direct‑from‑scratch trainer that renders every weight ternary:
$$
w_{ij}\;\in\;\{-\,1,\;\!\!0,\;\!+\,1\}.
$$
The key idea behind restricting weights comes straight forward yet powerful:
the dot product no longer demands any multiply unit,
only an addition conditioned on whether the weight is positive,
a subtraction conditioned on a negative sign,
or nothing when it’s zero.
Consequently every linear layer collapses into essentially an **add tree**, eliminating hundreds of floating‐point multiplier ASIC lanes.



-----------------------------------------------------------------------

#### Quantisation Scheme Summarised  

To clamp trainable continuous weights onto this set,
BitNet trains them using an **absmean** approach:
$$
\tilde W
= {\rm RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\varepsilon}\right),
\qquad 
{\rm RoundClip}(x,a,b)
= \max(a,\min(b,\lfloor x\rceil)),
$$
where $\gamma=\tfrac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|$ normalises our distribution before rounding.
Activations undergo a similar squeeze into eight integers per element,
keeping rounding deviance below $10^{-2}$ while removing any signed offset overall—
which simplifies SIMD lane control dramatically.

## Architecture Side Effects At Scale  

### Multipliers → Adders  
A generic transformer block requires $O(N^2)$ multiply–accumulate vcores.
Replacing every MAC buffer with an adder tree cuts power draw dramatically –
floating‐point sub‑nanometer circuits consume roughly $\times80$ more joule per operation than their integer counterparts.
Consequently modern GPUs hitting $10^{12}$ FLOPs per watt cannot rely solely on strength normalization anymore –
they become bottlenecked either upstream actuator heat core or bus serialization.



### Micro­Memory Levers  
Given $m$ model parameters originally stored one per two bytes ($FP_{16}$),
cost drops proportionally:
bitternous storage needs only $\log_2(3)\approx 1.\!585$ durable bits,
meaning a theoretical $\times31$\!-\!40 reduction compared against binary storage.
For instance,
a nominally \$70 B transformer falls from roughly \$140 GB FP\(^{0}\)
to under \$5 GB @ \('\textbf{{-\!0,+\!}}\)tally',
an entirely different consumer class once just dealing with DRAM access latency.
