\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### Why project linear weights onto non‑negative space via $	exttt{	extbackslash(max}(\mathbf{w}	exttt{	extbackslash ,0}	exttt{	extbackslash )}$?), i.e., skip negative weights?
Question:
Equation (4) forces learned weights onto $[0,	ildeho)$ guaranteeing all contributions increase likelihoods rather than decrease them. Why isn’t standard unconstrained linear/logistic regression sufficient here?   Any insight into whether allowing negative coefficients would harm interpretability or leak unwanted bias?”},{
Answer:
**Short answer**

FeatsLLM turns a handful of hand-made binary indicators—each one telling whether an *LLM-generated* rule holds—into a very simple linear-scoring function  

$$
\text{logit}_k=\Bigl[\max(\boldsymbol w_k ,\,0)\Bigr]\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol z_k .
$$

The `max(·,0)` simply bans *negative* coefficients.  
Because every feature $$\,{\boldsymbol z}\in\{0,1\}^{\, R}\,$$ says **“this sample satisfies a specific condition,”** FeatsLLM’s design philosophy is that satisfied conditions can only *increase* our belief in a particular class; it does **not** intend‐to make “a rule being true means *less* likely” an option.  
Constraining all weights be non‑negative therefore preserves this monotonic interpretation while giving the model enough flexibility (on top of an overall intercept) to separate classes through the soft-max layer.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why constrain the weights?

| Question | Usual practice | FeatsLLM rationale |
|----------|----------------|---------------------|
|   **Can we just let the linear layer learn any real-valued weight?** | Logistic regression / linear models routinely permit both signs because it gives maximum expressive power (a strictly positive logistic regression can approximate any convex decision boundary after adding an intercept). | In FeatsLLM we *know* what every feature means: it represents “the satisfiability of an LLM-derived condition”. From an interpretability standpoint it makes sound sense—if “Age > 70 AND Symptom = Yes” holds true we expect *more evidence* for Class A, never *less*. Allowing negatives forces us toward an interpretation “this particular condition suppresses Class A”, which contradicts how we built the rules. |
|   **What happens if we let negatives be learned?** | When training data are tiny ($k<10{-}20$), gradients try harder to push noisy marks toward large magnitudes in either direction before settling around zero—noise dominates and casting doubt on small signals leads to poor generalisation and dramatic sensitivity so \<10 shots misclassify even simple datasets when irrelevant columns appear (Fig 5). | By excluding negatives we funnel learning toward “additive evidence”. The model learns an intercept only through Softmax’s normalisation – hence each class’s logit grows monotonically as more matching rules fire – giving very stable behaviour even when $$k$$ is tiny and when extra spurious columns are added (see §3⁶). |

The relationship works out mathematically too:

*Without Sign Constraint:*  
If a feature $z_j$ had extreme ($<0$) weight $w_j<0$, its presence subtracts from $\ell_k$, making $\ell_k$ lower than some other class’s logit $\ell_{k'}+w_j'\dots$. In low-shot regimes this could swing predictions solely because one noisy bit happened during training.

*With Sign Constraint:*  
All contributes positively:
$$
{\ell_k^i}=%
   \sum_{j=1}^{R}
   {\underbrace{\Bigl[\max(w_{kj},\,0)\Bigr]}_{\text{non-\negative}}%
   }\,z_{kj}^i .
$$
This guarantees the model behaves like *an interpretable additive tree*: every fired rule adds evidence in favour of its own class only—there can never be “conflicting evidence” from within one class itself.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Interpretability impact

Because each coefficient now has one clear semantic meaning—“how much does satisfying this specific LLM-rule increase our certainty for this class?”—an analyst can walk through   ${w}>${°}${⁺}$ values such as

>   ⟦${z_2}\equiv$\emph{\mbox{"age ≥ 80 AND chest pain = ``typical''"} }→+${\(1\.5)} 		
>   ⟦${z_7}\equiv$\emph{\mbox{"has had heart attack before"} }→+${\(0\.8)} 		

If any coefficient were allowed negative,
the interpreter would have heard statements like

>   ⟦if ${z_5}\equiv$\emph{\mbox{"smoker"} }fires → attendance less likely ___this disease___⟧   

which usually contradicts medical knowledge unless carefully justified—a subtle source of error propagation when steering downstream decisions.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Bias and leakage concerns

Unconstrained coefficients do **not** automatically amplify latent bias in LLM-generated rules:
1. **Towards Zero:** Logistic regression under weak data drive all $w_j\simeq 0$; bias manifests purely through which indices turn out positive vs zero.
2. **With Non-negativity:** If many rules associated with socially sensitive attributes consistently get larger positive weights merely because LLM has memorised biased associations (e.g., ``age ≥ 50 & female → severe disease''), those biases stay visible rather than being hidden inside arbitrary signed variables.
   
Thus requiring non-negativity keeps bias *visible*, permitting specification-time checks (`rule set passed audit ?`) – without it you might obtain weirdly small correlated negatives hiding illicit bias in hard-to-explain ways.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Bottom line

FeatsLLM adopts the idea  
“**a satisfied rule should never reduce our confidence**” directly into its forward pass,
principally for three reasons:

1. **Simplicity & stability** – With few examples strong signals must dominate quickly; forbidding cancellations helps maintain low variance across trials.
2. **Human readable semantics** – Positive coefficients map cleanly onto “each fired condition gives us more evidence” and fit naturally into post–hoc explanations.
3. **Bias transparency** – Keeps any systematic favoritism explicit rather than buried inside hidden sign switches.

Standard unconstrained linear/logistic Regression works well when you have ample data so every useful feature can be found by optimizing both directions simultaneously.
But FeatsLLM operates under extreme few-shot setups and constructs its own **feature space** by shrinking complicated tabular attributes into clear Boolean tests that we expect either help or do nothing—but not hurt—any particular class prediction.
Constraining parameters accordingly enforces that expectation and keeps the learning surface largely benign.



So skip-or-replace ``negative weights'' ? ➜ keep them out unless you explicitly want some brain-teaching curriculum (“rule X means don’t give this patient disease Y”) — which was not part of what FeatsLLM intends at design time.
