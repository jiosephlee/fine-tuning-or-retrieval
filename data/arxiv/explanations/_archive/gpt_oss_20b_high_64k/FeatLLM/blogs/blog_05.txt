## Introduction  

In many practical settings—hospital record analysis, credit scoring, medical diagnosis—the amount of labeled data collected is limited due to privacy constraints or expensive annotation costs. Traditional supervised tabular models such as Logistic Regression or XGBoost normally rely on dozens or hundreds of labeled samples before presenting decent accuracy – a bottleneck when *only a handful* of examples are available.*

Large Language Models (LLMs), trained on billions of tokens from diverse sources, bring an implicit repository of domain knowledge that a conventional ML pipeline cannot exploit directly. Previous work has attempted to inject this knowledge into tabular models through end‑to‑end prompt injection or parameter fine‑tuning across trained exemplars – yet those approaches fare poorly when prompts become long (hundreds of feature columns) and still demand an LLM call per test instance.*

**FeatLLM** sidestep these pitfalls by turning the LLM *into a feature engineer* that produces succinct decision rules from a few examples and then hands those rules off to a lightweight downstream learner that performs predictions locally—all without querying the LLM again.*

---

## How FeatLLM Works  

The pipeline consists of three organized stages

| Stage | Purpose | Key Operation |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| **Rule Extraction** | Condense expert logic into interpretable conditions | An informative prompt feeds the few gold examples together with task wording *and* natural‑language definitions of every column into GPT‑3½ turbo-\(0613\). The model is instructed explicitly through a <bold>two–step reasoning chain</bold>: first infer general causal relations between columns and outcome **without** looking at the examples (capturing prior knowledge); second apply this understanding along with the demonstrations **to create ten target-specific rules** per class.*
| **Rule Parsing & Binary Feature Construction** | Turn erratic textual conditions into computable variables | Rather than hand‑crafting stubborn parsers, the same LLM receives a lightweight function specification paired with the rule set and produces short Python code snippets that evaluate every sample against each rule.* The generated snippets are executed locally (`exec()`), yielding a binarised matrix \(Z\) where each column indicates whether a particular rule fires.*
| **Learning & Ensembling** | Decide how strongly every rule influences predictions | For class \(k\) let \(w_k\ge0\) be learnable weights associated with its \(R\) rules—non‑negativity guarantees no rule ever hurts a class score.*  
Given binary vector \(z_k^{(i)}\), compute logit  
\(l_k^{(i)}=\sum_j w_{kj}\cdot z_{kj}^{(i)}\).  
Then convert logits jointly across classes through Softmax producing class probabilities.\*  
The above lightweight linear system is trained on the few available _train_ rows using cross–entropy loss.*  

Multiple independent chains—each sampling different permutations of training exemplars and snippets
with elevated temperature—produce distinct sets \(\left(Z^{(t)}, w^{(t)}\right)\). Averaging their class probabilities constitutes an ensemble akin to Random Forest bagging,
but realized entirely offline so once deployed every inference involves nothing more than two dot products.*

* The code snippet generation normalises linguistic variations (“greater than” vs “>”) automatically—a major source‐of–error gate avoided by earlier systems.*

## Real‐World Evaluation  

FeatLLM was tested systematically over **13** publicly available classification workloads spanning four domains  
*(e.g., adult income prediction, credit risk assessment, disease occurrence forecasting), including recently added UCI hosts so none were available during GPT pretraining.* → Maintaining unbiased experimentation.*

### Observed Ranking  

| Tier | Baseline | Avg AUC (FeatLLM + 4 shots) | Observation |
|------|----------|-----------------------------|-------------|
| Classical ML | Logistic Regression / XGBoost / RandomForest | Slightly lower than FeatLLM’s averageAUC↑1–3 % | Conventional models still benefit from full label set |
| Fine “few–shot” methods | TabPFN / STUNT / In–Context Learning / TABLET / TabLLM | Lower overall *and* collapse when #features > ≈90 – they either hit prompt limits *or* generate noisy outputs | Indicates sensitivity of end–to–end prompting |
| *FeatLLM* | — | **Top performer overall**, outruns even traditional models on most cards **💥** |

Under extreme paucity (*k=4* labels)—a realistic scenario where annotators supply merely one positive example per class—FeatLLM attains an average AUROC exceeding those achieved by Logistic Regression trained on *32* labelled rows (*≈8× fewer labels*) and *surpassing recently popular contrasts* such as TabPFN (+10 % relative gain reported across most benchmarks).

Notably, on data-rich scenarios featuring more than hundred engineered covariates—for instance “Communities” and “Myocardial” which have >120 attributes—classical baselines report unstable predictions while FeatLLM keeps its advantage thanks largely to its **bagged rule extraction**, which keeps every trial’s prompt under the token budget irrespective of raw dimensionality.

### Why Does It Beat Full Data Models With Only Four Annotated Examples?

1. **Prior Knowledge Amplification**  
   The two-step rational chain forces GPT hardwired commonsense (“older adults are more likely heart disease”) before inspecting numeric values distributed among thousands of examples. These priors bake themselves straight into ten concise rules whose open‐domain relevance outweighs what could ever emerge from four local observations alone wannabe logistic weights learned directly from data.*

2. **Coarse Successive Signal Reduction**  
   Turning statistical signals into Boolean tests compresses uncertain numeric dependencies into deterministic heuristic filters flanked by learned positivity weights – simplifying what would otherwise become N−dimensional continuous optimisation problems against sparse observational noise.*

3. **Built-In Robustness** –<|reserved_200313|> usual extremes simply illustrated below…

   • *Ensemble effect:* Even if one pass mistakenly proposes an invalid rule (“high BMI always signals cancer”),
   another trial weed properly ignores it owing its low weight after training → net effect negligible.*

   • *Bagging design*: By randomly sampling subsets out-of-features during each pass,
   severe aliasing prevents spurious patterns spurring when mass increases*

   • *Rule count*: With ten well-balanced settings it gave discovery space strong enough multiplicity*

These measures assure consistent ranking alone despite deprior divergence in traditional ensembles.)

> Because initial reasoning “understands what CAN influence outcome whereas randomness cannot,” Feature budget focuses almost exclusively onto genuine modalities ensuring exposure remains predominantly legitimate.

### Ablation Insights  

The authors systematically peeled away their pipeline bricks:


1️⃣ **Weight Prior(-Tune)** → Shifting away weighting produced target scores down ≈5% —establishing importance parameterization indeed matters less when label count small but grows surmountably when larger.


2️⃣ _Ensemble subtractor_ → Dropping aggregation reduced consistency dramatically (~9%) highlighting proven advantage put forth earlier.


3️⃣ _Task Description omitted_ → Precision loss cluster occurred mainly where nuance shared by instrumental fields kept SP but absent.



These layers confirm _all parts clearly contribute – none redundant._
