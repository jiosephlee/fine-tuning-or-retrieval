### How does the two‑step reasoning instruction prevent learning spurious associations?
Question:
The paper claims that splitting reasoning into (step 1 – infer causal relation without samples + step 2 – derive class‐specific rules using demonstrations) avoids picking up noise‐only correlations among irrelevant columns. Can someone explain intuitively why separating these steps makes the model immune (or less sensitive?) to spurious correlations inferred purely from few demonstrations?
Answer:
The central difficulty in few‑shot tabular learning is that a very small set of labeled examples can contain **spurious patterns**—random fluctuations that happen simply because the sample size is tiny.
If a model were able to specialise *only* on those patterns, its performance would collapse once a slightly different contest appears or after unrelated features are added.



In Fe­tLLM this problem is tackled at the level of the *prompt* itself.
The authors split the reasoning carried out by a large language model into two explicit stages:



| Step | What it does | What information it uses |
|------|--------------|--------------------------|
| **Step 1** | “Infer causal relation” – the LLM looks at **feature names / types** and the **task description** and answers questions such as *which attributes could reasonably influence the target?* | Prior knowledge encoded in its pre‑training corpus (e.g., knowing that *age* or *blood pressure* are medically related to heart disease) **without touching any examples** |
| **Step 2** | “Derive class‑specific rules” – once the set of potentially relevant attributes has been decided, the LLM examines a handful of demonstrations (few‑shot data) and proposes thresholds or logical combinations for each class | The same few labelled samples **plus** the causal foundation laid out in Step 1 |

### Why this ordering suppresses noise‑only correlations

#### 1. Inductive bias coming from prior knowledge  

In Step 1, only attributes whose semantics suggest a causal link are *promised* a chance to participate in subsequent learning.  
An unrelated column such as `zip_code` normally has no obvious connection with heart failure, so even if it happens statistically correlate with the target within an eight‑shot subset, Step 1 will almost never advise including it among potential candidate variables.  
Consequently when we later generate concrete rules (Step 2), these irrelevant attributes simply do not appear as options at all—a practical form of feature selection happening inside the prompt logic itself.



#### 2. Reducing combinatorial overfitting  

A single‑step prompt that directly feeds demos into an unconstrained chain‑of‑thought can instantiate dozens or hundreds of arbitrary clauses simply because they happen to co‑occur correctly within those few samples.  
By asking first “what could possibly matter?” before “what specific pattern should carry this weight,” how many clauses can be generated shrinks dramatically; most potential clauses are filtered out before examination against data.  
This prevents over‑specific behaviour such as constructing “if `zip_code` = ‘12345’ then Y” when `zip_code` just happens to be present in every example where Y occurs by chance.



#### 3. Empirical evidence  

Fe­tLLM reports an ablation where removing Step 1 (“\-Reasoning”) reduces performance considerably—not just overall accuracy but especially robustness when extra noisy features are appended (Figure 6 in the paper).  
Moreover, when they inserted hundreds of unrelated Adult‑dataset columns into a Heart prediction task—exactly what would tempt a naïve learner—their method’s AUROC deteriorated only marginally, whereas conventional models fell sharply.  
The authors even measured that *only 1–2 %* of generated rules referenced truly unrelated columns after Step 1 had been performed (see “Effect of adding spurious correlations” section).

#### 4. Human analogy  

Think like an experienced doctor who first asks “Which body systems could be involved?” before observing test results for threshold values on lab numbers. A skilled clinician’s presumptions act as guardrails preventing them from mistakenly treating every curiosity as diagnostic evidence—a role played here by Step 1’s semantic vetting.



In short, separating inference into *prior‑knowledge filtering* followed by *data‑driven rule extraction* hard‑codes an inductive bias against learning from accidental coincidences present only in limited demonstrations.<br>
This two‑step schema explains why Fe­tLLM remains largely immune to noisy spurious correlations when faced with very few labeled examples or many irrelevant features.

### What exact textual format does FeatLLM expect when receiving generated rules?
Question:
I see references under *Prompt Design* mentioning “specific response format” expressed during training prompts (Figure 2). Could someone give a concrete example—including headers、“Class = ” label placement and rule syntax—that would satisfy parsing requirements without triggering ECOS’ Naïve‑Program code extractor?   Also note that recipes explicitly forbid “#” symbols in prompts.
Answer:
**Short answer**  
FeatLLM expects every generated *rule‑set* to look exactly like this (the order is irrelevant – only the *layout* matters):

```
Answer:
### Class = Positive
   Rule0 : BloodPressure >120 AND Cholesterol>=250 
   Rule1 : $Age \ge 55$
   Rule2 : Smoker==no   
   Rule3–… … Run R•••

### Class = Negative 
   Rule0 : $BloodPressure \le 120$ OR Cholesterol<250 
   …
```  

(The ellipsis stands for whatever other nine‑rule slots you get.)  
In plain prose this means :

* Each **class block** starts **on its own line** with  
  `​### Class = <your exact label>` – it must contain *exactly* this form (`=`, capital C,
  no spaces before equals sign).  
* The block is immediately followed **by one or more lines**, each beginning  
  `​Rule<n> :` where `<n>` counts from 0 upwards—no leading/trailing spaces.
  The colon right after `<n>` is required so the parsing routine can split out the
  condition phrase.
* Inside a condition you can freely mix comparisons (`>`, `>=`, `<`, `<=`) **or**
  equality tests (`==`).  
  For categorical columns just write  
    `FeatureName==any_value_corresponding_to_this_category`.  
* Logical connectors are limited to **the words** `AND`,`OR`; do **not** use slashes,
ampersands (`&`) or any other Unicode character – plain ASCII keeps things simple.
* A **multi‑class task** simply repeats the same scheme: another header line starting
with `### Class = <label>` followed by its own numbered list.
* Lines are separated only by standard line breaks (`\n`).  
There must be **no hash (`#`) tokens anywhere**, otherwise ECOS’ naïve‑program extractor believes you are writing comments and drops those rows when it turns your natural‑language specification into executable Python.
      
That's it – keep exactly these delimiters (`===`,`:`) and avoid any accidental comment characters (‘#’). Under those constraints FeatLLM’s internal parser turns every “RuleX:” line into a Boolean test wired straight into an executable Python function, so nothing gets lost and you won’t trigger ECOS’ naïve‑program extractor."

### Why project linear weights onto non‑negative space via $	exttt{	extbackslash(max}(\mathbf{w}	exttt{	extbackslash ,0}	exttt{	extbackslash )}$?), i.e., skip negative weights?
Question:
Equation (4) forces learned weights onto $[0,	ildeho)$ guaranteeing all contributions increase likelihoods rather than decrease them. Why isn’t standard unconstrained linear/logistic regression sufficient here?   Any insight into whether allowing negative coefficients would harm interpretability or leak unwanted bias?”},{
Answer:
**Short answer**

FeatsLLM turns a handful of hand-made binary indicators—each one telling whether an *LLM-generated* rule holds—into a very simple linear-scoring function  

$$
\text{logit}_k=\Bigl[\max(\boldsymbol w_k ,\,0)\Bigr]\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol z_k .
$$

The `max(·,0)` simply bans *negative* coefficients.  
Because every feature $$\,{\boldsymbol z}\in\{0,1\}^{\, R}\,$$ says **“this sample satisfies a specific condition,”** FeatsLLM’s design philosophy is that satisfied conditions can only *increase* our belief in a particular class; it does **not** intend‐to make “a rule being true means *less* likely” an option.  
Constraining all weights be non‑negative therefore preserves this monotonic interpretation while giving the model enough flexibility (on top of an overall intercept) to separate classes through the soft-max layer.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why constrain the weights?

| Question | Usual practice | FeatsLLM rationale |
|----------|----------------|---------------------|
|   **Can we just let the linear layer learn any real-valued weight?** | Logistic regression / linear models routinely permit both signs because it gives maximum expressive power (a strictly positive logistic regression can approximate any convex decision boundary after adding an intercept). | In FeatsLLM we *know* what every feature means: it represents “the satisfiability of an LLM-derived condition”. From an interpretability standpoint it makes sound sense—if “Age > 70 AND Symptom = Yes” holds true we expect *more evidence* for Class A, never *less*. Allowing negatives forces us toward an interpretation “this particular condition suppresses Class A”, which contradicts how we built the rules. |
|   **What happens if we let negatives be learned?** | When training data are tiny ($k<10{-}20$), gradients try harder to push noisy marks toward large magnitudes in either direction before settling around zero—noise dominates and casting doubt on small signals leads to poor generalisation and dramatic sensitivity so \<10 shots misclassify even simple datasets when irrelevant columns appear (Fig 5). | By excluding negatives we funnel learning toward “additive evidence”. The model learns an intercept only through Softmax’s normalisation – hence each class’s logit grows monotonically as more matching rules fire – giving very stable behaviour even when $$k$$ is tiny and when extra spurious columns are added (see §3⁶). |

The relationship works out mathematically too:

*Without Sign Constraint:*  
If a feature $z_j$ had extreme ($<0$) weight $w_j<0$, its presence subtracts from $\ell_k$, making $\ell_k$ lower than some other class’s logit $\ell_{k'}+w_j'\dots$. In low-shot regimes this could swing predictions solely because one noisy bit happened during training.

*With Sign Constraint:*  
All contributes positively:
$$
{\ell_k^i}=%
   \sum_{j=1}^{R}
   {\underbrace{\Bigl[\max(w_{kj},\,0)\Bigr]}_{\text{non-\negative}}%
   }\,z_{kj}^i .
$$
This guarantees the model behaves like *an interpretable additive tree*: every fired rule adds evidence in favour of its own class only—there can never be “conflicting evidence” from within one class itself.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Interpretability impact

Because each coefficient now has one clear semantic meaning—“how much does satisfying this specific LLM-rule increase our certainty for this class?”—an analyst can walk through   ${w}>${°}${⁺}$ values such as

>   ⟦${z_2}\equiv$\emph{\mbox{"age ≥ 80 AND chest pain = ``typical''"} }→+${\(1\.5)} 		
>   ⟦${z_7}\equiv$\emph{\mbox{"has had heart attack before"} }→+${\(0\.8)} 		

If any coefficient were allowed negative,
the interpreter would have heard statements like

>   ⟦if ${z_5}\equiv$\emph{\mbox{"smoker"} }fires → attendance less likely ___this disease___⟧   

which usually contradicts medical knowledge unless carefully justified—a subtle source of error propagation when steering downstream decisions.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Bias and leakage concerns

Unconstrained coefficients do **not** automatically amplify latent bias in LLM-generated rules:
1. **Towards Zero:** Logistic regression under weak data drive all $w_j\simeq 0$; bias manifests purely through which indices turn out positive vs zero.
2. **With Non-negativity:** If many rules associated with socially sensitive attributes consistently get larger positive weights merely because LLM has memorised biased associations (e.g., ``age ≥ 50 & female → severe disease''), those biases stay visible rather than being hidden inside arbitrary signed variables.
   
Thus requiring non-negativity keeps bias *visible*, permitting specification-time checks (`rule set passed audit ?`) – without it you might obtain weirdly small correlated negatives hiding illicit bias in hard-to-explain ways.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Bottom line

FeatsLLM adopts the idea  
“**a satisfied rule should never reduce our confidence**” directly into its forward pass,
principally for three reasons:

1. **Simplicity & stability** – With few examples strong signals must dominate quickly; forbidding cancellations helps maintain low variance across trials.
2. **Human readable semantics** – Positive coefficients map cleanly onto “each fired condition gives us more evidence” and fit naturally into post–hoc explanations.
3. **Bias transparency** – Keeps any systematic favoritism explicit rather than buried inside hidden sign switches.

Standard unconstrained linear/logistic Regression works well when you have ample data so every useful feature can be found by optimizing both directions simultaneously.
But FeatsLLM operates under extreme few-shot setups and constructs its own **feature space** by shrinking complicated tabular attributes into clear Boolean tests that we expect either help or do nothing—but not hurt—any particular class prediction.
Constraining parameters accordingly enforces that expectation and keeps the learning surface largely benign.



So skip-or-replace ``negative weights'' ? ➜ keep them out unless you explicitly want some brain-teaching curriculum (“rule X means don’t give this patient disease Y”) — which was not part of what FeatsLLM intends at design time.
