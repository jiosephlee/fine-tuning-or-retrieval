# Sequence Leverage Wins Back!: Introducing Group Sequence Policy Optimization  

Large‑scale reinforcement learning (RL) has become the go‑to technique for pushing language models—think competition‑level math solvers or code generators that can reason over dozens of tokens in a row.  
Yet every time we crank up model size or lengthen generation steps, the flagship RL algorithms start sabotaging themselves: gradients blow up, optimism from long‑sequence rewards turns into runaway “collapse” where the learner can never recover even after shuffling checkpoints or tightening hyper‑parameters.



## The Motivation – Why Token‑Level Weights Fail  

Reinforcement learning boils down to learning a policy \(\pi_\theta\) that assigns higher probability mass to higher‑reward behaviours. Modern dense or Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) language models already generate an entire **sequence** \(y=(y_1,\dots ,y_{|y|})\) given a prompt \(x\).  
The reward \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\), however—derived from an external verifier—shines only at the end of the sequence.



### The classic mistake  

GRPO replaces this end‑of‑shot view with a **token‑wise** proxy:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\qquad \widehat{A}_{i}= \text{relative reward among } G \text{ samples}.
\]

Each Token gets its own weight even though the reward belongs to the whole sentence!  
This mis‑application violates importance sampling’s premise — we don’t have a “sample distribution” over full sentences here – and inevitably introduces **high‑variance noise:** the single sampled token can be wildly off–policy relative to old policy before scaling them into a tiny batch or train step.
Longer sequences amplify this danger because you’re multiplying many independent noisy ratios.


### Matching units gives stability  

The simple insight is clear:
> **The unit of your objective should match the unit of your signal**.
  
Rewards live at **sequence level**, so does any sensible policy correction weight should live there too.
When you use a scalar derived from full‑sentence likelihoods you avoid bootstrapping at each step;
you then crop entire outputs instead of curtailing individual words.


## Group Sequence Policy Optimization – The Core Idea  

GSPI was born from that insight: compute an *importance ratio over entire responses*, normalize it by length so responses of different sizes don’t get unfairly penalized, then clip whole sequences accordingly.


### Step 1 – Generate several replies per prompt  

For every query \(x\), collect a group \(\{ y_i \}\) (\(G\) answers) by rolling out old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).


### Step 2 – Compute **group advantage**  

Given scores \(r(x,y_i)\), normalize within each group:

\[
\widehat{A}_i
=
\frac{ r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\bigl(\{ r(x,y_j)\}\bigr)}
     {\operatorname{std}\bigl(\{ r(x,y_j)\}\bigr)} .
\]

So replies better than their cohort get positive \(\widehat A\), worse ones negative.


### Step 3 – Build a *sequence* importance ratio  

Rather than per‑token quotients we aggregate:

\[
s_i(\theta)
=
\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}
           {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)} \Bigr)
^{\,1/|y_i|}
=
\exp\!\Bigl(
       \frac{1}{|y_i|}
       \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
           \log
           \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
       \Bigr).
\]

The exponent \(1/|y|\) length‑normalizes the product expression so sequences do not differ wildly simply because they have more tokens.


### Step 4 – Apply proximal clipping on whole sequences  

Define a very tight clipping range (\(\varepsilon_{\text{low}}\approx3\times10^{-4},~\varepsilon_{\text{high}}\approx4\times10^{-4}\)) computed on \(s_i(\theta)\).  
The full **GSPI objective** writes simply:

\[
J_{\!GSPI}(\theta)
=
\E_{x,\;\{y_j\}}
\,\Biggl[
\,\frac{1}{G}\!\sum\nolimits_k
   \min
   \Bigl(
          s_k(\theta)\,\widehat A_k,\;
          \mathrm{clip}\!\bigl(s_k(\theta),\, 
                               1-\varepsilon_{\!low},
                               1+\varepsilon_{\!high}\bigr)
          \widehat A_k
   \Bigr)
\,\\[-2mm]
].
\]

The *only* random term comes from old-policy rollouts; every other part depends deterministically on that sample batch.



## Why Does This Fix Runaway Gradients?

Two intertwined reasons make GSPI far less volatile than its token–level cousins.



### Uniform contribution inside a response  

Token–level tricks multiply every word’s score by its own “on/off” factor \(w_{i,t}\). Those factors vary wildly between \(0< w <1+\varepsilon</sub>]\) for positive advantage and \( [1-\varepsilon,+∞)\] otherwise—semaphores hardly average out on long sentences.  
GSPI collapses them into one number \(s_k(\theta)\): every word receives exactly the same multiplicative factor (the derivative part still averages over words). After slogging through many mini‑updates no single lexical mistake can make an integer blow up or collapse because it is capped by \(s_k(\theta)\)’s limited range.\)*


### Off–policy correction applied where it matters  

Since rewards function through complete sentences, correcting *which* sentence will appear next is enough—talking about wrong words inside the continuation is irrelevant if the string overall is still unacceptable even after changing one letter.\*
The pipeline therefore needs fewer backward passes per rollout batch, lowering memory pressure without any value network.



#### Gradient structural insight – concise comparison  
For illustration let us drop clipping terms so we see clean forms (taken directly from derivations in the paper):

```
GRPO grad:
     E[ Σ_(k,i)
           Ĥₖ · w_(k,i)(θ)/w_(k,i)(θ_old)
           · ∇ log π_θ(y_(k,i)) ]

GSPI grad:
     E[ Σ_k
           Ĥₖ · [π_θ(y_k)/π_old(y_k)]^(1/|y_k|)
           · ( ∑ₜ ∇ log π_θ(y_(k,t))/|y_k|)
```

In GRPO each word gets a *different* multiplier `w`. In GSPI all words get the same multiplier `s`. Nothing else changes—yet emergent stability appears immediately.



## Practical Consequences Observed In Practice  

* Sequences rarely touch their saturation boundaries while dozens of short answers would be clipped heavily under GRPO; consequently more data keeps flowing into updates without blowing up gradients.
* Training runs never collapsed even when scaling answers sevenfold longer—a situation where older algorithms had failed catastrophically.
* When applied to MoE models—a notoriously volatile setting due TO fragmented `expert` activations—GSPI did away with an elaborate “routing replay” trick required by GRPO almost entirely via its robust seq‑likelihood construction.
* Because GSPI depends exclusively on full‐sequence likelihoods computed by inference engines, precision mismatches seen when distributing inference engines such as SGLang across multiple GPUs no longer matter; its stability lets us reuse already reported likelihoods straight away instead of recomputing them in training engines—a clear microservice simplification gap closed.



## Running Your Own Large Model With GSPI 🤗  

Below are some rough guidelines distilled from experiments reported in our benchmarks:\n\n```mermaid\nflowchart TD\nA[Collect panel queries]\nB[Rollout G=32 samples via old policy]\nC[Compute rewards via verifier]\nD[Normalise advantage per group]\nE[Length normalised seq weight]-->|clip ε≈3×10⁻⁴..4×10⁻⁴|\nF[Policy update via backprop]\nG[Iterate]\nA-->B-->C-->D-->E-->F-->G\n```\n*Use an optimizer such as AdamW with learning rate tuned separately.*

Key hyper‑parameters:
* `ε_low ≈ 3e−4`, `ε_high ≈ 4e−4`
* Group size `G` conservative enough (*≈32*) yet sufficiently large (larger groups push interior estimate variance lower).
* No value network required – savings ≈50 % GPU memory vs PPO style setups.
