\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Introduction to Mathematical Reasoning in Language Models

Mathematical reasoning demands that a language model steps beyond surface‐level pattern matching and engages in structured, multi‑step deduction that mirrors human problem solving. Unlike typical language tasks—categorization or translation—mathematics requires manipulating symbols rigorously, ordering operations correctly, and preserving logical dependencies across intermediate steps. Consequently, even large pre‑trained models often flounder when confronted with problems that span algebraic manipulation, integral calculus or intricate combinatorial proofs. This stark gap between natural language proficiency and quantitative rigor has motivated a sequence of research efforts aimed at closing the productivity chasm between generative large language models (LLMs) such as GPT‑4 \citep{gpt4} or Gemini Ultra \citep{gemini} and their free‑to‑access counterparts.

Recent work demonstrates that carefully curated data pipelines can substantially lift open‑source models’ mathematical performance without resorting to external solvers or majority voting schemes.\footnote{DeepSeekMath~7B attains $51.7\%$ on competition‐level MATH via pure generation alone}. Beyond the sheer volume of training examples—$120\,\mathrm{B}$ tokens mined from Common Crawl—architectural innovations such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) further refine policy behavior by leveraging comparative reward signals while mitigating critic‐based computational overhead.\citep{grpo}

The chapter below traces the major milestones that have shaped AI’s engagement with mathematics, introduces core benchmark suites used for evaluation—including both quantitative exercises and geometric proof challenges—and frames essential research questions motivating recent breakthroughs such as DeepSeekMath.\footnote{All benchmarks adhere to standard evaluation protocols defined by ``Self‐Consistency’’ or hard‐truth thresholds rather than fuzzy correctness scores}. Finally, it surveys representative state of the art baselines spanning closed‑source Giants (GPT‑4/Gemini Ultra) and leading open source models (DeepSeekMath~7B, Minerva 540B), highlighting key numerical gaps still uncovered by researchers.\newline 

# Historical progression of AI in mathematics
Mathematics has been a boon for machine learning since an early exposure found symbolic patterns could be exploited through neural networks trained on large corpora of problems scraped from textbooks and forums.\,\nIn its nascent stages most attempts were limited by modest token budgets ($\sim10^8$ tokens) and generic transformer architectures unspecialised for symbolic logic.\nThose early architectures achieved modest zero‑shot pass rates ($<10\%$) even on elementary graded problems.\nA notable leap came when pretraining shifted toward code corpora,\nwhich inherently share structural properties—such as syntax trees—with mathematical expressions.\nDeepSeek‐coder models originally grounded their weights on $850\,\mathrm{B}$ tokens of code paired with broad natural language data,\nand demonstrated that subsequent exposure to high quality mathematical web text could retain coding competence while boosting stepwise problem solving.\nThis trend culminated recently in DeepSeekMath~7B where code pretraining now precedes an expansive $120\,\mathrm{B}$ token math corpus derived primarily from Common Crawl.\nEmpirically this approach yields accuracy jumps comparable in magnitude to “size” increases of three orders:\nA $44\,\mathrm{B}$ token variant pretrained without mathematics achieves roughly $32\%$ on GSM8K,\nbut adding $\sim120\,\mathrm{B}$ well filtered index sentences pushes it above $83\%$.  

# Key quantitative benchmarks (MATH ✔️ GSM8K)
Benchmark suites encapsulate varying degrees of difficulty and domain coverage.\nThe smartphone Simpler challenge set GSM8K contains $\sim8\,000$ grade‐school style problems spanning arithmetic, algebraic identities and straightforward probability.\nIts evaluation metric is strict exact match against official solutions;\nthe test split consists mainly of diverse test inputs devoid of external data patterns therefore gauges pure deductive capability.\nIn contrast MATH offers a substantially larger ($28\,000$?) question set extracted from competitive mathematics sources ranging from AMC/APOS tests to national college competitions.\nEach MATH item can demand up to four deliberate reasoning steps:\na sequence beginning with problem interpretation,\nequation derivation,\nand final numeric reconstruction grounded purely in symbolic manipulation.\nIn practice state‐of‐the‐art closed source ensembles such as GPT‑4 reach roughly $55\%$ exact accuracy;\nas seen by experimental results this sits close behind Gemini Ultra’s $56$\%. 
Open source systems historically trail far behind:\ngenerally achieve near $30–35\%$, but DeepSeekMath~7B breaks the ceiling by reaching $51.7\%$ % using raw generation alone;
self consistency across grid sampling secures an additional boost up to $60\.9\%$.  
These contrasts underscore how maturity needs both sizeable quantity of curated math tokens *and* thoughtful pedagogical instruction derived from chain-of-thought or program-of-thought tutorials.\nThey also highlight that gleaned efficacy shines more dramatically when training pipelines embrace diversity across numerical ranges and multiple formal systems ([equation] format versus natural language proofs).\footnote{\citep{sote}}

# DeepSeekMath Overview  

DeepSeekMath 7B sits atop a careful lineage rooted in a state‑of‑the‑art **code‑trained** transformer family while simultaneously injecting an unprecedented volume of mathematically curated web content and a targeted reinforcement–learning recipe called **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**. The resulting model surpasses most open‑source rivals on competition‑level mathematics benchmarks and zips close behind commercial giants GPT‑4 and Gemini Ultra—all while keeping its size under seven billion parameters.



# DeepSeek-Coder‑Base v1\.five Initialization Rationale  

The decision to warm up from *DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base v1\.five* rather than any generic language encoder was guided by two observations that are backed up empirically inside the study.



1. **Structured Symbolic Semantics:**  
   Code tokens encode well‐defined syntactic rules (e.g., `for`, `{}`, `=`) that echo those found in mathematical expressions (`∑`, `∫`, matrix indices). By exposing the network during early optimisation phases primarily to this symbolicity, it learns positional patterns—left-to-right dependencies and nested scoping—that later translate into stronger rule‐based inference when tackling algebraic identities or calculus identities.



2. **Memory Efficiency During Subsequent Fine‑Tuning:**  
   A transformer pretrained on programming tables has been shown—within the paper—to converge faster when later mixed with natural‐language objective episodes because it already internalises complex operator precedence tables beneficial for longer coherent chain-of-thought narratives.



Consequently *DeepSeekMath‑Base* was instantiated exactly at this checkpoint (7 billion parameters), leaving *code* careful weighted — roughly twenty percent raw GitHub output — ready for an enrichment phase dominated entirely by mathematics.

# “500 B Total Continuation Steps” Schedule  

Beyond bootstrapping from *Coder*, *DeepSeekMath* entered an extended “continuation epoch” lasting **\(5\times10^{11}\)** accumulated next-token predictions—a fairly precise gauge equivalent here:

1. **Hardware & Micro-batching:**  
   Training employed an AdamW optimiser (\(\beta_1=0·9,\;\beta_2=0·95,\;\mathrm{weight_{decay}=0·1}\)) operating over batches holding **\(10\,M\)” slice‐waters**, with every example capped at a preserved context window (\(4\,000\) tokens).  
   This affords roughly \(50\,000\) full-batch iterations over epochs, each converting back slightly relaxed warm‐up peaks emulating step-wise learning rate — peak at two thousand warm-up points following an exponential decay schedule peaking again at \(31 6 %\) after \(80 %\) progress­ing then resting back respectively at \(10 %\) once per epoch.



2. **Alternation Strategy:**  
   In practice each optimizer step pulled shards sequentially through every defined mixture level described above—in columns deeper than simple Rossired split—so that at every micro-batch proportionally matched the global percentages listed previously.



3． **Final Validation Window:**  
   A held-out set containing equal swathes from mathematics Olympiads (*MATH*, *GSM8K*) and real-world challenge sets (~\(15 k\)) monitored accuracy every \(256\,000\) update steps so that plateauing signals triggered termination once gradual gains fell below one hundredth percentile thresholds statistically described in Section&nbsp;5.



Thus raw next-token counts directly corresponded mining memory consumption slotted out effectively while still enabling direct measurement against standard benchmark scores—in particular earning an impressive **\(51½ %\)** score on competition level *MATH*.

# Constructing the DeepSeekMath Corpus

Large‐scale mathematical reasoning requires a training set that is both extensive **and** faithful to genuine mathematical discourse. In DeepSeekMath, we addressed this challenge by developing an end‑to‑end extraction pipeline that mined an ever‑growing assortment of mathematics‑rich web pages from Common Crawl (CC). The process required careful curation at every step—starting from seed data creation, crafting a lightweight yet powerful classifier with fastText, iteratively recalling new content while safeguarding against duplication, selectively retaining only the highest quality samples within a predetermined token budget, and finally decontaminating any inadvertent overlap with downstream benchmarks. Each component was designed to be reproducible while ensuring linguistic durability for downstream large language models (LLMs).

---

## # Seed Corpus: OpenWebMath & Initial FastText Labeling Examples

The foundation of any successful web‑scraping strategy lies in a **seed corpus** with high precision—pure signals that teach the system what “math content” looks like before it dives into an unstructured search space. We leveraged **OpenWebMath** \citep{openwebmath}, an existing curated collection of shortmathematical texts harvested from reputable Q&A sites such as Math Stack Exchange and MathOverflow. This dataset provides dense mathematical expressions interleaved with natural language explanations—exactly what LLMs will later encounter during fine‑tuning or inference.

To bootstrap our classifier we paired **positive** samples from OpenWebMath with **negative** samples drawn uniformly at random from CC’s raw archive:
- **Positive set:** \textbf{500 k} unique snippets deemed highly mathy.
- **Negative set:** \textbf{500 k} arbitrary CC webpages devoid of overt mathematics by design (transparent negatives).

The stark contrast between these two sets gave us unmistakable signal vectors for what constitutes a mathematics‑oriented document—without requiring manual labeling beyond this initial split.



---

## # FastText Classifier Configuration (dims 256 → vectors)

FastText—an efficient bag‑of‑character adapters into word embeddings—was chosen because it can ingest thousands of text windows while maintaining constant memory consumption and lightning speed on commodity GPUs. Its architecture is linear: two matrices
\[
W^{\text{in}}, W^{\text{out}} \in \mathbb R^{V\times d},
\]
where \(V\) is vocabulary size and \(d=256\) was settled empirically through an ablation study balancing representational capacity against overfitting risk on web noise.

Key hyperparameters:
| Parameter | Value | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Vector dimension (\(d\)) | \(256\) | Strong enough to differentiate mathematical notation yet compact enough for rapid joins over billions of documents |
| Learning rate (\(\eta\)) | \(0.1\) | Allows quick convergence given the relatively small labelled budget |
| Max n‑gram length | \(3\) | Captures short character bigrams plus tri‑grams capturing common symbols \(\{=,\sqrt{},etc.\}\) |
| Min word count (“minn”) | \(3\) | Prunes ultra‑rare tokens that would contribute noise |
| Epochs | \(3\) | Faster training tuned for one pass over \(1\,\text{M}\) labeled samples |

With these settings we trained a binary classifier outputting
\(p(y=\textit{math}\mid x)\)
for any input snippet \(x\). The clean output scores were then forwarded into the next pipeline stage performing *recall*.



---

## # Iterative Recall: Domain Grouping + Near Deduplication (~40 B HTML pages)

Processing raw CC is infeasible without prefiltering because billions of crawled URLs compress into millions if accounting for redundant copies or near duplicates (e.g., paginated discussions). We therefore employed a two‑stage reduction:

1. **URL-based Deduplication**  
   All identical URLs were collapsed into unique entries—a trivial `[URL] → [content]` mapping whose hash ensures nothing is retained twice.
2. **Near‐Duplicate Pruning**  
   Using shingle hashing techniques—sliding windows over character sequences—we compared documents pairwise and merged any exceeding an overlap threshold (\(\approx92\,\%\)). This eliminated echo effects caused by subtitle variations or auto-generated signatures typical on forum threads.

The result was roughly **40 billion HTML documents**, which served as the search space over which our fastText scorer could operate.



*Recall Phase*  
Each document received a *mathness* score from FastText; documents were sorted descendingly into buckets across many thresholds corresponding to different effective token budgets:
- **Topʹs JSON** (top-ranked list).
  
During each *iteration*, we increased both our training corpus breadth and quality:

| Iteration | New Positive Source | Approximate Size After Recall |
|---|---|---|
|0 – Baseline|OpenWebMath seeds alone|~13 billion sents balanced|
|1 – First pass|FastText recall + dedupe set|≈30 billion sents|
|2 – Domain expansion|Identified domains >10% coverage→manual anthology addition e.g., `mathoverflow.net/questions`|≈50 billion sents|
|3 – Penultimate refine |Re‑train FastText further on enriched positives→unchanged majority coverage.|≈100 billion sents|
|4 – Final sweep |Stop when <2 % new domain found.|≈120 billion sents|

After four passes we amassed around **35 million** distinct webpages registering roughly **\(120\,\mathrm{B}\)** tokens following content cleanup.

## # Top–Scoring Page Filtering & Token–Budget Decisions (40 B → 120 B)

A raw list sorted by fastText score is essentially unbounded; we thus had to decide *how many* documents made it into pre‑training data while respecting GPU budgets during model training phases later on. To do so:

1. **Token Counting per Page**  
   For each ranked document we performed whitespace tokenization following the `DeepSeek` model's vocabulary (including math symbols), obtaining token counts \(t_i\).  
2. **Cumulative Sliding Window**  
   We maintained a cumulative sum
   \[
   S(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_i ,
   \]
   stopping once \(S(k)\approx B_{\max}\), where \(B_{\max}\in \{40\,\mathrm{b},80\,\mathrm{b},120\,\mathrm{b},160\,\mathrm{b}\}\).
3. **Early Selection Strategy**  

   In practice our first *training experiment* evaluated each budget variant “in isolation” by feeding models trained solely on that slice into downstream tests such as GSM8K or MATH after just one epoch.
   
4. **Adopted Budget**  
   The empirical curve revealed diminishing returns beyond ~\(80\,\mathrm{b}\); however due to additional safety margins in later stages where float‐point accelerators become routine, we settled finally on keeping roughly
   \[
       B_{\max}=120\,\mathrm{b}
   \]
   tokens—a tenfold expansion beyond OpenWebMath’s original size while still within realistic compute budgets.



*Why Top–Scoring First?*  
FastText assigns higher scores preferentially when characteristic mathematical patterns appear—multiple symbol clusters or explicit equation formatting—which directly correlate with higher reasoning quality in downstream tasks such as formal theorem proving or dynamic calculation scripting.

## # Decontamination Against Benchmark Passages Using Exact n‑Gram Blocking

A delicate pitfall when mining web text for mathematics datasets is *benchmark contamination*. If any paragraph employed verbatim during training matches exactly some prompt–answer pair in later evaluation suites like GSM8K or MATH, then test results would reflect memorization rather than genuine reasoning—a serious integrity violation across academic VE research.

To preclude this risk we applied stringent look‑ahead filtering:

1. **Benchmark Token Sets:**  
   All benchmark passage strings were preprocessed identically—tokenized under `DeepSeek`’s vocabulary—and stored in hash maps keyed by substrings.
2. **Exact N-Gram Blocking Routine**  
   For every candidate webpage:
   - Extract all consecutive subsequences up to length \(L=10\).
   - Check whether any :subsequence appears fully inside any benchmark passage.
    
    If either condition satisfied,
    - If whole phrase length \(\geq10\,guds,\) discard entire page.
    - If page length is <10 and shares ≥ 3‐grams … also discard.
    
    This loop ensures even very short question/answer fragments do not slip through.
    
3. **Deduplication After Blocking**  
    Since near‐duplication had already been performed earlier
    we rechecked blocks only on surviving documents,
    avoiding unnecessary rehashes.
4. **Resultant Corpus Integrity**  
    Post decontamination inspection confirmed no known benchmark excerpt remained,
    while keeping ~\(118\,\mathrm{b}\) useful math tokens—no more than
    a negligible loss relative to raw recall output.

## Validating Corpus Quality Through Ablation Training  

The goal of an ablation study is to translate a change in data design into measurable downstream benefit so that designers can judge whether a new dataset truly contributes value beyond merely increasing size or quantity. In this chapter we examine precisely how our construction pipeline transforms raw jump‑started media into a corpus that benefits mathematical reasoning language models when evaluated under realistic zero‑shot and few‑shot regimes.



---

# Comparative Corpora  

To understand what makes our curated *DeepSeekMath* collection effective it was compared side‑by‑side against three established mathematical datasets obtained previously from the public domain:

* **MathPile** – an $8\text{\textup{q}}$–token compilation extracted largely from textbooks, Wikipedia entries under “math”, ProofWiki and $>85\%$ drawn from arXiv LaTeX papers.  
* **OpenWebMath** – a $13\text{\textup{q}}$–token subset mined solely from Common Crawl pages whose surface form conforms strongly to mathematical topics via external classifiers previously reported in literature \citep{openwebmath}.  
* **Proof‑Pile‑2** – an expanded $28\text{\textup{q}}$–token mixture proposed later than OpenWebMath (\citep{llemma}). It unites OpenWebMath, \textit{AlgebraicStack} code parlays ($10\text{\textsuperscript{3}}\!$ tokens) and arXiv material selected according $(1:2)$ arXiv :\textit{Code} : Web ratios adopted during its creation.\footnote{In our experiments these ratios were reproduced following \citet{llemma}.}

The ablation protocol fixed everything else while only swapping corpus contents:

1. Every corpus was served manually through identical preparation pipelines (URL de‑duplication followed by near‑deduplication).  
2. A lightweight $1.3\!$ B parameter model (*DeepSeek‑LLM 1.3B*) was fine‑tuned *once*, exposing it to $150\!\times10^{9}$ training steps distributed evenly across all listed tokens within each corpus.  
3. AdamW was used with $\beta_1=0.^9$, $\beta_2=0.^9$, $\mathrm{weight\_decay}=0.^1$. A two‑stage linear scheduling warmed up over $5000$ steps retaining peak throughput until roughly $80\%$ then decayed exponentially down to $10\%$.  
4. evaluations employed zero‑shot chain-of-thought prompting across eight selected mathematics challenges highlighted below.

# Few‑Shot Chain‐of‐Thought Evaluation  

Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting forces models to explain intermediate reasoning steps before delivering an answer—a method proven effective for multi‑step calculations without relying upon external software tools \citep{cot}. Our comparative experiment spanned seven quantitative reasoning datasets spanning elementary arithmetic through graduate level algebra:

| Benchmark | Language | Task Type |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| GSM8K | English | Algorithmic word problems |
| MATH | English | Competition–level college mathematics |
| SAT | English | Standardized test numerical questions |
| OCW Courses | English | College curriculum exercises |
| MMLU-STEM | English | Discipline‐specific multiple choice |
| MGSM‑zh | Chinese | Grade‑school problem solving |
| CMATH | Chinese | College problem set |

For every benchmark a $32$‑example few‑shot prompt was constructed using high quality CoT solutions catalogued during instruction tuning; seed examples were selected randomly among training splits ensuring teacher prompts mirroring exactly those used atop instruction fine tuning pipelines (\citet{sft_data_curation}). Accuracy was measured per benchmark as typically defined in prior work (\citet{gsm8k}, \citet{MATH}) wherever available.



Across all eight problems every model trained on *DeepSeekMath* scored higher than its rivals trained on any of the three comparison corpora—a pattern succinctly captured both numerically by Table~\ref{tab:corpora_comparison} and visually by Figure~\ref{fig:corpora_comparisons}. Importantly this finish demonstrates *not only size,* but *data fidelity*: older collections cycling through repeated arXiv content offer many duplicate abstracts while web pages provide more varied syntax spanning formulas embedded within natural language context.\footnote{\citet{sfc} stresses importance of this variety when evaluating convoluted stepwise chain reasoning herein } Consequently downstream accuracy rises more steadily as hypotheses accumulate mirrored directly across sentences rather than inherited solely via template re-use.
