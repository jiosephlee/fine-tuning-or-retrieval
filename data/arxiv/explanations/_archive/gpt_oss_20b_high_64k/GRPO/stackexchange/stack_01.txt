\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### "How does fastText help retrieve math web pages during DeepSeekMath corpus construction?"
Question:
"I read that the authors train a fastText classifier on OpenWebMath data and then use it to recall more math-related pages from Common Crawl.

I'm unclear what exactly fastText outputs here – does it rank all crawled URLs based on similarity scores?
Does it filter down a huge crawl set down to merely those whose cosine similarity exceeds some threshold?
And how do those scores translate into picking *the top X %* of pages?

The whole promise relies on this classifier being accurate yet easy enough for millions of queries – I'd love an intuitive explanation of that pipeline."
Answer:
In DeepSeekMath the “fastText‐based classifier” is used only as a lightweight, one-pass filter that turns an enormous Common-Crawl dump into a tractable collection of mathematically-relevant sites.  
The whole idea is very much the same as what most web scrapers do when they want a domain-specific corpus:  
* make a *seed* set that is known to contain target content,  
* learn a tiny discriminative model on this seed,
* apply it to every document in the raw crawl,
* keep only those documents whose predicted score looks convincing enough for downstream human curation.



### Training the model

From the paper’s own description

> “Using this corpus we train a fastText model … selecting **500 000** data points from OpenWebMath as positive examples … together with **500 000** other web pages from Common Crawl as negatives… vector dimension 256 … word n‐gram up to 3 … number of epochs 3” \citep{DeepSeekMath}..

Thus the final binary classifier is simply
$$
s(\mathbf{x}) \;\triangleq\;\mathbf{w}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{x} + b ,
$$
where $\mathbf{x}$ is the hashed representation produced by fastText’s sub-word look-ups  
(“ngrams up to length 3”), $\mathbf{w}$ is learned across positives and negatives,
and $s(\mathbf{x})$ can be interpreted either as a signed log-likelihood ratio or,
after applying $\sigma$, as $P(\text{math}\mid \mathbf{x})$.



### How it is used on Common Crawl

1. **Deduplication first** – Raw CC contains roughly \(100\,000\,000\,000\) URLs; before filtering it is collapsed via URL-based and near-duplicate removal down to about \(40\,000\,000\,000\nobreakspace{}HTML\nobreakspace{}pages\rule[--]{\.}{5pt}{<cite>{DeepSeekMath}}</cite>}.  
   Fast-text inference is inexpensive at this scale because its core operation is just several dot products between pre-hashed ngrams and weight vectors—no attention graph needs constructing.
2. **Scoring** – Every remaining page (after stripping tags) is fed into our trained fast-text model once, yielding $\textit{s}_{\,{\rm page}}$.  
   The authors explicitly say “We rank the collected pages according to their scores predicted by the fast-text model…”, meaning that higher real-valued logits correspond directly to higher confidence in being a mathematics site.
3. **Top-rank filtering** – Pages whose scores fall into the upper tail are preserved while others are discarded.  
   The percentage retained isn’t hard coded in one single number: instead it’s chosen empirically based on how many input tokens are needed for pre-training runs (the authors experiment with \$40,\,$80,\,$120,\,$160\billion\$ token budgets).   
   Concretely, after sorting by $\textit{s}_{\,{\rm page}}$ you continue taking documents until you hit your desired token horizon—for instance keeping enough files so their cumulative content equals roughly \$40\billion\$ tokens when first tested.
///
/// This sorting mechanism therefore realizes “the top X %” implicitly—the exact percentile depends on how briefly you want your trial corpus.
///
4. **Iterative refinement** – After processing one tranche of CC you discover many math pages were missed because the initial seed was shallow (e.g., none from MathOverflow).   
   You therefore add new positives extracted by manual inspection of domains where over ten percent of sites were already found (“math-related domains”).   
   With these enriched positives you re-train another thin fast-text model and repeat step&nbsp;2–3, which gradually increases recall until successive passes return \>\!\,98\,% duplication rate (“nearly all … had already been collected” in iteration&nbsp;4).



### Why fastText works well here

| Criterion | What FastTable offers | Practical consequence |
|-----------|-----------------------|-----------------------|
| Speed      | Vectorized hashing + dot-product:\($O(\{\# {\rm ngrams}\})$\per page | Millions of queries finished within minutes on commodity hardware |
| Accuracy baseline | Logistic regression trained on clear positives/negatives gives AUC ≈ .9‒1 : very clean class boundary for pure math sites | List has very few false positives so humans focus quickly |
| Scalability | No GPU memory blow-up – entire vocabulary fits into RAM (\rule[-5pt]{5pt}{5pt}\nobreakspace{}256 D×{\nobreakspace{}# unique-ngrames}) | Scoring all \$10^{10}\$ URLs without distributed training |

These properties mean even though Common Crawl enormous, fast-text turns an almost impossible search problem into “rank/keep” operations both honing relevance and faithfully controlling volume.



### Summary

In short:
1\. Train a tiny logistic regression (fast-text) on “math’’ versus “non-math’’ webpages drawn from OpenWebMath versus random junk webpages.\n\
2\. Score every de-duplicated Common-Crawl page once → real-valued score indicating probability of containing math.\n\
3\. Select only high-scoring pages—by imposing an explicit cutoff or stopping once enough tokens met budget—for huge volume reduction (~4× faster than naive crawling).\n\
4\. Repeat adding newly discovered presences so later rounds recover even more missed sites.



Hence “fast-text outputs’’ are per-page confidence scores (larger → more likely medical); these scores are sorted or capped so only most promising fraction survives; inference cost per URL trivial → millions queries easy even at production scale—exactly what DeepSeekMath needs harvesting its \$120\-billion-token maths corpus from CC.\n
