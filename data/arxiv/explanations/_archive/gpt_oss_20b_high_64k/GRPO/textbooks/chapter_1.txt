\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 1: Introduction to Mathematical Reasoning in Language Models

Mathematical reasoning demands that a language model steps beyond surface‐level pattern matching and engages in structured, multi‑step deduction that mirrors human problem solving. Unlike typical language tasks—categorization or translation—mathematics requires manipulating symbols rigorously, ordering operations correctly, and preserving logical dependencies across intermediate steps. Consequently, even large pre‑trained models often flounder when confronted with problems that span algebraic manipulation, integral calculus or intricate combinatorial proofs. This stark gap between natural language proficiency and quantitative rigor has motivated a sequence of research efforts aimed at closing the productivity chasm between generative large language models (LLMs) such as GPT‑4 \citep{gpt4} or Gemini Ultra \citep{gemini} and their free‑to‑access counterparts.

Recent work demonstrates that carefully curated data pipelines can substantially lift open‑source models’ mathematical performance without resorting to external solvers or majority voting schemes.\footnote{DeepSeekMath~7B attains $51.7\%$ on competition‐level MATH via pure generation alone}. Beyond the sheer volume of training examples—$120\,\mathrm{B}$ tokens mined from Common Crawl—architectural innovations such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) further refine policy behavior by leveraging comparative reward signals while mitigating critic‐based computational overhead.\citep{grpo}

The chapter below traces the major milestones that have shaped AI’s engagement with mathematics, introduces core benchmark suites used for evaluation—including both quantitative exercises and geometric proof challenges—and frames essential research questions motivating recent breakthroughs such as DeepSeekMath.\footnote{All benchmarks adhere to standard evaluation protocols defined by ``Self‐Consistency’’ or hard‐truth thresholds rather than fuzzy correctness scores}. Finally, it surveys representative state of the art baselines spanning closed‑source Giants (GPT‑4/Gemini Ultra) and leading open source models (DeepSeekMath~7B, Minerva 540B), highlighting key numerical gaps still uncovered by researchers.\newline 

# Historical progression of AI in mathematics
Mathematics has been a boon for machine learning since an early exposure found symbolic patterns could be exploited through neural networks trained on large corpora of problems scraped from textbooks and forums.\,\nIn its nascent stages most attempts were limited by modest token budgets ($\sim10^8$ tokens) and generic transformer architectures unspecialised for symbolic logic.\nThose early architectures achieved modest zero‑shot pass rates ($<10\%$) even on elementary graded problems.\nA notable leap came when pretraining shifted toward code corpora,\nwhich inherently share structural properties—such as syntax trees—with mathematical expressions.\nDeepSeek‐coder models originally grounded their weights on $850\,\mathrm{B}$ tokens of code paired with broad natural language data,\nand demonstrated that subsequent exposure to high quality mathematical web text could retain coding competence while boosting stepwise problem solving.\nThis trend culminated recently in DeepSeekMath~7B where code pretraining now precedes an expansive $120\,\mathrm{B}$ token math corpus derived primarily from Common Crawl.\nEmpirically this approach yields accuracy jumps comparable in magnitude to “size” increases of three orders:\nA $44\,\mathrm{B}$ token variant pretrained without mathematics achieves roughly $32\%$ on GSM8K,\nbut adding $\sim120\,\mathrm{B}$ well filtered index sentences pushes it above $83\%$.  

# Key quantitative benchmarks (MATH ✔️ GSM8K)
Benchmark suites encapsulate varying degrees of difficulty and domain coverage.\nThe smartphone Simpler challenge set GSM8K contains $\sim8\,000$ grade‐school style problems spanning arithmetic, algebraic identities and straightforward probability.\nIts evaluation metric is strict exact match against official solutions;\nthe test split consists mainly of diverse test inputs devoid of external data patterns therefore gauges pure deductive capability.\nIn contrast MATH offers a substantially larger ($28\,000$?) question set extracted from competitive mathematics sources ranging from AMC/APOS tests to national college competitions.\nEach MATH item can demand up to four deliberate reasoning steps:\na sequence beginning with problem interpretation,\nequation derivation,\nand final numeric reconstruction grounded purely in symbolic manipulation.\nIn practice state‐of‐the‐art closed source ensembles such as GPT‑4 reach roughly $55\%$ exact accuracy;\nas seen by experimental results this sits close behind Gemini Ultra’s $56$\%. 
Open source systems historically trail far behind:\ngenerally achieve near $30–35\%$, but DeepSeekMath~7B breaks the ceiling by reaching $51.7\%$ % using raw generation alone;
self consistency across grid sampling secures an additional boost up to $60\.9\%$.  
These contrasts underscore how maturity needs both sizeable quantity of curated math tokens *and* thoughtful pedagogical instruction derived from chain-of-thought or program-of-thought tutorials.\nThey also highlight that gleaned efficacy shines more dramatically when training pipelines embrace diversity across numerical ranges and multiple formal systems ([equation] format versus natural language proofs).\footnote{\citep{sote}}  

# Geometric–proof challenges as a harder frontier
While arithmetic reasoning largely follows well defined algebraic rules,
geometric proof tasks expose LLMs’ capacity for visual abstraction—so far an unresolved obstacle for generative models.
Compared against GSM8K/MATH quantitative splits,
geometric benchmarks feature DAGs implying transitive evidence such as triangle similarity statements or circle coaxiality conditions.\,'Integrated prompt prompting results reveal particularly low baseline accuracies ($<10\%$) even towards closer profiling datasets such as GeoDifficulty,\nwhich reveals inherent proclivity towards sequential deduction unsupported by robust knowledge graphs often embodied within image-labeled data.\nThese observations align with earlier works citing geometry reasoning difficulty ({trinh2024solving}). 
Consequently contemporary research—including DeepSeekMath’s reinforcement learning—to address geometric inference remains only exploratory;\nthe model often fails simple congruence checks over triangles and ellipses due
to insufficient representation learned during pretraining—a manifestation reflecting axiomatic coverage deficits during data creation phase.~(The paper’s Section 5 notes “the model cannot handle triangles/ellipses”)  
Therefore geometry remains a nexus actively pursued due to its requirement for more than linear knowledge retrieval: spatial facts must be transposed into algebraic forms usable by language tokens before being reassembled—a process essentially faith versus faith more akin than `deep`.  

# Motivating research questions
Based upon gaps identified through large scale studies
the central investigative thrusts crystallize:\n*How does high quality mathematical web content extracted autonomously influence downstream deductive accuracy compared against curated academic sources such as arXiv?\n*To what extent does initial code training change internal inductive biases guiding token positioning during later arithmetic inference?\n*Why do reinforcement algorithms such as GRPO succeed while traditional PPO variants often plateau?\nFurther interrogations focus on hyperparameter schedules (\betas\ directionally influencing KL divergence penalties), group scoring mechanics (\hat A values discounted relative group scores), 
and whether offline versus online sampling confers performance differences\n—an observation corroborated via iterative RL deployed over instruction data subsets resulting in significant gains.*  
These queries surface directly within DeepSeekMath’s experimental design fieldwork detailing reward model iteration counts (\mu=1), group size \(G=64\), KL coefficient $\beta=0\.04$, where success is measured via competition-level benchmark accuracies over unmodified SFT outputs versus optimized RL outputs cultivated under prompt templates preserved across world lines (e.g.“chain-of-thought” vs “program-of-thought”).  

# Short overview of competing state‑of-the-art baselines
The modern LLM landscape distinguishes between closed source composites such as GPT‑4/Gemini Ultra,
which combine gigantic parameter budgets ($>$500 billion)
with proprietary reinforcement pipelines;\nand lighter open source sequelae including:\na 
* DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1\\.5 tuned via $\sim150\,\mathrm B$ tokens capable domain abstraction backed by Arithmetic symbol graphs,\nb\n * Minerva-540B—a PaLM extension trained against extensive arXiv coverage yet attaining $\sim36\,\%$ accuracy on MATH,\nc\n * InternLM2-Math 20 b featuring Math-Shepherd pipeline leveraging evolution-instruct + PPO,\nand {\sc WizardMath-s} fusing PPO onto Llama clusters with specific solver pattern recognition;\nd\n * Llama-2 + MetaMath blessings targeting $\sim70\,b$ weight regimes yet still limited numerically.*  
Relative performance skews illuminate A/B factors beyond sheer size:\nalphabetic coverage derived predominantly English lexicon yields consistent gains on English benchmark pools while leaving Chinese datasets comparatively stagnant unless multilingual corpora are explicitly integrated – a nuance addressed directly by DeepSeekMath’s simultaneous mix-in strategy using Mermaid grammar extraction synonyms across language boundaries.[^1]  

Through this lens one observes a revelatory pattern:\ndetails matters – amassing more tokens improves vanishing-error probability only when those tokens align structurally toward targeted symbolic calculi.
