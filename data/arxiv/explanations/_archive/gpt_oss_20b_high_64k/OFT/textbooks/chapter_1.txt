\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 1: # Introduction & Motivation  

Large‑scale text‑to‑image diffusion models have reached a point where they can synthesize strikingly photorealistic imagery from plain text descriptions alone.  These systems—such as Stable Diffusion or Imagen—are pretrained on billions of image–text pairs and thereby acquire an impressive internal representation of visual semantics coupled with language grounding.  Nevertheless, when an end‑user desires finer control over *which* subject appears in an image or *how* a particular appearance attribute is rendered, plain textual prompts proved insufficiently expressive.  In practice this leads either to images that are only loosely related to the requested content or to a lack of diversity within a fixed stylistic constraint.  

Two application scenarios have crystallized around this basic limitation:

1. **Subject‑driven generation** – often called “identity stylisation” or “few‑shot person re‑generation”, where a model is instructed to produce novel depictions of a specific person (or object) after being exposed only to a handful of images plus a short text prompt describing the desired surrounding context.
2. **Controllable generation** – where an additional perceptual signal (e.g., Canny edges, semantic segmentation maps or facial landmark layouts) is supplied alongside the textual prompt so that synthesis adheres closely to that auxiliary cue while still respecting textual semantics.

Both tasks demand that we harness a pretrained diffusion backbone while imparting new stylised or structural constraints *without* compromising its broad generative capacity—i.e., we want rapid convergence on limited data yet still preserve photorealism and diversity across all types of prompts it was trained on initially.

---

## Why controllability is hard for large diffusion models  

The primary obstacle stems from *the sheer scale and internal structure* of modern diffusion networks.  A pretrained model contains millions (or billions) of weights organised into transformer layers featuring many self‑attention heads and MLPs operating on latent vectors derived from high‑dimensional embeddings produced by vision encoders such as CLIP or VQ‑GAN encoders.  Simply nudging all these parameters via gradient descent—even with extremely small learning rates—drives tiny perturbations that accumulate across layers, potentially altering global semantic representations while simultaneously enabling catastrophic shifts in local feature usage.  

In standard fine‑tuning regimes described in prior works (\textit{DreamBooth}, LoRA), two crucial points emerge:

- **Flexibility vs instability** – Small learning rates are employed precisely because naïvely modifying weights can lead to a loss of useful knowledge learned during pretraining (“pretrained generative ability”).  However, an overly rigid prior confines how well new signals can be represented.
- **Euclidean distance proxy** – The implicit assumption that $\lVert W - W^0\rVert$ being small guarantees qualitative similarity falls short when representations are highly non‑linear and hierarchically organised across layers.

Thus out-of-the-box finetuning often fails early in convergence curves by either underfitting new control signals or collapsing textures and identities—a phenomenon observed experimentally when using LoRA’s low‐rank update on convolution layers within latent diffusion frameworks.[^1]

Beyond parameter instability lies *semantic drift*: higher‑level semantic encoding collapses when many parameter directions change simultaneously because gradients tend to modify both magnitude and orientation simultaneously across numerous neuron vectors inside each layer’s weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$.  Empirical observations point toward *directional invariance* as more critical than sheer magnitude—inspired by phenomena seen in frequency analyses such as Fourier phase preservation—thus calling for learning mechanisms that explicitly preserve pairwise angular relationships among neurons rather than relying solely on Euclidean proximity between weight updates.\[^2]

## Specific downstream task definitions  

### Subject‑driven generation  
Given:
- A set $S=\{\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_k\}$ comprising $k$ input images depicting the target subject,
- A textual prompt $\tau$ describing additional desired context,

the objective is to produce novel images
\[
    f_\theta(S,\tau)\;\rightarrow\;\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C}
\]
where each $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ contains *the same identity* but rendered within conditions defined by $\tau$.  
Key functional requirements:
  - Preserve fine identity details—facial features or object geometry—despite stark differences between training samples $S$ and target prompt contexts.
  - Retain high stochasticity so that multiple diverse samples are possible given $S$ and $\tau$.  

Popular approaches cast this setup as learning fresh tokens tied exclusively to identity features via **parameter adapters** or **reconstruction losses** structurally bound by class‑specific priors[^3].

### Controllable generation  
In this paradigm an inherently *structured* conditioning signal $c$ (edgemap $\mathcal{E}$, segmentation map $\mathcal{M}$, landmark vector $\ell$) accompanies traditional textual description $\tau$.  
Formally:
\[
    f_\theta(c,\tau)\;\rightarrow\;\hat{\mathbf{x}}
\]
where $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ should obey *both* semantic consistency with respect to $\tau$ *and* spatial/structural adherence dictated by $c$.  
The challenge lies in reconciling signals residing at different modalities:
- Text embeddings extracted through CLIP/Japanese XL (an LLM backbone),
- Control maps processed by separate convolution modules whose output forms early residual perturbations inside the diffusion network.

Practitioners adopt two canonical strategies:
1. **Condition injection modules** appended directly before cross–attention layers,
2. **Adaptive prompt tokens** inserted at embedding level.\[^4]

These modifications must be granted enough expressive power yet do not deviate so much as to erode the pretrained prior's competency across arbitrary prompts unrelated to $c$.

## Desired properties of a good finetune method  

The paper identifies two core desiderata emerging from practical experience when introducing domain-specific knowledge into pretrained corpora:

1. **Training efficiency** – Minimising both *(i)* the number \(P_{\text{train}}\) of trainable parameters relative to total model size \(P_{\text{total}}\), and *(ii)* the required number \(E\) of optimisation epochs needed before convergence reached usability thresholds (\(E_{\text{critical}} \ll E_{\text{baseline}}\)).  
   - A solution with fewer learnable parameters reduces overfitting propensity on miniaturised datasets typical in downstream tasks.
   - Accelerated convergence allows iterative experimentation without costly GPU hours; logistics become tractable even for individual researchers deploying locally rather than cloud‐based accelerators.
   - This efficiency metric is validated empirically against baseline schemes like DreamBooth or LoRA where each added parameter rapidly inflates memory usage without commensurate gains in fidelity ([Table X]).  

2. **Generalizability preservation** – Best construed as maintaining both
   - **High-Fidelity Generation** – measured via clipped LPIPS/CLIP metrics against ground truth imagery across diverse prompt families;
   - **Sample Diversity** – assessed through inter‑image distance metrics demonstrating uniform coverage over latent space;
   - The latent representation should remain unaltered enough so subsequent sampling techniques (e.g., classifier-free guidance) still function effectively upon unconstrained runs beyond specialized prompts.\[..] 

Practically this translates into imposing structural constraints upon updates rather than purely penalising raw differences between successive weight tensors:
   - A constraint enforcing negligible change in *hyperspherical energy*, i.e.,
     \[
         \mathrm{HE}(W_\theta)=\sum_{i\neq j}\Vert \hat w_i^\theta-\hat w_j^\theta\Vert^{-1} 
     \]
     where \(w_i^\theta = W_\theta \delta_i\), maintains inter‑neuron cosine similarities identical to those learned during pretraining.
   - Perturbations are required therefore only along rotation/reflection directions inside weight space—an intrinsic operation that respects layer-wise neuron geometry yet offers limited yet powerful degrees-of-freedom compared with full arbitrary shifts.\[^5]  

Additional theoretical diagnostics illustrate how such whitespace-constrained optimisation reduces variance across random initial seeds during early epochs; consequently even simple first-order optimisers converge smoothly irrespective of data complexity.”
