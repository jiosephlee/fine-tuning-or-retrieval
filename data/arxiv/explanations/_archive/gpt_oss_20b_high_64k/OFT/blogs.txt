# Orthogonal Finetuning Explained in Plain Language  

Text‑to‑image diffusion models such as Stable Diffusion are incredibly powerful, but editing a FIXED trained model so it can do a new task—say generate a specific person in arbitrary settings—or respect an external guide signal (Canny edge, mask, or pose) is surprisingly difficult. Changing the model’s weights too heavily can break its original ability while tiny adjustments often fail to bring any new behavior at all.  

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) offers a principled way around this dilemma by **rotating** every neuron inside the model instead of nudging it indiscriminately. Think of each neuron’s weight vector as a point on a high-dimensional sphere: just changing its direction changes what it represents while leaving its length—and hence its overall influence class—unchanged. Keeping every pairwise angle between neurons intact preserves the rich generic knowledge already learned during pre‑training.



## Why Angles Matter more Than Lengths  

Neural networks produce **directions** more than raw magnitudes when deciding what an input looks like after being processed through layers — especially when every neuron is Normalized:
$$
\hat{\mathbf w}_i = \frac{\mathbf w_i}{\|\mathbf w_i\|}\quad,
$$  
the collection $\{\hat{\mathbf w}_i\}$ lives on a unit sphere (the *hypersphere*). Empirical studies have shown that

* **Cosine similarity** between two normalized neurons reliably captures how semantically related they are.
* Replacing magnitudes entirely destroys visual perception, whereas swapping directions almost perfectly keeps semantics intact  

(This intuition is reinforced by a toy exercise where an autoencoder trained normally produced images accurately reconstructed **only** when the decoder used cosine similarity rather than plain dot products.)

When an entire set of neurons is multiplied by an **orthogonal** matrix $R$ ($R^\top R=RR^\top=I$), all pairwise inner products—and thus all angles—are preserved exactly.



## Tweaking the Whole Model With One Rotation per Layer  

Given a pretrained linear layer $W^0 \in \mathbb R^{d\times n}$,

```text
output z = {W^0}ᵀ x,
```

we keep $W^0$ fixed and learn an orthogonal tensor $R \in \mathbb R^{d\times d}$ so that

```text
finetuned W = R · W⁰          →          z = {W}ᵀ x .
```

Because $R$ acts identically on every neuron within that layer,
the **hyperspherical energy**
$$
HE(\mathbf W)=\sum_{i\neq j}\frac  
    {1}{\,\bigl\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\bigr\|\,}
$$
stays exactly unchanged—an invariant shared between any two rotations of the same set of points.


### Steer Without Breaking  
The hoof-footprint craning full gradient updates blindly often jumps far away from this invariant space, leading either to poor fidelity or outright collapse after many steps.  

Learning $R$ satisfies two critical conditions at once:
1️⃣ **No distortion of angular structure** – prevents mixing up semantic blobs learned during pre‑training;
2️⃣ **Minimal Euclidean deviation** – starts exactly from $W^0$ ($R=I$ initially), so early changes are gentle.




## How We Keep $R$ Orthogonal & Small  

Doing direct gradient updates while forcing $RR^\top=\!I$ would require costly projections step-by-step. Instead we parametrize $R$ efficiently via:

$$
R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1},
$$  

where $Q=-Q^\top$. This “Cayley transform” guarantees orthogonality whenever $(I-Q)$ is invertible—and computing $(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ costs roughly one matrix inverse per layer, cheap enough for typical diffusers.



### Making It Even Fewer Parameters  
A dense linear map would demand $\tfrac12 d(d-1)$ trainable entries just for one orthogonal matrix!  
We cut this down dramatically by imposing block‑diagonal structure:

$$
R=\text{diag}(R_1,R_2,\dots,R_r),
$$  

each block $R_k \in O(d/r)$ shares parameters across input channels (or channels typically align naturally). The parameter count drops linearly with $r$, often delivering an order‑of‑magnitude saving compared with naive finite‑rank adapters.



## Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)

Sometimes even small rotations hurt finer semantic nuances caused by overshooting too far along subtle manifolds.  
COFT therefore adds an explicit tolerance bound:
$$ 
\|R-I\|\leq \epsilon ,
$$ 
where $\epsilon$ is tiny compared to typical coordinate magnitudes ($10^{-1}$–$10^{-3}$).  
During training this extra “radius” restriction prevents \(R\) from drifting blindly away from its initial identity configuration—the later gradient steps stay local around pretrained knowledge.



## A Quick Comparison With Other Adapter Methods  

| Method | How it stores extra capacity | Key Idea | Typical Number Params |
|--------|------------------------------|----------|-----------------------|
| LoRA  | Rank‑*r'* updates added ad infinitum | Low‑rank increment \(L S^\top\) | \(r'(d+n)\) |
| OFT  | Single orthogonal matrix + optional diagonal scale | Global rotation `\(RW^0\)` | \(O(d^2/r)\) |
| ControlNet / T₂I‑Adapter  | Extra subnetwork coupled during inference | Condition‑aware extra MLPs | Hundreds-thousands |

*For example*, on a \(128\times128\) fully connected layer LoRA needs ≈ 2056 parameters (with \(r'=8\)), whereas OFT uses only ≈ 960 when employing 8 blocks – both share roughly comparable computational load yet OPF keeps angles fixed.



## What Does It Do In Practice?

### Subject‑Driven Generation  

DreamBooth/Finetuned diffusion proposed adding fresh token plus heavy penalty terms, surviving perhaps hundreds through thousands iterations and sometimes losing identity fidelity early (~200 × loops).  

Using OFT you often reach high-quality subject preservation once you hit ~400 iterations — largely because each rotation cleanly reorients whole feature maps without previous random drift.



***Quantitative sanity check*** – The authors evaluated three measures coined from perceptual embeddings:
* DINO & CLIP‐image similarity → how close generated subject looks;
* CLIP‐text→image cosine → how well narrative prompts comply;
* LPIPS diversity → variety without duplication;

OFT/COFT consistently beat DreamBooth and LoRA across DINO/CLIP‐image scores (“substantial margin”) while matching or slightly surpassing them in prompt fidelity/diversity.



### Controllable Generation  

Given extra inputs—a Canny edge map, segmentation mask or facial landmarks—we need every pixel produced obeys them faithfully without losing photorealism:



#### Easy Flexibility – No Extra Inference Overhead  

Unlike ControlNet you don’t schedule forward passes through auxiliary conditioning heads during sampling—you simply replace each layer’s weight matrix binarily:
```text
final W_l = R_l · W⁰_(l)
```
All post-training computation stays identical step-for-step including GPU speed.



#### Faster Convergence & Better Control  
On landmark-to-face experiments COFT reached comparable landmark error after just eight epochs versus Twenty epochs required even for T₂I‑Adapter / ControlNet OpenAI proposals—a double speedup evident both numerically and visually thanks to gradually aligning pose before shifting textures convincingly.



#### New Metric – Control Consistency  
Because edges/masks do not contain pixel values themselves they can't be compared directly against generated image pixels with traditional PSNR/L∞ metrics. The authors therefore introduced *control consistency*, where one runs an off-the–shelf detector/reconstructor back on the output image then matches results head-to-head against the original guides:
* IoU/F1 for Canny edges,
* accuracy/IoU on segmentation grids,
* Euclidean distance between recovered facial landmarks vs raw ones,

and COFT registered consistent wins over all independent baselines.



## When You Should Pick Orthogonal Finetuning

* You care about preserving most original photorealism even when curating highly specific subjects.
* Your downstream dataset contains only a handful of examples (< 5 sources).
* You want rapid convergence without extensive hyperparameter sweeps ‑ setting LR high works once ε is justified.
* Deployment resources are tight—you cannot afford compute-intensive auxiliary modules at inference time.


*(In practice this means finishing training within ~200–400 steps rather than tens of thousands.)*



## Open Questions Worth Looking At

1️⃣ **Scalable Inversion** – Computing $(I-Q)^{-1}$ per layer scales poorly ($O(d^3)$). Approximate ways such as truncated Neumann series could cut memory/time if proven reliable across deeper transformers common today.

2️⃣ **Compositional Power** – Rotations compose cleanly: $(AB)^⊤(AB)=B^\top A^\top AB=A^\top B^\top B A=A^\top A$. One can therefore perform successive downstream rotations (“enriched itinerary cycles”) without destroying existing knowledge—a promising direction for multi-task fine-graining!

3️⃣ **Parameter Budget Bias** – Current block-diagonal layout reduces overhead massively yet introduces non-uniform priors across channels randomly breaking subtle correlations between early-stage transformer heads originally trained together.


Addressing any one would push OFT’s applicability further into larger ensembles (e.g., Stable Diffusion XL groups).



## Bottom Line

Orthogonal Finetuning takes advantage of geometry rather than ad-hoc tricks:
* It preserves ***angles***—the core semantic glue—in pre-trained diffusion layers.
* It delivers robust control over subjects or conditioning signals *without brittle large learning rates*.
* It does so with fewer trainable bits (`≈ ½ parameter load`) and **no runtime slowdown** compared with classical adapter baselines (*ControlNet*, *LoRA*, …).

If your next project needs “real controllable” image synthesis without sacrificing fidelity—or you wish to fine-tune complex latent diffusion structures quickly—you’ve probably found your new gold standard solution._



# What Is Hyperspherical Energy?  
The New Tool for Ensuring Quality Diffusion Models  

Modern text‑to‑image diffusion models—Stable Diffusion, Imagen, GLIDE—are capable of producing fantastic photorealistic images from a single sentence.  When we try to steer these giant neural nets toward a *downstream* task—creating a new portrait of a specific person from only a handful of photos, or generating an image that follows an edge map—most approaches simply freeze the original weights and add a tiny trainable component or launch a slim‑tail learning rate schedule.  These tricks work roughly “in practice,” but they do *not necessarily keep the pre‑trained artistic intelligence intact*.  

The key idea explained in our recent work **“Controlling Text‑to‑Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning”** is that **the inner geometry of every layer matters**.  A layer’s neurons live on a *unit hypersphere*: each weight vector is normalized before it is applied during diffusion denoising, so what really matters are their directions rather than magnitudes.  The naive “distance‐to‐original weights” loss used everywhere else boils down to aligning magnitudes while completely ignoring how these directions interact with one another—a blind spot when even tiny angular shifts can wreck semantic meaning.



## What Is Hyperspherical Energy?

Consider one linear layer inside the denoiser sub‑network:

\[
\bm W = [\,\bm w_1,\ldots,\bm w_n\,] \in \mathbb R^{d\times n},
\]

where each column $\bm w_i \in \mathbb R^d$ represents one neuron’s weight vector.
After normalizing every vector

\[
\hat{\bm w}_i = \frac{\bm w_i}{\|\bm w_i\|},
\]

we view the set $\{\hat{\bm w}_i\}$ as points on an *unit sphere*.  
**Hyperspherical energy** ($\text{HE}$) is simply the sum over *reciprocal distances* between all pairs:

\[
\boxed{
    \operatorname{HE}(\bm W)\;\coloneqq\;
    \sum_{i\,\neq\,j}\!\bigl\|\,\hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\,\bigr\|^{-1}
   }                           \tag{1}
\]

An intuitive way to understand this definition is that all pairs being close together contribute *large terms*, inflating $\text{HE}$, whereas well–spaced neurons yield a smaller value.  In short—it captures how uniformly your neuron weights ‘fill’ the sphere.



### Why Such an Unusual Measure?

A deep look at prior work shows several facts:

| Observation | Takeaway |
|-------------|----------|
| **Angular features carry most semantics** – SphereNet trained with L2 \(=1.\) achieved state–of–the art classification while keeping unit norms [SphereNet]. | Direction matters far more than raw magnitude when representing images. |
| **Magnitude alone can be factored out** – In our toy autoencoder experiment (referenced in “Why Does Orthogonal Transformation Make Sense?”), replacing inner products with pure cosine similarity preserved almost all image detail. | If rotations preserve angles you preserve semantics —⇒ context‑sensitive generation stays intact. |
| **Uniform neuron spread correlates with robust generalisation** [Liu et al., 2018] | Roughly speaking, your network has learned everything it needs without redundant neurons densely clustered together.*

Thus keeping $\text{HE}$ unchanged across fine‑tune steps is precisely what we want: **the relative angular layout stays intact**, ensuring latent knowledge—language understanding, visual priors—remains unscathed.



## Why Does Maintaining It Matter?

When we ask “how different are my fine‑tuned weights from the original ones?”, many people simply compute Euclidean distance $\|\mathbf M-\mathbf M^0\|$.  
But this measure mixes magnitude and direction arbitrarily:

- It says *any change at all counts*, even shuffling two identical directions slightly.
- It penalises magnitudes even though magnitudes are essentially discarded during denoising when activations are re‑scaled back onto the sphere.
- Crucially, changing direction—the sine wave analogue—can flip keywords altogether even if Euclidean distance is small!

In contrast, preserving $\text{HE}$ guarantees you are moving each neuron along some rotation/reflection without distorting mutual angles:
if you rotate your entire set by an orthogonal matrix \(R^\top R=\mathbb I,\)
then every pairwise dot product—and thus every pairwise spherical distance—is conserved:

```
   W            =  W⁰            →   ℋ( W )   = ℋ( W⁰ )
   hat->         hat->                 (angles preserved)
```

That invariance turns into an *exact guarantee*:
as long as your finetuned parameters obey an orthogonal relationship,
its hyperspherical energy remains precisely equal to that of its pre‑trained counterpart.



## Orthogonal Finetuning – A Proven Strategy

**Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** learns one shared orthogonal matrix per layer \(R_\ell\):

```text
finetuned weight :   W_ℓ   = R_ℓ · W⁰_ℓ .
```

With identity initialization (\(R_\ell=\mathbb I.\)) we start at zero change; gradient updates move \(R_\ell\) along a manifold that strictly keeps all inter‑neuron angles fixed because:

- Multiplication by an orthogonal matrix rotates everything equally.
- Angles are invariant under such rotations → Equation (1) remains ≡ constant.
- The model view changes only at *feed‑forward odds*, not due to value drift inside layers.



### Parameter Efficiency

A naïve fully​-connected \(d\times d\,R_\ell\) would need \(d^2/2\) parameters—a lot compared with full rank LoRA updates!  
We address this with **block‐diagonal Cayley parametrisation**:

```text
ℜ     ≔ diag(ℜ₁,…,ℜ_r),   ℜᵢ∈ O(d/r).
```

The free parameter count shrinks roughly by factor \(r².\)  
Typical values reduce ranks from thousands down into hundreds—all while maintaining full orthogonality.



### Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)

Pure rotations can drift away from the original weights too far; introducing an extra bound on how far \(R_\ell\) leaves identity refines this:

```math
‖R_ℓ - I‖₂ ≤ ε .
```

With Cayley’s transformation \(R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\), this becomes directly a simple bound on skew–symmetric \(Q.\)
Projecting gradients onto this ball keeps fine‑tune movements subtle—effectively anchoring model abilities close enough for safety yet flexible enough for new roles.



## Real‐World Impact – From Theory To Practice

The method has two flagship experimental settings:

### 1️⃣ Subject‑Driven Generation  

Hybridising DreamBooth’s loss framework with OFT yields:
- One order–of–magnitude faster convergence (< 400 iterations vs > 2000).
- Higher semantic fidelity — both DINO and CLIP retained viewpoint metrics climb noticeably.
- Better prompt compliance — new prompts evoke textures correctly while staying true to user identity.

> **Key takeaway:** By preserving neuron angle layout you keep “personality” locked while letting few examples steer pose/style.



### 2️⃣ Controllable Generation  

Task setups included Canny edge → image / segmentation map → image / landmark → face.
Compared against ControlNet & T2I‑Adapter:
- All three converge within only ~8 epochs instead of > 20.
- Control consistency scores tip upward dramatically per our bespoke IoU & landmark error tests.
- No extra inference layers => latency unchanged versus vanilla Stable Diffusion.


In both realms OFT/COFT proves **faster learning**, **greater stability**, and **preserves existing artistry**, qualifying them as upgrade paths rather than replacements.



## How Does It Relate To Other Methods?

| Approach | Scaling Logic | Perspective |
|----------|--------------|-------------|
| **DreamBooth** | Small learning rate + class–prior loss | Update weights directly |
| **LoRA** | Add low‐rank matrix Δ=ABᵀ (`rank=r’`) | Adds capacity around pretrained center |
| **ControlNet** | Separate auxiliary conditioner subnetwork | A heavyweight plug-in |
| **OFT** | Multiply pretrained weight by an *orthogonal* transformer `R` | Rotates representation without altering internal geometry |

Notice how each method pushes against a different allocation horizon:
visibility assumption of small Δ vs manifold constraint of rotations versus double branching logic.
OFT sits somewhere “in between”: full flexibility via rotation yet bounded deviation ensures we do not rewrite semantics wholesale.



## Takeaways & Future Directions

1. **Axial geometry matters:** Angles encode high‑level concepts; keeping those intact guarantees continuity between pretraining knowledge & downstream adaptation.
2. **Orthogonality gives constancy:** Rotations are mathematically guaranteed to preserve pairwise similarities (hyperspherical energy).
3. **Practice pays back:** Faster convergence means far less compute per new brand or style change—a tangible win for commercial deployment.
4. **Weight overrides cost nothing:** After training finishes you can prepend `W=RW⁰` once offline and resume inference normally—no extra FP32 multiplies during sampling.
5. **Open horizon:** Improving efficient inverse calculations for larger gradients, exploring dynamic block sizes across self-attention heads,
and leveraging compositional chaining—several promising research threads grew directly out of these insights.



### Bottom Line

Hyperspherical energy rarely appears on introductory pipelines—but once you recognise its importance it becomes your reliability compass when nudging large diffusion models onto niche tasks.  
If you build your finetune around keeping this gray geometric invariant intact—as shown by Orthogonal Finetuning—you’ll see consistent request cycles hit quality targets sooner—and most importantly—it keeps your model’s artful heart beating strong behind any new prompt or control surface you dare compose next.



# Retaining Your Dog in Every Generated Picture:
## Subject‑Driven Generation with Orthogonal Finetuning

Creating a photorealistic photo‑like portrait of your dog in any setting you like—be it a sunny beach, a snowy mountain, or even floating in space—is a fun image‑generation task that has become surprisingly realistic thanks to diffusion models such as Stable Diffusion. However, when you want **you** (or “your dog”) to stay the same across all those new images while only changing the surroundings or description, naïve finetuning often ends up swapping identities or losing fine facial details.  

Enter **Orthogonal Finetuning** (OFT), a principled way to tweak large diffusion models without erasing their pre‑trained knowledge. This post walks through the problem, why it matters, how OFT works at an intuitive level, proof‑that‑its ideas are solid from theory and practice—and finally some quick tips if you want to run your own “dog‑in‑every‑scene” experiments next time you fire up your GPU.



---

## What is “Subject‑Driven Generation”?

In a standard text‑to‑image setup you give a prompt such as **“a golden retriever playing on a beach”** and let the model do its magic. In **subject‑driven generation**, you pre‑define *one* subject—say your dog—through a handful of images (typically 3–5). Then you ask the model to generate *new* scenes that still feature *exactly* that dog everywhere:

```
Prompt A    →  golden retriever strolling on a sunny beach
Prompt B    →  golden retriever leaning against an old oak tree at sunset
Prompt C    →  golden retriever perched on a skyscraper billboard at night
```

The trick is *preserving identity* during the adaptation step so that every generated image keeps subtle details such as eye shape or fur pattern while respecting new context and language cues.



---

## Why Traditional Finetuning Struggles

Most finetune methods tweak network weights by simply adding small updates (DreamBooth) or applying low‑rank corrections (LoRA). These approaches implicitly try to stay close – by keeping Euclidean distance between old and new weights small – but that metric alone does **not** guarantee semantic consistency:

| Method | Training tactic | Typical issue |
|--------|-----------------|--------------|
| DreamBooth | Naïve SGD on selected layers | Can drift too far after many epochs → loss of identity |
| LoRA | Low‑rank additive update | Often loses subject details early; may overfit noise |

The experiments in the original paper show that after around \(400\) optimisation steps DreamBooth retains some information but still ends up altering important facial features; at \(2000\) steps it starts collapsing into mode‑locked superfluous textures (“yellowish fur” instead of “yellow shirt”). LoRA sometimes converges quickly but never stays faithful – when it does it produces bland generic dogs.



---

## The Core Idea Behind Orthogonal Finetuning

### Angles matter more than magnitudes

The authors run a simple convolutional autoencoder toy experiment where they test three ways of building feature maps during inference:
1. **Inner product** – what was used during training  
2. **Magnitude only** – ignoring direction  
3. **Angle only** – just using cosine similarity  

They discover that angular information alone is enough to reconstruct nearly pristine images while magnitudes contribute almost nothing meaningful (“the magnitude contains no useful information”). This tells us that rotating neuron vectors changes semantics far more than scaling them does.



### Hyperspherical Energy – A Measure of Neuron Geometry

For a weight matrix 
\[
\mathbf{W} = [\,\mathbf{w}_1,\dots,\mathbf{w}_n\,]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
\]
let every neuron be normalised:
\[
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\lVert \mathbf{w}_i\rVert}.
\]
The *hyperspherical energy* aggregates inverse distances on the unit sphere:
\[
HE(\mathbf{W}) = 
   \sum_{i\neq j}\!\! \bigl\lVert \hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\bigr\rVert^{-1}.
\]
If two weight matrices differ only by an orthogonal transform \( \mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=I\), i.e.
\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0},
\]
then all pairwise angles are identical so \(HE(\mathbf{W})=HE(\mathbf{W}^{0})\).  
This invariant property gives us a clean target for finetuning:

**OFT learns exactly such an orthogonal matrix per layer** so the *pairwise relationships* among neurons stay untouched.



### How Orthogonal Finetuning Looks In Practice

Let \( \mathbf{W}^{0} \) be a pretrained weight matrix for some fully–connected layer (the same applies to attention matrices inside Transformers). In ordinary SGD we would minimise
\( \lVert \!\!\lVert \!\!\lVert \boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\rVert\rVert^2.\)

With OFT we impose:
\[
   \boxed{
      \begin{aligned}
          &(\textbf{k}) &&   z = (\mathbf R\,\!\!{\color{#00f}\{{ W ^ {0 }} }
                    )^{ \! T } x ,\\[4pt]
          &(\textbf{k}) &&   \underbrace{\!{\color {#4d90ff}{ R } ^ { T } 
              R}{= I } }_{\textit{\(O(N)\text{-style orthogonality}}\!}
      \end{aligned}}
   }
\]
where
* \( x\) is the input activation,
* \( z\) is the output activation,
* \( R_{\tiny N\times N}\) is an orthogonal matrix shared across all neurons within this layer.

The optimisation objective is simply minimising loss on your downstream task’s data set *while keeping \( R^\top R = I.\)* Because an orthogonal transform never rescales magnitude descriptors collectively (they fold into each other), **the hyperspherical energy stays exactly as it was before any changes!**

To keep training fast, they use an efficient Cayley parametrisation:
\[
      R = (\mathbb I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}, 
      ~~ Q^\top = -Q ,
\]
which guarantees orthogonality as soon as you differentiate w.r.t \(Q.\)



### Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)

While preserving angles keeps semantic structure intact, we still want some *rigidity* so that our new directions don't drift too far from their original ones—which can happen if we allow huge rotations at once. COFT introduces an extra bound:

>  
> \( {\color { #ff5599}{ }\|\!R-\mathbb I}\!|_{\ell_{2}\le\norm{{stuff}}{\epsilon}}\)

Intuitively this means we limit our rotation matrix’s “angular distance” from identity—think of it as cutting off continental drifting after just one long leap versus letting lands spin freely for months.

## Quick Implementation Guide

Below is sketchy Python-esque code captured from stable-diffusion reposterial notes—feel free to copy & paste inside your trainer wrapper.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
import math

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# Helper : Cayley parametrisation build
def cayley_matrix(Q):
    return torch.linalg.inv(torch.eye(Q.size(0)) - Q) @ (torch.eye(Q.size(0)) + Q)

# Neighboring function used inside forward pass
def centred_blockdiag(ranks):
    """Create block diag of ranks-sized SO(n)."""
    blk = []
    d = ranks[0]           # assume square blocks first
    n_blocks = len(ranks)
    for i in range(n_blocks):
        # initialize each sub-matrix as Identity
        blk.append(torch.eye(d))
    return torch.block_diag(*blk)

# ------------------------------------------------------------
class OrthOrthRepo(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, W):
        super().__init__()
        self.register_buffer("W_pretrain", W.clone())
        # pick block size r (#blocks)
        r = 8                               # hyperparameter-- can tune
        self.r = r
                          # Q shape [:r,:r] diagonally repeated below!
        self.Q_blk = torch.nn.Parameter( torch.zeros(self.r,self.r))
        
        # cyclically repeat for each rank bucket if desired --
        # simplest case r == rank dimension above means full densen
    
    def forward(self,x):
        R_blk = cayley_matrix(self.Q_blk)
        # if block diags needed:
        W_exc = R_blk @ self.W_pretrain            # shape d x n
        return torch.matmul(x,w.rate(-1))
```

After training for few dozen epochs (`optim.SGD` step size ~1e-4), freeze `Q` into constant `R`. Replace existing weight tensor by `R @ W_pretrain`. File achieves zero additional forward cost compared against vanilla diffusion 🌟

## Open Questions & Future Directions

The authors already list several interesting lines left unexplored:

1. **Fast invertibility** – Cayley transform requires computing $(I-Q)^{-1}$ each forward pass; although cheap for small blocks it scales poorly beyond academia fanship numbers.
2. **Composable Caretaking** – OG usage could stack multiple OFTs developed for different subjects/tasks because multiplying two orthogonals remains orthogonal; does ensemble transfer preserve all identities simultaneously?
3. **Beyond Block Diagonal** – Incorporating richer sparsity patterns while strict maintaining $O(d^2/r)$ complexity might push capacity even further without sacrificing generalization.

# From Sketches to Stunning Images – How OFT Enables Powerful Controllable Generation  

Controlling a diffusion‑based image generator has earned its place at the core of modern creative workflows—from turning a rough sketch into a photorealistic portrait to aligning a desired scene layout with a complex semantic prompt. Yet most large text‑to‑image models tend to ignore hand‑drawn outlines or loose segmentations altogether—their learned prior simply prefers plausible layouts generated solely from language cues.  

In this post we unravel **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**—a lightweight finetuning scheme that teaches a pretrained diffusion net how to honour user‑supplied control signals *without* sacrificing its original generative skill set or inference speed.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why conventional finetuners stumble  

| Approach | Idea | Limitation |
|----------|------|------------|
| **Parameter‑free conditioning** | Directly feed an extra tensor alongside language embeddings | The controller has little opportunity to retrain its own weights and hence offers weak guidance |
| **Adapters / Low‑rank updates (LoRA)** | Inject small rank‑\(r'\) matrices into attention layers | These updates shift internal linear transformations drastically; stability suffers when large amounts of control data are added |
| **ControlNet / T<sup>2</sup>I‑Adapter** | Append an entire backbone split off from the main diffusion net | While powerful, their added modules explode both memory usage (~10× model size) and inference time |

Even experts into diffusion conditioning feel trapped by two rules nobody wants broken:
1. *The learned joint image‑text prior should stay intact.*  
2. *Fine‑grained shapes described by Canny edges, binary segment maps or facial landmarks must actually emerge.*

A promising path offered by OFT tackles both constraints simultaneously.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Core Idea – Rotate neurons while staying aloft  

Bottom line:  
> **Keep every neuron’s *direction* fixed relative to its peers; move it *orthogonally* instead of scaling it arbitrarily**  

To explain what this means precisely let us look at one fully connected layer of size \(d\times n\):
*Each column \(w_i\in\mathbb R^{d}\) is a neuron.*  
Let us normalize every weight vector onto the unit hypersphere:
\[
\hat w_i=\frac{w_i}{\lVert w_i\rVert}.
\]

The **hyperspherical energy** measures how spread these directions are:
\[
E(W)=\sum_{i<j}\lVert \hat w_i-\hat w_j\rVert^{-1}.
\]
If two networks share identical angles between corresponding neurons then  
\(E(W)=E(W^0)\), even though their weight magnitudes could differ wildly.



### Orthogonality guarantees unchanged pairwise angles  
An orthogonal matrix \(R\in{\rm O}(d)\) satisfies \(R^{\!\top}R=RR^{\!\top}=I.\)
Multiplying every column by the same \(R\) rotates all vectors together,
hence \(E(R\,W^0)=E(W^0).\)

Mathematically the forward pass becomes  
\[z=(W^{0})^\top x \quad{\longrightarrow}\quad z=(R\,W^0)^\top x.\]

Learning only \(R,\!Z,\!S,\dotsc,\!B,\dotsc,\sigma(\dotsc)\)
seeks flights away from old orientations *without* disturbing pairwise relations—
which preserves high‑level semantics while enabling memorization required for downstream tasks.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## A practical recipe  

### 1️⃣ Block‐diagonal Cayley parametrization  

Forming an exact orthogonal matrix via QR is cheap if dimensions are tiny,
but \(d\simeq512–1024\) makes QR sluggish.
***Cayley’s trick* turns any skew‐symmetric matrix \(Q=-Q^\top\) into an exact rotation*
\[ R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}. \]
Its determinant is always +1 (no reflection), perfectly fine empirically.*

We further restrict each layer’s rotation matrix,
\(R=\mathrm{diag}(R_1,\ldots,R_r)\),
reducing parameters from \(O(d^2)\) down to roughly \(O(d^2/r)\).
With large \(r\,(\geq8)\) most layers stay numerically stable,
yet every group learns its own shared “dance” over its subspace.



### 2️⃣ Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)

Pure rotation leaves total freedom—given enough data you keep spinning until bias creeps back in.
Adding an explicit closeness penalty caps deviations:
\[ \lVert R-I\rVert_{{F}}\leqslant \epsilon.\]
Using Neumann series expansion,
\( R\simeq I+2Q + O(Q^2)\),
the same bound translates into simply keeping ‖Q‖₍F₎ small—a straight plug–in projected gradient step.

# Faster Phenomenon—OFT Trains Quicker and Stabler Than LoRA or ControlNet  

---

## Why fine‑tuning a diffusion model usually feels like a “kill‑the‑balance” problem  

Text‑to‑image diffusion engines have become the go‑to generator for photorealistic images guided only by words. Yet when you want *something specific*—say a particular character in a new scene—or you want the image to follow a Canny sketch or a segmentation map, merely adding training data rarely suffices.  

Existing work tackles this in two ways:

| Method | How it changes the base model | Typical downside |
|--------|------------------------------|------------------|
| **DreamBooth** | Afew‑shot L‑earning of all weights but a tiny learning rate | Very slow convergence — tens of thousands of gradient steps needed |
| **LoRA** | Adds low‑rank addendices (`U·V⁺`) on top of each weight matrix | Adds a separate network head at inference time & still requires careful tuning |
| **ControlNet** | Injects an entire auxiliary Streamer network conditioned on extra signals | Extra inference FLOPs; extra memory footprint |

All three approaches implicitly assume that staying *close* to the pre‑trained weights – measured by Euclidean distance ‑ guarantees that image style(semantic) stays intact. Unfortunately “close” here is defined only loosely because neural networks are highly non‑convex – minor changes can erase learned knowledge entirely.


---

## The key insight from geometry  

If you normalise every neuron vector \( \mathbf w_i = \frac{\mathbf w_i}{\|\mathbf w_i\|}\), you see them sitting on a unit hypersphere \(S^{d-1}\).  
The *pairwise* angular relationship between neurons,
\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W)=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\|^{-1},
\]
is called **hyperspherical energy** – it quantifies how uniformly distributed neuron directions are on that sphere[^1]. A model trained on a large corpus develops a characteristic pattern of such energies whose structure encodes semantics rather than raw magnitudes.

A remarkable property emerges next::
> **Any common orthogonal transformation \( \mathbf R (\mathbf R^\top\!\mathbf R=\mathbf I)\) leaves every dot product—hence every angle—to stay exactly the same**.  
> Consequently \( \operatorname{HE}(\mathbf R\!\cdot\!\mathbf W)=\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W)\).

Thus if it were possible instead of moving arbitrarily through parameter space we could *rotate* every layer’s neurons preserving their inner–products – semantics would stay preserved almost automatically.


---

## Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

### Core idea

For each layer’s weight matrix \( \mathbf W^0\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}\),
the finetuned weights are simply
\[
\label{eq:z}
\boxed{
    \mathbf z = (\mathbf R\,\!\cdot\,\,\! \mathbf W^0)^\top\,\! \boldsymbol x,\quad 
    \text{s.t.\ }\mathbf R^\top\!\,\mathbf R = \mathbf I },
}\]
where \(d=n=\) number of neurons per dimension sometimes differs but conceptually identical.

Because every weight change is just an orthogonally rotated version of its pre‑trained counterpart,
hyperspherical energy never changes — proving mathematically that **semantic structure survives** while still letting us impart new capabilities.


### Parameter efficiency

A fully‑connected matrix would normally require learning \(d\times d = d^{2}\) parameters if we let \( \mathbf R \) be dense—but we can impose a *block–diagonal* structure:
`\[
    \displaystyle 
    \mathbf R =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \!R_1&&\\[1mm]
        &&\,R_2\\[-1mm]
        &&&&\,R_r
    \end{bmatrix},
    ~~~
    R_k\in O(d/r).
`
This reduces free parameters to roughly  
\[ |\mathcal P_{\text{OFT}}|\;\approx\;\frac12\,d^{2}/r,
   ~~~~~~~|\mathcal P_{\text{LoRA}}|\;\approx r' (d+n).\]  
For instance, with a \(128\times128\) layer one obtains **960 trainable values** versus **2048** for an analogous LoRA rank‑8 update – *almost half the parameter budget.*

### Enforcing strict similarity – CONSTRAINED OFT (COFT)

While OFT already keeps angles intact, it could still drift far from the starting point if allowed unbounded rotations:
COFT adds an explicit radius constraint,
\[   {\lVert}\,\boldsymbol R-\!\boldsymbol I\,{\rVert}\;\leqslant\,{\epsilon},                          ]
easily rephrased via the Cayley transform so that optimisation proceeds through projected gradients on an unconstrained skew–symmetric matrix.
The extra bound guarantees **stability** at the cost of negligible hyper‑parameter tuning – you simply pick a modest epsilon instead of hunting for learning rates.

## Practical takeaways 

 * Add a single `torch.nn.Linear` enrichment stage when training — each layer (~512×…×512 size nets have just ~30 booleans off coherence?). Keep your training loops identical otherwise.
 * Pick epsilon small enough (`1e−3`) if you notice slippage early on; larger values will recover some power akin to FastRuntime while still staying under control.
 * No kernel patch required at inference time! 🎉 After training you multiply `R` onto your base weight matrix once offline then hand off this fused weight into your base SD pipeline—the total runtime stays unchanged.
 * Architecture agnostic: works unmodified on FFNs inside self-attention heads as well as convolution kernels (where block size equals input channel count).
 * For extreme compression consider hybrid `r` vs `r'`: half batteries then unify across reconstructive tasks without cross-symmetry anomalies.

## Closing remarks ─ Beyond Ours

Orthogonal perturbations offer undeniable benefits:

1️⃣ They keep neuron directions ***exactly unchanged*** unless purposely rotated → maintain learned frequency patterns intimately matched with guidance models such as CLIP embeddings guiding diffusion sampling pathways.

2️⃣ When multiple tasks require different “rotations,” their solution matrices combine via multiplication – still staying on ℕₑ² perfect group property – enabling simple sequential composition (“add person X then set lighting Y do it instantly”).

3️⃣ Counting tunable weights remains linear in effective rank `r`. Even full random seeds steal no fresh memory aside from module’s normal weight storage ― making it an attractive recipe for multi-task fine-tunings under strict resource budgets.



The open questions remain subtle yet compelling:

* How would mixed block shapes adapt architectures such as depth-wise attention modules?
* Is there an efficient way beyond Cayley transform saving GPU memory while keeping true orthogonality?
* Can we quantify how *exactly* preserving angles correlates with downstream metrics such as CLIP-$I$, DINO recall scores?

Our hope is that these curious puzzles spark follow-up research fostering even faster ‘designer cyclically proxy’ pipelines built on next-gen diffusion advertisements.

# Beyond The Paper – What’s Next for Orthogonal Finetuning?

After introducing *Orthogonal Finetuning* (OFT) as a principled way to preserve hyperspherical energy while adapting diffusion‑based image generators, the community is left with several concrete challenges.  
Below we dig into three key directions that naturally progress from the work and outline what needs to happen before OFT can become a turnkey solution for every downstream task.

---

## 1. Speeding Up the Cayley Transform

**What happens in OFT?**  
The core of OFT is learning an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) per layer:

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{W}^{\!\circ})^\top \!\mathbf{x}, \qquad 
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\]

where \(\mathbf{Q}\) is skew–symmetric.  
The under‑lying *Cayley transform* gives us an implicit way of enforcing \(\mathbf{R}^\!\top \! \mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\). While elegant mathematically, computing \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) at every iteration incurs a tangible overhead – especially for large latent‑space dimensions (\(d=512\), \(d^2/2\simeq 1\times10^5\) parameters).  

### Why this matters
- **Scalability** – on a 2048‑dimensional model the inverse costs 8× more ops.
- **Memory footprint** – a dense inverse requires extra GPU buffers.
- **Training time** – longer per‑epoch times make hyper‑parameter sweeps impractical.

### Hands‑on solutions that deserve exploration

| Approach | Strengths | Trade‑offs |
|--------|-----------|------------|
| **Truncated Neumann series** <br>\(\displaystyle \mathbf{R}\approx \mathbf{I}+2\!\mathbf{Q}+4\!\mathbf{Q}^2+\dots+\!k\bigl(2\bigr)^k \!\! \mathbf{Q}^k\) | Fast, no inversion; good if $\|\mathbf{Q}\|<1$ | Accuracy limited by order \(k\); sometimes diverges if $\|\mathbf{Q}\|\!\geq\,1$ |
| **QR decomposition** <br>\(\bigl[\hat{\mathbf Q},\,e\,]\!=\!{\rm QR}(\tilde{\boldsymbol W})\) | Exact orthogonality at every step | Adds two extra loops (QR + recombination); precision unstable when matrices are ill‑conditioned |
| **Householder reflections / Givens rotations**<br>Constructing \(r_i = \exp({{\rm skew}}_i)\) | Low‑dimensional updates when \(r_i\)'s are sparse | Implementation complexity rises with number of layers |
| **Learned “soft” orthogonality** <br>Penalty term \(|| \mathbf R^\top \! \rright - {\bf I}||_F^2\) | No inversion; flexible schedule | Requires careful tuning of penalty weight |

A pragmatic hybrid strategy would starts training with a low‑order Neumann rollout (fast!) and gradually transitions to exact QR or Householder steps once training stabilises. Library support from modern autograd engines (Broadcasting on NVIDIA AMP or Intel MKL’s `LAPACK` wrappers) will also alleviate inversion costs.

---

## 2. Composing Multiple Orthogonal Matrices Across Tasks

A compelling claim in the original work is that *the product of two learned orthogonal matrices remains orthogonal*. This opens up an entire toolbox:

```text
R_taskA = learn_from_A()        # optimised for task A
R_taskB = learn_from_B()        # optimised for task B

R_combined = R_taskA @ R_taskB   # still in O(d)
```

### Why this is exciting

- **Model multiplexing** – We could drop one set of weights at inference and apply `R_taskB` after `R_taskA` without retraining.
- **Zero‑shot transfer** – Suppose we’re only fine–tuned on “dog” images but someone asks for “cat” images too. A simple concatenation could produce plausible cat generations.
- **Compositional specification** – Combining (`photo realistic` @ `Canny control`) yields an image obeying both constraints simultaneously without re‑optimising from scratch.

### Practicalities & pitfalls

| Issue | Implications | Prospective workaround |
|-------|--------------|------------------------|
| **Non‑commutativity**<br>\( R_A R_B \neq R_B R_A \) | Order matters; fails if tasks intervene unexpectedly | Record both orders as separate checkpoints |
| **Accumulated numerical drift** | Multiplying many matrices can lead to rounding errors that slowly break exactness | Periodic reorthonormalisation via polar decomposition |
| **Expression capacity vs interference**<br>Two tasks may attack different semantics individually but misbehave when combined | Overlaps in latent subspaces produce artefacts | Multi‑task loss that monitors “task loss” postcompose then re‑fine tune |

A concrete next step would be benchmarking *serial composition* against *joint training*. If serial composition can match joint performance while preserving inferencing overhead, it could become the default workflow in production pipelines where new user requests arrive incrementally.

---

## 3. Steering Clear of Block‑Diagonal Biases

The paper’s *efficient* parametrisation leverages block–diagonal orthogonals:

```text
R = diag(R1 , … , Rr)
```

This cuts down parameter count from \(O(d^2)\) to \(O(d^2/r)\), but at a cost:

- **Channel isolation** – Each block only rotates a set of neurons (or feature maps). If semantic axes cross these blocks loose couplings get suppressed.
- **Limited expressiveness** – The model cannot learn joint rotations across blocks without incurring extra parameters or losing strict cheapness.
- **Bias towards depthwise correspondence** – When every block matches one channel (as done for convolutions), we essentially perform depthwise linear layers which may not capture global context shifts needed for some controls.

### How far do we need to go?

| Idea | Methodology | Expected Benefit |
|------|-------------|------------------|
| **Shared shortcuts across blocks**<br>E.g., learn one global rotation `G` appended after sub-blocks |\( R_{\text{shared}} = G\,diag(R_1,\dots,R_r)\)| Captures coarse global transformations without many parameters |
| **Low–rank coupling matrix**<br>Attach a rank‑\(k\) outer product between blocks |\( diag(R_1,\dots,R_r)+Z₁Z₂^\top|\)| Adds minimal degrees of freedom for cross‐block coordination |
| **Randomised block splitting**<br>Use random assignments for each fine-tune run and average results |\(\mathbb E[\,Diag(\cdot)\,]\)| Mitigates any particular splitting bias |

An empirical roadmap would start with those two tweaks—one adds a tiny “global spine”, the other PLL-like coupling—and measuring changes in *hyperspherical energy* and *DINO/CLIP fidelity* relative to vanilla block–diagonal OFT. If strong gains appear with negligible parameter growth (say < 3 % more trainable weights), this becomes a go‑to patch for any deployment looking for both speed and quality.

## Bottom Line – An Engineering Checklist

| Question | What To Do | Why It Matters |
|-|-|-|
| Is my training fast enough? | Adopt truncated Neumann approximation until convergence region, then flip back to QR or Householder updates | Reduces per–epoch wall clock time by ~30% on typical GPUs |
| Will my downstream tasks compose nicely? | Store learned `R`s per task and test multiplicative combinations before deployment | Enables modular fine‑tuning pipeline without hot restarts |
| Am I hurting performance with over‐constrained blocks? | Fine–tune shared or low‐rank couplings; roll back if no improvement on CLIP metrics | Prevents structural bias that limits semantic shift capacity |
