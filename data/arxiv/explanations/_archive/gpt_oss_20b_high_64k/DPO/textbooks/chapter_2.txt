\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 2: **2 | Background – From Unsupervised LMs to Preference Models**

Language models (LMs) have become ubiquitous components of modern artificial intelligence applications because they succinctly encode vast amounts of world knowledge and diverse linguistic competencies when trained purely in an unsupervised fashion on widely‑available text corpora [Brown et al., 2020]. Nonetheless, an LM trained solely to predict the next token may generate content that violates user intent or safety guidelines since its objective does not explicitly penalise undesirable behaviour [Chen et al., 2023]. The remainder of this chapter traces how practitioners move from such raw unsupervised checkpoints toward an aligned system that respects human preferences, detailing three critical junctures along the way:
transformer‑based language modelling, supervised fine‑tuning driven by prompt‑completion pairs,
and finally preference learning based on pairwise human judgments encoded through the Bradley–Terry model.



---

### # Transformer LMs pre‑trained on masked/unmasked objectives

Unsupervised language modelling begins by training an autoregressive transformer decoder network $\pi_{\phi}(y\,|\,x)$,
where $y=(y_{1},\dotsc ,y_{L})$ denotes a full sequence and $x=(x_{0},\dotsc ,x_{t})$ is its prefix.
The network maximises the product of conditional probabilities
$\displaystyle \prod_{t=0}^{L-1}\pi_{\phi}\!\bigl(y_{t+1}\,\big|\,x_{0:t}\bigr)$
under either a causal next‑token objective (“unmasked”) [Radford et al., 2019] or an autoregressive masked‑token prediction task (“masked”).  
In both cases the self‑attention layers learn contextual embeddings that capture syntax,
semantic relations, commonsense reasoning cues, and long‑range document structure simply because doing well requires predicting unseen symbols given their surroundings.\footnote{
Large pretrained checkpoints—PaLM [Chowdhery et al., 2022], Gemini/Bison [Bubeck et al., 2023], LLama Series [Touvron et al., 2023]—have demonstrated remarkable zero‑shot capabilities across many benchmarks due solely to the breadth of their data exposure.
}
These models are subsequently referred to as *unsupervised* checkpoints owing to their reliance solely on predictive next-token supervision.



---

### # Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT): illustration via prompting

Because different end tasks demand particular behavioural conventions—
e.g., polite answering manners in dialogue or concise wording in summaries—
mere predictive accuracy is insufficient.
Hence practitioners generally perform **Supervised Fine‑Tuning** (SFT) atop the pretrained backbone.
In SFT one collects instruction–completion pairs $\{(\mathbf{x}^{(i)},\,\mathbf{y}^{(i)})\}$ drawn from a curated dataset
(e.g., OpenAI’s *humorous assistant* dialogues or Reddit TLDR summaries).
A typical prompt template looks like:\n\n```\nInstruction:\n<task description>\nResponse:\n```  
The model generates $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ given $\mathbf{x}$,
and its parameters are updated so that $\log \pi_{\theta}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\mid\mathbf{x})$
is maximised pointwise across known human demonstrations.
After convergence one obtains a *base policy* $\pi_{\textsf{SFT}}$,   
which serves both during inference and as a starting point for subsequent training stages.



---

### # Human labeling schemes – pairwise preferences

Even after SFT,
the resulting policy typically lacks tight control over stylistic minimisation
or safety aspects which are largely determined ‘in situ’ by nuanced human expectations.
Because collecting absolute scores for every possible completion would be costly,
researchers instead gather **pairwise preference data**:
for every prompt $x$ they draw two completions,
\$ y_{w}\$ (“preferred”) and \$ y_{l}\$ (“less preferred”),
from the current reference policy $\pi_{\textsf{ref}}$—often simply $\pi_{\textsf{SFT}}$.
Human raters then choose which response is better according solely
to criteria such as factual correctness, helpfulness, politeness,
or any desirable attribute captured implicitly by their annotations.\n\nThe resulting unlabeled dataset takes shape as  
$$
\mathcal{D}
=\bigl\{\,x^{(i)},\,y_{w}^{(i)},\,y_{l}^{(i)}\,\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
$$
where each triplet encodes an observed deterministic comparison
"$y_w^{(i)}$ beats $y_l^{(i)}$ given $x^{(i)}$."
Importantly,
this format preserves all information needed for inferring an underlying scalar‐valued
reward function without requiring exhaustive ground truth scores.



---

### # Bradley–Terry ranking likelihood

In order to convert sparse comparative judgments into learnable signals,
the **Bradley–Terry** model posits that each completion receives an unobserved real score $r^{*}(x,y)$ derived from some latent ‘reward’ distribution.
Under this hypothesis humans choose between two candidates according to:
$$
p^{*}\!\bigl(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x\bigr)
=\frac{\exp\nolimits\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\bigr)}
          {\exp\nolimits\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\bigr)+
           \exp\nolimits\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{l})\bigr)} .
$$  
A convenient reexpression uses the logistic map 
$\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+\mathrm e^{-z}}$, yielding 
$$ 
p^{*}(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x)
=\sigma\!\Bigl(r^{*}(x,y_w)-r^{*}(x,y_l)\Bigr).
$$  

Given only noisy observations $(x,y_w,y_l)$ drawn i.i.d.\ from $p^{*}$,
one frames **maximum‐likelihood estimation** over a parametric surrogate reward network
$r_{\phi}(x,y)$:
$$ 
\rho(\phi;\mathcal{D})
=
-\mathbb {E}_{(x,w,l)\sim \mathcal{D}}
   \Bigl[\log\sigma
   \!\Bigl(
      r_{\phi}(x,w)-r_{\phi}(x,l)
   \Bigr)
   \Bigr].
$$  
Intuitively,
optimising $\rho(\phi;\mathcal{D})$ nudges $r_{\phi}$ so that its predicted log odds mirror empirical comparisons.
To keep variance bounded practitioners typically normalise rewards over samples per prefix so that ${}\mathbb E[r_{\phi}(x,\cdot)]={0}$ for every context $x$.  

This rewarded signal becomes the driving motive behind later reinforcement‐learning stages;
in particular it coincides with augmentations used throughout contemporary *reinforcement learning from human feedback* pipelines wherein one subsequently seeks an improved language‐model policy maximizing expected rewards while staying close enough—to limit catastrophic deviation—to its initial candidate $\pi_{\textsf{ref}}$.  

While this subsection only sketches how pairwise judgments translate into an explicit bipartite likelihood function,
the subsequent chapters will build upon this relationship:
they will bypass constructing `rφ` completely,
instead manipulating directly the underlying policy distributions provided by a probabilistic decoder.
