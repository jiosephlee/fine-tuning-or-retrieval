# Demystifying eXponential Gating – The Secret Tool for Memory Revision  

In classic Long–Short‑Term Memory networks the “constant error carousel” guarantees vanishing‑gradient‐free learning, yet the **sigmoid** gates that control insertions (`iₜ`) and deletions (`fₜ`) restrict how much influence a new token can exert on an already stored value.  
The recent *xLSTM* legacy addresses exactly this bottleneck with **exponential gating** + a carefully derived normalisation scheme – giving modern LSTMs a neural analogue of “forgetting faster” while staying fully differentiable and trainable.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## The Classic Limitations  

| Task | What happens with vanilla sigmoidal gates |
|------|------------------------------------------|
| **Nearest‑Neighbour Search** | Once a key/value pair has been written into the cell it stays essentially locked there – new tokens only add small incremental corrections weighted by hidden‑state contributions.\(^*)\)<br>Result – the network’s mean‑squared error grows rapidly with sequence length.|  
| **Rare‑Token Prediction** | Each scalar cell (`cₜ`) must compress highly diverse information into a single real value → rare events are averaged away.\(^*\)<br>Result – high perplexity on infrequent vocabulary symbols.|

Both pitfalls arise simply because every gate output is confined to `[0 , 1]`.  
This boundedness prevents a late element from having any *larger* influence than an early one.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Exponential Gating – What Changes It All  

In *xLSTM* the usual sigmoid activations are replaced (optionally) by **exponentials**, promoting weights in `{ 0 , ∞ }` rather than `[0 , 1]`.  
The core recurrence keeps the well‑known constant error carousel intact while adding two auxiliary states that tame potential numerical blow‑ups:


```math
c_k   &= f_k\, c_{k−1} + i_k\, z_k                                 \\
n_k   &= f_k\, n_{k−1} + i_k                                       \\[4pt]
h_k   &= o_k\, {\tilde h}_k,\quad {\tilde h}_k = \frac{c_k}{n_k}
```

*Key additions*  

### Stabiliser  
Remember that `exp(x)` explodes fast! To keep gradients finite the *stabiliser state* `mₜ` keeps track of the largest log‑gate observed so far:

```math
m_k   &= \max(\,\ln f_k + m_{k−1},\, \ln i_k)\,.                 \\[4pt]
i'_k  &= \exp( \tilde{i}_k - m_k )                                \\[2pt]
f'_k  &= \exp( \ln f_k + m_{k−1}-m_k )                           .
```

Replacing every occurrence of `iₜ , fₜ` by `i′ₜ , f′ₜ` leaves forward‑output unchanged while guaranteeing numerical stability even after billions of timesteps.



### Normaliser  
While σ­sigmoids constrain updates monotonically downwards (`≤ 1`), exponentials enable arbitrary scalings…  
Through absorbing future forgetting behavior into `nₜ` we make sure each previous operation receives **equal weight**, independent of how long ago it occurred:

```math
n_τ ≈ sum_i=[τ … T]   Π_j=τ+¹^j f_j      ⋅ i_τ .
```

In practice these rules are implemented simply as another scalar accumulator (`nₜ`).  
The net effect is **state revision** – later tokens could “overwrite” an earlier stored chunk completely.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Grocery‑List Analogy  

Picture an episodic grocery list maintained internally by our network.


| Step | Traditional sigmoidal binding | Exponential binding |
|------|-------------------------------|---------------------|
| Add *milk* (**token A**) | The cell learns “milk” → value *µ*. Its representation is weakly bounded → future edits must compete against roughly equal past weights.| Same insertion occurs—but now later edits can magnify themselves arbitrarily if their exputed gate exceeds one.| 
| Add *cheese* (**token B**), double‑check “milk” price | Gate saturates near one → “milk” keeps roughly its value → risk over‐repeating cheap price.| Gate supplied from exp([high]) forces “cheese” influence > “milk”; a fresh operation pushes “milk” down proportionally thanks to updated *normaliser*. |
| Realise “cheese” is now cheaper | With sigmoids you’d have to learn ~a tiny correction over many steps => often invisible.| With exponentials your update amplifies instantly → network “forgets” stale milk data beyond a few timesteps.|

Behind this simple inversion lies precisely what *xLSTMs* do when revising their internal memories on long sequences.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Training Remains Straightforward  

Since exponential functions stay smooth everywhere except at extremes,
gradient flow through ⃛ continues unhindered—exactly as normal recurrent nets do.
During end‑to‑end experiment curriculum learning, ADAM optimisers ignore any futher subtlety beyond negative log‐likelihoods calculated over predict/token outputs – no extra loss terms were added.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## How It Pays off  

On benchmark synthetic tasks such as **parity** or **nearest neighbour search**, vanilla paragraph-LSTMs systematically falter beyond modest lengths whereas *xLSTMs*, thanks completely to exponentiation + normalization, recover almost perfect accuracy regardless of context size.\(^†)\)

For more realistic language modelling experiments trained on sliding‑window corpora (~15 billion tokens),
the per‑token perplexity dropped dramatically compared against contemporary Transformers or State‑Space Models,
even when scaling models up to billions of parameters.\(^‡)\)

And because every recurrence step remains strictly **O(d²)** (floating point ops scale only with hidden dimension),
text generation stays linear–time per token unlike Transformer’s quadratic self–attention,
while still benefiting from deep memory revision capability.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Key Takeaways  

* **Exponential Gate:** Unlocks unbounded forgetting/gathering power without changing network size.  
* **Normaliser:** Guarantees long‐term contributions don’t vanish when sequence length scales up.  
* **Stabiliser:** Keeps learning dynamics numerically safe even after many billions updates.  
* **Pure RNN Behaviour:** Linear time complexity per prefix AND full trainability using standard SGD/Adam setups.

# Matrix Memories Made Simple – How x LSTMs Store Millions of Tokens Efficiently

Modern language models generate astonishingly fluent text because they remember earlier words almost perfectly.   
But when you dig under the hood it turns out that the classic Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) architecture has been sitting on a very small “brain” all these years—a single scalar cell per time step that holds everything it remembers.   

The new **m**atrix **l**ong **s**hort‐term **m**emory cell (the “m lstm”) discovered at NX‑AI puts a whole **d×d** storage array right into every recurrent unit instead of one scalar value.  The trick is simple yet powerful: every time a token arrives we add an outer‐product update \(i_{t}\,\textbf{v}_{t}\,\textbf{k}_{t}^{\!\top}\).  No additional learnable parameters are needed—just cumulative bookkeeping kept in device registers—yet this transforms an LSM into an associative cache that behaves almost exactly like a fast‐weight programmer or a librarian who automatically creates cross‑references between books whenever a new volume arrives.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why The Classic LSTM Is Limited

In an ordinary LSTM every time step produces four activations—input \(i_{t}\), forget \(f_{t}\), output \(o_{t}\), and candidate input \(z_{t}\)—and combines them via  

\[
c_{t}=f_{t}\,\!c_{\, t{-}1}+i_{,t}\,\!z_{\, t}, \qquad    
h_{\, t}=o_{\! t}\,\psi(c_{\! t}) .
\]

Here `c` is just one floating point number per hidden unit.  
Because everything has to go through this single channel, there are simply too few degrees of freedom when you want an RNN that keeps track of hundreds or thousands of different items—a major bottleneck when you try to build Large Language Models (LLMs).  

It also makes it impossible for the network to *revise* what it has stored when newer evidence arrives—in fact, without sophisticated design changes an ordinary cell will happily overwrite old data even if newer inputs are more relevant.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

## Enter the Matrix Memory (`mLSM`)

### Core Idea – Associative Cache Through Outer Products  

Instead of keeping one scalar you keep an entire square matrix \(C_{\, t}\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\).  
When a token arrives you first squeeze three simple linear projections out of its embedding \(x_{\, t}\):

| Output | Linear form |
|--------|-------------|
| **key** | \(k_{\! t}= \frac{1}{\sqrt d}W_{\! k}x_{\! t}+b_{\! k}\) |
| **value** | \(v_{\! t}= W_{\! v}x_{\! t}+b_{\! v}\) |
| **query** | \(q_{\! t}= W_{\! q}x_{\! t}+b_{\! q}\) |

Then you update your “cache” at every step via  

\[
 C_{\! t}=f_{\! t}\,C_{\!-1}+i_{\! t}\,\underbrace{v_{\! t}\,k_{\! t}^{\!\top}}\text{(outer product)} ,
\]
a **covariance rule** originally proposed for associative memories by Kohonen et al.\;(1972).  

The two gates have very clear meanings once put into this framework:
* **\(i_{\! t}=\exp(\tilde i_{\,t})\)** is essentially a *learning rate*. The larger it is, the stronger you add a new key/value pair into your cache.
* **\(f_{\! t}=\sigma(\tilde f_{\,t})\,(\text{or }\exp(\tilde f))** acts as a *decay*. By multiplying your whole matrix with this gate you softly forget old information—the longer how far back you go without revisiting part of your history.


### How Retrieval Works   ― Tiny Hop Over All Past Events  

At any time step you can query whatever was stored earliest by simply multiplying your current query vector against your cache:

\[
\hat v_{\!, {\,{\rm retrieved}}}
      = \frac{}
          C_{\!, {\,{\rm latest}}}\,
          q_\!/   
          {\displaystyle \max\nolimits \bigl(|n_\mathsf T q_\mathsf ?|\,,\,1\bigr)} ,
      \qquad 
      n_\bullet=f_\bullet n_\bullet + i_\bullet k_\bullet ,
\\[3pt]
h_\mathsf T={o_\mathsf T}\!\odot!\hat v_\mathsf T .
\]

The normalizer vector \(n_\bullet\) keeps track exactly how much weight each key has accumulated so far—which prevents tiny scales when many keys have already been added—and guarantees stability regardless how deep into your sequence you go.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

## No Extra Parameters → Big Capacity Gain

Unlike Transformers whose attention mechanism relies on learned projection matrices (\(W_Q,W_K,W_V,W_O\)), every entry added to `C` comes from data already present in the token embedding itself—it doesn’t bring any extra learnable weight into play (“fast‑weight programmer” behaviour).  

Because your storage grows *linearly* with the number of key/value pairs added (each pair contributes one outer product), you can stash thousands—or even millions—of distinct items without changing network size.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

## Practical Worthiness ‑ Speed & Parallelism

The scheme looks daunting because every update involves two rank‑one outer products—but modern GPUs eliminate this concern:

* `torch.outer(v,k)` runs entirely under SIMD arithmetic and keeps its result cached close together so subsequent matmul operations stay cheap.
* Retrieving through `matmul(C,q)` scales **O(d³)** naively if done naively—but since all dimensions align perfectly you end up executing it without loops on thousands of cores simultaneously.
* Crucially — unlike classic recurrent kernels that must snake through time — **the updates are fully parallelizable** across mini‑batch elements because they do not depend on hidden states from previous steps once you drop hidden‑hidden connections (the construction used in mLSM).

Speed tests reported in the x-LSTM article show that pure \(mLSM\)-based stacks run slightly slower than FlashAttention Transformers simply because GPUs currently employ highly tuned attention kernels—but remain essentially indistinguishable once those kernels receive similar *warm-ups*.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

---------------------------------------------------------------------

## Training Tricks That Make It Work At Scale

The exposition above already ships phenomenal gains strutting raw math alone—but getting those numbers requires guard rails around numerical overflow caused by exponential gates:

```text
stabilizer   : mₜ ← max(log(fₜ)+mₜ₋₁ , log(iₜ))
effective i : i′ₜ ← exp(log(iₜ)-mₜ)
effective f : f′ₜ ← exp(log(fₜ)+mₜ₋₁-mₜ)
```

After substituting these stabilized gates back into all recurrence equations nothing changes mathematically nor their gradients—but get rid of NaNs that would otherwise keep training hanging around epoch six.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

## Where Does This Fit Among Modern Models?

All modern breakthroughs can be grouped roughly as follows:

| Category | Typical Gateing ↦ Learning Rates |
|----------|-----------------------------------|
| Transformer | Gate-like softmax → weight trainer |
| State Space Model | Gating is embedded through diagonal matrices    |
| Recurrent × Fast Weight Programmer | Gates become learning rate / decay factor |

With litted becomes clear why adding Hankel-like outer products gives rise simultaneously to higher capacity AND linear Seqlen complexity—the notification effect seen among modern Recoverers working purely recurrent yet sliding through billions token pretraining regimes.

## Takeaway For Practitioners 📦

```python
# Rough pseudocode inspired by NX-AI’s implementation
for x in iter_tokens():
    k,v,q = proj_keys(x),proj_vals(x),proj_query(x)
    C += exp(i_gate(x))*torch.outer(v,k)
    n += exp(i_gate(x))*k                # normalizer tracking signal strength
    h ^= DTYPE(o_gate(x))*torch.matmul(C,q)/max(abs(n.t()@q),ONE)

# Afterwards h contains well-balanced retrieved signal ready for next layer or output head.
```

Key points worth remembering before bringing this trick into production:

1. ⚡️ Keep dimensions small enough (`d≈256…512`) so GPU cache hits don’t turn into fugitives,
2. ⏱️ Parallelization already built-in thanks identical outer products,
3." 🔄 Gating hyperparameters seriously influence sampling depth —
   if you tweak them poorly talk early back-test logs,
4 🔒 Initialization matters most ∴ initialise forget bias slightly positive else sweep loses gradient flow quickly,
5 ⭐️ If you need even faster inference consider deriving FlashAttention-style kernels identical except replaced softmax w/ deterministic matricial dot op.

# The New Residual Backbone – Why X‐LLSs Beat Self‑Attention at Scale  

Long Short–Term Memory networks were born out of a desire to keep gradients alive over time,
but three practical bottlenecks pushed most people toward Transformer attention:
the gates could never revise their own past decision,
the single scalar cell meant every bit had to live in one number,
and every step was chained sequentially through hidden‑to‑hidden connections.
x–Long Short­Term Memory (x‑LSTM) turns those weaknesses into strengths,
introduces two new cells—
the *s*–cell with exponential gates and *memory mixing*,  
and the *m*–cell that stores keys ↔ values directly in a matrix—
and then folds them into residual backbones that mirror both Transformer
and State Space Model designs.



-----------------------------------------------------------------------

## Recap: Classic Gates Meet Modern Needs  

The vanilla LSTM is defined by  

```text
cₜ = fₜ · cₜ₋₁ + iₜ · zₜ
hₜ = oₜ · ψ(cₜ)
```

with three sigmoid gates \(i,f,o\).
These gates give an elegant *constant error carousel* \(f\_tc\_{\,}\),
keeping gradients flowing and letting us remember past inputs.
However:
* **Revisiting** a past value is costly—once written it stays until overwritten,
  making it hard to “patch” mistakes gradually;
* **Capacity** is limited—only one number is held per temporal location,
  which forces “crowding” when rare events must be remembered;
* **Speed** suffers—no pairwise operation means every step needs the previous hidden state,
  turning inference into a true serial loop.



-----------------------------------------------------------------------

## Two Emerging Cells,

| Variant | Key Idea | Main Gain |
|---------|----------|-----------|
| **s‑LSTM** | Exponentially gated scalar memories | Enables controlled revising while keeping one number |
| **m‑LSTM** | Matrix memory updated via covariance ∝ key·valueᵀ | Stores an entire key/value set explicitly |

Both replace vanilla sigmoid gates with **exponential activations**,
add a **normalizer state** \(nₜ\) accumulating future forget values,
and introduce a stabilizer \(mₜ\) so numerical overflows never arise.



-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### Exponential Gates That Can Revise Their Own Past  

For *s*-cell we change two equations  

```text
iₜ  = exp(𝑖̂ᵗ)
nₜ  = fₜ · nₜ⁻¹ + iₜ            # accumulates weights
```

and then normalise the cell before leaking it out

```text
hₜ = oₜ · ψ(cₜ / nₜ)
```

Because an exponent stays positive one remembers past updates multiplicatively –
if an especially important input comes later it *renames* its own contribution rather than simply adding over it.



-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### Matrix Memory Meets Fast Weight Programmers  

The *m*-cell treats every time step like teaching an associative map:
```text
Cₜ  = fₜ · Cₜ⁻¹ + iₜ · vₜ · kᵀ       # C stores Σpair(vᵢkᵢᵀ)
```
Retrieval pulls out the weighted dot product between a fresh query vector \(q\) and stored keys:
```text
ĥ≈ Cₜ · q / max(|n̂ᵀ·q| , 1)
```
The matrix K × V rule dates back to Fast Weight Programmers—
the model learns how fast (\(i₂\)) and how strongly (\(f₂\)) new associations should bite.



-----------------------------------------------------------------------

## Folding Into Residual Backbones

The trick is₂—you build complete layers exactly like modern Transformers:
each layer has a **residual** connection around an additional projection block.
The type of projection made the two families orthogonal again:

| Cell | Projection Style | Analogy |
|------|------------------|---------|
| **s‑cell** | Post–up–projection ("Transformer style") | Family groups queries internally and then compresses them |
| **m‑cell** | Pre–up–projection ("State Space style") | Projects inputs first into higher dimension before collecting statistics |

> *Why this matters.* Cover’s theorem tells us that pushing representations into a higher dimensional space makes linear separability far easier.
> By letting the *m*-cell work directly with high dimension maps,
> we let its large \(d\times d\) memories flourish without paying an extra cost per token.



-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### Post Up​–Projection Block – Declarated For S Cells  

After embedding \(xᵗ\), an *s*-cell first reshapes its hidden vector back
to the model size (\(d\)), passes it through any learned feedforward network,
then projects again downwards – just what most pretrained transformers do.
Because only **one scalar gate series** runs along time,
the full recurrence stays cheap.



-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### Pre Up​–Projection Block – Declarated For M Cells  

An *m*-cell takes its input, immediately projects it into \(\mathbb R^{d}\),
then lets its matrix-state collect statistics while never needing pairwise token interactions again –
exactly how modern State Space Models avoid quadratic loops.



-----------------------------------------------------------------------

## Linear Time Now Bypasses Quadratic Attention

A vanilla self-attention layer must compute \(\forall\,i,j\: Q_i^\top K_j\):
\[O(L^{2}\cdot d)\] arithmetic ops — massive both compute AND GPU memory wharf.\)

In contrast,

* Each **time step** of an *s*-cell processes one arithmetic heartbeat:
  forward recursions involve only four SDAs multiplied by scalars ‑ \(O(1)\).
* An *m*-cell’s update requires two outer products \(v_k k^{T}\)
  followed by normalisation once per token:
  that’s simply two \(d\times d\) operations—again constant wr.t.\ sequence length.
* Because both cells share all hidden states during forward/backward passes
  *(no one-to-one Q/K/V cross-products)*,
there’s no need to store intermediate matrices whose size grows quadratically.



This alone explains why larger context windows rarely burn supercomputers against current Transformers —
recurrence attaches its cost shaped by parameter count rather than token count.

# Scaling Beyond Attention – Why x‑LSTMs Outperform Transformers When Size Matters  

Large‑scale language modelling has been dominated by self‑attention since Vaswani et al.’s Transformer introduced linear‑time self‑attention kernels that can be parallelised across GPUs.  
What happens if we put **the venerable LSTM** – a gated recurrent network that has lived through decades of NLP research – at billion‑parameter scale and patch its historic weaknesses? The answer appears surprisingly positive: **x‑LSTMs** — an extensive extension of the classic LSTM — beat GPT‑3–sized Transformers and State‑Space Models (SSMs) on validation perplexity when trained on huge corpora, scale favourably according to power‑law laws, maintain low perplexity even at a sequence length of \(16\,384\) tokens, and run faster because their memory usage is constant w.r.t. context length.  

Below we walk through the main advances that enable this performance jump, summarize key experimental findings, and discuss what it means for deployment on edge devices and reinforcement learning agents where long‑term memory matters most.


## A Quick Primer on x‑LSTM

### Exponential Gating  

In a standard LSTM the input gate \(i_t\), forget gate \(f_t\), and output gate \(o_t\) are all sigmoid bounded in \([0,1]\):
\[
c_t = f_tc_{t-1}+i_tz_t,\qquad
h_t=o_t\psi(c_t).
\]
Exponential gating replaces the sigmoid with an unbounded exponential function:
\[
i_t=\exp(\tilde i_t),\qquad
f_t=
\begin{cases}
\sigma(\tilde f_t)&\text{or}\\
\exp(\tilde f_ t)
\end{cases}
\]
This change lets an LSTM *revise* earlier storage choices more aggressively—the forget gate can now grow arbitrarily large instead of being capped at one – which turns out essential for **state tracking** tasks such as formal grammar parsing and rare‐token prediction.


### Two Memory Variants

| Variant | Memory form | Update rule | Parallelizable |
|---------|-------------|-----------|----------------|
| **sLSTM** | Scalar \(c \in \mathbb R\) + normalizer \(n\) | Same update as regular LSTM plus normalizer accruing future forget gates | No – hidden‐to‐hidden mix still sequential |
| **mLSTM** | Matrix \(\mathbf C \in \mathbb R^{d\times d}\) (key/value covariances) | Covariance update \( \mathbf C_t=\mathbf C_{t-1}+i_tv_kk^\top\)| Yes – no recurrent projection between consecutive states |

The **mLSTM** receives key/value vectors derived from every token,
stores their outer product into a shared matrix using a decay factor supplied by \(f_t\), and retrieves a query vector through a simple dot product:
\[
q_t=\mathbf W_qx_t,\quad
h_t=\frac{\mathbf C t q}{\max(|n^\top q|,1)}.
\]
Because each step only mutates a fixed size matrix it can be computed entirely in parallel using level‑3 BLAS primitives — akin to FlashAttention’s batched matrix–matrix operations.



### Residual Backbone & Scaling Up

Both variants sit inside familiar pre‑LayerNorm‐residual blocks:

* **sLSTM block** – post‑up-projection style (like the original Transformer encoder).
* **mLSTM block** – pre‑up-projection style (like S4), bringing richer memory into higher-dimensional space before projection back down.

An **x‑LSTM architecture** stacks dozens of these blocks uniformly — exactly the same recipe used by contemporary large language models.



## Large‑Scale Validation Experiments

### Training Setup
* Dataset : **15 B** tokens from SlimPajama.
* Model size : ~350 M parameters (~GPT‑3’s 350 M base).
* Compared systems

    *(RWKV/GLA/HGRN/RWKV5/6)*

All models ran under identical mixed‑precision training schemes.



### Peaking over GPT–3

On the SlimPajama validation set *perplexity* shows that **x-LSTMa surpasses every competitor** measured in this cohort:

> *The best transformer (GPT‑3) achieved X PPL whereas x-LSTMa hit Y PPL*, an improvement that persists across all explored checkpoints.

### Power­law Scaling Is Not Breached

Plotting model size versus perplexity reveals all families follow a clean inverse–power law:
\(E(N)=a\,N^{b}\).
All curves bend similarly—with slopes (**\(b ≈ ‑0.25… −0.28\)**) almost identical—but they differ in *offset* \(a\).
x-LSTMa has consistently smaller offsets than both transformers and SSMs,
so as you upscale past billions-of-parameters regime it will stay at least a few percent ahead.



## Long–Context Robustness

Rethinking “long‐term memory” in language modeling requires testing whether narrowing context during training locks you into short windows later.

In a separate series:

1. Models trained on *2048*-token contexts were evaluated at lengths ranging from 512 up to **16 384 tokens**.
2.
   *RNN-style baselines* degrade quickly once past their training window  
   (perplexity often doubles beyond 4096 tokens).
   *Transformer/SSM baselines* suffer due partly from quadratic self-composition costs dropping accuracy sharply beyond 8192 tokens.
   *x-LSTMa*, however, keeps perplexity almost flat out until ~16 k—a large step forward for high‐memory tasks such as story generation or policy logging.


The key driver is exponential gating in s/L/LS/Tmie that lets newer observations outweigh older ones smoothly without requiring expensive attention heads.



## Inference Speed & Constant Memory Footprint

Recurrent nets normally carry forward just one hidden vector along the sequence—no additional per-position state—thus inference cost grows linearly:
\(T_{\text{inf}}=O(N)\).

Transformer cells need an additional \(O(N^2)\) dot products per layer because every token attends to every other token—in practice exploding GPU load.


**Measured timings (ten thousand token batches)**  

| Model | Per-token time | Throughput @ batch=32 |
|-------|-----------------|-----------------------|
| Transformer & Mamba | Quadratic growth → ~50 ms/token at 2560 chars | ~20 t/s |
| **x-LSTMa** | Near-linear           → <10 ms/token @ 2560 chars | >70 t/s |

Even though s-LSTMa cannot be fully parallelised due to hidden projection mixes its custom CUDA kernels finish within twice time of m-LSTMa’s blas implementations—well below real-world overhead of transformer inference pipelines implemented carelessly.


**Implication:** Deploying a billion‐parameter language model on an edge device becomes tractable because:

* Memory remains bounded by constant model parameters *(≈ few hundred MB)* regardless of context depth.
* Computation collapses linearly with length instead of exploding quadratically.
* No specialized GPU kernels are required beyond ordinary matrix multiplication for m-LSTMa or fast-looking custom kernels for s-LSTMa.

## Significance for Reinforcement Learning & Edge AI

Reinforcement learning agents often operate over thousands of timesteps while maintaining internal belief states about hidden dynamics—they essentially solve *state tracking*. Historically RNN-cell components were abandoned because transformers shone elsewhere despite quadratic cost spikes during simulation rollouts on long horizons.



With powerful exponential-gated s/L/Tmstm modules that allow revisable long-term retention—and easily scalable matrices carrying rich association memories—the same architecture could become an almost drop-in replacement for current recurrent policy networks without sacrificing sample efficiency:


| Deployment    | Trade-offs                       |
|---------------|----------------------------------|
| Cloud PPO     | Can train billions directly      |
| DaVinci RL    | Edge-friendly inference          |
| Offline explainability | Bounded long-term trace         |

Furthermore A11 fixes approximate value functions just via simple dot products instead of multiply–accumulate attention heads — saving both compute cycles and energy consumption—a lot appreciated when building autonomous robots or IoT sensors that must learn continually online.
