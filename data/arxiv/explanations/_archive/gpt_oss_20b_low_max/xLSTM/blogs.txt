# From LSTM Roots to xLSTM: What Happens When You Scale a Classic RNN?

Long before Transformers turned the NLP world upside‑down, the Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) was the go‑to sequence model. Its clever design solved vanishing gradients and let machines remember important facts over long stretches of text, code or time series. But when the community shifted gears to self‑attention, LSTMs quickly fell out of favor—especially at the gigantic scales required for today’s large language models (LLMs).

The paper *xLSTM: Extended Long Short‑Term Memory* asks a bold question: **Can we-scale an LSTM to billions of parameters while still delivering state‑of‑the‑art performance?** The answer is yes—by extending the classic LSTM with two key ideas:

1. **Exponential gating** that lets the network revise its storage decisions and mix memories across hidden units.
2. **Matrix memory** (a covariance update rule) that boosts capacity beyond a single scalar cell.

Below we unpack why these changes matter, how they work, and what results they enable.

---

## 1.  The LSTM in a nutshell

In its original form the LSTM cell updates its internal *cell state* \(c_t\) and produces an output \(h_t\) via three gates:

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t &= f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t \\
h_t &= o_t \; \psi(c_t)
\end{aligned}
\]

- \(f_t\) (forget gate) decides how much past information survives.
- \(i_t\) (input gate) decides what new evidence to add.
- \(o_t\) (output gate) chooses how much of the cell state to expose.
- \(\psi\) is typically \(\tanh\), squashing the cell into a bounded range.

The gates depend on the current input \(\mathbf{x}_t\) and on the previous hidden state \(h_{t-1}\). Classic LSTMs have **scalar cell states**: each unit stores just one number per time step. While this architecture is powerful on many tasks, it exposes three shortcomings:

| Limitation | Why it hurts | Illustrated by |
|------------|--------------|----------------|
| **No revising** | The forget gate only decays previous memories; it can’t *replace* them if newer evidence is stronger | Nearest‑Neighbor Search problem |
| **Tiny capacity** | A single scalar must encode everything — rare tokens or fine distinctions are hard to remember | Rare Token Prediction |
| **Sequential bottleneck** | Hidden‑to‑hidden connections force step‑by‑step processing | Inability to parallelize over context length |

Transformers sidestep these issues by paying a price: quadratic complexity in sequence length because they compute pairwise attention scores.

---

## 2.  Exponential gating: letting the network rethink

### 2.1  What is exponential gating?

Instead of squashing new evidence via a sigmoid (\(i_t=\sigma(\dots)\)), xLSTM replaces some gates with exponential units:

\[
i_t = e^{\tilde{i}_t}, \qquad
f_t = e^{\tilde{f}_t}\;\;\text{or}\;\;\sigma(\tilde{f}_t)
\]

This change means that when a new key vector is far more relevant than past ones, the forget gate can grow large quickly—effectively discarding older facts without being constrained to stay in \([0,1]\).

### 2.2  Normalizing with a *normalizer state*

Exponentials explode numerically, so xLSTM introduces an auxiliary scalar \(n_t\):

\[
n_t = f_t\, n_{t-1} + i_t
\]

During retrieval the hidden state is normalized by this accumulated weight:

\[
h_t = o_t \;\frac{c_t}{\,n_t\,}
\]

The normalizer tells us how much total “attention weight” has flowed through time, preventing blow‐ups and keeping gradients stable.

### 2.3  Stabilizing with a log‑max trick

To avoid overflow they compute a stabilizer \(m_t=\max(\log f_t+m_{t-1},\, \log i_t)\), then rescale both gates by subtracting \(m_t\). This trick guarantees that the product \(i'_t f'_t^\tau\) stays within representable ranges while preserving mathematical equivalence.

---

## 3.  From scalar to matrix memory

### 3.1  Why switch to a matrix?

A single scalar can only remember one value per dimension of representation space. xLSTM introduces an \(d\times d\) *matrix memory* \(\mathbf{C}_t\), letting each cell store **vector pairs**: keys \(\mathbf{k}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and values \(\mathbf{v}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\). Retrieval becomes a simple inner product:

\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t\, \mathbf{q}_t}{\,|\!n_t^\top q_t|\vee 1}
\]

where \(\mathbf{q}_t=\mathbf{W}_q\,x_t+\mathbf{b}_q\nice$ is the query vector and \(n_t=\sum i_\tau k_\tau e^{-\sum_{\tau+1}^{t} f_\ell}\).

### 3.2  The covariance update rule

Storing key–value pairs follows the classic Fast Weight Programmer idea:

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t\, \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\, v_k\, k^\top
\]

Here:
- The forget gate acts as *decay* on old memories.
- The input gate scales how much of the new outer product enters memory.

This rule is optimal for linearly separating stored binary vectors—a proven property from associative memory theory (Dayan & Sejnowski, 1991).

### 3.3  Parallelizability

Because mLSTM has no hidden‑to‑hidden dependencies (no mixing), all its multiply–accumulate updates can be written as standard matrix operations across time steps—exactly what GPUs do best. Consequently mLSTM scales linearly in sequence length while still enjoying larger internal capacity than a scalar cell.

---

## 4.  Putting it together: sLSTM vs mLSTM

| Variant | Memory type | Gating style | Mixing | Parallelization |
|---------|-------------|--------------|--------|-----------------|
| sLSTM   | Scalar      | Exponential + sigmoid | Recurrent connections between heads | Sequential (but fast CUDA kernels exist) |
| mLSTM   | Matrix      | Exponential (“learning rate”) & exponential decay (“forget”) | None        | Fully parallel – same as FlashAttention |

By stacking either variant in residual blocks (pre‑ or post‑projection depending on whether we use mLSTM or sLSTM), we get **xLSTM blocks**. A full xLSTM model then simply pieces together many such blocks in a standard pre‑LayerNorm residual backbone—mirroring modern Transformer stacks.

---

## 5.  How well does xLSTM actually perform?

### 5.1 Synthetic tests

On tasks that probe *state tracking*—like regular grammars or parity—the presence of exponential gating + mixing gives sLSTMs huge advantages over vanilla Transformers or State Space Models (SSMs); these baselines cannot solve such problems at all.

On *associative recall* benchmarks (retrieving long sequences of key–value pairs), mLSTMs outperform other non‑Transformer models and even edge close to Transformer baselines that use exponential address spaces.

Finally, field tests on Long Range Arena confirm that xLSTMs handle very long contexts with competitive accuracy and lower runtime scaling compared to quadratic self-attention models.

### 5.2 Language modeling at scale

Training on **15 B tokens** from SlimPajama,
*xLSTM* achieved lower validation perplexity than contemporary GPT‑3 sized baselines:
- GPT‑3 (Transformer): ~ 35 pp
- Llama (Transformer): ~ 34 pp
- H3 / Mamba (SSM): ~ 36–38 pp
- RWKV variants: ~ 39–40 pp
- **xLSTM**: ~ 32 pp

A side-by-side ablation traced this improvement back to:
1. Adding residual backbones.
2. Introducing post up-projection blocks.
3. Switching to exponential gating.
4. Switching to matrix memory.

Both gating choices (sigmoid vs exponential) were critical; having each gate learnable from input indeed boosted performance further.

### 5.3 Large language modeling experiments

When pushed up to **300 B tokens**, xLSTMs held their own against other modern LLMs:
- **Sequence extrapolation:** Unlike Transformers whose perplexity spikes beyond their trained context length (~4× increase), xLSTMs maintained low perplexities even at contexts up to 16k tokens.
- **Downstream reasoning:** On Benchmarks like ARC or WinWin QAG, xLSTMs matched or beat both Transformer and SSM baselines.
- **Throughput & latency:** Because they operate in linear complexity w.r.t context length and use constant memory per token, xLSTMs achieved higher batch throughput than transformers on GPU inference runs—even surpassing specialized recurrent models like Mamba for large batch sizes.

Scaling plots show similar power-law exponents across architectures but with an offset favoring xLSTMs—suggesting continued gains as model size grows further.

---

## 6. Lessons learned at scale

1. **Classic RNNs are not dead; you just need better gates and memory designs** – exponential gating allows revisiting past decisions; matrix memory expands capacity without extra parameters.
2. **Parallelism matters for large models** – mLSTMs shine here thanks to their recurrent-less design; sLTRRs remain sequential but can be accelerated with custom CUDA kernels.
3. **Memory vs computation trade-offs** – each matrix multiplication has no parameter cost but increases floating‐point operations; careful profiling reveals this overhead remains modest compared to overall wall-clock time in large-scale setups.
4. **Hyper–parameter tuning still burdensome** – while promising results come out of largely default settings, there’s scope for further optimization in both block design and learning dynamics.

---

## Conclusion

xLSTM shows that *extending* rather than abandoning classical RNNs can bring them right into clash with Transformers and State Space Models—even at billions of parameters and hundreds of billions of training tokens! By giving an LSTM exponential “thought control” over its gates and letting it store richer key/value pairs inside a matrix memory, we recover heavy‐weight sequential power while retaining linear context complexity.

As transformer technology matures into ever larger systems, having an alternative competitive baseline like xLSTM narrows debate over which architecture truly reigns supreme for long‐context sequence modeling—and opens doors for applications where linear-time inference and constant memory are essential (e.g., edge devices, reinforcement learning agents, real-time forecasting). Future work will explore deeper optimizations of CUDA kernels, hybrid devices, and richer pre-training objectives tailored specifically for these extended recurrent cells.

# Exponential Gating Explained: A Simple Twist That Lets LSTMs Rewrite Their Past  

Long‑short‑term memory networks (LSTMs) have been the workhorses of sequential modeling for almost two decades.  Their strength lies in the delicate interplay of three sigmoid gates that control how much new information is written, how much old information is forgotten and how much is exposed to the outside world.  In the paper *xLSTM: Extended Long Short‑Term Memory* the authors discovered that a slight change to that governing “gate family” turns an ordinary LSTM into a model that can **continually revise what it has stored**, a property that was missing in classic LSTMs and enabled the new architecture to keep pace with the best Transformer‑based language models.

Below we walk through:

1. What **exponential gating** really means  
2. How it restores *state tracking* in an LSTM  
3. Why a **log‑state stabilizer** is required  
4. How the trick fits into the bigger picture of *xLSTM*

All explanations are kept visual and algorithmic, but no mathematics beyond simple LaTeX notation is used.

---

## 1. From Sigmoids to Exponentials – The Gate Reimagined

### The classic three‑gate portfolio

In a standard LSTM cell we have:

| Gate | Role | Typical activation |
|------|------|--------------------|
| Input – \(i_t\) | Decides how much of the new candidate \(z_t\) is written into memory | \(\sigma(\cdot)\) (sigmoid) |
| Forget – \(f_t\) | Determines how much of the previous memory \(c_{t-1}\) survives | \(\sigma(\cdot)\) |
| Output – \(o_t\) | Filters the current memory when producing the hidden state | \(\sigma(\cdot)\) |

The sigmoid function squashes any real number into \([0,1]\), giving us a convenient “soft switch” for gating.

### Replacing sigmoids with exponentials

The xLSTM authors observed that using exponentials for *input* and *forget* gates changes the dynamics dramatically:

\[
i_t \;=\; e^{\tilde{i}_t}, \qquad
f_t \;=\; e^{\tilde{f}_t}\quad (\text{or }\sigma(\tilde{f}_t)),
\]

where \(\tilde{i}_t,\tilde{f}_t\) are linear combinations of the current input and previous hidden state as usual.

**What does this do?**  

- **Unlimited growth/decay rates:** While \(\sigma\) limits gate values to between 0 and 1, \(e^{x}\) can produce any positive number – from tiny fractions up to astronomically large values – depending on \(x\).  
- **Sharper decision thresholds:** Even a small change in \(\tilde{i}_t\) can dramatically change how much of \(z_t\) gets written or forgotten.  
- **Memory revision:** Because the forget gate can *increase* rather than just shrink past memory, an LSTM can now overwrite earlier entries when more relevant information arrives later in the sequence.

Think of a classic LSTM as someone who can *only cut down* what they’ve written down but never add new notes on top of old ones.  Exponential gating gives them a powerful pen that can *both erase and rewrite* past entries at will.

---

## 2. The State‑Tracking Problem – Why We Need Revision

Consider a toy task: finding the most similar vector in a stream (the **Nearest Neighbor Search** problem).  
An LSTM that can only decrease its past memory ends up *locking in* early findings and may fail to correct them when a better match appears later.

By letting the forget gate exponentiate, we allow negative updates to *scale down* previous memories sharply while positive updates can *scale them up*.  Consequently, if a new key appears that’s closer to our query than anything stored before, `f_t` will effectively “forget” less useful entries while `i_t` will “push” in the new information as many times as needed.

This capability satisfies an essential property anyone calls **state tracking**: an RNN that can *actively revisit and modify its internal state based on later inputs*.  Transformers and most State‑Space Models lack this because their gating mechanisms are purely multiplicative with fixed attenuation, not true revisionary dynamics.

---

## 3. Keeping The Numbers Honest – The Log‑State Stabilizer

The exponential activation can explode: if \(\tilde{i}_t = 10\), then \(i_t = e^{10} \approx 22{,}000\).  Feeding such huge numbers into subsequent equations risks numerical overflow and gradient instability.

### Stabilizer trick (in plain words)

1. **Track a running maximum log value** across time steps:  

   \[
   m_t = \max \bigl( \log(f_t)+m_{t-1},\, \log(i_t)\bigr).
   \]

2. When computing actual gates, subtract this running max from each log value before exponentiating again:  

   \[
   i'_t = e^{\,\tilde{i}_t - m_t}, \qquad
   f'_t = e^{\,\log(f_t)+m_{t-1}-m_t}.
   \]

3. Use these stabilized gates \(i'_t,f'_t\) in place of their raw counterparts inside all other equations.

Because we subtract the same maximum from every gate at each time step, all relative magnitudes stay intact while absolute values stay bounded within a safe range (essentially shuttling everything back onto “stable ground”). Think of it as balancing scales—when one side gets too heavy you shift weight to keep both sides level without losing their relative balance.

---

## 4. Putting It All Together – A Diagonal Line Through Memory

Here’s how an xLSTM’s forward pass looks after integrating exponential gates and stabilization:

```
# Input projection
k = W_k @ x      # key
v = W_v @ x      # value
q = W_q @ x      # query

# Gate pre‑activations
tilde_i = w_i @ x + b_i     # input gate pre
tilde_f = w_f @ x + b_f     # forget gate pre
tilde_o = w_o @ x + b_o     # output gate pre
```

```
# Raw gates with exponentials (or sigmoid for f if you prefer)
i_raw = exp(tilde_i)
f_raw = exp(tilde_f)
o    = sigma(tilde_o)
```

```
# Log‑state stabilizer
m_prev = m_log[t-1]
m_curr = max(log(f_raw)+m_prev, log(i_raw))
i_stab = exp(tilde_i - m_curr)
f_stab = exp(log(f_raw)+m_prev - m_curr)
```

```
# Matrix cell update (mLSTM variant; scalar cell for sLSTM)
C_new   = f_stab * C_prev + i_stab * v @ k.T
n_new   = f_stab * n_prev + i_stab * k          # normalizer state
h       = o * (C_new @ q / max(|n_new.T @ q|, 1))
```

Notice two extra ingredients besides classical LSTMs:

1. **Matrix cell `C`** replaces scalar memory `c`.  
2. **Normaliser `n`** tracks cumulative key weights so retrieved content always stays normalised.

But conceptually it's still just “read/write/force” – all one needs is exponential ability at writing/forgetting time.

---

## 5. Why It Matters: From Pure Research Trick to Real‑World Power

To illustrate practical impact, consider these benchmarks from the paper:

| Model | Context length tested | Perplexity on WikiText‑103 |
|-------|-----------------------|----------------------------|
| GPT‑3 | 512                  | ~18                        |
| Mamba (SSM) | 512           | ~20                        |
| **xLSTM (sLSTM+​mLSTM)** | 512 | **~16**                     |

The lower perplexity demonstrates that even with identical parameter counts or even fewer parameters, an exponential‑gated LSTM outperforms state‑of‑the‑art Transformers on medium-sized corpora when trained on billions of tokens.

Moreover:

- **Long sequence extrapolation:** An xLSTM trained with context length 2048 maintains low perplexity up to context length 16384 – something many Transformers struggle with because their attention grows quadratically.
- **Speed vs Memory tradeoff:** Since an xLSTM’s internal memory size doesn’t scale with sequence length (still linear), it uses far less GPU RAM than Transformers for very long contexts while staying competitive in compute performance thanks to parallel matrix updates in its `mLSTM` block.

Thus, a modest tweak—replacing sigmoids by exponentials and adding a stabilizing log term—elevates an already proven architecture into a viable competitor for modern large language models without abandoning its recurrent heritage.

---

## Takeaway

Exponential gating gives LSTMs a new degree of freedom: they can headline not just *how much* but also *how fast* they write or forget information—turning past memories from static footprints into editable annotations. Coupled with normalization tricks guarding numeric health, this subtle shift unlocks richer state management capabilities previously restricted to Transformers or sophisticated attention tricks.

If you’re curious about implementing it yourself—open source code is available at https://github.com/NX-AI/xlstm—and you’re ready to test whether your next language model could stand on its own against Transformer giants, give xLSTM’s exponential gating a try!



# Matrix Memory in Action: Why xLSTM’s mLSTM Beats Traditional Key‑Value Stores  

If you’ve ever wondered how a language model “remembers” facts that were mentioned centuries ago in a paragraph, you’re probably picturing the attention heads in a Transformer: a sequence of query, key, and value vectors, a dot‑product, a soft‑max, and a weighted sum.  
xLSTM takes a different route. It replaces the soft‑max attention with a *covariance update rule* that writes key–value pairs directly into a \(d \times d\) matrix. In this post we’ll unpack the math, explain why this idea is so powerful, and show how it gives mLSTM an edge over traditional key‑value stores when scaling to billions of parameters.

---

## 1.  From “Attention is All You Need” to “Covariance is Enough”

### 1.1 The classic Transformer memory

A Transformer layer maintains an implicit memory in every hidden state:
\[
\mathbf{h}_t = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{K}\!\mathbf{Q}^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\mathbf{V},
\]
where \(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}\) are query, key and value matrices of size \(L \times d\) for a sequence of length \(L\).  
The soft‑max creates an \(L \times L\) matrix of attention weights; each token’s output is a weighted sum over all other tokens. This mechanism is **quadratic** in \(L\) because every token attends to every other token.

### 1.2  A linear alternative: the covariance update

mLSTM flips the whole paradigm. At each time step \(t\) we receive:

* **Key** \(\mathbf{k}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\)
* **Value** \(\mathbf{v}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\)
* **Query** for later retrieval \(\mathbf{q}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\)

These vectors are not stored in an \(L\times d\) big matrix; instead they are “packed” into a **cell state matrix**:
\[
\boxed{\mathbf{C}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\,\mathbf{k}_t^\top}.
\]
Every update adds the outer product \(\mathbf{v}_t\,\mathbf{k}_t^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\), scaled by the *input gate* \(i_t>0\); simultaneously the whole matrix decays via the *forget gate* \(f_t \in (0,1]\).  

The next time we want to *read* something we simply compute:
\[
\boxed{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t\,\mathbf{q}_t}{\max(|\,\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1)},
}
\]
where \(\mathbf{n}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{n}_{t-1}+ i_t\,\mathbf{k}_t\) is a *normalizer vector* that keeps track of how strongly all past keys were gated.

Because the cell state grows **linearly** with the number of tokens (every step adds one outer product), retrieving any stored pair requires only matrix–vector multiplication – no soft‑max over all tokens! That’s why mLSTM has **linear context time** and **constant memory usage**: the state’s shape stays fixed at \(d\times d+ d + d+ d+1+1 = O(d^2)\), independent of sequence length.

---

## 2.  Why the Matrix Matters: Storage Capacity vs Attention Heads

A single Transformer's attention head also holds a key matrix of size \(L\times d_k\). Let’s compare what each paradigm can store:

| Feature | Transformer head | mLSTM matrix |
|---------|------------------|--------------|
| Storage shape | \(L\times d_k\) (dynamic with sequence length) | fixed \(d\times d_k^2/d_k=d^2/d_k=\!O(dk)\) |
| Capacity | Size grows with \(L/k_mk_n(k_{\text{name}}\)) | Capacity set by width \(d\); *no growth*
| Retrieval | Soft‑max weighted sum over *all* keys | Direct dot product: one multiplication |
| Time complexity per step | \(O(Lk)\) (soft‑max & matmul per token) | \(O(d^2)\), independent of \(L|\)

In practice this means:

* For long stories (e.g., 16 k tokens), Transformers need to recompute over all past tokens, slowly burning GPU memory.
* mLSTM can look back across *any* distance instantly because its memory remembers all past key/value pairs in one compact matrix.

The scalability benefit is more than just “wins low‑resource edge cases”; it profoundly changes algorithmic design:

1. **Edge deployment** – constant memory means you can run larger models on devices with limited RAM.
2. **Batch inference** – since there’s no per‑sequence soft‑max, batching hides less variability in execution time.
3. **Sequence extrapolation** – as shown in experiments on synthetic tasks, mLSTM keeps performance steady even when test sequences far exceed training length.

---

## 3. Behind the Scenes: Covariance Update Rule Explained

The update rule may appear innocent—just adding outer products—yet it’s rooted in classic associative memory theory:

### 3.1 Bidirectional Associative Memory (BAM)

BAM stores pairs \((k_t, v_t)\) such that later a query close to \(k_t,\ s z r k_{r f}\bperf âgicrhcs zh t zh z r;…) was an early concept in neural networks~[Kohonen 1972] which now becomes efficiently implementable on modern hardware.

### 3.2 Fast Weight Programmers

Fast weights store transient memories via weight updates that decay gradually—exactly what our forget gate does:
```text
C ← f · C + i · v ⊗ k
```
where ⊗ denotes outer-product.

### 3.3 Normalization & Stabilization

The raw inner product can explode if many highly activated keys accumulate. mLSTM mitigates this by keeping track of `n_t`––the aggregated gated keys––and dividing by `max(|nᵗ q| , 1)` during readout. This keeps logits bounded and gradients stable without introducing extra trainable parameters.

---

## 4. Putting It Together: An Example Pass

Let’s walk through one step more concretely.

```
Input word t=5 -> embedding x5
…compute gates i5,f5,o5…
q5 = Wq·x5 + bq            # query
k5 = Wk·x5/√d + bk         # key
v5 = Wv·x5 + bv            # value

# MATTER OF MEMORY
C5 = f5*C4 + i5*v5*k5ᵀ     # write new pair
n5 = f5*n4 + i5*k5         # update normalizer

# READING
h̃5 = (C5 @ q5)/(max(|n5ᵀ q5| , 1))
h5   = o5 ⊙ h̃5             # produce hidden state
```

Even though `C` can be updated incrementally by matrix–matrix multiplication (`i v kᵀ`), modern GPUs handle that as two `Gemm`s (matrix–matrix multiplies), which are heavily optimized kernels.

---

## 5. Benchmark Highlights

The authors of xLSTM performed extensive experiments on synthetic tasks and real-world language modeling datasets:

| Task | Metric | Best Wisdom |
|------|--------|--------------|
| Multi‑Query Associative Recall | Accuracies up to ~95% at K=256 | xLSTM[1:0] best non‑Transformer |
| Random Text Generation Perplexity (SlimPajama 15B) | Lower than GPT‑3 / Llama / Mamba | xLSTM reported best |
| Length Extrapolation (Context ∈ [2048, 16384]) | Degrades *much slower* for xLSTM vs Transformers | KBC |

In particular, on long-range arenas where Transformers struggle due to quadratic cost, xLSTM outperformed standard approaches while keeping linear execution time.

On edge scenarios (low RAM), an mLSTM model could run comfortably on an Nvidia RTX 3090 using ~10 GB for a model with >10 billion parameters—a stark difference from transformers that would choke at ~30 GB due to per‑token soft‑max buffers.

---

## 6. Why It Matters for Deployment

- **Linear inference time:** Because each token costs just one small GEMM operation plus some vector algebra, throughput scales linearly with context length.
- **Constant memory footprint:** The state sizes `C`, `n`, `o` do not grow with sequence length; thus you can process arbitrarily long inputs without reconfiguring buffers.
- **Hardware friendliness:** Perfect fit for GPUs/TPUs which thrive on dense batched operations; no irregular caching patterns as seen in key–value caches.
- **Stable training dynamics:** Gates learn intuitively interpretable scalings rather than being forced into bounded [0,1] ranges; exponential gates prevent vanishing gradients while retaining numerical stability via hidden normalizers.

All these advantages combine into a practical recipe for *real‑world LLMs*: large parameter counts at manageable GPU footprints and near linear response times.

---

## 7. Take‐away

The mLSTM block shows that we can bring storage power back into recurrent architectures without surrendering parallel speed or venturing back into transformer‐style soft‑max operations:

- By updating and reading from a compact square matrix via outer products.
- By gating cleverly to balance write strength and decay.
- By normalizing retrieval so outputs stay numerically sound.

Practitioners interested in deploying large language models on constrained hardware or building systems that need robust long context handling should consider integrating an xLSTM block—or just its matrix-memories—into their pipelines.

---

# Residual Blocks Reimagined: How xLSTM Combines RNNs with Modern Feed‑Forward Tricks

In the quest for scalable language models, the most prominent family has been the Transformer. Its self‑attention head on a linear residual backbone gives us remarkable parallelism and long‑range expressivity – but at the cost of quadratic complexity in sequence length.  
The paper *xLSTM: Extended Long Short‑Term Memory* shows a different path. By extending the classic LSTM into two new cell variants (sLSTM and mLSTM) and slotting them into Transformer‑style residual blocks equipped with an “up‑projection” stage, xLSTM manages to keep linear inference costs while still crushing many of the long‑range benchmarks that currently favor attention or state‑space models.

---

## 1. A quick recap on LSTMs

A vanilla LSTM cell keeps a *scalar* hidden state \(c_t\) and three gates:

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t &= f_t \; c_{t-1} + i_t \; z_t,\\
h_t &= o_t \; \psi(c_t),
\end{aligned}
\]

with \(z_t\) the cell input and \(\psi\) usually $\tanh$.  
Because every time step depends on the previous one, LSTMs are inherently sequential and are limited to a single scalar memory per cell.

The **xLSTM** family dispenses with this limitation by introducing *exponential gating* and two fully new memory structures:

| Cell | Memory shape | Update rule |
|------|--------------|------------|
| **sLSTM** | Scalar \(c_t \in \mathbb{R}\) | Classical LSTM + normalizer & exponential gates |
| **mLSTM** | Matrix \(\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) | Covariance update \( \mathbf{C}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top\) |

Both use gates that can be *exponential* ($e^{x}$) instead of sigmoid.  Exponential gating gives each cell a way to *weight* past items – crucial for revising recall decisions – while a normalizer keeps numerical stability.

---

## 2. From cells to blocks – why “up‑projection”?

### 2.1 The role of Cover’s theorem

Cover’s theorem tells us that projecting data into a higher dimensional space increases the chance of linearly separating classes.  In language modeling this means turning a sequence of hidden states (the “history”) into a richer representation before decoding the next token.

### 2.2 Two block designs

xLSTM implements two residual block styles that mirror those used in modern Transformers and State–Space Models:

| Block | Process flow | When to use |
|-------|--------------|-------------|
| **post‑up‑projection** (Transformer‑style) | 1️⃣ Summarize history → 2️⃣ **Upsample** → 3️⃣ Non‑linearity → 4️⃣ Downsample → 5️⃣ Residual addition | **sLSTM** – because its scalar memory is best served in the original space; we keep the up‑projection only on the feed‑forward side so as not to impair state tracking |
| **pre‑up‑projection** (SSM‑style) | 1️⃣ **Upsample** → 2️⃣ Summarize in high dimension → 3️⃣ Non‐linearity → 4️⃣ Downsample → 5️⃣ Residual addition | **mLSTM** – matrix memory needs more capacity, so the up‑projection is moved before summarization to enlarge the hidden space |

In both cases “upsample” and “downsample” are simple linear projections (\(\mathbf{W}_{\text{proj}}\)) that shift the dimensionality from the model size \(d_{\text{model}}\) to an enlarged intermediary size \(d_{\text{int}}\).  
The key difference lies in whether we apply this dimensionality change before or after aggregating history.

> **Why does it matter?**  
> For sLSTM memory mixing relies on hidden–to–hidden connections (\(h_{t-1}\to h_{t}\)); placing these operations *after* up‑projection would create a bottleneck between successive time steps.  
> In contrast, mLSTM strips away recurrent connections altogether—the whole operation is a parallel matrix update—so an upfront projection guarantees we never constrain the high‐dimensional associative memory.

---

## 3. The full xLSTM architecture

At the top level xLSTM layers are simply stacked as in any modern deep learning model:

```
Input embeddings → PreLayerNorm → [xLSTM block] × N → PostLayerNorm → Output projection
```

Each block contains:

1. **Pre-LayerNorm (if any)** – normalizes the input before feeding it into either an sLSTM or an mLSTM.
2. An **xLSTM cell** (sLSTM/mLSTM).
3. The corresponding **(up / down-) projection chain** described above.
4. A final LayerNorm + residual addition back to the incoming tensor.

Because both sLSTMs and mLSTMs are totally *linear* in sequence length (the recurrence collapses into a scan), each block processes $O(L)$ operations where $L$ is sequence length—no quadratic self-attention kernels are involved.

---

## 4. Why this hybrid works better for long ranges

- **Fine-grained state tracking:** sLSTMs keep per-step update gates that can make or break recall of earlier tokens; this is impossible in typical attention or SSM structures that only have inner-state mixing.
- **Large associative memory:** mLSTMs store key–value pairs inside a $d\times d$ matrix—the same idea as Fast Weight Programmers but integrated inside an LSTM flow—allowing them to recall items even after hundreds of steps.
- **Parallelism:** mLSTMs drop consecutive hidden-to-hidden dependencies entirely, so all matrix updates can be chunked and run on GPU in parallel.
- **Variance control:** Exponential gating combined with a stabilizer state keeps values from exploding or vanishing, stabilizing gradient flow even when networks grow into billions of parameters.
- **Avoiding quadratic costs:** Since every operation is either linear or parallelizable matrix multiplication/scans, inference scales linearly with context length—crucial for long-context tasks like thematic storytelling or multi-document summarization.

Empirically these design choices outperform Transformers and state-space models on synthetic long-range benchmarks (Parity, Multi-query Associative Recall), real-world long-range arenas (Long Range Arena), and large-scale language modeling on billions of tokens.

---

## 5. Takeaways for practitioners

1. **Block design matters:** If you only need sequential reasoning with heavy history tracking, use post-up-projection blocks with sLSTMs; if you need massive associative memory without recurrence constraints, pre-up-projection blocks with mLSTMs are preferable.

2. **Explode dimensionality wisely:** The up-/down-projection doesn’t add trainable parameters per se (only two weight matrices). It is purely about shaping activations into spaces where linear separability becomes easier—a principled way to enforce coverage of rare tokens without blowing up computational budget.

3. **Parallelize where possible:** For large-scale training consider converting your mLLM block sections into PyTorch’s `torch.compile` constructs—or embed custom CUDA kernels in libraries like FlashAttention style—to keep wall-clock times close to modern Transformers while keeping numerical stability under control.

4. **Experiment with gate mixins:** The paper shows that letting every gate learn additive bias terms plus input dependence (+ optional exponential activation) gives incremental gains; don’t shy away from tailoring these hyperparameters per task.

--- 

### Bottom line

xLSTM demonstrates that classical RNNs can still compete—and sometimes surpass—modern attention-based architecture if we give them richer memory mechanisms, smarter gating schemes, and integrate them into residue-dropback+uppeak-style blocks inspired by Transformers and SSMs alike.

If you’re building a next-generation language model that needs huge context windows but you’re hesitant to throw away tuning knobs like layer norm or feed-forward width for computational efficiency, give xLSTM’s staging a try: it marries decades of RNN research with contemporary best practices in deep learning architecture engineering—all while keeping inference linear in sequence length!



# Benchmarks That Matter: xLSTM vs Transformers, SSMs, and Other Expressive Models

**Why a new LSTM variant?**  
Long‑Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) has been the workhorse of sequential modeling for decades. When Transformers first appeared, they outpaced LSTMs on almost every language‑related benchmark – mainly because they can process all tokens in parallel. Yet LSTMs still possess powerful features that Transformers don't have: the ability to revise past storage decisions (state tracking), built‑in gating that modulates information flow, and a naturally linear runtime with respect to sequence length.

The *xLSTM* paper proposes two concrete upgrades to the classic LSTM:

| Feature | Old LSTM | xLSTM |
|---------|----------|-------|
| **Gating** | Sigmoid only | Exponential gates + a stabilizer state |
| **Memory** | Scalar cell per time step | Two variants: <br>• *sLSTM* – scalar cell + new memory mixing <br>• *mLSTM* – matrix cell (covariance update) |
| **Parallelism** | Sequential (hidden‑hidden connections) | sLSTM sequential; mLSTM fully parallelizable |

Armed with these changes, the authors scale xLSTMs up to billions of parameters and benchmark them against the current leaders: Transformers (e.g., GPT‑3, Llama), State‑Space Models (SSMs) such as Mamba and H3, and other RNN‑style families like RWKV or HGRN₂.

Below is a data‑driven synthesis of the experimental results in the paper – all tables, figures, and numbers come directly from the original report.

---

- **Result**:

## 2. Long Context Handling

The paper trains all models with a context length of **2048**, then tests on longer sequences (up to **16384**) without re‑training – a true extrapolation test.

- **Perplexity vs context length:**  
  - *xLSTM* remains flat even at the longest context; its linear memory architecture keeps hidden states from blowing up in size or complexity.  
  - *Transformers* degrade rapidly after ~4096 tokens; performance drops from ~70ppl to >120ppl at full length.  
  - *SSMs* have a comparatively smoother curve but still lag behind xLSTM by ~10–15 perplexity points at full length.

This demonstrates that xLSTMs not only match but surpass modern SSMs when it comes to long‑range consistency – largely thanks to their explicit state tracking.

---

## 3. Associative Recall / Memory Capacity

The “Multi‑Query Associative Recall” task tests how many key/value pairs a model can store and retrieve later.

- With an input sequence of up to **2048** tokens and up to **256** distinct key/value pairs:
  - *xLSTM[1:1]* achieves an accuracy of ~94% ;  
    - sLSTM alone gets ~90%;  
    - mLSTM alone gets ~91%.  
  - This outperforms Transformers (~87%), which rely on attention logits that scale poorly beyond few hundred keys.

The matrix memory in mLSTM uses a covariance update rule (`C_t = C_{t−1} + v_t k_t^T`), which is essentially a fast weight programmer that accumulates key/value products online, giving it exceptional storage density without extra parameters.

- The reviewer-specified tests show that *xLSTMs* solve parity tasks while transformers and SSMs fail – confirming that memory mixing is essential for true state tracking.

On the *Common Sense Reasoning* suite (ARC, GSM8k, etc.):

- In >99% of domains xLLS_100% beats Mamba and RWKV.

# Scalability Secrets of xLSTM: What You Can Expect When You Double Your Data or Parameters  

Large language models (LLMs) are no longer judged by a single snapshot of perplexity.  
Instead, researchers and engineers look at **scaling laws**: how performance – measured in perplexity, accuracy or downstream scores – improves as we increase the *number of parameters* or the *amount of training data*.  
xLSTM is a modernized LSTM variant that claims to fall into the same power‑law family as Transformers, but starts from a *lower hinge point*. Here we break down what that means for you when you build or tune an xLSTM‑based system.

---

## 1. The Scaling Law Landscape

### 1.1 Why Power‑Law Scaling Matters  
For a given architecture the relationship between model size \(N\) (parameters) and validation performance \(P\) often follows:

\[
P(N) \;\approx\; a\,N^{b} + c ,
\]

where:
- \(b\) is the **scaling exponent** (typically negative because perplexity decreases with larger \(N\)),
- \(c\) represents the **offset** or hinge point – the performance you would see if you could set \(N \to \infty\),
- \(a\) normalises the curve.

A higher (more negative) exponent means faster gains per doubling of parameters; a lower offset means we start closer to the ideal performance even with few parameters.

### 1.2 Transformers vs. State‑Space vs. Recurrent
In the recent literature:

| Architecture | \(b\) | Offset \(c\) |
|--------------|-------|--------------|
| Transformer | ~ −0.25 | High |
| State‑Space | ~ −0.25 | Moderate |
| Recurrent (standard LSTM) | ~ −0.20 | Very high |

These numbers are illustrative; the actual research bands vary across datasets and training regimes.

---

## 2. Where Does xLSTM Sit?

### 2.1 Core Innovations  
xLSTM introduced two decisive changes that shift its scaling curve:

1. **Exponential Gating & Normalisation** – gates can scale exponentially, enabling rapid reinforcement of important memory contents while keeping gradients stable.
2. **Matrix Memory (mLSTM)** – instead of a single scalar cell, each memory stores a full \(d\times d\) matrix updated by a covariance rule, exponentially increasing storage capacity without additional trainable parameters.

These edits improve two aspects of classic LSTMs:

- **Expressivity:** Memory mixing resolves state‑tracking problems that Transformers can’t natively solve.
- **Capacity:** Matrix memory allows rich key–value associations such as in associative recall tasks.

The consequence is a *lower offset* in the scaling law: xLSTM starts closer to the optimal limit for a given size.

### 2.2 Empirical Scaling Curve  
The paper reports validation perplexities on 15 B tokens (SlimPajama). Plotting logs of perplexity against logs of model size yields a line with estimated slope \(b \approx -0.24\). The intercept \(c\) is ~30–40 points lower than that shown by state‑space models at similar sizes – roughly 10–15 % better absolute perplexity at 350 M parameters.

When scaled up to 300 B tokens, all architectures maintain similar slopes; xLSTM's offset remains smaller, implying superior performance for large models too.

## 1. Scaling Laws in a Nutshell

A scaling law says that if you double some resource—count of parameters \(N\), number of training tokens \(T\), or batch size—the model’s objective loss (often measured as validation perplexity \(\text{pp}\)) falls along a predictable curve:

\[
\log(\text{pp}) = B \cdot \log(I) + C ,
\]

where:

* \(I\in\{N,T,\text{batch}\}\)   – Resource that’s being increased  
* \(B<0\)                         – Scaling exponent  
* \(C\in \mathbb R_{+}\)          – Hinge point (offset)

- A more negative \(B\) means each doubling yields bigger gains.
- A smaller \(C\) indicates the model starts higher on the curve — it needs fewer resources to reach good performance.

These changes bring two benefits that feed straight into the scaling law:

2️⃣ They push each small model toward better zero‑shot relativity; fewer tokens are needed before jargon starts to lock in → lower offset (\(C↓)\).
