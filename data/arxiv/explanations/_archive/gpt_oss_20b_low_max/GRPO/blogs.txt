# Mining Math from the Web: How DeepSeekMath Built a 120 B‑Token Mathematical Corpus

Mathematicians and language‑model developers alike have long suspected that the internet holds a vast, untapped reservoir of mathematical knowledge. The trick is to separate the useful signal from the noise. DeepSeekMath offers a concrete answer: a data‑driven pipeline that turned raw Common Crawl into a 120 billion‑token corpus of high‑quality mathematical text. In this post we walk through the key stages that make this possible, from first‑principle filtering with fastText classifiers to a multi‑iteration refinement loop and careful de‑duplication.

---

## 1. Why *Common Crawl*?

Common Crawl (CC) is an open dump of everything indexed by search engines: web pages in dozens of languages, constantly updated, and often rich in technical content. Compared to curated datasets like OpenWebMath (~13 B tokens) or Mathematical Pile (~9 B tokens), CC offers orders of magnitude more data—but without any built‑in relevance filter.

**Challenge**: *How do you sift through billions of web pages and keep only the genuinely math‑heavy ones?*

---

## 2. Seeding the Search: FastText as an “Embeddor”

The DeepSeekMath team started from a small yet high‑quality seed set—OpenWebMath. This 13‑billion‑token collection contained well‑structured examples of math prose, problem statements, proofs, and code snippets that solve equations.

With these positive examples in hand, they trained a **fastText** binary classification model:

| Hyperparameter | Value |
|----------------|-------|
| Vector dim     | 256   |
| learning rate | 0.1   |
| n‑gram max    | 3     |
| min word freq | 3     |
| epochs        | 3     |

The model learns quickly how math text looks in terms of surface statistics—word co‑occurrence patterns, presence of symbols like `∑`, `√`, or language such as *calculate* or *prove*.

---

## 3. Cross-Referencing the Web

### 3.1 Deduplication

Raw CC contains redundant crawls of the same page across multiple snapshots. A first pass uses **URL-based deduplication**: all URLs are hashed and duplicates removed, reducing the corpus from ~200 B pages to about 40 B.

Next comes **near‑deduplication** (e.g., duplicate content with slight URL changes), implemented via hashing blocks of text and discarding near-duplicates.

Result: a clean pool ready for reverse retrieval.

### 3.2 Recall with FastText

The trained classifier scans each remaining page and outputs a confidence score indicating how math-like its content appears. They rank pages by this score and keep only the top tokens needed for training.

After the first run they keep about **40 B tokens**—a manageable subset that still covers most high-quality math sites (mathoverflow.net, arXiv.org front pages, Mathematics Stack Exchange).

---

## 4. Iterative Refinement Loop

The initial fastText model is biased toward its training data—the OpenWebMath seed—which is relatively narrow compared to the pan­-global web. To broaden coverage:

1. **Domain Clustering**  
   Web pages are grouped by domain (same base URL). For each domain they compute how many pages were captured in the first pass.
2. **Thresholding & Manual Review**  
   Domains where >10 % of pages were captured are flagged as math\-related (e.g., `math.stackexchange.com`). The team then manually reviews URLs within those domains for hidden math content.
3. **Seed Expansion**  
   Newly identified math pages are added to the seed set; fastText is re‑trained on this enriched set.
4. **Repeat**  
   Steps 3–4 are repeated four times.

Outcome: After four iterations they amass **35.5 M distinct math pages**, totalling roughly **120 B tokens**—almost seven times earlier open-source corpora like Minerva’s (~17 B).

The iterative loop ensures no “niche” math domains slip through; each pass brings fresh vocabulary and richer markup styles into consideration.

---

## 5. Content Filtering & Quality Control

Even after recalling efficient LLMs can hallucinate or use references not present in training data during evaluation checkpoints (benchmark contamination). To guard against that:

* Any text chunk containing a literal *10-gram* match with any evaluation benchmark (e.g., GSM8K or MATH) is removed.
* Shorter passages (<10 grams but ≥3 grams) are also filtered using exact string matching.

This step guarantees that the model’s high scores on MATH or GSM8K are earned purely from reasoning, not memorization.

---

## 6. Multilingual Richness

Unlike many math corpora that focus almost exclusively on English, DeepSeekMath’s pipeline pulls language data automatically from CC’s multi-lingual coverage:

* The top two languages (English & Chinese) dominate token counts but many other languages contribute rarer terms.
* This multilingual makeup has direct benefits: on Chinese benchmarks such as CMATH or Gaokao-MathQA, DeepSeekMath outperforms models trained on English-only corpora.

The approach demonstrates how a generic browsing index can be repurposed for highly domain‐specific needs without linguistic bias.

---

## 7. From Corpus to Model Performance

Training on this giant corpus yields impressive downstream gains:

| Benchmark          | Baseline Score | Score After Training |
|--------------------|----------------|---------------------|
| GSM8K             | ~75 %          | **82–88 %**         |
| MATH (competition)| ~35–36 %       | **51–52 %**         |
| CMATH             | ~70–71 %       | **88–89 %**         |

Notably:
* A 7 B parameter model trained on DeepSeekMath performs comparably to Minerva’s closed-source 540 B model.
* Code training prior to math training further boosts reasoning ability—reinforcing the long-held belief that coding practices sharpen logical decomposability.

---

## 8. Takeaway: Why This Pipeline Works

1. **Data Quality over Size** – The fastText classifier selectively curates high-quality samples rather than blindly feeding every page.
2. **Iterative Anchor Expansion** – Manual seed augmentation kills ambiguity early on so that subsequent recalls become more representative.
3. **De-duplication Hygiene** – Removing near duplicates prevents redundancy from drowning out new signals.
4. **Benchmark Decontamination** – Guarding against leakage keeps evaluation honest.
5. **Multilingual Richness** – Capturing diverse lexicons improves performance across languages without extra engineering.

These design choices collectively transform Common Crawl—from massive raw HTML noise—to an engineered goldmine specifically suited for training mathematically astute language models.

---

## Final Thoughts

Building such a corpus feels like mining for gold hidden beneath layers of sand—algorithms must sift violently through noise before uncovering precious nuggets of structured knowledge.

For researchers aspiring to build open-source models rivaling GPT‑4 on mathematical tasks, DeepSeekMath’s pipeline offers both a practical blueprint and evidence that quality data can offset raw scale limitations—a lesson for all domains beyond mathematics as well.

# Why Code First Yields Better Math: The Surprising Impact of Code Training on Mathematical Reasoning

Mathematical reasoning has long been a hard‑to‑match domain for open‑source language models. The latest study on DeepSeekMath reveals a counter‑intuitive but powerful insight: **starting a training pipeline with code first, and then adding math tokens, gives a 7 B model the edge it needs to tackle the competition‑level MATH benchmark**. In this post we unpack the experimental design, tease apart the two‑stage and one‑stage training regimes, and explain why code training makes all of math easier.

## 1. What “code first” really means

Before diving into the numbers, let’s clarify the two training paradigms that were compared:

| Paradigm | Steps |
|----------|-------|
| **Two‑stage** | 1️⃣ Pre‑train on *code* tokens (≈ 400 B) → 2️⃣ Fine‑tune on *math* tokens (≈ 150 B) |
| **One‑stage** | Train simultaneously on a *mixture* of code tokens (≈ 400 B) and math tokens (≈ 150 B) |

Both pipelines start from the same base: DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5, a 7 B model that already knows how to read and write code. The only variable is *when* the mathematical part is introduced.

## 2. The key experiments

The experiments were run on two problem families:

1. **Pure reasoning** – solving questions without any external tools, i.e., just generate the answer in text.
2. **Tool‑assisted reasoning** – writing short Python snippets that can be executed with libraries like `math` or `sympy`.

For each family we measured accuracy on two classic datasets:

- **GSM8K** – 8th‑grade math word problems
- **MATH** – competition‑level graduate‐style questions

### Two‑stage results

| Stage | GSM8K (CoT) | MATH (CoT) | GSM8K (PoT) | MATH (PoT) |
|-------|-------------|------------|-------------|------------|
| Code only (400 B) | 72.3 % | 40.1 % | 83.5 % | 58.3 % |
| +Math (150 B)    | **83.6 %** | **63.4 %** | **88.2 %** | **68.7 %** |

Key takeaways:

- Even before math training, code exposure elevates tool usage performance dramatically (e.g., GSM8K PoT jumps from 83.5 to 88.2 %).
- Adding math tokens yields substantial gains across both reasoning styles, especially on MATH where accuracy climbs from 40 % to over 60 %.

### One‑stage results

| Mix ratio | GSM8K (CoT) | MATH (CoT) |
|-----------|-------------|------------|
| Code + Math (450/150 B mix) | ~70‐75 % | ~42‐45 % |
| All code only               | ~66 %   | ~38 %   |
| All math only               | ~68 %   | ~44 %   |

When code and math tokens are combined in a single training stage, the performance falls back closer to “all code” or “all math”. The model struggles to reconcile these two very different signal distributions at once.

### Why does mixing break things?

A smaller model (1.3 B in the paper’s scaling study) can’t fully absorb both kinds of data simultaneously when trained together. Think of it as trying to learn calculus *and* imperative programming at exactly the same time without any pause; the model’s capacity gets stretched thin, leading to catastrophic forgetting of one domain or noisy representations in both.

In contrast, a staged approach lets the model first build a strong “code mindset” that implicitly learns useful abstractions—control flow, variables, type inference—that mirror mathematical logic structures. Then when math tokens arrive, they’re absorbed into an already primed symbolic reasoning scaffold.

## 3. The mechanistic motivation behind “code first”

The authors put forward several insights that help explain why code primes mathematical ability:

1. **Formal syntax as an intermediate grammar**  
   Code is one of the few formal languages that belongs to both programming and mathematics families: it’s text, but its syntax enforces strict rules similar to algebraic equations (e.g., `if`, `for`, `while`). This tight structure trains the model’s token predictor to respect constraints that are also present in mathematical expressions.

2. **Implicit variable binding and scope awareness**  
   Programming teaches handling references (`x += 1`) and scope (`function(x){...}`), which parallels variable substitution in algebraic manipulations or induction proofs.

3. **Execution as a proof-of-concept tool**  
   Even in pure reasoning experiments the presence of code knowledge helps because during training many prompt contexts involve executing short snippets—so the model is already familiar with entering code blocks correctly and knowing which libraries are available.

4. **Memory efficiency during reinforcement learning**  
   The paper’s Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm removed the need for an expensive critic network by estimating advantages over groups of sampled outputs—an advantage that aligns well with models that have learned efficient token dynamics from code tasks.

## 4. Resulting performance improvement

Putting all these pieces together we see why DeepSeekMath–Instruct eventually pips out most open‑source peers:

- **Without tools:** >50 % accuracy on MATH
- **With tools:** ~60 % accuracy on MATH
- **Competitive with closed‑source rivals** such as GPT‑4 / Gemini Ultra for a fraction of the parameter budget

These achievements would be impossible if we had started from a generic pretrained language model without any code pretraining component.

## 5. Practical takeaway for practitioners

If you’re building an LLM aimed at solving mathematical problems—especially at graduate or competition level—consider this recipe:

1. **Begin with a robust code-trained backbone**  
   Models such as DeepSeek-Coder are perfect candidates; they already know how to parse and produce syntactically correct snippets.
2. **Add mathematical data in a separate stage**  
   Keep your math token pool large enough (~120B tokens from Common Crawl) but train it after you’ve saturated your code exposure.
3. **Use reinforcement learning post tuning with group-level rewards**  
   GRPO or PPO—any method that benefits from pairwise or comparative rewards—will amplify gains, especially when you have clean step-side tool rewards.
4. **Keep an eye on capacity constraints**  
   For smaller scales (<10B parameters), avoid merging all domains together; otherwise you may lose half your gains.

## 6. Final thoughts

The “code first” finding underscores a broader lesson: *structured generative tasks share much more than surface form.* A language model trained to obey strict syntax rules in programming automatically acquires a significant portion of what it needs to freely rearrange mathematically meaningful symbols later on.

So if you ever wondered whether starting with something seemingly unrelated—code—could make an LLM smarter at math, look no further than DeepSeekMath’s results: yes, it does—and it does so dramatically enough to bring a toy-sized open‑source model close to closed‐source giants on some of the toughest benchmarks today.

# From Rules to Models: The Evolution of Reward Signals in LLM Fine‑Tuning

Large‑language models (LLMs) can answer trivia with a few tokens, but solving a multi‑step math problem requires a *training signal* that tells the model **which intermediate steps lead to a correct answer**. The evolution from a simple “did the final answer match the ground truth?” checkbox to a rich neural reward model that can grade every reasoning step is what makes DeepSeekMath’s reinforcement‑learning (RL) stage so powerful.

Below we trace that journey, explain the mechanics of each reward design, and show why *process* supervision – rewarding each reasoning step – gives the model a sharper sense of what “good reasoning” looks like.

---

## 1. Rule‑Based Rewards: The Invisible “Finish Line”

The earliest fine‑tuning stages still rely on **rule‑based correctness checks**:

| Stage | Data | Reward | How it works |
|-------|------|--------|--------------|
| Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT) | Human‑verified question–answer pairs | Implicit (teacher signal) | Model learns to imitate high‑quality demonstrations. |
| Rejection Sampling Fine‑Tuning (RFT) | Samples from SFT policy | Binary: 1 if correct, 0 otherwise | Only outputs that pass a *rule* (e.g., exact match with reference answer) are retained. |
| Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) | Human preferences (pairs of outputs) | Log₂ likelihood ratio between chosen and rejected pair | The model is nudged toward outputs that human annotators prefer. |

In each case, the *reward* is essentially derived from whether the **final string** matches the correct answer. That’s great for single‑step tasks or when you only care about the end state, but nowhere near enough for a chain of logical deductions.

> **Why it fails for multi‑step math**  
> A correct final answer can be reached through many different ways—some elegant and general, others accidental. Rule‑based rewards do not penalize a wrong intermediate step that happens to “coincide” with the right answer later in the chain. They also provide zero feedback on why an answer is wrong.

---

## 2. Neural Reward Models: Quantifying Whole Answers

To move beyond binary correctness checks, DeepSeekMath’s RL setup introduces **neural reward models** trained on *comparisons* between two candidate answers to the same question. They learn a scalar score \( r_\varphi(q,o) \) that reflects overall quality.

### 2.1 Training the Reward Model

1. **Collect Pairs**: For a set of questions \( Q \), sample two outputs \( o^{(1)} \) and \( o^{(2)} \) from an older policy.
2. **Label by Preference**: Human annotators or heuristic rules decide which output is better.
3. **Optimize**: Train \( r_\varphi \) to assign higher scores to preferred outputs while maintaining calibration across questions.

Once trained, \( r_\varphi \) can evaluate any fresh output without human intervention.

### 2.2 Integrating Into PPO / GRPO

- **PPO**: Uses both rewards and an *action value function* \( V_\psi \). The advantage estimate
  \[
  A_t = G_t - V_\psi(\cdot)
  \]
  where \( G_t \) aggregates terminal rewards.
- **GRPO**: Drops the value function entirely; instead, it samples *groups* of outputs for the same question:
  \[
  \hat{A}_{i,t} = \frac{r_i - \bar{r}}{\sigma}
  \]
  i.e., each token receives an advantage equal to its group‑normalized reward.

The key difference is that both methods now *weight* each token—not just the last one—according to how good or bad its corresponding output is judged overall.

---

## 3. Process Supervision: “Reward at Every Step”

Even with neural rewards over full answers, RL only sees what happens *after* all reasoning is finished. For problems that involve dozens of symbolic manipulations or logical assertions, this one‑shot signal may still misguide promising substeps.

#### What Process Supervision Adds

- **Step‑level Scoring**: Instead of one reward at token end, we assign a reward at every logical step boundary (e.g., after each equation):
  ```text
  Q: "Solve x^2 + 5x + 6 = 0."
  
  Step1: Factor → "(x+2)(x+3)=0"
      → reward r_1
  Step2: Set bracket → "x+2=0" or "x+3=0"
      → reward r_2
  Step3: Solve each → "x=-2" or "x=-3"
      → reward r_3
  ```
- **Normalization** within each group ensures tokens at all steps get proportional advantages—true errors are punished early rather than only at the finish line.

#### Mathematical Formulation

For a question \( q \), let  
- \( G = \{o_1,\dots,o_G\} \) be sampled outputs,
- \( R_i = [r_{i}^{(1)},\dots,r_{i}^{(K_i)}] \) be rewards per step,
- Normalized rewards:  
  \[
    \tilde{r}_{i}^{(k)} = \frac{r_{i}^{(k)} - {\rm mean}(R)}{{\rm std}(R)}
  \]
Then for any token within step \( k \), its advantage becomes:
\[
\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{\ell=k}^{K_i} \tilde{r}_{i}^{(\ell)}
\]
Thus early mistakes reduce later advantages—a natural way to back‑propagate error through time.

---

## 4. Why Process Rewards Make All the Difference

Empirical findings in DeepSeekMath’s RL experiments reveal:

| Training Stage | Metric | Improvement |
|----------------|--------|-------------|
| SFT only | GSM8K CoT accuracy | ~66% |
| + Offline RFT (rule + binary reward) | GSM8K CoT accuracy | +~6% |
| + GRPO with outcome supervision (neural global reward) | GSM8K CoT accuracy | +~9% |
| + GRPO with process supervision (stepwise neural reward) | GSM8K CoT accuracy | +~5% |

The gain from process supervision may seem modest compared to global outcome supervision—but consider its qualitative impact:

1. **Guides Intermediate Reasoning**  
   Tokens that contribute directly to an incorrect algebraic simplification receive negative gradients earlier, preventing error propagation.
2. **Encourages Chunked Solutions**  
   The model learns to segment its answer into meaningful steps resembling human mathematical thinking.
3. **Robustness to Hallucinations**  
   Since each step is evaluated individually, hallucinations that would otherwise slip through a global check are quickly caught and corrected.

---

## 5. Unified View: From Data Source → Reward → Policy Update

One useful lens is:

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}(\theta)
= 
\E_{(q,o)\sim D}
\Bigg[
\frac{1}{|o|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|o|}
GC(q,o,t,\pi_{\text{rf}})
\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_\theta(o_t|q,o_{<t})
\Bigg]
\]

- **Data Source \(D\)**  
   *Offline:* fixed dataset from SFT; *Online:* exploring new samples during RL.
- **Reward Function \(GC(\cdot)\)**  
   Converts raw scores into gradient coefficients—either binary (`RFT`) or continuous (`GRPO`, `Process`).
- **Algorithm**  
   PPO/GRPO dictate how often we clamp probabilities (`clip`) and how we compute advantages (`A_t`).

DeepSeekMath evolves this pipeline step by step:

1. Start with rule‐based `GC` from RFT – heavy clipping needed.
2. Replace `GC` with neural rewards – smoother gradients.
3. Move from `GC` per output (`outcome`) to per step (`process`) – richer signals.
4. Optimize over online sampled data batches instead of static SFT data – better generalization.

---

## 6. Takeaways for Anyone Building an LLM Research Flow

- **Don’t stop at final correctness:** Multi-step tasks need intermediate supervision; otherwise models learn “shortcut banners.”
- **Neural rewards beat binary rules:** Once you can judge quality relative between pairs rather than against a hard threshold, you unlock continuum learning.
- **Process supervision is cheap and effective:** Adding a few more forward passes per sample gives access to fine-grained feedback without complex new architecture.
- **Online sampling scales better:** The policy itself generates more diverse challenges as it improves—this feeds back into higher quality rewards.

By stepping through this evolution—from simple rule‑based picks to structured neural signals—the DeepSeekMath team demonstrates how carefully engineered reward designs can transform an open-source math model’s performance into competitive territory with closed‑source giants like GPT‑4 and Gemini Ultra.

> Next time you’re training an LLM on physics or biology problems, experiment with process‑level rewards—you’ll likely see your model not just get the right number at the end but *understand* why it’s right along every interesting intermediate checkpoint!



# Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO): A Memory‑Efficient RL Breakthrough for Math LLMs  

Mathematics remains one of the toughest areas for large language models.  Even when an open‑source model reaches the 50 % mark on the competition‑level MATH benchmark or handles most GSM8K questions, a new frontier has emerged: **reinforcement learning (RL)**.  Traditional RL methods for LLMs, like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), bring heavyweight memory demands because they require a separate *critic* to estimate the value of every token.  The recently released *Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)* sidesteps this bottleneck and delivers measurable gains on two of the biggest math benchmarks while making RL practical for 7 B‑parameter models.

---

## Why RL Matters for Math LLMs  

1. **Fine‑grained supervision** – Pure supervised fine‑tuning uses only labeled examples, but many mathematical problems can be answered with multiple plausible steps.  
2. **Reward consistency** – RL can align a model’s outputs with a reward model trained on comparisons (“Answer A is better than Answer B”).  
3. **Generalization to hard cases** – When the training data does not cover every type of problem, RL can push the policy toward stronger solutions without needing additional human annotations.

After training DeepSeekMath‑Instruct 7B—an instruction‑tuned base with deep mathematical reasoning—researchers applied PPO and saw noticeable improvements.  However, PPO’s *actor‑critic* architecture doubled GPU memory usage and slowed training by an order of magnitude.

---

## The Core Idea Behind GRPO  

> **Replace the critic with a group‑based baseline**.

Instead of learning to predict a value function \(V_\psi\) for each token (the classic actor‑critic trick), GRPO samples *multiple* outputs per question and uses their average reward as a baseline:

- For a question \(q\), sample \(G\) outputs \(o_1,\dots,o_G\) from the current policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  
- Compute rewards \(r_1,\dots,r_G\) using a trained reward model \(r_\varphi\).  
- Normalize these rewards by subtracting the group mean and dividing by the standard deviation.  
- The *advantage* at every token in an output is simply this normalized reward:
  \[
  \hat{A}_{i,t}= \widetilde{r}_i
  \]
  where \(\widetilde{r}_i=\frac{r_i-\text{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\text{std}(\mathbf r)}\).

The policy optimizer then maximizes the PPO surrogate objective using \(\hat{A}_{i,t}\) instead of value‑based advantages.  This eliminates the need to train and store a separate critic network.

---

## How GRPO Cuts Memory Overhead  

| Feature | PPO | GRPO |
|---------|-----|-------|
| Critic? | Yes (≈ model size) | No |
| Baseline estimation | Learned \(V_\psi\) per token | Group mean over > 64 outputs |
| GPU memory | Roughly double that required for pure policy updates | Same as pure policy updates |
| Training time per step | Longer (back‑prop through critic) | Faster (one forward pass per token) |

With GRPO, a batch that would have required ~16 GB just for its critic now fits comfortably on a single A100 GPU, enabling researchers to run multi–turn RL training cycles on models as large as DeepSeekMath-RL 7B.

---

## Concrete Gains on Algebraic Benchmarks  

The paper’s evaluation data show:

| Model        | GSM8K (CoT %) | MATH (CoT %) |
|--------------|---------------|--------------|
| DeepSeekMath‑Instruct 7B | 82.9 %  | 46.8 %   |
| DeepSeekMath‑RL 7B (GRPO)      | **88.2 %** (+5.3 pp) | **51.7 %** (+4.9 pp) |

Those numbers mean that *every* problem instance in GSM8K has more than five times higher chance of hitting the correct solution after just one round of GRPO RL fine‑tuning, while MATH sees an absolute double‑digit jump—no small feat given how many other open‑source models plateau around 40 %.

Because GRPO blocks overfitting to any particular answer format (the group baseline naturally penalizes wildly incorrect responses while rewarding subtle improvements), checkpoints remain robust when you try out *process supervision*—rewarding each reasoning step instead of only final answers—and still climb higher on out‑of‑domain tasks like CMATH or Gaokao question sets.

---

## How “Group” Makes it Work

You might wonder: why does averaging over dozens of outputs give a reliable baseline? Two key properties:

1. **Comparability** – The reward model is trained on pairwise comparisons of outputs for the same question; thus its scores already form a relative ranking rather than absolute scores.
2. **Variance reduction** – Subtracting the mean eliminates common bias; dividing by the standard deviation cancels out scale effects, turning raw rewards into standardized advantage signals that fit PPO’s clipping mechanism nicely.

Mathematically this is captured in Equation (6) from the paper’s Appendix:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{GRPO}(\theta)=\mathbb E_{q,\{o_i\}}\Bigg[\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^G
    \min\!\Bigg(
          \frac{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}
               {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}\,
               \hat A_{i,t},
          \operatorname{clip}\!\big(...,\varepsilon\big)\,
               \hat A_{i,t}
    \Bigg)
    -\beta\, D_{KL}\!\big[\pi_\theta\,||\,\pi_{\text{ref}}\big]\Bigg].
\]

The only difference from classic PPO is that there's no learned value function \(V_\psi\); everything else pulls from sampling itself.

---

## Practical Tips for Implementing GRPO

1. **Select an appropriate group size** – Experiments used \(G=64\). Too few outputs yield noisy baselines; too many blow up compute without proportional gain.
2. **Use self‑consistency during sampling** – Sample diverse “chains” by varying temperature or nucleus cutoffs; this improves exploration.
3. **Update your reward model incrementally** – After each RL epoch, retrain/replay your preference model on freshly visited trajectories; otherwise it becomes stale.
4. **Add KL regularization explicitly** – In contrast to PPO’s implicit penalty inside rewards, GRPO plugs β⋅KL into the objective directly (Equation (6)), which is easier to tune and avoids reward signal corruption.

Following these steps keeps training stable even when you start from a heavily tuned instruction base like DeepSeekMath-Instruct.

---

## What Comes Next?

The paper lists several future directions that promise even more effective reinforcement learning:

- **Advanced sampling & decoding strategies** (e.g., tree search) to produce higher–quality trajectories without exploding GPU footprints.
- **Noisy‐reward robust algorithms** that can tolerate imperfect preference data—essential when scaling beyond next‐generation benchmarks.
- **Process reward models** that assess intermediate reasoning steps instead of only final answers, opening doors to truly stepwise improvement.

But even at its current form, GRPO proves that careful algorithmic simplification can unlock new frontiers: strong math reasoning at affordable compute cost.

---

## Takeaway

If you’re building or fine–tuning math-focused LLMs on commodity GPU infrastructure:

- Replace PPO by GRPO: drop half your memory budget and keep your policy updates streamlined.
- Expect +5–10 % absolute accuracy boosts on GSM8K and MATH with only a single RL loop.
- Scale up your training set gradually; even off‑distribution problems benefit from strengthened reward signals.

GRPO shows that deep math reasoning isn’t just about adding more parameters or fancy architected tokens—it’s also about smarter training procedures that let us train powerful models without choking our GPUs.

Happy training!



# Unified RL Paradigm: Understanding SFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO in One Framework

Large Language Models (LLMs) can be nudged toward better behaviour by chaining together data, reward signals, and optimisation tricks. Traditionally these approaches—Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT), Rejection‑Sampling Fine‑Tuning (RFT), Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO), Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), and the newer Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)—look quite different on the surface. In fact, they can be expressed with a single, very simple mathematical recipe.

Below we show that all of these methods share a common “gradient formula”, and we unpack the three ingredients that make any method tick:

| Ingredient | What it is | Examples in each method |
|------------|------------|-------------------------|
| **Data source** | Where we obtain question–answer pairs | Human‑written prompts for SFT; critic‑free real‑time samples for online variants |
| **Reward function** | What tells the model “good enough” or “bad” | Exact answer correctness for RFT; a learned rank‐based reward model for DPO/PPO/GRPO |
| **Algorithm** | How we convert the reward into a gradient step | Maximise log‑probability vs back‑propagated gradients |

With this lens we can see *why* GRPO reliably pulls more weight from these ingredients than its cousins.

--- 

## 1. The unified objective

All approaches modify the policy’s parameters \(\theta\) by updating it in the direction of

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}(\theta)=
  \mathbb{E}_{(q,o)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\left[
    \frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o|}
      G_{\mathcal{A}}(q,o,t)\,
      \nabla_{\theta}\!\log\pi_\theta(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})
  \right].
\tag{1}
\]

- \(q\) is a prompt/question.
- \(o=[o_1,\dots,o_{|o|}]\) is a model output.
- \(\pi_\theta\) is the language‑model policy.
- \(G_{\mathcal{A}}(\cdot)\) is a *gradient coefficient* supplied by the algorithm \(\mathcal{A}\).  
  It either hard‑codes rewards (as in SFT) or scales them according to reward model predictions (as in RL).

The beauty of this expression is that it covers every method mentioned above—just replace the data source and gradient coefficient.

---

## 2. Data Sources: offline vs online

### Offline sampling  
SFT, RFT, and DPO start from a static dataset.  
These methods **sample** from an *initial* SFT model; no feedback from an evolving policy influences the data.  
Pros: easier to reproduce, lower compute per iteration.  
Cons: once the policy changes dramatically during fine‑tuning, its training data no longer reflects its current state.

### Online sampling  
PPO/GRPO (and “Online RFT”) keep drawing samples from the *current* policy as training proceeds.  
Pros: data are always tailored to the model’s present belief state; gradients are more informative.  
Cons: requires a running inference engine each step, higher latency, and careful safety checks to avoid noisy or adversarial generations.

The paper shows that online sampling consistently beats offline versions—especially after the early stages when the policy diverges from its humble SFT ancestor.

---

## 3. Reward signals: hard rules vs learned models

### Rule‑based rewards  
RFT uses a binary indicator:
```
reward = 1 if answer correct else 0
```
No nuance about *why* a sample is good or bad—the penalty/reward magnitude is flat.

### Model‑based rewards  
PPO/GRPO use a neural reward model trained on comparative pairs `(output_a , output_b)` on *the same prompt*. It outputs a scalar score \(r(o \mid q)\).  
This gives fine‑grained feedback: an answer slightly better than another receives only a modest boost; a much richer one gets a big one.

DPO also uses pairwise ranking but implements it as an explicit loss rather than sampling over many alternatives.

GRPO taps into this by **grouping** multiple outputs per prompt and using their relative scores as baselines—an elegant way to avoid training an extra value network.

---

## 4. Algorithms: simple change of gradient coefficient

> **SFT** – Gradient coefficient is simply `±0` or `+1` depending on teacher labels:
> \[
> G_{\text{SFT}} = +1~\text{for all tokens}
> \]
>  

> **RFT** – Hard filter: discard wrong answers before back‑prop:
> \[
> G_{\text{RFT}} = +1~\text{if output correct else }0
> \]
>  

> **Online RFT** – Same logic but draws samples online:
> \[
> G_{\text{OnlineRFT}} = +1~\text{if output correct else }0
> \]
>  

> **PPO** – Uses advantage \(A_t = r_t - V_\psi(o_{<t})\):
> \[
> G_{\text{PPO}} = A_t
> \]
>  

> **GRPO** – Uses group relative advantage
> \[
> G_{\text{GRPO}}(i,t)=\tilde r_i-\bar r
> \]
> where \(\tilde r_i\) is the reward for sample \(i\), and \(\bar r\) is the mean over the group.

The key insight? GRPO replaces the value function \(V_\psi(\cdot)\) with an *explicitly computed baseline*–the mean reward across multiple outputs per question. Since all rewards come from a learnt approval model that compares pairs of outputs on the *same question*, this group baseline perfectly aligns with how those rewards were generated in the first place. Hence every token receives a truly calibrated advantage signal without extra memory overhead.

---

## 5. Why GRPO shines

1. **Efficient baseline estimation** – no extra critic network means less GPU memory and faster forward passes.
2. **Fine‑grained instructions** – because each token’s advantage equals how much better/worse its final answer was compared to others, gradient updates sub‑tokenally focus on exactly where errors happened.
3. **Robustness to noisy rewards** – comparing several alternatives dilutes outlier predictions; gradients push consistently toward better solutions even if one toy output gets mis‑scored.
4. **Better match to training data distribution** – online sampling ensures that augmented data reflect the current policy’s strengths/weaknesses; GRPO’s relative baseline follows suit.

In practice, DeepSeekMath’s authors observed a jump from ~46 % to ~52 % on MATH after GRPO—even though they only used instruction data for reinforcement learning! When compared with PPO, GRPO was about half the memory footprint while delivering similar magnitude gains.

---

## 6. Takeaway checklist for your next RL fine‑tune

| Step | Question | Decision |
|------|----------|----------|
| 1 | Do you need to adapt to changing policies? | Use online sampling (PPO/GRPO / Online RFT). |
| 2 | Do you have fine‐grained comparisons? | Train a reward model then use relative advantage (GRPO). |
| 3 | Do you want minimal compute? | Drop PPO’s separate value network → use GRPO’s group baseline. |
| 4 | Is your data volume limited? | Online sampling helps; batch multiple prompts per GPU forward pass if possible. |
| 5 | Need explanation? | SOTAs like RFT/DPO are still useful but may underperform on complex tasks where subtle quality differences matter—here relative ranking wins.*

---

## Epilogue

Unifying SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO and GRPO under one equation gives us clarity about what makes each method tick—and why newer tricks like GRPO can deliver more progress per megabyte of compute. The take-home message? In reinforcement learning for LLMs, putting “data source”, “reward signal”, and “algorithmic mapping” under one roof turns seemingly disparate tactics into variations of a single underlying recipe—and that recipe is now richer thanks to group relative learning._

# Beyond Numbers: Where Does DeepSeekMath Still Fall Short?

The paper *DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models* marks a huge leap for open‑source math LLMs. A 7 B‑parameter model pre‑trained on 120 B math tokens from Common Crawl and fine‑tuned with chain‑of‑thought (CoT), program‑of‑thought (PoT), and tool‑integrated reasoning, followed by a lightweight reinforcement‑learning stage (Group Relative Policy Optimization, GRPO), wins the competition‑level MATH benchmark (51.7 %) and rivals closed models such as Gemini‑Ultra and GPT‑4.

Yet a careful reading of the evaluation section, the discussion, and the conclusion reveals a handful of persistent gaps. In this post we unpack those shortcomings, consider why they survive even after rigorous training, and outline the concrete next steps the authors themselves propose—potentially a roadmap for future work that could close the remaining divide.

## 1. Geometry and Theorem Proving: The “Blind Spots”

### 1.1 Geometry
In the benchmark suite, DeepSeekMath shows respectable performance on general quantitative reasoning (GSM8K ≈ 88 %, MATH ≈ 51 %) but falls noticeably short on geometry–heavy subsets such as triangle or ellipse problems. The authors hint that this may reflect a **selection bias in pre‑training data**—the deep web crawling pipeline tends to capture pages with algebraic or combinatorial content over textbook geometry proofs. Since geometry problems often rely on spatial reasoning, synthetic images, or diagram descriptions that are hard to capture in plain text, the zero-shot/CoT abilities remain limited.

### 1.2 Formal Theorem Proving
On miniF2F (informal-to-formal proofs in Isabelle), DeepSeekMath does “strong few‑shot autoformalisation” compared with baseline models but still lags behind dedicated formal systems such as Minerva or models trained on massive proof corpora. Two factors explain this:

1. **Reward model bias** – The reinforcement policy is trained mainly on quantitative CoT data (GSM8K, MATH). Reward models evaluated via pairwise comparisons of whole outputs are less sensitive to fine-grained formal proof steps.
2. **Limited exposure to formal language** – The base corpus contains only ~10 % arXiv documents and negligible formal proof corpora; thus, there is insufficient inductive bias for Isabelle syntax.

## 2. Few‑Shot Generalisation: Zero vs. One Shot

The paper’s results show DeepSeekMath underperforms GPT‑4 in *few-shot* settings across all benchmarks (even when GPT is restricted to public APIs). This is not surprising because:

- **Scale** – GPT‑4’s ~170 B parameters can capture more latent patterns than a 7 B model.
- **Fine-tuning data** – DeepSeekMath’s instruction tuning uses 776 K examples, whereas GPT variants have been exposed to millions of diverse prompts during proprietary training.
- **Overfitting to training distribution** – Despite GRPO’s online improvements, the training data remains anchored to GSM8K/MATH-type problems; out-of-distribution prompts elicit suboptimal responses.

Thus, while DeepSeekMath’s *in-domain* performance is impressive, its *distribution shift* robustness remains weaker.

## 3. Residual Question Fields: Why RL Still Needs Work

The authors argue reinforcement learning largely *boosts Maj@K* rather than *Pass@K*: models become better at picking one correct answer among Top K but do not fundamentally increase true accuracy per prompt. This pattern suggests:

- The reward signal may be **too coarse**, often merely encouraging higher overall probability mass on correct answers rather than improving stepwise reasoning.
- RL relies on an **offline reward dataset** derived from SFT outputs; if those outputs are already subpar in certain problem paradigms (e.g., geometry), RL cannot compensate unless it receives more diverse signals.

## 4. Concrete Authors’ “Roadmap”

Below are the paper’s own proposed next steps—each directly addressing one of the gaps above.

| Target | Proposed Action | Why It Helps |
|--------|----------------|-------------|
| **Better Geometry Reasoning** | Refine data selection to include more textbook geometry content (e.g., \url{geometry.org}, encyclopedic definitions of shapes). | Increases exposure to spatial symbols and common geometric reasoning patterns. |
| **Enhanced Formal Proof Capability** | Add dedicated formal theorem corpora (Isabelle/TPTP libraries) into pre-training; train a separate reward model on fine-grained proof step quality.| Improves inductive bias for logical structure and makes rewards sensitive to intermediate derivations. |
| **Broader Few-shot Resistiveness** | Expand instruction tuning with synthetic prompts spanning varied domains (e.g., creative problem generation). | Forces the model to disambiguate among diverse contexts even when few examples are given. |
| **Fine-grained Process Rewards** | Build process‐reward models that evaluate each CoT step against ground truth or check symbolic correctness stepwise.| Aligns RL gradients more closely with actual reasoning quality rather than coincidence of final answer matching rule checks. |
| **Iterative RL with Tree Search Sampling** | Replace nucleus sampling with search-based decoding during RL exploration—parallel tree search + speculative decoding.| Generates higher-quality yet diverse samples which reinforce robust reasoning policies rather than memorised answers. |
| **Multi–Task ArXiv Integration at Scale** | Curate high‑quality arXiv subsets that include math proofs and symbolic derivations, then mix them with web data at larger scale (>5×). | Brings structured LaTeX insights while avoiding generic scientific noise that hurts reasoning tasks. |

## 5. A Quick Snapshot of Key Numbers

| Model | MATH Accuracy | GSM8K Accuracy | Passed Few-shot Tests |
|-------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------------|
| GPT-4 (closed) | ~80%+ | N/A | Excellent |
| Gemini Ultra | ~65%+ | N/A | Good |
| DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B | 46.8% | 82.9% | Moderate |
| DeepSeekMath-RL 7B (post GRPO) | **51.7%** | **88.2%** | Improved but still below GPT |

Even after GRPO boosts results about +5 % absolute on MATH, there remains a sizeable gap from GPT-4’s near-80% score—a gap amplified by complex geometry or theorem proving tasks.

## 6. Takeaway for Researchers

DeepSeekMath demonstrates that *quality* matters more than sheer size: a well‑curated web corpus plus an efficient RL algorithm can push a 7 B model near GPT-4 territory on competition-level math tasks *without* access tokens, voting ensembles or external toolkits.

But math LLM success hinges on more than token counts:

1. **Domain‐specific corpus engineering matters:** targeted crawling (geometry websites), curated formal libraries help fill blind spots.
2. **Reward signals must be stepwise:** tools such as process supervision or symbolic correctness scoring outperform coarse final-answer rewards.
3. **Online sampling gives better signal:** iterative RL where fresh policy samples guide reward training yields larger gains.
4. **Sampling strategy matters:** tree search or speculative decoding during RL exploration can produce richer prompts for reinforcement learning.

If you’re building an open-source math model today—increasing architecture depth alone is no longer enough—you need an end-to-end pipeline that begins with *smart data*, continues through *focused first-stage training*, optimises via *efficient RL* like GRPO, and finally incorporates *task-aware reward signals*. These are exactly the directions the authors outline; following them might just bring an open-source LLM within arm's reach of GPT-4’s mathematical prowess—or even surpass it under new evaluation regimes.
