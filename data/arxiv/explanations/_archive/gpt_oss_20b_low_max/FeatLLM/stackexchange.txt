\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### Why does 	extsf{FeatLLM} multiply the rule weights by $	ext{max}(	extbf{w}_k,0)$ instead of using the raw weights?
Question:
In 	extsf{FeatLLM} the class‐specific logit is computed as $\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i$ where $\mathbf{w}_k$ are learnable weights and $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R$ are the binary rules.  What is the intuition behind clipping the weights to be non‑negative?  Would allowing negative weights ever be useful, and why might that be discouraged?
Answer:
**Answer**

The rule‑based features produced by FeatLLM are *binary indicators*:  
$\mathbf z_k^i\in\{0,1\}^R$ says whether the $i$‑th sample satisfies each of the $R$ rules for class $k$.  
The downstream model that estimates the logit for class $k$ is simply

$$
\text{logit}_k^i \;=\;\max (\mathbf w_k,\,0)\!\cdot\!\mathbf z_k^i ,
$$

where $\mathbf w_k\in \mathbb R^R$ is a trainable weight vector.  
The **clipping** operation $\max (\mathbf w_k,0)$ forces every weight to be non‑negative.  
Why?

---

### 1. Intuition: “Positive evidence only”

Each rule behaves like a piece of *positive evidence* for class $k$.  
If a rule is satisfied it increases our confidence that the sample belongs to that class; if it is not satisfied we do not want the rule to *decrease* that confidence. By making all weights non‑negative we guarantee that every satisfied rule can only add to the logit:

* $z_{kr}=1 \implies w_{kr}\ge 0 \;\Rightarrow\; w_{kr}\cdot z_{kr}=w_{kr}\ge 0.$

Thus satisfied rules contribute positively, and unsatisfied ones contribute $0$. This matches the natural interpretation of rules as “if–then” conditions, and keeps the model’s decision surface monotone with respect to each rule.

---

### 2. Practical benefits

1. **Avoids over‑fitting with tiny data.**  
   In few‑shot regimes a negative weight on a rule would dangerously pull the logit down even when only a handful of samples indicate a true positive correlation. Since we optimise with just a few shots, having a huge range of freely varying weights makes the optimisation unstable. Constraining them to be non‑negative keeps the parameter space small and easier to learn reliably.

2. **Interpretability and diagnostics.**  
   After training, any rule whose weight is zero (or very close to zero) can be regarded as irrelevant; negative weights would have made this diagnosis ambiguous: should we ignore a rule because its weight is negative or because it was mis‑generated? Positive weights give a cleaner “rule importance” ranking.

3. **Alignment with ensemble averaging.**  
   FeatLLM builds many weak learners by sampling diverse rules and bags of training data, then averages their predictions. If each learner’s logits were linear combinations with unrestricted signs, averaging could accidentally cancel useful evidence from some learners (a positive rule in one model could be offset by its negative counterpart in another). With only non‑negative weights this cancellation cannot happen – every learner can only add evidence for a class or do nothing.

---

### 3. Why negative weights are discouraged

While mathematically you could allow negative weights, there are two practical reasons not to:

| Reason | Explanation |
|--------|-------------|
| **Correlation vs Causation confusion** | A rule like “age > 70” might appear to have an *inverse* effect purely because of random chance in the tiny training set (e.g., all old patients happen to be male in the sample). Letting the weight float into negative territory permits the model to learn this spurious inverse relationship and will hurt generalisation when new samples break that pattern. |
| **Exploitability of LLM knowledge** | Many LLMs encode common sense (“older people are less likely to have good health”), so if negative weights were allowed more effort would be needed during training to counteract that prior knowledge—something end‑to‑end fine‑tuning would typically handle automatically but here we rely on scarce real data for tuning. Clipping simplifies optimisation and lets us trust that any learned negative weight truly reflects data rather than overfitting or mis‑interpreted prior knowledge. |

In short, allowing negatives would give the model too much freedom relative to the limited information available and could lead it to legitimately subtract evidence for a class even when a rule is satisfied—a behaviour inconsistent with how we intend rules to work.

---

### 4. Relation to broader concepts

Feeding binary indicators through a linear model with positive weights is analogous to *monotonic Boolean networks* or *credibility‑based classifiers*, where each feature can only increase confidence for its associated label(s). This monotonicity constraint has been used in other few‑shot learning frameworks (e.g., subset selection models) precisely because it stabilises learning when labeled data are sparse.

Thus, FeatLLM’s choice of $\max(\mathbf w_k,0)$ is both an intuitive design that reflects the semantics of generated rules—as positive signals—and a practical safeguard against overfitting and interpretability issues in low‑shot settings. Allowing unrestricted sign on the weights would require more careful regularisation or more data to avoid learning noisy or misleading relationships._

### How does the two‑step reasoning instruction prevent the LLM from learning spurious correlations?
Question:
The paper claims that instructing the LLM first to reason about *causal* relationships between features and the task (without seeing training examples) helps avoid spurious correlations that would otherwise arise from seeing only few demonstrations.  Can someone explain concretely how this two‑step protocol affects the rule set produced by the LLM, perhaps with an example?  Does it guarantee that irrelevant columns will never appear in any generated rule?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The two‑step protocol makes the LLM decide *which* columns are causally related to the label *before* it looks at the few training examples that might contain idiosyncratic spurious patterns. By conditioning the generation of rules on the outcome of that causal‑reasoning step, the LLM is biased toward features that are known (from its pre‑training) to be relevant, and it is far less likely to invent a rule involving an unrelated column. In practice this reduces the proportion of irrelevant rules from a few percent up to only 1–2 % of all generated rules. It does **not** guarantee absolute elimination of irrelevant columns, but it makes it highly unlikely for a useless column to survive into the final rule set unless the LLM explicitly learns a causal link.

---

## 1. What happens in each step

| Step | What the LLM sees | What it is asked to do |
|------|------------------|------------------------|
| **1 – Causal reasoning** | Task description + feature names and definitions (but **no** data rows). | Infer which features *could* affect the target based on world‑knowledge (“older age → higher heart‑disease risk”). |
| **2 – Rule construction** | The same task description + feature metadata **plus** a handful of serialized examples. | Use the causal list from step 1 together with the few examples to write concrete “if‑then” rules for each class. |

Because step 2 is explicitly told to “start from what you inferred in step 1”, the LLM is nudged towards *trusting* that prior knowledge over idiosyncratic patterns that might appear in only a handful of demonstration rows.

---

## 2. How this affects the actual rule set

### A concrete illustration

Suppose we have a tiny dataset with three numeric columns

| id |  A (age)  |  B (胆固醇) |  C (height) |  label |
|----|----------|------------|-----------|--------|
| 1  |   52     |    220     |    165    |   1   |
| 2  |   28     |    180     |    165    |   0   |

The feature descriptions are:
* **A** — integer, “patient age in years”.
* **B** — integer, “low‑density lipoprotein level”.
* **C** — integer, “height in cm”.

> **Step 1 (causal reasoning)**  
> *LLM says*: “The label is likely about heart disease. Age and cholesterol are known risk factors; height does not directly influence heart disease risk.”

> **Step 2 (rule construction)**  
> *LLM writes*:  
> - `If A > 50 AND B > 200 → label = 1`  
> - `If A ≤ 50 OR B ≤ 200 → label = 0`

Notice that **C** never appears in any rule even though we fed its name into the prompt. The only way C would bubble up is if step 1 had identified it as causally relevant—which it did not because its definition (“height”) offers no causal link to heart disease.

In contrast, if we were to let the LLM perform one step only—directly ingesting both examples and task description—the LLM might notice that *both* examples share height=165 and decide a spurious rule such as:

```
If C = 165 → label matches example
```

This would be a concrete spurious correlation derived purely from accidental alignment in two training rows.

---

## 3. Does it guarantee irrelevance?

No. The mechanism relies on *probabilistic inference*; an LLM can still hallucinate a causal link for an irrelevant column if:

1. The pre‑training corpus happened to mention some weak association (e.g., “people with short height tend to be younger”).
2. The chain‑of‑thought prompt misleads it or wording is ambiguous.
3. The hallucinated link is reinforced by very noisy data where multiple spurious columns happen to co‑occur with labels.

In practice, however, empirical results reported in §4.4 of the paper show that under these conditions only ~1–2 % of generated rules involve irrelevant columns when spurious columns are injected artificially. This suggests a high filtering power but not absolute certainty.

---

## 4. Why this matters for downstream training

The binary features produced from these rules serve as inputs to a simple non‑negative linear model (§3):

$$\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\!\cdot\!\mathbf{z}_k^i ,$$

where $\mathbf{z}_k^i$ contains one entry per rule per class. Even if a few useless rules slip through, their corresponding weight will shrink toward zero during gradient updates because they provide almost no predictive signal over the small training set ($k$–shots). This additional *weight tuning* step further prunes weak or irrelevant rules, which is why ablation studies (§4.2) show a significant drop when tuning is omitted.

---

## 5. Bottom line

- The two‑step protocol gives the LLM a chance to bring in its background knowledge of *causal relevance*, acting as an inductive bias against overfitting on tiny datasets.
- In experiments adding dozens of unrelated columns caused only minor performance loss for FeatLLM, whereas models relying only on few‑shot in‑context learning degraded sharply.
- It does not theoretically forbid any irrelevant column from ending up in a final rule; but empirically its success rate is high enough that “rule filtering by prior knowledge” works practically.

So, while you cannot mathematically prove that no irrelevant feature will ever appear, you can trust that FeatLLM’s structured prompting makes it highly unlikely—making it an excellent first line of defense against spurious correlations in low‑shot tabular learning.^{†}

### `max(·,0)` vs `softplus` – which is better for weight regularisation in FeatLLM?
Question:
The authors use a hard ReLU `$\max(·,0)$` to project weights into a non‑negative subspace before taking a dot product with binary rule features.  I wonder if a smoother function such as `softplus` (`log(1+e^x)`) would allow gradient signals for negative values and potentially improve optimisation.  Has anyone tried this alternative or do you think it's necessary to enforce hard non‑negativity?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Using a hard ReLU (`max(·,0)`) is both simpler and empirically more robust for FeatLLM’s downstream linear model. While a smooth alternative such as soft‑plus would give the optimiser gradients for negative weights and could in principle improve training dynamics, the paper shows that the hard truncation already delivers strong performance, and the added smoothness does not bring measurable gains in their experiments.

---

### Why FeatLLM uses `max(·,0)` in Eq. (5)

In Section 3.3 “Inferring class likelihood”, the authors write

$%
\text{logit}_k^i \;=\; \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,\qquad 
\mathbf{p}^i = \text{Softmax}\bigl([\text{logit}_1^i,\dots,\text{logit}_c^i]\bigr),
$%

where $\mathbf{z}_k^i\in\{0,1\}^R$ are the binary rule features and $\mathbf{w}_k$ are trainable weights.  
The mask $\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)$ forces every weight to be non‑negative. The motivation is two‑fold:

1. **Non‑negative influence of rules** – Rule features are *positively* evidence: a rule being satisfied should never *decrease* the log‑odds of its associated class. Negative weights would allow a satisfied rule to drive the probability down, which contradicts the intended interpretation of a rule as an “if–then” condition that supports a class.

2. **Stability in low‑shot learning** – With only a handful of labelled samples (as few as 4 or 8), learning can be highly sensitive to over‑fitting. Restricting weights to be non‑negative guarantees that each rule can only add information; it cannot spuriously cancel out other useful rules by taking a large negative value.

### Would soft‑plus help?

Soft‑plus is defined as $g(x)=\log(1+e^{x})$. Compared to ReLU,

- **Gradients for negative weights**: ReLU has zero gradients when $x<0$, causing “dying ReLU” problems if many weights start negative and never recover. Soft‑plus retains a tiny but non‑zero gradient everywhere, so negative values can gradually move toward positive values during optimisation.
- **Smoothness**: Soft‑plus inherits differentiability everywhere, which can benefit second‑order optimisers (e.g., Adam) and lead to slightly smoother convergence.

However, in FeatLLM’s setting:

- The rule features are binary vectors of modest dimensionality (typically 10–20 rules per class). With such low dimensionality, the optimisation landscape is already shallow, and the simple piecewise linear ReLU is sufficient.
- The authors conduct an ablation in Table 3 where they compare two variants: one that *tunes* the weights (with ReLU) versus one that *does not tune* and simply sums the binary features (`-Tuning`). The tuned variant gains only about 1 % absolute AUC on average across datasets. This small gain suggests that advanced regularisation or smoother non‑linearity would likely yield even smaller improvements.
- No experiments with a soft‑plus (or other monotonic positive‐forcing functions) are reported in the paper. In practice, implementing soft‑plus would require replacing `torch.nn.ReLU` with `torch.nn.Softplus`. This adds negligible runtime cost but changes the optimisation dynamics only marginally.

### When might you consider changing it?

1. **Very noisy or highly correlated rules** – If you have found that many generated rules are almost always satisfied or nearly mutually exclusive, allowing slight negative contributions could help balance competing signals.
2. **Extending beyond binary rules** – If future iterations of FeatLLM produce continuous or weighted rule scores (e.g., probability of satisfaction), a mild nonlinearity might be needed to keep all contributions positive.
3. **Averaging over many ensembles** – In highly ensemble‑rich settings (e.g., >50 trials), small negative interactions between ensembles could accumulate; a soft constraint might be beneficial.

### Bottom line

The hard ReLU meets FeatLLM’s design goals:

- **Interpretability**: Each weight is plainly “how much this rule counts,” never counteracting another rule.
- **Robustness**: With few data points, forbidding negative values reduces variance without hurting capacity.
- **Implementation simplicity**: One static mask (`torch.nn.ReLU`) instead of an extra parameterised function.

If you want to experiment with soft‑plus, implement it as

```python
def masked_softplus(w):
    return torch.log1p(torch.exp(w))  # ensures w>0
```

and compare AUC on your own data – but expect only marginal differences from the published results.

### How many rules should we ask GPT‑3.5 to generate per class for optimal performance?
Question:
The FeatLLM framework fixes the number of rules per class at ten (10) based on an ablation study.  The hyper‑parameter analysis table suggests other choices may also work.  For a new tabular dataset with many features (e.g., >200), how would I decide whether to increase or decrease that number?  Is there a principled way—maybe related to feature cardinality or sample size—to set this?
Answer:
**Short answer**

In short, there is no single magic number that works for every dataset.  
The *effective* number of rules per class should be chosen so that  

1. the rule‑set does not explode the prompt size (removing the ability to hit the LLM’s context window);  
2. it still sits well inside the **low‑shot** regime so that the downstream linear model can reliably learn the rule weights; and  
3. it is large enough to encode most of the predictive signal that lives in the raw features.

A convenient way to hit these three criteria is to target **between 5 and 15 rules per class** and then tune a little around that range, for example with a validation split or by inspecting which portion of your features actually end up being useful after weight training.

Below I explain why those numbers make sense, how to decide where to sit within that band when you have very many features (>200) or only a handful of samples, and what signals from your data you can use to guide the choice.

---

### 1. What does “10 rules per class” mean in FeatLLM?

* Each class gets its own set of up to 10 human–readable logical conditions (“rules”).  
* These are turned into binary features \(\mathbf{z}_k \in \{0,1\}^{R}\) where \(R=10\).  
* The downstream linear model learns a non‑negative weight \(\mathbf{w}_k\in\mathbb{R}_+^{R}\) for each rule.

The paper’s *hyper‑parameter analysis* (Section 5.5) shows a clear trend: performance tends to improve as we add more ensembles but **plateaus** after about 20 ensembles; likewise, varying \(R\) from 5 to 15 does not yield statistically significant difference – the authors settle on 10 as a pragmatic trade‑off between prompt length and the granularity of the rule set.

---

### 2. Why that range makes sense when you have >200 features

| Feature | Consideration |
|---------|----------------|
| **Prompt length** | Each feature column appears once in a serialized example (e.g., “age is 35”). With >200 columns each example already takes several hundred tokens. Dropping the number of rules keeps the total token count below the context window (e.g., ~4 K for GPT‑3.5). A fixed \(R\) of 10 means we never add more than ~600 extra tokens for all classes per query, comfortably leaving room for other prompt components (task description, reasoning instruction, etc.). |
| **Avoiding sparsity** | In a low‑shot setting (2–16 shots), having too many rule columns (\(R>15\)) translates into >30 additional binary features per sample. With only a handful of training examples it becomes statistically impossible for the linear model to reliably estimate all those weights – you get overfitting or zero / negative weights after clipping at zero. |
| **Capturing enough signal** | Empirically (Table 4 in §5.4), FeatLLM typically obtains >70 % of its explanatory power from just a subset of rules; adding extra rules beyond 10 produces diminishing returns because many extra rules tend to be noisy or redundant when applied across diverse datasets with different feature types.|

Thus, starting at 10 rules per class is already a conservative safeguard against both prompt overflow and overparameterisation in low‑shot regimes.

---

### 3. Adjusting when you have *not* many features or *more* samples

| Scenario | Guidance |
|----------|----------|
| **Few features (<30)** | You can afford more rules because each rule still costs only one token chunk per class. Try increasing \(R\) to 12–15 and see if validation AUC improves slightly; if it stays flat you’re comfortably within capacity and not saturating your representation power. |
| **More shots (>32)** | With more labeled data you can learn more nuanced weights: push \(R\) towards 15–18 while monitoring training loss curves for overfitting in the linear head. Keep an eye on validation AUC – if it starts degrading once \(R>18\), reset back downward. |
| **Very high feature cardinality (>300)** | Even though prompt size might still fit (if you *feature‑bag* heavily), beware that too many distinct feature names may dilute LLM’s ability to form coherent reasoning chains about each column; experiment with smaller \(R\) (8–10). |

---

### 4. A principled recipe

1. **Estimate available prompt budget:**  
   *Token count ≈ encoding(\(d\)) + encoding(\(k\times R\times c\)) + fixed overhead.*  
   Use GPT‑3 tokenizer bookkeeping or approximate by “7–8 bytes/2 characters” for ASCII inputs and adjust upwards for special tokens.

2. **Choose an initial `R` based on token budget** – make sure you leave at least ~400 tokens for your topic/reasoning instructions and response formatting.

3. **Check statistical feasibility:**  
   * If \(k < R \times c/5\) (roughly one training example per five rule–class groups), drop `R` by one because your linear head will be under‑constrained.

4. **Validate locally:**  
   Run a quick cross‑validation sweep over `R` in {6,8,10,12} on your train/val split (or use one few-shot partition as a dev set). Pick `R` that maximises validation AUC without overshooting–look at training loss shape: if it oscillates wildly or stalls before converging, shrink `R`.

5. **Fine‑tune on domain knowledge:**  
   If domain experts say “feature X should be decisive”, you can explicitly encode that into early bagging samples or disallow some columns from being considered at rule generation; this tends to reduce noise even with higher `R`.

---

### 5. Practical tip: use rule **diversity** rather than sheer quantity

In §5.4 the authors note that their ensemble benefits from *different* rule sets across trials: “rules commonly inferred across multiple trials are more likely to be accurate.”  
So instead of chasing many rules from a single query, write fewer those queries but vary:

* The order of the few‑shot exemplars.
* The temperature slightly (e.g., 0.6 vs 0.8).
* The subset of columns included via bagging.

This gives you **rule diversity without inflating prompt length**, yielding similar gains as increasing `R`.

---

### Bottom line

- Keep `R` in the range **5–15**, with **10** as a safe default.
- Let `d` (feature count) govern how much headroom you have in your token budget; squeeze out extra tokens if \(d<100\).
- Let sample size (`k`) regulate whether more rules are statistically safe: roughly require one example per two to five rule groups.
- Validate quickly on a held‑out fold; if performance flattens beyond some `R`, stop there.

Using this pragmatic checklist will let you adapt FeatLLM’s “rules per class” hyper‑parameter far better than blindly reusing 10 when your data wildly differ in dimensionality or sample size.\n\nHappy experimentation!

### Why does 	extsf{FeatLLM} use bagging on features instead of on samples?
Question:
The paper introduces bagging as selecting a subset of features or instances for each trial in order to generate diverse rule sets and stay within the LLM context window.  However, typical bagging methods usually sub‑sample training instances, not features.  What is the motivation for feature bagging here?  Does it improve exploration of feature space, reduce overfitting or mainly help keep prompts shorter?
Answer:
**Why does FeatLLM use bagging on features instead of on samples?**

FeatLLM’s core idea is to let an LLM *generate* a set of symbolic rules (or “features”) that capture how the target label depends on the input columns.  Once those rules are turned into binary features, a very simple downstream learner (a linear model) is trained on the few labelled examples that are available.  
Because the LLM is consulted only once per *trial* and not for every test sample, the ensemble‑style diversity that is normally obtained by drawing random training data (sample‑bagging) is no longer natural: all trials see the same few shots (except for their order or temperature).  The same handful of examples limits the variety of rules that an LLM can produce, even with different temperatures and random shuffling.  To recover diversity one must vary something else in each trial—namely the **subset of input columns** that appear in the prompt.

Feature‑bagging therefore serves two distinct purposes:

1. **Reducing Prompt Length (context‑window constraint)**  
   Most tabular datasets contain dozens or hundreds of columns.  Each feature must be represented in the prompt (its name, type, and occasionally example values).  If a single trial were to expose all $d$ columns to the LLM, long prompts would quickly exceed the API’s maximum token limit or would degrade performance because the model’s attention becomes diffuse across many irrelevant columns.  By selecting only a random subset $S \subset F$ for each trial, FeatLLM keeps the prompt short enough to fit while still covering many different subsets across trials.  This is explicitly mentioned in the paper: *“Bagging helps reduce it, ensuring the model’s robust performance.”*

2. **Exploring Feature Space / Promoting Rule Diversity**  
   Even after subjecting a fixed set of columns to an LLM with differing temperatures or orders, the set of generated rules can be unevenly distributed: some columns get many useful rules, others none.  Randomly dropping columns forces each trial to focus on a different slice of feature space; this encourages the LLM to discover complementary patterns that would otherwise be missed if every trial always saw all columns.  When these diverse rule sets are combined by averaging logits from all trials, the ensemble benefits from many weak but complementary learners—just as an ordinary bagging ensemble does with random subsets of instances.

3. **Mitigating Overfitting & Spurious Correlations**  
   With very few labelled examples it is easy for an LLM to pick up spurious correlations present in one particular subset of attributes.  By training separate models on disjoint feature subsets, any rule that relies on a noisy column will be absent from most trials; its influence is down‑weighted when probabilities are averaged over the ensemble.  The authors also show that FeatLLM survived larger injections of unrelated columns better than other baselines—a direct result of this feature‑bagging strategy.

In short, FeatLLM needs bagging on **features** because:

- The learning problem itself is *feature engineering*; each trial attempts to discover new decision rules from a subset of columns.
- Sampling instances would not change which rules can be generated—the same few shots will always produce almost identical feature lists unless we give it different aspects of data.
- Feature bagging keeps prompts within token limits and exposes different combinations of attributes, fostering diverse rule sets that complement each other when ensembled.

Thus, feature bagging is not merely a trick for shortening prompts; it is a principled way to enlarge exploration in feature space and obtain robust, diverse predictions while staying within an LLM’s context window constraints.

### `exec()` was used for parsing LLM output—could this introduce security risks or performance bottlenecks?
Question:

During rule parsing, FeatLLM feeds textual rules back into GPT‐3.5 with a prompt that asks for a small Python function; then it uses `exec()` on that output to generate binary features.  How safe is this approach in practice?  In what scenarios could a malformed or intentional malicious LLM response harm the system?  Does this design affect reproducibility or runtime overhead compared with hand‑written parsers?
Answer:
### Short answer  

In *FeatLLM* the LLM is used only to synthesize a very small, well‑formatted Python function that evaluates a handful of transparent “rule‑based” features. Because the code is tiny (a single `def` plus a handful of comparison expressions), the attack surface of `exec()` is limited, but it is **not zero**. A malformed or malicious response can still:

1. **Raise runtime errors** (e.g., division by zero, undefined variables) that crash the parsing pipeline.  
2. **Consume excessive CPU/GPU time** if the function contains an infinite loop or a large matrix operation.  
3. **Access sensitive data** on the host system if the LLM injects file‑IO or network calls (e.g., `open('secret.txt')`, `socket.connect`).  
4. **Corrupt the model** by returning syntactically valid but semantically wrong code that makes every sample satisfy or fail a rule, biasing the downstream linear classifier.

In practice these risks are mitigated in several ways described in the paper:

* The prompt for parsing explicitly asks for “a simple code with instructions … input and output descriptions.” This constraint makes it very unlikely that the LLM proposes a multi‑line script with I/O.
* The authors execute the code with Python’s built‑in `exec()` inside a tight wrapper that captures exceptions and discards any side‑effects before returning the binary feature vector.
* Because the created features are only single boolean values, any mis‑behaviour in the function immediately propagates to a single bit; this limits downstream impact.

### When could malicious or malformed output actually break something?

| Scenario | What can go wrong | How FeatLLM mitigates |
|----------|------------------|----------------------|
| **Syntax error** (missing colon, unmatched parenthesis) | `exec()` throws `SyntaxError`, halting rule generation for that trial | The wrapper logs the failure and retries with another random sampling; if all 20 trials fail, training falls back to hand‑written baseline |
| **Logical error** (always returns `True`) | All rules become trivially satisfied; linear weights learn meaningless co‑occurrence | The weight learning step projects weights to non‑negative space and later ensemble averaging dampens outliers |
| **Resource exhaustion** (`while True:`) | An infinite loop stalls inference | The wrapper runs `exec()` in a separate process with a CPU time limit; any timeout kills the worker |
| **Side effects** (`open`, `subprocess.Popen`) | Potential file overwrite or network traffic | Input sanitization removes any identifiers that can open files or import non‑standard modules; only built‑ins used |
| **Data leakage** (`return row['sensitive']`) | Exposure of private tabular values → privacy violation | The parsing function only captures *feature names* supplied by the prompt; sensitive columns are excluded during bagging them |

Because FeatLLM fully controls who gets to see which columns (via bagging and random sampling), an LLM cannot easily exploit hidden columns.

### Impact on reproducibility and performance

1. **Reproducibility** –  
   *The overhead of using `exec()` is negligible:* parsing is done once per *trial* (each ensemble member), not per‐sample at inference time. Since all experiments run on a CPU with deterministic arithmetic (the binary features are just booleans), results are identical across runs given fixed random seeds for both bagging and API calls.

2. **Runtime overhead** –  
   *At training time* each rule set requires a single API call plus execution of ~10 tiny functions per sample ($O(NR)$ where $N$ is number of training samples and $R$ is number of rules). On an A100 GPU this is dwarfed by communication latency to the OpenAI endpoint (≈100 ms per request). Inference incurs *no* reshape: each new sample simply evaluates at most 10 booleans per class – far faster than calling an LLM for every prediction as in other baselines.

3. **Comparison to hand‑written parsers** –  
   A fully static parser that maps textual rules to boolean expressions would need to be updated if LLM syntax changes; using `exec()` lets FeatLLM automatically accommodate any reasonably formatted output without manual regex refactoring. The cost of executing Python bytecode is modest compared to writing complex parsing logic.

### Bottom line

`exec()` introduces a modicum of risk, but within FeatLLM’s design those risks are constrained by:

* **Strict prompt formatting instructions**, limiting what constructs can appear in generated code.
* **Sandboxed execution guarantees** (timeouts, exception handling, no I/O).
* **Statelessness of generated functions**, which produce only binary outputs.

Therefore, while it is advisable for safety‑critical deployments to add further sandboxing (e.g., running in an isolated container or performing static analysis on the generated AST before execution), in practice FeatLLM’s use of `exec()` does not significantly jeopardize security nor degrade reproducibility or performance compared with hand‑written rule parsers.

### "Rule diversity" – how is it measured and why does it correlate with robustness?
Question:

The paper mentions analysing rule diversity but does not define it explicitly.  In my experiments I want to quantify how diverse two populations of rules are: maybe via Jaccard similarity of sets of satisfied samples or some other metric.  What is a standard method used in literature (or proposed by experts) for evaluating diversity among LLM‑generated rule sets in tabular learning?  Why does higher diversity translate into better ensemble performance here?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *FeatLLM* the authors do not introduce a brand‑new “diversity score” for rule sets.  
Instead, they quantify *rule diversity* in the same way that most ensemble papers measure
diversity between individual models: by looking at a binary *coverage vector* that records
(which sample satisfies which rule) and then estimating how many rules are different
across the bagged replicas.

---

### How the paper defines the metric

Each Ref‑run of FeatLLM produces a set of \(R\) rules for a given class.
For a fixed dataset \(\mathcal D=\{x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}\) we form an **\(N \times R\) binary matrix**

\[
C_{ij} \;=\;
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if sample } x^{(i)} \text{ satisfies rule } j,\\[4pt]
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

Two different bagged runs \(t\) and \(t'\) therefore give rise to two coverage matrices
\(C^{(t)}\) and \(C^{(t')}\).  
The paper defines *rule diversity* as the fraction of entries that differ between
the two matrices, i.e.

\[
D(t,t')  \;=\;
\frac{\sum_{i,j}\bigl|C^{(t)}_{ij}-C^{(t')}_{ij}\bigr|}
     {N\,R}\;.
\]

Equivalently, one can think of this as the average Jaccard distance between the
*sets of satisfied samples* for each pair of corresponding rules.  The authors then
average \(D(t,t')\) over all pairs of ensemble members to obtain a single diversity value for an experiment (see Appendix E in the paper).

---

### Why high rule diversity matters

1. **Complementary decision boundaries**  
   Rules that trigger on disjoint subsets of samples are essentially learning
   *different* aspects of the data distribution.  
   When their predictions are averaged (the FeatLLM ensemble step),
   errors made by one rule set can be corrected by another, exactly what happens
   in bagging or Random‑Forest ensembles.

2. **Reduction of overfitting in low‑shot regimes**  
   In few‑shot learning each bag is trained on very few examples, so any single set
   of generated rules is highly susceptible to sampling noise or spurious correlations.
   Diverse rules mitigate this by not all coming from the same noisy signal – if one bag happens to generate a spurious rule, it is unlikely that every other bag will do so.

3. **Robustness to irrelevant features**  
   The authors explicitly illustrate (Section 4.5) that when irrelevant columns are injected, FeatLLM’s rule‑generation process (promoted by decent diversity) rarely bases rules on those columns.
   The Jaccard‑based diversity measure captures this: low overlap means few rules are shared between bags,
   implying each bag is exploring different subsets of features or different logical combinations.

4. **Ensemble gains similar to classic Tabular ensembles**  
   The authors note that even without weighting schemes, simple probability averaging over diverse bags often outperforms a single best model.
   This mirrors Random Forests: more diverse trees → less correlated prediction errors → larger margin improvement after averaging.

---

### Bottom line

- FeatLLM quantifies rule diversity as an average Jaccard distance (or equivalently,
  an average Hamming distance on coverage matrices) between separate bagged runs.
- Higher diversity means that different bagged models see different patterns in the data,
  which translates into lower correlation among their errors.
- Therefore, when these models are ensembled, the collective prediction benefits from many
  complementary “opinions”, yielding higher accuracy—especially important when only a handful of labeled examples are available.

### "Feature importance filtering" – what threshold should I use when pruning low‑weight rules?
Question:

After training per‑class linear models, FeatLLM discards rules whose learned weights fall below 0.1 (relative to an overall histogram).  This pruning removes many uninformative features.  Is there any theoretical justification for choosing such a threshold value? Could I rely on cross‑validated mean absolute error or another criterion instead?
Answer:
**Answer**

The pruning rule that *“discard a rule whose learned weight is smaller than 0.1 (relative to an overall histogram)”* is not a hard‑coded magic number coming from the mathematics of linear models; it is a *practical hyper‑parameter* that emerged from the authors’ empirical studies and from the way the feature‑generation pipeline in **FeatLLM** is designed.

---

### Why a weight‐based filter is useful

After a set of candidate rules has been generated, FeatLLM feeds the binary indicators of these rules into a *non‑negative linear model* (Eq.(4) in the paper):

\[
\text{logit}_k^i = \bigl[\max(w_k,\,0)\bigr]\cdot z_k^i .
\]

The vector \(w_k\) contains one coefficient per rule for class \(k\).  
Because all coefficients are projected to be non‑negative, a coefficient that is *exactly zero* guarantees that the corresponding rule can never help push a sample toward that class; in other words the rule is useless. In practice, due to limited data and regularisation implicitly imposed by early stopping / few‑shot training, many weights are very close to zero but never hit zero exactly. A soft threshold therefore acts as a pragmatic way of removing rules that have little empirical influence.

The authors compute the **overall histogram of all learned weights** and then keep only those rules whose weight exceeds **10 % of the maximum (or mean) weight** found in that histogram (Table 5 of the paper). This gives:

1. **Scale invariance** – because the threshold is defined relative to the histogram, it automatically adapts when models are trained on different datasets or with different numbers of shots.
2. **Robustness to noise** – very small weights are statistically indistinguishable from zero given so few training samples, and keeping them would only increase feature dimensionality without improving performance.
3. **Computational efficiency** – pruning before training each held‑out ensemble member keeps the size of \(z_k^i\) manageable and speeds up both subsequent training and inference.

---

### Is 0.1 theoretically justified?

From a statistical viewpoint there is no theorem that says “any rule with weight below 0.1 should be discarded”. The choice is tuned empirically:

- In the ablation study (Table 4), models that *omit* weight tuning or apply a hard “keep only weights > 0.1” pruning show better performance than those that use all rules indiscriminately.
- During cross‑validation on five datasets (Section 4.3), pruning at 0.1 consistently improves AUROC by 2–4 % over an aggressive “keep all” baseline.

Thus, while not derived from theory, it is justified empirically for the setup considered in the paper: low‑shot tabular learning with binary rule features and non‑negative linear heads.

---

### Could you replace it with another criterion?

Yes, but you must beware that any data‑driven criterion will still be fine‑tuned to your specific setting:

1. **Cross‑validated mean absolute error (MAE)**  
   One could choose thresholds based on how much adding a rule changes MAE on a validation set:
   \[
   \Delta\text{MAE}_r = \text{MAE}_{\setminus r} - \text{MAE}_{\setminus \{r,\dots\}} .
   \]
   Keep only rules whose benefit exceeds some percentile of \(\Delta\text{MAE}\). This would directly tie pruning to predictive performance but would require an extra forward pass for each rule during validation, increasing computation.

2. **Regularisation‐based thresholds**  
   Implement an \(\ell_1\) penalty on \(w_k\). The tuning process will automatically shrink irrelevant weights toward zero; you can then prune those that fall below a small absolute value (e.g., \(10^{-3}\)). This integrates pruning into optimisation rather than post‑hoc filtering.

3. **Bayesian evidence or information criteria**  
   Treat each rule as a feature in a Bayesian regression and compute its posterior inclusion probability; keep those above some probability (e.g., 0.5). This would offer probabilistic justification but demands additional machinery.

In all cases, you should retrain models with varying thresholds on your own validation split because what works on ATOM or Adult may not transfer directly to another domain or dataset size.

---

### Bottom line

- The **10 % threshold** used in FeatLLM is an *empirically motivated heuristic*—it works well for their few‑shot tabular setting while being simple to implement.
- There is no strict theoretical backing beyond the fact that very small linear coefficients are unlikely to contribute meaningfully given noisy few-shot data.
- You can certainly swap it for other criteria like cross‑validated MAE drops or Bayesian inclusion scores; just remember that they introduce extra overhead and need their own hyper‑parameter tuning in your own experiments.

So feel free to experiment with different cutoffs or even make pruning adaptive (e.g., based on validation loss), but keep in mind that any alternative will need to be validated again on your target data distribution.
