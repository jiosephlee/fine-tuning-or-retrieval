# Why Angles Matter: The Secret Power of Neuron Orientation in Text‑to‑Image Diffusion

## The mystery of “direction” in a black‑box diffusion model

If you look at the arithmetic inside a transformer or a diffusion network, you’ll see a very familiar pattern: a weight matrix \(W\) is multiplied by an input vector \(x\). Usually, we think about changing the *values* in that matrix to steer the model. But what if the only thing that really matters are the *directions* of those weights? Think of each column of \(W\) as a point on a unit sphere – the weight’s angle relative to the others carries semantic information, while its length can be tuned later. Recent research has shown that preserving these angles during fine‑tuning keeps a model’s story intact.

## Hyperspherical energy: A quick tour

To formalise this intuition we use **hyperspherical energy** (HE). For a layer with \(n\) neurons \(\{w_1,\dots,w_n\}\), we first normalise each to unit length \(\hat w_i = w_i/\|w_i\|\). HE is then defined as

\[
\text{HE}(W) \;=\; \sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat w_i - \hat w_j\|^{-1}.
\]

Intuitively, HE counts how “crowded” the neurons are on the sphere: if two neurons point very close together, their contribution blows up because their angular distance is tiny. A low HE means neurons are spread out evenly—an ideal geometric distribution for capturing diverse concepts.

When you finetune a huge diffusion model for a niche task (say, generating your pet dog in different scenes or respecting a hand‑drawn sketch), you want to keep this well‑distributed geometry. If you let the angles drift wildly, the semantic relations you learned during pretraining collapse.

## Orthogonal finetuning (OFT): Rotate without re‑learning

The key insight: **any rotation or reflection keeps all pairwise angles exactly the same**. Mathematically, if we replace \(W\) by \(RW\) where \(R^\top R = I\), then every \(\|\hat w_i - \hat w_j\|\) remains unchanged, and consequently HE stays the same.

OFT turns this into a training recipe:

1. **Learn a single orthogonal matrix per layer** (\(R\)) that nudges all neurons together, but never disturbs their relative geometry.
2. **Fine‑tune** only \(R\), keeping the original weights \(W^0\) fixed as the base.
3. At inference time simply replace \(W^0\) by \(RW^0\); no extra overhead is introduced.

Because OFT preserves HE exactly, it guarantees that the dominant semantic knowledge stored during pretraining survives intact. Yet it still gives us enough freedom to adapt – for example, rotating all dog features slightly may make them appear in a new pose without losing recognisability.

## Constrained OFT (COFT): Keep it close

OFT can wander far from the original configuration if optimization is not careful. COFT adds a small radius constraint:

\[
\|R - I\| \le \epsilon.
\]

This caps how much any neuron direction can move away from its starting point. Think of it as performing a gentle wrist twist rather than an entire arm swing. In practice COFT yields even more stable convergence and is less likely to overfit weak data sets.

## Why rotations work better than learning arbitrary small changes

You might wonder why not just use tiny learning rates and let gradients adjust everything straightforwardly—that’s what DreamBooth or LoRA do. The problem is that *small changes* in weight values do not translate to *small changes* in angles; subtle Euclidean shifts can cause large angular swings when weights lie on the surface of high‑dimensional spheres.

In contrast, an orthogonal update guarantees that every angle change is under our control: all neurons rotate together by exactly the same amount. This makes training landscapes smoother and leads to faster convergence—as shown empirically in subject‑driven generation experiments where OFT reached stable performance after 400 iterations versus thousands for baseline methods.

## How OFT shines on two real tasks

| Task | Goal | What OFT/COFT does |
|------|------|--------------------|
| **Subject‑Driven Generation** | Generate new images of your pet using just five photos | Rotates feature directions so your dog’s identity stays strong while still allowing arbitrary backgrounds |
| **Controllable Generation** | Make images follow Canny edges or segmentation maps | Learns mappings from control signals into rotated feature space so that output images respect every input line or mask |

In both cases experiments show higher fidelity scores (CLIP‑I and DINO embeddings stay closer to real subjects) and lower control error (landmark deviation drops by 30 % versus baselines). Plus OFT needs fewer trainable parameters—just one orthogonal matrix per layer—making it lightweight and easy to deploy.

## Take‑away: Angles are the backbone of generative semantics

- Neurons live on an implicit unit hypersphere; their pairwise angles encode rich semantic relationships.
- Hyperspherical energy quantifies how well‑spaced these neurons are; preserving it keeps meaning intact.
- An orthogonal transformation rotates all directions simultaneously without touching HE—a principled way to fine‑tune large diffusion models.
- Adding a radius constraint (COFT) ensures stability while still leveraging full rotational flexibility.
- The result? Faster convergence, higher fidelity, and no extra inference cost.

If you’re finetuning text‑to‑image diffusion models for specific styles or constraints, try casting fine‑tuning as *rotation on the sphere*. It’s simple to implement (just learn an orthogonal *R*), mathematically sound (preserves hyperspherical energy), and practically powerful—helping you keep what you love and add what you need without losing either.
