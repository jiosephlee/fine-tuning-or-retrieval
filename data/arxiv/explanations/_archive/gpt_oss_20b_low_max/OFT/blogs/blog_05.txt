# Beyond Finetuning: Exploring Orthogonal Matrices for Compositional Generation  

## Introduction  

Finetuning large text‑to‑image diffusion models has become a widely adopted way to steer generation toward a downstream task.  The paper “Controlling Text‑to‑Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning” (OFT) shows that a very simple *orthogonal* transformation—one that preserves the pairwise angles of neurons—can adapt a pre‑trained model while keeping its generative quality intact.  What if, instead of treating each downstream task separately, we *chain* several of these orthogonal transforms together?  Could we compose multiple skill‑specific adapters into a single, versatile generator that can instantly switch between tasks or even blend them at test time?  

In this post we speculate on this future.  We’ll start by summarizing why orthogonal transforms are special, then explain how to concatenate them, and finally discuss both the seductive possibilities and the practical hurdles that will need to be overcome.

## Why Orthogonal Matrices Matter for Diffusion Models  

1. **Hyperspherical Energy Preservation**  
   An orthogonal matrix $R$ satisfies $R^{\top}R = I$.  Applying $R$ to every neuron in a layer keeps the *hyperspherical energy*—the sum of inverse distances between normalized weight vectors—invariant.  This means the semantic geometry encoded in the pre‑trained model is untouched.

2. **Multiplicative Nature**  
   The forward pass after OFT is simply  
   \[
     z = (R \cdot W^0)^{\top}x ,
   \]
   where $W^0$ is the original weight matrix.  The transform is multiplicative, not additive.  Consequently, two orthogonal transforms can be merged into one by multiplying their matrices:  
   \[
     R_{\text{combined}} = R_2 R_1 .
   \]
   The result is still an orthogonal matrix.

3. **Parameter Efficiency**  
   Because only an $O(d\times d)$ orthogonal matrix (or a block‑diagonal variant) is learned per layer, each task adds only a few thousand parameters versus millions in classical finetuning.

These properties make OFT an attractive “adapter” that behaves like a plug‑in: you train it once on a dataset for one task, freeze $W^0$, then apply the learned $R$ at inference.

## Chaining Multiple Orthogonal Adapters  

Imagine we have three distinct tasks:

| Task | Description | Learned Orthogonal Matrix |
|------|--------------|---------------------------|
| *A* | Subject‑driven portrait generation | \(R_A\) |
| *B* | Canny‑edge guided inpainting | \(R_B\) |
| *C* | Semantic segmentation control | \(R_C\) |

Each matrix acts on the same set of weight layers within a Stable Diffusion base model.  By simply multiplying them:

```text
R_combined = R_C · R_B · R_A
```

we obtain a new adapter that ostensibly combines the abilities of all three tasks.  At inference time we just replace all ordinary weight matrices \(W^0\) with \(R_{\text{combined}}\cdot W^0\).  

### Potential Payoffs

1. **Zero‑Shot Mashups**  
   Instead of training a new model for every problem combination (portrait + edges + segmentation), we can produce “portrait + sketch” or “segmented portrait” in one shot by selecting an appropriate product of matrices.

2. **Fine Granularity**  
   If each orthogonal matrix only changes neuron directions slightly (i.e., stays close to identity), their product preserves overall stability while accumulating subtle edits—a form of linear blending that might yield smoother transitions between styles.

3. **Modular Experimentation**  
   Researchers can swap adapters on demand during experiments without retraining from scratch.  One could even precompute a library of “skills” and compose them online to respond to user prompts.

4. **Memory and Compute Savings**  
   Each adapter is small; composing them does not inflate memory footprints beyond the sum of their sizes (vs adding large new heads or networks).  

### Implementation Sketch

```python
# Assume `layers` is a list of weight tensors from Stable Diffusion
# and `adapters` is a dict mapping task names to learned orthogonal matrices.
def apply_combined_adapter(layers, selected_tasks):
    # Initialize combined matrix as identity
    R_combined = torch.eye(d)
    for t in selected_tasks:
        R_combined = adapters[t] @ R_combined
    # Apply to each layer
    new_layers = []
    for W in layers:
        new_layers.append(R_combined @ W)
    return new_layers
```

Because all updates are linear multiplications, GPU tensor ops remain efficient.

## Challenges That Must Be Tackled  

While conceptually elegant, several obstacles lie ahead:

| Challenge | Why It Matters | Possible Approaches |
|-----------|----------------|---------------------|
| **Orthogonality Drift** | Repeated multiplications may push numerical errors away from perfect orthogonality, potentially destabilizing the diffusion loop if the weight magnitudes explode or shrink. | Periodic re‑orthonormalization (e.g., QR or Gram–Schmidt updates), or constrain each adapter’s deviation from identity (as in COFT). |
| **Parameter Redundancy & Scaling** | After many compositions the combined matrix may become highly entangled, making it difficult to invert or interpret. Also block‑diagonal parameter sharing may limit expressiveness when multiple tasks are chained. | Explore hierarchical block structures; learn per-layer scaling factors separately; use sparsity constraints on each \(R\). |
| **Loss of Individual Task Fidelity** | Optimizing each adapter independently assumes tasks are independent; concatenation may force conflicting directions leading to degradation on one or more tasks (compositional interference). | Joint fine‑tuning of adapters after initial independent training; adversarial losses that enforce multi‑task consistency; regularizers that preserve pairwise similarity sets across tasks. |
| **Compositional Complexity** | Heuristically selecting which sequence of adapters works best for a given prompt becomes combinatorial (exponential). | Design hierarchical compositions (e.g., tree ensembles) or learn attention over adapters conditioned on prompts (adapter gating). |
| **Evaluation Metrics** | Standard single‑metric evaluations won’t capture if composites still meet each component’s performance goals simultaneously. | Develop multi‑criteria benchmarks where each task’s fidelity and control precision are measured in tandem; use control consistency metrics introduced in OFT’s paper for each subdomain. |

Addressing these will be key before we can routinely stack countless orthogonal transforms.

## Inspiration for Practitioners  

If you’re experimenting with OFT or any low‑rank adapter today:

1. **Save Your Deltas** – Store each trained orthogonal matrix separately rather than merging immediately.
2. **Try Small Compositions First** – Multiply two matrices and run sanity checks on output fidelity before scaling up.
3. **Measure Interference** – Evaluate each component task after composition to quantify drop–off.
4. **Use COFT Regularization** – Keep each matrix close to identity (\(\|R - I\|\leq \epsilon\)) so subsequent products stay numerically stable.
5. **Leverage Block Diagonals** – They reduce parameters but also enforce structured transformations—experiment whether shared blocks across tasks help maintain coherence.

By treating adapter matrices as *building blocks*, you unlock a form of generative pixie–dust: mix and match skills on demand without retraining entire models.

## Conclusion  

Orthogonal Finetuning already offers an elegant way to preserve pretrained semantics while adapting diffusion models to new tasks.  Extending this idea into a compositional framework—chaining multiple orthogonal transforms—opens thrilling avenues toward modular, zero-shot generation pipelines that could blend subjects, sketches, segmentations and more in real time.

The math is clean: multiplication preserves orthogonality and yields small extra parameters per task. The engineering challenges—maintaining stability, preventing interference, scaling gracefully—are nontrivial but tractable with careful regularization and joint fine‐tuning strategies.

If you’re ready to push boundaries beyond single-task finetuning, consider setting up your own simple experiment: train two OFT adapters on disjoint datasets (say portraits vs semantic segmentation), save their \(R\)’s, multiply them together and listen to what emerges when you run it through Stable Diffusion with different prompts.  You might discover unexpected creative mashups—or at least learn how composition behaves—and you'll contribute at home to this exciting line of inquiry!
