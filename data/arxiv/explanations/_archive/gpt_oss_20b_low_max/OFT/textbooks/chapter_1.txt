\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 1: # 7. Theoretical Analysis and Practical Insights

The orthogonal finetuning framework (OFT) already offers a principled way of protecting the semantic fabric of a large text‑to‑image diffusion model while adapting it to downstream tasks.  Yet, in practical deployments the balance between *flexibility* (the ability to steer the model toward novel behavior) and *stability* (the preservation of original generative quality) can still be delicate.  The **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning** variant (COFT) addresses this tension by adding a radius constraint in addition to the orthogonality requirement of OFT.  In the remainder of this chapter we dissect COFT from three complementary angles:
1. its mathematical formulation and constraint handling;
2. its impact on convergence dynamics and stability;
3. the broader conceptual trade‑offs it introduces.

We adopt the notation used in the original OFT paper: \( \bm{W}^{0} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n} \) denotes a pretrained weight matrix whose columns are the neurons of a fully connected (or equivalently, linearized convolution) layer, and \( \bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} \) is an orthogonal matrix that transforms those neurons during finetuning.

## Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)
### Definition

While OFT forwards activations by
\[
\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\cdot\bm{W}^{0})^{\!\top}\!\bm{x},\qquad
\bm{R}^{\top}\!\bm{R}=\bm{I},
\]
COFT imposes an additional **radius** constraint on how far \( \bm{R} \) may stray from the identity:
\[
\boxed{\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|_{F}\leq \epsilon}.
\]
Here \( \|\cdot\|_{F} \) is the Frobenius norm and \( \epsilon>0 \) is a user‐defined tolerance that governs the degree of allowed rotation/reflection around each neuron’s direction.

Intuitively, this restriction keeps every transformed neuron at most \( \epsilon \) units away from its original position on the hypersphere.  In contrast to augmenting software‑level regularizers such as weight decay— which act on Euclidean distances—the radius bound directly controls angular deviation: since all neurons on the unit sphere have norm one, small Euclidean displacement coincides with small angular change.

### Enforcing Orthogonality via Cayley Transform

To enforce both constraints simultaneously we parameterize \( \bm{R} \) by a skew‑symmetric matrix \( \bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^{\!\top} \):
\[
\boxed{\bm{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\bm{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}},
\]
known as **Cayley transform**.  This representation guarantees orthogonality for any real skew‑symmetric \( \bm{Q} \).  The Jacobian of this mapping is well‑behaved, allowing us to apply standard gradient‑based optimisation.

#### Approximation for Radius Constraint

Directly constraining \( \|\bm{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_{F}\leq \epsilon \) is inconvenient because computing \( (\mathbf{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1} \) during training incurs overhead every iteration.  More importantly, there is no closed‑form projection from an arbitrary skew‑symmetric matrix back onto the set
\( \{\bm{Q}\mid \|\mathcal C(\bm Q)-\mathbf I\|_{F}\leq \epsilon\}, 
\)
where \( \mathcal C(\cdot) \) denotes Cayley transform.

A practical remedy exploits the Neumann series expansion:
if \( \|\bm Q\|_2 < 1/2 \), then
\( (\mathbf I-\bm Q)^{-1}
= 
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\!\!\! (\bm Q)^k
= 
\mathbf I + 2\,{\color{gray}{(the first two terms)} } +O(\|\bm Q\|^2).
$
Truncating after the linear term yields:
\
\( 
   (\mathbf I+\bm Q)(\mathbf I-\bm Q)^{-1}
   =  
   (\mathbf I+\bm Q)(\,\mathbf I+\!2\,{\color{gray}{\,}}\,)
   = 
   \mathbf I +2\,{\color{gray}{\,}}\!+\mathcal O(\|\b Q\norm{}^2).
   \
)
The approximation tells us that
\( 
    \|\bm R-\mathbf I 
     = 
     2\,{\color {gray}{}}\!\|\b Q\norm{},
       +
        O(\|Q\norm{}^2).
    \
)
Thus enforcing
**
\( 
    {\color {gray}{$||Q||_F}}\leq {\color {gray}{$c\,(\epsilon)}$}
}
*
is effectively equivalent to keeping \( R-I \) within radius‑\( \epsilon \). In practice we simply perform projected gradient descent:

```
Backward()     # standard gradients for loss w.r.t Q
Update(Q, lr)
Project(Q, epsilon):    # min ||Q - constrain||_F s.t. ||Q||_F <= c*eps
```

By selecting \( c<1/2         ),
the second‑order term remains negligible during training and COFT behaves as intended.

### Parameter Efficiency via Block Diagonalisation

If we let every layer share its own full orthogonal matrix, COFT would still entail millions of trainable parameters—each with dimensionality roughly proportional to \( d^2/2          ).  
To curtail this cost we partition \( d\times d             )
orthogonal matrix into r blocks:
\
\( 
   {\bf R}
   =
   diag( R_1,\dots,R_r ),
   \
)
so that each block covers a subspace of dimension \( d/r          ).

Adopting identical block matrices across all layers (parameter sharing), $r$ can be chosen arbitrarily; common choices are $r=4$ or $r=16$, which reduces trainable parameters from $\mathcal O(d^2)$ to $\mathcal O(d^2/r)$ or even $\mathcal O(d^2/r^2)$ if blocks are shared across layers, while preserving exact orthogonality.

### Impact on Convergence & Stability

Empirically COFT exhibits markedly smoother convergence than vanilla OFT or existing low‐rank adaption methods like LoRA:

| Metric | Vanilla OFT | COFT |
|--------|-------------|------|
| Time to reach 90 % control accuracy | ~12 epochs | ~6 epochs |
| Variance over random seeds | high | low |

The radius constraint acts as an implicit regularizer preventing overly aggressive rotations that might violate semantic structures encoded in pretrained weights.  This restraint results in **fewer oscillations** in loss trajectories and reduces susceptibility to catastrophic forgetting—a phenomenon where invasive fine‐tuning obliterates foundational generative capabilities.

Furthermore, because COFT allows only small deviations from identity, downstream loss functions such as reconstruction or prior‐preservation (used in DreamBooth or ControlNet training loops) require far fewer gradient steps before stabilising; one can even use larger learning rates without risking divergence.

### Trade‑Offs & Practical Guidance

1. **Choice of ε**  
   - A large ε grants greater freedom for finite adaptation but reintroduces instability; tuning ε typically falls into [0.5, 3] depending on task difficulty.
   - If prior knowledge suggests that semantic shift should be mild—e.g., just adding a new object class—ε ≈ 0.5 suffices.
  
2. **Block Size r**  
   - Smaller r → fewer parameters but coarser rotation granularity.
   - Larger r → more expressive transformations but increased param budget; find sweet spot around r ≈ 8 for most diffusion layers.

3. **Compatibility with LoRA**  
   - COFT can be combined with LoRA: first apply an orthogonal transformation then add a low‑rank residual – potentially yielding even higher performance at moderate parameter cost.
   
4. **Evaluation of Hyperspherical Energy Conservation**  
   - Unlike plain distance metrics (e.g., Euclidean norm), monitoring hyperspherical energy before/after finetuning ensures that significant angular relationships among neurons are maintained.
   - COFT’s radius constraint further tightens this conservation implicitly.

---

## Summary

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning bridges the simplicity of OFT with an explicit radius guardrail that directly bounds angular drift from pretrained weights. The method is mathematically tractable thanks to Cayley parametrisation and simple projected gradients on skew‑symmetric matrices; computationally efficient due to block‑diagonal structures; and empirically delivers faster convergence and stronger stability across diverse downstream tasks like subject-driven generation and controllable image synthesis. Modifying ε provides an intuitive knob for practitioners to trade off flexibility against semantic preservation—a decisive advantage over blunt small‑learning‑rate finetuning or unstructured low‑rank updates such as LoRA.
