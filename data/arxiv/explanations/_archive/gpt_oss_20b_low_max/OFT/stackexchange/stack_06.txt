\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### `COE` vs `COFT`: Are they equivalent if we set ε=0?
Question:
If we set ε=0 in COFT (i.e., enforce R=I), would COFT reduce exactly to standard fine‑tuning with no orthogonal transformation? How does this special case differ from purely training W itself without any constraint? Are there any subtle differences in optimization dynamics when R starts at identity versus initializing W directly to W⁰?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
When the radius‑constraint in COFT is set to zero ($\epsilon = 0$), the orthogonal matrix $R$ is forced to be exactly the identity matrix. In that special case the forward pass of COFT collapses to

$$
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{I}\!\cdot \!\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}= (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x},
$$

which is *identical* to using the frozen pretrained weights $\mathbf{W}^0$. Hence COFT with $\epsilon=0$ does **not** perform any fine‑tuning at all – it behaves as a non‑trainable baseline.

This is fundamentally different from “training $W$ directly with no constraint”. In a naïve fine‑tuning experiment you would initialise $\mathbf{W}$ as $\mathbf{W}^0$ and then let the optimiser update every element of $\mathbf{W}$ freely (subject only to the chosen learning rate). After a few updates $\mathbf{W}$ will drift away from $\mathbf{W}^0$, and the optimisation trajectory will be driven solely by the downstream loss. By contrast, COFT with $\epsilon>0$ keeps $R$ close to $\mathbf{I}$ while still allowing rotations; gradients are projected back onto the orthogonal manifold and then onto the $\|\!R\!-\!\!I\|\le\epsilon$ ball. The “effective step size” in weight space is thus bounded not by an explicit learning‑rate hyper‑parameter but by two geometric constraints.

---

### Why does setting $\epsilon = 0$ kill all adaptivity?

1. **Orthogonality plus radius constraint**  
   $$
   R^\top R = RR^\top = I,\qquad \|R-I\|_2 \le \epsilon .
   $$
   For $\epsilon = 0$ the second inequality forces $R=I$. The first equation then becomes trivial. No degree of freedom remains for $R$, so it can’t learn a rotation or reflection.

2. **Absence of weight update**  
   The hidden representation in each layer is obtained by multiplying $R$ and $W^0$. If $R=I$, this becomes simply \(M=W^0\). Since no sub‑matrix of \(M\) is updated, gradients computed on downstream loss are effectively discarded; training stops after initialization.

3. **Interpretation as “freezing”**  
   In practice COFT with $\epsilon=0$ is equivalent to *frozen* diffusion model inference: you have allocated extra trainable parameters (the $r$ block matrices in $R$), but they are never moved from their initial value. The memory and GPU time saved compared to a full fine‑tune are thus not compensated – you get no practical benefit.

---

### How is this different from “training only $W$”?

| Scenario | What changes during training? | What stays fixed? | Practical effect |
|----------|--------------------------------|--------------------|-------------------|
| **Naïve fine‑tune** | Every scalar entry of $W$ (or any selected layer) is updated. | Pretrained initialization (chosen as a starting point). | Unstructured drift, risk of catastrophic forgetting, high variance w.r.t learning rate. |
| **COFT ($\epsilon>0$)** | Orthogonal matrix $R$ (block diagonal) is updated under Cayley parametrization; an additional small scale matrix $D$ can also be learned if Re‑scaled OFT is used. | Pretrained weight matrix $W^0$ remains intact; only its direction on each neuron set changes via a global rotation per layer. | Controlled angular drift, preservation of hyperspherical energy, bounded deviation from pretraining. |
| **COFT ($\epsilon=0$)** | None – $R=I$. | Full original weights unchanged. | No adaptation; identical to frozen pretrain model.*

---

### Optimization dynamics when starting at identity vs randomised $W$

Even though both approaches initialise at the same point ($W=W^0$, or equivalently $R=I, D=I$), their trajectories differ because of the geometry enforced during optimisation:

- **Standard fine‑tune**: Gradient descent moves directly along the Euclidean tangent space of all weight entries. Small perturbations can break neuron's normalisation or destroy pairwise angular relationships in a very irregular way—hence often observed mode collapse or blurry images when fine‑tuning diffusion models.

- **OFT/COFT (with ε>0)**: Gradients are first applied to a low‑dimensional parameter set (entries of $Q$, blocks of $D$, etc.) and then projected back onto the orthogonal manifold (via Cayley or parameterisation). The update direction always preserves pairwise cosine similarities between neurons because any product \( R\, W^0 \) rotates all neuron vectors simultaneously. Consequently, optimisation unfolds on a *sphere manifold* instead of flat Euclidean space, which empirically yields smoother loss curves and faster convergence for diffusion models.

When you start COFT with \(R=I\) (i.e., exactly at the pretrained weights) and apply a small learning rate, early iterations will resemble those of naïve fine‑tuning but will immediately be constrained by the orthogonality/projected updates—so although the initial steps might look wild, very quickly they become gentle rotations limited by $\epsilon$. In contrast, starting with \(W=W^0\) in a plain fine‑tune gives no such projection mechanism: every parameter update can instantly move away from \(W^0\).

---

### Bottom line

- Setting ε = 0 collapses COFT into a *non‑trainable*, frozen model; it does not reduce to standard fine‑tuning since no weight update occurs.
- Training only \(W\) allows unrestricted drift and typically jeopardises pretraining knowledge.
- Even if both methods start from \(W=W^0\), their optimisation landscapes diverge: naive updates explore Euclidean directions while COFT moves along an orthogonal manifold with bounded deviation.

So, while ε → ∞ makes COFT behave like OFT (full rotation freedom), ε = 0 makes it degenerate into a frozen baseline—far from equivalent to unconstrained fine‐tuning.
