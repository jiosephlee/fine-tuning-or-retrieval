\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Why does preserving hyperspherical energy matter for diffusion model finetuning?
Question:
The paper claims that preserving the hyperspherical energy of a weight matrix guarantees that the pairwise neuron relationships remain unchanged, which is presumed to be crucial for maintaining the generative quality of a pre-trained diffusion model. I understand that hyperspherical energy measures the sum of inverses of distances between normalized neurons, but why is this particular geometric invariant more informative than just keeping the Euclidean distance between pretrained and finetuned weights small? Are there empirical studies showing that deviations in hyperspherical energy directly correlate with generation quality degradation?
Answer:
**Answer**

In a diffusion model every layer contains a weight matrix  
$W=[w_1,\dots ,w_n]\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}$.  
During inference the activation produced by this layer is  
$z=W^\top x=\sum_{i}w_i^\top x$.  
The *direction* of each neuron $w_i$ (its position on the unit
hypersphere) determines the *relative* contribution of that neuron to
all possible inputs, whereas its norm only scales its magnitude.

---

### Why Euclidean distance is insufficient

A common intuition is to keep the finetuned matrix $W$ close to the
pre‑trained one $W^0$ in Euclidean terms:
$\|W-W^0\|_F$ should be small.  However, this metric does not respect
the geometry that actually matters for the network’s internal representation:

* **Rotation invariance** – If we rotate every neuron by the same
  orthogonal matrix $R$, i.e. $W=RW^0$,
  then $\|W-W^0\|_F$ can be arbitrarily large while all *angles*
  between neurons remain identical.  Empirically such a rotation does not
  change the model’s output distribution at all: the network still sees
  exactly the same set of cosine similarities, and consequently keeps
  its generative semantics.

* **Direction vs. magnitude** – The inner product used in most layer
  computations depends on both magnitude and direction:
  $w_i^\top x= \|w_i\|\|\hat w_i^\top \hat x\|\).
  Changes in magnitude alter scaling but leave the directional structure,
  which carries semantic meaning, largely unchanged.
  A Euclidean penalty would encourage shrinking magnitudes to match a
  pre‑trained value, potentially destroying useful scaling that was learned.

Thus Euclidean closeness can penalise harmless deformations (like a global rotation)
while still allowing subtle changes in relative angles that have large semantic impact.

---

### Hyperspherical energy as an invariant

Hyperspherical energy (HE),
$$
{\rm HE}(W)= \sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|},
\qquad \hat w_i=\frac{w_i}{\|w_i\|},
$$
sums over inverse Euclidean distances of *normalised* neurons on the unit hypersphere.
Two observations make HE especially informative:

1. **Pairwise angle preservation** – For two unit vectors,
   $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\| = \sqrt{2(1-\cos\theta_{ij})}$.
   Therefore HE is a monotonic function of all pairwise angles
   $\theta_{ij}$.  If all those angles stay the same, HE stays unchanged.
   Orthogonal transformations preserve all angles simultaneously,
   so enforcing constant HE guarantees that the entire geometric
   configuration of neurons is preserved.

2. **Semantic stability** – Prior work on spherical CNNs and angular‑based loss functions has repeatedly shown that cosine similarity between feature vectors correlates strongly with semantic similarity (e.g., Liu et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2020).  Since HE aggregates these similarities across *every* neuron pair, it reflects how much of that joint semantic structure is maintained.

Because OFT learns a *single* orthogonal matrix per layer,
it explicitly enforces that every neuron’s angle relative to every other neuron stays exactly as in the pre‑trained state. This guarantees that all local representations that rely on relative angles remain intact for diffusion sampling, while still allowing global re‑orientation that can adapt to new downstream signals.

---

### Empirical evidence from the paper

The authors performed several ablations that connect deviations in HE with generation quality:

* **Fast convergence and stability** – Fig. 5 (convergence curves) shows that when the HE difference is held at zero (OFT) or bounded (COFT), finetuning converges much faster and without mode collapse compared to naive fine‑tuning or LoRA. Conversely, when orthogonality is removed (OFT without orthogonality constraint), training becomes unstable and generates unrealistic images even after many epochs.

* **Subject‑driven experiments** – In Table 3, OFT and COFT achieve higher DINO/CLIP‑I metrics—measures of subject fidelity—than DreamBooth or LoRA while keeping prompt fidelity comparable. This indicates that preserving HE retains more of the original model’s ability to keep subject identity intact when adapting to few images.

* **Controllable generation** – The control consistency metrics introduced in §5.2 show that OFT/COFT deliver higher IoU/F1 scores on Canny edge tasks and lower landmark error on face alignment tasks than LoRA or ControlNet. Since these tasks rely heavily on maintaining relative feature relationships within layers, preserving HE directly translates into better control fulfilment.

While the paper does not report a separate regression of “HE‑gap → quality” curve, these controlled experiments demonstrate a clear *correlation*: whenever HE deviates from its pre‑trained value by a large amount (as happens with unregularised fine‑tuning), generation quality drops or exhibits instability.

---

### Intuitive takeaway

Think of each weight column as a point on an $(d-1)$-sphere.
The collection of points encodes a *shape*: their mutual distances encode how different features interact.
Diffusion learning relies on this shape; rotating it keeps Semantic pathways intact while changing it erodes them.
Hyperspherical energy is one scalar summarising this shape’s integrity.
Therefore preserving HE is far more decisive for keeping generative quality than merely shrinking Euclidean distance between two whole matrices—because HF captures exactly what matters: the *angular relationships* between neurons.

### What is the intuition behind block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices for convolutional layers?
Question:
In Section 3.3 the authors suggest using a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix R with r blocks to reduce parameter count. For fully connected layers this seems straightforward, but for convolutional or transformer attention layers it's not clear how blocks interact with channel or head dimensions. Could someone explain how each block operates on a subset of channels or query/key/value subspaces, and why this still preserves the desired hyperspherical invariants?
Answer:
**Answer**

In OFT the goal is to replace a pretrained weight matrix \($W^{0}\$) with a new matrix
\[
W \;=\; R\,W^{0},
\]
where \($R\in {\rm O}(d)\$) is an orthogonal matrix that is shared across all neurons of that layer.  
The paper explains that for a *fully connected* layer this is trivial: each row (or column) of
\(R\) merely rotates the vector space in which the neurons live, so the pairwise cosine
similarities between neurons are unchanged.  The same idea can be applied to *convolutional*
or *attention* layers, but because those mechanisms have extra channel/head
structure we need to decide how the blocks of \(R\) act on the “sub‑spaces” that are
processed together.

---

### 1.  What is a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix?

Let a layer have \(c\) logical “sub‑spaces” (input channels in a convolution, or query/key/value heads in a multi‑head attention).  
If we split the full dimension \(d\) into \(r\) blocks,
\[
R \;=\; {\rm diag}\,(R_{1},\,R_{2},\,\dots ,\,R_{r}), \qquad R_{i}\in {\rm O}(d/r),
\]
then \(R\) acts independently on each block.  
Because the blocks are orthogonal, each sub‑space is rotated/reflected within itself,
and no mixing occurs between different blocks.

* **Convolutional layers**  
  Convolutions apply weight tensors of shape \((C_{\text{out}}, C_{\text{in}}, k,k)\).  
  If we treat each input channel separately (i.e. set \(r=C_{\text{in}}\)), every
  block \(R_{i}\) is just a scalar \(+1\) or \(-1\).  This is equivalent to learning a
  depth‑wise kernel – the weights for each channel are scaled independently but
  their angles relative to one another are preserved.  If we share one matrix across
  all channels (\(r=1\)), then the same orthogonal transform is applied to *every*
  channel, which couples them but still keeps all intra‑channel correlations intact.

* **Transformer attention**  
  A multi‑head self‑attention layer processes each head in parallel.  
  Think of each head as a separate sub‑space of dimension \(d_{\text{head}}\).
  Choosing \(r\$ equal to the number of heads lets each head be rotated by its own
  \(R_i\).  Alternatively, sharing a single matrix across all heads ($r=1$)
  couples them, but still preserves cosine similarities among Q/K/V vectors within the
  same head, because attention only uses dot products inside a head.

Thus every block operates on a specific subset of channels / heads and aligns or
flips their basis vectors while keeping all inner products unchanged.

---

### 2. Why does this preserve hyperspherical invariants?

Hyperspherical energy depends only on pairwise cosine similarities:
\[
E(W) = \sum_{i<j} f(\langle w_i,w_j\rangle), \qquad w_i = \frac{\hat w_i}{\|\hat w_i\|}.
\]
If we apply an orthogonal matrix \(R\),
\[
w'_i = R\,w_i.
\]
Because orthogonal matrices preserve dot products,
\( \langle w'_i,w'_j\rangle = \langle w_i,w_j\rangle \).
Hence **all** pairwise similarities stay exactly the same, so Hyperspherical Energy is unchanged.

When we use a *block‑diagonal* \(R\), we rotate **independently** within each block.
Inside any given block the dot products among neurons belonging to that block are preserved,
and neurons that belong to different blocks are never mixed, so their pairwise angles also do not change (they are multiplied by two independent rotations—again preserving the angle because rotations act the same way on both vectors). Consequently, **every** pairwise similarity in the entire layer remains intact even though different sub‑spaces may rotate differently.

---

### 3. Intuitive picture

Imagine each neuron as a unit vector pointing somewhere on a \(d-1\)-dimensional sphere.
OFT’s job is simply to rotate every vector around that sphere without changing
the angles between any pair.  
For convolutional layers this means we’re applying independent “rotations” to each spatial channel – like spinning each color channel around its own axis – so that the overall geometry of feature patterns stays exactly what it was before finetuning.
For attention layers it means rotating each head independently or jointly in ways that keep every query–key dot product unaltered, thereby preserving how much attention flows between tokens.

Because ORTHOGONAL transformations do not alter any angles,
the **hyperspherical invariants** – which encode how close or far apart features sit on the sphere –
remain locked. This explains why OFT can aggressively adapt a model (by rotating whole sub‑spaces) yet still keep the pre‑training semantics intact.

---

### 4. Broader picture

The block‑diagonal formulation is simply an efficient way to learn an orthogonal transform that satisfies two constraints:

1. **Low parameter count.**  
   A full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix would cost \(\mathcal O(d^2)\) parameters; splitting into \(r\) blocks reduces this to \(\mathcal O(d^2/r)\), and sharing blocks reduces it further to \(\mathcal O(d^2/r^2)\).

2. **Controlled flexibility.**  
   By choosing how many blocks and whether they are shared,
   we decide how much cross‑channel coupling is allowed: many small blocks → almost depth‑wise;
   few large shared blocks → strong coupling.
   The paper shows empirically that moderately coupled (\(r>1,\;\text{not shared}\)) usually works best: enough freedom to adapt without destroying pre-trained knowledge.

In short, block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices give you an elegant knob:

- **“How many degrees of freedom?”** – set by `r`
- **“Do I want cross‑channel interaction?”** – set by whether blocks are shared

and they keep every hyperspherical invariant intact because orthogonal transformations preserve angles regardless of how you block them.



--- 

So when you see `R = diag(R₁,…,R_r)` applied to convolution or attention weights in OFT,
just think: every `Rᵢ` rotates only its own group of channels/heads, leaving all pairwise angles untouched and thus leaving hyperspherical energy — and therefore semantic structure — perfectly preserved while still giving you learnable parameters for finetuning.

### How does COBOT handle the constraint ‖R−I‖≤ε when using Cayley parametrization?
Question:
Section 3.4 introduces Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) requiring both orthogonality and a small norm deviation from the identity matrix. The authors propose approximating R ≈ I+2Q via a Neumann series and then constraining ‖Q‖≤ε′. However, I’m unclear about two points: (1) What is the exact relationship between ε in ‖R−I‖≤ε and ε′ in ‖Q‖≤ε′? (2) How are we supposed to enforce this norm constraint during training? Do we project Q onto a ball after each gradient update, and how does this affect back‑propagation through the Cayley transform?
Answer:
**Answer**

The constraint \(\|R-I\|\le \epsilon\) in COFT is a *hard radius condition* on the orthogonal matrix \(R\) that keeps the finetuned weights close to the pretrained ones.  
The Cayley parametrisation maps a free matrix \(Q\) (skew‑symmetric) to an orthogonal matrix via  

\[
R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}.
\]

Because the inverse is costly we approximate the Cayley mapping with a Neumann series.  
If \(\|Q\|<1\) then

\[
(I-Q)^{-1}=I+Q+Q^{2}+Q^{3}+\cdots ,
\]

and truncating after the first‑order term gives

\[
R\simeq I+2Q .
\]

Thus

\[
\|R-I\| \le \epsilon
        \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;
        \|2Q\|\le \epsilon
        \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;
        \|Q\|\le \epsilon' ,
\]
with \(\epsilon'=\,\epsilon/2\).  
So in practice we set a bound on the operator norm of \(Q\) that is simply half of the desired radius on \(R\).  This gives the first part of your question: \(\varepsilon'\) is (roughly) \(\varepsilon/2\) under the single‑term Neumann approximation.

---

### Enforcing the bound during training  

COFT learns \(Q\) (or rather, its entries that form a skew‑symmetric matrix) by gradient descent while maintaining two constraints:

1. **Orthogonality** – guaranteed automatically by the Cayley transform, provided \(I-Q\) is invertible.
2. **Radius** – enforced *after every gradient step* by projecting \(Q\) onto the Frobenius‑norm ball of radius \(\varepsilon'\).

In code this looks like:

```python
# q ← current parameters (skew-symmetric)
q = q - lr * grad_q          # vanilla SGD step
q = project_onto_ball(q, eps_prime)   # ← projection onto {‖q‖≤ε'}
```

The projection is cheap: if `np.linalg.norm(q, 'fro') <= eps_prime` nothing happens; otherwise scale q by `eps_prime / norm`.  Because this operation is differentiable almost everywhere, backpropagation continues to flow through the projection (the subgradient at the boundary is zero).  In practice we do not observe any adverse effect on training dynamics; on the contrary, it stabilises COFT as reported in Section 3.4 of the paper.

---

### What happens to back‑propagation through \(R\)?

After projecting \(Q\) we recompute \(R=\(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\).  
The Jacobian of \(R\,w.r.t\, Q\) is well‑defined whenever \(I-Q\) is invertible (which holds for small \(Q)\).  PyTorch’s autograd automatically handles this because `torch.inverse` and matrix multiplication are differentiable.  The projection only kills gradients in directions that would violate the radius constraint; elsewhere gradients flow unchanged.  Consequently, learning proceeds exactly as in ordinary OFT until a step would push \(Q\nicefrac{}{}\)! out of its allowed ball, at which point we snap it back and continue.

---

### Summary

1. **Relation between \(\varepsilon}\) and {\varepsilon'}**: Using the first‑order Neumann expansion gives \(R\simeq I+2Q\), so imposing \(\|R-I_\|\le\uppi{}\)\ reduces to \(\|Q_\|\le\uppi'\)\ with 𝜖′≈𝜖/2.
2. **Enforcement**: Update \(Q\,w.r.t.\,\text{grad}\) and then project onto {\(‖\,q‖≤ϵ'\)}. The projection is cheap and differentiable, so it does not break back‑propagation through \(R.\)
3. **Effect on training**: The hard radius constraint makes COFT less prone to drifting too far from pretrained weights, yielding more stable finetuning while still preserving hyperspherical energy exactly via orthogonality.

These details align with Equations (4)–(6) and Figure 5 in Section 3.4 of “Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning.”

### Why is re‑scaled OFT more flexible yet still preserves hyperspherical energy?
Question:
Section 3.5 adds a learnable diagonal matrix D to scale neuron magnitudes: z = (R·W⁰·D)^⊤x. Since scaling doesn't affect HE, why does this addition help performance? Does it effectively allow per‑neuron temperature adjustments that enable learning of residual magnitudes while keeping angles invariant? Is there an intuitive explanation for why including D improves image quality without breaking the hyperspherical preservation guarantee?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Adding a diagonal scaling matrix \(D\) does not change the hyperspherical energy because the energy is computed from *normalised* neurons.  
The scaling gives each neuron a learnable length so that the model can adjust the *magnitude* of feature contributions while keeping all pair‑wise angles (hence the energy) fixed.  
This extra flexibility lets the network tune how strongly each direction is used, which improves image quality without violating the orthogonal‑preservation guarantee.

---

### 1. Why hyperspherical energy is unchanged by a diagonal scale

Hyperspherical energy (HE) of a weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}\) is

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) \;=\;\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1},
\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i \;=\;\frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|}\!,
\]

so it depends only on the *directions* of the neurons, not on their lengths.  
If we multiply every neuron by a positive scalar \(s_i\),

\[
\mathbf{w}_i' = s_i\, \mathbf{w}_i,
\]

then \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}'_i = \hat{\mathbf{w}}_i\) and therefore
\(\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}')=\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})\).  
Hence a diagonal matrix \(D=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)\) leaves HE unchanged.

---

### 2. What does the extra scale do for finetuning?

Orthogonal finetuning (OFT) learns a single orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf R\) per layer:

\[
z = (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^0)^{\!\top}\!x ,
\quad
\mathbf R^{\!\top}\!\mathbf R = \mathbf I .
\]

This preserves all pair‑wise angles, so the “semantic geometry” encoded in the pretrained weights is untouched.  
However, after an orthogonal rotation some neurons may be more useful for the downstream task than others; their *magnitude* in the forward pass determines how much influence they have on the next layer.

By allowing a per‑neuron scale \(s_i>0\), OFT learns

\[
z = (\mathbf R\, \mathbf W^0\, D)^{\!\top}x ,
\quad
D = \mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n).
\]

Each \(s_i\) can grow or shrink independently while all angles remain exactly what they were after rotation.  
In effect, we add a **temperature‑like** factor to each neuron: neurons whose directions become favourable for the new task can be amplified; those that are less useful can be dampened.  This gives OFT a modest but powerful extra degree of freedom without destroying the guarantee that pair‑wise similarities stay put.

---

### 3. Intuitive picture: phase vs magnitude

Section 3.5 of the paper draws an analogy to Fourier analysis: the *phase* (direction) carries most of the semantic structure, while modifying *magnitude* changes stylisation or intensity.  
OFT’s orthogonal step changes phase; re‑scaled OFT also adjusts magnitude locally, much like tuning per‑filter gains in a convolutional network.  The combination lets us perform strong style or control modifications while staying on the same hypersphere surface.

---

### 4. Why it improves image quality

Empirically (Appendix E), re‑scaled OFT consistently yields higher CLIP‑I / DINO scores and better control consistency than plain OFT or LoRA:

- **Higher fidelity** – because scaling lets neurons emphasise subtle semantic cues that were previously muted.
- **Better regularisation** – HE stays fixed, so we avoid catastrophic forgetting of pretrained knowledge.
- **Stable optimisation** – optimisation landscape is smoother: we’re still moving on a manifold but with an extra radial direction that can be adjusted more cheaply than changing angles.

In short, scaling grants a cheap way to modulate how much each learned direction participates in synthesis, enabling finer control over style and content while keeping semantic structure intact.

---

### 5. Summary

Re‑scaled OFT adds a learnable diagonal matrix \(D\).  Scaling does not affect hyperspherical energy because HE depends only on normalised neurons.  The additional degrees of freedom let each neuron adjust its contribution strength, akin to per‑neuron temperatures or gains, which improves controllability and visual fidelity.  Meanwhile, pairwise angular relationships—and thus the preserved semantic geometry—remain untouched, so OFT’s core theoretical guarantee is maintained.

### `COE` vs `COFT`: Are they equivalent if we set ε=0?
Question:
If we set ε=0 in COFT (i.e., enforce R=I), would COFT reduce exactly to standard fine‑tuning with no orthogonal transformation? How does this special case differ from purely training W itself without any constraint? Are there any subtle differences in optimization dynamics when R starts at identity versus initializing W directly to W⁰?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
When the radius‑constraint in COFT is set to zero ($\epsilon = 0$), the orthogonal matrix $R$ is forced to be exactly the identity matrix. In that special case the forward pass of COFT collapses to

$$
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{I}\!\cdot \!\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}= (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x},
$$

which is *identical* to using the frozen pretrained weights $\mathbf{W}^0$. Hence COFT with $\epsilon=0$ does **not** perform any fine‑tuning at all – it behaves as a non‑trainable baseline.

This is fundamentally different from “training $W$ directly with no constraint”. In a naïve fine‑tuning experiment you would initialise $\mathbf{W}$ as $\mathbf{W}^0$ and then let the optimiser update every element of $\mathbf{W}$ freely (subject only to the chosen learning rate). After a few updates $\mathbf{W}$ will drift away from $\mathbf{W}^0$, and the optimisation trajectory will be driven solely by the downstream loss. By contrast, COFT with $\epsilon>0$ keeps $R$ close to $\mathbf{I}$ while still allowing rotations; gradients are projected back onto the orthogonal manifold and then onto the $\|\!R\!-\!\!I\|\le\epsilon$ ball. The “effective step size” in weight space is thus bounded not by an explicit learning‑rate hyper‑parameter but by two geometric constraints.

---

### Why does setting $\epsilon = 0$ kill all adaptivity?

1. **Orthogonality plus radius constraint**  
   $$
   R^\top R = RR^\top = I,\qquad \|R-I\|_2 \le \epsilon .
   $$
   For $\epsilon = 0$ the second inequality forces $R=I$. The first equation then becomes trivial. No degree of freedom remains for $R$, so it can’t learn a rotation or reflection.

2. **Absence of weight update**  
   The hidden representation in each layer is obtained by multiplying $R$ and $W^0$. If $R=I$, this becomes simply \(M=W^0\). Since no sub‑matrix of \(M\) is updated, gradients computed on downstream loss are effectively discarded; training stops after initialization.

3. **Interpretation as “freezing”**  
   In practice COFT with $\epsilon=0$ is equivalent to *frozen* diffusion model inference: you have allocated extra trainable parameters (the $r$ block matrices in $R$), but they are never moved from their initial value. The memory and GPU time saved compared to a full fine‑tune are thus not compensated – you get no practical benefit.

---

### How is this different from “training only $W$”?

| Scenario | What changes during training? | What stays fixed? | Practical effect |
|----------|--------------------------------|--------------------|-------------------|
| **Naïve fine‑tune** | Every scalar entry of $W$ (or any selected layer) is updated. | Pretrained initialization (chosen as a starting point). | Unstructured drift, risk of catastrophic forgetting, high variance w.r.t learning rate. |
| **COFT ($\epsilon>0$)** | Orthogonal matrix $R$ (block diagonal) is updated under Cayley parametrization; an additional small scale matrix $D$ can also be learned if Re‑scaled OFT is used. | Pretrained weight matrix $W^0$ remains intact; only its direction on each neuron set changes via a global rotation per layer. | Controlled angular drift, preservation of hyperspherical energy, bounded deviation from pretraining. |
| **COFT ($\epsilon=0$)** | None – $R=I$. | Full original weights unchanged. | No adaptation; identical to frozen pretrain model.*

---

### Optimization dynamics when starting at identity vs randomised $W$

Even though both approaches initialise at the same point ($W=W^0$, or equivalently $R=I, D=I$), their trajectories differ because of the geometry enforced during optimisation:

- **Standard fine‑tune**: Gradient descent moves directly along the Euclidean tangent space of all weight entries. Small perturbations can break neuron's normalisation or destroy pairwise angular relationships in a very irregular way—hence often observed mode collapse or blurry images when fine‑tuning diffusion models.

- **OFT/COFT (with ε>0)**: Gradients are first applied to a low‑dimensional parameter set (entries of $Q$, blocks of $D$, etc.) and then projected back onto the orthogonal manifold (via Cayley or parameterisation). The update direction always preserves pairwise cosine similarities between neurons because any product \( R\, W^0 \) rotates all neuron vectors simultaneously. Consequently, optimisation unfolds on a *sphere manifold* instead of flat Euclidean space, which empirically yields smoother loss curves and faster convergence for diffusion models.

When you start COFT with \(R=I\) (i.e., exactly at the pretrained weights) and apply a small learning rate, early iterations will resemble those of naïve fine‑tuning but will immediately be constrained by the orthogonality/projected updates—so although the initial steps might look wild, very quickly they become gentle rotations limited by $\epsilon$. In contrast, starting with \(W=W^0\) in a plain fine‑tune gives no such projection mechanism: every parameter update can instantly move away from \(W^0\).

---

### Bottom line

- Setting ε = 0 collapses COFT into a *non‑trainable*, frozen model; it does not reduce to standard fine‑tuning since no weight update occurs.
- Training only \(W\) allows unrestricted drift and typically jeopardises pretraining knowledge.
- Even if both methods start from \(W=W^0\), their optimisation landscapes diverge: naive updates explore Euclidean directions while COFT moves along an orthogonal manifold with bounded deviation.

So, while ε → ∞ makes COFT behave like OFT (full rotation freedom), ε = 0 makes it degenerate into a frozen baseline—far from equivalent to unconstrained fine‐tuning.

### Could multiple orthogonal matrices from different tasks be composed safely?
Question:
The paper mentions that orthogonal matrices from separate finetuning tasks can be multiplied together while remaining orthogonal. If we have trained OFT models A → B and C → D separately, would composing their R matrices yield a model that retains knowledge from both tasks? Are there theoretical guarantees about preserving all downstream capabilities when chaining these transformations?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Yes, the product of two orthogonal matrices is again orthogonal, so you can safely compose the *R* matrices that are learned on separate downstream tasks without losing orthogonality.  
However, there is no theoretical guarantee that the resulting model will retain the full set of abilities (i.e., the “knowledge”) that each individual fine‑tuned model had.  The paper only proves that hyperspherical energy is preserved when an *individual* OFT update is applied; it does not argue that concatenating several such updates keeps the pre‑trained semantics intact.  In practice the composed model will usually preserve most of the pre‑trained behavior and add **some** of the new functions, but it may lose or distort aspects that were specific to each task.

---

### Why orthogonality is preserved

Orthogonal matrices satisfy \(Q^\top Q = I\).  
If \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) are orthogonal, then

\[
(R_2 R_1)^\top (R_2 R_1)
= R_1^\top R_2^\top R_2 R_1
= R_1^\top I R_1
= I,
\]

so \(R_{\text{composed}} = R_2 R_1\) is again orthogonal.  
This fact holds regardless of whether the matrices come from different fine‑tune runs or were learned jointly.

### What happens to the network weights

For a given layer with pretrained weights \(W^0\), OFT replaces them by  
\(W = R W^0\).  Suppose we fine‑tune two tasks sequentially:

1. **Task A → B**: learn \(R_{AB}\), obtain \(W^{AB} = R_{AB} W^0\).
2. **Task C → D**: learn \(R_{CD}\), apply to *the same* pretrained
   weights and produce \(W^{CD} = R_{CD} W^0\).

Now compose both transformations:

\[
W^{AB \circ CD} = (R_{CD} R_{AB}) W^0 .
\]

The forward pass uses a single weight matrix obtained by a single
orthogonal rotation of the original weights.  The neural network’s
hyper‑spherical energy remains unchanged because any orthogonal rotation
preserves pairwise angles among neurons.

### Why there is no safety guarantee for preserving *all* downstream
capabilities

In OFT, the intuition is that changing only directions preserves the
semantic structure learned during pretraining, while small magnitude changes
are still tolerated.  Yet fine‑tuning along one direction (say,
\(R_{AB}\)) can rotate a subspace of neurons in a way that encodes the
specialization to task B.  Another rotation \(R_{CD}\) may interfere with
that subspace: it can rotate it into a different location or even partially undo it.
The paper’s proof only shows that *each individual rotation* keeps
hyperspherical energy constant; it does **not** prove that rotations
commute with respect to their semantic effects.

Empirically, many papers (including this one’s ablations) observe that

* The composed model often reproduces most of the pre‑trained generative
  quality, because hyperspherical energy is preserved.
* It also shows some signal from both tasks, but there can be **conflicts**—
  e.g., mixing a subject–driven task with a style–controlling task can lead to artifacts or loss of identity.

Thus, while you can multiply orthogonal matrices safely in an algebraic sense,
the *information-theoretic* guarantees (“retain every downstream capability”)
are absent unless additional constraints are imposed (e.g., ensuring that the rotations act on disjoint subspaces or adding regularizers on their interaction).

### Practical implications

- **If you only need to keep general generation quality**, composing –
  e.g., `R_total = R_{CD} @ R_{AB}` – will usually keep the model working.
- **If you need both fine‑tuned behaviours simultaneously**, you should verify:
  - Does the composed model still generate images resembling both subjects?
  - Does it obey both control signals?
  - If not, consider training a joint (“composite”) OFT with a combined loss or using techniques such as block‑diagonal splits so each task changes separate neuron groups.

### Bottom line

Orthogonal finetuning matrices from different tasks can be composed without breaking orthogonality, and in practice this often preserves much of the pre-trained generative ability.  However, OFT doesn’t provide a formal theorem guaranteeing that every downstream capability survived; trying several compositions empirically and measuring downstream metrics is still necessary.
