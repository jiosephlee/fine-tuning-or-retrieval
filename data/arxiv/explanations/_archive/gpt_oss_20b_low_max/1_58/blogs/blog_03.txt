\title{Blogs about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

# The New Scaling Law: When 1‑Bit Models Beat 16‑Bit Counterparts

## What’s a “1‑bit LLM”?

Large language models (LLMs) are usually built with 16‑bit floating‑point weights and activations. That means every parameter takes two bytes, and every arithmetic operation is a costly floating‑point multiply‑add. The new twist is to force every weight into a tiny ternary set \{-1, 0, +1\}. The resulting model, called **BitNet b1.58**, floats around 1.58 bits per weight (the “+0” value gives us a small bit‐rate boost over pure binary). Still, the arithmetic turns into almost pure integer addition—no multiplications at all in the large rope of linear layers that dominate LLM inference.

The paper shows that this extreme quantisation does **not** hurt performance. At 3 billion parameters, BitNet b1.58 reaches perplexities and zero‑shot accuracies that match or surpass the baseline LLaMA models running at full 16‑bit precision.

## The surprising scaling dynamics

### 3B BitNet beats 70B FP16

A key set of experiments trains each model on the same 100 B tokens of RedPajama data and evaluates on standard benchmarks (ARC‑Easy/Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, BoolQ). The results are summed up in two tables:

| Model | Size (B) | Perplexity | Zero‑shot accuracy |
|-------|----------|------------|---------------------|
| LLaMA (FP16) | 3 | X | Y |
| BitNet b1.58 | 3 | ~X | ~Y |
| BitNet b1.58 | 3.9 | >X | >Y |
| LLaMA (FP16) | 70 | X' | Y' |

Surprisingly, **BitNet b1.58 with just 3.9 B parameters outperforms the full‑precision 70B model** on both perplexity and end‑task accuracy while using **less GPU memory** and being up to **4× faster** in latency on large GPUs.

The scaling law immaterial to precision looks like this:

* **13 B BitNet ≈ 3 B FP16** in terms of latency, memory and energy.
* **30 B BitNet ≈ 7 B FP16** in terms of latency, memory and energy.
* **70 B BitNet ≈ 13 B FP16** in terms of latency, memory and energy.

These equivalences show that every doubling of *parameter count* in a binary model can offset roughly threefold increases in *floating‑point size*. In plain words: **the cost per parameter drops by more than a factor of three when you go from FP16 to a ternary representation**.

### Why does it work?

There are two synergy points that make ternary weights viable without hurting expressiveness:

1. **Explicit feature filtering via the zero weight**  
   By allowing a weight to be exactly zero, the network can “drop out” entire connections during training, acting like an implicit sparse regulariser. This improves optimisation dynamics compared to pure binary networks where every connection must be active.

2. **Faithful gradient signal through absmean quantisation**  
   The authors quantise weights using an *average‑absolute* scaling factor before rounding to \{-1, 0, +1\}. This keeps the magnitude of weight updates stable while still enforcing strict discreteness during forward passes.

Because all matrix multiplications collapse into integer addition counts (no comparison or multiplication overhead), training remains stable while inference becomes embarrassingly cheap.

## What does this mean for future LLM design?

### 1. Latency ≈ Memory ≈ Energy

In modern data centres and edge devices alike, three constraints drive cost: how fast a request finishes (latency), how many GPUs are needed for a given workload (memory footprint), and how much electricity it burns (energy). BitNet reaches higher speeds with less GPU memory and orders of magnitude lower arithmetic energy consumption—especially as model size grows due to its purely additive kernels.

### 2. New “Pareto Frontier”

Because higher accuracy now comes without ever scaling out the precision budget to the size of the model, practitioners can push *larger* models without blowing up compute budgets.

   * A single GPU can run an almost tenfold larger model compared with its FP16 partner.
   * Multi‑GPU pipelines for MoE – Traditionally MoE hit inter‑chip communication bottlenecks; with one‑bit weights activated across all experts the transfer bandwidth shrinks dramatically.

### 3. Edge & Mobile AI

With only ~8 bytes per param for all but tiny embedding tables (still FP16 at present), loading an entire network into embedded RAM becomes feasible. Combined with negligible multiplication intensity, CPUs on phones or tablets could run these models “by hand” with comparable accuracy to server‐grade boards.

### 4. Hardware Innovation

The authors explicitly call for hardware tailored to this paradigm: specialized “LPU” engines that take signed ternary inputs and produce integer additions by default; further LPU stages could compress the activation pipeline from INT8 down to INT4 or even INT2 for maximum density.

## Caveats & Open Questions

* **Training cost vs inference win:** Training still requires maintaining full FP precision for gradients; quantisation is applied only during forward/backward propagation for weight updates. This has a mild extra cost compared with fully mixed-precision training.
* **Zero attention bias design:** Removing biases and calibrating rotary embeddings might need careful tuning across architectures.
* **Long sequence handling:** While reducing activation precision doubles KV-cache capacity in benchmarks, real‑world long context use cases remain unexplored.
* **Scaling law generality:** Do we see the same equivalence if we move beyond RedPajama or include multimodal data?

Despite these open questions—still a hot research area—the evidence shows that BERGER-style ternary nets can become not just Hype but real competitive alternatives as models keep scaling.

## Takeaway

Meanwhile BPAs engineering teams wrestle with petabyte data sets and hundreds of GPU-hours per experiment; researchers now have a provably efficient recipe: drop all full‑precision weights into locked-in –1/0/+1 trits while preserving or boosting performance stats up to *70 billion* parameters or more.

That means you can build an AI system as powerful as current large GPTs or LLaMA while paying only about one third of the GPU memory load—and you’ll hit higher throughput without any drop in quality. The day when “big is better” is giddy—they’re saying maybe “more precise is unimportant” is still today’s big idea for LLMs.
