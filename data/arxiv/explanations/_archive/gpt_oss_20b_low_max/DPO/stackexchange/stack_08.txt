\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### "$oldsymbol{	heta}$” vs "policy”: Are they literally identical and learnable via standard LM training?
Question:
"Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model" suggests that once we embed the LM in the DPO loss, it acts both as policy and reward model.  Does this mean I can take any pre‑trained transformer and fine‑tune it with cross‑entropy on labelled pairs, without treating it separately as policy?  How do we preserve parameters like logits or temperature during this joint optimisation?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Yes – a single transformer that is fine‑tuned with the DPO loss already plays the two roles that a policy and a reward model play in traditional RLHF.  
When you train the LM with

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\rm DPO}(\theta;\,\pi_{\rm ref})
=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\Bigl[
    \log\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta
      \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_w|x)}
     -\beta
      \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_l|x)}
      \Bigr)\Bigr],
\]

the network $\pi_\theta$ is *the* policy; the logits it outputs are turned into an “implicit reward”

\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y)
  = \beta\,\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)}
  = \beta\,\bigl[\log \pi_\theta(y|x)-\log \pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)\bigr].
\]

This expression is exactly the re‑parameterisation derived in the paper from the optimal policy under a KL‑constrained RL problem (Eq. (6) in the main text).  
Because the exponential family that maps rewards to policies is one‑to‑one up to an additive function of $x$, this implicit reward is *any* reward that would give rise to the same preference distribution, so we do not lose modeling power even though we never train an explicit $r_\phi$.

In practice you can therefore:

1. **Choose a reference policy $\pi_{\rm ref}$** – usually an SFT model or one trained by simply maximizing likelihood of all preferred completions in your dataset.  
2. **Fine‑tune the same transformer** with normal cross‑entropy on prompt–response pairs, but *weighted* with the DPO term above.  
3. **Keep or ignore temperature** – during training you normally pass temperature = 1 (i.e., no tempering of logits). After training, any downstream temperature can be applied; it does not alter $\hat r_\theta$ because $\hat r_\theta$ depends only on log‑probabilities, which are invariant to a multiplicative rescaling of logits.

Thus you do *not* need a separate discriminator or reward network, and you avoid sampling from $\pi_\theta$ during fine‑tuning.

---

### Why this works—intuitive picture

* In standard RLHF you first learn $r_\phi$, then solve  
  \[
  \max_{\pi}\mathbb E_{y\sim\pi}[\,r_\phi(x,y)\,] - \beta\,D_{\rm KL}(\pi\,||\,\pi_{\rm ref}).
  \]  
  The closed‑form maximiser of this objective is exactly Eq. (3) in the paper:
  \[
  \pi_{r}(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)\,
                   \exp\!\Bigl(\frac1{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr).
  \]
* Plugging this expression back into the Bradley–Terry preference model removes the partition function $Z(x)$ and leaves a preference probability expressed *only* through $\pi_{r}$ and $\pi_{\rm ref}$.  
  Consequently, if we write *predicted preference* as
  \[
  p^*(y_1\succ y_2\,|\,x)
   = \sigma\!\bigl(
     \beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_{r}(y_1|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_1|x)}
    -\beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_{r}(y_2|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_2|x)}
     \bigr),
  \]
  we can replace $\pi_{r}$ by any policy network and optimise it directly.  

Thus the DPO loss is simply cross‑entropy between *observed* human preferences and *predicted* preferences, where predictions are computed from the current policy itself. No separate reward network is needed because its role has been “absorbed” into a log‑probability ratio.

---

### What happens to logits, temperature, etc.

The quantity that matters for learning is

```
implied_reward(y) = β · [ log πθ(y | x) – log πref(y | x) ] .
```

If you would like to preserve a particular temperature during generation:

* **During training**: keep β fixed (often β≈0.05–0.5). Temperature at this stage does not affect gradients because it only scales logits after they have been transformed by `log`.  
* **After training**: when sampling you may apply any temperature τ>0 simply by scaling logits before converting them to probabilities. This scaling changes `log πθ` by adding a constant that cancels out in the difference `log πθ – log πref`; therefore, applying temperature at inference time does **not** alter the implicit reward nor the relative ordering produced by DPO—only unitless scaling of probabilities.

If you want to keep track of an explicit “temperature parameter” during fine‐tuning (e.g., β as part of a weighted KL term), just treat β as another hyperparameter; adjusting β effectively tightens or relaxes the KL constraint relative to the reference policy, similar to how typical PPO experiments adjust `p_loss_coef` or `clip_range`.  

---

### Bottom line for your workflow

1. **Pick/ train a reference LM** (`π_ref`).  
2. **Fine‑tune a copy of that LM** with loss (8) from the paper—this is ordinary cross‑entropy on `(prompt, preferred)` pairs but wrapped inside a logistic term that compares two completions per example.  
3. No additional modules are needed; all parameters live in one transformer.  
4. At inference you can generate with any sampling temperature without affecting the learned alignment because only ratios of log‑probabilities enter the implicit reward.

So yes—take any pretrained transformer, fine‑tune it with DPO’s cross‑entropy loss on labelled pairs, and you will have both your new policy and its implicit reward simultaneously trained in one step.
