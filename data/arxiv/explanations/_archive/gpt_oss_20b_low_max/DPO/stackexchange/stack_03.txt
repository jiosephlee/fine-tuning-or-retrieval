\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What exactly is being optimized when we minimize $	ext{L}_{	ext{DPO}}$?  Is it a softmax over all possible completions?
Question:
The DPO loss (Eq. 8) involves $eta	ext{log}rac{	heta(y|x)}{	ext{ref}(y|x)}$ inside a logistic function.  In language generation there are astronomically many possible completions $y$.  How does this loss still correspond to an expectation over all completions?  Does it implicitly only depend on the two completions present in each preference pair?
Answer:
**Short answer**

When you minimise the DPO loss  
$$
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta )
=-\mathbb E_{\mathcal D}\!\left[\log
 \sigma\!\Bigl(
 \beta\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_w|x)}
-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_l|x)}
 \Bigr)\right]
$$
you are maximising the likelihood of the observed *pairwise* preferences under a Bradley–Terry model whose “utilities” are
$$
u_\theta(x,y)= \beta\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)} .
$$
The loss does **not** involve a softmax over *all* completions; it only depends on the two completions that appear in each preference pair.  The reward that appears inside the logistic function is an *implicit* reward computed from the ratio between the current policy and the reference policy, not an explicit probability distribution over all $\;y$.

---

### 1.  Why no softmax over all completions?

The original RLHF objective (Eq. (4) in the paper) is a KL‑constrained expected‑reward maximisation
$$
\max_{\pi}\;
 \mathbb E_{x,y}\bigl[\,r(x,y)-\beta\,\text{KL}(\pi(\cdot|x)\Vert \pi_{\rm ref}(\cdot|x))\,\bigr].
$$
For any reward $r$, this problem has an analytically tractable optimum:
$$
\pi_r(y|x)=
\dfrac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)\exp\!\left(\tfrac1{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
$$
Here $Z(x)=\sum_y \pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)e^{r/\beta}$.  Directly evaluating $Z(x)$ would require summing over *every* completion, which is infeasible when the output space is exponentially large.

Instead of trying to learn or estimate $Z(x)$, DPO exploits that we only need a *relative* measure of how much better one completion is than another.  Substituting the Gibbs form into the Bradley–Terry probability (Eq. (2) in the paper)
$$p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 | x)=
\sigma(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)),$$
shows that the partition functions cancel out:
$$
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 | x)
=
\sigma\!\Bigl(
\, \beta \log \tfrac{\pi^*(y_1|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_1|x)}
- \beta \log\tfrac{\pi^*(y_2|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_2|x)}
\,\Bigr).
$$
Thus, if we parameterise a policy $\pi_\theta$ we can directly express a model of human preferences as a function of that policy and $\pi_{\rm ref}$, without ever writing down a softmax over all completions.  The only summation that remains is inside the normalisation constant of each policy itself (its own $Z(x)$), but that constant is implicit because it normalises $\pi_\theta$ already.

Equation (2) is exactly what DPO’s loss minimises: it drives $\pi_\theta$ so that its pairwise preference probabilities match the empirical labels in $\mathcal D$.  The logistic function therefore compares *ratios* of probabilities for two completions only.

---

### 2.  What does DPO optimise, technically?

The loss can be rewritten as a cross‑entropy between two Bernoulli distributions:
* The “data” Bernoulli has outcome 1 if the human prefers $y_w$.
* The “model” Bernoulli has success probability
  $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_w)-\hat r_\theta(y_l))$,
  where
  $$
  \hat r_\theta(x,y)\;=\;\beta
    \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)} .
  $$

Minimising this loss therefore maximises
the log‑probability that, for each preference pair,
the policy correctly ranks the preferred completion higher in terms of its *implicit reward* $\hat r_\theta$ than the disfavoured one.  
Because $\hat r_\theta$ itself depends logarithmically on the probability assigned to $y$ by $\pi_\theta$, updates naturally increase $\pi_\theta(y_w)$ and decrease $\pi_\theta(y_l)$, exactly as an RL value–function gradient would do – but without sampling trajectories or estimating returns.

The critical point is that **only** those two completions appearing in each example enter into the scalar quantity inside $\sigma$.  
All other completions are irrelevant for that gradient step; they influence gradients indirectly through how they affect the normalisation of $\pi_\theta$ during training, but they never appear explicitly in any per‑example computation.

---

### 3.  Intuition behind “softmax over all completions” being unnecessary

In standard max‑likelihood training of language models you do optimise a softmax over every token at every position:  
$p(y_t | y_{<t}, x) = 
   e^{z_t}/(\sum_v e^{z_v})$.
When you learn from *pairwise* preferences instead, you no longer care about how likely every other completion is, except for how they compare to your two candidates.  
The Bradley–Terry (or Plackett–Luce) model encodes just this comparison:
the odds ratio between two outcomes depends solely on their utilities, not on all other utilities simultaneously.

Consequently, DPO bypasses any explicit softmax over all completions because it never needs to normalise across them when computing losses or gradients; it relies on ratios that are naturally invariant to adding constants to all utilities (thanks to the equivalence class argument in § 4 of the paper).  

This also explains why DPO can train efficiently even when language models have billions of parameters: each gradient update considers only a handful of tokens (the ones that comprise $y_w$ and $y_l$), not an entire probability simplex over millions of possible outputs.

---

### 4.  Connection to broader optimisation frameworks

DPO’s reparameterisation reminds one of **control‑as‑inference** formulations where optimal policies take a Gibbs form with respect to a given reward plus entropy term.  
Here:

* Reward → implicit reward via log‑ratio,
* Entropy/regulariser → KL term between policy and reference model.

Because all these components appear in closed form, you can convert an RL objective into an exact maximum‑likelihood problem—exactly what DPO does by substituting Eq.(1) into a Bradley–Terry likelihood.

So **in summary**: minimizing \(L_{\text{DPO}}\) optimises an expectation over human preferences by matching pairwise Bradley–Terry probabilities derived from *ratios* of current and reference policies. It never requires a softmax over every possible completion; instead it exploits the mathematical property that those ratios suffice to model human ordering behaviour.
