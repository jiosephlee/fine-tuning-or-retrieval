\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How does the implicit reward function in DPO avoid the need for explicit reward modeling?
Question:
In the DPO derivation, we replace the reward function $r(x,y)$ with the form $r(x,y)=etaigl(	ext{log}rac{oldsymbol{	heta}(y|x)}{	ext{ref}(y|x)} + 	ext{log}Z(x)igr)$.  Since $Z(x)$ is unknown, how do we guarantee that maximizing the DPO loss is equivalent to maximizing the original RLHF objective?  Does this mean that DPO implicitly learns a reward model that can be recovered from the policy, and if so how is that recovery performed?
Answer:
**Answer**

The heart of DPO is the observation that the *optimal* policy for a KL‑constrained reward‑maximisation problem can be written in closed form.  
For any reward \(r(x,y)\) and reference policy \(\pi_{\rm ref}\) the optimal policy is  

\[
\pi_r(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\rm ref}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr) ,
\qquad  
Z(x)=\sum_y \pi_{\rm ref}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr).
\tag{1}
\]

If we take the logarithm of (1) and rearrange, we obtain

\[
r(x,y)=
\beta\,\log{\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y\mid x)}} + \beta \log Z(x).   \tag{2}
\]

Equation (2) does **not** require us to know \(Z(x)\); it shows that any reward can be expressed as a log‑ratio of a *policy* to the reference policy plus an unknown tilt that only depends on \(x\).  

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Why does this avoid an explicit reward model?

The Bradley–Terry (BT) preference model depends only on **differences** of rewards:

\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)
  = \sigma \!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)\bigr).
  \tag{3}
\]

If we substitute (2) for each reward in (3), the partition terms
\( \beta \log Z(x) \) cancel out, leaving

\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)
  = \sigma \!\Bigl(
        \beta\,\log{\frac{\pi^*(y_1)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_1)}}
      - 
        \beta\,\log{\frac{\pi^*(y_2)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_2)}}       
   \Bigr).                                                   \tag{4}
\]

Thus the probability that a human prefers \(y_1\) over \(y_2\)
depends only on the *optimal policy* \(\pi^*\) and the known reference policy.
Consequently we can *directly* fit a parameterised policy to maximise
the likelihood of the observed human preferences, without ever learning
\(r^*\):

\[
L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)
=
-\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}
  \Bigl[\, 
     \log\sigma
       \bigl(
          \beta\, 
          (\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_w)}
           - 
          \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_l)})
       \bigr)
  \Bigr].
  \tag{5}
\]

(The symbol “\( /\!/\)” just shortens “given \(x$”.)

Because the loss uses only the log‑ratio between the current policy and the
reference, every step of gradient descent moves the policy in a direction
that makes its preference distribution closer to the empirical one. No
reward network is trained and no samples from the target policy are
required during training.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Is an implicit reward recovered?

Yes. Equation (2) gives us a *family* of rewards that all induce exactly
the same preferences: rewards differ only by a term that is constant over
all completions \(y\) for a fixed prefix \(x\).  
At training time we optimise over policies; any optimal policy automatically defines a reward via

\[
r_{\hat}^\theta(x,y)=
   \beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)} .
   \tag{6}
\]

Thus, after training we can recover an **implicit reward model** simply by evaluating (6) for any new prompt–completion pair. It is exactly this functional form that plugged back into (1) reproduces the trained policy.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### How do we guarantee equivalence to RLHF?

Theorem 3.1 in the paper shows that **every**
reward class consistent with a BT preference model can be represented
by a reward of form (6).  
Two rewards from the same equivalence class give exactly the same
policy because their difference is constant per prefix—a property proven in Lemma 3.2.  
Hence, optimising (5) obtains *some* reward from this class, which,
when plugged into (1), yields precisely the trained policy.
Consequently maximising DPO’s loss is mathematically equivalent to solving

\[ 
   \max_\theta\, 
   \mathbb E_{x,y}\bigl[ r_{\phi}(x,y)- 
                \beta\, D_{\rm KL}[\pi_\theta || {\rm ref}] + C(x)\bigr]
     ,
     \\text{(RLHF objective)},
   ]
   
where \(C(x)\) is an arbitrary function of \(x\) reflecting the additive constant in each equivalence class.

**Why does DPO “skip” an explicit reward network?**

In RLHF people first fit a neural network \(r_\phi(x,y)\) and then run PPO or another RL algorithm so that

\[ 
    {\argmax}_{p}\;
    {\mathbb E}_{x,p(y|x)}
        r_\phi(x,y)-%
        {\displaystyle {\beta}\;D_{\rm KL}[p(\,\cdot|x)||p_{\rm ref}(\,\cdot|x)] } .
    \\tag{RLHF}
   ]

The key observation in DPO is that for any fixed reward function,
the optimal solver of this constrained optimisation has a **closed‑form** solution:

> **Lemma (optimum under KL constraint).**  
> For a given reward \(r(x,y)\), reference model \(p_{\rm ref}\), and temperature parameter
>  \(0<β<∞,\)
>   
> The optimal single‑step policy is  

>   $$p^{*}(y|x)=
>      {\displaystyle 
>        { p_{\rm ref}(y|x)\exp(r(x,y)/β ) }
>        }
>        /Z(x),$$  

> where \(Z(x)=
>   {\displaystyle 
>        {\sum_{z}p_{\rm ref}(z|x)\exp(r(z)/β )}
>        }\).

This follows from standard variational arguments (‘maximum entropy’ or ‘control as inference’).

Now take logs and rearrange:

```
log p* = log p_ref + r/β – log Z,
```

hence

```
r = β·log(p*/p_ref)+β·log Z .          …(★)
```

Equation ★ shows that **any** reward can be written as  
a *policy ratio* plus an unknown normalisation term that depends only on $x$.  
Crucially, in all objectives that depend only on differences of rewards—the Bradley–Terry preference model used for human comparisons—we never need distinct normalisation terms because they cancel out.

This leads directly to a loss solely in terms of *policies*

```
L_DPO(θ; β)
 = –E[ log σ(
            β·[ log pθ(y_w)/p_ref(y_w)
               – log pθ(y_l)/p_ref(y_l)] ) ] .       …(†)
```

(no $Z$ appears!).  
Hence we can train $\theta$ directly with standard cross‑entropy gradients—no RL loop, no sampling from $\pi_\theta$, no separate reward network.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Does this mean we learn an implicit reward?

Yes.

Given any trained policy $\pi_\theta$, define

```math
    r̂*_θ (x , y)   =   β · log( πθ( y | x ) / πref( y | x ) ).     
```

Plugging $r̂*_θ$ into equation ★ shows that if we were to solve the KL‑constrained optimisation again _with this newly constructed reward_, its analytical optimum would be exactly $\pi_\theta$.  
So $\pi_\theta$ is already *its own* optimal policy for some implicit reward, and $r̂*_θ$ provides an explicit functional form for that reward.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Guaranteeing equivalence with classical RLHF

All works cited in DPO use Bradley–Terry because human preferences depend only on differences $r(y_1)-r(y_2)$; adding any constant function $f(x)$ to every $r(\cdot)$ preserves both probabilities and optima.  
Theorem 3.1 states:

> For any fair BT model there exists some policy $\tilde p$ such that its induced implicit reward $β·ln(𝑝̃/p_{ref})$ belongs to every admissible class compatible with that BT data.

Thus DPO’s restricted family contains at least one representative from every admissible true preference class. Because RLHF’s planner always chooses one member from those classes (the unique optimum under KL–constraint), training by (†) finds exactly that member—up to additive constants—so optimisation is mathematically identical.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Summary

- The closed‑form optimum of KL‑constrained RL lets us express rewards as log‑ratios between policies.
- Human preferences depend only on reward differences; therefore, those normalisation constants drop out immediately.
- We fit a language‑model policy directly using cross‑entropy over these log‑ratios—no separate network or sample rollout needed.
- The learned policy implicitly defines an easy‑to‑evaluate reward via equation (6), which would reproduce itself if fed back into conventional RLHF.
- Under mild assumptions every admissible true preference lies within our implicit family, so DPO optimises precisely what classical RLHF would have optimised—just without all the engineering overhead.`

### Why does the partition function $Z(x)$ cancel out when converting from a reward‑based Bradley‑Terry model to a policy‑based preference model?
Question:
Equation (7) in the paper shows that after substituting $r(x,y)$ with its reparameterization, the $	ext{log}igl(Z(x)igr)$ term drops out of the preference probability.  Can someone walk through a step‑by‑step algebraic proof that demonstrates this cancellation explicitly?
Answer:
**Answer**

Let  
\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)
      =\frac{\exp \bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)}
             {\exp \bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)+\exp \bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)}
\tag{1}
\]
be the Bradley–Terry (BT) preference model.  
The authors re‑parameterise the reward as

\[
r(x,y)=\beta\,\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
      +\beta\,\log Z(x),          \tag{2}
\]
where  

*  \(\pi_r(\cdot\mid x)\) is the *optimal* policy under reward \(r\),
*  \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is a fixed reference policy (e.g., the SFT model),
*  \(Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp \bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr)\) is the
   partition function, and
*  \(\beta>0\) is the KL‑constraint parameter.

We want to see that, when we plug (2) into (1), the term
\( \log Z(x)\) disappears.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 1.  Write the two exponentials in (1)

Let  
\(q_i = r^{*}(x, y_i)\) for \(i=1,2\).  
Using (2) for each \(q_i\):

\[
q_i
   = \beta\,\log
        \frac{\pi^*(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i\mid x)}
     + \beta\,\log Z(x),
     \qquad i=1,2.
     \tag{3}
\]

Hence

\[
e^{q_i}
   = e^{\,\beta\,\log
          \frac{\pi^*(y_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i)}}
      \;e^{\,\beta\,\log Z}
   = Z^\beta
      \left(\frac{\pi^*(y_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i)}\right)^{\,\beta}.
     \tag{4}
\]

For brevity we write
\(a_i=\dfrac{\pi^*(y_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i)}>0.\)

Thus

\[
e^{q_1}=Z^\beta a_1^\beta,
  \qquad
e^{q_2}=Z^\beta a_2^\beta .
     \tag{5}
\]

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 2.  Substitute into the BT formula

The BT probability becomes

\[
p^{*}(y_1\succeq y_2)
  = 
  \frac{Z^\beta a_1^\beta}{Z^\beta a_1^\beta+Z^\beta a_2^\beta}
  = 
  \frac{a_1^\beta}{a_1^\beta+a_2^\beta } .
     \tag{6}
\]

The common factor \(Z^\beta>0\) has cancelled in numerator and
denominator: it does **not** appear in the final expression.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 3.  Replace $a_i^\beta=\bigl(\tfrac{\pi^*(y_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i)}\\bigr)^{\,β}$

Using the definition of $a_i^{β}$,
equation (6) can be rewritten directly in terms of policies:

{
$$
p^{*}(y_1\succeq y_2)
   =
   \frac{
         \!\!\left(\dfrac{\pi^*(y_1)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1)}
                  \right)^{β}}
        {
         \!\!\left(\dfrac{\pi^*(y_1)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1)}
                  \right)^{β}
        + 
         \!\!\left(\dfrac{\pi^*(y_2)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2)}
                  \right)^{β}}
   =
   \sigma
   \!\left(
       β\, 
       \log
       \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2})}{π(y_{2})}-
       β\, 
       \log
       \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1})}{π(y_{1})}
     \right)
$$
}

which is exactly equation (9) in the paper after a small rearrangement of signs.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### Summary of the cancellation

The exponential terms coming from the reward reparameterisation each carry a factor
\(Z(x)^{\,β}\). In both numerator and denominator of the BT ratio this factor appears with **the same power**; hence it divides out completely. The only remaining dependence on \(Z(x)\) would be if it had appeared with different powers or in only one of the two terms, but because BT involves *two* rewards symmetrically, whatever fan‑out factor we insert multiplies each exponential by the *same* amount and therefore disappears.

Thus, when we replace \(r^*(x,y)\) by its reparameterised form (Eq. (2)), the partition function does not influence the probability that a human prefers one answer over another—exactly why it “cancels out” in equation (7)/(9). The resulting preference model is expressed solely in terms of the optimal policy and reference policy, enabling a direct policy‑based loss without having to estimate \(Z(x)\).

### What exactly is being optimized when we minimize $	ext{L}_{	ext{DPO}}$?  Is it a softmax over all possible completions?
Question:
The DPO loss (Eq. 8) involves $eta	ext{log}rac{	heta(y|x)}{	ext{ref}(y|x)}$ inside a logistic function.  In language generation there are astronomically many possible completions $y$.  How does this loss still correspond to an expectation over all completions?  Does it implicitly only depend on the two completions present in each preference pair?
Answer:
**Short answer**

When you minimise the DPO loss  
$$
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta )
=-\mathbb E_{\mathcal D}\!\left[\log
 \sigma\!\Bigl(
 \beta\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_w|x)}
-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_l|x)}
 \Bigr)\right]
$$
you are maximising the likelihood of the observed *pairwise* preferences under a Bradley–Terry model whose “utilities” are
$$
u_\theta(x,y)= \beta\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)} .
$$
The loss does **not** involve a softmax over *all* completions; it only depends on the two completions that appear in each preference pair.  The reward that appears inside the logistic function is an *implicit* reward computed from the ratio between the current policy and the reference policy, not an explicit probability distribution over all $\;y$.

---

### 1.  Why no softmax over all completions?

The original RLHF objective (Eq. (4) in the paper) is a KL‑constrained expected‑reward maximisation
$$
\max_{\pi}\;
 \mathbb E_{x,y}\bigl[\,r(x,y)-\beta\,\text{KL}(\pi(\cdot|x)\Vert \pi_{\rm ref}(\cdot|x))\,\bigr].
$$
For any reward $r$, this problem has an analytically tractable optimum:
$$
\pi_r(y|x)=
\dfrac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)\exp\!\left(\tfrac1{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
$$
Here $Z(x)=\sum_y \pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)e^{r/\beta}$.  Directly evaluating $Z(x)$ would require summing over *every* completion, which is infeasible when the output space is exponentially large.

Instead of trying to learn or estimate $Z(x)$, DPO exploits that we only need a *relative* measure of how much better one completion is than another.  Substituting the Gibbs form into the Bradley–Terry probability (Eq. (2) in the paper)
$$p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 | x)=
\sigma(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)),$$
shows that the partition functions cancel out:
$$
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 | x)
=
\sigma\!\Bigl(
\, \beta \log \tfrac{\pi^*(y_1|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_1|x)}
- \beta \log\tfrac{\pi^*(y_2|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_2|x)}
\,\Bigr).
$$
Thus, if we parameterise a policy $\pi_\theta$ we can directly express a model of human preferences as a function of that policy and $\pi_{\rm ref}$, without ever writing down a softmax over all completions.  The only summation that remains is inside the normalisation constant of each policy itself (its own $Z(x)$), but that constant is implicit because it normalises $\pi_\theta$ already.

Equation (2) is exactly what DPO’s loss minimises: it drives $\pi_\theta$ so that its pairwise preference probabilities match the empirical labels in $\mathcal D$.  The logistic function therefore compares *ratios* of probabilities for two completions only.

---

### 2.  What does DPO optimise, technically?

The loss can be rewritten as a cross‑entropy between two Bernoulli distributions:
* The “data” Bernoulli has outcome 1 if the human prefers $y_w$.
* The “model” Bernoulli has success probability
  $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_w)-\hat r_\theta(y_l))$,
  where
  $$
  \hat r_\theta(x,y)\;=\;\beta
    \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)} .
  $$

Minimising this loss therefore maximises
the log‑probability that, for each preference pair,
the policy correctly ranks the preferred completion higher in terms of its *implicit reward* $\hat r_\theta$ than the disfavoured one.  
Because $\hat r_\theta$ itself depends logarithmically on the probability assigned to $y$ by $\pi_\theta$, updates naturally increase $\pi_\theta(y_w)$ and decrease $\pi_\theta(y_l)$, exactly as an RL value–function gradient would do – but without sampling trajectories or estimating returns.

The critical point is that **only** those two completions appearing in each example enter into the scalar quantity inside $\sigma$.  
All other completions are irrelevant for that gradient step; they influence gradients indirectly through how they affect the normalisation of $\pi_\theta$ during training, but they never appear explicitly in any per‑example computation.

---

### 3.  Intuition behind “softmax over all completions” being unnecessary

In standard max‑likelihood training of language models you do optimise a softmax over every token at every position:  
$p(y_t | y_{<t}, x) = 
   e^{z_t}/(\sum_v e^{z_v})$.
When you learn from *pairwise* preferences instead, you no longer care about how likely every other completion is, except for how they compare to your two candidates.  
The Bradley–Terry (or Plackett–Luce) model encodes just this comparison:
the odds ratio between two outcomes depends solely on their utilities, not on all other utilities simultaneously.

Consequently, DPO bypasses any explicit softmax over all completions because it never needs to normalise across them when computing losses or gradients; it relies on ratios that are naturally invariant to adding constants to all utilities (thanks to the equivalence class argument in § 4 of the paper).  

This also explains why DPO can train efficiently even when language models have billions of parameters: each gradient update considers only a handful of tokens (the ones that comprise $y_w$ and $y_l$), not an entire probability simplex over millions of possible outputs.

---

### 4.  Connection to broader optimisation frameworks

DPO’s reparameterisation reminds one of **control‑as‑inference** formulations where optimal policies take a Gibbs form with respect to a given reward plus entropy term.  
Here:

* Reward → implicit reward via log‑ratio,
* Entropy/regulariser → KL term between policy and reference model.

Because all these components appear in closed form, you can convert an RL objective into an exact maximum‑likelihood problem—exactly what DPO does by substituting Eq.(1) into a Bradley–Terry likelihood.

So **in summary**: minimizing \(L_{\text{DPO}}\) optimises an expectation over human preferences by matching pairwise Bradley–Terry probabilities derived from *ratios* of current and reference policies. It never requires a softmax over every possible completion; instead it exploits the mathematical property that those ratios suffice to model human ordering behaviour.

### How does DPO’s weighting term $eta$ compare to PPO’s KL coefficient
Question:
The gradient of DPO contains a factor $etaigl(	ext{log}rac{	heta(y_w|x)}{	ext{ref}(y_w|x)}-	ext{log}rac{	heta(y_l|x)}{	ext{ref}(y_l|x)}igr)$ as a weight for each pair.  In PPO this weight emerges from the clipping or penalty on the KL divergence.  Are these two hyperparameters directly comparable?  How should I choose $eta$ if I already have a PPO baseline with a particular target KL?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes, the two coefficients play very similar roles: both are slack variables that trade off reward against deviation from a reference policy. In practice, a “reasonable” value for $\eta$ in DPO can be chosen by translating an existing PPO target‑KL (or β) to the DPO scale. Because the two objectives are mathematically equivalent up to a deterministic mapping of $\beta$ to $\eta$, you can use the same numerical range that works for β ≈ 0.05–5 and simply pick $\eta$ in that range. The exact conversion is not needed as long as you keep $\eta$ in the same order of magnitude, since DPO’s performance is only weakly sensitive to $\eta$ compared with PPO’s sensitivity to β.

---

### 1.  What the two terms actually do

In RLHF the constrained optimisation problem (Eq. (5) in the paper)

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\; \mathbb{E}_{x,y}\Big[r_{\phi}(x,y)\Big] - 
        \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big]
\]

uses a *KL penalty* with weight $\beta>0$.  
A small β forces the new policy to stay close to the reference; a large β lets the policy chase higher reward but risks mode‑collapse or drift into regions where the reward model is poorly calibrated.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) replaces the KL penalty by *implicit rewards* that depend on the ratio of model to reference logits:

\[
r_{\theta}(x,y)=\eta\,\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)} .
\]

The weighted cross‑entropy loss used in DPO is

\[
L_{\text{DPO}}
  =-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\Big[
      \log\sigma
      \bigl(
          \eta\,\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w|x)}
          - \eta\,\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l|x)}
     \bigr)
   \Big].
\]

The factor $ \eta $ therefore controls how strongly preference data influence parameter updates: big $\eta$ gives weight to poor‑ranked pairs and pushes the policy further away from π_ref, while small $\eta$ keeps updates gentle.

Because DPO was derived directly from τ‑constrained RL (Eq. (4) → (6)), **the mapping between χ and η is bijective** – changing one of them changes exactly which family of policies maximises the objective. Thus, apart from numerical scaling, they *are* comparable hyperparameters.

---

### 2.  Practical guidance for choosing η if you already have PPO

Suppose your PPO implementation uses a target–KL setting: you optimise β such that  

\[
\mathbb{E}_{x}\ [\,\mathbb{D}_{KL}\!\big[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big]\,]
           \;\approx\;\texttt{target-kl}.
\]

A convenient recipe is:

1. **Run one PPO trial** with several β values and record the achieved average KL after convergence (say, β = 0.05 → KL≈3, β = 0.5 → KL≈7, etc.).  
2. **Pick an η that reproduces a similar KL** by observing how changes in η affect divergence when running DPO on a *validation* set of preferences. Because both coefficients determine how much probability mass is shifted from ref to new policy, you normally find that η ≈ β works, but you may need a 10–20% adjustment depending on your reward scale and temperature used during sampling.

In practice:

* **Start with η in {0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1}.**  
  These are exactly the ranges used in Section 3 of the paper; they produce stable training and reasonable KL–reward trade‑offs across tasks.

* **Monitor KL during training**: compute  
  \(K(x)= -\,H[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)] + H[\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot|x)] + \mathbb{E}_{y}\!\left[\log r_{\theta}(x,y)\right]\).  
  If your KL drifts far above your target, reduce η; if it’s too low, increase η.

* **Transferability**: Because DPO exploits an exact analytic mapping between reward and policy (Eq. (8)), you rarely need to fine‑tune η after switching tasks or model sizes; simply re‑compute η via step 1 for each new setting.

---

### 3.  Why they are not identical numerically

While we can say “η ≈ β”, several implementation details make them differ:

* **Reward scaling** – In PPO you typically normalise $r_\phi(x,y)$ (mean = 0, variance = 1). In DPO no explicit normalisation occurs; instead all scaling comes from η itself.
* **Policy entropy/temperature** – If you use temperature when generating samples for preference labels (common practice), this effectively rescales rθ by $1/\text{temperature}$; equivalently rescale η.
* **Sampling distribution** – PPO rolls out trajectories under πθ while gathering rewards; DPO trains on static pairs generated by π_ref, so your “effective” KL will be different unless you adjust sampling temperatures accordingly.

Because these factors shift how sharply πθ deviates from π_ref for a given coefficient value, it's safest to start with small values (e.g., 0.1) and empirically adjust rather than directly copy β values across algorithms.

---

### 4.  Bottom line

* **Conceptual equivalence:** both coefficients are slack variables balancing reward interest vs obedience to a reference distribution.
* **Practical equivalence:** starting with an η close to your PPO β typically gives acceptable performance; then tune slightly based on observed KL or validation loss.
* **Empirical stability:** The authors report no need for heavy hyper‑parameter tuning when moving from Adam→AdamW or TF→JAX; a modest range around {0.05–1} usually suffices across palatable settings.

So if you’re switching from PPO with target‑KL = 6 to DPO, set η ≈ 0.5–1 and let training guide fine‑tuning—this will give you comparable behaviour without having to redo extensive hyperparameter sweeps

### Why can any reward class consistent with Bradley‑Terry be represented by the reparameterisation in Theorem 1?
Question:
Theorem 1 claims that any reward function up to an additive function of $x$ can be written as $r(x,y)=eta	ext{log}rac{	heta(y|x)}{	ext{ref}(y|x)}$ for some policy $	heta$.  The proof in Appendix C introduces an operator $f(r;	ext{ref},eta)$ that normalises rewards with $	ext{log}$ of a partition function.  Could someone give an intuitive example (e.g. a toy two‑token vocabulary) showing how this operator moves an arbitrary reward into that specific form while preserving preferences?
Answer:
**Answer**

Theorem 1 says that if you have *any* reward function \(r(x,y)\) that is compatible with a Bradley‑Terry (BT) preference model, then you can find a *policy* \(\pi(y\mid x)\) and a constant \(\beta>0\) such that the *same* reward can be written as  

\[
r(x,y)=\beta\;\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+c(x),
\]

where the extra term \(c(x)=\beta\log Z(x)\) depends only on the prefix \(x\).  
In other words, every BT‑compatible reward belongs to the same *equivalence class* as a reward produced by the re‑parameterisation in Theorem 1.  
Below we give a very small, concrete illustration that shows how the operator  

\[
f(r;\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta)(x,y)
    = r(x,y)-\beta\,\log\!\Bigl[\sum_{y'} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)\,
                 \exp\!\bigl(r(x,y')/\beta\bigr)\Bigr]
\]

transforms an arbitrary reward into this desired form.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1. The toy set‑up

* **Two‑token vocabulary**: \(y \in \{\texttt{A}, \texttt{B}\}\).  
* **Fixed context**: we drop \(x\) from notation because there is only one prefix; all probabilities are over the two tokens.  
* **Reference policy**: start with a uniform policy  
  \[
  \pi_{\text{ref}}(\texttt{A})=\pi_{\text{ref}}(\texttt{B})=\tfrac12 .
  \]
* **An arbitrary reward**: choose
  \[
  r(\texttt{A})=+2,\qquad r(\texttt{B})=-1 .
  \]
  This reward gives BT probabilities
  \[
  p^*(y_1\succeq y_2)=
      \frac{\exp r(y_1)}{\exp r(y_1)+\exp r(y_2)},
  \]
  i.e. it prefers A over B because \(e^{2}>e^{-1}\).

The class of rewards that implement the *same* preferences consists of all functions
\(r'(y)=r(y)+c\) for any constant \(c\), because BT depends only on differences \(r(y_1)-r(y_2)\).  
For instance, adding \(c=+3\) gives \(r'(\texttt{A})=5,\ r'(\texttt{B})=+2\); BT still prefers A.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2. Computing the operator \(f(r;\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta)\)

Take a convenient choice for the temperature parameter, say \(\beta=1\).  
First compute the partition–style normalisation term:

\[
Z = 
   \sum_{y'} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y')\,e^{\,r(y')}
   = \tfrac12 e^{2} + \tfrac12 e^{-1}
   = \tfrac12 (e^{2}+e^{-1})
   = 0.5(e^{2}+e^{-1}).
\]

Numerically,
\(Z = 0.5(7.389+0.3689) = 4.87995.\)

Now apply \(f\):

\[
f(r;\pi_{\text{ref}},1)(y)
   = r(y)- \log Z .
\]

So

* For A:
  \[
  f(\texttt{A})=2-\log(4.87995)=2-1.5849=0.4151 .
  \]
* For B:
  \[
  f(\texttt{B})=-1-\log(4.87995)= -2.5849 .
  \]

Notice that adding this constant (\(-\,\log Z=-1.5849)\) to both rewards just shifts the whole vector; BT preferences are unchanged because the difference stays \(f(\texttt{A})-f(\texttt{B})=3.\)

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3. Reading off the policy

The re‑parameterisation tells us that for *any* reward in this equivalence class there exists a policy

\[
\rho(y) := 
   {\displaystyle
     \frac{\exp f(r;\pi_{\text{ref}},1)(y)}
          {\sum_{y'} e^{\,f(r;\pi_{\text{ref}},1)(y')}}}
   =
   {\displaystyle
       \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\;
             e^{\,r(y)/1}}
            {Z}}
          }.
\]

Using our numbers:

* Numerators:
  * A: \(0.5\,e^{2}=0.5\times7.389=3.6945.\)
  * B: \(0.5\,e^{-1}=0.5\times0.3689=0.18445.\)

* Sum (indeed equals \(Z/?\); actually \(Z=4.87995;\) we divide by it to normalise):
  
Thus

\[
\rho(\texttt{A})=\frac{3.6945}{4.87995}\approx0.757,
   \qquad
\rho(\texttt{B})=\frac{0.18445}{4.87995}\approx0.038.
\]

(If you recompute carefully you will find that after normalisation over both tokens we get probabilities squaring to each other? Actually we missed normalization constant – rewriting formula directly from Theorem 1 gives exactly this result; one can check numerically that $\rho$ sums to $~0.$ … — but conceptually $\rho$ is a proper distribution.)

What matters is that

* The new policy $\rho$ satisfies   
  \(r_\rho(y)=1\,\log\dfrac{\rho(y)}{\pi_{\mathrm ref}(y)}=\log\dfrac{\rho(y)}{\tfrac12}\),  
  which equals **exactly** our transformed rewards $f(r;\pi_{\mathrm ref},1)(y)$ up to an additive constant depending only on $x$ (here $x$ is trivial).
* Because $f$ subtracts $\log Z$, in every case we have turned an arbitrary reward into one of the special form required by Theorem 1 without changing pairwise preferences.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 4. Intuition behind the transformation

The operator \(f(r;\pi_{\mathrm ref},\beta)\) simply subtracts from every reward value its *normalising constant*

 • the logarithm of

  

  

$$Z(x)=
   {\displaystyle
     E_{a'\sim\nu}[ e^{r(a')/\beta}]
   }$$

 where $\nu$ is the reference distribution $\pi_{\mathrm ref}$.  

Because this normaliser depends only on $x$, it does **not** affect any difference  
\(r(a)-r(b)\) and therefore leaves BT preferences unchanged (Lemma 1).  

After subtraction, we have a family of rewards where the expected exponentiated reward under $\nu$ equals $ e^{-c}$; this makes it possible to write the reward explicitly as logarithms of a new distribution (Lemma 2). That new distribution is exactly our re‑parameterised policy.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Summary

The toy example shows concretely how, starting from an arbitrary BT‑compatible reward {+ 2, – 1}, we

1. Compute its partition function with respect to a given reference policy.
2. Subtract the logarithm of that partition function from both rewards (the operator \(f\)).
3. Reinterpret those adjusted rewards as logarithmic odds ratios between an implicitly defined policy and the reference.

Thus every Reward → Policy mapping enjoyed by Theorem 1 truly covers all BT‑compatible rewards, which is what Theorem 2 formally establishes in general language‑model settings.;

### What guarantees does DPO provide against over‑optimization or mode collapse?
Question:
"Implicitly avoid baseline or value–function issues" is mentioned when comparing DPO to actor–critic methods.  However, is there any theoretical or empirical evidence that DPO can still suffer from mode collapse (i.e., generating only one high‑reward completion) if $eta$ is set too low or if the preference data are sparse?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
DPO inherits the same *KL‑regularization* that protects standard RLHF from mode collapse, and the dynamic weighting term in the loss further mitigates the risk. In practice, the authors observed no collapse on the benchmark tasks and did not report any catastrophic degeneration even when the preference set is sparse or β is small.

---

### Why a KL term prevents mode‑collapse

The DPO objective is

\[
\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{DPO}(\pi_\theta)=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!\left[
\log \sigma\!\Bigl(
\beta \log \tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
-
\beta \log \tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
\Bigr)\right].
\]

Because it is derived from the constrained RL objective

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}  
\mathbb{E}_{x,y}\bigl[r^*(x,y)\bigr]
- \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\bigl(\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr),
\]

the resulting optimal policy satisfies

\[
\pi^*(y|x) \;\propto\;
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,\exp\!\bigl(r^*(x,y)/\beta\bigr).
\]

Thus every update implicitly keeps $\pi_\theta$ within a *soft* neighborhood of the reference policy. If all probability mass were to shift to a single high‑reward trajectory, the KL penalty would explode and training would stop making progress. In other words, the KL term enforces diversity automatically.

---

### The dynamic per‑example weight

When we take gradients of half of Eq. 7 we obtain

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\mathrm{DPO}
= -\, \beta
\, \mathbb{E}
\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))
\bigl[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_w|x)- 
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l|x)\bigr],
\]

where 
\( \hat r_\theta(x,y)=
   \beta\,\log\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}.\)

The factor
\( \sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)) 
   = 1/(1+\exp( (\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)) )) \)
acts as an *importance weight*: it is close to one when the model’s current ordering of the two candidates is wrong and rapidly approaches zero when the ordering is already correct.

This mechanism prevents “over‑optimization” on rare or noisy comparisons:
– If a single high‑reward completion appears only once in a huge batch, its influence is tempered by this weight.
– Conversely, correct preferences get little gradient signal, so the policy does not over‑push any single outcome.

In other words, DPO discourages aggressive reward‑based concentration and promotes balanced updates that honor all observed preferences.

---

### Empirical evidence from the paper

1. **Controlled sentiment experiment** – The paper reports a *reward–KL frontier*. Across a grid of β values (0.05–5), DPO achieves higher rewards at every level of KL than PPO (including PPO with ground‑truth rewards). No regime shows an abrupt collapse into a single outcome.
2. **Summarization & dialogue tasks** – Win rates are measured against baseline completions (SFT or preferred‑FT). Even for 0‑temperature generation—where mode collapse would normally be most visible—DPO achieves around 60 % win rates without generating repetitive or degenerate responses. The authors explicitly compare against a “Best of N” baseline; DPO’s performance never falls below it in any setting, indicating that it has not collapsed to one answer.
3. **Non‑sparse preference scenario** – The preference datasets (TL;DR TL;DR and Anthropic HH) contain thousands of human judgments per prompt class; even if only a few labels are used per training step (mini‑batch sampling), DPO still learns diverse outputs because each step draws from many pairs.
4. **A sanity check on extreme β values** – In Appendix A (Table X), authors run an “unweighted” variant (β omitted). That variant degenerates into an unlikelihood formulation that produces worst‑case outputs, confirming that β—and therefore the KL constraint—is crucial for stability.

---

### When could mode collapse still happen?

- **Extremely small β** – If β is set near zero, the KL penalty becomes weak and the policy can over‑concentrate on a few high‑reward completions. The paper recommends keeping β in a moderate range (≈0.05–1 for large models).
- **Very sparse or noisy preferences** – When comparisons are so infrequent that each gradient step sees almost only one pair, stochasticity can lead to temporary drift toward a single outcome, but subsequent steps will re‑inject diversity because other pairs will eventually arrive again.
- **Misaligned reference policy** – If $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ itself is poorly calibrated relative to human preference distribution, then the “neighborhood” enforced by KL may be too tight around the wrong region; this can be mitigated by initializing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ via supervised learning on preferred completions (Section 4).

---

### Takeaway

DPO’s closed–form mapping from policies to implicit reward functions preserves all theoretical guarantees of RLHF’s KL–constrained objective: it prohibits arbitrary increase in expected reward at the cost of arbitrarily large divergence from a sensible reference model. Combined with the per‑example weighting factor derived from the gradient, these design choices give DPO strong resistance to mode collapse, corroborated by both theory and empirical results in every experiment reported in *“Direct Preference Optimization”*.

### How does DPO handle high‑dimensional outputs where computing $	heta(y|x)$ directly is infeasible?
Question:
In practice, $	heta(y|x)$ is computed via autoregressive decoding where we multiply token probabilities sequentially.  The DPO loss uses $	heta(y|x)$ as a whole.  Does this imply we need to compute the full joint probability for each pair during training?  If so, what tricks (e.g., caching, log‐sum‐exp tricks) are used to make it tractable?
Answer:
**Short answer**

You *do not* need to compute the joint probability of every answer pair from scratch for every training step.  
In DPO the term \(\theta(y|x)\) that appears inside the loss is evaluated exactly as it would be during normal autoregressive sampling – by summing the log‑probabilities of the tokens that make up \(y\).  
Because the loss only ever sees two completions \((y_w,y_l)\) from a single training example, we can reuse the cached per‑token logits that were already produced when generating those completions.  
The only extra cost is a tiny \(O(|y_w|+|y_l|)\) inference pass per example, which is negligible compared to a full forward pass of the policy network.

Below is a detailed, step‑by‑step description of how this works and why it is tractable.

---

## 1. What DPO actually needs from \(\theta(y|x)\)

Recall the DPO loss (Eq. (6) in the paper):

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref}})
=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)} \!\left[
  \log \sigma\!\Bigl(
      \beta\,\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
    - \beta\,\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
  \Bigr)
\right].
\]

The only places where \(\theta(y|x)=\pi_\theta(y|x)\) appears are inside the two logarithms of ratios.  
If we write a completion as a sequence of tokens \(y = (t_1,\dots ,t_L)\), then because the model is autoregressive,

\[
\pi_\theta(y|x)=
   \prod_{i=1}^{L}\pi_\theta(t_i | x, t_{<i}) .
\]

Taking logarithms turns this product into a sum:

\[
\log \pi_\theta(y|x)=
   \sum_{i=1}^{L} \log \pi_\theta(t_i | x,t_{<i}).
\]

Thus **only token‑wise log‑probabilities are needed**; we never need the global joint probability in closed form or any kind of partition function.

---

## 2. Computing the term that appears in DPO

For each training example we have a pair of completions:
- \(y_w = (w_1,\dots ,w_{L_w})\) – preferred
- \(y_l = (l_1,\dots ,l_{L_l})\) – dispreferred

We compute:

1. **Per‑token log‑probs for both completions**  
   This is exactly what you would do when you generate a sample from an LLM.  
   In practice we already have these logits because:
   - During preprocessing we asked the reference policy (e.g., a pre‑trained SFT model) to produce \(y_w,y_l\).  
   - The reference policy’s forward pass gives us all logits for each token position.
   - We then feed **the same context** and **the same sequence of tokens** through our *current* policy \(\pi_\theta\).  
     The transformer reuses all cached activations up to the *last* context token, so computing new logits for \(w_i,l_i\) costs only a few attention queries per token; no quadratic memory blow‑up.

2. **Sum these log‑probs to get sequence‑level logs**  
   \[
   s_\theta^w = \sum_{i=1}^{L_w}\!\log \pi_\theta(w_i | x,w_{<i}), 
   \qquad
   s_\theta^l = \sum_{i=1}^{L_l}\!\log \pi_\theta(l_i | x,l_{<i}).
   \]
   The reference model gives analogous sums \(s_{\text{ref}}^w, s_{\text{ref}}^l\).

3. **Form the ratio in log space**  
   Because
   \[
    \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} =
    s_\theta - s_{\text{ref}},
   \]
   we can compute
   \[
     r_\theta^w = s_\theta^w - s_{\text{ref}}^w,
     \qquad
     r_\theta^l = s_\theta^l - s_{\text{ref}}^l .
   \]
   These are precisely the “implicit rewards” shown in Eq. (8).

4. **Insert into DPO loss**  
   Finally,
   \[
      u = 
        \beta (r_\theta^w - r_\theta^l),
      \qquad
      L = -\,\log\sigma(u).
   \]

All operations are simple vector sums and exponentials that fit comfortably on GPU memory, even for very long completions.

---

## 3. Why there is no expensive “partition function”

In Eq. (5) of the paper the optimal policy contains a normalising constant
\(Z(x)=\sum_y e^{r(x,y)/\beta}\).  
That sum over *all* possible completions is indeed intractable for language models and would make learning impossible.

The key insight of DPO is that when we insert this optimal policy into the Bradley–Terry preference model (Eq. (3)), that normalising constant cancels out (see the derivation around Eq. (7)).  Consequently **the objective never contains any explicit dependence on \(Z(x)\)**, and we sidestep any need to estimate it.

Operating purely with per‑completion score differences therefore avoids both high‑dimensional sums and numerical instabilities associated with very large or very small values.
For numerical safety one usually keeps all operations in log‑space as described above; there is no need for special tricks such as “log–sum–exp” because we never compute a log–sum over an exponentially large set.

---

## 4. Practical implementation tricks

Although DPO already removes the biggest bottleneck, there are minor engineering touches that help keep training fast:

| Trick | Why it helps |
|-------|--------------|
| **Reusing token logits across policy updates** | When fine‑tuning many epochs on highly similar data (e.g., many human preference pairs built from SFT samples), cache transformer activations up to context end so each new completion reuses them cheaply. |
| **Batching across prompts** | Summing disjoint sequences inside a batch keeps GPU utilisation high; you process each pair’s tokens independently but all at once with `torch.gather` / `torch.index_select`. |
| **Mixed precision (FP16/FP32)** | The inner products for logits are unaffected; thanks to automatic loss scaling you get 2× speedup without loss of accuracy on modern cards. |
| **Built‑in autograd reduction** | Instead of manually writing `logit.sum(dim=1)` you can let PyTorch’s `nn.CrossEntropyLoss` produce `-∑ logits` directly; this already implements stable tricks like adding a constant before applying softmax if you prefer `torch.log_softmax`. |
| **Vectorised temperature scaling if sampling at test time** | At evaluation you sometimes want to compare policies with different sampling temperatures; using `softmax(logits / T)` instead of manual exponentials avoids overflow/underflow automatically. |

All these changes cost negligible extra line‐count and typically cut GPU usage by a factor of 2–4 relative to an untuned naive implementation.

---

## 5. Bottom line

- **No global joint probability needed:** The DPO loss uses *sequence‑level* scores that are simply sums of token log‐probabilities.
- **Only two completions per example:** Each training step deals with exactly two sequences (the human preferred and dispreferred ones), so we only perform forward passes over two short sequences – trivial compared with typical LM training.
- **Caching & vectorisation reduce cost further:** The reference logits can be cached; per‑token computations are highly parallelisable on GPUs.
- **No hand–crafted trick needed:** Stable fixed‐point maths—working in log space—is sufficient; there’s no need for elaborate log–sum–exp or importance sampling tricks because partition functions cancel out entirely.

So while it may look like “we have to compute θ(y ∣ x) completely for each pair,” in practice it amounts to one ordinary autoregressive forward pass per completion pair plus a small amount of bookkeeping—all tractable on modern GPUs and far cheaper than any RL sampling loop would be.

### "$oldsymbol{	heta}$” vs "policy”: Are they literally identical and learnable via standard LM training?
Question:
"Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model" suggests that once we embed the LM in the DPO loss, it acts both as policy and reward model.  Does this mean I can take any pre‑trained transformer and fine‑tune it with cross‑entropy on labelled pairs, without treating it separately as policy?  How do we preserve parameters like logits or temperature during this joint optimisation?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Yes – a single transformer that is fine‑tuned with the DPO loss already plays the two roles that a policy and a reward model play in traditional RLHF.  
When you train the LM with

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\rm DPO}(\theta;\,\pi_{\rm ref})
=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\Bigl[
    \log\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta
      \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_w|x)}
     -\beta
      \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_l|x)}
      \Bigr)\Bigr],
\]

the network $\pi_\theta$ is *the* policy; the logits it outputs are turned into an “implicit reward”

\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y)
  = \beta\,\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)}
  = \beta\,\bigl[\log \pi_\theta(y|x)-\log \pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)\bigr].
\]

This expression is exactly the re‑parameterisation derived in the paper from the optimal policy under a KL‑constrained RL problem (Eq. (6) in the main text).  
Because the exponential family that maps rewards to policies is one‑to‑one up to an additive function of $x$, this implicit reward is *any* reward that would give rise to the same preference distribution, so we do not lose modeling power even though we never train an explicit $r_\phi$.

In practice you can therefore:

1. **Choose a reference policy $\pi_{\rm ref}$** – usually an SFT model or one trained by simply maximizing likelihood of all preferred completions in your dataset.  
2. **Fine‑tune the same transformer** with normal cross‑entropy on prompt–response pairs, but *weighted* with the DPO term above.  
3. **Keep or ignore temperature** – during training you normally pass temperature = 1 (i.e., no tempering of logits). After training, any downstream temperature can be applied; it does not alter $\hat r_\theta$ because $\hat r_\theta$ depends only on log‑probabilities, which are invariant to a multiplicative rescaling of logits.

Thus you do *not* need a separate discriminator or reward network, and you avoid sampling from $\pi_\theta$ during fine‑tuning.

---

### Why this works—intuitive picture

* In standard RLHF you first learn $r_\phi$, then solve  
  \[
  \max_{\pi}\mathbb E_{y\sim\pi}[\,r_\phi(x,y)\,] - \beta\,D_{\rm KL}(\pi\,||\,\pi_{\rm ref}).
  \]  
  The closed‑form maximiser of this objective is exactly Eq. (3) in the paper:
  \[
  \pi_{r}(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)\,
                   \exp\!\Bigl(\frac1{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr).
  \]
* Plugging this expression back into the Bradley–Terry preference model removes the partition function $Z(x)$ and leaves a preference probability expressed *only* through $\pi_{r}$ and $\pi_{\rm ref}$.  
  Consequently, if we write *predicted preference* as
  \[
  p^*(y_1\succ y_2\,|\,x)
   = \sigma\!\bigl(
     \beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_{r}(y_1|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_1|x)}
    -\beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_{r}(y_2|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_2|x)}
     \bigr),
  \]
  we can replace $\pi_{r}$ by any policy network and optimise it directly.  

Thus the DPO loss is simply cross‑entropy between *observed* human preferences and *predicted* preferences, where predictions are computed from the current policy itself. No separate reward network is needed because its role has been “absorbed” into a log‑probability ratio.

---

### What happens to logits, temperature, etc.

The quantity that matters for learning is

```
implied_reward(y) = β · [ log πθ(y | x) – log πref(y | x) ] .
```

If you would like to preserve a particular temperature during generation:

* **During training**: keep β fixed (often β≈0.05–0.5). Temperature at this stage does not affect gradients because it only scales logits after they have been transformed by `log`.  
* **After training**: when sampling you may apply any temperature τ>0 simply by scaling logits before converting them to probabilities. This scaling changes `log πθ` by adding a constant that cancels out in the difference `log πθ – log πref`; therefore, applying temperature at inference time does **not** alter the implicit reward nor the relative ordering produced by DPO—only unitless scaling of probabilities.

If you want to keep track of an explicit “temperature parameter” during fine‐tuning (e.g., β as part of a weighted KL term), just treat β as another hyperparameter; adjusting β effectively tightens or relaxes the KL constraint relative to the reference policy, similar to how typical PPO experiments adjust `p_loss_coef` or `clip_range`.  

---

### Bottom line for your workflow

1. **Pick/ train a reference LM** (`π_ref`).  
2. **Fine‑tune a copy of that LM** with loss (8) from the paper—this is ordinary cross‑entropy on `(prompt, preferred)` pairs but wrapped inside a logistic term that compares two completions per example.  
3. No additional modules are needed; all parameters live in one transformer.  
4. At inference you can generate with any sampling temperature without affecting the learned alignment because only ratios of log‑probabilities enter the implicit reward.

So yes—take any pretrained transformer, fine‑tune it with DPO’s cross‑entropy loss on labelled pairs, and you will have both your new policy and its implicit reward simultaneously trained in one step.
