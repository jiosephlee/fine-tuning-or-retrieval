\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 2: # 2. From Supervision to Preference Learning

Large language models (LLMs) are first trained on massive corpora using an unsupervised objective that rewards the next-token prediction. Although this pre‑training endows the models with broad factual knowledge and rudimentary reasoning skills, it also results in a distribution of behaviours that reflects the heterogeneity of the training data. Some of these behaviours—such as hallucinating facts, repeating user prompts verbatim, or following unsafe instructions—are undesirable when the model is deployed as an assistant, editor, or code generator. To correct for such mismatches between what humans deem useful and what the model is naturally inclined to do, we must guide the LM toward a narrower set of behaviours that aligns with user intent.

A practical two‑stage approach has become widespread in the industry: **(1) supervised fine‑tuning (SFT)** and **(2) preference‑based optimisation**. SFT introduces a small number of high‑quality demonstrations into the learning signal, while preference‑based optimisation injects explicit human judgments about which outputs are better relative to others. The following sections unpack each phase in detail and explain how preference data is modelled mathematically.

---

## # Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT) Objectives

After unsupervised pre‑training, the model already has a good prior distribution over text. SFT selectively biases this prior toward a target behaviour by providing labelled examples that embody the desired task. In practice:

1. **Task specification** – We collect short instruction prompts \(x\) together with canonical responses \(y^{\text{dem}}\). For instance, \(x\) may be “Summarize the following article …”, and \(y^{\text{dem}}\) is a hand‑written summary.

2. **Supervised objective** – The LM is fine‑tuned to maximise the *conditional likelihood* of these demonstrations:
   \[
   \max_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{\text{dem}})}\bigl[\log \pi_{\theta}(y^{\text{dem}} \mid x)\bigr].
   \]
   Here \(\pi_{\theta}\) denotes the language‑model policy parametrised by weights \(\theta\). Because we present natural language tokens sequentially, this objective decomposes into a sum over steps:
   \[
   \log \pi_{\theta}(y^{\text{dem}}\mid x)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_{\theta}(y_t^{\text{dem}}\mid y_{<t}^{\text{dem}},x).
   \]
3. **Data efficiency** – Since SFT uses *absolute* targets (there is only one correct token per step), training is straightforward and converges quickly with modest data—typically only thousands of examples for many downstream tasks.

The resulting fine‑tuned model—denoted \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\)—serves as both a more task‑aware base policy and *reference policy* for later stages. It reflects what humans consider good when grounded solely on demonstration data.

---

## # Generation of Candidate Completions

Fine‑tuned models often still generate unwanted outputs if used naïvely in interactive contexts: without further guidance they may be overly verbose or produce subtle errors. To assess human preferences over different possible responses, we generate diverse completions for each prompt:

1. **Sampling from \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\)**  
   For any test prompt \(x\), we draw two independent samples:
   \[
   y_1 , y_2 \;\sim\; \pi^{\text{SFT}}(\,\cdot\,|x).
   \]
   The sampling temperature controls diversity—higher temperatures yield more varied (but sometimes lower‑probability) outputs.

2. **Batching**  
   With thousands of prompts in hand (e.g., from an existing dataset or live user queries), we generate many candidate pairs in parallel, creating a large pool of *candidate completions* that capture different styles, lengths, and potentially flawed behaviours.

3. **Purpose**  
   These samples will be presented to humans so they can express *which one they prefer*, thereby turning raw LLM outputs into a relational dataset that can later be mapped onto a quantitative reward measure.

---

## # Human Labeling of Preferences

Instead of requesting *absolute ratings* per completion—a tedious process that scorers often judge as noisy—human labelers are asked to compare pairs:

- They see prompt \(x\) accompanied by two completions \(y_1\) and \(y_2\).
- They mark one as preferred: \(y_w \succ y_l | x\), indicating “\(y_w\) (winner) is preferred over \(y_l\) (loser)” given prompt \(x\).

This comparative labeling has several advantages:

| Property | Benefit |
|----------|---------|
| **Relative scale** | Easier for humans; reduces inter‑annotator variance. |
| **Efficiency** | One label gives us comparative information for two completions. |
| **Implicit ranking** | Multiple comparisons across prompts yield a global ordering without explicit numeric scores. |

The aggregated dataset looks like
\[
\mathcal{D}
=\bigl\{\, (x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\,:\, i = 1,\ldots,N\bigr\},
\]
where each triplet records that sample \(y_w^{(i)}\) was judged preferable to sample \(y_l^{(i)}\) under prompt \(x^{(i)}.\)

---

## # Bradley–Terry Model and Plackett–Luce Ranking Families

To convert these binary preferences into something that can drive optimisation of a neural policy, we embed them inside a probabilistic *ranking model*. Two families dominate this space:

### 1. Bradley–Terry Model

The Bradley–Terry model posits that each completion possesses an unobserved *score* proportional to an underlying latent reward function \(r^*(x,y)\). The probability that human prefers \(y_1\) over \(y_2\) given prompt \(x\) is:
\[
p^*(\,y_1 \succ y_2\,|\,x)
=
\sigma
   \Bigl(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)\Bigr),
   \qquad
\sigma(z)=\frac{e^z}{1+e^z}.
   \tag{2}
\]
In plain terms:
- If \(r^*(x,y_1)\!\gg r^*(x,y_2)\), then \(\sigma(\cdot)\!\approx 1.\)
- If both scores are equal, preference probability leans towards random noise.

Modeling preferences through Eq.~\(2'\) has two major consequences:

* **Likelihood interpretation** – We can treat Eq.~\(2'\) as defining a likelihood for observed preference data.
* **Parameterising rewards** – By fitting any function class (often neural networks) to mimic the difference-of-rewards formulation above, we recover a *reward model* for subsequent optimisation.

### 2. Plackett–Luce Family

When more than two completions are available per prompt, one sometimes uses stronger ranking schemes like Plackett–Luce:
- Given a set of scores \(\{s_i = e^{r^*(x,y_i)} : i=1,\dots,k\}\),
- The probability that item \(j^\*\) occupies rank 1 equals
  \[
  p(j^\*=j | x)
    = 
    \frac{s_j}{s_{j} + s_{j+1} + \dots + s_k}.
  \]
When only pairwise comparisons survive from human annotators, however, Bradley–Terry suffices because its pairwise form corresponds exactly to Plackett–Luce when considering *just two items*. Consequently, leveraging Bradley–Terry is often simpler without loss of expressivity.

---

## # Connecting Preferences to Policy Optimisation

Armed with Eq.~\(2'\)—the probability that humans prefer one completion over another—we can now embed this into an optimisation pipeline for our LM policy:

1. **Reward estimation**  
   Train a neural network approximating the latent reward function:
   \[
   r_\phi(x,y)
     = f_\phi(x,y;\,\texttt{backbone}\!-\!\pi)
     + c(x).
     \tag{3}
   \]
   Here:
   - The backbone may be a frozen or fine‑tuned language model,
   - A linear projection maps hidden activations to an abstract numerical score,
   - A baseline term \(c(x)\) normalises per–prompt rewards so that their mean equals zero across completions; this aids stability by reducing variance during RL.

2. **Maximum likelihood fit**  
    With Eq.~\(3'\), we construct the loss
    \[
    L_{\text{RM}}(\phi)
      = -\, 
        {\mathbb E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\in\bigr.\mathcal{D}} 
        [
          \log\sigma(
           r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l))
        ].
       \tag{4}
    \]
    Minimising this loss pushes weights toward generating higher rewards for preferred completions relative to dispreferred ones.

3. **Policy optimisation via RL**  
    Once we have learned this reward family we subject it to reinforcement learning against our reference policy:
    - The KL‐regularised objective balances reward maximisation against staying close to the pre‑trained prior.
    - Algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) are typically employed because they handle discrete action spaces well but can be computationally heavy on large transformers.

Branching out from this classical path leads us towards novel approaches like Direct Preference Optimization which eliminate intermediate reward estimation and RL steps entirely by exploiting analytical identities—an idea explored later in this text.

---

Through understanding these building blocks—the supervised fine‐tuning base model; generating diverse candidate completions; obtaining comparative human judgements; translating those judgements into probabilistic preferences via Bradley–Terry or Plackett–Luce—we lay foundations upon which contemporary alignment pipelines can rest and evolve toward higher efficacy and lower computational cost._END OF CHAPTER_
