Chapter 1: # Chapter: Foundations of Language Models and Human Alignment  

Large language models (LLMs) are the workhorses of modern natural‑language processing.  Their impressive few‑shot reasoning, translation, summarisation, and dialogue skills emerge from training on massive corpora that cover a broad range of topics and writing styles.  Yet, because this training is completely unsupervised – the models simply learn to predict the next token in every text they see – their behaviour can diverge from what we actually want when deployed in real systems.  This chapter introduces the key ideas that underpin the field of **human‑aligned language modelling**: we review how LLMs acquire knowledge and skills, why we need additional instruction tuning to shape user intent, and how we harness human preference data to steer these powerful models toward safer and more useful behaviour.

---

## 1. Large-Scale Unsupervised Training of Language Models  

Modern LLMs are trained through an unsupervised objective known as **language modelling**.  For every contiguous sequence of tokens \((w_1,\dots,w_T)\) in a large corpus, the model learns to maximise

\[
\prod_{t=1}^{T} P_\Theta(w_t\mid w_{<t}),
\]

where \(P_\Theta\) is the probability distribution defined by a transformer network parameterised by \(\Theta\).  By iterating over billions of tokens, the transformer learns a huge amount of statistical regularity: grammar, factual knowledge, world‑wide facts about brands and cities, even patterns of reasoning.  This foundation gives rise to surprising zero‑shot or few‑shot performance on tasks not explicitly seen during training.

Because the objective is unsupervised, we can scale it without needing specialised labels: we only need raw text.  Consequently, state‑of‑the‑art models such as PaLM‑2 (\(540B\) parameters), LLaMA‑2 (\(70B\)), or GPT‑4 (\(170B\)) all owe their size to this simple next‑word prediction objective.  Nevertheless, a model that simply guesses the next token it has seen most often will not necessarily respond appropriately to user instructions or behave safely in high‑stakes situations.

---

## 2. Instruction Tuning and Its Benefits  

Instruction tuning remedies one shortcoming of pure language modelling by **fine‑tuning** on labelled pairs \((x_i,y_i)\) where \(x_i\) is an instruction or prompt and \(y_i\) is an example answer supervised by human writers or curated data.  The classic approach trains a new decoder network (or adds adapters) to predict \(y_i\) conditioned on \(x_i\):

\[
\arg\max_{\Theta}\sum_{i}\log P_\Theta(y_i \mid x_i).
\]

This supervised step teaches the model two crucial behaviours:

1. **Prompt Following:** The model learns that embeddings corresponding to instructions should be mapped to outputs that satisfy those instructions.
2. **User Intent Generalisation:** Because many instruction datasets include prompts unfamiliar to the model at training time (e.g., “Summarise a legal brief”), instruction tuning gives the LM a fallback style for handling novel tasks.

Empirically, instruction tuning improves downstream performance across tasks: ChatGPT’s conversational fluency owes most of its skill to thousands of hours spent fine‑tuning on user transcripts; LLaMA followers show similar gains when fine‑tuned with Alpaca data.

However, instruction tuning remains limited: while it aligns models with explicit prompts, it does not suffice for *safety* or *quality* alignment where subtle trade‐offs exist (truthfulness vs reasonableness).  We need techniques that can incorporate nuanced human preferences—e.g., encouraging politeness while discouraging self‑harmful statements—beyond static supervision signals.

---

## 3. Challenges in Aligning LMs with User Intent  

Once an LM can follow instructions well, a new class of problems emerges:

### 3.1 Misalignment with Human Values  
The data used for unsupervised training contains text written by diverse people with differing priorities. An LM may inadvertently emulate harmful or biased patterns unless specifically corrected.

### 3.2 Reward Ambiguity  
In many situations there is no single “right” answer; humans may disagree or prefer different aspects (clarity vs brevity). Modelling this uncertainty requires more than binary correctness signals.

### 3.3 instability During Fine‑Tuning  
Reinforcement learning algorithms such as PPO can suffer from high variance gradients and mode collapse: an LM may begin producing sub–optimal but highly rewarded outputs (e.g., repetitive safe responses) if reward estimates drift during training.

### 3.4 Data Collection Bottlenecks  
Collecting absolute human ratings for millions of examples is expensive; relative judgments (“is response A better than response B?”) are far cheaper but harder to formalise into standard RL objectives.

Because of these challenges, researchers turned to **reward modelling** followed by **RL fine-tuning** (RLHF). The pipeline involves three main stages:

1. **Supervised Fine‐Tuning (SFT):** The LM is first fine-tuned on high-quality examples for a given domain.
2. **Reward Modelling:** Human annotators provide preferences between pairs of responses; a neural reward network \(r_\phi(x,y)\) is trained via maximum likelihood under a Bradley–Terry model.
3. **Reinforcement Learning:** A policy \(\pi_\theta(y|x)\) is updated with PPO to maximise expected reward minus a KL penalty relative to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).

Although effective, this RLHF procedure requires multiple training passes over large models, careful hyperparameter search for PPO’s clipping hyperparameter and reward scaling \(\beta\), as well as careful monitoring for collusion and distribution shift.

## 4. Human Preference Data: Collection and Representation  

Human preferences are usually presented as *comparisons* rather than absolute scores:

- For each prompt \(x\), two completions \((y_1,y_2)\) are generated (often by sampling from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)).
- Annotators label which completion they prefer: \(y_w \succ y_l | x\).

These comparisons implicitly encode a *latent reward function* \(r^*(x,y)\): human preferences reflect higher true reward for better completions:

\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 | x)=
\sigma(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)) ,
\]

where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\) is the logistic function (Bradley–Terry model).  

The dataset of triple tuples
\[
\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\,|\, i=1,\dots,N\}
\]
provides rich information about which responses humans find preferable across diverse contexts.

Because modelling absolute rewards directly would require generating thousands of independent samples from the same prompt—a costly endeavour—the reliance on pairwise judgements makes preference collection scalable while preserving ample signal about relative quality.

---

### Conclusion  

Large language models provide unprecedented coverage of automated text generation but lack precise alignment with user intent and safety norms because their training objective is purely unsupervised prediction. Instruction tuning adds flavour but still falls short when safety concerns arise or when nuanced trade‐offs demand more subtle guidance than any single supervised example can express. By learning from *human preference data*, researchers aim at building reward functions that capture subtler aspects of quality (truthfulness versus fluency), then leveraging reinforcement learning to shape the LM accordingly.

The pioneering RLHF framework demonstrates feasibility but introduces significant practical hurdles—complex optimisation pipelines and numerous hyperparameters—thereby motivating alternative approaches such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). Understanding how LMs scale up through unsupervised learning, how instruction tuning reshapes behaviour, and what human preference data looks like provides the essential groundwork upon which DPO—and other emerging alignment methods—build their foundations.So far this book has chronicled all these steps at length before turning toward one particular mathematical formulation that allows us to replace RL entirely.
