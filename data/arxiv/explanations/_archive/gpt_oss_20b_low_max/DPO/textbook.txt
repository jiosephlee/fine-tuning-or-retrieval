# Chapter: Foundations of Language Models and Human Alignment  

Large language models (LLMs) are the workhorses of modern natural‑language processing.  Their impressive few‑shot reasoning, translation, summarisation, and dialogue skills emerge from training on massive corpora that cover a broad range of topics and writing styles.  Yet, because this training is completely unsupervised – the models simply learn to predict the next token in every text they see – their behaviour can diverge from what we actually want when deployed in real systems.  This chapter introduces the key ideas that underpin the field of **human‑aligned language modelling**: we review how LLMs acquire knowledge and skills, why we need additional instruction tuning to shape user intent, and how we harness human preference data to steer these powerful models toward safer and more useful behaviour.

---

## 1. Large-Scale Unsupervised Training of Language Models  

Modern LLMs are trained through an unsupervised objective known as **language modelling**.  For every contiguous sequence of tokens \((w_1,\dots,w_T)\) in a large corpus, the model learns to maximise

\[
\prod_{t=1}^{T} P_\Theta(w_t\mid w_{<t}),
\]

where \(P_\Theta\) is the probability distribution defined by a transformer network parameterised by \(\Theta\).  By iterating over billions of tokens, the transformer learns a huge amount of statistical regularity: grammar, factual knowledge, world‑wide facts about brands and cities, even patterns of reasoning.  This foundation gives rise to surprising zero‑shot or few‑shot performance on tasks not explicitly seen during training.

Because the objective is unsupervised, we can scale it without needing specialised labels: we only need raw text.  Consequently, state‑of‑the‑art models such as PaLM‑2 (\(540B\) parameters), LLaMA‑2 (\(70B\)), or GPT‑4 (\(170B\)) all owe their size to this simple next‑word prediction objective.  Nevertheless, a model that simply guesses the next token it has seen most often will not necessarily respond appropriately to user instructions or behave safely in high‑stakes situations.

---

## 2. Instruction Tuning and Its Benefits  

Instruction tuning remedies one shortcoming of pure language modelling by **fine‑tuning** on labelled pairs \((x_i,y_i)\) where \(x_i\) is an instruction or prompt and \(y_i\) is an example answer supervised by human writers or curated data.  The classic approach trains a new decoder network (or adds adapters) to predict \(y_i\) conditioned on \(x_i\):

\[
\arg\max_{\Theta}\sum_{i}\log P_\Theta(y_i \mid x_i).
\]

This supervised step teaches the model two crucial behaviours:

1. **Prompt Following:** The model learns that embeddings corresponding to instructions should be mapped to outputs that satisfy those instructions.
2. **User Intent Generalisation:** Because many instruction datasets include prompts unfamiliar to the model at training time (e.g., “Summarise a legal brief”), instruction tuning gives the LM a fallback style for handling novel tasks.

Empirically, instruction tuning improves downstream performance across tasks: ChatGPT’s conversational fluency owes most of its skill to thousands of hours spent fine‑tuning on user transcripts; LLaMA followers show similar gains when fine‑tuned with Alpaca data.

However, instruction tuning remains limited: while it aligns models with explicit prompts, it does not suffice for *safety* or *quality* alignment where subtle trade‐offs exist (truthfulness vs reasonableness).  We need techniques that can incorporate nuanced human preferences—e.g., encouraging politeness while discouraging self‑harmful statements—beyond static supervision signals.

---

## 3. Challenges in Aligning LMs with User Intent  

Once an LM can follow instructions well, a new class of problems emerges:

### 3.1 Misalignment with Human Values  
The data used for unsupervised training contains text written by diverse people with differing priorities. An LM may inadvertently emulate harmful or biased patterns unless specifically corrected.

### 3.2 Reward Ambiguity  
In many situations there is no single “right” answer; humans may disagree or prefer different aspects (clarity vs brevity). Modelling this uncertainty requires more than binary correctness signals.

### 3.3 instability During Fine‑Tuning  
Reinforcement learning algorithms such as PPO can suffer from high variance gradients and mode collapse: an LM may begin producing sub–optimal but highly rewarded outputs (e.g., repetitive safe responses) if reward estimates drift during training.

### 3.4 Data Collection Bottlenecks  
Collecting absolute human ratings for millions of examples is expensive; relative judgments (“is response A better than response B?”) are far cheaper but harder to formalise into standard RL objectives.

Because of these challenges, researchers turned to **reward modelling** followed by **RL fine-tuning** (RLHF). The pipeline involves three main stages:

1. **Supervised Fine‐Tuning (SFT):** The LM is first fine-tuned on high-quality examples for a given domain.
2. **Reward Modelling:** Human annotators provide preferences between pairs of responses; a neural reward network \(r_\phi(x,y)\) is trained via maximum likelihood under a Bradley–Terry model.
3. **Reinforcement Learning:** A policy \(\pi_\theta(y|x)\) is updated with PPO to maximise expected reward minus a KL penalty relative to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).

Although effective, this RLHF procedure requires multiple training passes over large models, careful hyperparameter search for PPO’s clipping hyperparameter and reward scaling \(\beta\), as well as careful monitoring for collusion and distribution shift.

---

## 4. Human Preference Data: Collection and Representation  

Human preferences are usually presented as *comparisons* rather than absolute scores:

- For each prompt \(x\), two completions \((y_1,y_2)\) are generated (often by sampling from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)).
- Annotators label which completion they prefer: \(y_w \succ y_l | x\).

These comparisons implicitly encode a *latent reward function* \(r^*(x,y)\): human preferences reflect higher true reward for better completions:

\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 | x)=
\sigma(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)) ,
\]

where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\) is the logistic function (Bradley–Terry model).  

The dataset of triple tuples
\[
\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\,|\, i=1,\dots,N\}
\]
provides rich information about which responses humans find preferable across diverse contexts.

Because modelling absolute rewards directly would require generating thousands of independent samples from the same prompt—a costly endeavour—the reliance on pairwise judgements makes preference collection scalable while preserving ample signal about relative quality.

---

### Conclusion  

Large language models provide unprecedented coverage of automated text generation but lack precise alignment with user intent and safety norms because their training objective is purely unsupervised prediction. Instruction tuning adds flavour but still falls short when safety concerns arise or when nuanced trade‐offs demand more subtle guidance than any single supervised example can express. By learning from *human preference data*, researchers aim at building reward functions that capture subtler aspects of quality (truthfulness versus fluency), then leveraging reinforcement learning to shape the LM accordingly.

The pioneering RLHF framework demonstrates feasibility but introduces significant practical hurdles—complex optimisation pipelines and numerous hyperparameters—thereby motivating alternative approaches such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). Understanding how LMs scale up through unsupervised learning, how instruction tuning reshapes behaviour, and what human preference data looks like provides the essential groundwork upon which DPO—and other emerging alignment methods—build their foundations.So far this book has chronicled all these steps at length before turning toward one particular mathematical formulation that allows us to replace RL entirely.

# 2. From Supervision to Preference Learning

Large language models (LLMs) are first trained on massive corpora using an unsupervised objective that rewards the next-token prediction. Although this pre‑training endows the models with broad factual knowledge and rudimentary reasoning skills, it also results in a distribution of behaviours that reflects the heterogeneity of the training data. Some of these behaviours—such as hallucinating facts, repeating user prompts verbatim, or following unsafe instructions—are undesirable when the model is deployed as an assistant, editor, or code generator. To correct for such mismatches between what humans deem useful and what the model is naturally inclined to do, we must guide the LM toward a narrower set of behaviours that aligns with user intent.

A practical two‑stage approach has become widespread in the industry: **(1) supervised fine‑tuning (SFT)** and **(2) preference‑based optimisation**. SFT introduces a small number of high‑quality demonstrations into the learning signal, while preference‑based optimisation injects explicit human judgments about which outputs are better relative to others. The following sections unpack each phase in detail and explain how preference data is modelled mathematically.

---

## # Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT) Objectives

After unsupervised pre‑training, the model already has a good prior distribution over text. SFT selectively biases this prior toward a target behaviour by providing labelled examples that embody the desired task. In practice:

1. **Task specification** – We collect short instruction prompts \(x\) together with canonical responses \(y^{\text{dem}}\). For instance, \(x\) may be “Summarize the following article …”, and \(y^{\text{dem}}\) is a hand‑written summary.

2. **Supervised objective** – The LM is fine‑tuned to maximise the *conditional likelihood* of these demonstrations:
   \[
   \max_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{\text{dem}})}\bigl[\log \pi_{\theta}(y^{\text{dem}} \mid x)\bigr].
   \]
   Here \(\pi_{\theta}\) denotes the language‑model policy parametrised by weights \(\theta\). Because we present natural language tokens sequentially, this objective decomposes into a sum over steps:
   \[
   \log \pi_{\theta}(y^{\text{dem}}\mid x)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_{\theta}(y_t^{\text{dem}}\mid y_{<t}^{\text{dem}},x).
   \]
3. **Data efficiency** – Since SFT uses *absolute* targets (there is only one correct token per step), training is straightforward and converges quickly with modest data—typically only thousands of examples for many downstream tasks.

The resulting fine‑tuned model—denoted \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\)—serves as both a more task‑aware base policy and *reference policy* for later stages. It reflects what humans consider good when grounded solely on demonstration data.

---

## # Generation of Candidate Completions

Fine‑tuned models often still generate unwanted outputs if used naïvely in interactive contexts: without further guidance they may be overly verbose or produce subtle errors. To assess human preferences over different possible responses, we generate diverse completions for each prompt:

1. **Sampling from \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\)**  
   For any test prompt \(x\), we draw two independent samples:
   \[
   y_1 , y_2 \;\sim\; \pi^{\text{SFT}}(\,\cdot\,|x).
   \]
   The sampling temperature controls diversity—higher temperatures yield more varied (but sometimes lower‑probability) outputs.

2. **Batching**  
   With thousands of prompts in hand (e.g., from an existing dataset or live user queries), we generate many candidate pairs in parallel, creating a large pool of *candidate completions* that capture different styles, lengths, and potentially flawed behaviours.

3. **Purpose**  
   These samples will be presented to humans so they can express *which one they prefer*, thereby turning raw LLM outputs into a relational dataset that can later be mapped onto a quantitative reward measure.

---

## # Human Labeling of Preferences

Instead of requesting *absolute ratings* per completion—a tedious process that scorers often judge as noisy—human labelers are asked to compare pairs:

- They see prompt \(x\) accompanied by two completions \(y_1\) and \(y_2\).
- They mark one as preferred: \(y_w \succ y_l | x\), indicating “\(y_w\) (winner) is preferred over \(y_l\) (loser)” given prompt \(x\).

This comparative labeling has several advantages:

| Property | Benefit |
|----------|---------|
| **Relative scale** | Easier for humans; reduces inter‑annotator variance. |
| **Efficiency** | One label gives us comparative information for two completions. |
| **Implicit ranking** | Multiple comparisons across prompts yield a global ordering without explicit numeric scores. |

The aggregated dataset looks like
\[
\mathcal{D}
=\bigl\{\, (x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\,:\, i = 1,\ldots,N\bigr\},
\]
where each triplet records that sample \(y_w^{(i)}\) was judged preferable to sample \(y_l^{(i)}\) under prompt \(x^{(i)}.\)

---

## # Bradley–Terry Model and Plackett–Luce Ranking Families

To convert these binary preferences into something that can drive optimisation of a neural policy, we embed them inside a probabilistic *ranking model*. Two families dominate this space:

### 1. Bradley–Terry Model

The Bradley–Terry model posits that each completion possesses an unobserved *score* proportional to an underlying latent reward function \(r^*(x,y)\). The probability that human prefers \(y_1\) over \(y_2\) given prompt \(x\) is:
\[
p^*(\,y_1 \succ y_2\,|\,x)
=
\sigma
   \Bigl(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)\Bigr),
   \qquad
\sigma(z)=\frac{e^z}{1+e^z}.
   \tag{2}
\]
In plain terms:
- If \(r^*(x,y_1)\!\gg r^*(x,y_2)\), then \(\sigma(\cdot)\!\approx 1.\)
- If both scores are equal, preference probability leans towards random noise.

Modeling preferences through Eq.~\(2'\) has two major consequences:

* **Likelihood interpretation** – We can treat Eq.~\(2'\) as defining a likelihood for observed preference data.
* **Parameterising rewards** – By fitting any function class (often neural networks) to mimic the difference-of-rewards formulation above, we recover a *reward model* for subsequent optimisation.

### 2. Plackett–Luce Family

When more than two completions are available per prompt, one sometimes uses stronger ranking schemes like Plackett–Luce:
- Given a set of scores \(\{s_i = e^{r^*(x,y_i)} : i=1,\dots,k\}\),
- The probability that item \(j^\*\) occupies rank 1 equals
  \[
  p(j^\*=j | x)
    = 
    \frac{s_j}{s_{j} + s_{j+1} + \dots + s_k}.
  \]
When only pairwise comparisons survive from human annotators, however, Bradley–Terry suffices because its pairwise form corresponds exactly to Plackett–Luce when considering *just two items*. Consequently, leveraging Bradley–Terry is often simpler without loss of expressivity.

---

## # Connecting Preferences to Policy Optimisation

Armed with Eq.~\(2'\)—the probability that humans prefer one completion over another—we can now embed this into an optimisation pipeline for our LM policy:

1. **Reward estimation**  
   Train a neural network approximating the latent reward function:
   \[
   r_\phi(x,y)
     = f_\phi(x,y;\,\texttt{backbone}\!-\!\pi)
     + c(x).
     \tag{3}
   \]
   Here:
   - The backbone may be a frozen or fine‑tuned language model,
   - A linear projection maps hidden activations to an abstract numerical score,
   - A baseline term \(c(x)\) normalises per–prompt rewards so that their mean equals zero across completions; this aids stability by reducing variance during RL.

2. **Maximum likelihood fit**  
    With Eq.~\(3'\), we construct the loss
    \[
    L_{\text{RM}}(\phi)
      = -\, 
        {\mathbb E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\in\bigr.\mathcal{D}} 
        [
          \log\sigma(
           r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l))
        ].
       \tag{4}
    \]
    Minimising this loss pushes weights toward generating higher rewards for preferred completions relative to dispreferred ones.

3. **Policy optimisation via RL**  
    Once we have learned this reward family we subject it to reinforcement learning against our reference policy:
    - The KL‐regularised objective balances reward maximisation against staying close to the pre‑trained prior.
    - Algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) are typically employed because they handle discrete action spaces well but can be computationally heavy on large transformers.

Branching out from this classical path leads us towards novel approaches like Direct Preference Optimization which eliminate intermediate reward estimation and RL steps entirely by exploiting analytical identities—an idea explored later in this text.

---

Through understanding these building blocks—the supervised fine‐tuning base model; generating diverse candidate completions; obtaining comparative human judgements; translating those judgements into probabilistic preferences via Bradley–Terry or Plackett–Luce—we lay foundations upon which contemporary alignment pipelines can rest and evolve toward higher efficacy and lower computational cost._END OF CHAPTER_

# 3. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)

Reinforcement learning from human feedback has become the cornerstone of modern instruction‑tuned and alignment‑focused language models. In this chapter, we provide a self‑contained exposition of the RLHF workflow, taking the reader from the formal objective that drives policy optimization, through the construction of a reward estimator from noisy human judgments, to the practicalities and pitfalls of actually training a large language model with modern policy‑gradient methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Throughout we preserve the notation used in the seminal papers, so that readers can directly translate the discussion into code or paper analysis.

---

## Formulating the KL‑regularized reward maximization problem

The ultimate goal of RLHF is to obtain a language model \(\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\) that, given a prompt \(x\), produces a completion \(y\) which humans judge to be better according to some latent reward function \(r^{*}(x,y)\). Two intertwined requirements make this problem difficult:

1. **Reward uncertainty** – we never see \(r^{*}\) directly; we only observe **relative** preferences over pairs of completions.
2. **Distribution drift** – any policy update that diverges too far from its starting point can result in catastrophic failure—either loss of useful knowledge or mode collapse.

To address these constraints, RLHF casts inference as a *constrained* reinforcement learning problem:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\pi^{*}_{\theta} = \arg\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\;
& \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\, y\sim \pi_{\theta}}\!\bigl[r^{*}(x,y)\bigr] \\
& \quad - \beta\, \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\;\Vert\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr].
\end{aligned}\tag{3.1}
\]
Here:

- \(\mathcal{D}\) is a corpus of prompts drawn from deployment.
- \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is the *reference policy*: essentially the starting language model (often an SFT model). It embodies prior knowledge and serves as a safety net.
- The **KL term** penalizes deviation from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), limiting how much we are allowed to change probability mass.
- The scalar \(\beta > 0\) balances exploitation (maximizing reward) against safety (controlling drift).

Equation (3.1) is an example of *policy regularisation*. Its form can be derived by acknowledging that LLMs produce sequences by sampling tokens one after another; however, for brevity we treat each completion \(y\) as an atomic outcome.

---

## Translating human preferences into a reward function

Human labelers play an essential role: they compare two completions \(y_{1}\) and \(y_{2}\) for a given prefix \(x\), and indicate which one they prefer. This data is denoted \(y_{w}\succ y_{l} \mid x\), where “\(w\)” means winner and “\(l\)” means loser.

### The Bradley–Terry model

A principled way to encode preferential feedback is via the Bradley–Terry (BT) model:
\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
\frac{\exp(r^{*}(x,y_{1}))}
     {\exp(r^{*}(x,y_{1})) + \exp(r^{*}(x,y_{2}))}. 
\tag{3.2}
\]
If two completions have higher rewards under \(r^{*}\), they are more likely to win. The BT formulation reduces preference learning to binary classification: we treat the pair as two classes and predict which one should obtain higher logit.

### Parametric reward estimation

Because we cannot observe \(r^{*}\), we fit a parametric surrogate \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\) – typically a neural network that accepts a prompt/completion pair, tokenises it, and outputs a scalar reward. The negative log‑likelihood objective over all preference pairs \(\mathcal{D}=\{(x,y_{w},y_{l})_i\}_{i=1}^{N}\) is:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{R}(r_{\phi};\, \mathcal{D}) = - 
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})}[\, 
   \log\sigma(r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{l}))\,],
   \tag{3.3}
\]
where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\). Several practical tricks help stabilise training:

- **Reward anchoring** – one often normalises rewards so their mean is zero under \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).
- **Filter on temperature** – keeping per‑token likelihoods bounded avoids log‑divergence when logits diverge.

Once trained, \(r_{\phi}\) is interpreted as *the reward function that explains human preferences*, up to additive constants per prefix (the BT family is underdetermined by a constant shift).

---

## From reward to policy: KL‐bounded optimality

Equation (3.1) cannot be optimized directly because it requires gradient signals through sampling from an LM policy over all token sequences—a combinatorial nightmare. Classical RL solutions discretise using likelihood ratios or advantage estimates; RLHF routinely employs **PPO**, an actor‑critic method that samples trajectories from the current policy and adjusts parameters via clipped surrogate objectives.

Nevertheless, prior work has shown that when the reward function is *logistic*-like, Eq. (3.1)’s optimal solution admits an analytic form:
\[
    \pi_r(y|x)
    = \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
            e^{\, r(x,y)/\beta}}
           {Z(x)},     
    \qquad
    Z(x)=
         \sum_{y'} 
           \pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)
           e^{\, r(x,y')/\beta}.     
    \tag{3.4}
\]
This equation emerges from treating RLHF as *variational inference*: maximizing expected reward minus KL is equivalent to minimizing relative entropy between the new and reference distributions conditioned on weighted exponentiated rewards.

Key observations:

- **Proportional update rule** – if two completions swap order in terms of their exponentiated rewards, their relative likelihoods under the updated policy swap accordingly.
- **Partition function** – likewise to softmax normalization, \(Z(x)\) ensures probabilities sum to one; computing it exactly would necessitate summation over all possible completions—impossible in practice.

Large‑scale LMs therefore approximate this operator either via Monte‑Carlo sampling (counting how many times each token occurs across many trajectories) or via learning separate value functions as peractor baselines.

---

## Practical RLHF training with PPO

While Eq (3.4) reveals closed form for an *optimal* policy under fixed rewards, real training proceeds incrementally: every iteration samples new trajectories with current parameters, critiques them using on‑policy critics or baselines, and performs gradient updates on both actor and critic networks.

### PPO surrogate objective

PPO introduces an importance–sampling ratio
\(r_t = {\exp(\theta-\theta_\text{old})}\)
and clamps it to stay within [\(1-\epsilon,\; 1+\epsilon]\).
The PPO loss aimed at increasing expected reward while controlling policy shifts becomes:
```python
loss = -torch.mean(
            torch.minimum(
                r_t * adv_t,
                torch.clamp(r_t, 1-epsilon, 1+epsilon)*adv_t
            )
        )
```
where `adv_t` is an advantage estimate (reward difference shifted by learned value).

### Advantages of PPO in practice

- **Sample efficiency** – Each trajectory can be re-used several times.
- **Stability** – Clipping reduces variance due to large ratios.
- **Flexibility** – Can incorporate novel tricks like KL loss or action padding.

### Hyperparameter profile

Typical configurations for replacing SFT LMs in RLHF tasks:

| Hyperparameter | Setting |
|----------------|----------|
| batch size | 8192 |
| clip ratio ε | 0.2 |
| entropy bonus | 0 (often omitted due deterministic LMs?) |
| KL coefficient β | tuned per task |
| rollout length | full generated sequence |

Large models exacerbate sampling costs: generating sampled completions takes seconds per prompt; thus practitioners often cap rollout horizons or use prompt‑caching strategies.

---

## Challenges that motivate Direct Preference Optimization

Despite PPO’s ingenuity, several systematic difficulties persist:

1. **High variance gradient estimates** – Because trajectories are long sequences of discrete tokens, estimating advantages reliably requires careful baselining or variance reduction techniques.
2. **Reward estimator inaccuracies** – Training `r_phi` may misinterpret preferences if data lacks diversity; any systematic bias propagates into the learned policy.
3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity** – The balancing coefficient β must be tuned per task and per seed; small changes can tip training towards mode collapse or high-divergence behaviours.
4. **Computational burden** – Sampling thousands of completions at each step for large models severely limits scaling.

The DPO method presented later bypasses many of these pitfalls by directly modelling policies consistent with human preferences through a closed form reparameterization of rewards (§4). Nonetheless, understanding conventional RLHF remains essential because DPO inherits much of its intuition: it still optimises for maximizing expected latent reward while shrinking distance from a reference LM through KL regularisation.

---

### Summary

In this chapter we have walked through the entire pipeline of RLHF:

1. Collect human preference pairs between two completions.
2. Fit an implicit reward network via Bradley–Terry binary cross‑entropy.
3. Convert latent rewards into constrained optimisation over policies with KL penalties.
4. Train using advanced actor–critic methods such as PPO.
5. Grapple with practical difficulties including sample inefficiency and tuning burdens.

While each step appears straightforward at first glance—"train a stackable classifier", "run trust‐region updates"—the combinatorial explosion inherent in discrete text generation turns them into challenging engineering puzzles. Recognising these challenges sets the stage for more principled alternatives like Direct Preference Optimization, which will explore exactly why some manipulations peppered across RLHF may be unnecessary if we change how we formulate our objective in hindsight.\quad_endblockquote
