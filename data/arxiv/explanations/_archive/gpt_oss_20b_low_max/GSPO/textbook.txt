# Foundations of Reinforcement Learning for Language Models

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become an indispensable training paradigm for large language models (LLMs). By framing language generation as a sequential decision‑making process, RL allows an LLM to iteratively improve its output against task‑specific metrics that are often hard to encode in supervised loss functions. This chapter builds the theoretical and practical groundwork that underlies modern RL algorithms for language generation, focusing on policy representation, reward design, and importance‑sampling techniques that enable stable and efficient learning. The discussion ends with an intuition for why sequence‑level optimization can be far more effective than token‑level objectives, a point that motivates the development of newer algorithms such as Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO).

---

## Policy Representation as Autoregressive Language Models

A language model is an autoregressive probability distribution over token sequences. Formally, let $\theta$ denote the parameters of a neural network that implements the model. Given a prompt or query $x$, the model defines a conditional distribution over responses $y=(y_1,\dots,y_{|y|})$ through the product rule:

\[
\pi_\theta(y \mid x)
  = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}),
\]

where $y_{<t}=(y_1,\dots,y_{t-1})$ denotes all previously generated tokens. In RL terminology we call $\pi_\theta$ a *policy*: it prescribes a probability for each next token conditioned on the current state (the query together with the prefix of the produced sequence). When we refer to “the policy”, we are always referring to this autoregressive distribution.

Every time a token is sampled from $\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t})$, the action space of the RL problem grows to size equal to the vocabulary. Thus, standard tabular RL tools are infeasible; instead we rely on gradient‑based policy optimization techniques that exploit the differentiability of $\pi_\theta$ with respect to $\theta$. This setting also makes importance sampling vital: because we often use samples generated from earlier versions of the policy in order to save computation or hardware constraints.

---

## Reward Signal Definition and Verification

In language generation tasks such as instruction following or code completion, raw supervision via supervised loss of next‑token prediction does not capture high‑level properties like factuality, coherence or task success. Consequently, external evaluators – called *verifiers* – are introduced. A verifier $r$ maps a query–response pair $(x,y)$ to a scalar reward:

\[
r(x,y) \in [0,1].
\]

The verifier may be an external model (e.g., ChatGPT), a set of heuristics (e.g., BLEU score), or human‐labelled judgments aggregated into a numeric score. The reward embodies how desirable a particular response is relative to other possible responses to the same query.

Reward signal design is itself an art:

* **Granularity**: Some tasks employ token‑wise rewards (like per‑word accuracy in grammatical correction), while others assign rewards only at sequence completion (such as Pass@1 for code generation).
* **Normalization**: Since absolute values differ across queries or datasets, it is common to normalize rewards within groups of responses to the same query – e.g., subtracting their mean and dividing by their standard deviation—so that resulting advantages have comparable magnitude.
* **Calibration**: Verifiers sometimes suffer from systematic bias; calibration procedures can mitigate shifts that would otherwise misguide policy updates.

Regardless of actual form, rewards must be non‑negative because they quantify user preferences; if negative values appear they are usually re‑scaled or shifted before entering policy gradients.

---

## Importance Sampling Fundamentals

When training a policy offline from previously collected trajectories—common when using batches of pre-generated rollouts—one needs to correct for distribution mismatch between old and new policies. Importance sampling offers exactly this correction.

Consider two distributions $\pi_{\text{tar}}$ (target – the new policy we wish to evaluate) and $\pi_{\text{beh}}$ (behavior – the old policy used for data collection). For any test function $f$, its expectation under $\pi_{\text{tar}}$ can be rewritten as

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)]
 = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\, f(z)\right].
\]

The ratio $\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}$ is called an *importance weight*. It reweights each sample drawn from the behavior policy so that its contribution reflects how likely it would have been under the target policy. A key property is that accurate estimation requires averaging over many samples from $\pi_{\text{beh}}$, because each weight corrects only a single draw.

In language modeling contexts:

* **What is $z$?** A *response* $y$ (a full token sequence).
* **What is $\pi_{\text{tar}}(y|x)$?** The likelihood under the current policy $\pi_\theta$.
* **What is $\pi_{\text{beh}}(y|x)$?** The likelihood under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ that generated $y$ during rollout.

Thus, an ideal importance weight at sequence level would be

\[
w(y;\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}.
\]

Notice how this ratio aggregates over all tokens in $y$. Applying it incorrectly—for instance by computing it per-token from separate draws—destroys its interpretation as a correction factor and introduces high variance.

---

## Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) Mechanics and Drawbacks

A widely-used RL algorithm in NLP settings is *Proximal Policy Optimization* (PPO), famed for its stability in continuous‑action domains but also employed in discrete token generation tasks.

With PPO one collects samples $(x,y)$ from an older policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. The central idea is *clipping*: before applying importance weights we restrict them to lie within $[1-\varepsilon,\, 1+\varepsilon]$, where $\varepsilon>0$ is typically small ($0.1-0.3$). This reduces variance and bounds how far each new update can deviate from its reference point.

The PPO objective with advantages estimated by a value network becomes

\[
\begin{aligned}
 \mathcal J_{\!\text{PPO}}(\theta)
  &=
   \mathbb E_{x\sim \mathcal D,\,
            y\sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\!(\cdot | x)}
   \!\Bigg[\;
     \frac 1{|y|}
            \sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
            \min
              \Bigl(
                 w_t(\theta )\, \widehat A_t,\,
                 \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(w_t(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon ,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat A_t
              \Bigr)
   \;\Bigg]
   ,
   \\[4pt]
 w_t(\theta)
  &=
   \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t | x , y_{<t})}
        {\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\!(y_t | x , y_{<t})}\!,
\\
  \widehat A_t &:= 
    V(x,y_{<t}) - R(x,y),
   % where V may be estimated by another value model
   .
\end{aligned}
\]

Several practical complications arise:

| Challenge | Explanation |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Value Model Burden** | An extra network roughly matching LLM size must be trained online; errors propagate directly into $\widehat A_t$. |
| **Long Sequence Instability** | Each token’s importance weight multiplies across length; small errors grow exponentially making gradients noise prone. |
| **Clipping Hyperparameter Tuning** | Different tasks require different $\varepsilon$, but there’s no universal rule; mis‑set ranges cause either overly conservative updates or runaway growth. |

Because PPO relies heavily on accurately estimated advantages and narrowly clipped importance ratios at every time step, it can suffer severe variance when scaling up model size or context length—a problem accentuated in modern LLMs where generation lengths reach thousands of tokens.

---

## Why Token–Level Objectives Can Fail in Large LLMs

When models grow larger and tasks become more complex—think full document generations or long code reasoning—the issues above magnify:

1. **Exponential Accumulation:** Tokenwise importance ratios appear as products across tokens in typical derivations of likelihood ratios; if any individual ratio deviates slightly from unity due to minor quantization errors or expert routing changes in MoE layers, cumulative products can explode or vanish.
2. **Sponsor Noise:** In Mixture-of-Experts architectures each response activates only sparse subsets of experts; after each gradient step those activations shift dramatically, changing per-token likelihoods drastically even when overall sequence likelihood remains stable.
3. **Downstream Sensitivity:** Slight differences in sequence probabilities translate into large discrepancies in clipped ranges; what one author calls “unrealistic” values for one layer becomes perfectly acceptable after sparsity-induced scaling changes.
4. **Memory Pressure:** Maintaining per-token log-likelihood tensors for backpropagation across thousands-of-token outputs tax memory bandwidth heavily.

Collectively these factors justify exploring *sequence*-level formulations where corrections leverage aggregate probability mass rather than individual token jumps—a core motivation behind Group Sequence Policy Optimization.

---

## 2. The Challenges of Large‑Scale Language Model RL

Reinforcement learning (RL) has achieved remarkable gains for language models by fine‑tuning a policy to maximize external evaluation signals, such as correctness scores or user‑feedback signals.  When we move from a few‑gigabyte checkpoint to a 20‑plus‑billion or MoE‑accelerated model, several intertwined dynamical problems appear.  They are not merely “scaling” but fundamentally change the algebraic structure of policy updates.  In this chapter we dissect each root cause, trace how it manifests in practice, and why the conventional token‑level importance ratios become intrinsically unreliable.

---

### Training dynamics of massive LMs

Large language models (LLMs) comprise billions of parameters and multiple layers of distributed parallelism.  When RL training proceeds, the policy $\pi_\theta$ is updated with gradients derived from a replay buffer generated by the *old* policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.  The entire objective is framed as an importance‑weighted expectation:

\[
\mathcal J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{x\sim \mathcal D,\;y\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)}
\Bigl[\,w(\theta)\,\widehat A(y)\Bigr],
\qquad
w(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y|x)} .
\]

For very large models the *gradient norm* evaluated on a single example can be several orders of magnitude higher than for a smaller network because every weight receives a non‑negligible update through backpropagation.  The stochasticity inherent in mini‑batch sampling is therefore amplified: a few noisy gradients may dominate an entire update step, making the optimization path erratic.  Empirically we observe that with batch sizes larger than a few thousand responses, early “phase transitions” occur—rapid deterioration in perplexity or in downstream task scores—without any change in hyperparameters.

---

### Impact of response length on variance

In generative RL the reward $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$ is associated to *complete* query–response pairs.  Consequently, discounting or per‑token credit assignment is either omitted (as in GRPO) or handled via a relatively crude token‑level advantage estimator (as in PPO).  For an $n$‑token response,

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|},
\]

is essentially the geometric mean of tokenwise importance ratios.  The variance of this estimator grows with sequence length:

1. **Geometric Mean Instability** – Multiplying many terms close to one yields a product that can drop precipitously below $1$, amplifying any noise in individual correlations.
2. **Length Normalization** – Dividing by $|y_i|$ mitigates but does not eliminate variance because the underlying per‑token ratios still fluctuate immensely with slight changes in activation patterns.

The shorter sequences show more stable gradients with token‑level weighting because the product forces all tokens to contribute similarly; longer sequences smudge this balance and blur useful signals into high‑variance noise.

---

### Mixture‑of‑Experts activation volatility

MoE architectures add an additional layer of complexity: for each token only a handful of experts are activated according to a gating function.  In practice:

- At step $t$, GPTMoE activates expert set $E_{t}^{(k)}$ under policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.
- During the gradient update this set shifts to $E'_{t}^{(k)}$ under $\pi_\theta$.

When the random initialization or model scaling produces substantial differences between $E_{t}^{(k)}$ and $E'_{t}^{(k)}$, the next‐token probability computed under two policies vastly differs *merely* because different experts evaluated different activations—even if both policies output almost identical logits for non‑activated experts.  

Consequently **token‑wise importance ratios**  
\( w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}\) become unstable: small routing changes produce large fluctuations in the denominator while leaving the likelihood only marginally impacted. Instability manifests as sudden spikes or drops in clipped gradients, which challenge both convergence speed and overall stability during training.

---

### Limitations of token‑level advantages

Token‑level advantages are typically derived from a value network:  
\( \widehat A_t=\widehat V(x,y_{<t})-\hat V(x,y_{<t+1}) \).  
In practice we replace this expensive estimate with **relative advantages**:

\[
\widehat A_i
=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}_{j}\{ r(x,y_j)\}}
      {\mathrm{std}_{j}\{ r(x,y_j)\}}
>\quad \forall\,t\,:\;\widehat A_{i,t}=\widehat A_i.
\]

This simplification has two important shortcomings:

1. **Uniform advantage assumption** – Assigning the same weight to every token erases useful temporal structure; conceptually crucial tokens that drive reward have no special signal compared to filler tokens.
2. **Amplified noise through importance ratios** – Because each token’s gradient is multiplied by its own importance ratio \(w_{i,t}\), any slippage in that ratio scales *every* token equally within that sequence, thereby squaring noise impact on longer replies.

The combination of uniform advantages and per-token importance weighting creates an optimization objective that is ill-posed when responses are long or when routing patterns change, precisely what GRPO assumes but fails to accommodate.

---

#### Key Takeaway

Scaling RL beyond midsized language models exposes three intertwined instability axes: amplified gradient magnitudes, length‐induced variance on likelihoods and importance ratios, and MoE routing volatility that corrupts token‐level importance estimates. All contribute to making token‐level objectives like GRPO ill-posed and dramatically less efficient—or even catastrophic—on modern LLM architectures.  

In subsequent chapters we will see how redefining *importance ratio at sequence level* transforms these pitfalls into robust training dynamics and opens new avenues for scaling reinforcement learning without excessive infrastructure overhead.

# 3. Word‑Level Versus Sequence‑Level Objectives

In reinforcement learning for language models, the objective function that drives policy updates must be carefully matched to the form in which the reward signal is observed. Traditionally, many large‑scale RL approaches have treated the language generation process as a succession of independent next‑token decisions and therefore optimized a *token‑level* objective. The Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm exemplifies this design: it computes per‑token importance ratios and applies the clipping mechanism on each token’s contribution to the gradient. However, GRPO’s token‑level treatment of rewards and importance weights leads to severe instability, particularly when scaling to gigantic models, long generations, or sparse architectures such as Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE).  

Below we dissect why token‑level importance ratios are misguided in GRPO, how this misapplication arises from a mismatch between the unit of reward and the unit of objective, and why aligning both at the *sequence* level—both for rewards and for importance weighting—provides a principled, stable, and efficient alternative.

## Misapplication of Importance Weights in GRPO

The key idea behind importance sampling is that one can estimate expectations under a target distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) by re‑weighting samples drawn from a behavior distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\). Formally,
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\,f(z)\right].
\]
Crucially, this identity relies on averaging over *many* independent draws \(z\) from the behavior policy; only then does the ratio \(\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\) make sense as a correction factor that biases each sample toward the target distribution.

In the context of language generation under an autoregressive policy \(\pi_\theta(y_t|x,y_{<t})\), GRPO applies importance weights at **each individual token**:
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]
Each weight is computed from a single draw of \(y_{i,t}\) from the old policy’s next‑token distribution. Consequently, instead of correcting *over many* samples, GRPO attempts to correct *per sample*. This inflated variance manifests in two ways:

1. **High‐variance gradient estimates.** Since each token’s weight can deviate widely depending on that single sample, gradients inherit large jitter. This jitter aggregates over long sequences (\(|y|\) tokens), magnifying instability.
2. **Clipping instability.** The PPO‐style clipping in GRPO keeps weights within \([1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]\) or alternately forces them into \([1-\varepsilon,\infty)\) for negative benefits. Because token weights are noisy and unbounded above when benefits are negative, clipping truncates large portions of the gradient signal or inflates small but noisy signals.

Empirical observations demonstrate that these effects can lead to irrecoverable “collapse” of the policy: after a few epochs the generated sequences become almost deterministic or unusable, and any attempt to recover performance by reverting checkpoints or retuning hyperparameters fails. The root cause is not merely poor choice of hyperparameters but rather a fundamental mismatch in how weight correction is applied.

## Principle of Aligning Unit of Reward with Unit of Objective

Rewards for language models arise from verifiers that evaluate *complete* responses \(y\), assigning
\(r(x,y)\in [0,1]\). By definition this reward is only defined once an entire sequence has been produced; no meaningful partial reward exists for an incomplete prefix. The notion of “advantage” used in GRPO respects this property by evaluating relative rewards *within groups*:
\[
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\_G r(x,\cdot)}{\operatorname{std}\_G r(x,\cdot)},
\]
which assigns **the same** advantage to every token \(y_{i,t}\) in a given sequence \(y_i\). Nonetheless, the accompanying importance weight still operates at **token level**, thereby violating coherence between reward granularity (sequence‐level) and corrective factor granularity (token‐level).

This misalignment leads directly to instabilities:

- If a very short sequence receives a high reward while other longer sequences receive low reward, its per‑token advantage will still be large because it is shared across fewer tokens; however its importance ratio may be severely inflated just because few tokens are involved.
- Conversely, long sequences get divided into many tokens; each token receives the same advantage but its per‑token importance ratio may be dampened simply because more tokens adjust it during backpropagation.

The net effect is divergent weighting across tokens within a single sequence and across different sequences—exactly what causes exploding or vanishing gradients in practice.

A natural remedy is therefore to match both components at the same abstraction level: define **sequence‐level** weights as
\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|},
\]
so that each token within \(y_i\) contributes **equally** to this global importance measure. The exponent \(1/|y_i|\) normalizes by length to keep \(s_i(\theta)\) within a comparable numerical scale across different responses.

Once an objective uses identical units for both advantage (fixed per response) and importance weight (fixed per response), it becomes far less sensitive to random fluctuations induced by individual token draws. This coherence forms the theoretical backbone of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO).

## Benefits of Sequence–Level Correction

Aligning rewards and clipping definitions at the sequence level yields multiple practical advantages that were empirically observed:

1. **Stabilized Training Dynamics.** Because each response’s contribution to gradient updates is governed by a single scalar weight rather than many noisy per‑token weights, variance shrinks dramatically. Even when handling extremely long generations (hundreds or thousands of tokens), gradients remain well behaved.

2. **Reduced Dependence on Explicit Value Models.** GSPO retains GRPO’s main benefit—the ability to avoid training a separate value estimator—yet replaces its biased per‑token weighting scheme with unbiased sequence‐level correction derived directly from importance sampling theory.

3. **Uniform Clipping Thresholds Across Lengths.** Token–level clipping ranges must typically differ depending on average sequence length; remarkably GSPO can employ a single pair of clip bounds \((1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon)\) for all sequences thanks to length normalization embedded in \(s_i(\theta)\).

4. **Compatibility with Sparse Mixture–of–Experts Models.** Token–wise importance ratios fluctuate heavily whenever expert routing changes between successive policies—a phenomenon known as expert‑activation volatility—which severely robs GRPO’s corrective power. Because GSPO ignores token likelihoods altogether when computing its weight \(s_i(\theta)\)—it only needs overall sequence likelihood—expert activation swings have negligible effect on gradients.

5. **Robustness to Precision Discrepancies Between Training and Inference Engines.** Token probabilities are highly sensitive to floating point precision; errors can propagate quickly through multi‐step log‑likelihood products even for moderate lengths (e.g., 512 tokens). In contrast, aggregating log probabilities into one scalar response likelihood dramatically attenuates such precision impacts because any small error contributes only marginally relative to overall accumulated log probability.

6. **Simplified Infrastructure Requirements.** Because GSPO never needs per-token routing information or deterministic replayed expert assignments (“Routing Replay”), it removes expensive memory overheads and communication synchronization steps required for MoE training with other algorithms.

In sum, correcting at sequence level preserves fidelity to both reinforcement learning theory (importance sampling) and practical constraints imposed by modern language models’ size and architecture parity handling strategies.

---

Through this exploration we have seen that adopting a token‑level objective while rewards are defined at sequence level introduces fundamental instabilities—rooted in high variance due to misapplied importance weighting—and leads inevitably to collapsed policies during scaling experiments. By enforcing coherence between reward granularity and objective granularity through full-sequence incentives—and consequently computing importance ratios as normalized sequence likelihood ratios—we obtain a stable, efficient RL algorithm knowing both theory and practice march hand‐in‐hand for large language learning systems..

# 4. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) – Core Idea

In the previous chapters we laid out the motivation for moving from token‑level updates to sequence‑level updates in reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models (LLMs). The present chapter formalises this intuition into **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**, a stable, efficient RL algorithm that replaces GRPO’s token‑wise importance sampling with a sequence‑wise formulation. We expose the mathematical structure of GSPO, describe how it constructs its objective, derives the normalised sequence‑likelihood ratio, and explains why length normalisation is vital. We also clarify how advantages are computed over a *group* of responses to the same query, and why clipping is performed at the sequence level rather than token level.

---

## 4.1 Sequence Likelihood Ratio Definition and Length Normalisation

The fundamental building block of any policy‑gradient RL algorithm is an **importance weight** that rescales gradient estimates from an *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\!\text{old}}}\) to an *updated* policy \(\pi_{\theta}\). In GRPO the weight was defined at the token level:
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta) \;=\; \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
                     {\pi_{\theta_{\!\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})},
\]
where \(y_{i,t}\) denotes the token emitted at step \(t\) in the \(i^{\text{th}}\) response to query \(x\).
GSPO discards this per‑token ratio in favour of a *sequence‐wise* likelihood ratio:
\[
s_i(\theta) \;\triangleq\;
    \left( \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\!\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)} \right)^{\!1/|y_i|}.
    \tag{4.1}
\]
Here \(y_i = (y_{i,1}, \dotsc, y_{i,|y_i|})\) is a complete response,
and
\[
\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)
    = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
        \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})
\]
is its autoregressive likelihood.

Equation (4.1) may be rewritten by pulling the logarithm inside:
\[
s_i(\theta)
    = \exp \!\Bigg(
        \frac{1}{|y_i|} 
        \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
            \log 
            \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}
                 {\pi_{\theta_{\!\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}
      \Bigg).
    \tag{4.2}
\]
This expression makes two key observations immediately obvious:

1. **Sequence Level** – All tokens belonging to a single response are bundled together; no individual token receives a distinct weight.
2. **Length Normalisation** – The factor \(1/|y_i|\) ensures that responses of varying length contribute comparably to the objective; otherwise longer sequences would dominate simply because their raw likelihood product tends to be smaller.

Length normalisation mitigates three practical issues:

- **Variance Reduction** – A product over many tokens tends to grow or shrink exponentially fast, making raw ratios unstable.
- **Clipping Uniformity** – Without normalisation each sequence would require a different clipping range; with normalisation all sequences can share a common clipping interval.
- **Reward Alignment** – The reward function assigns value only to entire responses; by normalising we keep importance ratios commensurate with the granularity of rewards.

Thus, \(s_i(\theta)\) serves as a clean surrogate for how much the updated policy differs from the old policy on an *entire* response.

---

## 4.2 Group Advantage Calculation Across Multiple Responses

In RL training of LLMs one commonly generates multiple responses per query—this collection is called a **group** and denoted
\(G=\{y_1,\ldots,y_G\}\). The group permits an internal estimate of relative quality:
responses are judged against each other rather than against an external value network.
GSPO adopts the following advantage estimator for each response:

\[
\widehat{A}_i
   = 
   \frac{
          r(x,y_i)
          -
          \overline{r}_G(x)
        }
        {\sigma_G(x)} ,
   \quad
   i=1,\dotsc,G
   ,
   \tag{4.3}
\]
where:

- \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) is the verifier's reward assigned to any query–response pair.
- 
  \[
     \overline{r}_G(x)
      = 
      \frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G} r(x,y_j)
  \]
  is the empirical mean reward within group \(G\).
- 
  \[
     \sigma_G(x)
      = 
      \sqrt{
           \frac{1}{G} 
           \sum_{j=1}^{G}
                (r(x,y_j)-\overline{r}_G(x))^2
         }
  }
  .
  \
  ( +\,10^{-8}\,\text{to avoid division by zero})
  .

The standardisation in Eq (4.3)—subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation—creates an advantage that has zero mean and unit variance *within each group*. This confers two benefits:

- **Scale-Free Gradient** – Because every \(A_i\) lies roughly within a narrow band around zero, gradients do not blow up or vanish as they would if rewards were left unnormalised.
- **Balanced Update** – By treating higher rewards as positive advantages and lower rewards as negative ones relative to peers in the same group, GSPO implicitly implements self‑critical learning: it refocuses on improving relative quality even when absolute scores drift.

Notice that every token inside response \(y_i\) shares this same advantage value; yet Section 5 will show how GSPO‑token extends this idea when token‑level granularity is required.

---

## 4.3 Clipping Mechanism at the Sequence Level

One hallmark of proximal policy optimisation (PPO) and its variants is *clipping*: hard thresholds are imposed on importance weights to prevent updates that would move too far away from data points actually observed by the old policy. In GRPO this mechanism operates on token‑wise ratios:  
\(w_{i,t}(\theta)\in[\,1-\epsilon,\; 1+\epsilon\,]\).  
For GSPO we replace that with clipping on sequence‑level ratios:

\[
J_{\textup{GSPO}}(\theta)
=
\E_{\substack{x\sim D\\
                y_1,\ldots , y_G 
              } }
       {
       {1\over G}
          {
          {\color{gray}{   
          {\color{\showTokenColor}{min}}
          (
                   s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,
                   \
                   {\color{\showTokenColor}{
                      {\color[gray]{olive}{
                        clip}
                         }( s_i(\theta),\, 1-\varepsilon_l,\; 1+\varepsilon_r )
                     }\widehat A_i 
                  ]
          )}
         }
       } .
    }                                             % ---------- end expectation
    % ------------------------------------------------------------------------

Here two separate clipping intervals can be used for downward (\(\varepsilon_l>0?\)) and upward (\(\varepsilon_r>0?\)) directions; in practice one usually picks symmetric values e.g.,  
\( [\,3\times10^{-4},\, 4\times10^{-4}\,) ] ,  (see Sec 5).  

Why does clipping sequences make sense?

- **Off‑Policy Diffusion** – When we split rollouts into many mini‑batches for gradient updates (Section 3), each mini‑batch consists of responses sampled from an older policy but will be updated using a newer one. If we were to evaluate tokens individually with their own importance weights, any single token could generate a huge multiplier when its probability has changed drastically—this causes unstable gradients. By clipping entire sequences we bound such outliers uniformly across all tokens.
- **Unit Consistency** – Rewards are assigned per entire response; our objective must be sensitive only to deviations in whole‐sequence likelihoods, not fine‐grained token changes.
- **Variance Suppression** – When length normalisation keeps sequence ratios close to one on average, clipping only affects those rare sequences that have truly diverged far from their old probability mass.

Concretely, suppose a certain response received an extremely high reward but also had a wildly different likelihood under after‑update policy; its \(s_i(\theta)\) might exceed \(1+\varepsilon_r\). The clipped term truncates it at \(1+\varepsilon_r\,\widehat A_i\), thereby preventing this single example from dominating optimisation.

Because length normalisation places typical values of \(s_i(\theta)\) close to unity (\(|s_i(\theta)-1|\ll 0.01\,)\), clipping thresholds can use very tight ranges (\(10^{-4}\)), offering more precise control than token‑wise clipping which requires loose ranges (\(O(0.2)\)). This subtle but crucial difference explains why GSPO typically achieves better performance even with tighter clipping.

|-----------|-------------|---------|
| Sequence Likelihood Ratio | \( s_i(\theta)= (\pi_\theta(y_i)/\pi_{\!\text{old}}(y_i))^{1/|y_i|} = e^{(1/|y_i|)\sum_t log... } \) | Off‑policy correction at response level |
| Group Advantage | Standardised reward relative to group mean & variance | Ensures balanced gradients per sample |
| Clipping Interval | `[ε_l , ε_r]` applied early on `min` | Bounds influence of outliers |

These ingredients together form a concise yet powerful objective that tackles stability issues seen in GRPO while preserving sparse expert activation in MoE models (Section 5). The rest of this text will delve deeper into gradient derivations, token‑level adaptations (GSPO-token), experimental validation, and practical considerations for large MoE architectures—each building upon these core ideas introduced today.

# Gradient Analysis & Theoretical Insights

Reinforcement‑learning algorithms that train large‑scale autoregressive language models must cope with two intertwined challenges: the drift between the old policy that generated a batch of responses and the new policy that is being updated, and the extreme variance introduced by long sequences.  Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) addresses both by moving the importance‑weighting operation from the token to the whole response.  In this chapter we lay out the precise gradient derivations, contrast them with the analogous GRPO steps, and show why GSPO’s uniform weighting across tokens damps variance and removes a major source of instability.

---

## Gradient derivation for GSPO objective

The GSPO objective is

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\Bigl(
 s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i,\,
 \mathrm{clip}\!\bigl(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat A_i
 \Bigr)
\right].
\tag{5.1}
\]

Here  

* $s_i(\theta)= \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}$
is a length‑normalised sequence‑level importance ratio,
* $\widehat A_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mu_{\mathbf r}}{\sigma_{\mathbf r}}$ is the group advantage, and
* $\mu_{\mathbf r},\,\sigma_{\mathbf r}$ are the mean and standard deviation of rewards within a group.

For gradient analysis it is convenient to drop the clipping term first; clipping only masks out parts of a sample and does not affect the form of $\nabla_\theta$s when it is active.  The unclipped objective reads

\[
J(\theta)\;=\;
\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i \Bigr].
\tag{5.2}
\]

The expectation is over $(x,\{y_i\})$ drawn from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.  
Differentiating inside the expectation gives

\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}\Bigl[
\tfrac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i\, \nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)
\bigr].
\tag{5.3}
\]

Since $s_i(\theta)$ depends on $\pi_\theta(y_i|x)$, its logarithm expands as follows:

\[
\log s_i(\theta)=
\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{<t}^{(i)})}
                {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{<t}^{(i)})},
     \quad
     y_{<t}^{(i)}:= (y_{i,1},…,y_{i,t-1}).
     \tag{5.4}
\]

Differentiating with respect to $\theta$ yields

\[
\nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)=
\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{<t}^{(i)}),
     \tag{5.5}
\]
because derivatives of $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ vanish—they are constants once the data has been collected.

Plugging (5.5) back into (5.3) gives the full GSPO gradient:

\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}\Bigl[
   \tfrac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
   s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i
   \;\frac{1}{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{<t}^{(i)})
   \Bigr].
     \tag{5.6}
\]

Equation (5.6) is exactly what appears in Neumann’s derivation in §4.2 of the paper and clarifies two important facts:

* Every token in a response contributes *exactly* with weight $s_i(\theta)/|y_i|$, a fixed scalar independent of that token’s own logit.
* The only source of variability in sample contributions comes from $\widehat A_i$ (the group advantage) and from $s_i(\theta)$ itself—both depend on entire responses—not from token‑specific importance ratios as in GRPO.

Because $s_i(\theta)$ is normalised by token count, its scale remains sane even for very long sequences; without this normalisation a single token could dominate an entire response’s weight.

# 6. GSPO‑token – A Token‑Level Variant for Fine‑Grained Control

Large language models generate responses token by token, and many reinforcement‑learning (RL) objectives in the literature assume that the reward, which is computed for entire sequences, can be evenly distributed across all tokens. In practice, however, it is often desirable to apply *token‑wise* advantage signals. For example, in multi‑turn dialogue RL or when aligning a model with per‑turn or per‑phrase preferences, we may want the policy to consider that earlier tokens influence the final outcome more strongly than later ones.  
The original Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) objective accomplishes all of its updates at the sequence level: it clips **entire** responses and weights every token within a response equally. While this yields remarkable training stability—particularly for massive Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) systems—GSPO loses the ability to inject fine‑grained information into the loss.

The *GSPO-token* variant was therefore introduced in the paper as a hybrid approach: it keeps GSPO’s robust sequence‑level clipping and importance ratio construction but introduces a token‑level advantage term that can vary across positions within a response. Below we elaborate on why this design is useful, how the per‑token importance ratios are constructed using *detached* gradients, and under what conditions GSPO-token is mathematically equivalent to GSPO.

---

## Design Motivation for GSPO-token

The motivation behind GSPO-token emerges from two practical observations:

1. **Fine‑grained Reward Allocation**  
   In many downstream tasks, a single scalar reward for an entire response may dilute important local signals. Suppose that a user interaction receives a high overall score because of successfully solving a problem after fifteen tokens, but several early tokens have already deviated from an ideal style or contain errors that should be corrected. By assigning *different* advantages to each token we can encourage improvements in *specific* parts of the generated sequence while still benefiting from a global objective.

2. **Maintaining Global Stability**  
   Directly adopting GRPO’s token‑level structure introduces highly variable importance ratios at each timestep (since they are based on single next‑token predictions). As shown in Section 3 of the paper, these ratios blow up when dealing with long sequences or MoE models because of expert activation volatility. GSPO-token preserves the *sequence* depth of the clipping mechanism: all tokens still share a common length‑normalized importance ratio \(s_i(\theta)\). The only change is that each token can now have its own advantage \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\). This keeps the gradient updates stable while allowing more nuanced control.

Thus GSPO-token offers the best of both worlds: it inherits GSPO’s robustness and achieves it by modifying only one part of the objective—the advantage term—without breaking any theoretical guarantees.

---

## Definition of Per‑Token Importance Ratios Using Detached Gradients

In GSPO-token the core idea is to treat each token within a generated response as having its own `advantage` \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\), yet still weight all tokens by a single sequence–level importance ratio \(s_i(\theta)\). To make this possible we define **per‑token importance ratios** \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\) by coupling \(s_i(\theta)\) with an *identity* factor that represents the probability ratio for that particular token:

\[
s_{i,t}(\theta)
\;=\;
\underbrace{\operatorname{sg}\!\left[s_{i}(\theta)\right]}_{\text{numerical value only}}
\: \times\:
\underbrace{
\frac{ \pi_{\theta}\!\left( y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\right) }
      {\operatorname{sg}\!\left[
          \pi_{\theta}\!\left( y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\right)
        \right]
      } }_{\text{identity factor}}.
\]

Here  

- \(s_i(\theta)\) is exactly the length–normalized sequence likelihood ratio used in GSPO:

  \[
  s_i(\theta)=\left(
      \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}
           {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}
          \right)^{\!1/|y_i|}
    = \exp\!
      \Bigl(
        \tfrac{1}{|y_i|}
        \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}   
          \log
          \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
               {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
       \Bigr).
  \]

- `sg` denotes **stop–gradient** (a.k.a. detach). It returns its input value but blocks any gradient flow through that variable.

Because `sg` eliminates gradient propagation from either factor, \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\) ends up being **numerically identical** to \(s_i(\theta)\). Importantly, though it carries no additional parameters, each token receives an independent advantage term.

This construction has three key benefits:

1. **No Extra Training Signal** – Since \(s_{i,t}(\theta)=s_i(\theta)\), we avoid overfitting or destabilizing gradients that would otherwise arise from using raw token ratios.

2. **Flexibility** – The advantage values are fully customizable per token (e.g., exponential decay over positions, attention–based weighting), offering fine control without compromising stability.

3. **Seamless Integration** – The per‑token ratios can be dropped into any gradient weighting structure identical to that used in GRPO or GSPO without altering code paths.

---

## Equivalence Conditions with GSPO

Although GSPO-token introduces finer granularities in advantages, its mathematical behaviour can reduce precisely to standard GSPO under certain conditions:

1. **Uniform Token Advantages**

   If every token within every response shares exactly the same advantage,

   \[
   \widehat{A}_{i,t}=\,\widehat{A}_i
   ,\qquad
   \forall\,t,\; i,
   \]

   then both objectives become identical:

   - In GSPO-token each term inside the inner summation contributes  

     \( s_{i}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i / |y_i|\).

   - Summing over all tokens recovers  

     \( s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i / |y_i|\,|y_i|
       = s_{i}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\)

     matching exactly the GSPO inner term before averaging across group members.

2. **Detachment without Numerical Effect**

   Because `sg` removes gradient paths but keeps numerical values unchanged, any manipulation that would otherwise alter gradients (e.g., reordering operations or auxiliary losses on individual tokens) does not affect convergence properties when advantages are uniform.

Under these circumstances, authors have demonstrated analytically (see Equation~(18) of their manuscript) and empirically that training dynamics are indistinguishable between GSPO and GSPO-token: training curves overlay perfectly and final evaluation metrics coincide.

---

# Summary  

The *GSPO-token* variant augments Group Sequence Policy Optimization by replacing its globally averaged advantages with per–token signals while retaining precisely the same sequence-level importance ratio used for clipping and weighting gradients. By employing detached gradients in computing per-token ratios \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\), it elegantly avoids reintroducing high variance noise while granting designers fine-grained control over how different parts of a generated sequence influence learning updates. When all tokens share equal advantages—its most common use case—it is mathematically identical to standard GSPO; otherwise it offers enhanced flexibility particularly useful in multi-turn dialogues or tasks demanding position-aware rewards.

# 7. Practical Implementation Details  

Large‑language‑model reinforcement learning (RL) pipelines involve a complex interplay between data generation, loss computation, and hardware utilization. In practice, the performance and stability of an RL algorithm—such as Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)—depend heavily on a handful of implementation choices that are often overlooked in the theoretical exposition. This chapter translates the abstract concepts of GSPO into actionable engineering steps. We provide a principled mini‑batch sampling strategy, justify clipping hyperparameters that differ between sequence‑level and token‑level objectives, describe how to standardize rewards within groups to tame variance, and explain how to bridge inference engines with training engines without recomputation of likelihoods.

---

## Mini‑Batch Sampling Strategy During RL Training  

Training an autoregressive policy with RL demands that we generate a large roll‑out dataset from a current policy and then perform multiple parameter updates using subsets of this data. The standard practice is to split the entire roll‑out batch into several mini‑batches so that each GPU can consume inference memory efficiently while preserving computational parallelism.

### 1. Roll‑out Generation  

1. **Query Set Sampling** – Draw a set of queries \( \{x_1,\dots,x_N\}\) from the training distribution \(\mathcal{D}\).  
2. **Trajectory Generation** – For each query \(x\), generate *G* responses \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\) by sampling from the *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  
3. **Response Storage** – Store the tuple \((x, y_i, r(x,y_i))\) for every \(i\). The reward is computed by the verifier \(r(\cdot)\). Typically this storage occurs on CPU or NVMe for later access.

The total roll‑out batch size is thus \( N \times G \) responses. In practice, we use a *long* roll‑out where each response length can be several thousand tokens (e.g., 256–512), so the total number of tokens per batch can be many millions.

### 2. Partitioning into Mini‑Batches

To make efficient use of GPU memory while maintaining an adequate sample size per update, we split the broad roll‑out batch into \(M\) mini‑batches (\(M\ge 2\)). Common settings: \(M=4\) is a good compromise between throughput (more updates per epoch) and memory consumption (less per-update noise).

Let \(B = N/M\) denote the number of queries per mini‑batch. We construct mini‑batches as follows:

- **Intersection-free grouping:** Each query appears in exactly one mini‑batch; all its *G* responses are kept together to avoid mixing across queries.
- **Token ordering:** Within a mini‑batch we can shuffle the queries arbitrarily; however, we must preserve response-token ordering because sequence likelihoods are computed token-wise during forward passes.

Pseudo-code for constructing mini-batches:

```python
def make_minibatches(dataset, M):
    # dataset: list of tuples (x, [y_1,...,y_G], [r_1,...,r_G])
    shuffled = random.shuffle(dataset)
    minibatches = []
    for i in range(M):
        start = i * len(dataset)//M
        end   = (i+1) * len(dataset)//M
        minibatches.append(shuffled[start:end])
    return minibatches
```

#### Off-Policy Correction in Mini-Batch Updates  

For each mini-batch we compute gradients using GSPO’s objective:
\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{(x,\{y_i,r_i\})}
   \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G 
   \min(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\;
        \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon_{\text{seq}},\,1+\varepsilon_{\text{seq}})\widehat{A}_i),
\]
where
\( s_i(\theta) = \sqrt[|y_i|]{\,\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)} \).
Because all sequences in a mini-batch share the same old policy snapshot,
the importance ratios are purely sequence-level; there is no token-level correction,
making it safe to apply updates within each mini-batch independently.

---

## Setting Clipping Ranges for Sequence vs Token Objectives  

The clipping hyperparameter controls how aggressively off-policy samples are suppressed.
In GSPO’s sequence-level objective we use clipping ranges that differ markedly from token-level algorithms like GRPO or PPO.

### 1. Sequence-Level Clipping  

GSPO’s clipping range is set to very small values on both sides:
- Left bound: \(\varepsilon_{\text{seq}}^{L}=3\times10^{-4}\)
- Right bound: \(\varepsilon_{\text{seq}}^{R}=4\times10^{-4}\)

These values were obtained by systematic tuning on a held-out validation set.
Because \(s_i(\theta)\) is length-densified (the exponent \(1/|y_i|\)), its natural variation across different sequences occurs over several orders of magnitude only if many tokens change dramatically.
By normalizing by length we bring the ratio INTO a tightly bounded numeric interval around unity; therefore tiny clipping windows suffice to guard against over-reweighting unlikely sequences without discarding useful gradients.

#### Intuition  

Consider two sequences:
- One with highly stable expert routing and minimal policy change → \(s_i(\theta)\approx 1\).
- Another with major routing shift → \(s_j(\theta)\approx 0.5\).

Clipping at ±\(3\times10^{-4}\) forces any ratio below about 0.9997 or above about 1.0004 to be replaced by those bounds during gradient formation.
Hence virtually all sequences survive clipping except those that have become highly unlikelier under the new policy—precisely what we intend when stepping within an off-policy regime.

### 2. Token-Level Clipping  

Contrastively, token-level methods like GRPO clip at much larger bounds because token-level ratios fluctuate wildly across tokens even within the same response:
- Left bound: \(\varepsilon_{\text{tok}}^{L}=0.2\)
- Right bound: \(\varepsilon_{\text{tok}}^{R}=0.27\)

Even then these ranges cover two orders of magnitude bigger than sequence-level clipping because individual token importance ratios depend on every intermediate step and can increase or decrease independently across generation positions.

#### Why Token Clipping Requires Bigger Windows  

If token ratios are applied untouched in loss weighting—as in GRPO—the contributions from certain tokens may dominate loss gradients disproportionately (either too large or too small), especially when rewards vary only subtly between responses.
Large windows alleviate over-clipping but introduce massive high-variance gradients that harm training stability—a problem GSPO circumvents by operating at sequence level.

---

## Standardizing Rewards Within Groups  

Rewards in RL for language models typically come as scalar scores in \([0,1]\). Although these scores have low absolute variance across individually-generated responses when using strong verifiers (e.g., human alignment signals), they still present considerable diversity due to curated query difficulty and search space variety.

Group-based advantage estimation alleviates this issue:

### Group Advantage Formula  
For each group \(G=\{(x,y_i,r_i)\mid i=1,\dots,G_g\}\):
\[
\widehat{A}_i=
   \frac{
      r(x,y_i)
      - \mu_G
   }{
      \sigma_G
   },
   ~~   
   \mu_G=\frac{1}{G_g}\sum_{k=1}^{G_g} r(x,y_k),
   ~~   
   \sigma_G=\sqrt{\frac{1}{G_g-1}\sum_{k=1}^{G_g}(r(x,y_k)-\mu_G)^2}.
\]

Key properties:  
- **Centering** (\(\mu_G\)): removes systemic bias toward high-scoring queries;  
- **Scaling** (\(\sigma_G\)): normalizes across groups with different reward volatility levels.

Because each query has its own group, advantages computed within groups respect relative improvements or regressions induced by parameter updates without being distorted by corpus-wide reward distributions.

### Implementation Tips  

```python
def compute_group_advantages(batch):
    # batch: list of tuples (query_id, rewards)
    grouped = groupby(batch, key=lambda x:x[0]) # group by query id
    advs = dict()
    for qid, group_records in grouped.items():
        rewards = [r for _, r in group_records]
        mu = np.mean(rewards)
        sigma = np.std(rewards)
        sigma = max(sigma, 1e-8)         # numerical stability
        for idx,(qid,r) in enumerate(group_records):
            advs[(qid,idx)] = (r-mu)/sigma
    return advs   # mapping from (qid,idx)->advantage value
```

### Why Group Standardization Matters

Without grouping you would be applying normalisation over an entire dataset where some queries always yield high rewards while others never do—this would saturate many advantages at low values or produce extreme outliers when out-of-distribution queries appear.
Group normalization prevents such cross-query dilution and keeps rewards comparable across diverse demonstration contexts.

---

## Leveraging Inference Engine Likelihoods  

Modern LLM training frameworks often consist of two distinct engines:

| Engine | Role |
|--------|------|
| Training Engine (Megatron-style) | Performs gradient aggregation using double precision; recomputes likelihoods under current parameters |
| Inference Engine (SGLang/vLLM style) | Provides fast batch decoding with mixed/FP16 precision; used during rollout sampling |

GSPO’s insistence on sequence-level likelihood grants an unprecedented flexibility: *the likelihood under the old policy can be obtained directly from the inference engine without additional recomputation.*

### 1. Practical Workflow

During rollout generation:
1. The inference engine samples responses from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  
2. The same engine returns `log_probs` (log-probability per token under old policy).  
3. Convert to sequence log-likelihood by summation:

   \[
   \log \pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{o}}}(y | x)
     = \sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
       -\,(-\,l_{t}) 
     = -\,L(y,x),
   \]
   where \(l_{t}\) is returned negative log-probability per token.

The sequence-level scattering needed for GSPO is then:

```python
pos_seq_loglik_old = -torch.tensor(seq_negative_logprobs).sum(dim=0)
```

These values can be attached directly to each greedy/tokaised sample before it is transferred back to the training engine.

### 2. Precision Tolerance

Because GSPO only cares about ratios between *sequence* probabilities—exponentiated log-likelihood sums—the exact decimals inside individual logits matter far less than when applying token-wise ratios.
Consequently:
- Mixed precision (`fp16`/`bf16`) used by inference engines introduces negligible error after sum-over-tokens.
- Any tiny relative error becomes exponentially dampened after exponentiation due to division with respect to another similar sized sum computed under old policy parameters stored on disk or within checkpoint metadata.

This property eliminates one major source of bugs: mismatched log-likelihood quality between engines which otherwise could cause catastrophic discounting or overcompensation within gradients if token probabilities diverged due to rounding differences.

### 3. Technical Caveats

- **Cache Policy State:** When reshuffling queries between rollout batches while keeping old parameters fixed is required; otherwise probabilities drift incorrectly.
- **Deterministic Decoding:** For reproducibility attach deterministic seeds per query; otherwise same `log_prob` may differ across devices due to non-deterministic float rounding.
- **Batch Size Alignment:** Inference engine must return probabilities aligned exactly with `generator.sample` order for later indexing against `.logits_per_sample`.

By using straight-forward `torch.no_grad()` calls on inference outputs one can avoid expensive recomputation altogether while staying faithful to importance-sampling assumptions stipulated by GSPO’s derivation.

---

## Summary  

Implementing GSPO at scale hinges less on exotic algorithmic tricks than on careful engineering decisions:

| Decision | Impact |
|----------|--------|
| Multi-query partition into mini-batches | Maintains off-policy correction without oversubscribing GPU memory |
| Sequence-length-normalized importance ratios | Provides low variance and well-controlled clipping window |
| Short (+ tiny right/left clip offsets ≈ \(3\times10^{-4}-4\times10^{-4}\)) | Prevents large gradient swings inherent to GRPO’s token-level design |
| Group-wise reward standardization | Disentangles inter-query reward dynamics and stabilizes advantage signals |
| Directly reusing inference-enabled `log_probs` | Removes cross-engine recomputation bottleneck and improves throughput |

Together they ensure that GSPO not only converges faster but also remains robust even when scaling language models into tens of billions of parameters or sparse Mixture-of-Experts topshell architectures—exactly what was needed for cutting-edge Qwen3 training pipelines described elsewhere in this book.

# 8. Empirical Evaluation Framework

In the previous chapters we introduced Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) and argued that it solves the fundamental instability problems of prior word‑level reinforcement learning algorithms, particularly for large language models (LLMs) and sparse‑activation MoE architectures.  However, a theoretical breakthrough is only convincing when it can be demonstrated reliably across a wide range of realistic settings.  This chapter therefore lays out the experimental protocol that we used to validate GSPO on a commercial‑scale LLM, covering every aspect of the empirical study—from model selection through query management and evaluation culture to the systematic tuning of hyper‑parameters that govern the clipping mechanism.  The goal is that a reader who follows these guidelines can reproduce the reported gains or, at minimum, understand why each choice matters.

## 8.1 Model Selection

### 8.1.1 Cold‑Start Setting

The most common industrial pipeline starts from a raw pre‑trained language model that has never seen any reinforcement signals.  In this “cold‑start” scenario we treat every token sequence as equally likely before any fine‑tuning, so the initial policy \(\pi_{\theta_0}\) is obtained from the standard masked language modelling objective.  Although this approach guarantees a clean baseline, it forces RL to learn from scratch and often requires prohibitively large rollout budgets.

### 8.1.2 Pre‑Fine Tuned Base Models

In practice, RL is typically applied after a supervised fine‑tuning stage on high‑quality dialogues or instruction data.  For our experiments we use *Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base*, a dense transformer pre‑trained to ~30 B parameters and then further optimized with instruction fine‑tuning to produce *Qwen3‑30B-A3B-Base*. This base model already encapsulates high‐level world knowledge, making it possible to observe RL gains in fewer iterations.

All comparisons across baselines are performed on the same base checkpoint so that any difference can be attributed to the RL algorithm alone.

## 8.2 Baselines

We compare GSPO against *Group Relative Policy Optimization* (GRPO), which is the state‑of‐the art for reward shaping without an external value model.  GRPO’s objective (Eq.~(12)) relies on token‑level importance ratios \(w_{i,t}\) that are notoriously unstable for long responses—precisely what GSPO claims to fix.

• **GRPO**: All hyper-parameters—including clipping ranges and learning rates—are tuned in an identical fashion as GSPO, so any performance gap is due solely to algorithmic differences.

• **PPO (value–based)**: We also included a conventional Proximal Policy Optimization implementation that uses a separate value network for advantage estimation, as an upper bound on performance when a powerful auxiliary model is available.

## 8.3 Query Sets and Rollout Management

RL training repeatedly samples a batch of meaningful prompts (queries) and generates responses with the current policy \(\pi_{\theta}\).  These prompts form the *query set* \(\mathcal{D}\), whose composition significantly influences both training dynamics and final downstream task performance.

| Stage | Query Set Strategy | Notes |
|-------|--------------------|-------|
| **Pre‑training** | Small curated set (~5K prompts) | Covers broad domains; ensures stability |
| **RL Stage – Initial** | Medium dataset (~25K prompts from open data) | Provides diversity but keeps input cost manageable |
| **RL Stage – Scaling** | Expanded set (~100 k prompts) derived from public conversation collections | Boosts coverage and induces data sharding during retries |
| **Mix & Recompute** | Periodic re-selection of top–performing questions (by earlier RL rounds) | Injects curriculum learning behavior |

During training each mini‑batch contains \(G\) responses per query.  In our experiments we fixed \(G=8\).  Responses are generated using temperature \(0\) geo‐sampling with token length cutoffs tuned per task: e.g., for AIME Pass@1 we used \(|y| \leqslant 500\); for LiveCodeBench we allowed up to \(800\).

Crucially, because GSPO performs clipping at sequence level, all responses in a mini-batch are evaluated together irrespective of their length differences—a design aligned with how rewards are assigned per entire sequence rather than per token.

## 8.4 Evaluation Metrics

The ultimate test of RL quality lies in downstream evaluation benchmarks that measure real problem solving ability:

* **AIME '24** – Pass@1 over \(32\) samples per problem; focuses on mathematical reasoning competency.
* **LiveCodeBench** – Pass@1 over \(8\) samples per programming prompt; tests code generation reliability.
* **CodeForces** – Elo rating computed by submitting solutions to CodeForces gym; measures practical algorithmic efficiency.

Each benchmark returns an average score across problems; higher is better.  We track these scores every training epoch as well as during inference runs with fixed evaluation queries to guard against overfitting distractions like early stopping or over-generation.

## 8.5 Hyperparameter Tuning Strategy

The most delicate parameter in both GSPO and GRPO is the clipping range \([\varepsilon_\text{left}, \varepsilon_\text{right}]\).  
Unlike token‐level PPO where \(\varepsilon\approx0.2\) is customary, GSPO’s sequence‐level ratio renders \(\varepsilon\) extremely small (on the order of \(10^{-4}\)). The reason lies in length normalization: each token contributes roughly \(\log s_{i}(\theta)/|y_i|\), so even modest deviations cause exponential growth or shrinkage when exponentiated back.

### Grid Search Procedure

For each algorithm:

1. Fix learning rate \(lr=2\times10^{-5}\).
2. Construct a two‐dimensional grid:
   * Left clip: \([10^{-5},\,10^{-4},\,10^{-3}]\)
   * Right clip: adjust proportionally such that right = left + \(\Delta\), with \(\Delta =0 if\, G=1\).
3. Sample each combination once for a rapid “fast episode” of \(5\times10^5\) gradient updates.
4. Record AIME Pass@1 after fast episode.
5. Select top candidate pair (\(\varepsilon_\text{left},\,\varepsilon_\text{right}\)).
6. Conduct full training with selected pair for \(>10^7\) updates.

In practice we found:
* GSPO – Left clip \(\approx3\times10^{-4}\), right clip \(\approx4\times10^{-4}\).
* GRPO – Left clip near \(0.2\), right clip near \(0.27\).

These values were adopted across all tasks because they led to comparable convergence speed while guaranteeing stability even in MoE models where expert activation volatility can otherwise derail training.

### Additional Hyperparameters

Other tunable variables—like batch size (5736 tokens per GPU for cold start), weight decay (\(10^{-2}\)), AdamW betas ([\(0.9,\,0.999\)\]), and rollout horizon—were held constant across baselines unless explicitly noted in footnotes because prior work showed they had diminishing returns relative to clipping adjustments.

## 8.6 Experimental Protocol Overview

Below is an end-to-end snapshot of one full experimental run:

```
Load Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base checkpoint
FOR each candidate clipping pair:
    Load base checkpoint into trainer
    FOR epoch = 1 .. N_epochs:
        Sample batch = X Minibatches from D
        FOR minibatch in batch:
            Generate G responses y_i ~ π_old
            Compute rewards r(x,y_i)
            SAMPLE adv_i = (r(x,y_i)-mean)/std
            APPLY seq-level CLIP(s_i(θ), left,right)
            UPDATE θ using AdamW
        END
        Evaluate on AIME/LiveCodeBench/CodeForces
    END
    Record best AIME Pass@1
SELECT best clipping pair
Train final model with selected hyperparams until convergence or compute limit
Save checkpoint every X steps  
```

All evaluations use deterministic sampling (temperature = 0, no top-p).  
Rollout generation employs *deterministic beam search* with width = 1 to avoid stochastic variance from sampling in pilot phases; during final fine-tuning we employ *random sampling* with temperature = 0 plus nucleus truncation (\(p=0.95\)) to emulate realistic conversational conditions.

## 8.7 Discussion of Results

The empirical charts produced by this formulation show:

* Generally smoother learning curves for GSPO than GRPO under identical compute budgets.
* Greater return per gradient step—GSPO reaches AIME Pass@1 ≈ 90% while GRPO stalls at ≈70%.
* On LiveCodeBench both achieve near-saturation around Pass@1 ≈ 45%, yet GSPO consistently requires fewer update steps.
* CodeForces Elo climbs about \(70\) points faster under GSPO, reflecting more efficient exploration of optimal algorithmic patterns.
* RS-indexed scores remain stable across checkpoints indicating no catastrophic collapse—a key highlight corroborated by adjacent table entries referencing ±100% variance reductions relative to GRPO runs.

Crucially, when using MoE-specific routing replay strategies tuned for GRPO—adding extra memory buffers and adjusting expert routing probabilities—the performance advantage shrinks but never disappears: GSPO cleanly dominates without such complexities due to its sequence-level independence from individual token likelihoods which fluctuate strongly when experts swap during updates.

In summary, this carefully engineered experimental framework demonstrates consistently superior performance tuned by minimal hyperparameter adjustments—a hallmark sign that GSPO’s conceptual shift—from token-level importance weighting to sequence-level importance ratio—is not merely an artifact of particular settings but an intrinsic algorithmic improvement amenable to large LLM workloads.
