# The Secret Sauce of Qwen3’s Success: An Inside Look at Group Sequence Policy Optimization

Large‑language models (LLMs) are reaching a new frontier of capability thanks to reinforcement learning (RL) applied at scale. Yet the path to that frontier is peppered with stability pitfalls that can turn a promising RL run into an irreversible collapse.  The latest Qwen3 family owes much of its performance leap to a surprisingly simple change in the RL algorithm: **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**.  Below we unpack what GSPO does differently, why that matters, and how it translates more compute into higher accuracy.

## 1. The Problem With Token‑Level Off‑Policy Correction

Most off‑policy RL for language modeling—think PPO and its variant GRPO—corrects for the difference between the *old* policy $\pi_{\text{old}}$ that generated a reply and the *current* policy $\pi_\theta$ being optimized.  In token‑level methods this correction is done per next‑token:

\[
w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t|x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_t|x,y_{<t})}.
\]

The intuition is simple: weight the gradient contribution of each token by how unlikely it became under the new policy.  Unfortunately, tokens are generated one after another; each token’s importance weight is computed from **a single sample** of its next‑token distribution.  When responses become long—as they do for math or programming tasks—the accumulated product of noisy weights blows up.  Clipping (the safety net in PPO/GRPO) cannot fully correct for this because it works on individual tokens rather than on an entire sequence.

The net result?   The variance introduced by token‑level importance ratios can cause *catastrophic collapse*, especially in gigantic models or sparse Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures where expert activation patterns shift dramatically from step to step.

## 2. GSPO’s Core Insight: Match Reward to Random Variable

Rewards in language‐model RL are awarded to **entire** response sequences.  Therefore, the natural unit of off‑policy correction must be the full sequence rather than each token tick.  GSPO tackles this mismatch head‑on:

- **Sequence-level importance ratio**  
  \[
  s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
      =\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}
          \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\!\log
          \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
          \right).
  \]
  By normalizing by sequence length we keep $s_i$ within a tight numeric range and reduce variance.

- **Sequence‐wise clipping**  
  All tokens in a reply inherit the same clipped importance ratio:
  \[
    \min\bigl(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\;
             \text{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat A_i\bigr),
  \]
  where $\widehat A_i$ is a group normalised advantage derived from rewards comparisons across $G$ responses to the same query.

In short, GSPO replaces per‑token corrections with a *single* correction per reply—exactly what the reward really matters for.

## 3. Training Curves that Speak Volumes

We measured Qwen3 training with a cold‑start base model fine‑tuned on our standard query set, comparing **GSPO** against **GRPO** under identical compute budgets.

| Measure | GRPO | GSPO |
|---------|------|------|
| **Reward growth** | Rapid early rise then plateau/decline | Monotonic rise throughout |
| **AIME'24 Pass@1** | Stabilises at ~25% | Reaches ~33% after doubled compute |
| **LiveCodeBench Pass@1** | Flat after few epochs | Continual climb to ~40% |
| **CodeForces Elo** | Drops after few updates (due to collapse) | Keeps improving |

The most striking observation is that GSPO’s reward curve keeps climbing linearly with training time—unlike GRPO’s erratic plateau and eventual collapse.  Even when we double the computation budget (more GPU hours, longer generation lengths), each additional epoch pushes performance further for GSPO but yields diminishing returns for GRPO.

### Why Does More Compute Pay Off?

Because GSPO’s gradients are stable: each reply contributes a single well‑behaved weight rather than many noisy σ‐shaped token weights that can cancel or amplify unpredictably. This translates *every* extra optimizer step into useful signal rather than noise or overfitting fearfully small samples.

## 4. Efficiency Gains Across the Board

It might seem counterintuitive that GSPO can achieve higher efficiency while clipping a *larger* fraction of tokens—our figures show up to two orders of magnitude more clipped tokens than GRPO—but there’s an important nuance:

- **Token‐level clipping discards partially useful information.** When many per‑token ratios hit the clipping bounds, their contributions vanish despite partially correct next‑token predictions that should still inform learning.
- **Sequence‐level clipping retains full gradient signal when only the overall likelihood deviates sufficiently** – it discards entire replies only when they’re truly off policy relative to their length.

Thus GSPO sacrifices fewer overall learning opportunities and pushes every retained reply as hard as possible toward higher reward without exhausting memory or compute on token buzz.

## 5. MoE Models Get a Full House

Dense LLMs suffer less from expert activation volatility; sparse MoE models are notorious for drastic shifts in which experts are invoked per token after each gradient update. GRPO reacted by inventing *Routing Replay*: cache old routing diagrams and force the new policy to use them when computing token importance ratios. This trick added a suitcase of memory traffic and limited orthogonality because we were basically freezing part of the model.

GSPO sidesteps all of this:

- By focusing on sequence likelihood alone ($\pi_\theta(y_i|x)$), it ignores which experts fired on which token.
- Sequence likelihood remains smooth even when expert routing spins around: you still know how likely an entire reply was under both policies.
- No caching or locking is needed; we drop Routing Replay entirely.

The impact?   MoE model training becomes as stable as dense counterpart training—no exotic tricks required—and we unlock full capacity benefits of MoE scaling without slashing them down artificially.

## 6. Simplifying RL Infrastructure

Real-world RL pipelines routinely juggle two engines: one for training (Megatron‑LMI style) which uses high precision arithmetic, and one for inference (sllang/vLLM) which may use lower precision or different software stacks. Reconciling these engines demands recomputation of exact token logprobs—a costly step refulling up communication bandwidth and CPU/GPU cycles.

Because GSPO only needs *sequence-level* likelihoods—which are far less sensitive to tiny floating point discrepancies—it can safely reuse logprobs computed by an inference engine even if precision differs slightly from training engine expectations. This removes an entire layer of recomputation and aligns our RL pipeline closer to production serving environments (partial rollouts, multi-turn dialogues), dramatically cutting wall time per iteration.

## 7. Takeaways For Practitioners

1. **Check your unit alignment:** Reward → sequence; correction → sequence; ignore per-token mismatches unless you have reliable value estimates.
2. **Length-normalise importance ratios:** It keeps numbers in control and stabilises gradients.
3. **Clip sequences, not tokens:** Less noise; more information preserved.
4. **Leverage MSOE benefit:** If you’re training MoE models stop using routing replay; just use GSPO.
5. **Reuse inference logprobs:** Because they’re tolerant thanks to sequence focus—great for multi-turn RL or deployment-inference “blueprint” scenarios.

## Final Thought

GSPO distils what turned out to be most distracting in previous algorithms—a misaligned unit of correction—and repairs it with an elegant yet practically grounded fix: treat partial probability updates at the same scale as rewards themselves. That modest shift lets Qwen3 glide smoothly along longer horizons, squeeze more accuracy out of extra compute and bring MoE models off their unstable rocketry trajectory toward steady climb throttles instead of catastrophic bursts—a true secret sauce turned everyday practice.*
