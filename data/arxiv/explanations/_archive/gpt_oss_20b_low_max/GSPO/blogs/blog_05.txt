# Beyond Basics: Token‑Level vs. Sequence‑Level Advantages in RL for LLMs

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the de facto method for turning raw language models into “smart assistants” that can reason, solve math problems, or write clean code.  In practice, the efficiency and stability of RL hinge on how we estimate *advantages*—the signal that tells the policy which actions (or tokens) to favor.  Traditionally, algorithms such as GRPO have used *token‑level* advantages, while the newer Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) framework replaces them with *sequence‑level* advantages.  In this post we dive into the mathematics behind these two views, discuss why a *GSPO‑token* variant can still be useful, and clarify how each formulation shapes gradients during training.

---

## 1. The core idea: aligning the reward unit with the optimization unit

Rewards in text generation are usually attached to **entire responses**; a verifier assigns a score \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) to a full query–response pair \((x,y)\). Consequently, any sensible policy gradient should operate at the same level of granularity. Two strategies dominate:

| Algorithm | Advantage source | Level | Clipping target |
|-----------|------------------|-------|-----------------|
| **GRPO** | Relative ranking among responses to a *single* query | Token | Token-wise importance ratio \(w_{i,t}\) |
| **GSPO** | Normalized relative reward within a group of responses | Token (for log‑likelihood) but *sequence‑level* importance ratio \(s_i\) | Sequence-wise ratio \(s_i\) |

The mismatch between token‑wise clipping and sequence rewards is at the heart of GRPO’s instability, especially when dealing with long responses or sparse MoE architectures. GSPO remedies this by reintroducing importance sampling at the sequence level.

---

## 2. GRPO’s token‑level advantage (Equation (4))

GRPO starts from a vanilla PPO objective but replaces the *value function* by an advantage estimate computed **within a group** \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\) generated for one query \(x\):

\[
\widehat{A}_{i,t}
= \widehat{A}_i
= \frac{ r(x,y_i) - \mu_r }{ \sigma_r },
\qquad 
\mu_r = \mathop{\rm mean}\!\bigl(\{ r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{G}\bigr),
\;
\sigma_r = \mathop{\rm std}\!\bigl(\{ r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{G}\bigr).
\]

Every token in response \(y_i\) receives the same advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\). The left‐hand side of GRPO’s objective is

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_i|}
      \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
      \min
     \left(
         w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\,
         \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat{A}_i
     \right)
\right].
\]

Here each token’s importance ratio

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)
=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\]

acts as an *off-policy correction factor*.  

> **Why this is problematic.**  
> The ratio \(w_{i,t}\) uses only **one** sample of next-token probability; it cannot correct for distributional mismatches that accumulate across long sequences. Moreover, because every token shares the same advantage but receives its own distinct weight, gradients become heterogeneously scaled—especially when some tokens are already far from their old-policy probabilities.

---

## 3. GSPO’s sequence‑level advantage (Equation (10))

GSPO keeps the same advantage definition—normalizing rewards within each query group—but shifts importance sampling to *sequence space*. Define for response \(y_i\)

\[
s_i(\theta)
=
\left(
   \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}
        {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}
\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=
e^{\,\frac{1}{|y_i|}
         \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
            \log
              \frac{
                    \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
                  }{
                    \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
                  }
        } .
}
\]

The objective becomes

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\left[
   \frac{1}{G} 
   \sum_{i=1}^{G}
   \min
     \bigl(
           s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\,
           \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat{A}_i
     \bigr)
   \right].
   \tag{10}
\]

Notice that:

* The clipping operates on whole responses (\(s_i(\theta)\)), not individual tokens.
* Length‐normalization (the exponent \(1/|y_i|\)) keeps ratios comparable across different lengths.
* Every token’s gradient inherits the **same** weight via an overall factor \(s_i^{\,1/|y_i|}\).

---

## 4. GSPO‑token: fine‑grained control on top of sequence weighting

Some RL settings—particularly multi‑turn dialogue or instruction following—may benefit from rewards that vary token by token. GSPO introduces *GSPO-token*, a hybrid that keeps sequence-level importance ratios but allows per‑token advantages:

```text
J_GSPO-token(θ)=E_x,{yi}[  sum over i  (1/|yi|)
              sum over t  min( s_it(θ)*Ã_it,
               clip(s_it(θ), 1-ε, 1+ε)*Ã_it ) ]
```

where

```
s_it(θ)= sign(s_i(θ)) * π_θ(y_it | …)/sign(π_θ(y_it | …))
```

In practice `sign(.)` denotes detaching gradients; thus numerically `s_it(θ)=s_i(θ)` while preserving backprop flow.

This variant satisfies two desiderata:
* The clipping still guards against overly off‑policy sequences.
* Token‐specific advantage `Ã_it` can encode context‐aware signals—e.g., penalizing premature termination or rewarding term completion.

When all `Ã_it` are equal across tokens of a response, GSPO-token collapses back to plain GSPO—demonstrating theoretical equivalence.

## 5. Side‑by‑side gradient derivations

For clarity let us compare the two gradients explicitly.

### 5.1 Gradient of GRPO (token level)

Dropping clipping for exposition:

```tex
∇_θ J_GRPO =
E_x,{yi}[  (1/G) ∑_i ∑_t [ w_it(θ)*êA ]
           · ∇_θ log w_it(θ) ]    --
== E_x,{yi}[  (1/G) ∑_i êA/∑_j w_ij … ]
```

Cartesian product notation omitted; key point: each token carries its own weight \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\).

### 5.2 Gradient of GSPO (sequence level)

```tex
∇_θ J_GSPO =
E_x,{yi}[ (1/G) ∑_i s_i^{1/|yi|} êA
          · (1/|yi|)
             ∑_t ∇_θ log π_θ(y_{it}|…)]
```

All tokens in `yi` share identical scaling by `s_i`. There is no per-token weight; only an overall factor that reflects how far the entire sequence differs from its old-policy counterpart.

### 5.3 Comparison table

| Feature                | GRPO                                           | GSPO                                              |
|------------------------|-------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------|
| Importance ratio       | `w_{i,t}` per token                            | `s_i` per sequence + length normalisation          |
| Advantage              | Group‐normalized `êA` per response             | Same `êA` per response                            |
| Clipping target        | Token ratio (`w`)                              | Sequence ratio (`s`)                               |
| Gradient weighting     | Token‐wise weights vary across tokens           | Uniform weight across all tokens within response    |
| Computational complexity per update | O(|yi|·log …)                          | O(|yi|·log …) *but no extra per-token stochasticity|
| Stability in MoE models | Sensitive to expert activation volatility       | Robust because only overall likelihood matters    |

The essential conceptual shift is moving from many small stochastic influences (token-wise weights) to one smooth influence per entire sequence.

---

## 6. When do we want fine‐grained token control?

While sequence-level advantages stabilize training broadly, PETER-like tasks demand more nuanced signals:

- **Turn constraints**: In multi-turn dialogue you might penalize gratuitous continuations after a question “What’s next?” while rewarding concise answers.
- **Error propagation**: If early hallucinations cause downstream tokens to suffer heavily, giving each token its own penalty can encourage better early predictions.
- **Alignment with user preferences**: A user could specify “Be concise” which maps naturally onto penalizing later tokens if prefixed tokens are already supplied.

In these cases GSOP-token is invaluable because it lets you keep a single sequence-level clipping policy while tailoring local encouragement or discouragement via unnecessary fine grained advantages.

---

## 7. Takeaway & practical guidance

- **Match reward granularity & policy updates**: If your reward is at sequence level, avoid token-level corrections unless you have very fine-grained, reliable signals.
- **Caution with long sequences & MoE models**: Token-wise ratios fluctuate dramatically when experts switch; use sequence ratios instead.
- **Pick GSOP-token when you need local nuance**: It is essentially interface neutrality; just set your per-token advantages differently.
- **Memory and compute trade-offs**: Both approaches require similar loop structures over tokens; major differences lie in how many times you recompute likelihoods and how many ratios you store.

Ultimately, moving towards sequence-level importance ratios untethers RL training from volatile per-token estimation noise and unlocks smoother convergence paths for ever larger language models—including those with sparse experts and multi-turn architectures.
