# From Tokens to Stories: Why Sequence‑Level Rewards Fix RL for LLMs

## The problem with token‑wise optimization

When we teach an autoregressive language model (LLM) to generate better text by reinforcement learning (RL), we usually collect a *batch* of full responses, then update the model policy “offline”. Each response is a sequence of tokens \((y_1,\dots,y_{|y|})\) drawn from the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). The RL signal, however, is the *reward* \(r(x,y)\) that the verifier gives to the **entire** response.  

In the classic approach called *Group Relative Policy Optimization* (GRPO), the algorithm tries to keep the policy change small by clipping *token‑wise* importance ratios

\[
w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}
                  {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}.
\]

These ratios are multiplied by the same advantage for every token in a single response. The hope is that policy updates will not stray too far from the old policy.

But token‑level weighting brings a hidden issue: **importance sampling** works only when the weight is a global correction factor, averaged over many samples. Here each weight is based on a **single token** from its next‑token distribution. For long responses, the product of these per‑token weights will explode or vanish completely. The clipping mechanism can then either discard most tokens or let a few wildly off‑policy tokens dominate the gradient—both situations drive huge variance into updates. When you stack many such noisy gradients over time, training collapses: probabilities that were once sensible become wildly distorted.

An everyday analogy helps: imagine grading an essay. If you penalise each sentence separately based on how well it matches some “ideal” sentence, you’re ignoring that an excellent paragraph can still contain one weird sentence that gets stuck in place. Grading the entire essay as one coherent piece provides a more stable, representative assessment.

## Re‑thinking importance ratios: from tokens to whole sequences

The key insight behind Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) is that the reward already lives at the *sequence* level. Therefore, our importance ratio should also compare whole sequences, not scattered tokens.

The GSPO ratio is

\[
s_i(\theta)
  = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|},
\]

where we normalise by sequence length to avoid extreme values. This ratio is nothing more than a deterministic “how different has this whole response become?” check: if our new policy favours this exact response much more than it did before, \(s_i(\theta)\) will be > 1; if it favours it less, it will be < 1.

Because we clip based on \(s_i(\theta)\) rather than individual \(w_t(\theta)\), any odd behaviour of one token cannot unduly affect the whole update—every token in a response gets weighted equally by the same factor derived from its parent sentence’s overall likelihood.

### What’s in the gradient?

With GSPO, the gradient of an update looks like

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{GSPO}
 = \mathbb{E}\!\Big[
     s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i
     \;\cdot\;
     \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t} \nabla_\theta\!\log
          \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{<t})
   \Big].
\]

Notice how each token’s log–gradient is multiplied by **the same** scalar \(s_i(\theta)\). There’s no token‑level importance weight hanging around to inject variance. Contrast this with GRPO where each token would carry its own factor \(\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{old}}}\).

## Why sequence weighting matters in practice

### 1. Stability with huge models and long answers
Large LLMs often generate responses dozens or even hundreds of tokens long. Minor drifts early in training can compound across tokens and lead to catastrophic model collapse—something observed frequently with GRPO on MoE architectures or when pushing compute budgets higher.

GSPO’s sequence‑level ratio naturally damps this compounding effect: an entire response can only influence gradients as much as its *overall likelihood shift*. Even if one prefix of a long answer becomes off‑policy, it doesn’t blow up because it remains part of a single weighted unit.

### 2. Cleaner MoE training
Mixture‑of‑Experts models activate different experts per token; their activation pattern can change abruptly after each policy update. This volatility wreaks havoc on token‑level importance ratios because permutations of active experts directly alter \(\pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid \cdot)\). GSPO sidesteps this altogether—its ratio depends only on overall sequence likelihood, which remains smooth even when expert masks wiggle.

### 3. Less engineering overhead
With GRPO you had to store routing information (which expert fired for which token) and replay them during gradient estimation—a technique called *Routing Replay*. That extra bookkeeping parasitically inflated memory usage and slowed down training pipelines.

GSPO eliminates the need for such workarounds; its gradient involves only standard log–likelihoods per token and a scalar sequence weight computed once per response.

## An intuitive story analogy

Think of teaching a student who must write essays:

- **Token‑wise grading**: You read each sentence and give it an isolated score based on how well it matches your template sentences. One badly phrased sentence can handicap the whole essay because you penalise it heavily while discarding otherwise good sentences that happen to be off‑topic simply due to context drift.

- **Essay‐wise grading**: You read the entire composition first, decide if it reminds you of high quality writing overall (using your eye for flow, logic, tone), and then apply that verdict uniformly across all sentences when giving feedback.

The second approach tends to produce steadier improvements because you’re aligning your feedback scale (the reward) with how you evaluate students (the sequence). Exactly what GSPO does for language models: align thing-to-be-optimised (reward) with how we measure change (sequence likelihood).

## Bottom line

Reframing RL updates from “token trouble” to “story shape” gives SDM stability across gigantic dense and sparse models alike. By moving all important ratios and rewards into Sequence Level Domain:

- We dramatically reduce variance.
- We avoid nasty degradation paths that plague token‐level methods.
- We streamline infrastructure needs (no routing caches needed).
- We keep Hallucination alarms low – because overly optimistic gradients aren’t being introduced by brittle token pushes.

In short, **GSPO turns reinforcement learning into a story‑training exercise rather than a sentence‑training one**, allowing large language models to grow gracefully as we give them more data and compute.
