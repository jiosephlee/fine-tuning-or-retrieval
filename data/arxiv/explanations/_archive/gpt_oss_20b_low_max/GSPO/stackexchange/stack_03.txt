Answer:
**Why GSPO can avoid the high‑variance token‑level weighting that plagues GRPO**

Both GRPO and GSPO play with the same PPO idea: a clipped importance‑ratio term multiplied by an advantage.  
The crucial difference is *what* the importance ratio represents and *where* it is applied.

---

### 1.  Token‑level ratios in GRPO

GRPO uses  
$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)
   = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
          {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
$$
Each token in a sampled response is re‑weighted independently.  
A single token’s likelihood under the new policy can vary wildly from the old one, especially when a language model has changed its **expert routing** or when it has been fine‑tuned for longer generations. Consequently the ratio $w_{i,t}$ can jump from a value close to zero to a very large number in one step.

Because the gradient of GRPO’s objective contains a *sum over tokens*

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}
 =\mathbb E\Bigg[
   \frac1G\sum_{i}\frac1{|y_i|}
     \sum_t w_{i,t}(\theta)\,
     \nabla_\theta\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
   \Bigg],
$$

every token’s contribution is weighted by its own $w_{i,t}$.  If some tokens have an extremely large ratio, their gradients are amplified; if they are near zero, their gradients vanish.  Over many minibatches these unequal weightings add up and inject **high‑variance noise** into the overall update.  The clipping mechanism does not help because it only restricts the ratio within $[1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]$ when the risk of going outside is already high; when a ratio exceeds this range, all tokens from that response are discarded, but still the surviving tokens carry biased gradients.

---

### 2.  Sequence‑level ratio in GSPO

GSPO replaces $w_{i,t}$ by a single **sequence‑level** importance ratio

$$
s_i(\theta)
   = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)} \right)^{1/|y_i|},
   =
   \exp\!\Bigl(
     \tfrac1{|y_i|}\sum_t 
       \log
       \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
            {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     \Bigr).
$$

Thus every token in the same response contributes *equally* to the weight:  
the whole response is either kept or discarded as a unit.

The resulting gradient is

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}
=
\mathbb E
\Bigg[
  \frac1G
   s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i
   \,\frac1{|y_i|}
    \sum_t
      \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\Bigg].
$$

Notice that **the same scalar $s_i(\theta)$ multiplies all tokens in $y_i$**. The variability of individual token ratios no longer propagates into the update: extreme values on one token cannot over‑weight or under‑weight its neighbours.  
Because each response’s likelihood is an exponentially weighted product of many terms, length‑normalizing with $1/|y_i|$ keeps the scale of $s_i(\theta)$ tight (typically within a few‐hundredths), drastically reducing variance compared to per‑token ratios that can be unbounded.

---

### 3.  Intuition: matching unit of reward and unit of correction

Rewards in language‑model RL are awarded to entire responses (e.g., pass@1, BLEU, verifier score).  
Applying an off‑policy correction that matches this granularity—namely at sequence level—achieves two things:

1. **Correctness of importance sampling**:  
   Importance sampling restores expectations only when the weight is defined over the full sample space from which data were drawn. A token lives inside the distribution of *the next token given prefix*, not under the joint distribution over an entire response; using its own weight thus breaks this principle.

2. **Stability against expert volatility**:  
   For Mixture‑of‑Experts models, different policies activate different experts for each token.  
   The per‑token ratio suddenly flips whenever routing changes, amplifying noise.  
   The sequence ratio depends only on end‐to‐end likelihood; routing changes across a sequence typically smooth out in its product and do not explode outside reasonable bounds.

These two properties explain why GSPO routinely tolerates higher clipping fractions yet remains efficient: it discards entire noisy responses but never lets highly variable individual contributions poison updates.

---

### 4.  Practical evidence

* **Clipping fraction** – Figure 3 shows that GSPO clips *more* tokens than GRPO while still training faster and more stably (Section 4.2). This confirms that per‑token “good” updates are being lost for no benefit, whereas GSPO’s discarded tokens were already unreliable due to noisy $w_{i,t}$.

* **MoE training** – Section 4.3 demonstrates that GRPO requires Routing Replay to hold onto old experts; GSPO works without it because sequence likelihoods stay coherent even when routing shuffles (Fig. 4).

* **Hardware friendliness** – GSPO only needs sequence probabilities from an inference engine (Section 5), whereas GRPO must recompute token probabilities from scratch.

---

### 5.  Bottom line

GSPO’s avoidance of high variance stems primarily from **eliminating unequal per‑token weighting** and replacing it with a single sequence-level importance scalar that is length‑normed and bounded. This aligns importance sampling with reward granularity, removes dependence on volatile token dynamics (especially in MoE models), and yields stable gradients that converge even under massive batch sizes and long sequences.

So while simply “not weighting tokens” certainly helps, it is precisely because those weights would otherwise be wildly uneven—and because rewards themselves are at sequence granularity—that removing them guarantees more stable learning for large language models.
