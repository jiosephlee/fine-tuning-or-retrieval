\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### $s_{i,t}(	heta)$ as defined in Eq.(12) contains an `sg` operation – what exactly is its purpose?
Question:
`sg` denotes stop‐gradient; thus $rac{	heta(y_{i,t}ig|x,y_{i,<t})}{sg[	heta(y_{i,t}ig|x,y_{i,<t})]}$ evaluates numerically to 1 but contributes no gradient flow. Why do we need this sacrifice of gradient for implementation purposes? Does it simply prevent double counting of gradients from $
abla_	heta s_i$, or does it serve another role?
Answer:
In the GSPO‑token objective the ratio  
\[
s_{i,t}(\theta)=\underbrace{\mathrm{sg}\!\bigl[s_i(\theta)\bigr]}_{\text{numerical value only}}
   \cdot 
   \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
        {\mathrm{sg}\!\bigl[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\bigr]}
\tag{12}
\]
is a *hybrid* of the sequence‑wise ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) and the token‑wise likelihood ratio.  
The first factor, $\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$, is a *detached* copy of \(s_i(\theta)\): it evaluates to the same number as \(s_i(\theta)\) but its gradient is set to zero.  
The second factor
\[
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     {\mathrm{sg}\!\bigl[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\bigr]}
\]
is exactly 1 in value but carries the gradient of
$\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$ (because the denominator is a detached constant).

Consequently every token inherits the *same* numerical weight as the whole sequence,
\(s_i(\theta)\), yet its gradient is modulated only by the local log‑probability of that token.
This construction has two intertwined purposes:

1. **Avoid double‑counting gradients**.  
   If we used the true token‑wise importance weight
   $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid \cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_{i,t}\mid \cdot)}$
   in addition to the sequence weight \(s_i(\theta)\), each token would be multiplied by two different ratios whose gradients would both flow.  
   The sequence factor already accounts for how much the *whole* response probability changes, so applying another token‑level ratio would over‑weight certain positions and introduce unnecessary variance.  
   By detaching the sequence quantity we guarantee that only a single, consistent weight enters the gradient.

2. **Keep user‑defined advantages per token**.  
   GSPO‑token allows users to give different advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ to different tokens (necessary for multi–turn RL or step‑wise credit assignment).  
   Because \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\) equals \(s_i(\theta)\) numerically, scaling each token with its own advantage does not alter the overall direction of the update, but it lets finer rewards influence which tokens are pushed more strongly.

Mathematically, substituting (12) into Eq.(10) and dropping clipping gives

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E} 
\, s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i
= \mathbb{E}
\, s_i(\theta)
  \Bigl[\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_t 
          \widehat A_{i,t}
          \nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}) \Bigr],
\]

exactly as derived in Eq.(14).
The `sg` operation therefore has no effect on the *value* of \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\); it simply prevents its scalar part from contributing a second gradient term that would otherwise duplicate and distort the update coming from the token probabilities.

In short, `sg` delivers a clean separation between **how much** a response should be weighted (the sequencewise normalised importance ratio) and **which** probability gradients should propagate through that weight (the per‑token logits). This design eliminates double counting while preserving fine‑grained advantage control—exactly what GSPO‑token needs to behave like its sequence‑level counterpart when all token advantages are equal, yet flexibly extendable otherwise.
