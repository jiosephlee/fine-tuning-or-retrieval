\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 4: # 4. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) – Core Idea

In the previous chapters we laid out the motivation for moving from token‑level updates to sequence‑level updates in reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models (LLMs). The present chapter formalises this intuition into **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**, a stable, efficient RL algorithm that replaces GRPO’s token‑wise importance sampling with a sequence‑wise formulation. We expose the mathematical structure of GSPO, describe how it constructs its objective, derives the normalised sequence‑likelihood ratio, and explains why length normalisation is vital. We also clarify how advantages are computed over a *group* of responses to the same query, and why clipping is performed at the sequence level rather than token level.

---

## 4.1 Sequence Likelihood Ratio Definition and Length Normalisation

The fundamental building block of any policy‑gradient RL algorithm is an **importance weight** that rescales gradient estimates from an *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\!\text{old}}}\) to an *updated* policy \(\pi_{\theta}\). In GRPO the weight was defined at the token level:
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta) \;=\; \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
                     {\pi_{\theta_{\!\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})},
\]
where \(y_{i,t}\) denotes the token emitted at step \(t\) in the \(i^{\text{th}}\) response to query \(x\).
GSPO discards this per‑token ratio in favour of a *sequence‐wise* likelihood ratio:
\[
s_i(\theta) \;\triangleq\;
    \left( \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\!\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)} \right)^{\!1/|y_i|}.
    \tag{4.1}
\]
Here \(y_i = (y_{i,1}, \dotsc, y_{i,|y_i|})\) is a complete response,
and
\[
\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)
    = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
        \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})
\]
is its autoregressive likelihood.

Equation (4.1) may be rewritten by pulling the logarithm inside:
\[
s_i(\theta)
    = \exp \!\Bigg(
        \frac{1}{|y_i|} 
        \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
            \log 
            \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}
                 {\pi_{\theta_{\!\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}
      \Bigg).
    \tag{4.2}
\]
This expression makes two key observations immediately obvious:

1. **Sequence Level** – All tokens belonging to a single response are bundled together; no individual token receives a distinct weight.
2. **Length Normalisation** – The factor \(1/|y_i|\) ensures that responses of varying length contribute comparably to the objective; otherwise longer sequences would dominate simply because their raw likelihood product tends to be smaller.

Length normalisation mitigates three practical issues:

- **Variance Reduction** – A product over many tokens tends to grow or shrink exponentially fast, making raw ratios unstable.
- **Clipping Uniformity** – Without normalisation each sequence would require a different clipping range; with normalisation all sequences can share a common clipping interval.
- **Reward Alignment** – The reward function assigns value only to entire responses; by normalising we keep importance ratios commensurate with the granularity of rewards.

Thus, \(s_i(\theta)\) serves as a clean surrogate for how much the updated policy differs from the old policy on an *entire* response.

## 4.2 Group Advantage Calculation Across Multiple Responses

In RL training of LLMs one commonly generates multiple responses per query—this collection is called a **group** and denoted
\(G=\{y_1,\ldots,y_G\}\). The group permits an internal estimate of relative quality:
responses are judged against each other rather than against an external value network.
GSPO adopts the following advantage estimator for each response:

\[
\widehat{A}_i
   = 
   \frac{
          r(x,y_i)
          -
          \overline{r}_G(x)
        }
        {\sigma_G(x)} ,
   \quad
   i=1,\dotsc,G
   ,
   \tag{4.3}
\]
where:

- \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) is the verifier's reward assigned to any query–response pair.
- 
  \[
     \overline{r}_G(x)
      = 
      \frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G} r(x,y_j)
  \]
  is the empirical mean reward within group \(G\).
- 
  \[
     \sigma_G(x)
      = 
      \sqrt{
           \frac{1}{G} 
           \sum_{j=1}^{G}
                (r(x,y_j)-\overline{r}_G(x))^2
         }
  }
  .
  \
  ( +\,10^{-8}\,\text{to avoid division by zero})
  .

The standardisation in Eq (4.3)—subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation—creates an advantage that has zero mean and unit variance *within each group*. This confers two benefits:

- **Scale-Free Gradient** – Because every \(A_i\) lies roughly within a narrow band around zero, gradients do not blow up or vanish as they would if rewards were left unnormalised.
- **Balanced Update** – By treating higher rewards as positive advantages and lower rewards as negative ones relative to peers in the same group, GSPO implicitly implements self‑critical learning: it refocuses on improving relative quality even when absolute scores drift.

Notice that every token inside response \(y_i\) shares this same advantage value; yet Section 5 will show how GSPO‑token extends this idea when token‑level granularity is required.

## 4.3 Clipping Mechanism at the Sequence Level

One hallmark of proximal policy optimisation (PPO) and its variants is *clipping*: hard thresholds are imposed on importance weights to prevent updates that would move too far away from data points actually observed by the old policy. In GRPO this mechanism operates on token‑wise ratios:  
\(w_{i,t}(\theta)\in[\,1-\epsilon,\; 1+\epsilon\,]\).  
For GSPO we replace that with clipping on sequence‑level ratios:

\[
J_{\textup{GSPO}}(\theta)
=
\E_{\substack{x\sim D\\
                y_1,\ldots , y_G 
              } }
       {
       {1\over G}
          {
          {\color{gray}{   
          {\color{\showTokenColor}{min}}
          (
                   s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,
                   \
                   {\color{\showTokenColor}{
                      {\color[gray]{olive}{
                        clip}
                         }( s_i(\theta),\, 1-\varepsilon_l,\; 1+\varepsilon_r )
                     }\widehat A_i 
                  ]
          )}
         }
       } .
    }                                             % ---------- end expectation
    % ------------------------------------------------------------------------

Here two separate clipping intervals can be used for downward (\(\varepsilon_l>0?\)) and upward (\(\varepsilon_r>0?\)) directions; in practice one usually picks symmetric values e.g.,  
\( [\,3\times10^{-4},\, 4\times10^{-4}\,) ] ,  (see Sec 5).  

Why does clipping sequences make sense?

- **Off‑Policy Diffusion** – When we split rollouts into many mini‑batches for gradient updates (Section 3), each mini‑batch consists of responses sampled from an older policy but will be updated using a newer one. If we were to evaluate tokens individually with their own importance weights, any single token could generate a huge multiplier when its probability has changed drastically—this causes unstable gradients. By clipping entire sequences we bound such outliers uniformly across all tokens.
- **Unit Consistency** – Rewards are assigned per entire response; our objective must be sensitive only to deviations in whole‐sequence likelihoods, not fine‐grained token changes.
- **Variance Suppression** – When length normalisation keeps sequence ratios close to one on average, clipping only affects those rare sequences that have truly diverged far from their old probability mass.

Concretely, suppose a certain response received an extremely high reward but also had a wildly different likelihood under after‑update policy; its \(s_i(\theta)\) might exceed \(1+\varepsilon_r\). The clipped term truncates it at \(1+\varepsilon_r\,\widehat A_i\), thereby preventing this single example from dominating optimisation.

Because length normalisation places typical values of \(s_i(\theta)\) close to unity (\(|s_i(\theta)-1|\ll 0.01\,)\), clipping thresholds can use very tight ranges (\(10^{-4}\)), offering more precise control than token‑wise clipping which requires loose ranges (\(O(0.2)\)). This subtle but crucial difference explains why GSPO typically achieves better performance even with tighter clipping.

### Summary

We have delineated all mathematical components required to implement GSPO:

| Component | Definition | Purpose |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| Sequence Likelihood Ratio | \( s_i(\theta)= (\pi_\theta(y_i)/\pi_{\!\text{old}}(y_i))^{1/|y_i|} = e^{(1/|y_i|)\sum_t log... } \) | Off‑policy correction at response level |
| Group Advantage | Standardised reward relative to group mean & variance | Ensures balanced gradients per sample |
| Clipping Interval | `[ε_l , ε_r]` applied early on `min` | Bounds influence of outliers |

These ingredients together form a concise yet powerful objective that tackles stability issues seen in GRPO while preserving sparse expert activation in MoE models (Section 5). The rest of this text will delve deeper into gradient derivations, token‑level adaptations (GSPO-token), experimental validation, and practical considerations for large MoE architectures—each building upon these core ideas introduced today.
