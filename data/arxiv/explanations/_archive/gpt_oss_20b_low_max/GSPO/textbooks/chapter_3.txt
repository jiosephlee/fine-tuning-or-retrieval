\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 3: # 3. Word‑Level Versus Sequence‑Level Objectives

In reinforcement learning for language models, the objective function that drives policy updates must be carefully matched to the form in which the reward signal is observed. Traditionally, many large‑scale RL approaches have treated the language generation process as a succession of independent next‑token decisions and therefore optimized a *token‑level* objective. The Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm exemplifies this design: it computes per‑token importance ratios and applies the clipping mechanism on each token’s contribution to the gradient. However, GRPO’s token‑level treatment of rewards and importance weights leads to severe instability, particularly when scaling to gigantic models, long generations, or sparse architectures such as Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE).  

Below we dissect why token‑level importance ratios are misguided in GRPO, how this misapplication arises from a mismatch between the unit of reward and the unit of objective, and why aligning both at the *sequence* level—both for rewards and for importance weighting—provides a principled, stable, and efficient alternative.

## Misapplication of Importance Weights in GRPO

The key idea behind importance sampling is that one can estimate expectations under a target distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) by re‑weighting samples drawn from a behavior distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\). Formally,
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\,f(z)\right].
\]
Crucially, this identity relies on averaging over *many* independent draws \(z\) from the behavior policy; only then does the ratio \(\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\) make sense as a correction factor that biases each sample toward the target distribution.

In the context of language generation under an autoregressive policy \(\pi_\theta(y_t|x,y_{<t})\), GRPO applies importance weights at **each individual token**:
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]
Each weight is computed from a single draw of \(y_{i,t}\) from the old policy’s next‑token distribution. Consequently, instead of correcting *over many* samples, GRPO attempts to correct *per sample*. This inflated variance manifests in two ways:

1. **High‐variance gradient estimates.** Since each token’s weight can deviate widely depending on that single sample, gradients inherit large jitter. This jitter aggregates over long sequences (\(|y|\) tokens), magnifying instability.
2. **Clipping instability.** The PPO‐style clipping in GRPO keeps weights within \([1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]\) or alternately forces them into \([1-\varepsilon,\infty)\) for negative benefits. Because token weights are noisy and unbounded above when benefits are negative, clipping truncates large portions of the gradient signal or inflates small but noisy signals.

Empirical observations demonstrate that these effects can lead to irrecoverable “collapse” of the policy: after a few epochs the generated sequences become almost deterministic or unusable, and any attempt to recover performance by reverting checkpoints or retuning hyperparameters fails. The root cause is not merely poor choice of hyperparameters but rather a fundamental mismatch in how weight correction is applied.

## Principle of Aligning Unit of Reward with Unit of Objective

Rewards for language models arise from verifiers that evaluate *complete* responses \(y\), assigning
\(r(x,y)\in [0,1]\). By definition this reward is only defined once an entire sequence has been produced; no meaningful partial reward exists for an incomplete prefix. The notion of “advantage” used in GRPO respects this property by evaluating relative rewards *within groups*:
\[
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\_G r(x,\cdot)}{\operatorname{std}\_G r(x,\cdot)},
\]
which assigns **the same** advantage to every token \(y_{i,t}\) in a given sequence \(y_i\). Nonetheless, the accompanying importance weight still operates at **token level**, thereby violating coherence between reward granularity (sequence‐level) and corrective factor granularity (token‐level).

This misalignment leads directly to instabilities:

- If a very short sequence receives a high reward while other longer sequences receive low reward, its per‑token advantage will still be large because it is shared across fewer tokens; however its importance ratio may be severely inflated just because few tokens are involved.
- Conversely, long sequences get divided into many tokens; each token receives the same advantage but its per‑token importance ratio may be dampened simply because more tokens adjust it during backpropagation.

The net effect is divergent weighting across tokens within a single sequence and across different sequences—exactly what causes exploding or vanishing gradients in practice.

A natural remedy is therefore to match both components at the same abstraction level: define **sequence‐level** weights as
\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|},
\]
so that each token within \(y_i\) contributes **equally** to this global importance measure. The exponent \(1/|y_i|\) normalizes by length to keep \(s_i(\theta)\) within a comparable numerical scale across different responses.

Once an objective uses identical units for both advantage (fixed per response) and importance weight (fixed per response), it becomes far less sensitive to random fluctuations induced by individual token draws. This coherence forms the theoretical backbone of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO).

## Benefits of Sequence–Level Correction

Aligning rewards and clipping definitions at the sequence level yields multiple practical advantages that were empirically observed:

1. **Stabilized Training Dynamics.** Because each response’s contribution to gradient updates is governed by a single scalar weight rather than many noisy per‑token weights, variance shrinks dramatically. Even when handling extremely long generations (hundreds or thousands of tokens), gradients remain well behaved.

2. **Reduced Dependence on Explicit Value Models.** GSPO retains GRPO’s main benefit—the ability to avoid training a separate value estimator—yet replaces its biased per‑token weighting scheme with unbiased sequence‐level correction derived directly from importance sampling theory.

3. **Uniform Clipping Thresholds Across Lengths.** Token–level clipping ranges must typically differ depending on average sequence length; remarkably GSPO can employ a single pair of clip bounds \((1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon)\) for all sequences thanks to length normalization embedded in \(s_i(\theta)\).

4. **Compatibility with Sparse Mixture–of–Experts Models.** Token–wise importance ratios fluctuate heavily whenever expert routing changes between successive policies—a phenomenon known as expert‑activation volatility—which severely robs GRPO’s corrective power. Because GSPO ignores token likelihoods altogether when computing its weight \(s_i(\theta)\)—it only needs overall sequence likelihood—expert activation swings have negligible effect on gradients.

5. **Robustness to Precision Discrepancies Between Training and Inference Engines.** Token probabilities are highly sensitive to floating point precision; errors can propagate quickly through multi‐step log‑likelihood products even for moderate lengths (e.g., 512 tokens). In contrast, aggregating log probabilities into one scalar response likelihood dramatically attenuates such precision impacts because any small error contributes only marginally relative to overall accumulated log probability.

6. **Simplified Infrastructure Requirements.** Because GSPO never needs per-token routing information or deterministic replayed expert assignments (“Routing Replay”), it removes expensive memory overheads and communication synchronization steps required for MoE training with other algorithms.

In sum, correcting at sequence level preserves fidelity to both reinforcement learning theory (importance sampling) and practical constraints imposed by modern language models’ size and architecture parity handling strategies.

---

Through this exploration we have seen that adopting a token‑level objective while rewards are defined at sequence level introduces fundamental instabilities—rooted in high variance due to misapplied importance weighting—and leads inevitably to collapsed policies during scaling experiments. By enforcing coherence between reward granularity and objective granularity through full-sequence incentives—and consequently computing importance ratios as normalized sequence likelihood ratios—we obtain a stable, efficient RL algorithm knowing both theory and practice march hand‐in‐hand for large language learning systems..
