\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 1: # Foundations of Reinforcement Learning for Language Models

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become an indispensable training paradigm for large language models (LLMs). By framing language generation as a sequential decision‑making process, RL allows an LLM to iteratively improve its output against task‑specific metrics that are often hard to encode in supervised loss functions. This chapter builds the theoretical and practical groundwork that underlies modern RL algorithms for language generation, focusing on policy representation, reward design, and importance‑sampling techniques that enable stable and efficient learning. The discussion ends with an intuition for why sequence‑level optimization can be far more effective than token‑level objectives, a point that motivates the development of newer algorithms such as Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO).

---

## Policy Representation as Autoregressive Language Models

A language model is an autoregressive probability distribution over token sequences. Formally, let $\theta$ denote the parameters of a neural network that implements the model. Given a prompt or query $x$, the model defines a conditional distribution over responses $y=(y_1,\dots,y_{|y|})$ through the product rule:

\[
\pi_\theta(y \mid x)
  = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}),
\]

where $y_{<t}=(y_1,\dots,y_{t-1})$ denotes all previously generated tokens. In RL terminology we call $\pi_\theta$ a *policy*: it prescribes a probability for each next token conditioned on the current state (the query together with the prefix of the produced sequence). When we refer to “the policy”, we are always referring to this autoregressive distribution.

Every time a token is sampled from $\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t})$, the action space of the RL problem grows to size equal to the vocabulary. Thus, standard tabular RL tools are infeasible; instead we rely on gradient‑based policy optimization techniques that exploit the differentiability of $\pi_\theta$ with respect to $\theta$. This setting also makes importance sampling vital: because we often use samples generated from earlier versions of the policy in order to save computation or hardware constraints.

## Reward Signal Definition and Verification

In language generation tasks such as instruction following or code completion, raw supervision via supervised loss of next‑token prediction does not capture high‑level properties like factuality, coherence or task success. Consequently, external evaluators – called *verifiers* – are introduced. A verifier $r$ maps a query–response pair $(x,y)$ to a scalar reward:

\[
r(x,y) \in [0,1].
\]

The verifier may be an external model (e.g., ChatGPT), a set of heuristics (e.g., BLEU score), or human‐labelled judgments aggregated into a numeric score. The reward embodies how desirable a particular response is relative to other possible responses to the same query.

Reward signal design is itself an art:

* **Granularity**: Some tasks employ token‑wise rewards (like per‑word accuracy in grammatical correction), while others assign rewards only at sequence completion (such as Pass@1 for code generation).
* **Normalization**: Since absolute values differ across queries or datasets, it is common to normalize rewards within groups of responses to the same query – e.g., subtracting their mean and dividing by their standard deviation—so that resulting advantages have comparable magnitude.
* **Calibration**: Verifiers sometimes suffer from systematic bias; calibration procedures can mitigate shifts that would otherwise misguide policy updates.

Regardless of actual form, rewards must be non‑negative because they quantify user preferences; if negative values appear they are usually re‑scaled or shifted before entering policy gradients.

## Importance Sampling Fundamentals

When training a policy offline from previously collected trajectories—common when using batches of pre-generated rollouts—one needs to correct for distribution mismatch between old and new policies. Importance sampling offers exactly this correction.

Consider two distributions $\pi_{\text{tar}}$ (target – the new policy we wish to evaluate) and $\pi_{\text{beh}}$ (behavior – the old policy used for data collection). For any test function $f$, its expectation under $\pi_{\text{tar}}$ can be rewritten as

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)]
 = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\, f(z)\right].
\]

The ratio $\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}$ is called an *importance weight*. It reweights each sample drawn from the behavior policy so that its contribution reflects how likely it would have been under the target policy. A key property is that accurate estimation requires averaging over many samples from $\pi_{\text{beh}}$, because each weight corrects only a single draw.

In language modeling contexts:

* **What is $z$?** A *response* $y$ (a full token sequence).
* **What is $\pi_{\text{tar}}(y|x)$?** The likelihood under the current policy $\pi_\theta$.
* **What is $\pi_{\text{beh}}(y|x)$?** The likelihood under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ that generated $y$ during rollout.

Thus, an ideal importance weight at sequence level would be

\[
w(y;\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}.
\]

Notice how this ratio aggregates over all tokens in $y$. Applying it incorrectly—for instance by computing it per-token from separate draws—destroys its interpretation as a correction factor and introduces high variance.

## Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) Mechanics and Drawbacks

A widely-used RL algorithm in NLP settings is *Proximal Policy Optimization* (PPO), famed for its stability in continuous‑action domains but also employed in discrete token generation tasks.

With PPO one collects samples $(x,y)$ from an older policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. The central idea is *clipping*: before applying importance weights we restrict them to lie within $[1-\varepsilon,\, 1+\varepsilon]$, where $\varepsilon>0$ is typically small ($0.1-0.3$). This reduces variance and bounds how far each new update can deviate from its reference point.

The PPO objective with advantages estimated by a value network becomes

\[
\begin{aligned}
 \mathcal J_{\!\text{PPO}}(\theta)
  &=
   \mathbb E_{x\sim \mathcal D,\,
            y\sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\!(\cdot | x)}
   \!\Bigg[\;
     \frac 1{|y|}
            \sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
            \min
              \Bigl(
                 w_t(\theta )\, \widehat A_t,\,
                 \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(w_t(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon ,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat A_t
              \Bigr)
   \;\Bigg]
   ,
   \\[4pt]
 w_t(\theta)
  &=
   \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t | x , y_{<t})}
        {\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\!(y_t | x , y_{<t})}\!,
\\
  \widehat A_t &:= 
    V(x,y_{<t}) - R(x,y),
   % where V may be estimated by another value model
   .
\end{aligned}
\]

Several practical complications arise:

| Challenge | Explanation |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Value Model Burden** | An extra network roughly matching LLM size must be trained online; errors propagate directly into $\widehat A_t$. |
| **Long Sequence Instability** | Each token’s importance weight multiplies across length; small errors grow exponentially making gradients noise prone. |
| **Clipping Hyperparameter Tuning** | Different tasks require different $\varepsilon$, but there’s no universal rule; mis‑set ranges cause either overly conservative updates or runaway growth. |

Because PPO relies heavily on accurately estimated advantages and narrowly clipped importance ratios at every time step, it can suffer severe variance when scaling up model size or context length—a problem accentuated in modern LLMs where generation lengths reach thousands of tokens.

## Why Token–Level Objectives Can Fail in Large LLMs

When models grow larger and tasks become more complex—think full document generations or long code reasoning—the issues above magnify:

1. **Exponential Accumulation:** Tokenwise importance ratios appear as products across tokens in typical derivations of likelihood ratios; if any individual ratio deviates slightly from unity due to minor quantization errors or expert routing changes in MoE layers, cumulative products can explode or vanish.
2. **Sponsor Noise:** In Mixture-of-Experts architectures each response activates only sparse subsets of experts; after each gradient step those activations shift dramatically, changing per-token likelihoods drastically even when overall sequence likelihood remains stable.
3. **Downstream Sensitivity:** Slight differences in sequence probabilities translate into large discrepancies in clipped ranges; what one author calls “unrealistic” values for one layer becomes perfectly acceptable after sparsity-induced scaling changes.
4. **Memory Pressure:** Maintaining per-token log-likelihood tensors for backpropagation across thousands-of-token outputs tax memory bandwidth heavily.

Collectively these factors justify exploring *sequence*-level formulations where corrections leverage aggregate probability mass rather than individual token jumps—a core motivation behind Group Sequence Policy Optimization.
