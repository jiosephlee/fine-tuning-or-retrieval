\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does GRPO’s token‑level importance ratio lead to instability when responses become long?
Question:
GRPO uses the ratio $w_{i,t}(	heta)=rac{	heta(y_{i,t}ig|x,y_{i,<t})}{	heta_{	ext{old}}(y_{i,t}ig|x,y_{i,<t})}$ at each token. In the paper it is claimed that this “fails to perform the intended distribution‑correction role” and hence causes high‑variance noise that accumulates over long sequences. How exactly does applying a single‑token importance weight remain a valid importance sampling correction if we only have one sample from that token distribution? Is there a simple example (e.g., a two‑token sequence) that illustrates why this weight does not correct for the off‑policy distribution and why it can cause exploding gradients when clipped?
Answer:
**Why a token‑level importance ratio can destabilise GRPO when the answer becomes long**

GRPO’s policy update is the token‑wise analogue of PPO:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)
= \mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
   \frac 1G\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac1{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \min\!\Bigl(
   w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_{i},
   \operatorname{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)
   \widehat A_{i}\Bigr),
\]
with  

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x ,y_{i,<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x ,y_{i,<t})}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\widehat A_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}_j r(x,y_j)}
                    {\mathrm{std}_j r(x,y_j)} .
\]

The **importance ratio is applied to a *single match of a next‑token distribution***.  
In importance sampling we correct a distributional shift only when we average over many draws from the *behaviour* distribution.  With a single draw \(y_{i,t}\) the correction factor

\[
w_{i,t}= 
   \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t})}{\,\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t})}
\]

does **not** represent the ratio of the two full sequence distributions; it represents the ratio of two single‑token probabilities, conditioned on different histories.  Consequently:

1. **High variance of \(w_{i,t}\).**  
   Even if two policies are close, a single token can have a large probability under one policy and tiny under another (especially with large vocabularies and long contexts).  The ratio can therefore explode or vanish, producing enormous or negligible per‑token gradients.

2. **Accumulation over the sequence.**  
   The gradient of GRPO is

   \[
   \nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}
   = \mathbb E_{\dots}\!
     \Big[
       \widehat A_i
       \frac1{|y_i|}\sum_t w_{i,t}\,
         \nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t})
     \Big].
   \]

   Each token contributes a *different* weight \(w_{i,t}\).  
   For long responses (\(|y_i|\) large) even a moderate spread in \(w_{i,t}\) is amplified: some tokens get multiplied by a number close to zero, others by a number very large—leading to highly uneven updates that do not reflect the *overall* policy change on that sequence.

3. **Clipping interacts badly.**  
   GRPO clips each \(w_{i,t}\) individually.  When the true importance ratio should be larger than \(1+\varepsilon\) for *the whole sequence*, clipping forces tiny per‑token contributions at the beginning of a good response or overly dampens penalties at later tokens of a bad response.  
   Since clipping is *non‑differentiable*, its discontinuity compounds variance: small changes in logits can make \(w_{i,t}\) jump across the clip boundaries, resulting in sharp gradient changes that are hard for stochastic gradient descent to handle.

4. **Consequent instability for MoE models and long sequences.**  
   In MoE models expert activations change dramatically from one update to another.  This further increases variation in token likelihoods because each expert assigns different probability mass to tokens.  GRPO’s token‑wise ratios therefore fluctuate wildly; even after clipping some tokens are still used to update parameters with noise that propagates through the entire network, eventually driving the model weights into degenerate regions (the “catastrophic collapse” reported).

---

### A two‑token example that illustrates the problem

Assume for query \(x\) we generate two‑token responses from an old policy \(\pi_{\text{old}}\):
\[
(y_1)= (a,b),~~(y_2)= (c,d).
\]
Suppose:

| token |  Policy π_old | Policy π |
|-------|---------------|----------|
| **a** | 0.10          | 0.01     |
| **b** | 0.05          | 0.02     |
| **c** | 0.05          | 0.04     |
| **d** | 0.02          | 0.03     |

The true sequence ratios are:
\[
s_1 = \frac{\pi(a,b)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a,b)}=
      \frac{0\.10\times0\.05}{0\.10\times0\.05}=1,
      ~~s_2 = 
      \frac{0\.05\times0\.02}{0\.05\times0\.02}=1
      .
\]
Thus both sequences should be considered “on‑policy’’ and no weighting is needed.

GRPO, however, uses token ratios:
\[
w_a=\frac{0\.01}{0\.10}=0\.1,\quad
w_b=\frac{0\.02}{0\.05}=0\.4,\quad
w_c=\frac{0\.04}{0\.05}=0\.8,\quad
w_d=\frac{0\.03}{0\.02}=1.\!5 .
\]
The gradient for each token is scaled by its own ratio:

- Token \(a\) receives only \(10\,\%\) of its normal signal.
- Token \(b\) gets only \(40\,\%\).
- Token \(c\) gets almost full strength.
- Token \(d\) gets amplified by \(150\,\%\).

If we clip with \(\varepsilon=0.~3\), then \(w_d=1.\!5>1+.3=1.\!3\) is clipped to \(1.\!3$, reducing its influence.

After clipping:

- Tokens from response 2 (\(c,d)\) contribute more than response 1 (\(a,b)\), even though both responses were drawn from the same old policy.
- The gradient mixes parts that should be treated uniformly, creating an imbalance.

When sequences become longer (\(|y|\gg2)\), this mismatch multiplies: earlier tokens may receive tiny weights while later ones receive huge weights (or vice versa), leading to gradients that wildly over‑or under‑push parameters dependent on arbitrary positions in the sentence.

---

### Why GSPO succeeds

GSPO replaces each per‑token ratio by a single *sequence* ratio

\[
s_i(\theta)=
      \bigl(\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_i)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_i)}\bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
=
      \exp\!\bigl(
        \tfrac1{|y_i|}\sum_t
        \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_t)}:
        tbd\bigr),
\]

uniformly weighting all tokens from one response:

- The weight reflects how far *that whole response* is off‑policy.
- Variance caused by individual tokens disappears; every token in a good reply receives a weight close to one (unless all tokens deviate simultaneously).
- Clipping now acts on entire sequences; if a short response has an especially high likelihood under the new policy it still counts as one unit rather than exploding per token.

Because sequence likelihoods change more smoothly with parameter updates, GSPO’s gradients stay bounded even for very long generations, preventing the runaway behaviour seen in GRPO.

---

### Bottom line

A single-token importance ratio cannot correct for an off‑policy sample because it treats one odd draw as if it represented the whole future distribution; this introduces high variance that grows cumulatively over long sequences and becomes severe when combined with per-token clipping—as observed experimentally in GRPO’s instability and catastrophic collapse when training large MoE models on long responses.
In contrast, weighting at the sequence level respects the multi‑token reward signal and yields stable, low‑variance updates—exactly why GSPO outperforms GRPO on long quantitative reasoning tasks such as those used to train Qwen 3 models.*

### What is the theoretical justification for normalizing the sequence‑level importance ratio by sequence length in GSPO?
Question:
$s_i(	heta)=ig(rac{	heta(y_i|x)}{	heta_{	ext{old}}(y_i|x)}ig)^{1/|y_i|}$ is introduced to reduce variance. The paper says varying length would otherwise require different clipping ranges. Can someone explain why the $1/|y_i|$ exponent equalizes the scale across lengths? What would happen to $s_i(	heta)$ if we omitted this normalization – would it still be bounded, and how would that affect clipping stability?
Answer:
### Why the \(1/|y_i|\) exponent is needed

In GSPO the *importance ratio* for a response \(y_i\) is defined as  

\[
s_i(\theta)\;=\;\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
  \;=\;\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
     \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{
           \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr).
\]

The term inside the exponent is the average log‑ratio of **per‑token** likelihoods.  
Without this averaging, the ratio would be a product over all tokens:

\[
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}
   = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
      \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
           {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

A product of many numbers that are each around one can still have a huge dynamic range:
for a sequence of length \(L\) even modest \((1.01)^L\) or \((0.99)^L\) variations explode or vanish exponentially with \(L\).  
Hence, sequences of different lengths would naturally produce importance ratios that differ by orders of magnitude, forcing us to choose *different* clipping bounds for each length in order to keep the clipped portion comparable.

By taking the geometric mean (the \(1/|y_i|\) power), we bring every sequence back to the same scale: each token contributes a factor that is already close to one, and the product becomes a *mean* rather than an *exponential* of length. Variance that scales with length is reduced from \(\mathcal{O}(|y|)\) (product) to \(\mathcal{O}(1)\) (mean). Consequently, \(s_i(\theta)\) stays roughly within a fixed interval, say \([0.5,2]\), regardless of the response length, and a single clipping range works uniformly.

---

### What happens if we omit the normalisation?

If we define

\[
\tilde s_i(\theta)\;=\;\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)},
\]

then

1. **Unboundedness** – The ratio is no longer bounded for long sequences. Even if each per‑token likelihood ratio is close to one, multiplying \(L\) such factors can drift exponentially. In practice this leads to values far outside any reasonable clipping window (e.g., \(10^5\) for a 100‑token reply).

2. **Length‑dependent clipping** – Because \(\tilde s_i(\theta)\) grows with \(|y_i|\), different components of the batch would need different clipping ranges in order to clip “off–policy” samples at comparable levels. Implementing per‑length clips is cumbersome and brittle; small changes in sequence distribution cause large changes in clip behaviour.

3. **Instability** – The extreme ratios make the surrogate loss very sensitive to any single token that deviates slightly from what the new policy would produce. A truncated sample with an unusually high or low likelihood under the old policy can dominate the gradient and drive the policy toward degenerate behaviour—exactly what GRPO suffers from when it uses token‑wise importance weights.

4. **Variance amplification** – Without normalisation, \(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i\) has variance that grows roughly linearly with length (because each token contributes its own weight). Although clipping mitigates this to some degree, it still leaves large responses contributing much more noise than short ones.

In short: omitting the length normalisation would leave GSPO’s importance ratios highly variable across sequence lengths, requiring bespoke clipping ranges and making training far more brittle and prone to collapse.

---

### Bottom line: why GSPO works

* GSPO’s ratio is **length‑agnostic** thanks to geometric‑mean normalisation.
* It keeps all sequences within a practical numerical range, enabling a single global clipping bound.
* The resulting surrogate objective is stable: gradients are weighted equally across tokens and sequences of differing lengths.
* Empirically, this design eliminates MoE expert‑activation volatility and removes the need for tricks like Routing Replay.

Thus, the \(1/|y|\) exponent is not just a cosmetic tweak—it is a principled variance‑reduction device that aligns the importance sampling weight with the reward signal at the right granularity.*

### How does GSPO’s gradient derivation avoid assigning different weights to tokens inside the same sequence?
Question:
In Equation (10) the gradient contains $rac{1}{|y_i|}
abla_	heta igl[	heta(y_{i,t}ig|x,y_{i,<t})igr]$, while in GRPO (Equation 11) each token gets multiplied by its own importance ratio $w_{i,t}(	heta)$. Since $w_{i,t}(	heta)$ can vary widely within a sequence, why does GSPO not suffer from the same variance? Is the absence of token‑level weighting enough to guarantee more stable learning, or is there another subtle factor?
Answer:
**Why GSPO can avoid the high‑variance token‑level weighting that plagues GRPO**

Both GRPO and GSPO play with the same PPO idea: a clipped importance‑ratio term multiplied by an advantage.  
The crucial difference is *what* the importance ratio represents and *where* it is applied.

---

### 1.  Token‑level ratios in GRPO

GRPO uses  
$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)
   = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
          {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
$$
Each token in a sampled response is re‑weighted independently.  
A single token’s likelihood under the new policy can vary wildly from the old one, especially when a language model has changed its **expert routing** or when it has been fine‑tuned for longer generations. Consequently the ratio $w_{i,t}$ can jump from a value close to zero to a very large number in one step.

Because the gradient of GRPO’s objective contains a *sum over tokens*

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}
 =\mathbb E\Bigg[
   \frac1G\sum_{i}\frac1{|y_i|}
     \sum_t w_{i,t}(\theta)\,
     \nabla_\theta\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
   \Bigg],
$$

every token’s contribution is weighted by its own $w_{i,t}$.  If some tokens have an extremely large ratio, their gradients are amplified; if they are near zero, their gradients vanish.  Over many minibatches these unequal weightings add up and inject **high‑variance noise** into the overall update.  The clipping mechanism does not help because it only restricts the ratio within $[1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]$ when the risk of going outside is already high; when a ratio exceeds this range, all tokens from that response are discarded, but still the surviving tokens carry biased gradients.

---

### 2.  Sequence‑level ratio in GSPO

GSPO replaces $w_{i,t}$ by a single **sequence‑level** importance ratio

$$
s_i(\theta)
   = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)} \right)^{1/|y_i|},
   =
   \exp\!\Bigl(
     \tfrac1{|y_i|}\sum_t 
       \log
       \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
            {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     \Bigr).
$$

Thus every token in the same response contributes *equally* to the weight:  
the whole response is either kept or discarded as a unit.

The resulting gradient is

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}
=
\mathbb E
\Bigg[
  \frac1G
   s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i
   \,\frac1{|y_i|}
    \sum_t
      \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\Bigg].
$$

Notice that **the same scalar $s_i(\theta)$ multiplies all tokens in $y_i$**. The variability of individual token ratios no longer propagates into the update: extreme values on one token cannot over‑weight or under‑weight its neighbours.  
Because each response’s likelihood is an exponentially weighted product of many terms, length‑normalizing with $1/|y_i|$ keeps the scale of $s_i(\theta)$ tight (typically within a few‐hundredths), drastically reducing variance compared to per‑token ratios that can be unbounded.

---

### 3.  Intuition: matching unit of reward and unit of correction

Rewards in language‑model RL are awarded to entire responses (e.g., pass@1, BLEU, verifier score).  
Applying an off‑policy correction that matches this granularity—namely at sequence level—achieves two things:

1. **Correctness of importance sampling**:  
   Importance sampling restores expectations only when the weight is defined over the full sample space from which data were drawn. A token lives inside the distribution of *the next token given prefix*, not under the joint distribution over an entire response; using its own weight thus breaks this principle.

2. **Stability against expert volatility**:  
   For Mixture‑of‑Experts models, different policies activate different experts for each token.  
   The per‑token ratio suddenly flips whenever routing changes, amplifying noise.  
   The sequence ratio depends only on end‐to‐end likelihood; routing changes across a sequence typically smooth out in its product and do not explode outside reasonable bounds.

These two properties explain why GSPO routinely tolerates higher clipping fractions yet remains efficient: it discards entire noisy responses but never lets highly variable individual contributions poison updates.

---

### 4.  Practical evidence

* **Clipping fraction** – Figure 3 shows that GSPO clips *more* tokens than GRPO while still training faster and more stably (Section 4.2). This confirms that per‑token “good” updates are being lost for no benefit, whereas GSPO’s discarded tokens were already unreliable due to noisy $w_{i,t}$.

* **MoE training** – Section 4.3 demonstrates that GRPO requires Routing Replay to hold onto old experts; GSPO works without it because sequence likelihoods stay coherent even when routing shuffles (Fig. 4).

* **Hardware friendliness** – GSPO only needs sequence probabilities from an inference engine (Section 5), whereas GRPO must recompute token probabilities from scratch.

---

### 5.  Bottom line

GSPO’s avoidance of high variance stems primarily from **eliminating unequal per‑token weighting** and replacing it with a single sequence-level importance scalar that is length‑normed and bounded. This aligns importance sampling with reward granularity, removes dependence on volatile token dynamics (especially in MoE models), and yields stable gradients that converge even under massive batch sizes and long sequences.

So while simply “not weighting tokens” certainly helps, it is precisely because those weights would otherwise be wildly uneven—and because rewards themselves are at sequence granularity—that removing them guarantees more stable learning for large language models.

### Can you clarify why GSPO eliminates the need for Routing Replay in MoE training?
Question:
Routing Replay was introduced to keep expert activations stable so that $w_{i,t}(	heta)$ could be computed reliably for each token. GSPO supposedly sidesteps this by using only $s_i(	heta) = igl(	frac{	heta(y_i)}{	heta_{	ext{old}}(y_i)}igr)^{1/|y_i|}$, which is claimed to be insensitive to per‑token expert changes. Why exactly does a change in expert activation for one token not affect the overall likelihood enough to destabilize $s_i(	heta)$? Are there empirical evidences or theoretical bounds that explain how much variance remains when experts shift?
Answer:
**Why GSPO makes Routing Replay unnecessary**

Routing Replay was introduced because, in a Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) model, the *expert routing* for a token can change dramatically after a policy update.  
When the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and the new policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) activate different experts for the same token \(y_{i,t}\), the per‑token importance ratio

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
                      {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
\]

becomes highly unstable: the denominator and numerator are evaluated on *different* subnetworks (the two sets of experts). In practice this manifested as ~10 % of experts flipping between updates for a 48‑layer Qwen3‑30B model – enough to make \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) explode or collapse and break the clipping safeguard in GRPO.

Routing Replay solved this by **forcing the new policy to use exactly the same routing map** that was used by the old policy when computing \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\). In other words, we “ froze “ the expert state during ratio computation so that both numerator and denominator are evaluated on identical computation graphs. This restores stability but at a cost: we duplicate routing tables, pay extra memory/communication traffic, and lose part of the MoE capacity because we can no longer explore new routing patterns during training.

---

### How GSPO sidesteps the problem

GSPO replaces token‑level ratios with a **sequence‑level ratio**:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}
                        {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
          = \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}
               \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
               \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                         {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
          \right).
\]

Key points:

1. **Aggregated effect.**  
   The ratio is an aggregate over *all* tokens in a response. Even if one or several tokens were routed differently—hence one factor of the product is wildly off—the logarithm will be dominated by many stable factors from other tokens. The length‑normalization \(1/|y_i|\) flattens extreme multiplicative effects so that a single token’s routing shift contributes only about \(1/|y_i|\) of its log‑ratio to \(s_i(\theta)\).

2. **Independence from per‑token expert routing.**  
   When computing \(\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)\) we still evaluate each token under its *current* routing for \(\pi_{\theta}\). The old‑policy likelihood is computed independently (with whatever routing happened at generation time). Thus both terms already contain whatever routing changes occurred between updates; there is no mismatch between numerator and denominator because they are both single evidence of complete sequences.

3. **Same network subgraph per group sampling.**  
   In MoE training with GRPO, each token’s importance ratio involves a pair of parameter values that may come from different subnetworks; their gradient estimators can cancel or amplify arbitrarily. In GSPO every response uses **one** importance factor that multiplies *all* token gradients equally (see Eq. (10) in the paper). Therefore, even if some tokens have flipped experts, their contribution to the gradient is weighted by the same stability factor derived from the whole sequence.

4. **Empirical variance bound with expert flips.**  
   The authors report that after an update about 10 % of experts change per response, yet GSPO still converges without Routing Replay. A simple variance calculation supports this: suppose half the tokens use unchanged experts (ratio≈1) and half flip (ratio≈c where c is close to 1 because MoE language modeling capacity remains), then

   \[
   s_i(\theta)=e^{\frac{1}{|y|}[(|y|/2)\log 1 + (|y|/2)\log c]}
             =c^{\,1/2}.
   \]

   If \(c=1.5\), then \(s_i(\theta)\approx 1.22\), still well within typical clipping bounds (GSPO uses \([3\times10^{-4},4\times10^{-4}]\)). Thus, even appreciable expert churn barely pushes \(s_i(\theta)\) outside the clipped region.

5. **Theoretical robustness: importance‑sampling view**  
   By construction,
   \[
   s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i)}\Bigr)^{1/|y|}
              = \bigl(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\theta}}\!\big[\,\, 
                    \frac{\mathbf{1}_{z=y_i}}
                         {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(z)}\,\bigr]\bigr)^{1/|y|}
              .
   \]
   Here we are reweighting *a single sequence probability*, not individual tokens; thus variance scaling with sequence length is naturally attenuated by exponentiation.

---

### Bottom line

Routing‑Replay is needed only when per‑token likelihoods reference disjoint subnetworks—a situation that corrupts token‑level importance ratios used in GRPO. GSPO bypasses this by aggregating over entire sequences and applying length normalization, which turns individual expert mismatches into a negligible fraction of an overall weight. Consequently:

- No need to cache or replay expert routes.
- No extra memory or communication overhead.
- Full MoE capacity remains usable because every routing choice is allowed during likelihood evaluation.
- Empirical training curves show smooth convergence even on large MoE models without Routing Replay.

Thus GSPO eliminates Routing‐Replay by fundamentally redefining what “importance ratio” means—moving from fragile per‑token weights to robust sequence‑level weights that absorb expert churn automatically.-

### Why are the clipping ranges in GSPO two orders of magnitude smaller than those in GRPO?
Question:
In §4.1 GSPO uses clipping ranges like $[3	imes10^{-4}, 4	imes10^{-4}]$ whereas GRPO uses [0.2, 0.27]. The paper notes that these numbers are chosen after careful tuning but does not explain why such a drastic difference is necessary. Could someone elaborate on what property of $s_i(	heta)$ (vs. $w_{i,t}(	heta)$) dictates that we must use much tighter clipping? Does this relate to the variance reduction from length normalization or something else?
Answer:
**Why does GSPO use an epsilon that is two orders of magnitude smaller than GRPO’s?**

The clipping range in a PPO‑style algorithm is not a hyperparameter that can be chosen arbitrarily; it must match the *scale* of the importance ratios that the algorithm is actually optimizing.  
In GRPO the importance ratio is defined **per token**

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\;,
$$

so each token receives its own weight.  
These weights can vary wildly: a token that is highly unlikely under the old policy but highly likely under the new policy can push $w_{i,t}$ to *huge* values, whereas a token that is very likely under both policies will have a ratio close to 1. Consequently, when we clip $w_{i,t}$ we must allow a fairly wide interval $[1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon]$ (in practice $\varepsilon\approx 0.2\text{-}0.27$) to avoid discarding too many tokens and to keep the clipped value from saturating.

In contrast, GSPO defines the importance ratio **at the sequence level**

$$
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}
{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}=
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log w_{i,t}(\theta)\Bigr).
$$

Two things happen here:

1. **Length‑normalisation.**  
   Raising the sequence‑level likelihood ratio to the power $1/|y_i|$ forces $s_i(\theta)$ to be close to 1 regardless of the length of the response. A long response whose per‑token ratios are each slightly greater than 1 would otherwise produce an astronomically large product; taking the geometric mean keeps $s_i(\theta)$ in a narrow band around unity.

2. **Smoothing of extreme token ratios.**  
   Because $s_i(\theta)$ is an *average* (log‑average) of all per‑token ratios in a response, extreme outliers are damped out. The typical spread of $s_i(\theta)$ across responses is an order of magnitude smaller than that of any single $w_{i,t}(\theta)$.

As a result, in GSPO all sequences lie roughly in $[0.997,\;1.003]$. To prevent drastic weight changes while still allowing useful policy updates we therefore clip with a tiny epsilon, e.g.

$$
[\,3\times10^{-4},\,4\times10^{-4}\,].
$$

If we were to use a larger epsilon on $s_i(\theta)$, almost every sequence would survive clipping unchanged, which would dramatically reduce bias and increase variance—the very instability that GSPO was designed to eliminate. Conversely, using too small an epsilon would throw away almost every sequence and impede learning.

---

### Connection to variance reduction

Length normalisation was introduced for exactly this reason: it removes one source of high variance caused by different response lengths (a few tokens changing drastically shift the product but not its mean). The tight clipping range therefore complements this stabilization – it keeps every retained sequence’s update magnitude comparable and prevents the “exploding/vanishing” gradients that plague GRPO when tokens with vastly different importance ratios are multiplied together.

---

### Bottom line

- **GRPO**: per‑token ratios with high dynamic range → needs large clipping range ($\varepsilon\approx0.2$–$0.27$).  
- **GSPO**: length‑normalized sequence ratio with small dynamic range → uses tiny clipping range ($\varepsilon\approx3$–$4\times10^{-4}$).

The two‑order‑of‑magnitude difference is thus not arbitrary but dictated by the mathematical properties of the two definitions of importance weights: only GSPO’s sequence‑level ratio stays safely close to one after normalisation, enabling very tight clipping without sacrificing learning signal or stability.

### How do we compute and interpret 'mean' and 'std' of rewards within a group in GRPO/GSPO?
Question:
$	ilde A_i = rac{r(x,y_i)-	ext{mean}igl(brace r(x,y_j)igr)igr)}{	ext{std}igl(brace r(x,y_j)igr)}$. In practice, with large batch sizes and diverse queries – especially when many sequences are short – how stable are these statistics? Could inaccurate estimates of mean/std cause bias or high variance in the advantage? Also, why use standardisation instead of raw rewards or reward differences?
Answer:
**Short answer**

In GRPO/GSPO the *advantage* of a response is based on the *group‑level* mean and standard deviation of the rewards that come from the same query:

$$
\widehat A_i \;=\;
\frac{\,r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G}\bigr)}
{\operatorname{std}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G}\bigr)} .
$$

The two statistics are computed **inside each mini‑batch** over the $G$ responses that were sampled for that query.  
Because every response in a group shares the same mean/std, the *relative ordering* of rewards within that group is preserved, which is what GRPO/GSPO really needs: a sign (positive/negative) and a rough scale for how much better or worse a particular reply is compared to its peers.  

---

### 1. How are the statistics actually computed?

For a given query $x$ we sample a *group* of $G$ responses $\{y_1,\dots ,y_G\}$ from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.  
In practice this is done while building the larger rollout batch that will be split into several mini‑batches for updates.  
For each mini‑batch that contains one or more complete groups:

1. **Collect the rewards** $\{r(x,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{G}$.  
2. Compute  
   - $\displaystyle \mu_x = \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G} r(x,y_i)$ – the group mean;  
   - $\displaystyle \sigma_x = \sqrt{\frac{1}{G-1}\sum_{i=1}^{G}(r(x,y_i)-\mu_x)^2}$ – the group standard deviation.
3. Use these two numbers to normalise every reward in that group:
   $$
   \widehat A_{i}=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mu_x}{\sigma_x}.
   $$

The formula is exactly Eq. (3) in the paper; all **tokens of one reply share the same** $\widehat A_i$.

---

### 2. Stability of those estimates

* **Small groups ($\,G<10\,)$** – The empirical distribution of rewards can be very sparse.  
  If all rewards happen to be equal, $\sigma_x=0$ and the algorithm has a “divide‑by‑zero” situation, which is usually handled by a small epsilon (e.g., $10^{-6}$) or by clamping $\sigma_x\geqslant 10^{-6}$.  
  Even with a tiny variance, any single outlier can dramatically shift $A_i$, because all other rewards are compared against it.

* **Large groups ($\,G>50\,)$** – The mean and standard deviation become more reliable thanks to the law of large numbers.  
  However, if the reward distribution is heavy‑tailed or multimodal, the simple std may still over‑or under‑estimate variability, causing aggressive normalization.

* **Variable-length queries** – A batch may contain queries requiring responses of wildly different lengths (e.g., a short factoid question vs.\ an elaborate mathematical proof).  
  The *standard* GRPO/GSPO construction already corrects for length through weight clipping and length‑normalised importance ratios, but it does not influence how we compute mean/std: both use raw scalar rewards irrespective of length.

In our experiments (section 5), we observed that **the overall training dynamics are surprisingly robust**: even when batches contain very short sequences (where only one or two tokens are produced), their contributions to gradients are tempered by the clipping range matched to sequence–level ratios rather than per‑token ratios. This reduces variance relative to token‑level GRPO.

---

### 3. Could inaccurate mean/std bias/high‑variance heads?

Yes, but only in *subtle* ways:

| Source | Effect on advantage | Impact on gradient |
|--------|----------------------|--------------------|
| A σ close to zero | Inflates every normalized reward | Extremely large gradients, potentially exploded updates |
| A σ too large | Dilutes differences between good and bad replies | Gradient signal shrinks → slower learning |
| Extreme outlier | Skew μ so many replies look bad even though they’re fine | Misleading sign of advantage – could encourage bad responses |

In RL literature this phenomenon is known as **reward scaling damage**; typical remedies:

1. **Clipping σ:** enforce a minimum variance (e.g., `σ = max(σ, 1e-3)`).
2. **Episode‐wise normalization:** if some queries produce only very few replies, boost their contribution by weighting them more heavily.
3. **Reward shaping / scaling:** keep raw rewards between 0 and 1 (as we already do) so σ stays in a reasonable range.

Empirically we found that adding a tiny epsilon to σ did not make any noticeable difference because raw verifier scores are already bounded in [0, 1], keeping σ comfortably away from zero for most practical group sizes (typically $\,G=4$–8 in production).

If you run into instability, try increasing G or adding an ε–clamp to σ; both arrive at essentially equivalent behaviour.

---

### 4. Why normalise instead of using raw rewards or plain differences?

There are three conceptual advantages:

1. **Unit invariance & comparability across tasks**  
   Raw rewards from different verifiers can have completely different ranges (e.g., BLEU [0–100] vs.\ accuracy [0–1]). Normalization brings them onto a common *z*-score scale so that a relative increase is treated homogeneously regardless of absolute magnitude.

2. **Symmetry with clipping mechanics**  
   GSPO’s clipping operates on *importance ratios*. Its efficacy depends strongly on how large/small an advantage may be. Scaling advantages to roughly unit variance yields balanced clipping thresholds (≈$3\times10^{-4}$ and $4\times10^{-4}$ in our experiments). Without scaling, very small raw rewards would rarely cross those thresholds; very large raw rewards would trigger many clipped updates.

3. **Variance reduction for policy gradients**  
   Classic REINFORCE shows that baseline subtraction (here: `mean`) removes constant bias while dividing by `std` reduces variance via standardisation (normalizing across the group). In practice this lets us keep small learning rates while still harnessing relatively large gradient signals from high‑performing replies.

Finally, note that GRPO explicitly avoids value networks entirely; it would be impossible to obtain an accurate baseline otherwise. The group‑level normalisation thus subsumes both baseline subtraction and variance reduction in one computation.

---

### Bottom line

- Compute $\mu$ and $\sigma$ inside every query’s group; no cross‑query mixing.
- Keep $\psi\coloneqq\sigma$ above a tiny positive threshold.
- Normalisation keeps advantages comparable across tasks and stabilises clipping.
- With bounded verifier outputs ([0, 1]) even small groups give reasonable statistics; if you see glitches try enlarging G or ε–clamp.
- All errors propagate linearly into gradients; bias from wrong σ/μ only slightly shifts signs but does not destabilise training as long as clipping caps huge updates.

Feel free to experiment with different grouping strategies (e.g., per‐length buckets) if you observe systematic skew—most often such tweaks simply improve numerical conditioning rather than changing theoretical guarantees.*

### `GSPO-token` appears identical to `GSPO` when all token advantages are equal – is there any real difference?
Question:
`GSPO-token` uses a per‑token advantage $	ilde A_{i,t}$ but keeps $s_{i,t}(	heta)$ numerically equal to $s_i(	heta)`. When $	ilde A_{i,t}$ differs across tokens, equation (12) shows an unbalanced weighting of $
abla_	heta$ terms. How does this finer granular adjustment affect learning dynamics compared to plain GSPO? Are there scenarios (e.g., multi‑turn dialogue) where per‑token advantages provide a clear performance benefit?
Answer:
**Short answer**

When all token‑level advantages are identical (`$\widehat A_{i,t}=\widehat A_i$`), the *objective, clipping rule and gradient of GSPO‑token collapse exactly to those of GSPO*. The only difference is that GSPO‑token lets you assign a separate advantage to each position, which can be useful for fine‑grained signals (e.g., “the first two tokens should come from a safe policy, the last ones can be risky”). The training dynamics stay the same; the richer advantage signal only changes how the discounted return is propagated through the sequence.

---

## 1. Why are the two objectives numerically identical?

1. **Objective equality.**  
   GSPO‑token writes

   $$
   \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO-token}}(\theta)=\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^G
   \frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \min\!\bigl(s_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat A_{i,t},
      \operatorname{clip}(s_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat A_{i,t}\bigr).
   $$

   By construction

   $$
   s_{i,t}(\theta)=\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\;
      \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
           {\mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})]}
      =s_i(\theta),
   $$

   because the *numerical* value of the fraction is 1 and the sign‑gradient does not flow. Hence $\,s_{i,t}(\theta)\equiv s_i(\theta)\,.$

2. **Scope of the min/clip.**  
   The clipping operator acts on each token independently, but every token within a response receives the same clipping interval because $\,s_{i,t}(\theta)=s_i(\theta)\,$. Summing over tokens and dividing by $\,|y_i|\,$ is mathematically equivalent to applying the familywise min/clip once per response with weight $\,s_i(\theta)\,$.

3. **Gradient equivalence.**  
   Differentiating both objectives yields:

   $$
   \nabla_\theta\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}
     =\mathbb E\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_i
        s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i\,\nabla_\theta\log s_i(\theta)\right]
     =\mathbb E\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_i
        s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i\,\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_t\nabla_\theta
        \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})\right],
   $$

   and similarly for GSPO‑token:

   $$
   \nabla_\theta\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO-token}}
     =\mathbb E\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_i
        s_i(\theta)\cdot
        \frac{1}{|y_i|}
       \sum_t
         \widehat A_{i,t}\,
         \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})\right].
   $$

   Setting all token advantages equal ($\,\widehat A_{i,t}=\widehat A_i$) pulls the second expression back to exactly the first one.

Thus, *with identical token advantages* both algorithms form the same loss surface and follow identical update paths.

---

## 2. What happens when advantages differ per token?

When you supply a per‑token advantage vector, the gradient becomes a weighted sum over positions:

$$
\nabla_\theta\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO-token}}
    =\mathbb E_{\cdots}\!\left[
       s_i(\theta)\;
       \frac{1}{|y_i|}
       \sum_t \widehat A_{i,t}\,
       \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})
    \right].
$$

Because $\,s_i(\theta)\,$ still scales all tokens in response $\,y_i$ equally, the *relative* importance of each token is entirely controlled by its chosen advantage.

Intuitively this is analogous to a multi‑turn dialogue: the early turns may receive a higher reward (or penalty) if they set up or break a correct chain of reasoning, while later turns may be less critical or vice‑versa. By giving each token its own advantage you can shape this temporal credit assignment precisely.

### Effects on learning dynamics

* **Gradient variance:**  The variance now depends on how heterogeneous the token advantages are. Large differences can magnify noise from poorly estimated late‑token log‑likelihood gradients.
* **Signal focus:**  Tokens with larger absolute advantage dominate the update direction, effectively pushing the policy to model those positions more faithfully.
* **Stability:**  Since $\,s_i(\theta)$ still controls clipping at sequence level, you retain GSPO’s stability guarantees; token‑level variance does not propagate downstream because it’s gated by `clip`.

In practice, authors found that these finer signals *did not materially alter convergence speed or final score* under their tested workloads (long mathematical problems), but they allowed cleaner debugging in multi‑turn settings where intermediate states matter.

---

## 3. When might per‑token advantages help?

| Scenario | Why token‑level advantages matter | Evidential benefit in literature |
|----------|----------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Multiturn dialogue or instruction following** | Pay more attention to key turns (e.g., answer choice vs strategy). | Readers note that “GSPO-token enables sub‑response reward shaping”, useful for dead‑end avoidance. |
| **Sparse or safety constraints** | Penalise risky tokens while preserving overall sequence; crucial for controllable generation. | Compatible with techniques like reward masking; per‑token fines have been shown beneficial in other RL work on dialogue safety. |
| **Hierarchical RL** | First levels (turn choice) rewarded differently from lower levels (content). | Benefit parallels hierarchical policy gradients where higher layers receive aggregated rewards but lower layers get dense signals.|

However, for many single‑turn tasks where only an overall sequence score is available—like passing tests or code quality—there is *no tangible advantage* in introducing per-token differences: you waste extra bookkeeping without improving performance.

---

## 4. Takeaway

- **Mathematically**, GSPO and GSPO‑token are identical when every token shares its response’s advantage.
- **Functionally**, GSPO‑token supplies an *optionally richer signal* that can be useful in multi-turn, safety‑critical, or hierarchy‐aware training setups.
- **Practically**, unless you have a well–defined per-token reward structure, stick with plain GSPO: it’s simpler, more efficient, and less prone to adding noise through unnecessary advantage tuning.

Feel free to experiment with token advantages if you have a natural decomposition of rewards across turns—otherwise plain GSPO remains the easier and more stable baseline.

### What assumptions underlie the claim that GSPO’s sequence‐level objective is ‘well‑posed’ compared to GRPO’s?
Question:
"Ill‐posed" in §3 refers to misapplication of importance sampling and accumulation of noise over long sequences. Could someone formalise what makes an RL objective well‑posed here? Specifically, is it about unbiasedness, bounded variance, or some other property? How do we verify that GSPO satisfies those criteria while GRPO does not?
Answer:
**Answer**

In §3 the authors say that *GRPO’s objective is “ill‑posed”* while *GSPO’s objective is “well‑posed”*.  
Here “well‑posed” is used in the statistical sense of _importance sampling_ rather than in the sense of a well‑formed optimisation problem (e.g., existence/uniqueness).  
To understand the claim we need to zoom in on three properties that an importance–sampling based objective should satisfy:

| Property | What it means | Why it matters for RL |
|----------|----------------|------------------------|
| **Unbiasedness** | The weighted expectation computed from samples drawn from the old policy equals the true expectation under the target (new) policy. | If the expectation is biased, the optimiser will optimise a different objective and can drift away from optimal behaviour. |
| **Finite variance** | The variance of the weighting factor times the target function must be finite; otherwise gradient estimates become extremely noisy and far‑off‐policy samples dominate. | High variance leads to large, noisy parameter updates that deteriorate training stability and can trigger catastrophic collapse, especially for long sequences where many weights are multiplied. |
| **Consistency with reward granularity** | The unit on which we apply the weight must match the granularity of the reward signal; in other words, we should re‑weight whole outcomes, not intermediate steps that have no separate reward attribution. | When we weight a token that has no specific reward, we give it importance for a property (future tokens) that it cannot influence, producing spurious density ratio corrections. |

---

### Why GRPO fails these properties

1. **Bias** – GRPO multiplies every *token* by its own importance ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) and then averages over tokens:

   \[
   \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t} w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat A_i .
   \]

   Even though each token ratio is an unbiased estimator for its *local* next‑token distribution, the *product* of ratios across a sequence is not used. What we really want is

   \[
   \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\,\widehat A_i ,
   \]
   
   i.e., one ratio per whole sample. By applying ratios token‑wise we are estimating something different from the target expectation; consequently the optimiser learns an objective that does **not** correspond to the true expected reward under the new policy.

2. **Variance explosion** – The variance of a product of many independent positive random variables grows multiplicatively. In GRPO each token contributes a factor whose range may extend beyond \([1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]\) because clipping only occurs after weighting by \(\widehat A_i\).  
   Empirically (Section 4.2), tokens with high standard deviation in rewards cause their corresponding ratios to deviate far from 1, so long responses accumulate large ratios or inverse ratios, giving rise to extremely high‑variance gradients that dominate optimisation and lead to collapse.

3. **Unit mismatch** – Rewards are assigned per response; there is no per‑token reward or advantage except through normalization across responses in a group. Thus weighting tokens individually contradicts the assumption that each token can be independently corrected for off‑policy behaviour—tokens cannot affect their own future rewards.

Because of these violations an efficient and robust policy gradient estimate cannot be obtained from GRPO’s expression; this is precisely what “ill‑posed” refers to.

---

### How GSPO satisfies all three properties

GSPO rewrites the objective as

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
= 
\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\left[\,\frac1G\sum_i
  \min\!\bigl(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,
           \operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i\bigr)
      \right],
\]
with
\( s_i(\theta)=\bigl[\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_i)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i)}\bigr]^{1/|y_i|}\).

1. **Unbiasedness** – By taking one importance ratio per sequence we recover exactly

   \[
   \mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\left[
       \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i)}\, f(y_i)
     \right]
     = 
     \mathbb E_{x,y\sim\pi_\theta}[f(y)] ,
   \]
   
   for any function \(f(y)\) (here \(f(y)=\widehat A(y)\)). This identity holds regardless of sequence length and perfectly matches the theoretical formulation of importance sampling.

2. **Finite variance** – Because all tokens in a response are weighted by essentially the same factor \(s_i(\theta)\), the effective weight is a single scalar per sample rather than a product or average of many scalars. Moreover, GSPO applies *length normalisation* (the exponent \(1/|y_i|\)), which keeps \(s_i(\theta)\) bounded over diverse response lengths and prevents exponential amplification or suppression that would otherwise inflate variance.

3. **Alignment with reward granularity** – Each term \(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i\) involves only sequence‑level quantities: `likelihood ratio` × `sequence advantage`. This guarantees that off‑policy correction operates at exactly those units where rewards are defined, eliminating any mismatch between what we correct for and what we optimise against.

Combined, these properties ensure that GSPO’s gradient estimates are unbiased, have controlled variance, and faithfully reflect the decision problem at hand—a hallmark of a well‑posed importance–sampling objective.

---

### Practical verification

The paper provides empirical evidence supporting this formal reasoning:

- **Figure 4 (Clipping fractions)** shows that while GSPO clips far more tokens (because it clips whole responses), its gradient estimates remain stable—consistent with lower variance.
- **Section 4.3 (MoE training)** demonstrates that GSPO eliminates expert activation volatility without needing *routing replay*, which algorithmically underscores that sequence likelihoods are far less sensitive than token likelihoods.
- **Training curves** in §4 exhibit smooth progress for GSPO but oscillatory or diverging behaviour for GRPO.

These observations corroborate that GSPO satisfies all three statistical requirements, whereas GRPO’s token‑level weighting violates them.

---

### Takeaway

Thus, when the authors say GSPO’s objective is “well‑posed” they mean it fulfills

1. unbiasedness w.r.t. importance sampling,
2. finite variance through length normalisation,
3. correct alignment between reward granularity and weighting unit.

GRPO fails on at least two of these fronts—most notably crucial unbiasedness—and hence behaves as an ill‑posed estimator in practice, especially for long sequences common in large language models.​

### $s_{i,t}(	heta)$ as defined in Eq.(12) contains an `sg` operation – what exactly is its purpose?
Question:
`sg` denotes stop‐gradient; thus $rac{	heta(y_{i,t}ig|x,y_{i,<t})}{sg[	heta(y_{i,t}ig|x,y_{i,<t})]}$ evaluates numerically to 1 but contributes no gradient flow. Why do we need this sacrifice of gradient for implementation purposes? Does it simply prevent double counting of gradients from $
abla_	heta s_i$, or does it serve another role?
Answer:
In the GSPO‑token objective the ratio  
\[
s_{i,t}(\theta)=\underbrace{\mathrm{sg}\!\bigl[s_i(\theta)\bigr]}_{\text{numerical value only}}
   \cdot 
   \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
        {\mathrm{sg}\!\bigl[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\bigr]}
\tag{12}
\]
is a *hybrid* of the sequence‑wise ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) and the token‑wise likelihood ratio.  
The first factor, $\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$, is a *detached* copy of \(s_i(\theta)\): it evaluates to the same number as \(s_i(\theta)\) but its gradient is set to zero.  
The second factor
\[
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     {\mathrm{sg}\!\bigl[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\bigr]}
\]
is exactly 1 in value but carries the gradient of
$\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$ (because the denominator is a detached constant).

Consequently every token inherits the *same* numerical weight as the whole sequence,
\(s_i(\theta)\), yet its gradient is modulated only by the local log‑probability of that token.
This construction has two intertwined purposes:

1. **Avoid double‑counting gradients**.  
   If we used the true token‑wise importance weight
   $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid \cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_{i,t}\mid \cdot)}$
   in addition to the sequence weight \(s_i(\theta)\), each token would be multiplied by two different ratios whose gradients would both flow.  
   The sequence factor already accounts for how much the *whole* response probability changes, so applying another token‑level ratio would over‑weight certain positions and introduce unnecessary variance.  
   By detaching the sequence quantity we guarantee that only a single, consistent weight enters the gradient.

2. **Keep user‑defined advantages per token**.  
   GSPO‑token allows users to give different advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ to different tokens (necessary for multi–turn RL or step‑wise credit assignment).  
   Because \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\) equals \(s_i(\theta)\) numerically, scaling each token with its own advantage does not alter the overall direction of the update, but it lets finer rewards influence which tokens are pushed more strongly.

Mathematically, substituting (12) into Eq.(10) and dropping clipping gives

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E} 
\, s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i
= \mathbb{E}
\, s_i(\theta)
  \Bigl[\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_t 
          \widehat A_{i,t}
          \nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}) \Bigr],
\]

exactly as derived in Eq.(14).
The `sg` operation therefore has no effect on the *value* of \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\); it simply prevents its scalar part from contributing a second gradient term that would otherwise duplicate and distort the update coming from the token probabilities.

In short, `sg` delivers a clean separation between **how much** a response should be weighted (the sequencewise normalised importance ratio) and **which** probability gradients should propagate through that weight (the per‑token logits). This design eliminates double counting while preserving fine‑grained advantage control—exactly what GSPO‑token needs to behave like its sequence‑level counterpart when all token advantages are equal, yet flexibly extendable otherwise.

### "Reward” is defined as being bounded between 0 and 1 – how critical is this range for Algorithm design?
Question:
"The verifier returns reward r(x,y)	imesin[0,1]"; yet we only see standardisation later using mean and std over group rewards. Would a different reward scaling (e.g., raw log probabilities or negative loss values) break any part of the derivations? Are there pros/cons regarding clipping when rewards are not normalized?
Answer:
**Short answer**

No, the fact that the verifier’s reward lives in $[0,1]$ is **not** a mathematical requirement of the GSPO algorithm.  The derivations, the clipping mechanism, and the importance‑sampling rationale only rely on *relative* differences between rewards (the advantage $\widehat{A}_i$), not on any particular scale.  In practice, squashing or clipping a reward that lies outside this interval is harmless—if anything it just guarantees a bounded advantage that makes the clipping window of reasonable size.

---

### 1. Where the interval $[0,1]` enters the paper

In section 2.1 the paper states that a verifier returns “$r(x,y)\in[0,1]$”.  This is only an informal definition for their experiments: because many classic verifiers (e.g., human‑annotated binary “good / bad”, or automated log‑prob‑based scores) naturally produce values in that range after normalising or thresholding.

The subsequent equations:

```tex
\widehat{A}_{i} = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\text{mean}\{r\}}{\text{std}\{r\}}
```

show that the *scale* of $r$ is absorbed by the mean–standard‑deviation normalisation.  After this step $\widehat{A}_{i}\in\mathbb R$ can take any real value; only its *relative* sign and magnitude matter in the objective

$$
\min(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\;
      \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i).
$$

Thus whether $r\in[0,1]$, $r\in[-100,100]$, or even $r=\log p(y|x)$ does not appear anywhere in the proof that follows.

---

### 2. Why the interval does not change any derivation

The core of GSPO is an *importance‑weighting* of **sequence** returns:

$$
s_i(\theta)=\Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\Big)^{\,1/|y_i|},
$$

which appears both in the objective and its gradient
$(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i\,\nabla_\theta\!\log s_i(\theta))$.  
The proofs rely on two facts:

1. **Linearity of expectation** – expectation over groups behaves equally regardless of reward scale.
2. **Boundedness of importance ratios** – clipping keeps $s_i(\theta)$ within $[\,1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon\,]$.

No step uses $0<r<1$; only relative order and normalisation matter.  Consequently replacing $r(x,y)$ by any other score (raw log‑probabilities, negative loss values, etc.) merely changes the *distribution* of $\widehat A_i$.  As long as this distribution has finite mean and variance (so that normalisation is well‑defined) and you choose a clipping width $\varepsilon>0$, GSPO remains mathematically sound.

---

### 3. Practical reasons for keeping rewards in $[0,1]$

While mathematically optional, clipping to $[0,1]$ offers several engineering conveniences:

| Aspect | Benefit of bounded rewards |
|--------|---------------------------|
| **Numerical stability** | The standardisation step uses sample mean & std; with wildly negative or large positive rewards these statistics can drift or be dominated by outliers. |
| **Usable learning rates** | If advantage magnitudes become extremely large (e.g., raw log‑prob ≈ –200 for many tokens), a single update could drive parameters far off before clipping kicks in. |
| **Interpretability** | A scaled score makes it easier to interpret “how good” a response is across different tasks or verifiers. |
| **Clipping tightness** | Clipping operates on importance ratios; but if rewards are huge in magnitude relative to ratios, very few samples will survive clipping even with a moderate $\varepsilon$. Bounded rewards help maintain comparable advantage scales across groups of different lengths or temperature settings. |

If you nonetheless wish to use raw log‑probabilities or other unbounded signals:
- Apply a simple squashing function first (`tanh`, `sigmoid`, or min–max scaling).
- Alternatively compute advantages directly as `adv = raw_score - mean_raw`, optionally clip them manually before normalisation.

Both approaches preserve the formal correctness of GSPO while allowing you to work directly with model‑produced scores.

---

### 4. Do different reward scalings affect convergence?

Empirically we have observed two effects:

1. **Latitude vs clipping** – If rewards are too small (e.g., all close to 0), after standardisation advantages become unstable because `std` may be close to zero; this leads to exploding gradients unless gradients are clipped manually.
2. **Scale mismatch with likelihood ratios** – Large positive advantages combined with high likelihood ratios can push updates to an extreme regime before clipping takes effect; this might happen when using raw negatives like `-cross_entropy`. Again manual scaling fixes it.

In both cases these are not due to an inherent requirement for $[0,\,1]$ but rather due to ensuring that *advantages remain in a reasonably sized numerical range*.  

---

### 5. Bottom line for your use case

- **Theorem‑wise:** Any reward function can replace $r(x,y)$, as long as you keep its mean and std finite so that $\widehat A_i$ is well‑defined.
- **Implementation wise:** Scale signals into a bounded interval or normalise them aggressively when you form advantages.
- **Clipping wise:** Because GSPO clips entire responses based on $s_i(θ)$ rather than token‐wise ratios, there is no need to adjust epsilon for different reward scales—only `ε` needs to be tuned relative to how much variability you expect from `s_i`.
- **Practical tip:** When experimenting with non‑bounded rewards, watch the distribution of `adv` during training; if it becomes skewed heavily left/right blame a mismatch between your reward scale and the default learning rate or optimizer’s weight decay schedule.

Thus, while keeping rewards in $[0,1]$ simplifies experimentation and debugging, it is far from essential for GSPO’s theoretical soundness or its practical feasibility with other reward signals.
