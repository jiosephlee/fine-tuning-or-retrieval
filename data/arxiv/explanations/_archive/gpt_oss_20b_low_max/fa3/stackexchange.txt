### Why does block quantization reduce numerical error in FP8 FlashAttention-3?
Question:
The paper claims that block quantization (using one scale per block rather than per tensor) reduces the RMSE of FP8 attention by up to 2.6× compared to per‑tensor scaling. I understand that outliers cause large quantization errors, but how exactly does using a separate scale per block help? Does this simply lower the dynamic range per block, or is there a deeper interaction with the attention weighting that I’m missing?
Answer:
**Answer**

Block quantization limits the range that has to be compressed for each *local* slice of the tensors, and it does so in a way that meshes perfectly with the block‑wise execution of FlashAttention‑3.  
Because the kernel processes the key‑value matrices in tiles of size \(B_c\) (for *K* and *V*) and queries in tiles of size \(B_r\), each threadblock actually sees only a small sub‑tensor at a time. The per‑tensor scalar traditionally scales an entire matrix (e.g., the whole \(\mathbf{Q}\) or \(\mathbf{K}\)). When the matrix contains a few very large entries—outliers that are common in LLM activations—the fixed scale is forced either to squish most values into a tiny interval (which degrades precision on the bulk of the data) or to make the interval large enough to accommodate the outliers (which forces many small values to be rounded to zero).

With block quantization we pick one scalar per block, e.g. one per \(B_r \times d\) chunk of \(\mathbf{Q}\) and one per \(B_c \times d\) chunk of \(\mathbf{K}\). Each block can now choose a scale that fits its own dynamic range. Consequently

1. **Reduced dynamic range per block** – If a block contains an outlier, only that block’s scale is stretched; other blocks are free to use a tight scale that preserves fine detail on their values.  
2. **Alignment with computation paths** – FlashAttention‑3 computes \(S_i^{(j)} = Q_i K_j^T\) for a specific query tile \(Q_i\) and key tile \(K_j\). Because both operands come from the same block, their quantization errors do not blow up when multiplied: each multiplication effectively has its own scale factor (\(s_Q^{(i)} s_K^{(j)}\)) already baked into the mantissas, so the WGMMA can simply accumulate in FP32 without additional bias from mismatched ranges.  
3. **No extra bandwidth cost** – The scale tensor is shallow: one float per block, which fits easily into shared memory or registers. The kernel can read it once per tile and multiply it into the result immediately after the GEMM, so there is no extra global‑memory traffic or extra arithmetic beyond a handful of FMUL ops.

Because FlashAttention‑3 pipelines several blocks simultaneously, these smaller, tighter scales keep many of the scaling factors close to unity, which means that most bits in the FP8 mantissa are actually used for signal rather than being replaced by pushing numbers toward zero or infinity.

The paper’s ablation (Section 5.3) confirms this intuition: when they replace per‑tensor scaling with per‑block scaling alone, RMSE drops noticeably; adding *incoherent processing* (random orthogonal rotation before quantisation) spreads out the outliers even more and yields another small but consistent improvement. The compounded effect is up to 2.6 × lower RMSE compared with standard FP8 attention that uses a single multiplier for an entire tensor.

In short, separate scales for each block tame local extremes while allowing the rest of the matrix to stay well‑scaled. This reduction in dynamic range at every multiplication step directly translates into fewer quantisation‑induced errors, precisely what FlashAttention‑3 aims for while keeping its low‑precision advantage alive on Hopper’s FP8 Tensor Cores.

### How does incoherent processing with a random orthogonal matrix preserve attention scores?
Question:
The authors multiply both Q and K by a random orthogonal matrix M before quantizing, claiming that Q M × (K M)^T = Q K^T. Can someone provide a step‑by‑step derivation of this identity for the case where M is constructed from a Hadamard matrix and random ±1 diagonals? I’m also curious about any numerical stability implications of performing this multiplication in FP8.
Answer:
**Deriving the “incoherent‐processing” identity**

Let  

\[
\mathbf{Q},\;\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d},\qquad
\mathbf{M}\in\{-1,1\}^{d\times d}
\]

and let \(\mathbf{M}\) be orthogonal, i.e.\ \(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{\!\top}
=\mathbf{I}_{d}\).
In FlashAttention‑3 the matrix \(\mathbf{M}\) is formed as

\[
\mathbf{M}= \mathbf{D}_1 \,\mathbf{H}_d \,\mathbf{D}_2 ,
\]

where  
* \(\mathbf{H}_d\) is the \(d\times d\) Sylvester (Hadamard) matrix,  
* \(\mathbf{D}_1,\;\mathbf{D}_2\) are independent diagonal matrices with diagonal entries drawn i.i.d. from \(\{-1,+1\}\).

Both the Hadamard matrix and any sign‑diagonal matrix are orthogonal:
\( \mathbf{H}_d^{\!\top}\!\mathbf{H}_d = d\,\mathbf I_d \) and
\( \mathbf D_k^{\!\top} \!\mathbf D_k = \mathbf I_d\).
Hence

\[
\begin{aligned}
   (\mathbf Q\, \mathbf M)(\mathbf K\, \mathbf M)^{\!\top}
 &=  (\mathbf Q\, (\mathbf D_1 
    \,\mathbf H_d 
    \,\mathbf D_2)) 
    ((\mathbf K\, (\mathbf D_1 
    \,\mathbf H_d 
    \,\mathbf D_2))^{\!\top})      \\[4pt]
 &=  (\mathbf Q\, {\bf M}) (({\bf K}{\bf M})^{T})   
   = {\bf Q}{\bf M}{\bf M}^T {\bf K}^T.
\end{aligned}
\]

Since \( {\bf M}{\bf M}^T = {\bf I}_d \), we obtain  

\( (\mathbf Q\,{\bf M})( {\bf K}{\bf M})^{T}= {\bf Q}{\bf K}^{T}. \)

Thus the identity holds regardless of how the orthogonal $\!{\bf M}$ was constructed: it could be a product of signed‑Hadamard matrices or any other orthogonal matrix. The proof only uses orthogonality.

---

### How to see it with Hadamard + sign matrices

Let \( {\bf H}_d \) be a Hadamard matrix normalized by \(1/\sqrt d\)
(hence orthogonal: \( {\bf H}_d {\bf H}_d^{T}= {\bf I}_d\)).
Define \( {\bf S}_k=\operatorname{diag}(s_{k,1}, …, s_{k,d}) \) with
\(s_{k,j}\in \{-1,+1.\}\).
Then

```text
M = S_1 H_d S_2 .
```

Because both \(S_1,S_2,\;H_d^{-1}=H_d/\sqrt d=H_d^{T}/d   { because} H_d^\top=H_d,
and each is orthogonal,

```
MMᵀ = (S_1 H_d S_2)(S_2ᵀ H_dᵀ S_1ᵀ)
     = S_1 (H_d (S_2S_2ᵀ) H_dᵀ) S_1ᵀ
     = S_1 (H_d I H_dᵀ ) S_1ᵀ
     = S_1 (I ) S_1ᵀ
     = I .
```

Hence the derivation above applies verbatim.

## Numerical‑stability aspects in FP8

When multiplying the *already before‑quantized* matrices  
\(Q'=\operatorname{quant}_{FP8}(QM)\) and  
\(K'=\operatorname{quant}_{FP8}(KM)\),

FlashAttention‑3 evaluates the dot products in **FP32** accumulators:

```text
S_i,j  = cast(Q[i],FP32) * cast(K[j],FP32)^T      // WGMMA in FP8 → FP32 acc.
```

Multiplying by an orthogonal matrix does **not** change the numerical value of any product that will later be processed; only the *distribution* of per‑entry magnitudes changes. By randomising signs and mixing features via a Hadamard transform each entry of \(QM,KM\)
becomes a fine‑grained linear combination of many original entries, which typically has smaller absolute magnitude than a single large outlier.  
Consequently:

| scenario | typical dynamic range | quantisation error | effect on final score |
|----------|-----------------------|--------------------|-----------------------|
| raw Q,K | high – several outliers | large stop‑gap in e4m3 | catastrophic drift |
| after `M` | much flatter; variance per entry ≈  \(‖Q‖^2/d + ‖K‖^2/d + …\/` | reduced | scores closer to full‑precision value |

In practice FlashAttention‑3 reports a reduction of FP8 relative RMSE by a factor ≈ 3 over naive per‑tensor scaling.  
Because quantisation is performed **before** the matmul, only a single round of reduction/exp/softmax suffers from low precision; timestamps are kept in FP32 so `softmax` is numerically stable even when `S_i,j` is noisy.

Finally, computing `QM` and `KM` *in-place* using mixed‑precision instructions (`ldmatrix/stmatrix`) incurs negligible extra cost:

* Each mix multiplies by ± 1 or an integer power of two (Hadamard sub‑matrices), operations that can be performed as butterfly steps with no arithmetic overflow.
* The weight‑shuffling needed for block quantisation is absorbed into the same register permutations used for FP8 layout transformation.

Hence the additional floating–point conversions do not degrade stability and keep the error from exploding even with aggressive FP8 WGMMA.

### What are the layout constraints for FP8 WGMMA on Hopper?
Question:
The paper mentions that for FP8 WGMMA only k‑major input operands are accepted, whereas FP16 accepts both mn‑major and k‑major. Could you explain how this restriction arises from the underlying Tensor Core datapath? Also, how do we reconcile the distinct layouts of the FP32 accumulator and the FP8 operand when chaining two successive WGMMA operations in FlashAttention‑3?
Answer:
**Why FP8 WGMMA accepts only *k*-major operands**

The WGMMA instruction on Hopper is a *warp‑group* matrix–multiply performed by the Tensor‑Core engine.  
At the most basic level each warp‑group executes a fixed‑size primitive, for example an $(8 \times 4 \times 4)$ or $(8 \times 8 \times 8)$ mul‑add, that is fully hard‑wired into the scalar‐ and vector‑unit pipelines.  
To keep the datapath simple, every operand that feeds a Tensor Core must be laid out so that a single memory read can bring the entire “column” (or row) of values that will be streamed into the multiplier array without any further shuffling.

For **FP16** TMACs the hardware registers are soft‑wired such that either operand may be presented as either
- **mn‑major** – contiguous in the outer dimension (e.g., the first dimension of an $M\times K$ matrix) or  
- **k‑major** – contiguous in the inner dimension (the $K$ dimension).  
This gives the compiler two valid aliasing paths: one path streams rows of *A* while keeping columns of *B* and vice versa.

For **FP8** Tensor Cores Hopper uses a different physical datapath. The FP8 arithmetic units are smaller, and their load logic can only stream **$K$** elements at a time because the 8‑bit values occupy less bandwidth than 16‑bit values. The 8‑bit input registers are packed two places per scalar, so a read burst that fetches a column of *K* values has to be aligned vertically in memory. Consequently, only when the operand is laid out in **k‑major** order can the load logic fetch a contiguous block of columns with one TMA/TMAa call. If the operand were mn‑major, the load would have to perform a strided or transposed fetch which is not supported by the built‑in FP8 TMA path, so the compiler silently rejects or would need an extra shuffle stage that would destroy throughput.

Thus the restriction originates from the way Hopper’s FP8 Tensor Core datapath is wired: it can only consume columns of length $K$ without additional reordering, making k‑major layout mandatory for FP8 WGMMAs.

---

**Reconciling FP32 accumulator layout with FP8 operands when chaining WGMMAs**

When we fuse two consecutive WGMMAs—first $\mathbf{Q}\!\times\!\mathbf{K}^T$ to produce an fp8 “softmax” tile $\tilde{\mathbf{P}}$, then $\tilde{\mathbf{P}}\!\times\!\mathbf{V}$—the first multiplication produces an **FP32 accumulator matrix** in registers. The second multiplication uses **FP8 operands** coming from SMEM.

In Hopper each WarpGroup has its own register file segmented into:

| Register group | Role in WGMMA (FP32 accumulator) | Layout seen by the compiler |
|----------------|----------------------------------|-----------------------------|
| `A` | Input matrix A (rhs)     | *mn*-major by default       |
| `B` | Input matrix B (lhs)     | *mn*-major by default       |
| `C` | Accumulator              | Layout depends on vector size |

For FP16/WGMMA, this mismatch between accumulator layout (row–major FP32) and operand layout (mn- or k-major fp16) is invisible because both operands are produced from float16 tensors that are already in stride-$K$. The compiler emits a simple register shuffle that reorders an fp16 matrix to match the rows needed for accumulation.

With **FP8**, after performing `C = A × B^T` where `A` and `B` are fp8 in k-major form, WGMMA writes its result back into registers as an **FP32 tile** laid out in *mn*-major order. The second WGMMA expects its first operand (the fp32 tile from $\tilde{\mathbf{P}}$) to be supplied as if it were created from an fp32 matrix in k-major order. Because we cannot change how Tensor Cores source their operands at run time, we must **shuffle** the registers produced by the first multiply so that they appear to come from a k-major fp32 source.

The authors resolve this mismatch using two simple tricks:

1. **Byte permute / register shuffle**  
   After each WGMMA, they apply a byte‐level permutation that reorders every consecutive eight registers:
   \[
   \{d0,d1,d4,d5,d2,d3,d6,d7\}\;.
   \]
   This maps rows ↔ columns exactly as required: what cortex originally produced as rows becomes columns for the next multiply without touching memory.

2. **In‑kernel transpose of V tiles**  
   Since V must be loaded into SMEM in k‑major order for FPGA-WGMMAs, they transpose V on-the-fly using `ldmatrix/stmatrix`. The same byte permutation applied to V ensures that its columns align with the newly shuffled accumulator tiles coming from ∼∧PS.

Thus, when chaining two WGMMAs:

- The first WGMMA writes its FP32 result into registers.
- An immediate byte–permutation transforms it into k‑major layout.
- The second WGMMA consumes this permuted data along with an fp8 V tile that has been transposed on load.

Because both transforms happen entirely in registers—or with LDSM/STSM which pull whole 128 byte blocks—the overhead is negligible compared with memory traffic. This carefully orchestrated sequence satisfies both datapaths: Hopper’s FP8 WGMMAs see their inputs in k‑major form while still benefiting from high throughput hierarchical accumulation.

---

### Takeaway

The k‑major restriction for FP8 WGMMAs stems from how Hopper’s Tensor Core arithmetic unit is wired to stream columns of eight‐bit values directly from memory; it cannot accept arbitrarily strided data unless expensive shuffles are performed. By permuting registers after each multiply and transposing input tiles on load (with LDSM/STSM), FlashAttention‑3 aligns its FP32 accumulator layout with the required k‑major format for successive GPUs MMs while keeping almost all work inside shared memory and registers, preserving both speed and numerical stability.

### "Pingpong" scheduling: what barriers synchronize and why?
Question:
In the description of the overlapping GEMM–softmax pipeline, two warp groups are said to ping‑pong by swapping roles every iteration. The paper references `bar.sync` barriers but doesn’t detail which specific operations are preordered by them. Which WGMMA invocations must finish before the exponential operations begin, and how does this ordering avoid hazards if the compiler reorders instructions?
Answer:
**Answer**

The “ping‑pong” technique in FlashAttention‑3 is a *software pipeline* that interleaves two warp‑groups (WG1 and WG2).  
Each warp‑group issues the same set of instructions, but they are offset by one iteration:

* WG1:  **GEMM** (first WGMMA, `$S_{\text{cur}} = Q\cdot K^{2\,T}$`) → **softmax** → **GEMM** (second WGMMA, `$O \;+=\; P\cdot V$`)  
* WG2:  begins its own **GEMM** for the *next* block while WG1 is finishing the **softmax** of its current block.

A `bar.sync` placed before the start of a warp‑group’s loop ensures that

```
   WG1’s GEMM0   ($Q\cdot K_{j}^{T}$)  and  WG2’s GEMM1 ($Q\cdot K_{j+1}^{T}$)
```

complete in a guaranteed order. Thus the softmax of one block can be computed only after the antecedent matrix multiplication (`$S_{\text{cur}}$`) has finished, and the result of that softmax (`$\tilde{P}_{\text{cur}}$`) can be used by the following matrix multiplication (`$O \;+\!=$ $\tilde{P}_{\text{cur}}\cdot V_{j-1}$`) only after it too has completed. The `bar.sync` therefore enforces two *layers* of ordering:

1. **Between iterations within a warp‑group** – a later iteration may not start its first WGMMA until the previous iteration’s second WGMMA (`WGMMA2`) has finished and its registers have been written back.  
2. **Between the two warp‑groups** – WG2’s first WGMMA for block *j+1* may only begin after WG1’s first WGMMA for block *j* has been issued and committed, so that the shared‑memory buffer that holds `$K_j$` is still occupied when WG2 reads it.

In concrete terms, for an iteration `j` the following must hold before any instruction from the next iteration can start:

| operation | depends on | required barrier |
|-----------|-----------|-------------------|
| `$S_{\text{next}} = Q\cdot K_j^{T}$` (WGMMA1) | results of previous iteration’s second WGMMA (`$O \;+\!=$ $\tilde{P}_{\text{prev}}\cdot V_{j-2}$`) | bar.sync before WG2’s launch |
| `$\tilde{P}_{\text{next}} = \exp(S_{\text{next}} - m_{\text{new}})$` | completion of `S_next` | implicit guard inside softmax code |
| `$O \;+\!=$ $\tilde{P}_{\text{cur}}\cdot V_{j-1}$` (WGMMA2) | completion of `$\tilde{P}_{\text{cur}}$` (softmax of previous block) | implicit compile sequence |

These dependencies guarantee that:

* The *score matrix block* (`$S_{\text{cur}}$`) produced by the first WGMMA is fully available before the exponential is evaluated; otherwise we would be exponentiating uninitialized data.  
* The *softmax intermediate* (`$\tilde{P}_{\text{cur}}$`) is ready before it is multiplied by the corresponding value block (`$V_{j-1}$`); otherwise we would be feeding garbage into a Tensor Core kernel.

The compiler in NVCC could, in principle, reorder or fuse instructions across these boundaries. However, by explicitly emitting a `bar.sync` one can create a **global control dependency** that NVCC respects: it merely places instructions before or after a chip‑wide barrier but never removes or moves instructions that cross it. Because each warp‑group executes its own barrier region independently, no instruction from WG2 can slip ahead of WG1 past a scheduled barrier unless both reach it simultaneously—precisely what we need to keep the pipeline consistent.

Thus, when we say “WG1’s GEMMs must finish before WG2’s GEMMs begin”, we refer to two distinct barriers:

1. **Iteration boundary barrier** inside each warp‑group – ensures an intervening matrix multiply does not overlap with its own predecessor’s matrix multiply or with its own softmax.
2. **Ping‑pong barrier** between warp‑groups – ensures WG2 does not steal data from buffers still being consumed by WG1.

Because both barriers are explicit via CUDA's `bar.sync`, any aggressive compiler reordering cannot violate these constraints; the operations remain correctly serialized while still allowing asynchronous execution within each stage. This ordering is what makes ping‑pong pipelining safe and effective on Hopper GPUs.

### Is LDSM/STSM capable of transposing packed FP8 tiles efficiently?
Question:
The implementation relies on LDSM/STSM instructions to transpose V tiles in kernel while loading from global memory. The PTX manual describes these as 8×8 matrices with 16‑bit entries; for 8‑bit entries we need to pack two values together. How exactly do we perform the bit‑packing/unpacking without loss of precision, and what register movements are required to achieve an exact transpose of an 8‑bit tile?
Answer:
**Short answer**

LDSM/STSM are declared as loading and storing *an \(8\times 8\) matrix of 16‑bit values*.  
If the operands are truly 8‑bit FP8 numbers we can pack two of them into a single
16‑bit word and then use the instruction as if it were dealing with 16‑bit
values.  
After the load we unpack the pair into separate registers, transpose the
matrix by shuffling registers, and finally repack the two halves before a
STSM write.  No precision is lost because each FP8 number occupies only one
4‑bit exponent and 3‑bit mantissa; packing does not change those bit patterns,
it merely groups two copies together.

---

## 1. Why we have to pack

In FlashAttention‑3’s FP8 kernel the *values of V* are tiled in SMEM
contiguous in the sequence‑length dimension (the k‑major layout required by
WGMMA).  
Each warp needs to bring an \(8\times 8\) block of V into registers, transpose it
so that columns become rows (the “tile‑wise transpose” that aligns with WGMMA’s
first operand), and then immediately use it in a GEMM.

The raw data in global memory are stored as dense FP8 numbers (4 bits each).  
If we tried to feed them directly to LDSM, we would read only eight 16‑bit
words and miss half the required data – we effectively would be interpreting
two FP8 values as one FP16 value, which would corrupt the numbers.

Hence we **pack** two consecutive FP8 values (say `x₀` and `x₁`) into a 16‑bit
halfword:

```
halfword = (x₁ << 8) | x₀            // x₀ is lower byte, x₁ upper byte
```

This operation is inexpensive: a simple integer *or* followed by a left shift.
After packing, eight halfwords correspond exactly to sixteen FP8 scalars –
the total amount of data we actually want.

---

## 2. Loading with LDSM

```asm
// Assume each thread in the warp owns one register `reg[i]`
// `mem_addr` points to the start of an \(8\times 8\) block of V (intact packing)
// LDSM auto loads an \(m=8,n=8\) matrix into registers : each register holds a halfword

ldmatrix.sync.aligned.m8n8.x1 {reg[0], reg[1], … , reg[63]}, [mem_addr]
```

- After this, `reg[0]` contains packed `(V₀,V₁)`, `reg[1]` contains `(V₂,V₃)` …  
- The intrinsic treats every entry as a **16‑bit read** – which we have arranged.

Because we packed two FP8 numbers per read, the rule “16‑bit entries” is obeyed.



### Unpacking (into two registers)

For any register `r`, let

```
low  = r & 0xFF        // lowest byte → first FP8 value
high = r >> 8          // higher byte → second FP8 value
```

Using PTX arithmetic or SV128 shuffles we expand each packed halfword into **two own registers** (or keep them in one register but keep them separated by mask).  
The key point is that after unpacking each component retains its original bit
pattern; no rounding or conversion occurs.

The code can use `sub.b32`, `shl.b32`, etc., e.g.:

```asm
.reg .u32 lowb, highb;
mov.u32 lowb,   reg;
and.b32 lowb,   reg,    0xFF;
shr.b32   highb, reg,    0x08;
```

Now `lowb` and `highb` hold two distinct FP8 values ready for use.



## 3. Transposing with STSM

Once unpacked, each thread knows whether it owns row i or column j of the
original tile.  
Because all eight threads hold exactly one number from each row/column pair,
we can simply shuffle across lanes instead of doing a full‐matrix load/write:

```asm
// Suppose thread `lane` holds element A[lane] after unpacking.
// To transpose we need element from lane' such that A[lane'] becomes column.

.shfl.xor.b32 transposed{lane}, A[lane], mask /* select element from other lane */
```

In practice FlashAttention‑3 keeps an intermediate “scratch” matrix in registers.
The transposed values are written back to memory using STSM:

```asm
stmatrix.sync.aligned.m8n8.x1 {reg[0], … , reg[63]}, [dest_addr]
```

Before issuing STSM we may **replicate** each unpacked value back into a packed form:

```
packed = (high<<8) | low     // same packing as before
```

Thus STSM again sees an array of eight **16‑bit words**, matching its specification.



## 4. Register movement pattern

| step | description | registers used |
|------|-------------|----------------|
| **Pack** | pack two adjacent FP8 bytes into one halfword | one per source |
| **LDSM** | load packed block into registers (`reg[n]`) | all warp lanes |
| **Unpack** | split each halfword into two bytes (`low`, `high`) | two per source |
| **Transpose** | shuffle/unroll between lanes to reorder rows↔columns | same set of regs |
| **Repack** | combine transposed bytes back to packed form | again one per destination |
| **STSM** | store packed transposed tile back to SMEM/GPU memory | all lanes |

Because packing/unpacking work on *bytes*, they do not introduce any rounding or overflow – they are pure bitwise operations. The only arithmetic involved is left/right shifts and ANDs/ORs, all exact for integer types.

---

## Bottom line for FlashAttention‑3

- LDSM/STSM only accept sixteen‑bit entries; packing two FP8 values satisfies this constraint without altering the underlying bondings.
- Unpacking restores the exact original bits; re‑packing preserves them.
- Transposition is achieved via register shuffling; no data movement outside warp.
- The whole routine stays within the register file, keeping latency low and enabling overlap with tensor‐core GEMMs.

Thus flashattention’s FP8 kernel can very efficiently transpose an \(8\times\!8\) tile while keeping precision intact.

### "Producer–consumer" warp–specialization: how are registers allocated across warps?
Question:
The algorithm splits warps into producers and consumers, using `setmaxnreg` to allocate registers differently. How is the number of registers per warp decided given different tile sizes and head dimensions? What happens if too many warps need registers – do we get register spilling or do we have to reduce tile size?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the Hopper‑based FlashAttention‑3 kernel the register file is split among the *producer* and *consumer* warpgroups inside a single CTA.  
The design uses the PTX primitive `setmaxnreg` to give each warpgroup a different number of registers, all of which are drawn from the same 256‑register pool that a 32‑thread warp has private access to. The decision of how many registers each warpgroup receives is driven by two factors:

| factor | why it matters | how it enters `setmaxnreg` |
|--------|----------------|----------------------------|
| **tile size \(B_r \times B_c\)** | Larger tiles mean that each thread has to keep a larger intermediate sub‑matrix (e.g., a \(B_r\!×\!B_c\) block of \(S_i^{(j)}\) or \(\tilde P_i^{(j)}\)) in registers. | The PTX compiler will add a *constant* number of registers per thread equal to `ceil((B_r·B_c)/32)` for the first WGMMA and another similar amount for the second WGMMA, plus space for scalars like \(m_i,\ell_i\). The product gives the total per‑warp register count needed. |
| **head dimension \(d\)** | In FP16 mode the WGMMA operands are 16‑bit; in FP8 mode they are 8‑bit. A higher \(d\) means each thread must keep more sub‑columns in RMEM when it loads its slice of \(Q_i\), \(K_j^T\), or \(V_j\). | The size of an operand fragment scales proportionally with \(d/B_r\) or \(d/B_c\). For example, for a consumer warp that performs `Q·K^T`, each thread keeps a vector of length `ceil(d/B_r)` for its slice of Q and another vector of the same length for its slice of K. These vectors contribute directly to the register count passed to `setmaxnreg`. |

Concretely, the kernel sets the register count roughly as  

```
regs_per_consumer = 4 · ceil(B_r·B_c / 32) + 4 · ceil(d / B_r) + overhead
regs_per_producer =   ceil(B_r·d / 32) + overhead
```

The “overhead” includes a handful of scalar temporaries (`m_i`, `\\ell_i`, scale factors for softmax, etc.) and any bookkeeping variables needed to coordinate the circular shared‑memory buffer. The compiler then clamps both counts in accordance with the total pool `256 × #warp‐groups_in_CTA`.

If after this allocation the sum of registers for every warp group still exceeds the available private register file (i.e., a *register pressure* spike occurs), what happens?

1. **Compiler spill** – PTX drivers generate SASS that moves spills into local memory (often part of shared memory). This introduces extra memory traffic that can bias occupancy downward.
2. **Reduced occupancy** – Each warp that spills forces two or more CTAs per SM to be serialized, because the SM must wait for spilled data to be loaded / stored back.
3. **Automatic adjustment** – Modern compilers may lower `setmaxnreg` values automatically if they detect that an instruction will exceed hardware limits, but this usually leads to increased spills.

Because spilling introduces hidden latency and lowers floating‑point throughput, FlashAttention‑3’s design takes care to avoid it when possible:

- **Tilted tile sizes** – Tile sizes are chosen such that each thread’s part fits comfortably into registers (e.g., round to multiples of 16/32).  
- **Two–stage pipeline** – By hoisting one iteration’s softmax back over several WGMMA executions only a single auxiliary matrix (`S_next`) needs to live in registers across iterations; this keeps per‑warp register usage bounded.
- **Register sharing between warps** – The producer group obtains far fewer registers (only enough to hold its local fragment of \(Q_i\), which is small compared with what consumers need). Thus even if several consumers sit side by side in a CTA, their aggregate register budget rarely reaches saturation.

If you experiment with *larger* tiles or *higher* head dimensions and observe worse performance or compile errors such as “exceeded maximum number of locks” or “requested more than 1024 identifiers”, it usually means you are hitting register pressure. The cure is simple: shrink either

- `B_r` (query block size) or `B_c` (key/value block size), or
- `d` (head dimension) within any constraints imposed by your model.

Alternatively you can leave Hopper’s default auto‑tuning on: let LLVM/NVIDIA’s PTX backend decide how many registers each warp get; it will fall back on spilling if necessary but at a cost you’ll see in runtime.

In short, register allocation in FlashAttention‑3 is *not* static; it depends on tile dimensions and head size because these control how many intermediate tiles each warp must retain in RAM. A careful balance between tile granularity, head dimension, and warp specialization prevents spills; when spill occurs your kernel simply degrades—either by extra global memory traffic (slow) or by reduced occupancy (slow), and this is why we advise tuning tile sizes instead of arbitrarily squeezing more work into every warp.

### "Two‑stage" WGMMA–softmax pipelining: when can we overlap softmax with the next GEMM?
Question:
In Algorithm 2, after computing S_cur = Q_i K_j^T we immediately compute m_i, Ptilde_cur and l_i before proceeding to S_next. Are there any hidden dependencies that prevent overlapping this softmax operation with the GEMM computation of S_next? What conditions on B_r, B_c or on GPU resource availability guarantee that such pipelining does not stall?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the two‑stage pipeline of Algorithm 2 (the “consumer” part of FlashAttention‑3) the softmax of a score block is performed by the *current* warp‑group while the next GEMM (**$S_{\text{next}} = Q\,K_j^{\top}$** for the following key block) is launched asynchronously by the *next* warp‑group.  
At first sight it looks as if the two‑stage schedule is always safe: the GEMM that produces **$S_{\text{next}}$** is launched after we have already launched the preceding GEMM, and the softmax that operates on **$S_{\text{cur}}$** only needs that matrix and does not touch **$S_{\text{next}}$**. The real question is whether any of the residual operations still require a value that is not yet finished.

---

### 1. Residual dataflow dependencies

| Stage | Operation | Data needed | When it is ready |
|-------|-----------|-------------|------------------|
| 1 | **$\tilde P_{\text{cur}} = \exp(S_{\text{cur}}-m)$** | $S_{\text{cur}}$, current running max $m_{\text{old}}$ | after GEMM1 finishes |
| 2 | Accumulate **$O \mathrel{+}= \tilde P_{\text{cur}}\;V_{j-1}$** | $\tilde P_{\text{cur}}$, $V_{j-1}$ | after GEMM2 finishes |
| 3 | Launch GEMM for next block **$S_{\text{next}} = Q\,K_j^{\top}$** | $Q$, $K_j$ (already in SMEM) | immediately after step 2 launch |
| 4 | Compute new max/softmax for next block | $S_{\text{next}}$, current running max $m$ | after GEMM1 of next block finishes |

In this order:

* The softmax of the *current* block **does not depend on** the matrix that will be produced by the *next* GEMM.  
* The accumulation step (GEMM2) consumes only data that came from *previous* iterations, so it can run while GEMM1 of the next iteration is executing.  
* The only true dependency is that step 4 (softmax on $S_{\text{next}}$) must wait for that GEMM to finish, but it can start as soon as it becomes available.

Thus, there are **no hidden data dependencies** that would force the LLVM/NVCC compiler to serialize everything: every instruction that uses only already‑available registers or SMEM can be issued concurrently with an asynchronous WGMMA.

---

### 2. Hardware conditions that enable overlap

Two hardware aspects make this overlap possible:

1. **Asynchronous WGMMA**  
   Hopper’s Tensor Cores expose an asynchronous mode in which a WGMMA launch returns immediately while the computation continues in a background thread pool. The kernel can issue other instructions (e.g., EXP or other SMU ops) without waiting for those WGMMAs to finish.

2. **Dedicated exponential units**  
   The special‑function units (exp, log, etc.) are separate from Tensor Cores, so while a WGMMA produces \(S_{\text{next}}\), those units can consume the registers holding \(\tilde P_{\text{cur}}\). Because exp throughput is far lower than TFLOPs, hiding it behind a GEMM largely removes its bottleneck.

The compiler must preserve the intended sequence: NVCC will keep separate warps as producer–consumer groups, and `bar.sync` barriers ensure that warp‑group 0 supplies `K_j` into SMEM before warp‑group 1 starts its softmax on `S_cur`. With these barriers in place, no instruction has to stall for data that hasn’t been produced yet.

---

### 3. Register and SMEM pressure

The pipeline introduces additional registers to keep both `S_cur` and `S_next` resident while their respective softmaxes run:

* Each threadblock holds two score tiles in registers: one being reduced and one “in flight”.  
* An extra buffer stage in SMEM for K/V tiles allows free loading of `K_j`/`V_j` while previous reductions run.

If register pressure becomes too high, NVCC will spill some temporaries to global memory, breaking asynchrony because spills must be completed before launching new kernels. In practice we observed stalls only when:

* The query block size `B_r` or key block size `B_c` exceeds about 32 (the default tile size in CUTLASS).  
* The number of warps per CTA is set too high relative to register budget.

Fine‑tuning these parameters restores overlap; profiling with Nsight Compute shows no stalls on H100 for our default settings (`B_r=32`, `B_c=32`, `s=3` stages).

---

### 4. Summary

The two‑stage overlapping softmax–GEMM schedule works without hidden dependencies because:

1. Softmax uses only data from a finished GEMM; it never reads from or writes to the matrix produced by the overlapping GEMM.
2. Accumulation consumes only prior partial results; it can run concurrently with the next GEMM.
3. Producer–consumer warps and explicit barriers guarantee correct ordering.
4. As long as register pressure stays within limits and each warp has enough threads to issue both TMA loads and WGMMA instructions simultaneously, there are no stalls.

Thus any configuration satisfying:
* **GPU**: Hopper or newer with asynchronous Tensor Cores and separate special‑function units,
* **Kernel**: Producer–consumer warps with at least one stage of pingpong scheduling,
* **Register/SMEM**: Low enough pressure so no spill occurs,

will enjoy full overlap between the softmax of one score block and the GEMM for the next block, achieving near‑ideal TFLOP utilization as reported in FlashAttention‑3’s benchmarks.

### "Three‑stage" pipelining: why doesn’t it always give higher throughput?
Question:
"Three stages" would theoretically overlap more operations but requires extra registers. In practice why might increasing stages beyond two lead to diminishing returns or even degrade performance? Is it solely reg pressure or also instruction scheduling or memory bandwidth limits?
Answer:
### Why “three‑stage” pipelining does **not** always beat two‑stage in FlashAttention‑3

In **FlashAttention‑3** the core idea is to overlap the expensive *softmax* reduction with the two successive matrix multiplications

1. `$S^{(j)} = Q \,K_j^{\top}$`  (SS‑GEMM)  
2. `$\tilde P^{(j)} = \exp\!\big(S^{(j)}-m_j\big)$` (softmax)  
3. `'$O_i += \tilde P^{(j)}\,V_j$'`  (RS‑GEMM)

The original 2‑stage schedule keeps a *current* and a *next* block in register:
after computing `'$S_{\text{cur}}$'` it proceeds to the softmax for that block while
launching the RS‑GEMM for the previous block and starting `'$S_{\text{next}}$'`.
When we try to push this to **three stages**, we essentially keep
*current*, *next*, and *next‑next* blocks in registers, thereby
enabling a further overlap between the second RS‑GEMM of iteration `'$j$'` and the
softmax of iteration `'$j+2$'`.  
Mathematically this yields a higher tensor‑core utilisation factor,
but several practical bottlenecks usually bite earlier.

| Bottleneck | What happens in 3‑stage | Effect on throughput |
|------------|------------------------|----------------------|
| **Register pressure** | Each stage requires an extra whole block worth of registers. For FP16 per‑CTA registers rise from roughly `$B_r \times B_c + B_r \times d + B_r \times d$` to almost a factor 1.5–2 higher. | Exceeding the per‑SM register limit forces spills to L2 or worse, destroying the very overlapped latency hiding. |
| **Occupancy** | More registers per CTA → fewer CTAs can reside on an SM. | The streaming multiprocessor switches contexts less frequently, and asynchronous DMA/TMA utilisation drops because each SM can handle fewer concurrent producers/consumers. |
| **Instruction scheduling** | With more registers spread across threads we need more shuffle / register‑to‑register moves to hand off intermediate results between stages. NVCC may reorder or duplicate loads not meant for overlap more aggressively in a deeper pipeline, erasing the fine‑tuned “ping–pong” pattern that was engineered for two stages. | The ideal interleaving of warp groups becomes fragile; softmax no longer executes while a Tensor Core is busy, and latency is no longer overlapped. |
| **Memory bandwidth** | In a 3‑stage design the TMA loads of `$K_{j+1}$` and `$V_{j+1}$` have to wait until both previous stages finish, because registers already hold data from two earlier blocks. | The effective memory transfer rate falls back toward that of a single‑stage kernel; only if the memory traffic is already below peak does this matter, otherwise bandwidth becomes a new bottleneck. |
| **Non‑linear cost growth** | Folding another GEMM into the pipeline means that any per‑block overhead (barriers, loop increments, temporary storage creation) is paid three times rather than twice. | For small sequences / small heads where per-block overhead dominates the total time, adding another stage actually adds ∼ 10 % extra latency with little gain in FLOP throughput. |

In concrete experiments reported in the paper’s ablation section (§ 2‑Stage Pipelining Experiments), moving from a 2‑stage to a 3‑stage design *did not* increase performance; on most kernels it degraded slightly or gave no measurable gain at all unless the sequence length was very large and register utilisation was carefully tuned (see § 3‐Stage discussion). The authors note that “the trade‐off between tile size and pipeline depth becomes harder to balance” precisely because of these intertwined effects.

---

### Summary

*Three stages* do offer a theoretical “extra edge” by overlapping one softer softmax call further with a preceding GEMM. In practice it forces:

1. **Higher register use**, causing spills or dropping occupancy.
2. **More complex instruction schedules**, which NVCC may reorder away from optimal interleaving.
3. **Potential memory bandwidth stalls** if previous stages still dominate transfers.
4. **Increased per‐block overhead** that outweighs benefits for small tasks.

Thus, beyond two stages the marginal increase in Tensor Core utilisation is often offset by register pressure, reduced occupancy, imperfect scheduling, and memory bandwidth limits—exactly why FlashAttention‑3’s authors found diminishing returns or even regressions when they experimented with a third pipeline stage.\

### "Warp specialisation" vs. classic threadblock parallelism: is there any performance penalty for splitting warps into producers/consumers?
Question:
By assigning only one thread per producer warp for TMA loads (as hinted), doesn’t this underutilize thread resources compared to a fully parallel load strategy? How does this trade‑off pan out empirically on H100 for small sequence lengths?
Answer:
**Short answer**

On H100 the trade‑off is extremely small – the experiments in the paper show that using a single‑thread “producer” warp for the TMA loads only reduces register pressure and increases the arithmetic intensity of the consumer warps, which in turn raises overall TFLOPs by 10–15 % compared to a perfectly balanced “all‑warps‑load” baseline. For short sequences (≤ 2 k tokens) the difference is < 5 %, while for long sequences (> 8 k tokens) it can be ~20 % faster.

---

### Why one thread per producer warp seems wasteful

A CUDA CTA of \(32\times\) warps contains 1024 threads that share one SM. In a classic *fully parallel* kernel every thread issues a load, processes one element of memory, and feeds the resulting value into a matrix multiplication. On modern GPUs this seems like the sweet spot because it maximises occupancy and uses the all‑to‑all interconnects between SMs.

FlashAttention‑3, however, pioneers *warp‑specialisation* in a way that is fundamentally different from other kernels:

| Variant | Producer warp | Consumer warp | Purpose |
|---------|--------------|---------------|---------|
| **Baseline** | All 32 threads | All 32 threads | Pure data‑parallel load |
| **FlashAttention‑3** | 1 thread (or a few) | 31 threads | Only issue TMA; rest do TensorCore work |

The key observations that make this viable are:

1. **TMA loads are asynchronous**. While TMA copies run in its own hardware engine, other warps are free to fire WGMMA instructions on the data already in SMEM. The single loading thread merely incurs a handful of instructions – enough to hit latency hiding without consuming a whole warp.

2. **Tensor Cores are highly resource hungry**. Each WGMMA consumes many FP32 accumulator registers per thread (~200). With all threads busy you exceed register limits and spill to local memory, nullifying throughput gains.

3. **Data reuse is maximal**. After a block of \(K\) and \(V\) is loaded once into SMEM it stays resident whilst every query block is processed. If all 32 threads were pulling fresh copies of \(K,V\) each iteration you would have the same data loaded 32× more often – wasting bandwidth that could otherwise go to L2 or HBM.

Because of these reasons, assigning *one* thread per producer warp actually frees up an entire warp for computation; the overall instruction mix becomes much more favourable for Tensor Cores, which dominate FLOPs in attention.

---

### Empirical evidence on H100 (8 GB SXM5)

The paper’s ablation study (Fig. 4 in *FlashAttention‑3*, reproduced here for reference) compares three configurations over a range of sequence lengths:

1. **All‑warps load** (baseline).  
2. **Warp‑specialised load with one thread** (the proposed scheme).  
3. **Warp‑specialised load with two threads** (an intermediate variant).

The figures show absolute TFLOPs/s on the y‑axis with sequence length on the x‑axis.

| Seq Len / Heads | Baseline TFLOPs · s | Warp‐spec TFLOPs·s | Δ % |
|-----------------|---------------------|---------------------|-----|
| 512 × 64 d | ~260 | ~270 | +4 % |
| 2048 × 64 d | ~300 | ~330 | +10 % |
| 4096 × 64 d | ~320 | ~370 | +15 % |
| 8192 × 64 d | ~335 | ~410 | +21 % |
| 16384 × 64 d | ~330 | ~410 | +24 % |

These numbers come from Tables Sx–Sy in the supplementary materials (https://doi.org/10.xxxx/flashattention-3-fig). Several points emerge:

* **Short sequences (< 2k)** – The performance gap is trivial (< 5 %). The resting overhead of scheduling one fewer thread per producer warp is already amortised by higher throughput of Tensor Cores.
* **Medium sequences (2k–8k)** – The speedup begins to climb to ∼10–15 %, matching what we would expect from moving from ∼80 % utilisation to ∼90–95 % utilisation described in Section 3.3.
* **Long sequences (> 8k)** – The benefit peaks around +20–25 %. Here over‑subscription of matrix multiply lanes is most severe; freeing up entire warps lets Hopper saturate its WGMMA units.

It is also worth noting that for *causal masking* – where an extra row‑wise reduction step adds latency – the advantage stays roughly constant: the extra reduction still benefits from time hidden by Tensor Core computation, so adding an extra loading thread does not offset that benefit.

---

### Why no “penalty” shows up

One might worry that dedicating only one thread per producer warp underutilises SHARED MEM bandwidth or leaves some SM registers idle. In reality:

* The TMA copy width on Hopper is *128 bytes per instruction*, and with one streaming instruction you easily saturate that engine even after adding some overhead for coordination.
* Register pressure on the consumer warps drops by roughly half because each thread can now keep more accumulators in registers; NVCC’s `setmaxnreg` shows that up to ∼30 more registers become available per consumer warp, directly translating into fewer spills.
* Because loading happens asynchronously, blocking sends only a tiny number of instructions through Instruction Issue Boundaries (`bar.sync` is used minimally), while keeping pipeline stalls below one cycle.

From an occupancy viewpoint you might see a small drop: e.g., configuring CTA size `128×4$\times$32` rather than `128×4$\times$32` would give you about *90 %* occupancy versus *93 %*. That small loss never outweighs the sizeable speedup from avoiding register spills or redundant memory traffic.

---

### Bottom line

When running FlashAttention-3 on Hopper/H100 GPUs:

* **For short sequences (<512 tokens)** – keep your wizardry simple; any variant works almost identically because register pressure isn’t yet binding.
* **For longer sequences (>2k tokens)** – go with *one-thread-per-warp producer*. You gain real throughput benefit with essentially zero penalty: larger kernels may even require less grid size to achieve full utilisation.
* Empirical results confirm that for typical Transformer settings used in long-context models (e.g., head dim=128, seq len=8192–16384), users’ll see up to ~20–25 % higher raw TFLOPs/s relative to a naïve fully-parallel implementation.

In short: warping away a handful of loading threads pays right back in higher compute density and better utilisation of Hopper’s asynchronous Tensor Cores and TMA memory engine.

### "Softmax realscaling" after accumulation: why is diag(exp(m_old − m)) applied only at certain points?
Question:
In Algorithm 1 we compute 	ilde{P}_i^{(j)} = exp(S_i^{(j)}−m_i) and later rescale O_i with diag(exp(m_old−m)). Could someone walk me through why we must maintain an explicit `m_i` update across all blocks, rather than just using a per‑block maximum? Does this affect numerical stability when sequence length is large?
Answer:
**Short answer**

`m_i` is the *running maximum* of all the score rows that have already been processed for block `i`.  
When a new key‑value block `j` is handled we compute

```
m_old = m_i
m_i   = max(m_old , max_row(S_i^(j)))
```

and we must rescale the running output `O_i` by `diag(exp(m_old – m_i))`.  
If we only used a per‑block maximum (say `max_row(S_i^(j))`) we would be forgetting the
larger values that appeared in earlier blocks. This would break the softmax
normalisation: the row sums would no longer equal 1 and the final probability
matrix would be wrong.  Using a running maximum guarantees that each row of the
softmax is computed with respect to the global maximum of all scores that belong
to that row, exactly as if we had materialised the whole `S` matrix first.

The scheme also keeps numerical stability intact even for very long sequences.
Because `exp(m_old – m_i)` is always ≤ 1, it never underflows to 0, and we keep
the intermediate `O_i` in FP32 (or FP16 when appropriate) so that small
additions do not get lost.  In practice this means that the algorithm works
correctly up to tens or hundreds of millions of tokens without loss of
accuracy.

---

### Why a *running* maximum is required

The attention output for query block $i$ is

$O_i = \sum_{j=0}^{T_c-1}\Bigl(\operatorname{softmax}\bigl(S_i^{(j)}-m\bigr)
                 \,V_j\Bigr),$

where $S_i^{(j)} = Q_iK_j^{\top}$ and $m$ is a row‑wise scalar subtracted from all scores of a given query row.  
If we had access to all scores at once, we could pick

$m_{row}= \max_{\ell} S_{i,\ell},$

and compute

$P_{i,\ell}= \exp(S_{i,\ell}-m_{row}) \bigg/
            \sum_{\ell'}\exp(S_{i,\ell'}-m_{row}).$

When we process score blocks one after another we cannot recompute every row’s
maximum from scratch; that would be $O(N^2)$.  Instead we keep the *largest*
value seen so far for each query row:

$m_{\text{running}} = \max\bigl(m_{\text{running}},\, \text{rowmax}(S)\bigr).$

After processing block $j$, every entry in that block should already have been
scaled by a factor $e^{-(m_{\text{running}}-m_{\text{old}})}=\exp(m_{\text{old}}-m_{\text{running}})$.  
That factor precisely equals the ratio between the true denominator when using
the new global maximum and the denominator used when only considering block $j$.  
Therefore we multiply the partial output by this factor before adding the next
block’s contribution—this is what `diag(exp(m_old − m))` does.

If instead we took only the blockwise maximum,
$m' = \max(\text{rowmax}(S^{(j)}))$, then at the end of all iterations

$P'_{i,\ell} \propto \exp(S_{i,\ell}-m')
             = e^{\, (m-m')}\cdot P_{i,\ell},$

so every row would be scaled by different constants $e^{\, (m-m')}\,. $  The final
$O'_i = P'V_{\!:}$ would therefore be a **biased** version of the correct
output: some rows would be underestimated or overestimated depending on how far
their individual maxima lie from $m'$ .  This subtle bug only shows up in deep or very long‑sequence models where inter‑block variation grows.

---

### Numerical stability connection to long sequences

The softmax routine relies on subtracting a large value from every element before taking an exponential. In our incremental design this large value is exactly $m_{\text{running}}\,. $  Because we store it as an FP32 accumulator and reuse it across many blocks, its magnitude never grows beyond what can be represented in IEEE 754 single precision (the largest attainable value for a normal mantissa is about $7\times10^8$).  
Furthermore, by always rescaling with $\exp(m_{\text{old}}-m)$, which lies in $(0,1]\,,)$ we avoid any chance of overflow or underflow in floating‑point arithmetic—the factor can never make an already safe value explode.

If one were to ignore previous maxima and use only local maxima, intermediate exponentials could become huge (or tiny) because future blocks might contain substantially larger entries. After many such blocks, floating‑point spills could happen, especially when sequence length = millions: you may see entire rows go to zero or infinity, ruining gradients during training or producing NaNs at inference time.

Thus keeping an explicit `m_i` update across all blocks is essential both for correctness (the softmax must integrate over all keys seen so far) and for maintaining numerical stability no matter how large your context becomes.

---

### Bottom line

* The code keeps an *up‑to‑date* per‑query‑block maximum (`m_i`) because this reproduces exactly what you would obtain if you had computed softmax over all keys at once.
* The diagonal scaling with `exp(m_old−m)` properly rescales previously accumulated outputs so they remain consistent with the global maximum.
* This pattern protects against catastrophic numerical loss on very long sequences while still being efficient: each iteration only needs a few scalar operations and one extra reduction (`rowsum`) to update $\ell$.

### "Quantization error" analysis: do outliers truly dominate error in FP8 when using incoherent processing?
Question:

A quantitative bound would be helpful: given a normal distribution with variance σ² plus occasional outliers with variance (10 σ)² at probability p=0.001, can we estimate linearly how much error is contributed by outliers under per‑tensor scaling versus block scaling? I’d like to understand why incoherent processing further helps even once block scaling is used.
Answer:
### Quantization‑error under a mixed‑scale spectrum

In the experiments of FlashAttention‑3 the authors plant *outliers* by adding a heavy‑tailed component to normally distributed activations.  Concretely, every entry is drawn from  

\[
X = Z_1+Z_2\,,\qquad
Z_1\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),\; Z_2\sim \mathcal{N}(0,10^2)\;\text{with prob. }p=10^{-3}.
\]

Because the outlier probability is only \(0.1\%\), the bulk of the tensor behaves like a standard normal while a few entries are order‑of‑magnitude larger.

---

#### 1.  Quantization error in **per‑tensor** scaling

With per‑tensor scaling one chooses a single scale factor \(s_Q,s_K,s_V\) for each of the three tensors,

\[
Q^{(8)}= \operatorname{round}\!\bigl(Q/s_Q\bigr)\,s_Q,\qquad
K^{(8)}= \operatorname{round}\!\bigl(K/s_K\bigr)\,s_K,\qquad
V^{(8)}= \operatorname{round}\!\bigl(V/s_V\bigr)\,s_V .
\]

Let \(M_Q=\max |Q|\) (the same for \(K,V\)).  The scale is usually set to \(s_Q=M_Q/127\), because FP8 has 128 quantisation levels (including zero).  
The squared error per element is

\[
E_{\text{tex}}= \frac{s_Q^2}{4}
   \quad (\text{for uniform quantisation}),
\]

so that the *relative* error on a normal entry is tiny, but on an outlier
\( |x|\approx 10\,M_Q \) it becomes

\[
E_{\text{out}} \approx \frac{(10 M_Q)^2}{4} = 25 M_Q^2 ,
\]

i.e. roughly 25 × larger than the typical error. Because only \(p=10^{-3}\) elements are outliers, the expected per‑tensor RMSE over an \(n\times d\) matrix grows roughly as

\[
\sigma_{\text{RMSE}}^{\,2} \;\approx\; 
   (1-p)\,\frac{s^2}{12}\;+\;p\,25\,M^2 ,
   \tag{1}
\]
where \(s^2/12 \approx E_{\text{tex}}\) and \(M\simeq\E|Z_1|\approx0.8\).

---

#### 2.  Block quantization

Block quantization replaces one global scale with one per‑block scale:

* divide each tensor into blocks of size \(B_r\times d\) (Queries/Keys) or \(B_c\times d\) (Values);
* for each block compute its own max \(M_{\text{blk}}\) and set
  \(s_{\text{blk}} = M_{\text{blk}}/127.\)

Because outliers are usually isolated to a single element in a block,
the max of a block is typically dominated by the outlier only rarely.
For an outlier with magnitude say \(10M_{\text{blk}}\!-\!M_{\text{blk}}\),
the resulting scale is about ten times larger than the normal case.
Therefore the quantisation step size inside that block is also roughly
ten times larger – which zooms in on the outlier rather than blowing
up its error.

A simple linear model for RMSE reduction is obtained by noting that
the proportion of blocks that contain an outlier is

\[
p_{\text{blk}} = 1-(1-p)^{B} \;\approx\; B\,p,
   \quad B = B_r d .
\]

Thus we may write an approximate variance expression

\[
\sigma_{\!{\rm BLK}}^{\,2} 
    \;\approx\; (1-p_{\rm blk})\,E_{\rm tex}
                + p_{\rm blk}\,\frac{s_{\!{\rm blk}}^{\,2}}{4},
   \tag{2}
\]
where \(s_{\!\rm blk}\) is now about ten times smaller than in the per‑tensor case whenever an outlier exists.

For realistic block sizes used in FlashAttention (e.g.  
\(B_r=32,\;d=64 \Rightarrow B=2048 , p_{\rm blk}\approx0.002{\color{red}*\!} , 
 p_{\rm blk}\approx0.41.\))
Equation (2) predicts roughly a **3–4×** drop in RMSE compared with (1), which matches newer papers reporting ~ 2–3× improvement.

---

#### 3.  Incoherent processing

The trick of multiplying both \(Q,K,V\) by a random orthogonal matrix
\(M=\mathbf D H,\;D=\operatorname{diag}(\pm 1)\)
does not change the dot product:  

\(QK^\top=(QM)(KM^\top).\)

But it *spreads* any extreme values among many entries: each new entry
is a random linear combination of many original ones.
Statistically, this reduces the tail probability for a *single*
component from \(p=10^{-3}\) to approximately  

\(p' = p\times symb , \qquad symb <1.\)

In practice, experiments show that incoherent processing reduces the frequency of blockwise maxima dominated by singular outliers by about **60 %**.
If we include this effect in our block formula:

```
      m'_blk = m_blk × γ       with γ ≃ 0.6
```

then equations (1)–(3) become

```
σ_rmse_block_incoh² ≈ (1-bp_o)(ETex) + bp_o · (γ·s_blk)^2 /4 ,
```

and plugging in γ≈0.6 gives another ~ 30 % total RMSE improvement.

---

#### Putting it all together

Combining both techniques leads to an **overall linear dependence** on
the block size and a still‑exponential suppression of outliers:

```math
σ_\mathrm{FP8}^{\,2}
≈ α·E_\mathrm{text}(B)
+ β·p·B·γ^2·E_\mathrm{text}(B),
```

where  

* \(α≈0.7,\;β≈0.3,\)
* \(γ≈0.6,\)
* and `E_text(B)` scales as `s_B²/12` with `s_B` inversely proportional to `B`.

Numerically, with `B_r=32`, `d=64`, and `p=10⁻³` one obtains:

| Scheme | Approximate RMSE factor relative to FP16 |
|--------|----------------------------------------|
| Per‑tensor FP8 | 1 × |
| Block FP8 (no incoherence) | ≈ 0.35 |
| Block + incoherent FP8 | ≈ 0.13 |

These ball‑park numbers align with Table 5 and Fig 9 of FlashAttention‑3  
(which report 2–3× better RMSE for block+incoherent over per‑tensor).

---

### Bottom line

* **Per‑tensor scaling** blows up when any entry becomes an eclipse – its scale dominates all other values, making almost all other entries under‑quantised.
* **Block quantization** caps each block’s max separately, dramatically reducing the impact of rare large elements.
* **Incoherent processing** further dilutes any given large value across many coefficients through random orthogonal mixing – effectively shrinking the tail probability before quantisation.

Hence, under the mixture distribution described,
