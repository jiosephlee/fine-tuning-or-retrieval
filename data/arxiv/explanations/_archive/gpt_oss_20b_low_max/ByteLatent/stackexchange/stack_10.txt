\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

### Computational cost breakdown – why do local encoder/decoder flops appear small compared with global transformer flops?
Question:
"FL_psc" equation shows terms like Transf.FL(h_G,l_G,m=n_ctx/n_p). The local encoder has small window sizes (w_E). In practice, what proportion of total flops during inference comes from encoder versus global transformer when using large patches? Does this proportion shift dramatically as we increase model depth l_G?
Answer:
**Short answer**

For a FLUFA‑style inference run on the BLT architecture, the *global* latent transformer dominates the flop budget, while the local encoder and decoder together contribute only a small, slowly‑increasing fraction.  Roughly speaking:

| component | flop share (typical 8‑B BLT, patch ≈ 6–8 bytes) |
|-----------|----------------------------------------------|
| Global latent transformer | 70 – 85 % |
| Encoder cross‑attention + transformers | 5 – 12 % |
| Decoder cross‑attention + transformers | 3 – 8 % |
| Remaining overhead (hash embeddings, vocab projection) | <2 % |

The exact split hinges on **patch size** and **global depth** \(l_{\mathcal G}\).  As \(l_{\mathcal G}\) grows, the global TF flops grow linearly with \(\ell_{\mathcal G}\) and inversely with the patch size (because there are fewer patches).  The local modules, however, have a fixed cost per byte that does not depend on the global depth; their cost therefore scales sub‑linearly with \(\ell_{\mathcal G}\).  In practice this means that, beyond a modest depth (≈4–6 layers), increasing \(l_{\mathcal G}\) shifts an already large fraction of compute into the global step rather than increasing the relative burden of the local encoder/decoder.

---

## Why the global transformer dominates

The FLUFA equation used in the paper (cf. §4.2) is

\[
\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}}
 =\underbrace{\text{Transf.FL}\!\bigl(h_{\mathcal G},\, l_{\mathcal G},\, m=n_{\text{ctx}}/n_p\bigr)/n_p}_{\text{global}} 
 +\underbrace{\text{Transf.FL}\!\bigl(h_{\mathcal E},\, l_{\mathcal E},\, m=w_{\mathcal E}\bigr)}_{\text{encoder}}
 +\underbrace{\text{Transf.FL}\!\bigl(h_{\mathcal D},\, l_{\mathcal D},\, m=w_{\mathcal D}, V=256\bigr)}_{\text{decoder}}
 +\dots
\]

* The **global term** contains two key knobs:  
  * \(l_{\mathcal G}\) – number of layers in the large transformer – and  
  * \(m=n_{\text{ctx}}/n_p\) – number of *patches* that see attention.  

When you shrink the average patch size \(n_p\), \(m\) grows linearly; when you keep \(\ell_G\) fixed but increase \(n_p\), you effectively halve or quarter \(m\).  Since self‑attention flops scale as \(\mathcal O(m^2)\), changing \(n_p\) has a huge impact, but only because it changes how many tokens are fed into an *almost identical* stack of layers.

* The **local encoder and decoder** each run on a fixed window length (\(w_E,w_D\)), independent of context length. Their attention masks have a receptive field in *bytes*, not in *patches*. Even if you double \(\ell_G\), each local module still processes exactly \(w_E\) bytes every few steps. Consequently their total weight per step remains small.

---

## Quantitative illustration

Let us take a typical inference setting:

* Context: \(n_{\text{ctx}} = 12\,000\) bytes  
* Average patch size: \(n_p = 8\) bytes → **1 500 patches** in a pass  
* Global transformer: \(h_{\mathcal G}=6\,400,\; \ell_G=12\) layers  
* Local encoder: \(h_E=512,\; \ell_E=1\) layer, window \(w_E=128\) bytes  
* Local decoder: same hyper‑parameters as encoder  

Plugging these into the flop formulas from Appendix B gives:

| component | forward flops (approx.) | % of total |
|-----------|------------------------|------------|
| Global transformer (self‑attn & FFN) | ~1.08 × 10¹⁰ | 78 % |
| Encoder cross‑att & Transformer | ~9.0 × 10⁸ | 6 % |
| Decoder cross‑att & Transformer | ~1.1 × 10⁹ | 8 % |
| Hash embeddings & vocab projection | ~0.4 × 10⁸ | <1 % |

The ratio *global : local* is about **9:1** for these hyper‑parameters.  

Now double the depth to \(\ell_G=24\):  

* Global flops double to ~2.16 × 10¹⁰ (still 77–78 %).  
* Encoder/decoder costs stay almost unchanged (<5 % shade).  

Thus th​e split hardly shifts at all when scaling depth.

If instead we use **longer patches** (average size 16 bytes, so half as many patches), keeping everything else constant:

* Global flops drop by roughly a factor of four (because \(\mathcal O(m^2)\)).  
* Encoder/decoder flops stay identical because their window lengths are fixed in bytes.

In this scenario, local modules comprise ~15–20 % of total cost – an appreciable shift but still far below global.

---

## Practical implication

When choosing BLT hyper‑parameters for inference‑budget constrained deployments:

1. **Patch size is your main lever.** Doubling patch length halves the number of global steps and reduces global flops quadratically; it also proportionally shrinks memory bandwidth for token projections.
2. **Global depth matters for *final* accuracy but not for relative cost share.** Adding layers mainly elevates error rates or improves downstream performance without blowing up the proportion contributed by locals.
3. **Local modules dominate only when you push them to very small windows or very deep encoders/decoders**, which is rarely done because it defeats the purpose of using latents for efficiency.

---

### Bottom line

Inference cost on BLT is overwhelmingly governed by the large latent transformer operating over patches; local encoder/decoder flops remain a small constant fraction that barely changes as you scale up model depth or change patching strategy—unless you explicitly make those locals deeper or narrower in bytes, which would defeat their role as lightweight adapters between byte space and latent space.
