Chapter 1: # Chapter 3: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized Large‑Language Models – QLoRA and the Guanaco Family

## Introduction

Large‑language models (LLMs) have achieved a remarkable level of performance on a wide range of natural‑language tasks, but they also pose a significant barrier to entry for researchers and developers who wish to adapt them to new domains or applications. The core of this barrier is the sheer size of the models: a 65‑billion‑parameter LLaMA model occupies around 780 GB of GPU memory when fine‑tuned in full precision (16 bit). In contrast, modern consumer GPUs offer roughly 48 GB of memory, while even high‑end professional cards rarely exceed this limit. Consequently, the most powerful open‑source models remain inaccessible to anyone without a large GPU cluster.

**Why does finetuning matter?**  
Beyond improving performance on a target task, finetuning allows practitioners to shape undesirable behaviors (e.g., bias or hallucinations) and inject domain‑specific knowledge (e.g., medical terminology). Conventional fine‑tuning would require updating every weight in the model, a process that is both memory‑intensive and computationally expensive. Recent advances in *parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning* (PEFT), such as Low‑Rank Adapters (LoRA), have drastically reduced the number of trainable parameters. Yet, even *LoRA* still incurs substantial memory overhead when applied to large models because backpropagation through each frozen weight must still be stored during training.

The **QLoRA** methodology addresses these limitations by combining three key innovations:

1. **4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4) quantization** – an information‑theoretic encoding tailored for normally distributed weights that preserves accuracy while reducing storage.
2. **Double quantization** – applying a second layer of quantization to the per‑block scaling constants, saving up to 0.37 bits per parameter.
3. **Paged optimizers** – leveraging NVIDIA unified memory to offload optimizer states to host RAM when GPU memory becomes fragmented, eliminating out‑of‑memory spikes during gradient checkpointing.

Together, these improvements bring training of the largest publicly available LLMs down from >780 GB to <48 GB per GPU while retaining the full predictive performance of a 16‑bit fine‑tuned baseline. Moreover, **QLoRA backpropagates gradients through the frozen 4-bit backbone into LoRA adapters**, enabling accurate adaptation without ever touching the quantized weights.  

The practical fruits of this method are embodied in the **Guanaco** family—a suite of instruction‑tuned chatbots trained from LLaMA across scales from 7 B to 65 B parameters. With less than 24 hours on a single consumer GPU for a 33 B model and about 12 hours for the 65 B version, Guanaco attains over 99% of ChatGPT’s performance on the Vicuna benchmark while occupying only one third of the memory used by comparable open-source models.

In this chapter we unpack the motivations behind efficient finetuning, examine how QLoRA circumvents traditional memory barriers, describe its architectural components in detail, and present an empirical portrait of its impact through experiments on Guanaco.

## Why Fine‑Tuning Matters

Fine‑tuning refers to training pre\-trained neural networks on task‑specific data while keeping most *source* weights fixed or restrictively updated. For large language models acquired from self-supervised pretraining—in which millions or billions of tokens have been processed—straightforward transfer learning can dramatically improve downstream task performance:

- **Accuracy gains** – Words or phrases that were rarely seen during pretraining can be reinforced via supervised signals.
- **Behavior control** – Bias mitigation or alignment techniques can be baked into policy through supervised data or reinforcement learning from human feedback.
- **Domain specialization** – Medical or legal language requires specialized terminology; finetuning adapts general language understanding to such niches.

Thus, irrespective of whether we aim for academic benchmarks or commercial products, effective finetuning is essential for actionable deployment.

However, scaling *fine-tune* methods reveals two intertwined challenges:

1. **Memory footprint** – Storing parameters and activation gradients dominates RAM usage during training. The base model’s forward pass requires only its weights; yet during backpropagation we must keep intermediate activations for gradient computation unless checkpointing is used. Even with gradient checkpointing, forward passes for each mini-batch consume enormous buffer space.
2. **Computational cost** – High parameter counts translate directly into longer training times; further overhead comes from reduction in batch size due to memory constraints.

Existing PEFT approaches such as LoRA mitigate part of these issues by introducing lightweight adapters that scale with rank \(r\). Nevertheless they were primarily developed at scale ranges up to ~3–4 B parameters; beyond this threshold the base model’s frozen weights become so large that they dominate memory usage even with adapters frozen.

## Memory Barriers: From >780 GB to <48 GB

A raw computation graph for a self-attention block in transformer-based LLMs involves thousands — sometimes tens of thousands — matrix multiplications involving tensors that contain hundreds of millions of elements. Consider a single attention layer from LLaMA-65B:

- Weight matrices are around \(2^{34}\) entries (~16 bit each).  
- In full precision training with no quantization one needs at least two copies: one for forward pass and one storing gradients.
- Storing activations using gradient checkpointing still requires reading and re‐computing large parts which incurs *spikes* in GPU memory usage when long sequences appear.

Quantized inference has been well explored; methods like GPTQ or SmoothQuant reduce inference FLOPs by partitioning weights into small blocks and rescaling them locally via *quantization constants*. But naive application fails during *training*: during backward traversal we need multiply-by-weight terms that are implicitly stored in low-precision format – a non-trivial operation if our storage format cannot be efficiently dequantized on-the-fly.

QLoRA solves this hierarchy:

| Stage | Storage size | Posted GPU requirement |
|-------|--------------|------------------------|
| Full FP16 + Adam | ~4860 MB | >780 GB |
| NF4 + Dequant BF16 + LoRA | ~5048 MB | <48 GB |

Key drivers:

1. **BF16 computation vs NF4 storage**: Storing weights with NF4 allows us to retain significantly more information per byte compared to naive Int4 because NF4 matches normal distribution tails asymmetrically.
2. **Double quantization offsets constant overhead**: Quantization constants themselves cost μl bits per parameter; after applying double quantization they become negligible.
3. **Paged optimizers reduce optimizer spikes**: The steady turnover of optimizer slots is relocated away from active GPU RAM via unified paging whenever needed.

Net effect: We can fine-tune gigantic LLMs within the budgetlined constraints afforded by consumer-grade GPUs without external clusters.

## QLoRA Overview

In practice QLoRA deploys four modules:

1. **NF4 quantization pipeline**
2. **Double quantization helper**
3. **Paged optimizers**
4. **Low-rank adapters added at every transformer block**

### 1) NF4 Quantization

Unlike standard integer quantization (Int8) where all values are projected onto uniform bins defined by \(\text{absmax}\), NFk explicitly matches histogram bins with equal probability mass under a standard normal distribution \(N(0,\sigma^2)\). For 4-bit NF (NF4) we obtain eight unique values derived via:

\[
q_i = \frac{1}{2}\left(Q_{N(0,\sigma)}\!\left(\frac{i}{9}\right)+Q_{N(0,\sigma)}\!\left(\frac{i+1}{9}\right)\right), \quad i=0,\dotsc,7
\]

where \(Q_{N(0,\sigma)}\) denotes the inverse cumulative density function (quantile function). This produces bins that cluster more densely near zero—reflecting where pretrained weights typically lie—while leaving wider spacing for extreme values but ensuring every bin has an equal share probability mass under normality assumptions.

A blockwise rescaling step is applied:

\[
c_{\text{scale}} = \frac{127}{\text{absmax}(\mathbf{X}^{FP32})}
\]
\[
\mathbf{X}^{NF4} = \text{round}(c_{\text{scale}}\cdot \mathbf{X}^{FP32})
\]

Thus all blocks share the same NF4 quantile mapping but maintain independent scaling constants.

### 2) Double Quantization

Every block requires its own scaling constant (\(c_2^{FP32}\)). Those constants would normally consume \(32\,{\text{bits}}\times B^{-1}\) per parameter if stored as FP32 (with blocksize \(B=64\)). QLoRA perceives these constants as another linear tensor deserving compression: before storing them we normalize them about zero:

\[
c_2' = c_2 - \bar{c}_2
\]

Then we apply an eighth-bit floating-point format (FP8) across larger blocks (\(256\)), producing new constants \(c_8^{FP8}\). A final dequantification step yields originals used at runtime:

\[
c_1^{FP32}=c_8^{FP8}\times s
\]

Where \(s=10^k\times c_{max}^{FP8}\) normalizes FP8 numbers back into full precision range (the calculation hinges upon fixed FP8 exponent ranges). This "double" layer cuts constant overhead from \(0.5\)→\(0.127\,{\text{bits}}\)/parameter on average, liberating several gigabytes even for a gigantic model like LLaMA-65B.

### 3) Paged Optimizers

During training it is necessary maintain optimizer state arrays such as momentum or Adam “\(m,v\)” terms per parameter — often larger than just doubles because they need high precision and are updated every batch step. When long sequence encodings overwhelm *activation checkpoints*, temporary allocation might push memory past capacity; GPUs traditionally throw OOM crashes rather than evict buffers quietly. NVIDIA’s Unified Memory compensates by automatically swapping pages between DRAM and VRAM on demand—effectively paging optimizer matrices out when they are not actively used—and bringing them back upon access during weight updates. This non-invasive strategy eliminates most small OOM episodes without sacrificing throughput because page faults are amortized over long iteratives cycles.

### 4) LoRA Adapters at Every Transformer Layer

Instead of limiting adapters only to key/value projection matrices—as many earlier LoRA efforts did—QLoRA places parametric adapters at each Linear layer across all transformer submodules (self-attention Q/K/V/outputs; MLP layers; projection matrices etc.). This yields roughly ≈\(0.2\,%\) additional parameters relative to total model size: e.g., gauging LLaMA-65B produces ~130M adapter parameters (~240MB BF16). Since each adapter’s query-space rank \(r=64\), this configuration yields high expressive flexibility allowing adaptation without simulating full weight updates.

The forward expression becomes:

\[
Y = XW_{\text{base}} + S \cdot X L_\text{1}L_\text{2},
\]
and gradients flow through dequantified base block \(W_{\text{base}}\)—but only Marcus gets updated whether $L$’s produce approximate residual corrections capturing new task characteristics.

## The Guanaco Family

Using QLoRA one can fine-tune frequently used instruction-following corpora on openly available datasets:
 · OASST1 – crowd-sourced dialogues,
 · HH-RLHF – human-labeled instruction replies,
 · Alpaca/Unnatural/Instruct etc.,
across seven datasets and multiple architectures (LLaMA & T5).

The cumulative experimental effort amounts to over **1000 unique adapter generations**, enabling systematic analysis across:
– Model scale,
– Dataset quality vs quantity,
– Benchmark alignment (MMLU vs Vicuna/OA).

Key observations include:

| Model Size | Training Time | Memory Usage | Vicuna Eval (%) |
|------------|---------------|--------------|------------------|
| LLaMA-7B   | <12h          | ~5 GB        | ~80%             |
| LLaMA-13B/20B | <12h        | ~17 GB       | ~88%             |
| Guanaco-33B | <12h          | ~21 GB       | ~95%             |
| Guanaco-65B | <24h          | ~27 GB       | ~99%*            |

(*relative score vs ChatGPT on Vicuna benchmark.)

Interestingly Guanaco’s OASST1-trained models outperform all previously released open-source chatbots (Vicuna, Alpaca, etc.) while matching close-to-commercial systems in Elo ratings derived from pairwise GPT-\(4/ human evaluation.\)

Notably low computational budgets suffice: using SOTA NVIDIA GPUs with just 24–48 GB ram yields top performing models after two days—making state-of-the-art chatbots accessible even in limited cluster environments.
