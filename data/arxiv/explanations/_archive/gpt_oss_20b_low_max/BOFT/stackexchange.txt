\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### What is the main motivation behind replacing the block‑diagonal structure in Orthogonal Finetuning with a butterfly factorization?
Question:
In the paper, OFT uses a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix to limit trainable parameters but this introduces expressivity issues. The authors then propose BOFT which factors a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of butterfly matrices. I’m confused about why a butterfly structure is expected to be more expressive yet still keep the number of parameters low. How does the butterfly topology enable dense connectivity while maintaining sparsity compared to the block‑diagonal approach?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
The block‑diagonal scheme cuts the number of parameters by forcing the orthogonal matrix to act independently on disjoint groups of neuron dimensions. This creates a *fixed* sparsity pattern—each row can only mix a handful of columns—so the resulting transformation is far less expressive than a generic dense orthogonal matrix.  
A butterfly factorization, on the other hand, composes $O(\log d)$ sparse orthogonal factors that are **interleaved** in a way that every input coordinate can influence every output coordinate after the whole product is applied.  The sparsity pattern in each factor is very simple (it has exactly $d$ non‑zeros, one per row/column), but because the factors are repeatedly *mixed* and *re‑permuted*, their product becomes fully dense.  Therefore we retain only $O(d\log d)$ trainable numbers while still being able to represent any dense orthogonal matrix (or, more precisely, a richer subset of it than block‑diagonal OFT).  

---

### 1.  What block‑diagonal OFT actually does  

In OFT each layer’s refactorisation matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is constrained to  

\[
\mathbf{R}= \text{diag}\!\bigl(\mathbf{R}_1,\dots,\mathbf{R}_r\bigr), \qquad 
\mathbf{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}, \; b\,r=d .
\]

Only $r$ tiny rotations are trained; every input dimension belongs to one pre‑defined group and never interacts with any dimension outside its group during forward or backward passes.  
This drastically reduces parameters (only $\frac{rb(r-1)}2$ skew‑symmetric entries), but it imposes an inductive bias that the *architecture* should be respected when adapting – an assumption that rarely holds for arbitrary downstream tasks.

---

### 2.  Why expressivity is lost  

Because each group is isolated, the mapping $\mathbf{x}\mapsto \mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}$ can only mix values inside its own block.  A dense orthogonal matrix requires at least one path from every input coordinate to every output coordinate; block‑diagonal matrices have many zero paths.  
Mathematically, if we write the product of $m$ butterfly factors as

\[
\mathbf{R}= \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)} ,
\]

every $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}$ itself has exactly $d$ non‑zeros (one per row and column).  In contrast, a block‑diagonal factor has $rb$ non‑zeros – only one chain of depth‑1 connections per block – so its rank is limited by $b$ and cannot span the whole space.

---

### 3.  The butterfly topology as an efficient communication network  

Think of the multiplication as a communication network on a $d\times(m+1)$ grid: each source node in layer 0 sends messages to nodes in layer $m+1$ through $m$ intermediate layers (one per factor).  
A **butterfly graph** (the same pattern that Cooley–Tukey uses for FFT) connects nodes in adjacent layers by a *perfect match* that then swaps positions (*permutes*) before the next level:

```
layer 0 ──► layer 1 ──► layer 2 ──► … ──► layer m+1
```

Each arrow contains exactly $d$ edges, so over $m=\log_2 d$ stages we use $d\log_2 d$ edges in total – far fewer than the $d^2-d$ edges needed for a single dense orthogonal matrix.

---

### 4.  How this keeps parameters low yet dense

- **Parameter count**: Each butterfly factor is itself two \(b\times b\) rotation blocks arranged along a diagonal ($\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,k)}$).  
  With \(m=\log_2 d\) layers and \(b=2\), we need only

  \[
  \frac12(b-1)d m = \frac12(2-1)d\,\log_2 d = \tfrac12\, d\, \log_2 d
  \]

  trainable real numbers – asymptotically \(O(d\log d)\), versus \(O(d^2)\) for an arbitrary dense orthogonal matrix or \(O(db)\) for block‑diagonal OFT.

- **Dense reachability**: The permutation built into each stage ensures that after all stages the mapping between inputs and outputs is fully connected.  
  In graph terms, every node in the first column can reach every node in the last column via at least one directed path.  
  Thus although no individual factor mixes all coordinates, their product does.

- **Orthogonality preserved**: Each factor is itself orthogonal by construction (rotations or Cayley parametrisation of a \(b\times b\) skew‑symmetric matrix).  
  Orthogonality is closed under multiplication, so the final product remains orthogonal without extra constraints or re‑normalisation.

---

### 5.  Intuition behind “more expressive yet sparse”

- **Expressiveness**: By interleaving multiple stages of simple rotations and permutations you effectively approximate any rotation that could be written as a product of many small rotations – which is essentially all orthogonal matrices when you increase $m$.  
  Unlike block‑diagonal OFT where rotations are confined to fixed subspaces, butterfly BOFT lets subspaces interact across stages.

- **Sparsity**: Each stage contains at most one non‑zero per row/column.  
  This means each intermediate activation vector only needs to pass through two neighbouring neurons (in its small $b\times b$ block) before being shuffled; you never need full dense linear transformations at any intermediate step.

- **Trade‑off control**: By choosing how many stages ($m$) and how large each local block ($b$) you set up BOFT($m$, $b$), you can smoothly interpolate between pure block‑diagonal OFT (one stage, large $b$) and a full orthogonal update (many stages, small $b$).  
  Empirically this gives better performance–parameter tradeoffs because it adds just enough expressivity to adapt while keeping parameter count bounded.

---

### Bottom line

The transition from block‑diagonal to butterfly factorisation replaces rigid “within‑block” mixing with a cascade of lightweight “between‑block” permutations followed by tiny rotations.  
This cascade allows an inexpensive number of parameters ($\sim O(d log d)$) to weave together a truly dense orthogonal transformation – something impossible with a single sparse block‑diagonal pattern. The resulting BoFT therefore keeps parameter efficiency while dramatically improving expressivity and avoiding the inductive bias imposed by fixed grouping of neurons.

### How does the information‑transmission view formalize the construction of a dense orthogonal matrix?
Question:
Section 3 introduces an information‑transmission framework where multiplying several sparse matrices corresponds to sequential communication on a $d 	imes (m+1)$ grid. I understand the analogy qualitatively, but I cannot see how this perspective translates into concrete sparsity constraints on each factor $m{B}_i$. Could someone walk through an explicit mapping from a butterfly factorization to an information graph and explain why each node eventually has a path to every other node?
Answer:
**Answer**

The information–transmission viewpoint turns a factorised orthogonal matrix  
\[
\mathbf R
  = \mathbf B_{m}\,\mathbf B_{m-1}\cdots \mathbf B_{1}
\]
into a directed graph whose vertices are the rows (or columns) of the intermediate
matrices and whose edges are the non‑zero entries of each $\mathbf B_i$.  
Let us formalise this mapping and then see why a butterfly factorisation
satisfies the two desiderata that the paper lists:

1. **“Dense connectivity”** – every source node (row of $\mathbf B_{1}$)
   must be able to reach every destination node (row of $\mathbf B_{m}$)
   via a path that traverses one edge in each of the $m$ layers.
2. **“Minimum free edges”** – we want as few non‑zero off‑diagonal elements as
   possible while keeping each $\mathbf B_i$ orthogonal.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1. From a multiplication to a transmission graph

Consider a single non‑trivial example where $d=4$ and we factorise
$\mathbf R=\mathbf B_{5}\mathbf B_{4}\mathbf B_{3}\mathbf B_{2}\mathbf B_{1}$.
Create a rectangular grid with $m+1=6$ vertical columns (indexed
$0,\dots,5$) and $d=4$ nodes in each column.
  
* Node $(j,i)$ in column $i$ represents the $j^{th}$ row of matrix
  $\mathbf B_i$; thus node $(j,0)$ are the source nodes (rows of
  $\mathbf B_1$) and node $(j,5)$ are the destination nodes
  (rows of $\mathbf R$).
* For every non‑zero entry $(p,q)$ in matrix $\mathbf B_i$ we draw a
  directed edge from node $(q,i-1)$ to node $(p,i)$.

Multiplying matrices is exactly multiplying adjacency matrices:
the product
\[
\bigl(\!\!\prod_i [\text{edges from level }i-1\text{ to }i]\!\bigr)_{uv}
\]
counts how many ways an element in row $u$ of the *first*
matrix can reach row $v$ of the *last* one.  
For $\mathbf R=\prod_i \mathbf B_i$ to be dense we require that for every
pair of source–destination nodes the total number of paths is strictly
positive, which in graph terms means there must be at least one directed
path that visits exactly one edge per layer.

Figure 3 in the paper illustrates this construction. In particular,
the factorisation with only four factors would leave an isolated edge:
the projectile from node 1 cannot reach node 3, hence the resulting product
is not dense.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2. Orthogonality imposes a minimum number of “free’’ edges

Each pair $i,i+1$ of consecutive layers must connect every source node in
layer $i-1$ to *exactly one* node in layer $i$; otherwise the intermediate
matrix would not be invertible and hence non‑orthogonal. Concretely,
an orthogonal $d\times d$ matrix has at least $d$ non‑zero entries per
row (the “fixed’’ edges). The remaining **free** entries are those that can be set freely while maintaining orthogonality; for instance, when we permute rows by a **swap matrix**, we introduce two new non‑zeros but also introduce another zero somewhere else because orthogonality forces rows to remain orthonormal.

Thus, counting only free edges,
a single orthogonal matrix needs at least $d(d-1)=O(d^2)$ free entries, but by factoring it into many sparse orthogonal pieces we can reduce this count drastically.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3. Butterfly factorisation as an efficient transmission network

The radix-$k=2^N$ Cooley–Tukey butterfly is built recursively:
each factor has size $k\times k$, split into two block‑diagonal submatrices and a mixing layer (Equation (4)).
In graph terms each mixing layer is exactly one “butterfly component’’:
it connects each pair of *consecutive* nodes within the same block by two directed edges, forming a complete bipartite subgraph inside each block.

Because each butterfly component contains only **two** non‑zero entries per row,
and there are exactly $N=\log_2 d$ such components, the total number of free edges equals  
\(2d\,N = O(d\,\log d)\).  
Moreover, by construction every source node can reach every destination node:
the recursive halving pattern guarantees that after $N=\log_2 d$
mixing steps all pairs become interlinked – just as required for dense connectivity.

Thus, compared with an arbitrary sparse factorisation that may need many more free entries merely to stitch together all paths, the butterfly network achieves **low‑edge** yet **full connectivity**.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### 4. Explicit mapping: butterfly → information graph

Take for example $d=8,\;m=3,\;b=2$.
Each butterfly component is a block diagonal matrix with four $2\times2$
blocks; inside every block an element has two non‑zero entries,
creating exactly two directed edges per source node within that block.
Stacking three such components yields an 8 × 8 grid with three layers.
A quick walk through the graph shows:

* Step 1: from any source node we move either left or right inside its local pair (two choices).
* Step 2: we again split pairs according to the next level’s pairing rule.
* Step 3: similarly for level 3.

Because at every step we preserve parity within increasingly large groups,
after three stages any initial index can route to any final index.
The associated adjacency matrices multiply by definition into a dense orthogonal matrix—precisely what BOFT exploits.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### 5. Why every node eventually reaches every other node

The butterfly topology is mathematically equivalent to the radix‑2 Fast Fourier Transform’s data‐flow graph.
Its permutation pattern ensures that after $\log_2 d$ stages every input influences all outputs;
no matter which input index you start from, at level $\ell$ you have already interacted with *all* indices that differ in their lowest $\ell$ bits.
Consequently, by time $\ell = \log_2 d$ you have mixed all bit positions and thus every pair has shared information.
Hence any two products over different levels have unlocked connectivity between them.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### Bottom line

The information–transmission view reinterprets multiplication by sparse factors as routing on a layered directed graph.
Choosing the butterfly structure gives us:

* A deterministic — i.e., systematic construction guaranteeing dense output,
* Only \(O(d\,\log d)\) trainable parameters – since each component contributes few free edges,
* Orthogonality enforced locally (each component is orthogonal),
* An implicit inductive bias because many classical transforms are special cases.

This is precisely why BOFT can replace a huge dense orthogonal matrix with only logarithmically many sparse ones while still being fully expressive enough for practical finetuning tasks.

### "Cayley parameterization" – how is it applied to each butterfly component?
Question:
The paper says that each butterfly component $	ilde{m{B}}^b(d,k)$ must be orthogonal, and one can enforce this by Cayley transform. For a $2 	imes 2$ block, it’s straightforward, but for larger blocks (e.g., $b=4$) or higher‐level butterfly factors ($k>2$), how exactly do we parameterize them with skew‑symmetric matrices? Is there a closed‑form representation for the non‑zero elements, or do we treat each $2b	imes 2b$ subblock independently?
Answer:
**Short answer**

For every butterfly component \(\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}(d,k)\) the paper prescribes that the *\(b\times b\) diagonal blocks* inside the \(k\times k\) butterfly factor are multiplied pairwise to form a *\(2b\times 2b\)* block.
That \(2b\times 2b\) matrix must be orthogonal, and we enforce this by writing it in Cayley form

\[
\mathbf R_{\text{block}}
   =(\mathbf I_{2b}+\mathbf Q )(\mathbf I_{2b}-\mathbf Q )^{-1},
   \quad
   \mathbf Q=-\mathbf Q^{\top}\in\mathbb R^{\,2b\times 2b}.
\]

Thus each butterfly component is assembled from independent \(2b\times 2b\) orthogonal blocks,
each of which is parametrised with a skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf Q\).
There is no additional “closed‑form” closed‑form beyond this; the entries are
derived by multiplying the Cayley fraction as usual.

Below I unpack the idea carefully and show why the authors do it this way and how to implement it.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1.  What a butterfly component looks like

Recall the definition in Eq.\(7\)–\(8\) of the paper.

* A **butterfly factor** \(\mathbf B^F(k)\in\mathbb R^{k\times k}\) is a block‑matrix

\[
\mathbf B^F(k)=
\begin{pmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_2) \\
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_4)
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
k=2\,n .
\]

* A **\(d\)-dimensional butterfly matrix** is obtained by recursively
probing the two halves of size \(d/2\):

\[
\bB(d)= \tilde{\bB}(d,d)\;
          \begin{pmatrix}
          \bB_1(d/2)&0\\
          0&\bB_2(d/2)
          \end{pmatrix}
      = \tilde{\bB}(d,d)\tilde{\bB}(d,d/2)\cdots
        \tilde{\bB}(d,2).
\]

* The **butterfly component** at level \(k\) is simply a diagonal block–matrix
with the same sparsity pattern as a butterfly factor but with a larger
internal block size:

\[
    \tilde{\bB}^{\,b}(d,k)=
    \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\, 
             \underbrace{
             \begin{pmatrix}
              {\textrm{diag}}(A_1)&{\textrm{diag}}(A_2)\\[1mm]
              {\textrm{diag}}(A_3)&{\textrm{diag}}(A_4)
             }_{\,k=2\,n},\, 
           \dotsc ,\, 
           {\textrm{same}}\,
       \bigr)\!,
   \]
   where each \(A_i\in\sR^{n\times n}\).

When \(b=1,\;k=2,\;n=1,\;\tilde{\bB}^{\,1}(d,2)\)
is just a pure permutation of identity elements with two non‑zero entries
per column – exactly what one would use to enclose an ordinary \(a\times a\)
orthogonal \(a=1,\,Q=[0]\).
At larger blocks (\(k>2,b>1\)) we have **\(2b\times 2b\)** blocks that contain
the actual trainable parameters.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2.  Why only the \(2b\times 2b\) blocks need to be orthogonal

Each factor in the butterfly matrix multiplication has *exactly one* pair of columns that interact;
the rest of the columns merely pass through unchanged.
Consequently, if every such interacting pair forms an orthogonal map,
the *product* of all factors remains orthogonal (orthogonality is preserved under multiplication).
Therefore we only need to enforce orthogonality on those small sub‑matrices, not on every entry
of the full \(d\times d\) factor.

In other words:

```
for every level k (i.e. each tilde-B(d,k)):
    look at its non‑zero pattern → it consists of disjoint (kw allowed ?) 
    overlapping pairs of size (kw?? – actually here 2*b × 2*b).
    Ensure that each such pair is itself an orthogonal matrix.
```

Because the pattern is a permutation of the pattern for `tilde-B(d,2)` (the base level),
enforcing orthogonality on `tilde-B(d,2)` automatically guarantees
orthogonality for all higher levels – you just permute rows/columns.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### What does "effective trainable parameters" mean in BOFT($m,b$) and why is it $(b-1)dm/2$?
Question:
The algorithmic description states that BOFT($m,b$) uses $rac{1}{2}(b-1)d m$ effective trainable parameters when finetuning a $d	imes n$ layer. The paper mentions that elements that must be $igl\pm1igr/$ in any $d	imes d$ orthogonal matrix with only one non‑zero per row/column are not counted. How does this counting work across all butterfly components? A step‑by‑step derivation would clarify this.
Answer:
**What does “effective trainable parameters” mean in BOFT and why is it $(b-1)d m/2$?**

In BOFT a linear layer of size \(d\times n\) is finetuned by multiplying the frozen pretrained weight matrix \(W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) on the left with an orthogonal matrix  
\[
R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,i)} .
\]
Each factor \(\tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\) is a **butterfly component** that has a very specific sparsity pattern and is itself orthogonal.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1.  What are the trainable degrees of freedom in one component?

A butterfly component of block size \(k=2^p\) is constructed from a block‑diagonal matrix of \(2\times2\) rotations (or larger \(b\times b\) orthogonal blocks when we use the *block‑butterfly* extension).  
Let us look at the simplest case – the \(2\times2\) block – because every butterfly component can be permuted into this shape.

A  \(2\times2\) real orthogonal matrix is parameterised as
\[
Q=\begin{bmatrix}
c & s\\
-s & c
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad c^2+s^2=1 ,
\]
which has **one** free parameter (the rotation angle \(\theta=\arccos c\)).  
If we replace the scalar entries by full \(b\times b\) orthogonal blocks, each such block still has \(b(b-1)/2\) free parameters: an orthogonal matrix of size \(b\times b\) can be produced, for example, by Cayley’s transform from a skew‑symmetric matrix of the same size, and a skew‑symmetric matrix has exactly that many independent entries.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2.  Counting parameters in a single butterfly component

Inside one butterfly component \(\tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\) there are

* \(\frac{d}{k}\) diagonal blocks of size \(k\times k\).  
* Each of those blocks, after we expand it to block‑size \(b\times b\), contains exactly two non‑identical off–diagonal subblocks: each subblock is an arbitrary orthogonal matrix of size \(b\times b\).

Thus every block contributes
\(2 \times \frac{b(b-1)}{2}=b(b-1)\)
free parameters. Dividing by two because the two subblocks are *related* via symmetry (they are transposed copies in the butterfly factor), we obtain

\[
\text{parameters per block} = \frac{b(b-1)}{2}.
\]

Now multiply by the number of diagonal blocks:
there are \(\tfrac{d}{k}\) of them.  
Because we typically take \(k=b/2=1,\;1,\; … ,m\), effectively every butterfly component introduces exactly

\[
\dfrac{d}{k}\times\dfrac{b(b-1)}{2}=\frac{(b-1)d}{2}
      \quad(\text{free parameters per component}),
      \tag{1}
\]
since \(\tfrac{b}{k}=1+​(p-1)\cdot 0=1\:.\)

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3.  Summing over all components

BOFT uses *m* such butterfly components in series:
```
R(m,b)=˜B^(b)_((d,1)) · ˜B^(b)_((d,2)) · … · ˜B^(b)_((d,m)).
```
All components share the same number of free parameters because their block sizes follow the power‑of‑two schedule that preserves the permutation pattern.

Hence the total number of **effective** trainable parameters is simply

\[
m \times \frac{(b-1)d}{2}
   = \boxed{\frac{(b-1)d\,m}{2}} .
\]

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 4. Why “effective” and not all elements?

A dense butterfly component still contains many entries that are *not* free:

* The *non‑zero pattern* (the where‑to edges in the information‑transmission view) imposes that exactly $d$ places in each $k\times k$ block must be ± 1 or zeros; these do not carry learnable degrees of freedom.
* Orthogonality further forces interrelations between entries: if one entry is set to zero, another must also be zero so that columns stay orthonormal.  

The counting above excludes those constrained positions; only the genuinely independent angles (or skew‑symmetric entries for Cayley’s transform) survive as trainable parameters.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 5. Intuition through an example

Take $d=8$, $m=3$, $b=4$ (so $k=4$). The first factor $\tilde B^{\,4}_{(8,1)}$ consists of $\tfrac{8}{4}=2$ diagonal $4\times4$ blocks. Each such block contains two $4\times4$ orthogonal sub-blocks; each sub-block has $6$ free rotational parameters (since $4(4-1)/2=6$). Because the two sub-blocks are transposed copies, we count only half: $3$ per diagonal block.

So this factor contributes $2\times3=6$ free parameters.
Doing this for each of the three factors gives precisely  
\(3\times6=\frac{(4-1)\cdot8 \cdot 3}{2}=36/{3}\), matching formula (1).

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 6. Connection to broader concepts

The count mirrors what happens with simple structured matrices:

* **Block‑diagonal OFT** with block size \(b_{BD}\): each block has $\tfrac12(b_{BD}(b_{BD}-1))$ parameters, and there are $\tfrac{d}{b_{BD}}$ such blocks, giving $\tfrac12(b_{BD}-1)d$, identical to our per‑component formula when \(m=1,\; k=b_{BD}\).
* **Full orthogonal OFT** (\(m=\log_2 d,\; b=2,\; k=2^{m}\)) gives $\tfrac12(d-1)d$, which is exactly $\mathcal O(d^2)$.

Thus BOFT generalises between these extremes by choosing any intermediate pair $(m,b)$.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

**Bottom line:**  
The “effective trainable parameters” in BOFT count only those degrees of freedom that truly change during training—namely, the angular variables defining each orthogonal subblock inside every butterfly component—while ignoring positions forced to be zero or ± 1 by sparsity and orthogonality constraints. Adding up these variables over all $m$ components yields exactly $(b-1)d m/2$.

### Why is multiplicative dropout used in BOFT instead of standard dropout?
Question:
BOFT multiplies many orthogonal matrices in succession, and the authors propose a "multiplicative dropout" that sets chosen butterfly components or their sub‑blocks to identity during training. How does this differ from applying dropout to activations or weights as in LoRA? What effect does turning part of the butterfly factorization into an identity have on the overall orthogonality and expressivity?
Answer:
**Answer**

In BOFT the trainable part of a layer’s weights is an *orthogonal* matrix  
$R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde B_{(d,i)}^b$.  
Each factor $\tilde B_{(d,i)}^b$ is itself a block‑diagonal matrix of
$2b\times 2b$ orthogonal “butterfly” blocks.  
Because the overall update to a frozen weight matrix $W^0$ is a *product*
of these matrices, we cannot simply drop out the entries that would be
masked by a conventional (additive or activational) dropout; doing so would
break the strict orthogonality guarantee and would make the forward‑pass
no longer a simple rotation of the pretrained features.

---

### What “multiplicative dropout” does

1. **Selects and (temporarily) replaces factors.**  
   In each training step a random subset of the $m$ butterfly components
   and, within each selected component, a random subset of its diagonal
   blocks are set to the identity matrix.  

2. **Leaves the remaining parts untouched.**  
   The non‑identity blocks are still multiplied together – so every forward
   pass still uses a product of orthogonal matrices – only some factors are
   neutralised.

3. **Retains full‑matrix orthogonality during back‑propagation.**  
   Because an identity factor is itself orthogonal, the whole product remains
   orthogonal at every step; gradients are therefore back‑propagated over an
   orthogonally constrained path.

In contrast, LoRA (or any standard PEFT that uses additive low‑rank updates)
has a *free* multiplicative factor that can be dropped out densely (by zeroing
a fraction of its entries). Dropping entries in LoRA simply scales or nullifies
the low‑rank update; it does not have to preserve an exact orthogonal group.
Multiplicative dropout in BOFT must keep each partial product an orthogonal
matrix, so it operates on whole sub‑blocks rather than individual weights.

---

### Effect on orthogonality and expressivity

- **Preserves global structure.**  
  Even when some butterfly blocks are replaced by identities,
  every factor remains orthogonal, so the overall rotation is still a valid
  element of $\mathcal{O}(d)$.  The identity components act as if no
  transformation were applied along those “channels”. Thus, at any training
  step we are exploring a *subspace* of rotations obtained by selectively
  deactivating some sub‑transformations.

- **Regularises coefficient learning.**  
  By forcing certain sub‑blocks to be identity temporarily, the optimiser has
  to learn parameters that work well even when only part of the butterfly
  circuit is active. This is akin to weight sharing and coreset learning,
  which helps prevent overfitting and encourages smoother learnt rotations.

- **Maintains expressive power.**  
  A block is set temporarily to identity only during that mini‑batch; after
  training it is restored to its learned value. Consequently, after training,
  all butterfly blocks are fully active and their product can represent any
  element in the structured subset defined by the given parameters.
  The multiplicative dropout therefore *does not* reduce final expressive power;
  it merely injects noise during optimisation to improve generalisation.

- **Implicit inductive bias towards classical transforms.**  
  The butterfly pattern already contains permutations that recover fast FFT,
  Hadamard or DCT transforms. Turning off subsets during training reinforces this bias and keeps the network from drifting too far into arbitrary rotations – exactly what OFT was designed for: preserving pairwise angles while still allowing a dense transformation.

In summary, multiplicative dropout in BOFT randomly armed parts of the butterfly factorization with identity matrices, keeping every intermediate factor strictly orthogonal. Unlike standard dropout which simply zeros or scales activations or weights, this scheme respects the multiplicative nature of BOFT’s update and preserves global rotational structure while still regularising training. It does not hurt representational capacity because all blocks become active again after training, but it encourages smoother, more generalisable rotations than a raw product of arbitrary orthogonal matrices would allow.

### "Expressivity of BOFT"—does a larger $m$ always mean better approximation of any orthogonal matrix?
Question:
Theorem 1 states that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size and that by multiplying many pairs $m{B}_{i,j}$ and their transposes one can approximate all orthogonal matrices. However, empirical results (Figure 6) suggest diminishing returns for large $m$. Why doesn’t increasing $m$ further always reduce approximation error? Is there an inherent limit due to the fixed sparsity pattern of butterflies?
Answer:
**Short answer**

No. A larger *m* (more butterfly layers) does not guarantee a lower approximation error because the butterfly pattern imposes a fixed sparsity/graph topology. Increasing *m* adds more trainable parameters, but only certain graph structures guarantee that every entry of the final product can be freely tuned. If the chosen butterfly layout fails to create a *full* connectivity between the two ends of the grid (i.e., if some nodes in the first layer cannot reach some nodes in the last layer through the directed edges implied by the non‑zeros), then those entries of the final matrix can never be changed, no matter how many training steps you perform. Thus, beyond a certain point the extra degrees of freedom are wasted and the approximation error plateaus.

---

### 1.  Theoretical background

Recall that a BOFT transformation is a product

\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}_{(d,i)} ,
\]

where each \(\tilde{\mathbf B}_{(d,i)}\) is an orthogonal butterfly component built from $b\times b$ blocks.  
If we write each component as a sparse adjacency matrix on a grid that has $d$ nodes on level 0 and $d$ nodes on level $m+1$, then every entry of $\mathbf R(m,b)$ corresponds to a path from some node in level 0 to some node in level $m+1$. This is exactly the *information‑transmission view* introduced in Section 3 of the paper.

For a dense orthogonal matrix every path must exist; equivalently, for every pair $(j,k)$ we need at least one directed path $j \to \cdots \to k$.  
A single butterfly component alone generally *does not* provide such connectivity: by construction it only swaps or mixes pairs of adjacent indices (the classic radix‑2 “butterfly” pattern). Only when we compose several components with complementary permutations do we obtain full connectivity.

---

### 2.  Why larger *m* does **not** always help

1. **Fixed sparsity pattern** – Each $\tilde{\mathbf B}_{(d,i)}$ has exactly $d$ non‑zero entries per column (the permutation part) plus $b-1$ trainable parameters inside each block (the scalar weights).  
   These non‑zero patterns are *fixed* by design; we never add new edges beyond what the butterfly topology provides.

2. **Reachability constraints** – If for a given *m* the concatenated graph still contains disconnected components, some entries of $\mathbf R(m,b)$ will remain zero no matter how we train.  
   The paper’s Figure 6 shows that for very large $b$ (larger block size), increasing *m* further does not add new effective edges, so the reachable set does not grow.

3. **Diminishing returns** – Even when all entries are reachable, many entries in an orthogonal matrix are highly constrained by orthogonality: changing one parameter forces correlated changes elsewhere due to orthogonality constraints on each bilevel block.  
   Once a certain number of parameters is exceeded, adding more layers yields only marginal improvements because we are now fitting parameters that are redundant under the fixed topology.

4. **Expressiveness vs regularity** – Theorem 1 guarantees that BOFT with enough layers can generate *any* orthogonal matrix if we allow both “forward” and “transpose” butterfly components:
   \[
   \mathbf R^G = \prod_{i=1}^{d-1}\mathbf B_{i,1}(d)\,\mathbf B_{i,2}^\top(d).
   \]
   However this construction requires $2(d-1)$ alternating factors and is not what is used in practice (where all factors have identical structure). Practically, most BOFT configurations use far fewer factors, so they lie in a strict subset of $O(d)$. As soon as you exhaust that subset’s capacity, more layers do nothing but add trivial reparameterizations within the same sub‑group.

Hence there exists an **inherent limit**: once all reachable positions have been driven to their optimum under orthogonality constraints, further increases in *m* cannot reduce error. This limit depends on both $b$ and on how well the chosen butterfly layout satisfies full connectivity.

---

### 3.  Empirical illustration (Figure 6)

In Figure 6, error curves for several $(m,b)$ pairs quickly converge to a plateau:

- For small block size ($b=2$), increasing $m$ beyond roughly $\log_2 d-2$ gives negligible improvement because the butterfly graph already provides dense connectivity.
- For larger block size ($b=16$), even fewer layers suffice; yet an over‑parameterized setting ($m>4$) produces almost no gain because most additional degrees of freedom lie inside large blocks that already span plenty of directions.
- Curves for different $b$’s with comparable total parameter counts tend to intersect: this shows that it is not merely “more parameters” but rather **which angles** those parameters can adjust that matters.

The empirical plateau matches precisely the theoretical argument above: once all entries are controllable within an orthogonal sub‑space defined by our particular butterfly layout, adding more layers cannot change any entry further.

---

### 4.  Broader implications

The situation mirrors other structured reparameterizations such as Low‑Rank Adapter (LoRA), where beyond a certain rank adding more channels does not help because the learned subspace already captures most useful directions. In BOFT’s case:

- The *butterfly network* supplies an efficient routing scheme between dimensions.
- Orthogonality imposes global constraints that reduce independent degrees of freedom compared to arbitrary matrices.
- Consequently, there is an upper bound on achievable expressivity given any fixed sparsity pattern.

Future work could explore alternative sparse topologies (e.g., irregular trees or network designs from communications theory) that might yield higher reachability with fewer parameters or might dynamically rewire during training to adaptively increase expressivity while still staying within storage limits.

---

### Takeaway

A larger *m* does **not** automatically equate to a better approximation because BOFT’s expressiveness is limited by its fixed butterfly sparsity pattern and orthogonality constraints. Once all reachable entries have been tuned to their optimum under those constraints—often at moderately small values of *m*—adding extra layers simply produces redundant transformations and results in diminishing returns, as observed empirically in Figure 6.

### How does BOFT preserve the spectral norm of pretrained weights compared to LoRA?
Question:
The paper claims that both OFT/BOFT multiply an orthogonal matrix from the left, thus preserving singular values. Is this property preserved when we use multiple butterfly components multiplied together? Does the product of several orthogonal matrices remain orthogonal? If yes, does it still guarantee identical spectral norms after finetuning?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
Yes – the spectral norm of a pretrained weight matrix is preserved by BOFT just as it is preserved by OFT.  
The reason is that the weight update in both methods is a *left‑multiplication* by an orthogonal matrix.  
A product of orthogonal matrices is itself orthogonal; hence the Frobenius‑norm–preserving property that guarantees identical singular values (and thus spectral norm) holds for BOFT as it does for OFT.

---

### Why a product of orthogonal matrices remains orthogonal

Let  
\[
\mathbf{R}_1,\mathbf{R}_2,\dots,\mathbf{R}_m \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
\]
be orthogonal, i.e. \(\mathbf{R}_i^\top \mathbf{R}_i = \mathbf{I}_d\) for every \(i\).  
Define
\[
\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{R}_m \mathbf{R}_{m-1}\cdots \mathbf{R}_1 .
\]
Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R}
&= (\mathbf{R}_1^\top \cdots \mathbf{R}_m^\top)(\mathbf{R}_m \cdots \mathbf{R}_1)   \\
&= \mathbf{R}_1^\top (\mathbf{R}_2^\top \cdots \mathbf{R}_{m-1}^\top)  
   (\mathbf{R}_{m-1}\cdots \mathbf{R}_2)\,\mathbf{R}_1          \\
&= \underbrace{\mathbf{I}}_{\text{sub‑product}}\,\underbrace{\mathbf I}_{\text{sub‑product}}   = \mathbf I .
\end{aligned}
\]
The induction step uses the fact that each pair
\((\mathbf{R}_{k+1}^\top\,\mathbf R_{k+1})=\mathbf I_d\); therefore the whole product evaluates to the identity.  
Thus, **any fixed product of orthogonal matrices is again an orthogonal matrix**.

BOFT’s construction uses exactly this fact: each butterfly component is parameterised so that it is an individual orthogonal matrix, and the final update matrix
\[
\hat{\mathbf R}(m,b)=
   \tilde{\boldsymbol B}^{b}_{(d,1)}\, 
   \tilde{\boldsymbol B}^{b}_{(d,2)}\, 
   \cdots 
   \tilde{\boldsymbol B}^{b}_{(d,m)}
\]
is simply a short chain of such matrices. Consequently every intermediate partial product is orthogonal and the full product stays orthogonal.

---

### Spectral–norm preservation

Write the pretrained weight matrix as a singular–value decomposition (SVD)
\[
\mathbf W^0 = \boldsymbol U\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\boldsymbol V^{\top},
      \qquad
      \|\!\|\!\|\boldsymbol W^0\|\!\|\!\|_2 = 
             \|\!\|\!\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|\!\|\!\|_2.
\]
OFT and BOFT update it by left multiplying with an orthogonal matrix:
\[
    \hat{\mathbf W}
    =  (\hat {\boldsymbol R}(m,b)\,)\,
       (\boldsymbol U\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\boldsymbol V^{\top})
      =  (\hat {\boldsymbol R}(m,b)\,\boldsymbol U)\,
         \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,
         \boldsymbol V^{\top}.
\]
Because \(O = (O_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\) are all orthogonal, the product \(O=O_m O_{m-1}\dots O_1=\hat {\boldsymbol R}(m,b)\) is itself orthogonal. Therefore \(O\,U=\tilde U\) remains an orthogonal matrix. The SVD of the updated weight still contains the same diagonal matrix of singular values:
\[
    {\rm SVD}(\hat{\boldsymbol W}) = 
    (\tilde U)\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,(V)^{\top},
      \quad
    {\rm diag}(\sigma)
    = ({\rm diag}(\sigma^0)) .
\]
Hence
\(||\,||_{\!2}(\hat{\boldsymbol W})=||\,||_{\!2}(\boldsymbol W^0)\).

From the paper’s Sec. 4 “Spectral properties”, this preservation has two practical effects:

| Aspect | Effect | Why? |
|--------|--------|------|
| Training stability | Less drift from initial Jacobian conditioning | Orthogonality keeps gradients from exploding/vanishing |
| Generalisation | Better distance‑metric matching between old and new rather than arbitrary re‑weighting | Spectral norm preservation maintains total energy/power in weight space |

---

### Intuition for why BOFT still keeps this property

* Each butterfly component operates on a small \(k\times k~(k=2^l)\) block or on a \(b\times b~(b=2,4,8,\dots)\) sub‑block when a block–butterfly extends to larger dimensions. Since those blocks are each parametrised using Cayley transforms that enforce exact orthogonality, their entries are *exactly* unitary.
* Even though many blocks are multiplied together (as many as \(m=\mathcal O(\log d/b)\)), the multiplicative chain has no source of drift: numerically one may worry about floating‑point accumulation errors, but these are negligible with modern deep‑learning libraries that preserve unitary constraints to machine precision.
* The *intended* induction bias encoded by butterfly sparsity does not interfere with orthogonality because each sparsity pattern still obeys the unitary constraint: a sparse diagonal or off‑diagonal block can never have two non‑zero rows/columns unless they linearly depend on each other; enforcing skew‑symmetry in Cayley’s form guarantees that each block remains unitary regardless of its sparsity shape.

Thus every step — from initialization (identity), through gradient descent updates (which keep skew‑symmetry), to the final multiplication—maintains orthogonality. This means BOFT inherits all nice spectral properties of OFT, notably identical singular values and unchanged spectral norm.

---

### Summary

* **Orthogonality preservation**: BOFT’s learned transformation matrix is a product of several butterfly components; because each component is individually orthogonal and the set of orthogonal matrices is closed under multiplication, their product remains an exact member of \(O(d)\).  
* **Spectral norm**: Since left multiplication by any member of \(O(d)\) does not change the singular values of any weight matrix it acts on, BOFT preserves every singular value—including the largest one—exactly as OFT does.  

Consequently, BOFT benefits from all stability and generalization advantages that were attributed to OFT’s spectral‑norm preservation while providing higher parameter efficiency thanks to its butterfly factorisation.
