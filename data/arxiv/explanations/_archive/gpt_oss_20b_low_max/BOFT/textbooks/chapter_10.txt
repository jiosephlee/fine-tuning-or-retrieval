\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 10: # 10. Discussion – Inductive Biases & Limitations

The Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) paradigm extends the principled orthogonal finetuning framework by endowing the orthogonal transformation with a butterfly‑shaped sparse factorization. Within this chapter we analyze the inductive biases that arise from this structural design, compare them to the implicit regularization discovered in other parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT) families such as low‑rank or sparse adapters, and identify practical barriers that warrant further investigation. All insights presented here are derived from the original paper and its empirical or theoretical findings; no external materials are invoked.

## # Implications of Structured Sparsity on Generalization

BOFT inherits a rich family of classical fast linear transforms through its butterfly construction. The butterfly graph—originally introduced in the radix‑2 Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm—encodes a set of power‑of‑two‑size diagonal blocks \(\mathbf{B}^F(k)\) that are recursively interleaved and permuted across repeated layers. Because many familiar transforms (discrete Fourier, Hadamard, sine/cosine) can be expressed as a product of such butterfly layers with appropriate parameter choices, BOFT is capable of reproducing this family of operations exactly.

When a pretrained model is edited by an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}\), the model’s weight columns are rotated in a highly constrained subspace of the full orthogonal group \(\mathcal{O}(d)\). This constraint exhibits two complementary properties:

1. **Preservation of Pairwise Angles**  
   Orthogonality guarantees that for all vectors \(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\),
   \[
   \langle\mathbf{Ru},\,\mathbf{Rv}\rangle = \langle\mathbf{u},\,\mathbf{v}\rangle,
   \]
   which shows that the bilinear similarity—and hence the underlying distance metric—between all neuron embeddings remains unchanged. Empirically, this sharp spectral preservation translates into more stable training trajectories and reduced overfitting compared to additive rank‑\(k\) updates.

2. **Implicit Regularization via Exploreable Transform Families**  
   Because BOFT’s hypothesis space is effectively a low‑complexity manifold formed by the successive composition of sparse orthogonal blocks, random initializations of these blocks lie close to identity matrices but can explore richer regions as training progresses. This structured exploration aligns the finetuned model space with well‑studied signal processing bases, which may act as a form of *implicit inductive bias* favouring representations that respect both locality (due to the sparsity pattern) and global coherence (achieved after several butterfly stages). Such properties have already been shown to improve generalization in neural networks trained from scratch; BOFT transfers these benefits to fine‑tuning without incurring extra inference cost.

The paper validates these intuitions empirically across vision transformers, large language models, and diffusion generative models. Across all domains, BOFT consistently outperforms block‑diagonal OFT and LoRA when operating under identical parameter budgets, suggesting that the inductive biases encoded by butterfly factorization indeed generalize beyond a single architecture type.

## # Comparison to LoRA Low‑Rank Training

LoRA (Low‑Rank Adaptation) is arguably the most widely adopted PEFT technique for large language models. LoRA formulates a weight update as
\[
\mathbf{W}^\ast = \mathbf{W}^0 + \Delta \mathbf{U}\Delta \mathbf{V}^\top,
\]
where \(\Delta \mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\) and \(\Delta \mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}\) are trained with \(k = O(1)\). The key desiderata for LoRA are twofold: 
* Low per‑layer parameter cost proportional to \(O(k(d+n))\), and 
* Simple additive updates that preserve most of the pretrained weight magnitude.

While LoRA’s low‑rank structure inherits an implicit bias towards low-frequency or smooth perturbations in weight space—due to its small effective rank—it does not explicitly enforce orthogonality or pairwise angle preservation. Consequently, LoRA can inadvertently introduce drifts in spectral norms, which may impair training stability or lead to feature collapse if not carefully regularized.

BOFT achieves comparable sparsity benefits but through an *orthogonal* multiplicative transformation rather than an additive decomposition:
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{W}}^\ast = (\prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)})\,\mathbf{W}^0.
\]
Here each multiplicative factor is itself an \(O(b)\)-by-\(O(b)\) rotation embedded in a larger \(d\times d\) space via block diagonals or butterfly patterns. The net parameter count scales as
\[
O(dm(b-1)/2)
\]
for general \(m,b\), which yields roughly \(O(d\,b)\) parameters when \(m=1\) (block‑diagonal OFT). In contrast to LoRA’s linear spectral perturbation, BOFT preserves any singular value due to orthogonality:
\[
\sigma_i(\tilde{\mathbf{W}}^\ast)=\sigma_i(\mathbf{W}^0),
\]
ensuring that training dynamics remain controlled even under extensive adaptation.

From a representation standpoint, whereas LoRA perturbs weight columns additively—effectively moving them within an affine subspace—BOFT rotates columns within a closed manifold defined by butterfly chains. These rotations cannot interpolate arbitrary linear combinations but can express any rotational transform expressible by a product of sparse orthogonals; such transformations tend toward preserving feature manifold geometry rather than shrinking or exploding axes.

Moreover, numerical experiments reported in the paper show that for identical parameter budgets (e.g., 4 % of total weights), BOFT achieves higher downstream task performance across vision recognition benchmarks and text generation perplexities than LoRA variants with either fixed or adaptive rank settings.

## # Potential Use of Alternative Network Topologies

The information transmission view introduced in Section 3 reframes sparse orthogonal factorization as constructing directed graphs whereby messages pass between successive layers of nodes. Under this abstraction, butterfly graphs represent one instance where dense connectivity over two dimensions can be achieved using only \(O(d\,\log d)\) edges: each level connects each node to just two nodes in the next level according to recursively defined permutation patterns.

Given that this framework does not preclude other topologies with comparable sparsity but different connectivity properties, several alternatives merit consideration:

1. **Expander Graphs**  
   Expander graphs possess strong connectivity properties while maintaining extremely small degrees per node (often constant). Utilizing expander-based adjacency matrices as butterfly factors might allow faster mixing between source and target nodes with even fewer non-zero entries per matrix while still guaranteeing dense overall connectivity after \(O(\log d)\) stages.

2. **Random Sparse Orthogonal Matrices**  
   Generating each factor via random permutations followed by randomized Givens rotations could produce diverse connectivity patterns possibly hardening generalization against specific preprocessing biases inherent to fixed butterflies.

3. **Hierarchical Tree-Like Factorizations**  
   Substituting lower-triangular or upper-triangular sparse matrices arranged in hierarchical trees yields alternative combinatorial structures that may offer asymmetric yet dense transformations suitable for attention-based architectures where directional flow matters.

While robust experimental evidence is absent at present—since all evaluations reported rely on coherent butterfly sequences—the graphical framework permits systematic exploration: any proposed topology could be encoded as directed edges between successive node layers; verifying dense reachability reduces to checking whether every source node can connect to every sink node via paths composed solely from those edges across all levels. Balance between edge count and guarantee of density would then motivate theoretical analysis similar to results shown for butterflies: e.g., determining minimal path-length conditions or cycle-free constraints necessary for full invertibility under orthogonality constraints.

## # Runtime Overhead vs Inference Savings

A salient practical advantage presented in the paper is that BOFT incurs no inference latency once the learned orthogonal matrix has been merged into the pre-trained weights—multiplication by an already applied transformation carries no additional computational cost during deployment. However this convenience comes at two distinct costs:

- **Training Overhead**  
  Each forward and backward pass now requires additional matrix multiplications by factor matrices \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}\). Since each factor is sparse but involves permutations and small block-wise rotations, Jacobians are more expensive than vanilla forward passes over frozen weights alone but still cheaper than full fine-tuning where all weights must be updated directly. Empirical runtime studies indicate that training speed degrades roughly proportionally to \(m=b=O(1)\); yet exact wall-clock penalties remain unquantified for extremes such as \(m=\log d,\ b=2.\)

- **Memory Footprint**  
  Storing each factor matrix along with intermediate activations requires additional GPU memory proportional to \(O(m\,db^2)\). While still far below storing full weight updates as in low-rank adapters (which require storing two dense rank-\(k\) tensors), memory constraints become nontrivial when scaling BOFT beyond midsized models (e.g., > 11 B parameters).

There is thus an inherent trade-off: increasing depth \(m\) improves expressivity but adds linear overhead; enlarging block size \(b\) reduces number of factors yet boosts per-factor complexity quadratically due to larger block rotations within each butterfly component.

Future work may involve identifying optimal pairs \((m,b)\) per layer automatically through hyperparameter search or using adaptive mechanisms analogous to Rank-Adaptive LoRA where low-rank dimension adapts during training—here “block size” could adapt instead.

## # Open Research Questions  

Several important questions naturally arise given what has been demonstrated:

| Question | Significance | Possible Direction |
|----------|--------------|--------------------|
| What is the minimal number of butterflies required (\(m_{\text{opt}}\)) for different architecture depths? | Guarantees sufficiency without excessive overhead | Theoretical bounds using graph expander properties; empirical profiling |
| Is there an optimal *per-layer* block size \(b_\ell\) that balances expressivity vs memory? | Tailors induction bias per layer | Layer-wise validation curves; learning-to-learn meta-learners |
| Can we replace butterflies with learned sparse topologies while still guaranteeing dense overall transformations? | Expands design space beyond radix‑2 patterns | Search over permutation families; evolutionary algorithms |
| How does BOFT behave under extremely hostile tasks (e.g., domain shift or adversarial perturbations)? | Assesses robustness conferred by structured rotations | Adversarial fine-tuning experiments; worst-case analysis |
| Are there theoretical guarantees on generalization bounds tied explicitly to butterfly depth? | Connects structure theory with learning theory | PAC-Bayes bounds conditioned on factorization complexity |

Addressing these will deepen understanding not only of BOFT itself but also elucidate how strict structure versus flexibility affect finite-sample performance in foundation model adaptation.

## # Future Research Directions

Drawing from both empirical successes and identified limitations, several lines warrant further inquiry:

1. **Dynamic Butterfly Depth Adaptation**  
   During early training epochs, lightweight factors might suffice; later epochs could incorporate additional butterflies if loss plateaus earlier than desired—a “curriculum” style approach mirroring adaptive rank selection in LoRA variants.

2. **Hybrid PEFT Schemes**  
   Combine BOFT’s rotational layer with low-rank additions from LoRA: 
   \[
   \tilde{\mathbf{W}}^\ast = (\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)})(\,\mathbf{W}^0 + U V^\top).
   \]
   This could harness benefits from both spectral preservation and local perturbation flexibility.

3. **Hardware Acceleration via Structured Sparsity**  
   Investigate custom kernels exploiting diagonal-plus-butterfly sparsity patterns—for example shared memory banks aligned with block rotations—to reduce runtime overhead further.

4. **Theoretical Exploration via Matrix Product States**  
   Recasting butterfly factorizations as tensor network sketches may reveal hidden hierarchies capturing multi-scale correlation structures—potentially aligning well with transformer attention mechanics.

5. **Cross-Modal Generalizability Studies**  
   Extending BOFT beyond vision-language pairs into multimodal task suites (e.g., video-language generation) will test whether inductive biases derived from classic transforms continue to confer benefits when data modalities exhibit markedly different statistical characteristics.

In conclusion, Orthogonal Butterfly finetuning introduces a principled way to embed classical signal processing priors into modern deep learning finetuning pipelines while preserving computational simplicity at inference time. Its success invites deeper theoretical interrogation into structured sparse transformations within large model adaptation yet simultaneously underscores practical considerations such as runtime overhead and latent inductive bias tuning—all fertile territories for future research endeavors.
