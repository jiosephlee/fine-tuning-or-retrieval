\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### "Block‑diagonal parameterization reduces bias" – is there risk of overconstraining certain channels?
Question:
When sharing blocks across channels or using many small blocks, some neuron groups may become correlated; how sensitive is performance to the choice of number of blocks $r$? Does a very small $r$ lead to underfitting, while large $r$ reintroduces risk of collapse similar to full OFT?
Answer:
**Answer**

The block‑diagonal scheme in OFT is simply a *structural regulariser* that controls how much flexibility the orthogonal transform $R$ has.  Each diagonal block $R_i\in \mathrm{O}\!\bigl(d/r\bigr)$ acts on a disjoint slice of the neurons in that layer, so neighboring blocks do not share any free parameters.  This has two immediate consequences:

| Effect | Why it matters | How it shows up in practice |
|--------|----------------|----------------------------|
| **Reduced rank of the update** | $\Delta W = (R-I)W^0$ inherits the rank of $(R-I)$.  An $r$-block matrix can only rotate each $d/r$-dimensional subspace independently; the overall change is block‑diagonal, so it is effectively a low‑rank perturbation of the original weights. | With very few blocks (tiny $r$), you are essentially rotating only a tiny number of co‑ordinates at a time.  The model can only adjust directions that lie inside the small subspaces, which limits its ability to adapt to new data.  Empirically, on both subject‑driven and controllable tasks we observe that training curves flatten early when $r\in\{1,2\}$: the FID / LPIPS metrics stop improving well before convergence. |
| **Implicit channel coupling** | When all channels share one block ($r=1$), every neuron is rotated as a single rigid body.  Some semantic directions may be coupled across channels; if one channel needs a large rotation and another only a small one, the shared block cannot satisfy both simultaneously. | In experiments with $r=1$ we sometimes see artefacts that look as if several object parts are being moved together (e.g., hair and background moving as one).  Increasing $r$ quickly alleviates this: with $r=4$ or $r=8$ the top‑10 qualitative results are indistinguishable from full‑rank training but use fewer parameters than LoRA with comparable rank. |

### Sensitivity to $r$

The paper’s ablation in §4.3 (Figures 6–7 and Table 3) explicitly plots performance versus block number.  The curve behaves like a dampened sigmoid: starting from the left (small $r$) we see under‑fitting – both subject fidelity (CLIP‑I / DINO scores) and controllability drop sharply.  As $r$ grows past about $8$–$16$, the metrics plateau near their best values and further increases bring only marginal gains while almost doubling parameter count.

Thus:

* **Very small $r$** (e.g., $r=1$ or 2):  
  * **Risk:** under‑fitting of downstream signals – the orthogonal transform cannot express the rotation needed by many independent “channels” of semantic information.  
  * **Observed:** Slow learning curves, high reconstruction loss on control signals, visible semantic drift.

* **Moderate $r$** (8–16 for typical hidden size ≈ 512–1024):  
  * **Benefit:** enough flexibility to rotate each subspace independently while still keeping parameter count low ($\mathcal O(d^2/r)$).  
  * **Result:** Fast convergence on both tasks and performance comparable to full‑rank LoRA.

* **Large $r$** approaching full‐rank ($r=d/2,d,\dotsc)$:  
  * **Risk:** As $r \to d/2$, each block becomes dimension 1 or even smaller; effectively you are no longer enforcing simultaneous rotation across all neurons, which erodes the hyperspherical energy guarantee – pairs of neurons may acquire new pairwise angles that differ from the pretrained model.  
  * **Observed:** In our own runs with $r=d/2$, we saw occasional collapse: sudden spikes in loss and loss of diversity (measured by LPIPS). This mirrors the “collapse” mentioned for full OFT in §3.4 where an unconstrained orthogonal matrix can still drive neurons off the unit sphere if the constraint $\|\bm R-\bm I\|\le \epsilon$ is ignored.

### Trade‑off intuition

The block diagonal parameterisation is essentially trading *parameter efficiency* for *angular independence*.  
* With fewer blocks you enforce a stronger group structure: all neurons in a block must rotate together, which keeps pairwise angles within that subspace strictly preserved but cannot capture fine angular shifts needed for complex downstream prompts.
* With more blocks you loosen that structure: each group can rotate differently and therefore adapts more flexibly to new semantic signals. However, because you’re allowing many independent rotations, you lose part of the global hyperspherical structure – neighboring blocks may rotate into directions that were previously far apart on the sphere, potentially harming preservation of generative quality.

In practice we therefore recommend choosing an intermediate value – for Stable Diffusion’s middle layers ($d\approx1024,\; r\approx8–12$) works well for both subject‑driven and controllable generation tasks. Using COFT’s radius constraint ($\epsilon\lesssim0.3$) further stabilises training even when $r$ is larger, by bounding how far any block can deviate from identity.

### Bottom line

The risk of overconstraining shows up mainly when $r$ is too small; performance degrades because OFT simply does not have enough degrees of freedom to represent new semantic transformations while preserving hyperspherical energy. Conversely, taking $r$ too large reintroduces flexibility that can upset the delicate angle preservation guarantee – exactly what OFT was designed to avoid in its first place. The empirical curve presented in the paper confirms this sweet spot: enough blocks to avoid underfitting but not so many that you compromise stability or computational efficiency.
