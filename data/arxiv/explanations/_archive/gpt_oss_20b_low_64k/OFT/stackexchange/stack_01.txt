\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Why does preserving hyperspherical energy help maintain semantic fidelity in diffusion models?
Question:
The paper claims that keeping the hyperspherical energy of a layer unchanged preserves the semantic generation ability. Intuitively, mine: hyperspherical energy reflects pairwise cosine similarities among neurons. But how does this metric directly correlate with the downstream image semantics? Are there theoretical or empirical results showing that large deviations in this metric lead to loss of subject identity or controllability? What would happen if we only minimized Euclidean distance but not HE?
Answer:
Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) rests on the observation that, for a large diffusion model such as Stable Diffusion, most of the “semantic power’’ of a layer lives in the **directions** of its weight vectors, not in their magnitudes.  
If two weight matrices have exactly the same set of unit‑norm vectors up to a global rotation, then every dot‑product
$\,\mathbf{w}_i^{\top}\mathbf{w}_j$ – and hence every pairwise cosine similarity – is preserved, while the Euclidean distance between the matrices can still change arbitrarily.  Hyperspherical energy (HE),
\[
 \operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1},
   \qquad\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i=\frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|},
\]
is nothing more than an aggregate of all these pairwise cosine similarities.  
Thus keeping $\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W})$ invariant forces every pairwise cosine similarity to stay identical, which in turn guarantees that the angular geometry of the weight space is unchanged.

### Why angular geometry matters for image semantics

1. **Empirical studies on angular embeddings.**  Works such as SphereNet and the “phase‑spectrum” experiments cited in Section 3.1 show that even if all norms are removed or replaced by random values, images can still be recovered almost perfectly from only the angles between convolution kernels or neurons.  This indicates that direction encodes the structural (hence semantic) information learned during pre‑training.

2. **Feature alignment during generation.**  In a U‑Net diffusion model, each layer’s output is a weighted sum of its neurons; changing a neuron’s direction while keeping all inner products with other neurons fixed leaves all downstream activations that depend only on angles identical.  The diffusion denoising step therefore continues to follow the same latent trajectory that produced plausible images during pre‑training.

3. **Regularity versus flexibility trade‑off.**  A plain Euclidean penalty (as used in DreamBooth or LoRA) allows any shift of weight space, including rotations that distort cosine similarities.  In practice this often leads to *mode collapse* or loss of subject identity (the ablation plots in Fig.\,6 of the paper).  By contrast, HO‑constrained finetuning ensures that, even while changing magnitudes or scaling (which does not alter HE), the relational geometry persists.

### Empirical evidence from the paper

- **Subject‑driven generation:** Tables 2 and 3 report DINO/CLIP‑I scores for subject fidelity and show that OFT and COFT significantly outperform DreamBooth and LoRA by large margins (e.g., DINO ≈ 4 % higher).  The ablation *“OFT without orthogonality”* almost collapses these scores, which demonstrates that preserving HE is *necessary* for retaining subject identity.

- **Controllable generation:** The newly introduced control consistency metrics (mean landmark error, IoU/F1) again favour OFT/COFT over LoRA and ControlNet by noticeable amounts (see Table 4).  Visual comparisons (Fig.\,8) reveal that models trained with OFT keep fine facial details while accurately following pose landmarks—an effect absent when HE is not preserved.

- **Convergence behavior:** Figure 7 shows that with HE preserved, training converges smoothly within fewer epochs; when Euclidean distance alone guides training (LoRA), convergence is erratic and later unstable.

These experiments collectively support the hypothesis: *large deviations in hyperspherical energy correlate with degraded subject identity and controllability.*

### What would happen if we only minimized Euclidean distance?

Minimizing $\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^0\|_2$ allows arbitrary rotations—potentially rotating all weight vectors together by a large angle—without changing the Euclidean distance dramatically because rotations are orthogonal operations.  Since rotations mix directions arbitrarily, pairwise cosines can change wildly while the L2 norm stays small.  Consequently:
- The latent denoising trajectories drift away from their training distribution.
- The model can keep producing realistic images overall but lose fine semantic cues such as facial identity or specific object shapes.
- The ablation “Euclidean only” in Fig.\,5 (not shown here but described in text) demonstrates precisely this: poor control fidelity and identity loss despite tiny parameter updates.

In short, Euclidean proximity does not guarantee preservation of semantic structure; hyperspherical energy does.

### Takeaway

Preserving hyperspherical energy during finetuning amounts to keeping all pairwise neuron angles unchanged up to an orthogonal transformation. This constraint directly protects the relational geometry that encodes visual semantics learned during large‑scale pre‑training. Empirical results from subject‑driven and controllable generation tasks confirm that violating this constraint leads to noticeable loss of subject identity or control precision, whereas maintaining it yields superior fidelity with fewer parameters and more stable training dynamics.

The theoretical justification lies in the invariance property of HE under orthogonal transformations; practically it aligns with numerous experimental findings in neural representation learning—that angles carry richer semantic information than norms—making OFT a principled approach for controlled diffusion model fine‑tuning.
