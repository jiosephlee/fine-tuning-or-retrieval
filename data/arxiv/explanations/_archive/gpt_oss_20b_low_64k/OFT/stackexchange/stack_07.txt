\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### How does OFT preserve pretraining knowledge while providing strong control signals?
Question:
When training on subject‑driven tasks, control signals are hard; yet OFT seems to keep both semantic consistency and prompt fidelity high. Is this because the orthogonal transform allows re‑orientation of neurons without changing relative geometry? How would you justify that a rotation of neurons suffices to encode new subject information while retaining prior proficiency?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
OFT preserves the knowledge learned during pre‑training because it only *rotates* (or reflects) the set of neuron weight vectors in each layer, not adding, removing or substantially rescaling them.  
A rotation is an isometry of the unit hypersphere: it keeps all pair‑wise angles and therefore keeps the *hyperspherical energy* of the layer exactly the same as in the pre‑trained model.  Because the semantic information encoded in diffusion models mainly lives in those angles (as shown by the toy auto‑encoder experiment and by the success of SphereNet), a pure rotation can modify what the model generates for a new task while leaving its overall semantic structure untouched.  The additional radius constraint of COFT guarantees that the rotated vectors do not drift too far from their original positions on the sphere, making fine‑tuning even more stable.

---

### 1.  Why a rotation alone can add a subject

A diffusion model can be viewed as a collection of linear mappings followed by nonlinearities.  
Let $\mathbf W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}$ be a pre‑trained weight matrix whose columns $\{\hat{\mathbf w}^0_i\}$ lie on a unit sphere.  
The output of this layer on input $\mathbf x$ is
\[
\mathbf z=(\mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf x.
\]
During fine‑tuning we replace $\mathbf W^{0}$ with
\[
\mathbf W=\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0},
\]
where $\mathbf R\in O(d)$ is an orthogonal matrix learned for that layer.

- **Preservation of pair‑wise angles**  
  For any two neurons,
  \[
  \hat{\mathbf w}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf w}_j
    =({\mathbf R}\hat{\mathbf w}^0_i)^\top({\mathbf R}\hat{\mathbf w}^0_j)
    =(\hat{\mathbf w}^0_i)^\top\hat{\mathbf w}^0_j ,
  \]
  because $\mathbf R^\top \mathbf R=\mathbf I$.  
  Thus every cosine similarity—and hence every cosine distance—between neurons stays identical.  
  Hyperspherical energy, defined as a sum over these cosines, is unchanged.

- **Semantic impact is limited to direction**  
  The “phase” between an input vector and a neuron (its direction on the sphere) carries most of the semantic information (cf. §3.1 toy AE experiment and the phase‑vs‑magnitude intuition from Fourier analysis).  
  By rotating all neurons simultaneously we change this phase uniformly across the whole layer, which is exactly what is needed to shift a diffusion model from generating generic scenes to generating images that adhere to a new subject or control signal.

- **No scaling or amplitude changes**  
  Because we multiply by an orthogonal matrix, norms are preserved ($\|\mathbf R\,\hat{\mathbf w}^0_i\|=\|\hat{\mathbf w}^0_i\|=1$).  
  Thus we avoid altering feature magnitudes, which would break remaining layers that have been trained under a specific scale distribution.

### 2.  Flexibility vs. stability

A pure rotation leaves every neuron *exactly* where it has been positioned on the sphere up to orientation.  
If we were to apply an arbitrary small perturbation instead, we would still preserve closeness in Euclidean space but might break many angle constraints—leading to catastrophic forgetting or mode collapse.

The constrained version (COFT) imposes
\[
\|\mathbf R-\mathbf I\|\leq \epsilon.
\]
In essence this bounds how far any neuron can be rotated away from its original direction; typically $\epsilon=0.2-0.4$.  
This extra slack keeps the fine‑tuned model “close” to its pre‑trained counterpart in both Euclidean and angular senses, yielding extra robustness without sacrificing expressivity.

### 3.  Orthogonal transformation as “phase” tuning

Fourier analysis teaches us that *phases* carry high‑level structure while *magnitudes* encode low‑level detail; perceptual changes mostly come from shifting phase while keeping magnitude fixed.  
The same principle applies to deep layers: registering each neuron as a point on a unit sphere positions each as a “phase vector”.  
Rotating these vectors changes how they interact with future layers’ dot products—exactly what enables OFT to introduce new subject semantics while still re‑using almost all existing weights.

### 4.  Relating intuition back to experiments

- **Subject–driven generation** – OFT learns an orthogonal matrix per attention block that reorients attention directions so that they attend more strongly to features belonging to the unseen subject images; because angles remain consistent with pre‑trained weights, other visual concepts stay intact.
- **Control–based generation** – OFT learns rotations that tilt features toward those corresponding to control signals (edges, masks); again only directional relationships shift while overall geometry is preserved.
- **Faster convergence** – Optimizing over rotations lies on a smooth manifold (the orthogonal group), which makes gradient descent easier than optimizing arbitrary weight perturbations.
- **Better stability** – Empirical ablations show OFT/COFT do not collapse even after thousands of updates, whereas vanilla finetuning often breaks identity or control signals.

---

### Bottom line

`OFT` keeps pre‑training knowledge by **exactly preserving neuron pairwise angles** via an *orthogonal* transformation—so relative geometry stays unchanged—and by additionally bounding how far any neuron rotates via COFT’s radius constraint. This combination gives both sufficient flexibility to encode new subjects or controls and enough regularization that semantic consistency and prompt fidelity remain high even when training data or labels are scarce.)
