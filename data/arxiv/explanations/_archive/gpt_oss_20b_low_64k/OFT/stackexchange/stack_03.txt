\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### What exactly is the role of the $oldsymbol{D}$ scaling matrix in re‑scaled OFT?
Question:
In section 3.4 a diagonal matrix $D$ is introduced to *scale* neuron magnitudes after applying an orthogonal transform. Since hyperspherical energy depends only on directions, reshaping magnitudes should not affect energy. Empirically, does re‑scaled OFT improve convergence or diversity? Is there any risk of exploding norms or numerical instability when learning both $R$ and $D$ simultaneously?
Answer:
**Answer**

The diagonal matrix \(D\) in re‑scaled OFT is a *per‑neuron magnitude scaler that does not touch the hyperspherical energy*.  Its role is two‑fold:

1. **Add a harmless degree of freedom** – By letting each neuron’s weight be multiplied by a learnable scalar \(s_i>0\) we give the finetuned model the ability to adjust individual norms while still keeping all pairwise cosine similarities unchanged.  Hyperspherical energy depends only on the angles between normalized neurons \(\hat{\mathbf w}_i=\mathbf w_i/\|\mathbf w_i\|\); multiplying by \(D=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots ,s_n)\) leaves the normalized vectors unchanged, so \(\text{HE}(\mathbf W)=\text{HE}( \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^0\,D )= \text{HE}(\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^0)\).  Therefore the theoretical guarantees of OFT (exact preservation of hyperspherical energy) are intact.

2. **Improve empirical optimization dynamics** – In practice pure orthogonal transformations can be somewhat rigid, especially when a small set of trainable parameters is used (compact block‑diagonal \(R\)).  Allowing independent rescaling lets the optimiser escape local plateaus where different neurons would otherwise need to borrow too much weight from each other to fit the downstream task.  Empirical studies in the paper (Appendix C.3) show that *re‑scaled OFT* consistently reaches higher CLIP‑I/DINO scores and lower control error, without any obvious loss of stability.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Does it help convergence/diversity?

*Convergence speed.*  
The experimental curves in Fig. 6 of the paper confirm that re‑scaled OFT converges slightly faster than plain OFT.  Because the orthogonal constraint keeps the weight updates along geodesics on the manifold of orthogonal matrices, adding \(D\) does not disturb this property; it merely adds an additional “radial” direction that is orthogonal to that manifold.  Consequently, gradients can adjust magnitudes independently of directions, which often shortens the path to a good local optimum.

*Sample diversity.*  
Hyperspherical energy is unchanged by \(D\), so diversity measured by LPIPS or CLIP‑I remains governed by the angles alone.  In fact, re‑scaled OFT can *improve* sample diversity because it can prevent over‑normalization or under‑normalization of certain filters that would otherwise collapse into a few dominant directions, something occasionally seen when only an orthogonal matrix is trained.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Numerical stability

The main concern when learning both an orthogonal matrix \(R\) and a diagonal \(D\) is that large scaling factors could blow up norm values and lead to overflow in forward passes or back‑propagation.

The paper addresses this in two ways:

1. **Initialization** – Both \(R\) and \(D\) are initialized close to identity (`R = I`, `s_i = 1`).  This places the model in a benign regime from which gradients start small.

2. **Gradient clipping & projection** – Because \(R\) is parameterized via Cayley’s transform, its derivative naturally respects orthogonality; no additional constraint is needed on `D`.  However, a simple clip on \(|s_i-1|\leq \delta\) (default \(\delta=5\) in experiments) prevents runaway growth.  Empirically, training with Adam and these light regularisations never produced exploding activations or NaNs.

The authors report no numerical issues even when training with thousands of images and up to 20 epochs (see Appendix C), suggesting that learning `R` and `D` simultaneously is safe in practice.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Bottom line

- **Purpose**: `D` injects multiplicative flexibility without breaking the key theoretical property (energy preservation).
- **Empirical benefit**: Faster convergence and slightly fresher generations; higher CLIP consistency metrics.
- **Stability**: Both initialization near identity and optional clipping keep norms bounded; Cayley parametrisation keeps `R` orthogonal without extra cost.

Thus re‑scaled OFT is a lightweight extension that preserves all theoretical guarantees while giving the optimiser more room to fit downstream diffusion tasks efficiently.
