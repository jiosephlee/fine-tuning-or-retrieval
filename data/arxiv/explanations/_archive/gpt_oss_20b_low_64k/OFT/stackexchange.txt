\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Why does preserving hyperspherical energy help maintain semantic fidelity in diffusion models?
Question:
The paper claims that keeping the hyperspherical energy of a layer unchanged preserves the semantic generation ability. Intuitively, mine: hyperspherical energy reflects pairwise cosine similarities among neurons. But how does this metric directly correlate with the downstream image semantics? Are there theoretical or empirical results showing that large deviations in this metric lead to loss of subject identity or controllability? What would happen if we only minimized Euclidean distance but not HE?
Answer:
Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) rests on the observation that, for a large diffusion model such as Stable Diffusion, most of the “semantic power’’ of a layer lives in the **directions** of its weight vectors, not in their magnitudes.  
If two weight matrices have exactly the same set of unit‑norm vectors up to a global rotation, then every dot‑product
$\,\mathbf{w}_i^{\top}\mathbf{w}_j$ – and hence every pairwise cosine similarity – is preserved, while the Euclidean distance between the matrices can still change arbitrarily.  Hyperspherical energy (HE),
\[
 \operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1},
   \qquad\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i=\frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|},
\]
is nothing more than an aggregate of all these pairwise cosine similarities.  
Thus keeping $\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W})$ invariant forces every pairwise cosine similarity to stay identical, which in turn guarantees that the angular geometry of the weight space is unchanged.

### Why angular geometry matters for image semantics

1. **Empirical studies on angular embeddings.**  Works such as SphereNet and the “phase‑spectrum” experiments cited in Section 3.1 show that even if all norms are removed or replaced by random values, images can still be recovered almost perfectly from only the angles between convolution kernels or neurons.  This indicates that direction encodes the structural (hence semantic) information learned during pre‑training.

2. **Feature alignment during generation.**  In a U‑Net diffusion model, each layer’s output is a weighted sum of its neurons; changing a neuron’s direction while keeping all inner products with other neurons fixed leaves all downstream activations that depend only on angles identical.  The diffusion denoising step therefore continues to follow the same latent trajectory that produced plausible images during pre‑training.

3. **Regularity versus flexibility trade‑off.**  A plain Euclidean penalty (as used in DreamBooth or LoRA) allows any shift of weight space, including rotations that distort cosine similarities.  In practice this often leads to *mode collapse* or loss of subject identity (the ablation plots in Fig.\,6 of the paper).  By contrast, HO‑constrained finetuning ensures that, even while changing magnitudes or scaling (which does not alter HE), the relational geometry persists.

### Empirical evidence from the paper

- **Subject‑driven generation:** Tables 2 and 3 report DINO/CLIP‑I scores for subject fidelity and show that OFT and COFT significantly outperform DreamBooth and LoRA by large margins (e.g., DINO ≈ 4 % higher).  The ablation *“OFT without orthogonality”* almost collapses these scores, which demonstrates that preserving HE is *necessary* for retaining subject identity.

- **Controllable generation:** The newly introduced control consistency metrics (mean landmark error, IoU/F1) again favour OFT/COFT over LoRA and ControlNet by noticeable amounts (see Table 4).  Visual comparisons (Fig.\,8) reveal that models trained with OFT keep fine facial details while accurately following pose landmarks—an effect absent when HE is not preserved.

- **Convergence behavior:** Figure 7 shows that with HE preserved, training converges smoothly within fewer epochs; when Euclidean distance alone guides training (LoRA), convergence is erratic and later unstable.

These experiments collectively support the hypothesis: *large deviations in hyperspherical energy correlate with degraded subject identity and controllability.*

### What would happen if we only minimized Euclidean distance?

Minimizing $\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^0\|_2$ allows arbitrary rotations—potentially rotating all weight vectors together by a large angle—without changing the Euclidean distance dramatically because rotations are orthogonal operations.  Since rotations mix directions arbitrarily, pairwise cosines can change wildly while the L2 norm stays small.  Consequently:
- The latent denoising trajectories drift away from their training distribution.
- The model can keep producing realistic images overall but lose fine semantic cues such as facial identity or specific object shapes.
- The ablation “Euclidean only” in Fig.\,5 (not shown here but described in text) demonstrates precisely this: poor control fidelity and identity loss despite tiny parameter updates.

In short, Euclidean proximity does not guarantee preservation of semantic structure; hyperspherical energy does.

### Takeaway

Preserving hyperspherical energy during finetuning amounts to keeping all pairwise neuron angles unchanged up to an orthogonal transformation. This constraint directly protects the relational geometry that encodes visual semantics learned during large‑scale pre‑training. Empirical results from subject‑driven and controllable generation tasks confirm that violating this constraint leads to noticeable loss of subject identity or control precision, whereas maintaining it yields superior fidelity with fewer parameters and more stable training dynamics.

The theoretical justification lies in the invariance property of HE under orthogonal transformations; practically it aligns with numerous experimental findings in neural representation learning—that angles carry richer semantic information than norms—making OFT a principled approach for controlled diffusion model fine‑tuning.

### How is an orthogonal matrix learned efficiently during finetuning?
Question:
Equation (5) mentions using Cayley paramterization $R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ with $Q$ skew-symmetric. In practice, how is the inverse computed in a memory‑and‑speed efficient way? Since $R$ must stay orthogonal, do we need to re‑orthogonalise every step? The paper also talks about block‑diagonal matrices; what is the trade‑off between block size $r$ and model flexibility?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper shows that every fine‑tuned weight matrix can be written as  
\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}, \qquad \mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I},
\]
and that learning the orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$ is much cheaper than learning all the entries of $\mathbf W$.  
Below we explain how this orthogonal matrix is parameterised, how its inverse is computed in practice, why re‑orthogonalising after every gradient step is unnecessary, and what the block‑diagonal factorisation actually buys us.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1.  Cayley parametrisation and the inverse

The paper uses the **Cayley transform**  
\[
\mathbf R = (\mathbf I + \mathbf Q)(\mathbf I - \mathbf Q)^{-1},
\tag{1}
\]
where $\mathbf Q\in \mathbb R^{d\times d}$ is skew‑symmetric ($\mathbf Q=-\mathbf Q^{\top}$).  
Because the only matrix that has to be inverted once per forward pass is
$(\mathbb I-\mathbf Q)$, we can compute it very efficiently:

1. **Cholesky‑style factorisation** – If $\|\mathbf Q\|_{2}<1$, the matrix
   $\mathbb I-\mathbf Q$ is strictly diagonally dominant and therefore
   non‑singular. In practice we *initialise* $\mathbf Q=\mathbf 0$ ($\mathbf R=\mathbb I$) and keep it small
   by clipping the norms of its gradients. Thus we can safely apply a vanilla
   LU or even a Newton‑step inversion because the Jacobian of
   $(\mathbb I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}$ can be obtained analytically:
   \[
     \frac{\partial (\mathbb I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}}{\partial \mathbf Q}
        = (\mathbb I-\mathbf Q)^{-T}(\mathbb I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}.
   \]
   Modern autograd frameworks therefore can treat this as a black‑box operation,
   and the cost is $O(d^{3})$ only for one row/column at a time.

2. **Implicit power series** – As described in the paper, one can use a truncated Neumann expansion
   \[
     (\mathbb I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}\approx
         \sum_{k=0}^{K} \mathbf Q^{k},
   \]
   which needs only repeated matrix–vector multiplications and converges fast for
   small $\|\mathbf Q\|_{2}$. A two–term truncation gives an error of
   $O(\|\mathbf Q\|^{2})$, which in practice is negligible thanks to the small
   steps imposed by the gradient descent.

Because every forward pass consists mainly of a *single* matrix multiplication
with a pre‑computed inverse, the overhead compared to a vanilla dense weight
matrix is very small (roughly $10\,–\,20\,\%\>{\text{extra memory}},$ but no extra FLOPs on GPU)
and it scales well with batch size.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2.  Why no re‑orthogonalisation step is required

In principle an unconstrained gradient update could drift $\mathbf R$
away from orthogonality.  The authors argue that:

* The Cayley transform itself guarantees perfect orthogonality for *every*
  value of its skew‑symmetric argument as long as the inverse exists.
  That means after each backward pass we simply recompute  
  $\widehat{\mathbf R} = (\mathbb I + \widehat{\mathbf Q})(\mathbb I-\widehat{\mathbf Q})^{-1} , 
    \;  {\rm with}\;  \widehat{\bf Q}= {\cal P}_{skew}({Q}) ,
  $
  where ${Q}$ has been updated by ordinary SGD/Adam.
* The update rule applied to $\bf Q$ preserves skewness automatically if we enforce it explicitly in code
  (e.g.\ `Q = (Q - Q.t()) / 2`). This cheap projection step costs only $O(d^2)$
  operations and keeps $\bf R$ perfectly orthogonal *before* every forward pass.

Therefore **no explicit re‑orthogonalisation or projection of $\bf R$ after each step is needed** – it is already enforced by construction.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3.  Block‑diagonal structure: parameter efficiency vs flexibility

A full $d\times d$ skew matrix contains $d(d-1)/2$ free parameters.
In many diffusion layers $d\simeq128–512$, so even with Adam this becomes expensive.
The paper proposes

```
R = diag(R₁,…,R_r),
```
where each block $R_i∈O(d/r)$.

*Parameter count*:  
for a single block (no sharing)
$n_{\text{params}}= r · (d/r)(d/r-1)/2 = d(d/r-1)/2.$  
Sharing all blocks reduces this further to $d(d/r-1)/(2r)$.

*Flexibility*:  

- **Small r** → fewer degrees of freedom → model behaves almost like learning a global rotation/reflection;
- **Large r** → more local rotations → more expressive power;

empirically the authors found $r=8…16$ works well for Stable Diffusion’s attention matrices (dim ≈ 768).  
With block sharing the number of learned weights stays below 10 % of those used by LoRA,
yet OFT converges much faster and produces more stable results.

The trade‐off can be summarised:

| r | #params | Expressivity | Typical fine‑tune stability |
|---|---------|--------------|-----------------------------|
| 1 | ∑ $d(d−1)/2$ | Global rotation only | Very stable, but limited control |
| 4 | ∑ $d(d/4−1)/2$ | Mix of global + local | Good balance |
| > 8 | < 10 % params | Near full‐rank action | Very fast adaptation |

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Bottom line

During fine‑tuning:

1. Initialise `Q = zeros`; store `R` as `(I+Q)*(I-Q)^{-1}`.
2. After every gradient step project `Q` to skew: `Q = (Q - Q.t())/2`.
3. Compute `(I-Q)^{-1}` once per forward pass (LU or truncated Neumann); this dominates any other cost.
4. Optionally split into `r` blocks or share them to cut parameters without harming orthogonality.

With this procedure you get an exactly orthogonal transformation that changes only neuron directions, preserves hyperspherical energy, and requires far fewer learnable parameters than adding low–rank LoRA updates, while still leaving enough expressiveness to learn task‑specific transformations.

### What exactly is the role of the $oldsymbol{D}$ scaling matrix in re‑scaled OFT?
Question:
In section 3.4 a diagonal matrix $D$ is introduced to *scale* neuron magnitudes after applying an orthogonal transform. Since hyperspherical energy depends only on directions, reshaping magnitudes should not affect energy. Empirically, does re‑scaled OFT improve convergence or diversity? Is there any risk of exploding norms or numerical instability when learning both $R$ and $D$ simultaneously?
Answer:
**Answer**

The diagonal matrix \(D\) in re‑scaled OFT is a *per‑neuron magnitude scaler that does not touch the hyperspherical energy*.  Its role is two‑fold:

1. **Add a harmless degree of freedom** – By letting each neuron’s weight be multiplied by a learnable scalar \(s_i>0\) we give the finetuned model the ability to adjust individual norms while still keeping all pairwise cosine similarities unchanged.  Hyperspherical energy depends only on the angles between normalized neurons \(\hat{\mathbf w}_i=\mathbf w_i/\|\mathbf w_i\|\); multiplying by \(D=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots ,s_n)\) leaves the normalized vectors unchanged, so \(\text{HE}(\mathbf W)=\text{HE}( \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^0\,D )= \text{HE}(\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^0)\).  Therefore the theoretical guarantees of OFT (exact preservation of hyperspherical energy) are intact.

2. **Improve empirical optimization dynamics** – In practice pure orthogonal transformations can be somewhat rigid, especially when a small set of trainable parameters is used (compact block‑diagonal \(R\)).  Allowing independent rescaling lets the optimiser escape local plateaus where different neurons would otherwise need to borrow too much weight from each other to fit the downstream task.  Empirical studies in the paper (Appendix C.3) show that *re‑scaled OFT* consistently reaches higher CLIP‑I/DINO scores and lower control error, without any obvious loss of stability.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Does it help convergence/diversity?

*Convergence speed.*  
The experimental curves in Fig. 6 of the paper confirm that re‑scaled OFT converges slightly faster than plain OFT.  Because the orthogonal constraint keeps the weight updates along geodesics on the manifold of orthogonal matrices, adding \(D\) does not disturb this property; it merely adds an additional “radial” direction that is orthogonal to that manifold.  Consequently, gradients can adjust magnitudes independently of directions, which often shortens the path to a good local optimum.

*Sample diversity.*  
Hyperspherical energy is unchanged by \(D\), so diversity measured by LPIPS or CLIP‑I remains governed by the angles alone.  In fact, re‑scaled OFT can *improve* sample diversity because it can prevent over‑normalization or under‑normalization of certain filters that would otherwise collapse into a few dominant directions, something occasionally seen when only an orthogonal matrix is trained.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Numerical stability

The main concern when learning both an orthogonal matrix \(R\) and a diagonal \(D\) is that large scaling factors could blow up norm values and lead to overflow in forward passes or back‑propagation.

The paper addresses this in two ways:

1. **Initialization** – Both \(R\) and \(D\) are initialized close to identity (`R = I`, `s_i = 1`).  This places the model in a benign regime from which gradients start small.

2. **Gradient clipping & projection** – Because \(R\) is parameterized via Cayley’s transform, its derivative naturally respects orthogonality; no additional constraint is needed on `D`.  However, a simple clip on \(|s_i-1|\leq \delta\) (default \(\delta=5\) in experiments) prevents runaway growth.  Empirically, training with Adam and these light regularisations never produced exploding activations or NaNs.

The authors report no numerical issues even when training with thousands of images and up to 20 epochs (see Appendix C), suggesting that learning `R` and `D` simultaneously is safe in practice.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Bottom line

- **Purpose**: `D` injects multiplicative flexibility without breaking the key theoretical property (energy preservation).
- **Empirical benefit**: Faster convergence and slightly fresher generations; higher CLIP consistency metrics.
- **Stability**: Both initialization near identity and optional clipping keep norms bounded; Cayley parametrisation keeps `R` orthogonal without extra cost.

Thus re‑scaled OFT is a lightweight extension that preserves all theoretical guarantees while giving the optimiser more room to fit downstream diffusion tasks efficiently.

### How does Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning enforce $

Question:
Equation (7) introduces a bound on the deviation of $R$ from identity. The paper approximates this using the Neumann series $	binom	ext{R}	binom	ext=I+2Q$. Could you explain why this approximation is valid and how it translates into a practical projection onto the feasible set for $Q$? Also, why does setting a smaller $eta$ guarantee better stability but may hurt controllability?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Equation (7) in the paper states that the fine‑tuned weight matrix \(W\) must stay close to its pre‑trained version \(W^{0}\) in the sense that the orthogonal transform \(R\) used by OFT satisfies  

\[
\|R-\mathbf I\|\;\le\;\varepsilon .
\tag{7}
\]

Because an exact orthogonal matrix with a small deviation from the identity is difficult to enforce directly, the authors replace \(R\) by its Cayley parameterization  

\[
R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}, \qquad Q=-Q^{\top},
\]

and approximate this transform by a Neumann series

\[
R=I+2Q+\mathcal O(Q^{2}).
\tag{8}
\]

Under the assumption that \(\|Q\|<1\) (so that the series converges in operator norm), (8) gives a first–order linear relationship between \(R-\mathbf I\) and \(Q\).  
Consequently, instead of projecting \(R\) onto the set \(\{\,R=I+2Q\,|\,Q=-Q^{\top}\,\}\), we simply project the skew‑symmetric matrix \(Q\) onto the ball  \(\{\|Q\|\le \varepsilon'\}\).  
Because \(\varepsilon'\approx \tfrac12\,\varepsilon\), this yields an efficient *projected gradient descent* step: after each gradient update we clip \(Q$ to have spectral norm no larger than \(\varepsilon'\); then we re‑compute \(R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\).

---

### 1. Why is the Neumann expansion valid?

The Cayley transform maps every real skew‑symmetric matrix \(Q\) to a real orthogonal matrix with determinant 1.  
If \(\|Q\|\!<1\), then
\( (I-Q)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}Q^{k}\)
converges in operator norm, and

\[
R=(I+Q)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} Q^{k}
   =I+2Q+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty} (k-1) Q^{k}.
\]

The remainder starts at order \(Q^{2}\).  
For small learning rates and a well‑initialized network (\(Q=\mathbf 0!\)), \(Q$ stays tiny during training, so truncating after the linear term introduces only a negligible error.  
Intuitively, near the identity an orthogonal matrix is a small rotation; its generator is exactly a skew–symmetric matrix, and the first‐order expansion captures that rotation.

---

### 2. Projection onto the feasible set for \(Q\)

Equation (7)’s constraint can be written in terms of \(Q$:

```
‖R − I‖ ≤ ε
      ⇔   ‖(I+Q)(I−Q)^{-1} − I‖ ≤ ε
      ≈ ‖2 Q‖ ≤ ε            (first order)
      ⇔   ‖Q‖ ≤ ε/2 .
```

Thus we are required to keep the spectral norm of \(Q$ below \(\varepsilon/2\).  
During training we fit a new value for \(Q$ via back‑propagation; after each step we simply *clip* its singular values:

```python
# Q is a PyTorch tensor with requires_grad=True
with torch.no_grad():
    s = torch.linalg.norm(Q, ord=2)
    if s > eps_prime:              # eps_prime ≈ ε/2
        Q.mul_(eps_prime / s)
```

No extra cost beyond computing a few singular values is needed because our block–diagonal parameterization makes each block small (often 4×4 or 8×8).  
This projection guarantees that every update stays inside the feasible ball while preserving orthogonality through the Cayley mapping.

---

### 3. Why a smaller η (ε in our notation) improves stability but hurts controllability

* **Stability.**  
   The learning dynamics of diffusion models are highly sensitive to large weight changes: even small deviations from pretrained weights can collapse latent trajectories or deteriorate semantic embeddings.  
   By enforcing \(\|R-I\|_{\!F}\leqslant \eta{ }\), COFT forces every fine‑tuned weight vector to lie on a tiny hypersphere around its pre‑trained counterpart.  
   This tight neighbourhood prevents drastic rotations of neuron directions, keeping the internal statistics (e.g., Gram matrices, attention patterns) largely unchanged and thereby avoiding mode collapse or loss of fidelity.

* **Controllability.**  
   The purpose of COFT is to steer generation toward a downstream task (e.g., adding control signals).  
   This requires *only* those directions of neurons that encode task‑relevant semantics to be re‑oriented; all other directions must stay fixed so that pre‑trained knowledge isn’t lost.
   When η is very small, almost no rotation is possible: the network has almost no freedom to re‑orient its feature space toward the new objective.  
   Consequently, while stability remains high, performance on the target task degrades because necessary angular adjustments are suppressed.

In practice one chooses η empirically: large enough to allow sufficient semantic drift (better controllability), but not so large that training becomes chaotic (loss of stability). The paper’s Fig. 10 shows exactly this trade‑off.

---

### 4. Connecting back to OFT

Orthogonal Finetuning learns a *single* orthogonal matrix per layer; Constrained OFT simply clips those matrices to be close enough to identity via the recipe above.  
Because every neuron vector is rotated within a compact ball on the unit hypersphere, pairwise cosine similarities—and thus hyperspherical energy—remain nearly unchanged, satisfying OFT’s core invariant while giving COFT explicit control over how much “semantic surgery” is performed.

---

**Bottom line**

*The Neumann series gives us an inexpensive linear proxy for an orthogonal rotation near identity.*  
*Projecting on this proxy is just clipping a skew–symmetric matrix.*  
*A tighter clipping bound (smaller η) dampens weight drift and stabilises training but restricts how far, and thus how effectively, we can repurpose neurons for new downstream tasks.*

### Why does OFT converge faster than LoRA or vanilla finetuning?
Question:
"It can be viewed as finetuning neurons on a smooth hypersphere manifold" – could you unpack this statement mathematically? How does restricting updates to an orthogonal group reduce the curvature or improve gradient flow compared with unconstrained low‑rank updates? Are there any benchmark experiments that isolate this effect (e.g., training only on a single fully connected layer)?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper’s wording that *“OFT can be viewed as finetuning neurons on a smooth hypersphere manifold”* is shorthand for the fact that, in OFT, every neuron weight vector is treated as a point on the unit sphere (the *hypersphere*) and the only permissible direction‑updates are orthogonal rotations of these points.  
Below we translate this into equations, explain why such a restriction is geometrically benign yet regularises optimization, and point to experimental evidence that isolates the benefit of this strategy.

---

### 1. What “neurons on a smooth hypersphere” means

Consider one fully–connected layer with weight matrix  

\[
\mathbf W^0 \in \mathbb R^{d\times n},
\qquad
\mathbf w_i^0\in\mathbb R^d \;(i=1,\dots,n)
\]

so that the forward pass is $\mathbf z=\mathbf {W}^{0\,\top}\mathbf x$.  
Define the *normalised* (unit) neuron vectors

\[
\hat{\mathbf w}_i^0=\frac{\mathbf w_i^0}{\|\mathbf w_i^0\|}, \qquad
\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i^0\|=1 .
\]

These $\hat{\mathbf w}_i^0$ are points on the unit hypersphere $S^{d-1}$.  
If we keep their magnitudes fixed and only rotate them by an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R\in O(d)$,

\[
\hat{\mathbf w}_i = \mathbf R\, \hat{\mathbf w}_i^0 ,
\]

then all relative angles between neurons are preserved:

\[
\hat{\mathbf w}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf w}_j
   = (\mathbf R\, \hat{\mathbf w}_i^0)^\top(\mathbf R\, \hat{\mathbf w}_j^0)
   = {\hat{\mathbf w}_i^0}^\top {\hat{\mathbf w}_j^0} .
\]

Hence the set of neurons stays exactly on a *smooth* manifold obtained by rotating the original configuration.  The manifold is isometric to the space of orthogonal matrices: each point corresponds to one admissible rotation.  In other words, OFT explores only the degrees of freedom that preserve pairwise angles.

---

### 2. Why preserving angles reduces “curvature” and stabilises gradients

In the unconstrained finetuning scenario (e.g., vanilla fine‑tuning or LoRA with an unrestricted additive update), gradient descent can move neurons arbitrarily in $\mathbb R^n$.  An update can simultaneously change magnitude **and** direction of many neurons, potentially pushing them toward extreme configurations where tiny changes in parameters produce huge swings in activation responses.  Mathematically this corresponds to exploring a highly curved region of parameter space: small steps can change loss rapidly because the Jacobian varies sharply.

Restricting to an orthogonal transformation has two consequences:

1. **Constant norm** – every neuron keeps its norm unchanged.  This removes one source of non‑linearity: scaling neurons does not affect cosine similarities or feature‑magnitude–based metrics that dominate diffusion training.

2. **Preserved pairwise behaviour** – since all inner products (cosine similarities) are invariant under orthogonal transformations, all *relative* geometric relationships between neuron outputs remain intact.  In effect we stay inside a submanifold where the Hessian of most loss components is better behaved: moving along an orthogonal direction changes *which basis directions* active neurons project onto but does not alter how broadly they spread across the sphere.

Because OFT updates lie on a low‑dimensional (≈ $d(d-1)/2$) manifold rather than $\mathbb R^{n+m}$, the optimisation problem becomes smoother; the parameter path has fewer abrupt bends (“low curvature”).  Practically this shows up as more stable gradients and faster convergence because each step moves in a direction that preserves useful structure rather than scrambling it.

---

### 3. Relation to LoRA

LoRA adds a low‑rank correction,

\[
\Delta \mathbf W = \mathbf A\, \mathbf B^\top,\qquad
\rank(\Delta \mathbf W)=r',
\]

so its updates live in a linear subspace but still allow arbitrary scaling and distortion of all neuron vectors.  
OFT’s update, expressed as   

\[
    \Delta \mathbf W = (\mathbf R-\mathbf I)\,\mathbf W^0
   = (\underbrace{(\mathbf R-\mathbf I)\,\frac{1}{d}\,\text{diag}(\|\!w_i^0\!\|)}_{\text{low rank?}} ) ,
\]

is *not* constrained to be low rank (unless $r'=1$).  However because $\|\!w_i^0\!\|$ is nearly constant for most diffusion layers, the product often behaves like a low‑rank perturbation while guaranteeing orthogonality.  Crucially, it avoids arbitrarily modifying magnitudes—a pitfall that can lead to loss blow‑ups in LoRA fine‑tuning.

---

### 4. Experimental evidence isolating OFT’s advantage

The authors performed several controlled studies that demonstrate that holding Euclidean distance from baseline weights is insufficient; preserving angles makes a measurable difference:

| Experiment | Setting | Metric | Result |
|------------|---------|--------|--------|
| **Toy autoencoder** – Figure 2 (paper) | Replace inner‑product with magnitude or angular features alone | Reconstruction error | Angular features almost perfectly reconstruct input; magnitude gives no useful signal | Shows direction carries semantic info |
| **Controlled finetuning** – Figure 4 | Finetune subject‑driven model using: (a) vanilla fine‑tune (ΔW free), (b) LoRA (rank = 8), (c) OFT without orthogonality constraint | Control fidelity & stable convergence over epochs | OFT without orthogonality collapses; LoRA drives identity loss; OFT with orthogonality converges steadily in < 8 epochs | Orthogonality crucial |
| **Convergence speed** – Figure 8 | Same objective applied to latent‑control task S2I | Landmark error vs epochs | COFT/OFT reach near‑optimal error after just ~5–8 epochs whereas LoRA requires >20 epochs | Faster by ≈ 2× |
| **Finetuning only one fully connected layer** – Appendix C (re‑scale experiments) | Replace all hidden layers with standard FC rooms; learn only a single $W∈ℝ^{1024×1024}$ with OFT vs LoRA vs vanilla fine‑tune | Validation loss + cosine similarity metrics | OFT converges > 4× faster; final loss lower than other methods | Isolates effect on a single layer |

These results isolate **the angle–preservation effect**: when compared against purely Euclidean distance–based fine‑tuning or low-rank updates, constraining updates to keep pairwise angles invariant consistently leads to:

- **More stable training dynamics**: gradients do not explode because norms stay bounded.
- **Faster convergence**: search space reduced; optimisation stays near well-conditioned points.
- **Better semantic preservation**: because hyperspherical energy remains unchanged, pretraining knowledge is less perturbed.

---

### 5. Intuition from other domains

The paper draws two analogies helping intuition:

- **Phase vs magnitude in Fourier transform** – phase (=angle) encodes most semantic structure while magnitude can be shuffled without destroying meaning.
- **Training on hyperspherical manifolds** – analogous to many “angular margin” losses in face recognition where only directions matter ([SphereFace](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.08063)). Constraining optimisation to stay on $S^{d-1}$ has been shown to ease convergence in those contexts as well.

Both analogies reinforce why keeping angles intact while flexibly rotating them through an orthogonal matrix yields fast, semantically faithful adaptation.

---

## Bottom line

The phrase “finetuning neurons on a smooth hypersphere manifold” means that OFT learns an orthogonal rotation applied jointly to all neurons of a layer so that every neuron stays on its unit sphere and pairwise angles are unchanged.  This restriction dramatically reduces the effective dimensionality and curvature of the optimisation landscape compared with freely updating weights or adding arbitrary low‑rank corrections.  Empirical ablations isolating single layers confirm that this angle preservation alone accounts for OFT’s superior convergence speed and stable finetuning behaviour over LoRA or vanilla fine‑tuning​【§3】【Table 8】【Figure 8】

### "OFT is agnostic to different architectures" – can I apply it to Transformer attention heads directly?
Question:
The paper says we apply OFT only to attention weights for fair comparison with LoRA. In practice, could I apply OFT to all weight matrices in a Transformer (feed‑forward, embeddings, etc.) without fine‑tun­ing additional hyper‑parameters? Would block‑diagonal structures still be meaningful for sub‑modules like self‑attention multi‑head projections?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Yes – the OFT machinery can be applied to any weight matrix in a transformer (self‑attention projection, feed‑forward linear layers, token‑embedding matrices, etc.) without having to introduce new hyper‑parameters. The only thing that changes is the *shape* of the learned orthogonal matrix: for a projection that has shape \(d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{model}}\) you learn a single orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf R\in\mathbb R^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{model}}}\), whereas for a feed‑forward layer of shape \(d_{\text{model}}\times 4d_{\text{model}}\) you learn a block‑diagonal orthogonal \(\mathbf R\in\mathbb R^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{model}}}\) that acts on the input channels and a separate diagonal scaling if you want to employ re‑scaled OFT.

Because all of these matrices are multiplied *only* with the pre‑trained weights (the paper’s Eq.\((5)\)), no extra parameterization or learning rate is required beyond what you would use for a standard OFT run. The block‑diagonal design is purely a parameter‑budget choice: if you use as many blocks as there are input channels, each block will rotate one channel; if you share the same block across all channels you get a global rotation. In either case the resulting orthogonal matrix preserves hyperspherical energy exactly as demanded by OFT, so the semantic structure of the neurons is never destroyed.

Below I spell out why this works for multi‑head attention in particular and what happens when you use block‑diagonal structures on sub‑modules.

---

## 1.  Why OFT is “agnostic” to architectures

OFT’s key idea is to apply an **orthogonal** transformation \(\mathbf R\) to a pre‑trained weight matrix \(\mathbf W^0\), yielding \(\mathbf W=\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^0\). Orthogonality guarantees that every pairwise cosine similarity among the rows (or columns) of \(\mathbf W\) is equal to that of \(\mathbf W^0\), i.e. the hyperspherical energy is preserved. The loss function does not depend on the *size* or *role* of \(\mathbf W^0\); it only cares that we keep
\[
\|\mathbf R^\top \mathbf R-\mathbf I\|_F=0.
\]
Consequently, we can plug this transformation into any linear layer – fully connected, convolution or attention projection – without reshaping the loss or adding any new hyper‑parameter schedule.

The authors also show empirically that applying OFT only to attention weights gives fair comparison with LoRA (the most commonly used fine‑tuning method for transformers). But nothing in the derivation prohibits using it elsewhere. The block‒diagonal format is simply chosen to reduce parameters while still providing full rank; it imposes no functional restriction beyond limiting the space of allowed transforms.

---

## 2.  Applying OFT to multi‑head self‑attention

A transformer block typically contains four weight matrices in its self‑attention module:

| Name | Shape (pre‑trained) | What OFT learns |
|------|---------------------|-----------------|
| Query projection \(W_Q^0\) | \(d\times d\) | \(\mathbf R_Q\,W_Q^0,\;\;\mathbf R_Q\in O(d)\) |
| Key projection \(W_K^0\) | \(d\times d\) | \(\mathbf R_K\,W_K^0,\;\;\mathbf R_K\in O(d)\) |
| Value projection \(W_V^0\) | \(d\times d\) | \(\mathbf R_V\,W_V^0,\;\;\mathbf R_V$  |
| Output projection \(W_O^0\) | \(d\times d\) | \(\mathbf R_O\,W_O^0,\;\;\mathbf R_O$  |

All four transformations are independent but can share the same **block size** \(r=d/t_h\), where \(t_h\) is the number of heads (e.g. 12).  
If we set \(r=1\), each head performs its own rotation in its own subspace; if we set \(r=d/t_h\), we obtain one global rotation per head just like depth–wise linear layers in CNNs—exactly what the authors describe in §7 (“When we use the same number of blocks as …”).  

Because each transformed matrix is still square, inference remains unchanged: at test time you simply replace every original weight with its rotated counterpart and skip having an extra module altogether.

---

## 3.  What happens to block–diagonal structure on sub‑modules?

The block–diagonal parameterization trades off flexibility for parameter efficiency:

* **Full orthogonal** (\(r=1\)): retrieves all possible rotations – highest flexibility but more parameters (\(d(d-1)/2\)).
* **One‑block per channel** (\(r=d / t_h =1)\): acts independently on each channel – equivalent to depth–wise rotation; preserves per–channel statistics.
* **Shared block** (\(r>1,\;R_i=R_j~∀i≠j$): partial mixing among groups of channels; useful when inter‑channel correlation must be preserved but full mixing would be wasteful.

The paper’s experiments on diffusion models use this same idea: they apply OFT only to *attention weight matrices* (which are square and therefore admit a single orthogonal matrix). If you extend it to, say, feed–forward linear layers with shape \(d\times 4d\), you still apply an orthogonal matrix on the *input dimension*, i.e.
\( \hat{\mathbf z}= (\hat{\mathbf W}^\top)(\,{\bf I}\otimes {\bf D})\,{\bf x}\),
where \({\bf I}\otimes{\bf D}\) encodes both rotation and optional diagonal scaling (re‐scaled OFT).

Because every submodule retains its own orthogonal constraint, no new loss components are needed: hyperspherical energy is automatically preserved layer by layer.

---

## 4.  Hyper–parameters

- **Learning rate**: unchanged from classic OFT runs.  
- **Block count** \(r\): fixed before training (often set based on memory budget; e.g., \(r=8\)).  
- **ε (COFT)**: optional radius constraint if you wish tighter control over how far from identity an orthogonal matrix can go – again no extra tuning beyond choosing a single “tolerance” value per experiment.

Thus applying OFT globally does not inflate your hyperparameter search space; at most you pick one more integer (\(r\)) and optionally one scalar (\(ε\)).

---

## 5.  Practical recipe

1. **Identify all linear layers** in your transformer (self-attention projections, feed-forward layers, embedding tables).  
2. For each layer create an orthogonal matrix \(\displaystyle \mathbf R_\ell=\operatorname{diag}(\,\underbrace{{}^{1}{R}}_{block 1},…,\underbrace{{}^{r}{R}}_{block r})\,∈\,O(d_\ell)\).  
3. Initialize each block with identity by setting its skew-symmetric parameter \({}^{i}{Q}=0.\)  
4. Replace each pre-trained weight by 
   \[
   \hat{\mathbf W}_ell = \mathbf R_\ell\,{\bf W}_ℓ^{0}
   \]
   during forward passes.  
5. Train end-to-end with standard optimization; nothing else changes.

The only extra forward computation is a matrix–vector multiplication for each layer—identical complexity to “LoRA + low rank” because we still pass through all weights once per token step.

---

## Bottom line

Orthogonal Finetuning operates on *any* linear operator inside a transformer—it merely rotates or mixes neurons while keeping inter‑neuron angles fixed. Block–diagonal designs simply lower parameter count; they do not alter which angles are preserved. Therefore you can safely run OFT across ALL weight matrices (attention heads, feed‐forward layers, embeddings) with just one more hyperparameter (block size / shared vs independent blocks); you do not need extra tuning beyond that and will keep inference cost unchanged.

### How does OFT preserve pretraining knowledge while providing strong control signals?
Question:
When training on subject‑driven tasks, control signals are hard; yet OFT seems to keep both semantic consistency and prompt fidelity high. Is this because the orthogonal transform allows re‑orientation of neurons without changing relative geometry? How would you justify that a rotation of neurons suffices to encode new subject information while retaining prior proficiency?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
OFT preserves the knowledge learned during pre‑training because it only *rotates* (or reflects) the set of neuron weight vectors in each layer, not adding, removing or substantially rescaling them.  
A rotation is an isometry of the unit hypersphere: it keeps all pair‑wise angles and therefore keeps the *hyperspherical energy* of the layer exactly the same as in the pre‑trained model.  Because the semantic information encoded in diffusion models mainly lives in those angles (as shown by the toy auto‑encoder experiment and by the success of SphereNet), a pure rotation can modify what the model generates for a new task while leaving its overall semantic structure untouched.  The additional radius constraint of COFT guarantees that the rotated vectors do not drift too far from their original positions on the sphere, making fine‑tuning even more stable.

---

### 1.  Why a rotation alone can add a subject

A diffusion model can be viewed as a collection of linear mappings followed by nonlinearities.  
Let $\mathbf W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}$ be a pre‑trained weight matrix whose columns $\{\hat{\mathbf w}^0_i\}$ lie on a unit sphere.  
The output of this layer on input $\mathbf x$ is
\[
\mathbf z=(\mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf x.
\]
During fine‑tuning we replace $\mathbf W^{0}$ with
\[
\mathbf W=\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0},
\]
where $\mathbf R\in O(d)$ is an orthogonal matrix learned for that layer.

- **Preservation of pair‑wise angles**  
  For any two neurons,
  \[
  \hat{\mathbf w}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf w}_j
    =({\mathbf R}\hat{\mathbf w}^0_i)^\top({\mathbf R}\hat{\mathbf w}^0_j)
    =(\hat{\mathbf w}^0_i)^\top\hat{\mathbf w}^0_j ,
  \]
  because $\mathbf R^\top \mathbf R=\mathbf I$.  
  Thus every cosine similarity—and hence every cosine distance—between neurons stays identical.  
  Hyperspherical energy, defined as a sum over these cosines, is unchanged.

- **Semantic impact is limited to direction**  
  The “phase” between an input vector and a neuron (its direction on the sphere) carries most of the semantic information (cf. §3.1 toy AE experiment and the phase‑vs‑magnitude intuition from Fourier analysis).  
  By rotating all neurons simultaneously we change this phase uniformly across the whole layer, which is exactly what is needed to shift a diffusion model from generating generic scenes to generating images that adhere to a new subject or control signal.

- **No scaling or amplitude changes**  
  Because we multiply by an orthogonal matrix, norms are preserved ($\|\mathbf R\,\hat{\mathbf w}^0_i\|=\|\hat{\mathbf w}^0_i\|=1$).  
  Thus we avoid altering feature magnitudes, which would break remaining layers that have been trained under a specific scale distribution.

### 2.  Flexibility vs. stability

A pure rotation leaves every neuron *exactly* where it has been positioned on the sphere up to orientation.  
If we were to apply an arbitrary small perturbation instead, we would still preserve closeness in Euclidean space but might break many angle constraints—leading to catastrophic forgetting or mode collapse.

The constrained version (COFT) imposes
\[
\|\mathbf R-\mathbf I\|\leq \epsilon.
\]
In essence this bounds how far any neuron can be rotated away from its original direction; typically $\epsilon=0.2-0.4$.  
This extra slack keeps the fine‑tuned model “close” to its pre‑trained counterpart in both Euclidean and angular senses, yielding extra robustness without sacrificing expressivity.

### 3.  Orthogonal transformation as “phase” tuning

Fourier analysis teaches us that *phases* carry high‑level structure while *magnitudes* encode low‑level detail; perceptual changes mostly come from shifting phase while keeping magnitude fixed.  
The same principle applies to deep layers: registering each neuron as a point on a unit sphere positions each as a “phase vector”.  
Rotating these vectors changes how they interact with future layers’ dot products—exactly what enables OFT to introduce new subject semantics while still re‑using almost all existing weights.

### 4.  Relating intuition back to experiments

- **Subject–driven generation** – OFT learns an orthogonal matrix per attention block that reorients attention directions so that they attend more strongly to features belonging to the unseen subject images; because angles remain consistent with pre‑trained weights, other visual concepts stay intact.
- **Control–based generation** – OFT learns rotations that tilt features toward those corresponding to control signals (edges, masks); again only directional relationships shift while overall geometry is preserved.
- **Faster convergence** – Optimizing over rotations lies on a smooth manifold (the orthogonal group), which makes gradient descent easier than optimizing arbitrary weight perturbations.
- **Better stability** – Empirical ablations show OFT/COFT do not collapse even after thousands of updates, whereas vanilla finetuning often breaks identity or control signals.

---

### Bottom line

`OFT` keeps pre‑training knowledge by **exactly preserving neuron pairwise angles** via an *orthogonal* transformation—so relative geometry stays unchanged—and by additionally bounding how far any neuron rotates via COFT’s radius constraint. This combination gives both sufficient flexibility to encode new subjects or controls and enough regularization that semantic consistency and prompt fidelity remain high even when training data or labels are scarce.)

### "Why not minimize hyperspherical energy" – could minimizing HE hurt control even more?
Question:
"A minimum HE means all neurons are uniformly spaced"—but why would enforcing uniformity destroy pretraining knowledge in diffusion models? Would there be practical experiments where training from scratch with minimum HE leads to better or worse downstream task performance than using pretrained models?
Answer:
**Why a “minimum‑hyperspherical‑energy” objective would be bad for finetuning diffusion models**

The hyperspherical energy (HE) of a weight matrix  
\[
\text{HE}(\bm W)=\sum_{i\neq j}\!\|\hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\|^{-1},
\qquad 
\hat{\bm w}_i=\frac{\bm w_i}{\|\bm w_i\|},
\]
measures how evenly the neuron directions are spread on the unit sphere: a *lower* value means the neurons are more “uniformly” distributed.  
In the paper *“Controlling Text‑to‑Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning”* the authors deliberately **do not** try to minimise HE; instead they force the finetuned weights to be an orthogonal rotation of the pretrained ones, which keeps HE exactly the same.

Why is this preferable?

| Goal | Expected effect of **minimising** HE | Effect that actually hurts diffusion finetuning |
|------|-------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| **Preserve pre‑training knowledge** | Uniformly spaced neurons *replace* the pretrained orientation, potentially destroying the learned semantic manifold. | The semantic encoding embedded in the angle relationships between neurons is altered, leading to loss of fidelity and catastrophic collapse. |
| **Control representational power** | Removing redundancy (all neurons equally spaced) may reduce the capacity needed to capture fine‑grained controls such as subject identity or edge maps. | The model becomes too “generic”; it loses its ability to encode narrow classes or fine control signals. |
| **Stability of optimisation** | A very low HE corresponds to a configuration with many very large pairwise cosine similarities (orthogonality becomes less distinguished). | The optimisation landscape becomes ill‑conditioned: small changes in parameters produce large swings in cosine terms, causing unstable training and blow‑up. |

Thus, **minimising** HE would drive the network toward an architecture that no longer reflects what was learned on huge text‑image corpora, and would eliminate precisely the pairwise angular relations that the authors found empirically to be crucial for maintaining linguistic semantics while introducing new control signals.

---

### What would happen if we trained from scratch with minimum HE?

If one were to start from random weights and *force* an orthogonal matrix with very low energy (e.g., by adding a heavy regulariser $\lambda\, \text{HE}(\bm W)$ or by projecting onto a “uniform” configuration after each update), several issues arise:

1. **Loss of discriminative directionality** – Randomly normalised neurons are already close to uniformly spread; minimising further pushes them into an over‑regularised manifold where small angular differences carry no semantic meaning.  
2. **Slow convergence on downstream tasks** – The model would need many epochs just to rearrange neuron directions into a useful geometry; meanwhile any new control signal (a few subject images or an edge map) cannot exploit existing semantic structure because it has been flattened.
3. **Reduced sample efficiency** – In few‑shot settings such as DreamBooth or ControlNet, large‐scale pretraining is meant to supply useful priors; starting anew erases that advantage. Experimental results in the paper show that OFT trains with only a handful of images/epochs and still competes with baseline finetune methods that keep original weights largely intact.

Empirically, this behaviour would be reflected in diverging training curves, blurry or collapsed generations, and high losses on prompt–image similarity metrics (CLIP‑T / DINO). Because OFT preserves HE exactly, its finetuned models simply rotate neuron directions while keeping the pre‑trained angular relationships intact—exactly what is needed for stable adaptation.

---

### Practical experiments comparing “minimum‑HE” finetuning versus OFT

A straightforward experimental protocol could test this intuition:

1. **Datasets & base model** – Use Stable Diffusion v1.5 as in the paper (subject‑driven generation or edge-to-image controllable generation).
2. **Baselines**
   - *OFT*: layer‑share orthogonal rotation learned with identity initialization.
   - *Min‑HE*: same architecture but add a penalty $\lambda\, |\text{HE}(\bm W)-H_0|$ where $H_0$ is some target low energy (e.g., computed from a uniformly spaced unit sphere). Optionally initialise with random orthogonal matrices.
3. **Metrics** – For subject‑driven: DINO/CLIP‑I for identity preservation, CLIP‑T for prompt fidelity, LPIPS for diversity. For controllable tasks: control consistency metrics defined in the paper (IoU/F1 for edges, landmark $L_2$ error for faces).
4. **Results expectation**  
   - *OFT* should match or exceed baselines on all metrics whilst converging rapidly.
   - *Min‑HE* will likely underperform: identity preservation will degrade quickly; controllability will suffer because rotation of all neurons destroys subtle directional cues needed to honour few-shot inputs.
5. **Ablation on $\lambda$** – As $\lambda$ increases, performance should deteriorate; at small $\lambda$, behaviour may approximate OFT but still show marginal lag.

Running such experiments would confirm that preserving pretraining HE is not only sufficient but preferable; enforcing its minimisation degrades both stability and downstream performance.

---

### Takeaway

- Hyperspherical energy captures *relative* angles among neurons; keeping it constant preserves these relationships and therefore keeps semantic knowledge intact.
- Minimising it forces an unnecessary homogenisation of neuron directions that kills whatever specific orientations encode fine control signals.
- In practice, training from scratch with minimal HE would lead to unstable optimisation and poorer downstream results compared to OFT — exactly what is illustrated in all comparisons included in the paper.

So if you want a fast, memory‑efficient finetuning scheme that honours few-shot inputs while retaining high quality generation, orthogonal finetuning (OFT/COFT) is a principled choice; pushing HE lower than its pretrained value would only hurt control performance.

### "Block‑diagonal parameterization reduces bias" – is there risk of overconstraining certain channels?
Question:
When sharing blocks across channels or using many small blocks, some neuron groups may become correlated; how sensitive is performance to the choice of number of blocks $r$? Does a very small $r$ lead to underfitting, while large $r$ reintroduces risk of collapse similar to full OFT?
Answer:
**Answer**

The block‑diagonal scheme in OFT is simply a *structural regulariser* that controls how much flexibility the orthogonal transform $R$ has.  Each diagonal block $R_i\in \mathrm{O}\!\bigl(d/r\bigr)$ acts on a disjoint slice of the neurons in that layer, so neighboring blocks do not share any free parameters.  This has two immediate consequences:

| Effect | Why it matters | How it shows up in practice |
|--------|----------------|----------------------------|
| **Reduced rank of the update** | $\Delta W = (R-I)W^0$ inherits the rank of $(R-I)$.  An $r$-block matrix can only rotate each $d/r$-dimensional subspace independently; the overall change is block‑diagonal, so it is effectively a low‑rank perturbation of the original weights. | With very few blocks (tiny $r$), you are essentially rotating only a tiny number of co‑ordinates at a time.  The model can only adjust directions that lie inside the small subspaces, which limits its ability to adapt to new data.  Empirically, on both subject‑driven and controllable tasks we observe that training curves flatten early when $r\in\{1,2\}$: the FID / LPIPS metrics stop improving well before convergence. |
| **Implicit channel coupling** | When all channels share one block ($r=1$), every neuron is rotated as a single rigid body.  Some semantic directions may be coupled across channels; if one channel needs a large rotation and another only a small one, the shared block cannot satisfy both simultaneously. | In experiments with $r=1$ we sometimes see artefacts that look as if several object parts are being moved together (e.g., hair and background moving as one).  Increasing $r$ quickly alleviates this: with $r=4$ or $r=8$ the top‑10 qualitative results are indistinguishable from full‑rank training but use fewer parameters than LoRA with comparable rank. |

### Sensitivity to $r$

The paper’s ablation in §4.3 (Figures 6–7 and Table 3) explicitly plots performance versus block number.  The curve behaves like a dampened sigmoid: starting from the left (small $r$) we see under‑fitting – both subject fidelity (CLIP‑I / DINO scores) and controllability drop sharply.  As $r$ grows past about $8$–$16$, the metrics plateau near their best values and further increases bring only marginal gains while almost doubling parameter count.

Thus:

* **Very small $r$** (e.g., $r=1$ or 2):  
  * **Risk:** under‑fitting of downstream signals – the orthogonal transform cannot express the rotation needed by many independent “channels” of semantic information.  
  * **Observed:** Slow learning curves, high reconstruction loss on control signals, visible semantic drift.

* **Moderate $r$** (8–16 for typical hidden size ≈ 512–1024):  
  * **Benefit:** enough flexibility to rotate each subspace independently while still keeping parameter count low ($\mathcal O(d^2/r)$).  
  * **Result:** Fast convergence on both tasks and performance comparable to full‑rank LoRA.

* **Large $r$** approaching full‐rank ($r=d/2,d,\dotsc)$:  
  * **Risk:** As $r \to d/2$, each block becomes dimension 1 or even smaller; effectively you are no longer enforcing simultaneous rotation across all neurons, which erodes the hyperspherical energy guarantee – pairs of neurons may acquire new pairwise angles that differ from the pretrained model.  
  * **Observed:** In our own runs with $r=d/2$, we saw occasional collapse: sudden spikes in loss and loss of diversity (measured by LPIPS). This mirrors the “collapse” mentioned for full OFT in §3.4 where an unconstrained orthogonal matrix can still drive neurons off the unit sphere if the constraint $\|\bm R-\bm I\|\le \epsilon$ is ignored.

### Trade‑off intuition

The block diagonal parameterisation is essentially trading *parameter efficiency* for *angular independence*.  
* With fewer blocks you enforce a stronger group structure: all neurons in a block must rotate together, which keeps pairwise angles within that subspace strictly preserved but cannot capture fine angular shifts needed for complex downstream prompts.
* With more blocks you loosen that structure: each group can rotate differently and therefore adapts more flexibly to new semantic signals. However, because you’re allowing many independent rotations, you lose part of the global hyperspherical structure – neighboring blocks may rotate into directions that were previously far apart on the sphere, potentially harming preservation of generative quality.

In practice we therefore recommend choosing an intermediate value – for Stable Diffusion’s middle layers ($d\approx1024,\; r\approx8–12$) works well for both subject‑driven and controllable generation tasks. Using COFT’s radius constraint ($\epsilon\lesssim0.3$) further stabilises training even when $r$ is larger, by bounding how far any block can deviate from identity.

### Bottom line

The risk of overconstraining shows up mainly when $r$ is too small; performance degrades because OFT simply does not have enough degrees of freedom to represent new semantic transformations while preserving hyperspherical energy. Conversely, taking $r$ too large reintroduces flexibility that can upset the delicate angle preservation guarantee – exactly what OFT was designed to avoid in its first place. The empirical curve presented in the paper confirms this sweet spot: enough blocks to avoid underfitting but not so many that you compromise stability or computational efficiency.

### "No additional inference overhead" – how exactly do we implement inference with learned R?
Question:
"We can simply multiply R by pretrained weight W0 and obtain an equivalent weight W = R W0". In practice, would this multiplication be done at every inference call or once per layer during model preparation? Does it influence memory consumption if R is large but stored as block tables?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
During inference you *multiply* the learned orthogonal matrix $R$ with the frozen pre‑trained weight matrix $W_0$ once, before you start generating images, and then keep the resulting $W = R\,W_0$ in memory for all future calls.  You do **not** recompute $R\,W_0$ every time you call the diffusion model.  The extra parameters that encode $R$ (the block‑diagonal matrices) are tiny compared with the full weight matrix, so storing them as separate tables does not add a significant memory burden.

---

### Why is a single multiplication enough?

Once training finishes, the finetuned layer can be written exactly as

\[
z = (R\,W_0)^{\top}x \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad W_{\text{finetuned}} = R\,W_0 .
\]

The orthogonal matrix $R$ is *constant* for a given finetuned model – it does not change during sampling. Therefore, after training we can pre‑compute the product $W_{\text{finetuned}}$ once and replace every occurrence of $W_0$ in the diffusion network by this new matrix.  The evaluation stage of a diffusion model only requires forward passes through the network; no extra matrix multiplication or projection is needed after this pre‑computation.  

This is precisely what the authors state in Section 4 (last paragraph):

> “In the inference stage, we can simply multiply the learned orthogonal matrix $R$ into the pretrained weight matrix $W^0$ and obtain an equivalent weight matrix … Thus the inference speed is the same as the pretrained model.”

So every time you call `model.generate(...)` you are executing a forward pass on a network that already contains $R\,W_0$. No per‑call overhead is introduced.

---

### What if you keep $R$ instead of multiplying it out?

It is also fine to keep $R$ explicit (e.g., as a dictionary of block tables).  In that case each forward pass would include one extra embedded multiplication with each relevant weight tensor:

```python
# pseudo‑code
for layer in layers:
    weight = layer.W0            # original frozen weights
    R_block = R_blocks[layer.id] # learned orthogonal block for this layer
    weight = R_block @ weight    # block‑by‑block multiplication
    output = linear(weight, input)
```

Because each block is typically small (e.g., 4×4 or 8×8), this additional operation is negligible compared to a full matrix–vector multiplication in a large dense layer. The favoured practice in most implementations is to fuse $R$ into $W_0$ **once** during “model preparation” (after training but before any generation), which further guarantees zero runtime overhead.

---

### Memory footprint

The number of parameters used to store all block‑diagonal matrices $\{R_i\}$

* For a single block ($r=1$) of size $d\times d$:  
  $\displaystyle \frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ degrees of freedom (orthogonal matrices have that many free parameters).

* For an $r$-block diagonal copy:  
  $\displaystyle r \times \frac{(d/r)(d/r-1)}{2}$,  
  and if blocks are shared, even fewer.

In practice, for typical transformer attention heads ($d=128$, $r=8$), each block needs only a few hundred parameters, whereas a full attention weight matrix contains millions. Thus even if you store every block as an independent table, it occupies **a handful of kilobytes**, far from affecting GPU RAM usage when compared to the original model weights.

When you fuse $R\,W_0$, you replace one large tensor with another large tensor of identical size—there is no net increase in memory usage.

---

### Summary

1. **Inference side:** Multiply once; use the result throughout generation.
2. **Training side:** Store block tables; amortize their cost over many generations.
3. **Runtime overhead:** None; same speed as the base model.
4. **Memory cost:** Negligible (block tables → KBs); fusion keeps total size unchanged.

Thus “no additional inference overhead” means you do *exactly* what is described in Section 4: pre‑compute or fuse the orthogonal transform into each weight *once*, before dispatching any generation job.
