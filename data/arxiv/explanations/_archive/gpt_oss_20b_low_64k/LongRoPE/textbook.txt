# Foundations of Positional Encoding in Transformers

Transformers, since their introduction, have become the dominant architecture for natural language processing tasks. Their remarkable ability to model long‑range dependencies relies heavily on an implicit or explicit understanding of the order of tokens. This chapter explores the necessity of position information in sequence modeling, how it is incorporated into Transformers, and why Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) has become ubiquitous in modern large language models (LLMs). We will derive the RoPE formulation, discuss key design choices such as the base value \(\theta\), compare RoPE with other positional encodings, and explain the implications for attention mechanisms.

---

## 1. Introduction to Sequence Modeling and the Need for Explicit Position Information

Sequences are inherently ordered; the meaning of an expression often depends on the relative positions of its constituents. For example, “don’t run into it” versus “run into it” conveys distinct actions because of word order. Classical recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) models implicitly encode position through recurrence: each hidden state depends on the previous one, thereby preserving a notion of sequential progression.

The Transformer architecture, introduced by Vaswani et al., eschews recurrence entirely in favor of self‑attention. In self‑attention, every token attends to every other token simultaneously; there is no inherent sense of left‑to‑right or temporal progression unless provided explicitly. Consequently, Transformers require a mechanism to inject positional information so that the model can distinguish between \((x_i, x_j)\) at positions \(i\) and \(j\), even if their embeddings are identical.

Two categories of positional encodings have emerged:

1. **Absolute Encodings** – where each position has its own unique vector (Learned Positional Embeddings or fixed sinusoidal vectors).  
2. **Relative Encodings** – where the attention computation is conditioned on position differences (e.g., relative bias).  

While relative encodings can capture *relative* distance relationships more naturally, absolute encodings are simpler to implement and have proven effective in practice. The modern trend favors **Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE)** – a variant that blends absolute encoding with a rotation mechanism aligned with query–key pairwise multiplication used in attention.

---

## 2. The RoPE Formulation: Cosine/Sine Rotations and Frequency Scaling

### 2.1 Basic Idea

RoPE augments token embeddings by rotating them in a multi‑dimensional complex plane. Formally consider a 4‑dimensional vector split into two sub‑vectors \((x_1, x_2)\) and \((x_3, x_4)\). If we associate these with real and imaginary parts of two complex numbers,
\[
z^{(1)} = x_1 + i\,x_2,\quad z^{(2)} = x_3 + i\,x_4,
\]
RoPE applies a rotation by an angle proportional to the token’s position \(n\). After rotating all sub‑vectors independently,
the overall embedding becomes
\[
\begin{bmatrix}
x'_1 \\ x'_2 \\ x'_3 \\ x'_4
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
x_1 \cos(n\theta_0)-x_2\sin(n\theta_0)\\
x_1 \sin(n\theta_0)+x_2\cos(n\theta_0)\\
x_3 \cos(n\theta_1)-x_4\sin(n\theta_1)\\
x_3 \sin(n\theta_1)+x_4\cos(n\theta_1)
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
This rotation preserves the Euclidean norm of each pair but embeds positional phase information in angular coordinates. When used as both query \(q_n = \text{RoPE}(k_n)\) and key \(k_n = \text{RoPE}(k_n)\), their dot product incorporates a term \(\cos((n-i)(\theta_d))\), which depends on relative distance; thus RoPE naturally encodes *relative* distances while retaining absolute positions.

### 2.2 Generalization to Arbitrary Dimensionality

Suppose we have an embedding dimension \(d\), which must be even due to pairwise rotation pairs. For each dimension pair \(i = 0,\dots,\frac{d}{2}-1\), we associate a frequency \(\theta_i > 0\). The RoPE application can then be compactly written as
}\[
   \operatorname{RoPE}_n(\mathbf{x}) 
   = 
   \Bigl[
      \cos(n\,\theta_{0})\,x_{0} - \sin(n\,\theta_{0})\,x_{1}, 
      \,\sin(n\,\theta_{0})\,x_{0} + \cos(n\,\theta_{0})\,x_{1}, 
      \\[6pt]
      \dots,
      %
      % replicating pattern for each pair:
      % apply cos/sin with theta_i
   \Bigr] .            % details omitted for brevity
{\small}
Here we make explicit that each pair contributes two rotated components.

This formulation is equivalent to equation (1) in the LongRoPE paper’s preliminary section:
}\[
   [\, 
     \cos(n\,{\color{gray}{}}^{-\!{2i}/{d}}), 
     ~ \sin(n\,{\color{gray}{}}^{-\!{2i}/{d}}), 
     ~ 
     % … repeat for all i …
   ] .
{\small}

The paper further rewrites it as
}\[
   [\, 
     \cos(\tfrac{n}{\beta^i}), 
     ~ \sin(\tfrac{n}{\beta^i}), 
   ],
{\small}
with  
}\[
   \beta^i = 10000^{2i/d},
{\small}
assuming the default base value {\color{gray}10000}.

### 2.3 Frequency Scaling Derivation

Let \(d_\text{emb}\) denote the full embedding dimension (\(= 4096,\;8192,\;\dotsc;\)). We construct frequencies as geometric progression:
}\[
   f_i = f_{\min} * (\alpha)^i,
{\small}
where \(f_{\min}\) sets the lowest frequency and \(\alpha > 1.\)

In RoPE’s original implementation,
}\[
   f_{\min}=\frac{10^4}{d_\text{emb}},
        ~~f_{\max}=10^4,
{\small}
and one chooses \(f_i = f_{\min} * (\tfrac{f_{\max}}{f_{\min}})^{i/(d/2-1)}.\)
For simplicity in many LLMs this reduces to
}\[
    f_i = (10000)^{-\,2i/d}.
{\small}

The resulting phase angles span a wide range: lower dimensions rotate rapidly with position changes; higher dimensions rotate slowly, effectively encoding coarse spatial relationships.

---

## 3. Base Value of θ and Its Effect on Embedding Range

The *base value* \(c=10000\) controls how quickly roational angles progress across token indices:

- **Large Base**: With larger \(c,\) frequencies are smaller (\(f_i \downarrow.\)) Therefore rotations advance slowly across many positions – this helps capture global dependencies but may cause distinct tokens far apart to appear similar.
- **Small Base**: With smaller \(c,\) frequencies rise sharply; rotations vary quickly across tokens – suitable for capturing local patterns but potentially leading to over‑crowding when extended beyond training length.

Mathematically, if we double \(c,\)
}\[
    f_i' = c'^{-\,2i/d} = (k c)^{-\,2i/d} = k^{-\,2i/d}\! f_i ,
{\small}
which scales all frequencies by the factor \(k^{-\,2i/d}\). Consequently, angles at high indices shrink proportionally weaker than at low indices because they involve higher powers of this factor.

In practice LLMs fix \(c=10000.\) However, extending context length drastically introduces *new* positions beyond those seen during pretraining; this may push angles toward extreme ranges leading to catastrophic numerical values or loss of discriminative power – precisely why methods like LongRoPE adjust rescaling factors per dimension rather than globally scaling by a single factor.

---

## 4. Comparison With Other Positional Encodings

| Encoding | Nature | Implementation | Pros | Cons |
|----------|--------|----------------|------|------|
| **Absolute Learned** | Each position gets a learnable vector | linear layer indexed by position | Very flexible; adapts empirically | Huge parameter cost for long windows ($O(L)$); no extrapolation possible |
| **Sinusoidal** | Deterministic cosine/sine functions | precomputed table | No parameters; extrapolates gracefully | Frequencies fixed argue may not match learned patterns |
| **Relative Bias** | Adds bias depending on distance | small scalar per bias index | Captures relative dependence well; no per‑position storage | Requires separate storage for all distances; can be expensive |
| **Rotary (RoPE)** | Rotations applied over pairs | simple matrix multiplications with sine/cosine functions per dimension | Preserves length invariance; works well within attention dot product; supports simple extrapolation via rescaling |\,- Need careful frequency design for very long extensions |

Key distinctions:

- **Extrapolation**: Learned embeddings cannot generalize beyond their maximum index without retraining; sinusoidal and Rotary can produce embeddings for arbitrary positions because they are analytic functions.
- **Relative Distances**: In self‐attention dot products using Rotated queries/keys, cosine terms naturally translate into relative distance encoding:  
    \[
        q_n^\top k_m =
        ||q||~||k||~F(\varphi_n - \varphi_m),
    }`$\varphi$ being phase angles`.   
    By contrast absolute sinusoidal embeddings cannot easily separate relative from absolute components without extra conditioning.
- **Computational Overhead**: All three require only element‑wise multiplications after precomputing sine/cosine tables once per batch or caching them.

Thus Rocpe’s blend of efficiency and compatibility makes it prevalent in frameworks such as LLaMA, Mistral, BLOOM etc.

---

## 5. Implications of RoPE for Attention Computations

Self‐attention computes scores between query–key pairs:
}\[
    e_{ij}=\frac{{q}_i^\top {k}_j}{\sqrt{d_k}},
{\small}
where \(q_i,k_j∈ℝ^{d_k}\).

Applying RoPE multiplicatively before dot product modifies queries and keys:
]\[
 q'_i=R(i)\cdot q_i,\quad k'_j=R(j)\cdot k_j,
]{/math}

Here \(R(i)\) is a block diagonal matrix consisting of two‑by‑two rotation blocks with angle \(n·θ_v.\)

Because rotations preserve norms:
]\[
 ||q'_i||=||q_i||,\quad||k'_j||=||k_j||,
]{/math}

the dot product transforms into
]\[
 q'_i^\top k'_j=
     r(i,j),
]{/math}
where  
]\[
 r(i,j)=q_i^\top k_j
          + ∑_{\text{pairs}}\textstyle{
          q_{\mathrm{subpair}}^\top R_{\!pair}(i-j)
          k_{\mathrm{subpair}}
          },
]{/math}

and $R_{\!pair}$ contains cosines/sines depending on $(i-j)$ times $\theta_v$. Thus every attention score inherently reflects the *relative distance*: $\cos((i-j)\theta_v)$ terms appear across all dimensions $v$.  

When sequences grow longer than those seen during training:

- Rotations for larger $|i-j|$ shift far out along the unit circle → Cosine values oscillate more finely.
- If $\theta_v$’s are too large relative to sequence length, adjacent positions may map to very similar plane orientations → **crowding problem**, leading to indistinguishable attention weights.
- If $\theta_v$’s are too small compared with sequence length → Asymptotic values flatten → loss of discriminative power over distance.

Hence adjusting $\theta_v$ per dimension (as LongRoPE does via evolutionary search for $\lambda$ factors) is essential when extending context windows to millions of tokens: it preserves relative distinction without inflating new angular space too aggressively.

---

## 6. Summary

Rotary Position Embedding stands as a cornerstone positional scheme enabling large language models to operate over massive sequences efficiently while enabling both relative-distance inference within self-attention and graceful extrapolation beyond seen contexts. Understanding its construction—pairwise rotations governed by geometrically spaced frequencies—illuminates why small adjustments in frequency scaling critically affect model performance at extreme context lengths and shapes future methods like LongRoPE that exploit non-uniform interpolation strategies across dimensions and token indices.

With this foundation, one can now appreciate subsequent advances that refine rotational embeddings through search-based optimization or progressive fine-tuning strategies focused on achieving remarkably long context windows—such as those presented in LongRoPE’s extension beyond two million tokens.\

# Motivation: The Challenge of Ultra‑Long Context Windows  

Large Language Models (LLMs) have revolutionised natural‑language processing, but their usefulness is still bounded by the length of the sequence that can be presented to the model at inference time.  State‑of‑the‑art LLMs such as LLaMA 2 and Mistral were trained with a positional embedding budget of only **4 096 tokens**.  When a document, a conversation history, or a batch of in‑context examples exceeds this size, the model must truncate or chunk the text, which destroys valuable long‑range dependencies.  Consequently, many real‑world applications are limited by this cap.

The motivation behind extending LLM context windows therefore stems from *what* we want to do with language models and *how* we can do it without incurring prohibitive costs or compromising performance on familiar, short‑context tasks.

## Use‑cases Requiring 10⁴+ Tokens  

A growing set of use cases demands that an LLM process **tens or even hundreds of thousands** of tokens in one call:

| Scenario | Typical Input Length | Problem without Ultra‑Long Context |
|----------|----------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **In‑Context Learning (ICL)** | 20–30 examples × 200–300 tokens each → > 12 000 tokens | Models must drop or window examples, losing curriculum information. |
| **LLM Agents** | Many turns + simulated observations + world state < 15 000 tokens | Replacing memory buffers forces agents to forget earlier decisions. |
| **Document Summarisation & Retrieval** | 1–10 M characters (~300 k+ tokens per book) | Must split and summarize chunks independently—artifacts appear at chunk boundaries. |
| **Code Generation/Analysis** | Large codebases spanning many files → > 200 000 tokens | Cross‑file dependencies are ignored when contexts are trimmed. |

While ten thousand token context has already pushed research beyond the original 4k limit (see \citet{pi} achieving ~128k), many practical deployments still sit well below the true amounts required for cohesive reasoning over long narratives or full practical documents.

## Catastrophic Positions: A Distributional Shift

When a model is asked to attend to positions far outside its training range, the underlying positional encoding produces *catastrophic* values—angles that exceed those seen during pre‑training.  Mathematically, RoPE rotates a query–key pair by an angle \(n\theta_i\). With large \(n\), these angles wrap around densely; the mapping becomes highly nonlinear and introduces *out‑of‑distribution* representations.  The embedding statistics shift dramatically:  
- Rotational key vectors become nearly orthogonal only in very high dimensions;  
- Lower frequencies suffer from angle collision;  
- Attention weights are dominated by new positions regardless of content relevance.

Empirical studies (\citet{pi}) show a steep increase in perplexity once \(\frac{L'}{L} > 2\), confirming that models fall ill when encountering unseen positions.

## Scarcity of Training Data over 100k Tokens

Fine‑tuning is the common pathway to enlarge context windows: one fine‑tunes a pre‑trained LLM on longer sequences so that it learns to utilize its expanded embeddings.  However, several constraints block this approach:

1. **Data Availability** – Standard corpora (OpenWebText, Books3, PG19) rarely contain contiguous sequences exceeding one million tokens.  
2. **Granularity Requirements** – Even if long texts exist, they are typically broken into shorter paragraphs; assembling them into long contiguous samples can distort language continuity.  
3. **Cost Implications** – Training on vastly larger batches (e.g., 256k token length) demands more GPU memory and longer training hours—orders of magnitude beyond standard budgets.

These limitations mean that empirical evidence for *effective* learning patterns beyond ~128k is sparse, making it difficult to trust extensions derived purely from interpolation or heuristics.

## Attention Dilution in Large Windows  

Transformers rely on self‑attention: every token attends to every other token in the sequence. In practice, there is an implicit locality effect—tokens closer together preferentially exchange information—shaped by positional encodings and learned biases. When we greatly expand the window:

- The attention matrix becomes large (\(O(L^2)\)) but its mass dilutes across a broader set of positions.
- High positional variance can cause gradients to become unstable; gradients for genuine long‑range dependencies diminish relative to “noise” farther out.
- Downstream tasks that hinge on precise alignment across distant spans (e.g., answering questions about earlier parts of a text) experience degraded performance.

Prior work (\citet{yarn}) noted that beyond roughly \(4\times\) extension ratios neural‐network dynamics begin to shift toward this dilution regime; similarly, \citet{longlora} observed exploding loss landscapes when extending beyond ~200k tokens without mitigating attention spread.

## Existing Upper Bounds (~128k) and Their Limitations

Recent papers have managed to push context windows up to about **128k tokens** by adjusting RoPE scaling factors (e.g., LongLoRA, Yeat’s LoRA). These methods rely heavily on either:

- Linear interpolation (PI): straightforward but generates crowded positional assignments for long sequences;
- NTK‐guided interpolation: introduces differential scaling across dimensions but still fixed for all positions;
- YaRN’s manual grouping: mixes extrapolation and interpolation based on frequency bands but depends on hand‑crafted heuristic thresholds.

All these approaches share common shortcomings when scaling further:

1. **Uniformity Assumption** – Treat all higher positions equally; ignore that early tokens may need preserved attention.
2. **Limited Exploitation of Dimensional Non‑Uniformity** – Lower frequency dimensions already encode coarse positional information; forcing them too much extrapolation squashes useful phase relations.
3. **Fixed Plans Across All Training Objectives** – Results in a suboptimal tradeoff between short‑context fidelity and ultra‐long contextual capacity.

Consequently, while these works demonstrate feasibility up to ~128k returns significant performance drops when moving to finer granularity or fixed small contexts—a critical issue for many operational systems that must support both short chats and full book scans.

---

By summarizing these obstacles—*catastrophic new positions*, *data scarcity*, *attention dilution*, and *uniform interpolation strategies*—we motivate the need for a principled method capable of pushing LLM context windows beyond two million tokens while preserving utility both at extreme lengths and within the conventional few thousand token realm. This chapter sets the stage for understanding how LongRoPE attempts to satisfy these demands through explicit exploitation of non‐uniformities in RoPE and progressive fine‐tuning strategies.

# Chapter: Baseline Methods for Context Extension

Large‑scale language models have become increasingly successful across a myriad of downstream tasks, yet one enduring limitation remains the size of the context window that a model can consume. Classic transformers encode positional information through explicit embeddings; the most popular scheme in modern LLMs is *rotary position embeddings* (RoPE). When the sequence length used during training exceeds the window the model has seen, its performance degrades sharply because the model has never encountered the corresponding position indices. Researchers have therefore proposed several “positional interpolation” strategies that adapt a pretrained RoPE to longer sequences without retraining or fine‑tuning for every new length. This chapter focuses on the foundational techniques that have been used as baselines in this line of work:

1. **Linear Positional Interpolation (PI)**  
2. **NTK‑based Interpolation and Extrapolation**  
3. **Dynamic NTK**  
4. **YaRN (Grouped Frequency‑Aware Interpolation)**  

For each method we discuss its motivation, mathematical formulation, and practical implications (e.g., computational overhead, generality). We also provide an objective comparison highlighting where each excels and where it falls short.

---

## 1. Linear Positional Interpolation (PI)

### 1.1 Motivation

The simplest way to force a pretrained model to tolerate longer sequences is to *scale* every positional angle so that new positions are “compressed” into the range on which the model was trained. This idea stems from the observation that if every angle is divided by a constant factor \(s\) (the *extension ratio*), then no single dimension experiences a dramatically different frequency pattern than seen during pre‑training.

### 1.2 Formulation

Assume a transformer with hidden dimension \(d\) uses RoPE coordinates
\[
\ \mathbf{r}_{n} = \bigl[
\cos(n\theta_{0}), \sin(n\theta_{0}), \dots,
\cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}), \sin(n\theta_{d/2-1})
\bigr], 
\]
where \(\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}\) and \(\theta=10{,}000\) is the default base set in most RoPE‑based LLMs.  
Given an extended context length \(L' = sL\), PI rescales *every* angle by dividing it by \(s\):
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{n}
     = \bigl[
       \cos(\frac{n}{s}\theta_{0}), \sin(\frac{n}{s}\theta_{0}), \dots,
       \cos(\frac{n}{s}\theta_{d/2-1}), \sin(\frac{n}{s}\theta_{d/2-1})
     \bigr].
\]
Equivalently, we modify each block’s rotation matrix by multiplying its rotational frequency by \(1/s\). No hyperparameter beyond \(s\) is involved.

### 1.3 Intuition

By halving every frequency (\(s=2\)), we double the density of angle values inside the original span; thus tokens at positions \( n \in [0,L') \) are mapped into angles that lie between two samples from the training distribution. The hope is that distances preserve enough structural information for attention computations to remain stable.

### 1.4 Strengths

- **Simplicity**: A single scalar controls all dimensions; implementation requires only biasing all sinusoid arguments.
- **Zero Training Overhead**: PI works directly at inference time; no additional fine‑tuning or data is required.
- **Deterministic Behavior**: The interpolation ratio is known exactly, enabling reproducible extensions for any length.

### 1.5 Weaknesses

- **Crowding Effect**: For large \(s\), many newly inserted positions share nearly identical angles, causing their embeddings to become indistinguishable in higher frequency dimensions.
- **Uniform Treatment Ignored**: Lower frequency dimensions receive excessive compression while higher ones still carry almost discrete information, disrupting the balance across dimensions.
- **Rapid Performance Drop**: Empirically, PI runs into significant perplexity explosions beyond roughly a 4× extension.

---

## 2. NTK‑Based Interpolation and Extrapolation

### 2.1 Motivation

The *Neural Tangent Kernel* (NTK) framework views transformer self‑attention as a kernel operation after projecting keys and queries into a frequency space determined by RoPE angles. NTK analysis reveals how changes in rotational frequencies affect gradients during pre‑training, suggesting a principled way to modify angles while preserving the underlying behavior learned by the network.

### 2.2 Formulation

Define
\[
\rho_i = \beta^i = (\theta^{2/d})^i,
\]
and let \( s > 1 \) be the desired extension ratio. NTK suggests setting each dimension’s scaling factor according to its index:
\[
\lambda_i = s^{\,i},
\]
and then applying
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_n
 = 
  \bigl[\cos((n / (\lambda_i))\, p_i), 
        \sin((n / (\lambda_i))\, p_i)\bigr]
   , 
   ~~p_i = \frac{1}{a^{\,i}},\, a=\sqrt{\beta}.
\]
In practice one can express this via:
\[
( n / (\lambda_i))\, p_i
 = n / (\beta^i)^{(i+1)/i},
\]
which reduces exactly to Eq.\eqref{eq:unified_rope} in the paper using brightness weights derived from NTK theory.

### 2.3 Intuition

Higher index dimensions encode lower frequencies; scaling them more aggressively essentially *extrapolates* their patterns beyond what was seen during training while keeping lower‐frequency ones relatively close to their original magnitudes. This way, different parts of RoPE handle situationally distinct degrees of “crowding”: low‑index dims suffer less dilution than high‑index dims.

### 2.4 Strengths

- **Frequency Awareness**: Adjustments depend on each dimension’s base frequency, providing a better fit than uniform scaling.
- **Leverages Analytical Insight**: Grounded in NTK calculations rather than empirical tuning.
- **Moderate Extension Capacity**: Empirical studies show NTK works up to around `4x` or `8x` extensions without training.

### 2.5 Weaknesses

- **Over‑Extrapolation Risk**: Some high‐frequency dims may still become too compressed if `s` is very large.
- **Fixed Rule Gave Limited Flexibility**: The exponentiation function `λ_i = s^i` is rigid; it cannot adapt per‐dimension optimal scaling if certain contexts deserve different treatment.
- **Still Suffers Under Large Extensions**: Performance degrades beyond ~`8×`, especially when attention patterns spread thinner across many token slots.

---

## 3. Dynamic NTK

### 3.1 Motivation

Standard NTK prescribes a single set of scaling factors based purely on distance from epoch zero (pre‑training). However, as sequence length grows during training or inference, these same constants may not remain optimal due to changing relative attention patterns among tokens far apart versus near tokens.

Dynamic NTK introduces *sequence‐length dependent* adjustment, realising that interpolation pressure should diminish with distance into training lengths.

### 3.2 Formulation

Let current maximum sequence length be \(L_{\text{max}}\). For any target length \(L'\), define an adaptive extension ratio per dimension:
\( s'_i(L') = f(i,L') \),
where `f` is often constructed as:
```
if i < k      → s'_i(L') = s_low
else         → s'_i(L') = s_high × (L'/L)^α
```
with hyperparameters `k`, `α`. In effect, higher frequencies get tuned more conservatively as `L'` increases.

Alternatively one can write:
\[ 
    \lambda_i(L') = s^{\,g(i,L'/L)},
    g(\cdot) =
      \begin{cases}
        i & i<k,\\
        i(1 + β(L'-L)/L ) & i≥k,
      \end{cases}
\]
leading to an interpolation scaling factor that grows sublinearly with extended length for slow dimensions.

### 3.3 Intuition

Tokens at earlier positions interact more strongly with all subsequent tokens—they must be stored faithfully over long horizons—so their encoding should retain higher frequencies for longer extents of sequence expansion than later token positions do.

Dynamic NTK seeks to preserve this nuance by thining out interpolation pressure early on and allowing more leeway later when interactions are less critical.

### 3.4 Strengths

- **Adaptivity**: Responds explicitly to concrete sequence lengths rather than relying only on fixed pre-training hyperparameters.
- **Less Crowding Overly Early Positions**: Mitigates information loss for tokens at front of sequence.
- **Effective Up To Roughly Four Times Extension** without fine-tuning.

### 3.5 Weaknesses

- **Complex Hyperparameter Tuning**: Requires choosing segmentation points and growth shapes manually per target length.
- **Still Relies on Per-Dimension Uniform Rules**: Lack of per-token adjustment means it cannot fully protect early facing tokens statistically.
- **Limited In-the-Wild Gains for >8× Extensions**

---

## 4. YaRN (Grouped Frequency‑Aware Interpolation)

### 4.1 Background & Motivation

YaRN stands for “Yarn - Grouped Frequency‐Aware Rotations.” It recognizes that RoPe channels can be naturally divided into three regimes:

| Group | Frequency Range | Treatment |
|-------|-----------------|-----------|
| High | Highest frequencies (low index) | Extrapolate (`λ=1`) |
| Medium | Mid frequencies | Apply NTK (`λ=s^i`) |
| Low | Lowest frequencies (high index) | Apply linear PI (`λ=s`) |

The grouping was defined empirically by analysing which group produced best performance on standard benchmarks when observing how different dimensions react to different sequence lengths.

### 4.2 Formal Procedure

For each group G:
```
for i in dims(G):
    if group == High:
         λ_i = s^( ε )   # typically ε≈0 → λ=1
    elif group == Medium:
         λ_i = s^i
    else if group == Low:
         λ_i = s
```
This yields three distinct scalings across dimension indices — uppercase capitals emphasize discretisation rather than continuous tuning per dimension.

A post‑processing step often applies smoothing across boundaries between groups to avoid sharp jumps in rotor angles that could hurt gradient flow during fine-tuning phases.

### 4.3 Intuition Each Group Serves Different Roles

High-frequency channels are most sensitive to short positional differences and are seldom used over very long distances; extrapolating them conservatively preserves angular discrimination among proximate tokens while allowing farthest positions them more freedom to expand temporally without blowing up numeric ranges.

Medium-frequency channels navigate both local and seasonal interference patterns; letting them adhere precisely to NTK guided scaling keeps their frequency product stable while introducing mild compression where necessary.

Low-frequency channels tend only to differentiate very long-range relationships; linear PI with large scale factor prevents them from saturating too quickly but also prevents accidental aliasing with extremely distant frames—hence why YaRN applies direct linear interpolation here rather than complete extrapolation or heavy compression.

### 4.4 Strengths

- **Empirically Skilled Balance Across Frequency Bands**: Captures improvements over both naive PI and plain NTK.
- **User-Friendly Implementation**: Does not require costly evolutionary search; just histogram cutting points.
- **Relatively Robust Against Very Long Scenarios without Extensive Fine-Tuning**, achieving ~`8×` favorably over naive methods in some LLM families like LLaMA or Mistral variants tested in prior literature.

### 4.5 Weaknesses

- **Design Heuristic Only Accepting Binary/Piecewise Splits**: The partition boundaries may not be optimal for all LLM architectures or downstream tasks.
- **Suboptimal On Highest Extent Ratios (~512×)** due either to lost fidelity after grouping or insufficient anchor points within each group.
- **Extra Coupling Between RC Axes**: Because groups are static over all dimensions, they cannot rectify specific dimensional affinities suggested by finer-granularity analyses within research papers like LongRoPE’s evolutionary search.

None of these conventional baselines yields stable results without extensive fine-tuning nor additional dimensional adaptive search—but newer research (e.g., LongRoPE’s search framework) has demonstrated feasible paths reaching valuable territories such as CERN-length windows (`2048k`).

## Closing Remarks   

The baseline techniques outlined above provide distinct trade-offs between simplicity, adaptability, and top performance under specific domain constraints:

* If one needs absolute zero overhead—e.g., fast prototyping or casual inference—PI provides immediate access but can hurt quality markedly beyond tension limits early in training;
* When slight extra compute budget can be exchanged for steady lean improvements on larger-than-original windows (`~4–8x`), integrating simple module like Dynamic NTK or YaRN yields substantial gains;
* Full-fledged contextual working beyond `160k+` demands either deep fine-tuning campaigns guided by refined interpolations or reinforcement by evolutionary optimisations such as those employed in LongRoPE literature—for which baseline insight still acts as reference backbone upon which researchers benchmark new innovations.
