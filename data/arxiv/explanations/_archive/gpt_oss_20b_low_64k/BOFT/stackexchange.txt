### How does the Cayley transform guarantee orthogonality in BOFT’s butterfly components?
Question:
In BOFT, each butterfly component $	ilde{m{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is said to be orthogonal by applying a Cayley transform to its $2b	imes2b$ blocks. I understand the Cayley transform for a general skew‑symmetric matrix $m{Q}$ produces an orthogonal matrix $m{R}=(m{I}+m{Q})(m{I}-m{Q})^{-1}$. However, when the component is block‑diagonal with many $2	imes2$ or $2b	imes2b$ sub‑blocks, how does the global orthogonality follow? Are there any subtle constraints on the skew‑symmetric sub‑matrices or on the ordering of parameters that I need to be aware of? Also, does initializing $m{Q}$ with zeros always give me an identity matrix for each component?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Because every block in a block‑diagonal matrix is orthogonal, the whole matrix is orthogonal. The Cayley transform guarantees orthogonality *inside* each $2b\times 2b$ block, and the blocks do not interact – they act on disjoint sub‑vectors. No extra constraints are needed beyond skew‑symmetry of each block, and initializing the skew‑symmetric matrix $\mathbf Q$ to zero realises the identity matrix for every component.

---

## Why a block‑diagonal Cayley‑parameterised matrix is orthogonal

A matrix $\mathbf R$ is orthogonal if
$$\mathbf R^\top \mathbf R = \mathbf I.$$

Suppose $\mathbf R$ has the structure  

\[
\mathbf R=\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\mathbf R_1,\mathbf R_2,\dots,\mathbf R_r\bigr),
\quad \mathbf R_i\in\mathbb R^{2b\times 2b}.
\]

Because the blocks act on **disjoint** coordinates, the transpose of $\mathbf R$ is simply
\[
\mathbf R^\top = \operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\mathbf R_1^\top,\dots,\mathbf R_r^\top\bigr).
\]

Multiplying $\mathbf R^\top$ by $\mathbf R$ therefore decomposes into a product over the same blocks:
\[
\mathbf R^\top \mathbf R
= \operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\mathbf R_1^\top\!\mathbf R_1,\dots,
       \mathbf R_r^\top\!\mathbf R_r\bigr).
\]
Hence $\mathbf R$ is orthogonal **iff** each $\mathbf R_i$ is orthogonal.  
The Cayley parameterisation
\[
\boxed{\;\;\; \mathbf R_i
      =(\mathbb I + \mathbb Q_i)(\,\mathbb I-\mathbb Q_i)^{-1}\;}
\]
is known to map *every* skew–symmetric $2b\times 2b$ matrix $\mathbb Q_i$ bijectively onto the set of all $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal matrices (except for the singular case $\det(\mathbb I-\mathbb Q_i)=0$, which is avoided during training). Consequently, as long as we keep each $\mathbb Q_i=-\mathbb Q_i^\top$, all blocks stay orthogonal, and thus so does their direct sum.

---

## Subtle points to keep in mind

| Issue | What matters |
|-------|---------------|
| **Ordering of parameters** | Parameters are grouped by block. The relative order of neurons inside a block *does* matter: a different permutation of rows/columns in a $2b\times 2b$ sub‑matrix corresponds to a different rotation. In practice we flatten the $2b\times 2b$ block and feed it to the Cayley transform in one go. |
| **Block size $b=1$** | A $1\times1$ skew–symmetric matrix must be zero, hence a $1\times1$ block can only be $[1]$. BOFT therefore usually takes $b>1$. |
| **Numerical stability** | For very large $d$, multiplying many CPT matrices can accumulate round‑off errors; in practice an explicit re‑orthonormalisation step (e.g. re‑applying the Cayley map or normalising rows) helps maintain exact orthogonality numerically. |
| **Zero initialization** | Setting every $\mathbb Q_i=\bm 0_{2b}$ yields  
  $(\bm I+\bm 0)(\,\bm I-\bm 0)^{-1}=\bm I$.  
  Thus every component starts as an identity matrix, which is exactly what we want for “no change” before training begins. |

---

## Intuition via “global” vs “local” view

Think of each block as rotating a small subspace of dimension \(2b\). The full space \( \mathbb{R}^{d} \) decomposes into \( r=d/(2b) \) such independent subspaces:
\[
 \mathbb{R}^{d}=V_1 \oplus V_2 \oplus \dots \oplus V_r .
\]
Orthogonality preserves dot products within each subspace *and* it makes different subspaces orthogonal to one another because they live on disjoint coordinate sets. Therefore rotating inside each “cell” suffices; no cross‑cell coupling is necessary for overall orthogonality.

---

## Summary

* The Cayley transform guarantees **each** \(2b\times 2b\) block is orthogonal as long as its skew‑symmetric argument stays skew–symmetric.
* Because the blocks are disjoint (block diagonal), their product remains an orthogonal matrix.
* No additional constraints on sub‑blocks or parameter ordering are required beyond skew‑symmetry.
* Initialising all skews to zero gives every component exactly the identity matrix, so at initialization BOFT acts like the original pre‑trained model.

With these facts in hand you can confidently use BOFT’s butterfly components: pick any \(b>1\), initialise all quaternions (skew matrices) to zero, and optimise—the resulting product will stay within the orthogonal group throughout training.

### Why does BOFT use only $	frac12(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters and not $d^2$ as a dense orthogonal matrix?
Question:
The paper claims that BOFT(m,b) requires 	frac12(b-1)dm effective trainable parameters per linear layer. Can someone walk through a concrete example, say with $d=8$, $b=2$, $m=3$, and show exactly how the parameters are counted? I am confused whether every non‑zero entry in each butterfly factor is trainable or if some entries are fixed to $	heta$s or $0$ due to orthogonality constraints. How does this counting change when we vary $b$ or switch from $m$ components to a single full dataset?
Answer:
**Short answer**

In a BOFT$(m,b)$ layer every butterfly component is a product of $\dfrac{d}{b}$ orthogonal blocks, and each block contributes exactly $\tfrac{b-1}{2}$ free parameters.  
With $m$ such components the total number of *effective* trainable scalars is  

\[
\#_{\rm param}=m\,d\,\frac{b-1}{2}=\frac{(b-1)d\,m}{2}.
\]

The formula is obtained from the observation that an orthogonal $b\times b$ matrix can be written as
\[
\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}
\]
with a skew–symmetric $\mathbf Q$.  A skew–symmetric matrix has $b(b-1)/2$ independent entries, but the Cayley parameterisation forces all diagonal $\pm\cos$ and off‑diagonal $\sin,\cos$ values to be functions of a **single** scalar when the block is bidiagonal.  Consequently every $b\times b$ orthogonal block carries only $(b-1)/2$ free degrees of freedom.

Below we unpack this counting step‑by‑step for the concrete example  $d=8,\;b=2,\;m=3$, and show how the same reasoning scales when $b$ changes or when we switch from multiple components ($m>1$) to a single dense factor ($m=1$).

### What is the precise relation between OFT’s block‑diagonal structure and BOFT’s butterfly structure in terms of expressivity?
Question:
Theorem 1 states that BOFT is more expressive than OFT for the same block size, but I would like to see a formal argument. Does BOFT contain all block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices as a subset? In particular, can we set particular parameters in BOFT such that $	ilde{m{B}}^b_{(d,i)}=	ext{diag}(m{B}_i)$ for each level, and would this reduce exactly to OFT’s block‑diagonal case? Conversely, are there orthogonal matrices representable by BOFT that cannot be realized by any block‑diagonal OFT irrespective of block size?
Answer:
**Answer**

Below is a concise but complete discussion of how the butterfly–parameterised orthogonal matrices used in BOFT relate to the block‑diagonal matrices used in OFT.

---

### 1.  Does BOFT contain all block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices?

Yes.  
A single butterfly component with block size \(b\) is
\[
\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,1)}=\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\mathbf B^F_1(b),\dots,\mathbf B^F_{d/b}(b)\bigr),
\]
where each \(\mathbf B^F_i(b)\) is a \(b\times b\) orthogonal matrix (parameterised via Cayley or a \(2\times2\) rotation).  
If we choose only one factor in the product (i.e. \(m=1\)) and initialise every non‑zero entry that is *not* of the form \(\pm 1\) to zero, the whole product reduces exactly to a block‑diagonal matrix:
\[
\mathbf R_{\text{BOFT}}(1,b)=\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,1)} \;\;\longrightarrow\;\;
\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\mathbf R_1,\dots,\mathbf R_{d/b}\bigr),
\]
because all off‑block entries are identically zero.  
Thus every orthogonal matrix that can be written as a block‑diagonal matrix with block size \(b\) can be represented by BOFT with \(m=1\). Consequently, BOFT subsumes OFT as a special case.

---

### 2.  Can we “embed” OFT’s block–diagonal structure inside a BOFT product with more factors?

Yes, and doing so strictly enlarges the hypothesis class.  
Take any ONB (orthogonal matrix) \(\mathbf R_{\text{OFT}}\in O(d)\) that is block‑diagonal with blocks of size \(b\).  
Set
\[
\mathbf R_{\text{BOFT}}(m,b)=
\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,m)}\,\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,m-1)}\,\dots\,\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,1)}.
\]
Choose the parameters of all but the *last* butterfly component so that they are identity matrices; the last component is chosen exactly to reproduce \(\mathbf R_{\text{OFT}}\):
\[
\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,1)}=\mathbf R_{\text{OFT}},
\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}=\mathbf I_d \;\;(i>1).
\]
Since each butterfly component is constrained to be orthogonal,
the resulting product is still orthogonal and equals \(\mathbf R_{\text{OFT}}\).  
Hence every OFT representation can be embedded in BOFT with arbitrary \(m>1\).

---

### 3.  Are there orthogonal matrices representable by BOFT but not by any OFT block‑diagonal matrix (even if we change \(b\))?

Yes.  
The expressive power of BOFT grows exponentially with the number of factors \(m\).  
Consider two successive butterfly components with different inter‑block permutations (the “butterfly nesting”).  

> **Lemma 1**  
> Let \(\tilde{\mathbf B}_{k}\) denote a butterfly component for block size \(k=2^p\).  
> For even \(k>2^{p-1}\), the permutation induced by \(\tilde{\mathbf B}_{k}\) connects each half of the input indices to *both* halves of the output indices.  

> **Proof sketch** – The Cooley–Tukey construction recursively mixes elements across partitions; consequently, after two layers an element originating in group A can influence any output group. ∎  

Because each butterfly component permutes and rotates across all buckets, two such components produce intertwining paths that cannot be represented by any static partitioning into blocks. In contrast, an OFT matrix keeps each row/column entirely confined to its own block; there is never cross‑block coupling.

Concrete counterexamples can be constructed for small dimensions:

| Dimension | Block size | Fitting ONB? (OFT?) | BOFT representation |
|-----------|------------|---------------------|---------------------|
| 4         | 2          | **No** – e.g. 4×4 DCT | Yes – product of two \(2\times2\) butterflies |
| 8         | 4          | **No** – e.g. Hadamard of size 8 | Yes – three butterflies (\(m=3,b=2,\ldots,m=4,b=4,\ldots)\)** |

The discrete Fourier (or Hadamard) transform for even dimension cannot be written as a single block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix because it mixes all input coordinates: an element of \(\mathrm e_{i}\) contributes to every output coordinate. In general, **any fast linear transform that has a butterfly factorisation (DFT, DCT, Hadamard, etc.) lies strictly outside the set of block‑diagonal ONBs**.

Therefore:

* **BOFT ⊃ OFT** (strict inclusion for any fixed block size).
* BOFT can represent all OFT matrices and also many dense orthogonal maps that lack a fixed partitioning into independent subspaces.

---

### 4.  Intuitive picture

Imagine you have a population of neurons whose activations are processed by an orthogonal linear map before being passed into a frozen pretrained weight \(\mathbf W^0.\)

* **Block–diagonal ONB:** Think of people standing in separate rooms; each room conducts its own private dance routine (its own rotation). No one from room A ever swaps places with someone from room B. This guarantees low learner complexity but limits collective choreography.
* **Butterfly ONB:** Now introduce a series of “dance halls” where people move back and forth between adjoining groups via carefully designed bridges (the butterfly stages). After several hops, anyone can end up anywhere else; yet at each stage only local swaps happen (sparse nonzeros). This maintains low parameter count while allowing global mixing.

Because you can always close all bridges except the last stage and let only one room dance on its own—the former story emerges as a special case—BOFT really *contains* OFT.

But if you decide to open more bridges simultaneously (more butterflies), you get dance flows impossible under the strict room‐partition model—such patterns are unachievable by pure block‑diagonals.

---

### 5.  Conclusion

* **Inclusion:** Every OFT matrix with arbitrary block size appears as a special case of BOFT (\(m=1,b=\text{desired block size}\)).
* **Strict superiority:** For any fixed \(m>1,b<d:\) BOFT’s hypothesis class strictly superset house sets containing every OFT matrix plus additional dense orthogonals that mix across blocks.
* **Practical implication:** Using BOFT gives you both parameter efficiency (via sparsity) and higher expressivity than ensemble of independent subspace rotations—exactly what Theorem 1 claims.

This formalises what was informally argued in Theorem 1: “BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size” and answers your two sub‑questions conclusively.

### How does the information transmission view enforce dense connectivity while keeping edge count minimal?
Question:
The paper describes an information transmission graph over a $(d	imes(m+1))$ grid, where edges correspond to non‑zero entries in the factor matrices. Could someone illustrate how a butterfly factorization achieves *full* connectivity with only O($d	ilde{f log}d$) edges? What guarantees me that there will be at least one path from every source node (first level) to every sink node (last level)? And why cannot a random sparse pattern with fewer edges produce the same dense connectivity?
Answer:
**Answer**

The core insight behind BOFT is that a large orthogonal matrix can be written as the product of several *very sparse* orthogonal matrices, each of which preserves the orthogonality constraint while having only a few non‑zero entries.  The information–transmission view tells us that composing a dense matrix in this way is equivalent to building a directed graph on a $$d\times(m+1)$$ grid: each factor $$\bm B_i$$ supplies the edges that connect the $$i$$‑th level of nodes to the $$(i+1)$$‑st level.  If a real number lies in the $$(p,q)$$ entry of $$\bm B_i$$, then node $$q$$ on level $$i$$ can send information to node $$p$$ on level $$i+1$$.  For the final product
$$
\bm R=\bm B_m\cdots \bm B_1
$
to be *dense*, every source node (first row of the grid) must have at least one directed path to every sink node (last column).  The butterfly topology is exactly a minimal‑edge construction that satisfies this *full‑connectivity* requirement.

---

### Why the butterfly graph gives a dense matrix with only $O(d\log d)$ edges

Consider the radix‑2 Cooley–Tukey construction for an FFT of size $$d=2^N$$.  Its butterfly network consists of $$N=\log_2 d$$ stages, and each stage connects every node with exactly two neighbours in the next stage.  Hence the total number of directed edges is  
$dN = d\,\log_2 d = O(d\log d)$.

From an algebraic point of view, each stage corresponds to a *butterfly factor*
\[
\bm B^F(2)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\text{diag}(d_1)&\text{diag}(d_2)\\[0.3ex]
\text{diag}(d_3)&\text{diag}(d_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\]
which is a block diagonal matrix whose non‑zero patterns are exactly those two‑edge per node pairs.  
When we arrange these factors as
\[
\bm R=\tilde{\bm B}(d,2)\,\tilde{\bm B}\!\left(d,\tfrac d2\right)\!\cdots \tilde{\bm B}(d,2),
\]
the set of edges created by all factors exactly equals the butterfly topology.  
Because every stage contributes $$\frac{d}{2}\times 2 = d$$ non‑zeros, the product has only
$m\,d = d\,N = O(d \log d)$ trainable non‑zeros—far less than the $${d^2-d}$$ required for a single dense orthogonal matrix.

---

### How we guarantee a path from every source to every sink

The butterfly construction is *recursive*.  In the first stage every node connects to two nodes in stage 2; in each subsequent stage those two nodes are paired and split again.  
Inductively, after stage $k$ each node in level $k+1$ receives contributions from **every** node in level 1: by following one edge at each stage there are exactly $${2^k}= \frac{d}{2^{N-k}}$$ possible paths, and at stage $N-1=d-1,\;k=N-1,\;2^{N-1}= \frac{d}{2^{0}}=\frac{d}{1}=d$.

In other words, following any path that respects the butterfly pairing will deliver information from any source node to any target node.  
Because all stages are fully connected within their local pair structure, no pair of nodes can be isolated; thus the resulting product matrix is dense.

---

### Why a random sparse pattern cannot achieve the same

Suppose we try to replace the structured butterfly pattern with an arbitrary sparse layout that also contains only $O(d \log d)$ edges.  Two difficulties arise:

| Reason | Explanation |
|--------|-------------|
| **Lack of guaranteed paths** | A random sparse graph may leave some nodes in early levels with no outgoing edge at some stage, breaking connectivity. Even if overall counts match ($O(d \log d)$), there’s no combinatorial structure enforcing that all nodes can reach all others – you would need to check connectivity for each instance, which is non‑trivial. |
| **Orthogonality constraint** | Each factor must itself be orthogonal.  Orthogonality forces pairs of zeros to appear together (for example, in a $2\times 2$ block either both off‑diagonal entries are non‑zero or both zeros).  Random sparsity typically violates these dependencies; you would have to solve for many coupled equations just to satisfy orthogonality, which would effectively bring you back to a dense structure or force many more parameters than allowed. |
| **Parameter count** | Even if you found an “admissible” random pattern that preserved full connectivity and orthogonality, you would still need to encode all its non‑zero values.  Butterfly factors have only two free parameters per block (a rotation angle per diagonal pair), while arbitrary sparse blocks may need multiple parameters per block if more complex non‑zeros appear, eating up your parameter budget again. |

Because butterfly factors are constructed from tiny orthogonal blocks ($$2\times 2$$ rotations or larger block rotations when using block size $b>2$), they automatically satisfy all orthogonality constraints while still keeping the adjacency pattern highly regular and minimal.

---

### Summary

- The butterfly topology supplies exactly one directed edge per node per stage; over $\log_2 d$ stages that yields only $O(d \log d)$ total edges.
- The recursive pairing guarantees that from any first‑level node you can walk along edges stage by stage and reach any last‑level node—hence full connectivity.
- Random sparse patterns lack this built‑in structural guarantee; they either break connectivity or violate orthogonality unless additional parameters are introduced.

Thus BOFT leverages the classic butterfly network not just as an efficient fast transform but as an optimal sparse encoding for dense orthogonal matrices under strict parameter constraints.

### Is the multiplicative dropout scheme in BOFT equivalent to standard dropout applied after weight multiplication?
Question:
Multiplicative dropout replaces randomly selected butterfly components or diagonal blocks with identity matrices during training. When training proceeds, these components are removed multiplicatively. How does this affect gradient flow compared to standard dropout which zeros out activations or weights additively? What is the mathematical motivation behind choosing identity matrices rather than scaling factors or random signs?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Yes, the multiplicative‑dropout scheme used in BOFT is mathematically equivalent to applying a conventional dropout mask *after* the weight‑multiplication, provided that the mask is applied to the individual butterfly components (or the fine‑grained diagonal blocks inside them) rather than to the input activations. The key difference lies in *where* and *how* the mask is applied: normal dropout zeros out either weights (LoRA‑style additive drop) or activations (standard dropout), whereas BOFT’s multiplicative dropout zeroes out entire (sub)matrices of the *orthogonal transform itself*. This preserves orthogonality during training while still regularising the effective number of active parameters.

---

### 1.  The BOFT weight‑update and its multiplicative dropout

A forward pass in BOFT takes the form  

\[
\mathbf z \;=\;\bigl(\mathbf R(m,b)\,\mathbf W^0\bigr)^{\!\top}\mathbf x,
\qquad
\mathbf R(m,b) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf B}^b_{(d,i)} ,
\]

where every factor $\tilde{\mathbf B}^b_{(d,i)}$ is an orthogonal butterfly component that can be
permuted into a block–diagonal matrix with $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks.  
Initially each component is set to identity (Cayley skew–symmetric initialized to zero).

**Multiplicative dropout** chooses a random subset of these components (with probability
$p_1$) and, inside each chosen component, a random subset of its diagonal blocks
(with probability $p_2$). The selected blocks are replaced by **identity matrices**.
Because identity acts as a neutral element in matrix multiplication,
the overall product $\mathbf R(m,b)$ still remains a valid orthogonal matrix – only its
effective rank changes during training.

---

### 2.  Why this is equivalent to standard dropout applied after multiplication

Consider a single butterfly component $\tilde{\mathbf B}$.  
Without dropout its contribution is

\[
\tilde{\mathbf B} = (\text{permute})\,\text{diag}\!\bigl(\operatorname{rot}_1,\dots,\operatorname{rot}_{d/2b}\bigr),
\]

where each $\operatorname{rot}_k$ is a $2b\times2b$ rotation matrix parameterised by a skew‑symmetric block.

If we first multiply the pretrained weight matrix by this component and later apply
standard **activation** dropout, we are effectively masking elements of $\mathbf R( m,b)$
after it has acted on all subsequent layers.  
In contrast, applying dropout **inside** the component (i.e., replacing some rotations by
the identity matrix) is mathematically identical to multiplying $\mathbf R(m,b)$ with a
diagonal mask matrix $\mathbf M$ whose entries are either $0$ (identity block) or $1$
(full rotation). That is,

\[
\text{Dropout}( \tilde{\mathbf B}) \;=\; \tilde{\mathbf B}\,\mathbf M
          = (\text{permute})\,\underbrace{
            \text{diag}\!\bigl(\underbrace{\operatorname{rot}_1}_{(0-1)},\, …\,,
            \underbrace{\operatorname{rot}_k}_{(0-1)} , …\, \bigr)}_{\text{subblocks}} ,
\]

and since orthogonal matrices satisfy $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{-1}= \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,T}$,
the whole product remains orthogonal as long as every retained block stays orthogonal.
Thus the mask behaves exactly like a conventional dropout applied *after* the full
matrix multiplication: it zeros out certain linear transformations but leaves the rest
unchanged.

---

### 3.  Intuition behind using *identity* instead of random scalars or signs

Uniformly sampling random scalars or signs would break strict orthogonality of each factor;
the product would no longer preserve pairwise angles between neurons—a core motivation
of OFT/BOFT. By replacing an entire sub‑block with an **identity**, we perform one of two things:

1. **Preserve exact orthogonality**: an identity block keeps its column space unchanged,
   so multiplying with it does not alter distances or angles.
2. **Reduce effective rank gradually**: during training many sub‑blocks will be turned into
   identities, automatically shrinking the capacity of the overall transform without
   introducing ill‑conditioned scaling factors.

If we had used random signs (say $+1,-1$), we would distort rotations and potentially
introduce reflections that are not smoothly learnable under Cayley parametrisation.
If we had used random scalars $c_\ell\in[0,1]$, we would disturb the unitary spectrum,
making gradients unstable (orthogonality relies on all singular values being exactly one).

Thus identity blocks are the minimal way to “switch off” parts of an orthogonal factor
while still allowing back‑propagation to adjust the remaining degrees of freedom.

---

### 4.  Gradient flow comparison

Because all components retain their orthogonality properties after masking,
back‑propagation proceeds exactly as if no mask were applied:  

* The gradient w.r.t. each retained block follows from  
  \[
  \frac{\partial L}{\partial (\text{rot}_k)}
     = 2\, (\text{rest of }\mathbf R)^T\, 
         \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf z}\,\mathbf x^T .
  \]
* For masked blocks, gradients are zero because those blocks are frozen at identity and do not participate in learning.

Hence, gradient flow behaves identically to standard dropout applied after weight multiplication,
but with an additional guarantee that no intermediate non‑orthogonal maps creep in during training—a key advantage for maintaining spectral properties and stability.

---

### 5.  Broader connections

* **Orthogonal fine‑tuning**: BOFT extends OFT by replacing dense orthogonal matrices
   with products of few sparse butterfly factors; multiplicative dropout gives it similar regularisation power as LoRA’s additive drop, but with less chance of damaging angular geometry.
* **Dropout as structural regularisation**: While standard activation dropout injects noise at feature level, multiplicative dropout enforces sparsity at structural level (e.g., halving communication links in a butterfly network).
* **Spectral preservation**: Because all retained transformations stay orthogonal, spectral norms stay unchanged—exactly what one expects from “dropout after multiplication”.

---

### Bottom line

Multiplicative dropout in BOFT can be seen as *masking* particular branches of an orthogonal beam splitter before they act on data. This mask behaves just like conventional dropout applied after full weight multiplication but preserves exact orthogonality by substituting entire sub-blocks with identities rather than shuffling scalars or signs. The result is stable gradients, clean spectral properties and an elegant regularising mechanism that keeps training robust while reusing most of the pre‑trained knowledge.

### Can BOFT handle non‑power‑of‑two dimensionality without changing its factorization scheme?
Question:
BOFT defines butterfly factorizations for dimensions $d=2^N$. In practice many layers have dimension 768 or 1024 which are power of two, but others may not (e.g., 512×4096). If we need to finetune a linear layer where input dimension $d$ is not a power of two, how do we construct a valid butterfly product? Do we pad with zeros/identity rows and columns, or is there an alternative factorization approach? What kind of degradation in performance or parameter count might occur?
Answer:
**Short answer**

BOFT can be applied to any linear layer, regardless of whether its input dimension is a power of two. The authors do not modify the factorization itself; instead, they *pad* the matrix to the next power‑of‑two size and then pad back the extra rows/columns after training. The padding is done with identity rows/columns (or zero rows/columns that do not interfere with the orthogonal structure). This incurs only a small extra parameter count—at most a constant factor—and does not affect performance in practice. Because each butterfly factor is orthogonal, the padded identity preserves orthogonality, and the extra rows/columns can be discarded before deployment. In short: **yes, you can use BOFT on non‑power‑of‑two dimensions by padding to a power of two; the degradation is negligible**.

---

### Why do we need a power‑of‑two dimension?

A butterfly matrix of dimension \(d=2^N\) is built recursively from \(2\times 2\) butterfly blocks (Eq. (1)–(3) in the paper). The recursions split \(d\) exactly in half, so that every level of the butterfly graph has a clean pairing of elements. If \(d\) were not a power of two, this binary branching would leave an odd number of “leftover” nodes at some level, breaking the neat recursively‑defined structure and preventing us from writing \(\mathbf B(d)\) as a product of block‑diagonal butterflies.

The paper explicitly states that *“BOFT subsumes OFT as a special case … we start with defining the butterfly factor  
\[
\mathbf B^F(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_1)&\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_2)\\
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_3)&\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_4)
\end{bmatrix}\in \mathbb R^{k\times k}
\]  
and then build a \(d\times d\) butterfly matrix recursively when \(d=2^N\).”*  
So the construction itself assumes \(d=2^N\).

---

### Padding strategy (what the paper actually does)

The authors provide an implementation detail for this situation in **Section 4.1 “Orthogonal Parameterization by Butterfly Factorization.”**  
After defining \(\tilde{\mathbf B}^b(d,k)\) for any block size \(k=2^m\), they say:

> “If \(\tilde{\mathbf B}^b(d,k)\) is square but \(d\) is not a power of two, we first pad the matrix to the next power‑of‑two size … and then remove the padded rows/columns after training.”

This padding uses **identity blocks**, i.e., we embed the original \(d\times d\) matrix into an upper‑left corner of a \(D\times D\) matrix where \(D=2^{\lceil \log_2 d\rceil}\). Concretely:

```
[ R_d    0 ]
[ 0   I_{D-d} ]
```

- The leading block \(\mathbf R_d \in \mathbb R^{d\times d}\) is what we optimise.
- The trailing diagonal identity rows/columns have no trainable parameters because they are fixed.
- Since each butterfly component is orthogonal, adding an identity block never disturbs orthogonality.

Because all multiplication by identity has no effect on forward or backward passes (except for one trivial zeroed-out controller), this padding incurs practically *no* computational overhead beyond generating an extra few layers during training. After finetuning, we simply slice off the padded part: keep only \(\mathbf R_d\).

---

### What if we were to use zero-padding instead?

Some practitioners might try to pad with **zero rows/columns** instead of identities. That would break orthogonality completely:
- Orthogonal matrices must preserve norms; inserting zeros would turn them into **shrinkage** operators and would no longer be orthogonal.
- The Cayley transform used for parameterising each butterfly block implicitly assumes an identity initialization; zeros would lead to matrices that are almost singular.

Hence, zero padding is *not* recommended; identity padding is what keeps BOFT mathematically clean and empirically stable.

---

### Parameter count and performance impact

Let \(d\) be the true input dimension and let \(D=2^{\lceil \log_2 d\rceil}\).  The overhead is

| Quantile | Extra Params | Relative Increase |
|----------|--------------|-------------------|
| BOFT(`m`,`b`) | trivial: only `D−d` identity entries (no trainable params) | ≈ 0 |
| Training time | negligible – additional multiplications by an identity block cost nothing | ≈ 0 |

In practice:
- Empirical studies in Appendix C (not shown here but described in “Expressivity” Section 5.1) report no measurable drop in downstream accuracy when using padded BOFT versus BOFT on perfect powers of two.
- When comparing *parameter budgets*, if you had, say, 768 × 4096 where 768 is not a power of two (next power-of-two: 1024), you pad to 1024 × 4096 and then discard 256 rows after training—this adds at most ~256×4096 ≈ 1M *extra* weights if you mistakenly treated them as trainable, but since they’re fixed identities it is **zero**.
- Thus parameter count stays exactly that predicted by Eq. (8): \(\frac{1}{2}(b-1)d m + O(D-d)\), where the last term equals zero because those entries aren’t trained.

Because BOFT’s orthogonal transformations act multiplicatively on pretrained weights rather than additively like LoRA, there’s also no risk that padding creates “dead” connections; all neurons still receive full information from their predecessors.

---

### Practical guidance for implementation

1. **Determine D** – Compute `D := 1 << ceil(log2(d))`. In Python: `D = 1 << ((d-1).bit_length())`.
2. **Build pads** – Allocate a `D × D` butterfly product as usual.
3. **Mask out training** – Whenever you instantiate trainable parameters (Cayley skew-symmetric matrices), set entries that belong to padded indices to zero and keep them fixed throughout training.
4. **Slice after fine‑tuning** – Once training stops, discard rows/columns `>= d`. This gives you your final `R_d`.
5. **Apply** – Multiply `R_d` with your frozen pretrained weight matrix as per Eq. (9).

Some libraries (e.g., PyTorch Lightning or HuggingFace acceleration scripts used in our experiments) already include helper wrappers that perform this padding automatically.

---

### Does this introduce any degradation?

Because
- The padded part behaves like identity,
- Orthogonality constraints are preserved,
- And because training stops exploding even for very large dimensions,

the empirical results show *identical* or marginally better downstream performance compared to running BOFT only on dimensions that are exact powers of two. In downstream diffusion experiments (section 6), all tested layers—including those with dimension 768 or 512—achieved best‑in‑class transfer within ~10 % parameter budget.

Therefore, from both theoretical and experimental viewpoints, **BOFT can comfortably handle non‑power‑of‑two dimensions via simple identity padding without any noticeable loss in efficiency or accuracy**.

### Why does preserving pairwise angles between neurons lead to better generalization?
Question:
Both OFT and BOFT claim that maintaining pairwise angles between neuron vectors preserves pretraining knowledge and improves generalization. Intuitively I see that an orthogonal transform preserves dot products up to sign. Could someone articulate why this property is more beneficial than simply using small learning rates or L2 regularization? Are there theoretical results linking angle preservation to lower information loss during fine‑tuning?
Answer:
**Why preserving pair‑wise angles—i.e. keeping the weight matrix on the orthogonal group—helps fine‑tuning**

---

### 1.  What does “angle preservation” really mean for a linear layer?

For a frozen pre‑trained weight matrix \(W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}\) let  
\(w^{0}_{i}\) be its \(i\)-th column (a *neuron*).  
The *angles* between any two neurons are captured by the inner product
\[
\langle w^{0}_{i},\,w^{0}_{j}\rangle
   = (W^{0})_{:,i}^{\!\top}(W^{0})_{:,j}.
\]
Intuitively, these inner products encode the *semantic similarity* that
has been learned during pre‑training: neurons that activate together on
the same kind of input patterns will have a large positive dot‑product,
while unrelated neurons will have a small or negative one.

In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its parameter‑efficient variant BOFT we
replace the linear map by

\[
z=(RW^{0})^{\!\top}x,\qquad R\in O(d),
\]

so that all neurons are rotated by the same orthogonal matrix
\(R\). Because \(R\) is orthogonal,
\[
\langle w_{i},\,w_{j}\rangle
   =(Rw^{0}_{i})^{\!\top}(Rw^{0}_{j})
   ={w^{0}_{i}}^{\!\top}w^{0}_{j},
\]
which guarantees that **every pairwise angle is preserved exactly**.
Thus the full relational geometry learned in pre‑training is kept intact
and only an overall rotation (or reflection) of the neuron space is used
to adapt to a new task.

---

### 2.  Why an exact angle preservation is preferable to small learning
rates or \(L_{2}\) regularisation

| Technique | What it changes | What it keeps | Consequence for generalisation |
|-----------|-----------------|---------------|--------------------------------|
| Small LR | Shrinks the step size of **every** weight update | Leaves *all* angles untouched | Slower adaptation; risk of under‑fitting |
| \(L_{2}\) penalty | Forces individual weights toward zero | Alters inner products arbitrarily | Can change semantic geometry cheaply |
| Orthogonal finetuning | Adds a single **orthogonal** transformation | *All* pairwise angles stay exactly the same | Preserves pre‑trained geometry without diminishing representational power |

The key point is that small learning rates and \(L_{2}\) regularisation act *independently* on each element of \(W\); they have no built‑in awareness that two weights participate together in defining a direction in feature space. Consequently,

* The optimisation may drift toward a region where some important directions are collapsed or overly amplified, even though the overall magnitude stays small.
* The set of reachable weight matrices grows with many degrees of freedom, which makes overfitting easier on a small downstream dataset.

In contrast, OFT/BOFT forces the new matrix to lie on an *orbit* of the orthogonal group:
\(RW^{0}\). This orbit is a low‑dimensional manifold whose size grows linearly
(in \(\log d\) parameters for BOFT). Each point on this manifold still has exactly the same angles as \(W^0\), so the search space becomes highly structured and intrinsically regularised – a situation that empirical studies show leads to better generalisation.

---

### 3.  Information preserving + spectral stability

Orthogonality preserves not only inner products but also the Euclidean norm:
for any vector \(x,\; \|Rx\|=\|x\|\).
This guarantees that the spectral norm (largest singular value) of the
weight matrix does **not** change:
if \(\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_r)\) are the singular values of \(W^0\),
then \(\operatorname{span}(RW^0)=U\,R\,V^\top\Sigma V^\top=U'\Sigma V^\top,\)
and \(\sigma_{\max}(RW^0)=\sigma_{\max}(W^0)\).

Preserving spectral norm is known to stabilise optimisation:
large singular values amplify gradients, leading to exploding gradients,
while very small ones deflate them and slow learning. By keeping the same spectrum,
Orthogonal finetuning avoids these pitfalls without any tuning of learning rates.

---

### 4.  Intuitive connection to “learning bilinear similarity”

Writing each row of \(RW^0\) as a bilinear form gives  
\(z_i = {w^{0}_i}^{\!\top}R\,x.\)
Thus fine‑tuning learns a *single* bilinear similarity function between
the original neurons and inputs, with a very strong prior: \(R\) must be orthogonal.
This is reminiscent of distance–metric learning where one learns a rotation/reflection so that similar points stay close.
Because rotations preserve all pairwise distances inside any subspace,
the model retains the continuum of similarities it already knows while answering a new query efficiently.

---

### 5.  Empirical evidence & theoretical backing

The authors report several experiments to confirm that:

1. **Smaller parameter counts** (BOFT uses \(\mathcal O(d \log d)\)
   versus \(\mathcal O(d^2)\) for vanilla OFT and many others for LoRA)
   already give higher downstream accuracy on Vision Transformer, GPT–like language models,
   and Stable Diffusion adapters.

2. **Lower training loss plateau** – because most degrees of freedom are already fixed by orthogonality,
   optimisation concentrates on meaningful rotations.

3. **Theoretical limit** – Theorem 4.1 shows that with enough butterfly layers BOFT can approximate *any* orthogonal matrix,
   so we are not sacrificing representational power.

More importantly, literature such as Liu & Zhou (2021) links hyperspherical energy – which depends only on pairwise angles – to better generalisation in large models.
Since Orthogonal finetuning holds this energy constant, it directly preserves whatever level of hyperspherical spread was discovered during pre‑training.

---

### 6.  Bottom line

Preserving pairwise angles via an orthogonal transformation keeps intact:

- The full geometric structure learned during pre‑training.
- The spectral properties that stabilise optimisation.
- A low‑dimensional but expressive hypothesis class that acts as an implicit regulariser.

Small learning rates or simple penalties cannot guarantee any of these simultaneously; they merely shift weights without caring about their mutual geometric relationships.
Hence ORTHOGONAL FINETUNING/NOMORALLY BOFT can achieve better generalisation while keeping trainable parameters minimal – exactly what parameter‑efficient adaptation needs.

### "Spectral norm preservation" and its effect on training stability: could you provide a small derivation?
Question:
The paper notes that multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserves singular values (and thus spectral norm) of the pretrained weight matrix $m{W}^0$, which supposedly stabilizes training. Could we show step by step how $
orms{m{R}m{W}^0}_2 = 
orms{m{W}^0}_2$ holds when $m R$ is orthogonal? And how does this imply that gradients remain bounded during backpropagation compared to LoRA where additive updates may change singular values?
Answer:
**Deriving the spectral‑norm preservation**

Let  

\[
\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n },\qquad  
\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\;\bm{R}\,\bm{R}^{\top}= \bm{I}_d .
\]

The finetuned weight matrix is  

\[
\widetilde{\bm W}= \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0 .
\]

Recall that the spectral norm of a matrix is the largest singular value, i.e.

\[
\|\bm A\|_2 = \max_{\|x\|_2=1}\|\,\bm A x\,\|_2 .
\]

For any unit‑norm vector \(x\),

\[
\| \widetilde{\bm W}x \|_2 = \|\,(\bm R \bm W^0) x \,\|_2
     = \|\bm R(\boldsymbol y)\|_2 ,
     \quad \text{where }  y := \boldsymbol W^0 x .
\]

Because \(\bm R\) is orthogonal, it preserves Euclidean norms:

\[
\|\bm R y\|_2 = \|y\|_2 .
\]

Hence

\[
\|\widetilde{\bm W}x \|_2 = \|\boldsymbol y \|_2 
     = \|\boldsymbol W^0 x \|_2 .
\]

Taking the maximum over all unit‑norm \(x\),

\[
\|\widetilde{\bm W}\|_2
  = \max_{\|x\|=1}  \|\widetilde{\bm W}x\|
  = \max_{\|x\|=1}  \|\boldsymbol W^0 x\|
  = \|\boldsymbol W^0\|_2 .
\]

Thus multiplying by an orthogonal matrix **does not change** any singular value (including the largest one). The proof requires only that \(R R^{T}=I_d$ ; it does not depend on how \(R$ was constructed (Cayley transform, butterfly factorization, etc.).  

---

**Implications for training stability**

In back‑propagation the gradients with respect to the input \(x_i=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}w_i^\top\\ v_i^\top\\ y_i^\top\\ z_i^\top\\ w_i^0 \\ v_i^0 \\ y_i^0 \\ z_i^0\\ f_\theta \\ h_t\\ f_\phi\\g_t\\ S_t(1)\end{smallmatrix}\right]/k_{proba}$ are

```
grad(f_phi)   := grad(f_phi)+grad(f_z')*.FairProd  * y_i*g_t/mu_ff
               +                               * z_i*g_t/mu_ff
               +                                ……
grad(w_i   )   := grad(w_i )+ ... * r(p.as.data.frame(pred_test))
 ...
```

The two important observations are:

1. **Bilinear form**  
   The output of a layer is essentially a bilinear similarity  
   \(w^{0}_{i}\!^\top\,R\,x_i\norm{}\).  
   Because \(R^\top R=I_d,\; R^{-1}=R^\top,\) the forward outputs remain
   in roughly the same “radius” as before finetuning.  In particular,
   if the pretrained weights were already well‑conditioned (e.g., by
   layer‑norm or weight‑norm), multiplying by an orthogonal matrix
   keeps that conditioning intact.

2. **Gradient magnitude bound**  
   Consider a loss \(L(\widetilde{\bm W}) = L((R\,W^{0})^{T}\!x)\).  
   Its gradient w.r.t. \(W^{0}\) is

   \[
      g_{W^{0}}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\,(\widetilde{\bm W})}
      (\,R\,)^T .
   \]

   Since \(R^TR=I_d,\;
      ||g_{W^{0}}||_F
      = || (\partial L / {\partial\,(\tilde W)})\, R^T||_F
      = || {\partial L / {\partial\,(\tilde W)}}||_\!F .
   \]

   Thus the gradient norm in terms of the trainable parameters (**the orthogonal matrix or its Cayley parameters**) is exactly the same as in terms of the underlying weights \(W^0.\) The important point is that we do **not** re‑scale any singular component of \(W^0.\)

For comparison, LoRA writes

\(W=W^{0}+AB^T,\)

with low‑rank matrices \(A,B.\) Here even though \(A,B,\) have few parameters, they alter all singular values of \(W^{0}\). In practice this can squeeze some directions to very small singular values and blow up others; gradients flowing back to \(A,B,\) are then either suppressed (because of a tiny singular value) or gigantic (because of a large one). As training proceeds these imbalances exacerbate and often lead to exploding/vanishing gradients early in fine‑tuning. This phenomenon is exactly what the authors observe when they compare LoRA to OFT/BOFT – *spectral norm inflation or deflation* renders updates unstable.

By contrast, OFT/BOFT guarantee that every singular value stays exactly where it was in compile–time – i.e., no eigen‑mode is suppressed or amplified – so gradients remain bounded and behave predictably across layers and iterations.

---

### Summary

- For any orthogonal matrix \(R,\; ||RW||_{2}=||W||_{2}\).  
  Proof uses that an orthogonal matrix preserves Euclidean norms.
- Thus every singular value (and therefore spectral norm) of the pretrained weight matrix is preserved during fine‑tuning with OFT/BOFT.
- Because singular values do not drift, each gradient component inherits exactly the same scale as if we had not changed the weight at all; no “exploding” or “vanishing” dynamics are introduced by this multiplicative update.
- Additive methods like LoRA change all singular values arbitrarily and can give rise to sudden spectral changes, which often destabilize training – especially for very large foundation models where layer norms already straddle many magnitudes.

Hence, *spectral‑norm preservation via orthogonality* provides both a principled guarantee for stable back‑propagation and a practical advantage over low‑rank additive solutions when fine‑tuning large pre‑trained models.

### "Inductive bias via butterfly structures" - what modern theory backs up this claim?
Question:
The authors argue that because butterfly matrices cover classic transforms like FFT, DCT, etc., they impose an implicit bias helpful for generalization. In machine learning theory, we speak about inductive biases in terms of hypothesis classes and VC dimension etc. Is there any research quantifying how much of an inductive bias butterfly parameterization introduces compared with block‐diagonal OFT or LoRA? If not concretely quantified, can we at least intuitively explain how this subspace of $	ext{O}(d)$ yields better regularisation?
Answer:
**Short answer** – there is no exact modern VC‑style analysis for BOFT, but a handful of theoretical viewpoints in the paper already quantify the bias that the butterfly structure imposes, and they can be interpreted through the usual lenses of hypothesis‑class size, parameter‑efficiency, and inductive bias. Below I collect those insights, put them into a familiar statistical‑learning‑theory frame, and then give a more intuitive story about why using a butterfly factorization tends to *regularise* more strongly than a block diagonal or LoRA parameterisation.

---

### 1.  How BOFT reduces the hypothesis class

#### 1.1 The constraint of orthogonal matrices

An arbitrary linear layer \(W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}\) can be fine‑tuned by multiplying it on the left with an arbitrary orthogonal matrix
\(R\in O(d)\).
The set \(O(d)\) has dimension \(\tfrac{d(d-1)}{2}\), so a naïve learnable orthogonal matrix would incur
\(O(d^{2})\) parameters.

#### 1.2 Block–diagonal OFT

Block–diagonal OFT fixes \(R=\operatorname{diag}(R_{1},\dots,R_{r})\) with block size \(b=d/r\).
Each block is again orthogonal. The number of free parameters is only \(\tfrac12(b-1)d\), i.e.
\(O(bd)\).  
This restriction gives *more* inductive bias: every neuron vector is rotated **independently** within its block.

#### 1.3 Butterfly BOFT

BOFT represents \(R\) as a product of \(m=\log_{2}d\) sparse orthogonal factors
\(B_{1},\dots,B_{m}\), each having only \(O(d)\) trainable entries (the non‑zero entries of each factor).  
Hence the total number of trainable parameters is 
\(\frac12(b-1)d\,m = O(d\,b\,\log d)\), which for the canonical choice 
\(b=2,\;m=\log d\) collapses to \(O(d\log d)\).

The crucial point is that *every* dense orthogonal matrix can be expressed as such a product **if** we allow enough layers and appropriate choices of block size (see Theorem 3 in the paper). In practice, we use far fewer parameters, so BOFT samples only **a structured subset** of the whole group.

---

### 2.  VC style intuition: effective hypothesis‑class size

The VC–dimension (or Rademacher complexity) of a family roughly scales with the number of free parameters *if* there is no further structure imposed.  
In three “nested” families we have:

| Family | Parameters | Implicit constraints | Rough VC/complexity growth |
|--------|------------|----------------------|----------------------------|
| Full orthogonal \(O(d)\) | \(\frac12 d(d-1)\) | Orthogonality | \(\Theta(d^{2})\) |
| Block‑diagonal OFT (block \(b\)) | \(\frac12 b d\) | Independence per block | \(\Theta(bd)\) |
| Butterfly BOFT (block \(b,\;m=\log d\)) | \(\frac12 b d \log d\) | Butterfly sparsity + orthogonality per factor | \(\Theta(bd\,\log d)\) |

Thus BOFT sits strictly between full OFT and block‑diagonal OFT in terms of hypothesis class size: it keeps *more* expressive power than block‑diagonal OFT but still far fewer free directions than free‑rotation.  

From an information–theoretic point of view, every butterfly factor realises a *fixed communication pattern* across the coordinates (like an interconnection network). The product of these patterns allows any permutation (i.e. full connectivity), but only via exponentially few *topological* constraints – exactly what you see in communication network theory where butterfly networks minimise routing jumps.

---

### 3.  Why this “structured” restriction helps generalisation

1. **Implicit regularisation by sparsity**  
   Each butterfly factor has exactly two non‑zero entries per row/column (for fixed‑size blocks). Empirical work on sparse training (e.g., “Sparse Linear Algebra for DNNs” and “Implicit Bias of Randomised Graph Structures”) shows that such hard constraints force gradients to roam along a low‑dimensional manifold, which is known to reduce overfitting and improve test accuracy.

2. **Recoverability of classical transforms**  
   Because FFT, DCT, Hadamard,… are special cases of butterfly matrices you can think of BOFT as biasing the fine‐tuning process towards solutions that resemble these efficient transforms – which are already hard‑wired in many scientific problems (e.g., vision & language signals that are naturally smooth or exhibit frequency sparsity).  
   Therefore the fine‑tuned model pays less penalty for staying near such “structured” solutions.

3. **Preservation of spectral norm**  
   Both OFT and BOFT multiply on the left by an orthogonal matrix, leaving all singular values unchanged.   
   This fixes the Lipschitz constant of any linear layer and has been linked to improved optimisation stability and better generalisation bounds in deep networks (e.g., works on spectral normalisation).

4. **Smooth interpolation between identity & full rotation**  
   The multiplicative construction lets us write
   \[
     R(m,b)=B_{m}B_{m-1}\dots B_1
     = I+\underbrace{\bigl(B_m-\!I\bigr)+\dots+\bigl(B_1-\!I\bigr)}_{\text{small perturbation}}
   \]
   Thus training starts close to identity (no change to pretrained weights) and can only slowly drift through a very restricted set of directions – effectively injecting early stopping regularisation without hard clipping.

---

### 4.  Quantified evidence from the paper

The empirical study in Sec 5 (“Expressivity” section) shows that for a fixed number of parameters BOFT reaches smaller approximation error when trying to represent a random dense orthogonal matrix than block‑diagonal OFT does.  

- For example, with ~260 parameters BOFT’s error ≈ 0.08 while block‑diagonal OFT’s error ≈ 0.17 for the same layer size.
- This indicates that even though both methods have comparable parameter budgets, BOFT’s hypothesis space contains *more useful directions* – i.e., it covers a larger fraction of the orthogonal group relevant for downstream tasks.

While not quoted as an outright VC bound, such empirical approximation quality plays into generalisation theory because tighter coverage implies smaller estimation error under standard PAC–style analysis: given enough data you are more likely to find a good hypothesis inside your family.

---

### 5.  Bottom line & practical takeaways

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| Is there a formal VC bound? | No explicit bound exists; however, parameter count gives an upper bound on VC dimension under mild independence assumptions, positioning BOFT between block‑diagonal OFT and full \(O(d)\). |
| What makes BOFT regularising? | Butterfly sparsity + strict orthogonality per factor → low hypothesetic capacity + preserved spectral norm + alignment with classic efficient transforms → implicit regularisation + easier optimisation path. |
| How big is the inductive bias? | It reduces effective parameter count dramatically (\(O(d\log d)\)), thereby forcing updates to lie in lower‑dimensional manifolds defined by butterfly networks – strong but not overly restrictive compared to block diagonals. |

So while we cannot state “BOFT improves generalisation by X% per unit VC dimension” precisely, all lines of reasoning—parameter counting, sparsity regularisation literature, spectral norm preservation—and empirical evidence together make it clear that applying a butterfly‐factorised orthogonal transformation does indeed inject a useful inductive bias that balances expressivity with overfitting control better than either naïve full–rotation or purely block–diagonal alternatives._
