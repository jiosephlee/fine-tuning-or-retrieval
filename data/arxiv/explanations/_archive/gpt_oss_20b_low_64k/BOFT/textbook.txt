# Foundations of Model Adaptation

Large foundation models sit at the heart of modern artificial intelligence.  They are built by training on orders of billions—or even trillions—of parameters using massive compute clusters and terabytes of unlabeled data.  The cost of training a new foundation model from scratch can easily reach millions of dollars and weeks or months of GPU time.  Consequently, most researchers and practitioners now view existing large models as immutable assets that can be harnessed for a variety of downstream tasks without retraining them entirely from scratch.  This practical reality has sparked an intense focus on **model adaptation**: the process of molding an off‑the‑shelf foundation model to perform well on a specific task such as image classification, language understanding, or text‑to‑image generation.

Three major paradigms have emerged for adapting large models without incurring the overhead of full‑scale training:

1. **Full fine‑tuning** – the original pretrained weights are treated exactly as they were learned, and all parameters are updated under a reduced learning rate or with regularization techniques to avoid catastrophic forgetting.  While simple to implement—one merely hooks into the existing training loop—the price is that the entire parameterset must be stored in memory during both training and inference.  For the largest models this is infeasible in many deployment settings.

2. **Adapters** – these introduce tiny neural modules (often a few linear layers) inserted into each layer of the pre-trained model.  The backbone remains frozen, while only the adapter weights are tuned.  Adapters have proven powerfully efficient because they impose an inductive bias that modulates the representation flow in a controlled manner, yet they add a structural burden (the extra tensors) during inference and increase memory usage proportionally to the number of adapters.

3. **Prompt tuning** – originally devised for transformer‑based language models, prompt tuning prepends learnable vectors (or “prefix tokens”) to the input sequence so that the foundational body can be guided toward task‑specific behavior without any weight updates inside the backbone.  Prompt tuning has extraordinary efficiency at inference but is limited in representational capacity compared to weight changes.

While each paradigm offers trade‑offs between computational overhead, memory footprint, and adaptivity, **parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning (PEFT)** has emerged as a compelling middle ground: it relaxes hardly retraining all weights but still directly adjusts parameters of interest in a structured way that preserves much of the backbone’s learned semantics.

## Large Foundation Models & Training Cost

The soaring size trend in foundation models—GPT‑3 with ~175 B parameters, PaLM with over 540 B—comes hand in hand with escalating compute budgets.  Even modest scaling from millions to billions of parameters multiplies data collection, optimizer stability checks, and hyperparameter sweeps commands hours or days on large GPU fleets.  As such it is prohibitive for any but a handful of organizations to train such models anew; for most developers who intend to apply state‑of‑the‑art capabilities to domain problems (healthcare imaging, legal document classification), adapting an existing model is both economically and practically necessary.

Moreover, when passing from pretraining tasks (e.g., next word token prediction) to downstream uses (e.g., image caption generation), naive transfer often leads to catastrophic forgetting: freely adjusting weights disrupts semantic structures learned over massive corpora or image distributions.  Therefore we need adaptation techniques that **retain as much as possible** the knowledge encoded by the original training while injecting task‑specific adjustments.

## Three Mainstream Adaptation Paradigms

The three principal adaptation paradigms differ primarily in *where* and *how much* changes are applied within the network:

- **Finetuning / Full Update**  
  Every element \(w_{ij}\) in every weight matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}\) receives gradient updates during downstream training. This imposes no structural constraints; however it means storing duplicated copies of all \(W\)’s gradients (tens/hundreds of gigabytes for state‑of‑the‑art nets), adversely affecting scalability.

- **Adapter Tuning**  
  Each transformer layer \(L\) is augmented with a small module \(A_L\), typically parameterized as two linear projections with dimension reduction \(d \rightarrow r \rightarrow d\). Only \(A_L\)’s weights are updated; \(L\)’s original weights stay frozen. Because adapters re‐parameterize through a bottleneck dimension \(r\), their overall count scales roughly as \(O(r^2 L)\), markedly smaller than full fine‑tuning but still nontrivial when \(L\) is large.

- **Prompt Tuning**  
  Here we learn contiguous *prefix* vectors \(\{p_t\}_{t=1}^{T}\) added before each token embedding into transformer layers; effectively we only tune a handful of vectors independent from weight matrices themselves. In practice prompts must be sufficiently long or contextually rich to meaningfully alter internal activations—otherwise downstream performance drops precipitously.

Each strategy represents a different trade between *model capacity*, *memory consumption*, and *training overhead*. But common among all modern PEFT methods is an underlying desire: reparameterizing existing weights or embeddings so that updates remain compact yet powerful enough to encode task success while avoiding destabilizing core representations.

## Why Model Fine‑Tuning Matters

Despite its apparent simplicity—just “tell it what you want” via gradient descent—model fine‑tuning occupies a privileged position among adaptation methods for several reasons:

1. **Zero Inference Overhead**  
   Since fine‑tuned weights ultimately replace pretrained ones at inference time, there is no extra computational graph added; predictions can run exactly as before. Conversely adapters add an extra linear pass per layer yielding marginal latency spikes; prompts induce extra computation only on embeddings but may incur attention overhead depending on implementation.

2. **Preservation of Learned Semantics**  
   When fine tuning is performed conservatively (e.g., low learning rates or explicit constraints), it can rectify small deviations from desired outputs while maintaining global structural properties such as spectral norm or orthogonality which contribute to stability during inference.

3. **Uniformity Across Architectures**  
   Fine tuning works seamlessly on any architecture: convolutional nets, transformers, diffusion models, etc., because it simply adjusts weight tensors following whichever operator they define (matrix multiplication, convolution). Adapters or prompts may require architectural tweaks (extra projection layers or embedding offsets).

4. **Fine Control Over Weights**  
   Since all updates directly modify weight values linearly contributing to forward activations, neither sparsity induced by pruning nor masked updates complicate optimization dynamics beyond standard backpropagation—the optimizer sees exactly what everyone else sees.

These attributes underscored why fine tuning had historically been dominant among PEFT efforts: its straightforwardness matched the simplicity praised by early transfer learning literature (e.g., freezing convolutional layers while retraining fully connected heads).  

However once performance budgets demanded *extreme parametric thrift*, researchers began looking for more disciplined ways to restrict how many variables actually move during adaptation while keeping them expressive enough to preserve global structure—a key motivation behind structured reparameterization techniques such as LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) and Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT).

## Role of Structured Reparameterization (LoRA & OFT)

Structured reparameterization tags every degree-of-freedom we allow to change with some mathematical object that intrinsically enforces desirable properties:

- **LoRA** injects low-rank updates into each layer's weight matrix:
  \[
    W_{\text{fin}} = W_0 + A B^\top,
  \]
  where \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r}\) and \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\).  
  The rank \(r\) controls how many degrees are freely altered; automatically this shrinks trainable parameters from \(O(dn)\) down to \(O(dn r/(d+n))\). Because rank deficiency preserves spectral norm properties differently from full update—it keeps columns approximately orthogonal but can benefit from overparameterized regimes—LoRA has gained traction for efficient downstream finetuning across vision transformers and language models alike.

- **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** takes inspiration from ideas in hyperspherical energy minimization: instead of merely adding corrections we *left-multiply* all columns by an orthogonal transform:
  \[
    W_{\text{fin}} = R\,W_0,
  \]
  with \(R^\top R = I_d\). This keeps pairwise angles between neuron vectors constant throughout training – therefore preserving relational geometry inherent in pretraining representations – while affording powerful transformations via rotation/reflection without altering singular values weighted by Frobenius norms or spectral norms.

Both LoRA and OFT thus encode *structured constraints*: LoRA through low-rank modifications sparsifying across dimensions; OFT through orthogonality at every update step ensuring no drift away from pretraining norms.

Yet each method still suffers from practical inefficiencies worthy of attention:

- LoRA introduces additional parameters solely related to rank choice; too small rank undermines expressing needed transformations.
- OFT’s orthogonal matrix can be huge (\(d\times d\)), costing up to \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) trainable variable counts unless block-diagonal restrictions reduce size at cost of expressiveness deficits.

The quest hence becomes clear: design an *orthogonal* parameterization that remains dense enough to imitate arbitrary rotations but uses only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) trainable entries—a challenge elegantly addressed by butterfly factorized orthogonal matrices introduced later in this volume under BOFT (‘Orthogonal Butterfly’).

**2. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) in Depth**

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a principled approach to adapt a pretrained neural network to a downstream task while preserving the geometric structure of the original weight matrices. Instead of adding or scaling individual weights, OFT applies a single orthogonal transformation to each entire layer. This simple multiplicative update guarantees that pair‑wise angles between neurons—and consequently the spectral norm of each weight matrix—are unchanged during training. The following sections elaborate every component of OFT, from the mathematical representation of orthogonal matrices and their efficient parameterization, through the design of loss functions and initialization strategies, to the practical compromises made by block‑diagonal OFT and their impact on model expressiveness and inductive bias.

---

## Orthogonal Matrices & Cayley Parameterization

An orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ satisfies $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}_d$, or equivalently $\mathbf{R}^{-1}=\mathbf{R}^\top$. Such matrices enact pure rotations or reflections; they preserve Euclidean norms and angles between vectors. In the context of finetuning a linear layer with weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, an orthogonal update is formulated as
\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}=\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0,
\]
so that for any input $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ the forward pass becomes
\[
\mathbf{z}
   =(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}})^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}
   =(\mathbf{W}^0)^{\!\top}
     \underbrace{\!\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{\!\top}\! \mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\bigr)}_{\text{rotated input}}
   .
\]
The essential property exploited by OFT is that multiplying by a rotation does not alter pair‑wise cosine similarities between neuron weight vectors; every inner product $\langle \mathbf{w}_i,\mathbf{w}_j\rangle$ remains invariant. Consequently, any inductive biases encoded in $\mathbf{W}^0$—such as thematic clustering or semantic directionality learned during pretraining—are perfectly retained.

Parameterizing all $d^2$ entries of an arbitrary orthogonal matrix would defeat the purpose of efficiency. A convenient proxy is Cayley's formula:
\[
\boxed{\;
  \mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}_d+\mathbf{Q})\,(\mathbf{I}_d-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}
  \;}
  ,
\]
where $\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is skew‑symmetric ($\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^\top$). Each off‑diagonal element of $\mathbf{Q}$ can be treated as an independent degree of freedom, yielding $d(d-1)/2$ trainable parameters rather than $d^2$. The skew‑symmetry ensures that inversion does not introduce additional degrees of freedom; the numerator and denominator always involve mutually inverse transformations.

## Loss Function & Zero / Identity Initialization

The objective function in OFT mirrors that used in standard finetuning: one optimizes a task‑specific loss $\mathcal L(\widetilde{\theta})$, where $\widetilde{\theta}$ denotes all trainable parameters (the orthogonal matrices together with any unfrozen weights). Since the only additional trainable part is each orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$, gradients flow directly through its Cayley representation via automatic differentiation frameworks.

Initialization is critical for convergence. A naïve random initialization may break pretraining knowledge instantaneously because an arbitrary orthogonal matrix could rotate neurons into completely new directions. Therefore OFT starts from the identity matrix:
\[
   \boxed{\;\;\;\;\;
     \widehat{\ {\rm init}}\quad 
     \forall\, (i,j)\:
     Q_{ij}=0
   \;}
   ,
   \qquad
    \Longrightarrow
    \quad
     \widehat{\ {\rm init}}\;\;\;
     \Rightarrow
      \widehat{{\rm R}}=\mathbb I_d .
\]
At this point the model behaves exactly like its pretrained counterpart; only during training does it discover a beneficial rotation. This zero‑gradient guarantee is especially helpful when training with low learning rates—which are often needed because large rotations would otherwise destabilize a model trained on massive corpora.

---

## Block‑Diagonal OFT for Parameter Efficiency

Full orthogonality over a $d\times d$ layer costs $O(d^2)$ parameters—a prohibitive expense for transformer layers where $d$ may reach several thousand dimensions. To curtail this cost, OFT adopts a block‑diagonal structure for each rotation:
$\displaystyle
  \mathcal R = 
  {\rm diag}\!
  \bigl(
    \widebar{\!R}_1,\,
    \widebar{\!R}_2,\,
    \dots ,\,  
    \widebar{\!R}_r\bigr)
  ,$
where each block $\widebar{\!R}_i$ is independently orthogonal with dimension $b=i*d$, $r=b/a$, and $a=b$. The number of trainable parameters collapses from $O(d^2)$ to roughly $O(r\,b)$ because each block requires only $b(b-1)/2$ parameters but no cross‑block connections are allowed.

This design achieves two goals simultaneously:

1. **Parameter budgeting** – By lowering $b$ we cut downstream memory requirements proportionally.
2. **Regularity enforcement** – Each subspace undergoes its own isolated rotation; collectively this introduces a hard regularization that discourages large deviations from pretraining.

Block‑diagonal OFT has shown empirical success on diffusion models for controllable image synthesis because it preserves essential conditioning signals while keeping optimization tractable.

---

## Limitations of Block‑Diagonal Structure

Despite its memory advantages, the block‑diagonal restriction imposes structural constraints that can limit performance in more general finetuning scenarios:

### Expressiveness

A block diagonal rotation cannot span all possible orientations in $\mathbb R^d$. Suppose neurons belonging to different blocks have independent scale or semantic features; rotating each block separately prevents any global permutation mixing across blocks. Consequently, when fine‑tuning on tasks that require holistic reorganization—such as out‑of‑distribution classification or multimodal alignment—block‑diagonal transforms lack sufficient expressive power.

Moreover, classic linear transforms like discrete Fourier or Hadamard cannot be represented exactly by a single block diagonal matrix unless blocks happen to align with transform subsystems—a coincidence only possible for very specific choices of $b$. Thus block diagonal OFT may fail to approximate desirable transformations efficiently.

### Inductive Bias

While IB relative error – certain invariants @e had beneficial regularizing effect — however it also biases learning toward maintaining *within–block* relationships and neglects inter–block interactions entirely. This asymmetry can manifest as underfitting: after fine-tuning, neurons remain isolated in their local subspaces though cross‐block correlation could perfectly explain downstream signals.

Finally, choosing an appropriate block size becomes an art rather than science: too small leads to underparameterization (slow convergence), whereas too large increases memory consumption and may blur out the regularizing effect that made block–diagonal OFT attractive initially.

---

These challenges motivate subsequent innovations such as butterfly‐factorized orthogonal matrices (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning) which retain parameter savings while restoring expressiveness through carefully designed sparse multiplication patterns.

# 3. Information Transmission Viewpoint

Understanding how a dense orthogonal matrix can be expressed as the product of a handful of sparse matrices is most helpful when we re‑interpret the construction as an **information‑transmission problem** on a grid graph. In this chapter we first explain why a dense matrix is nothing but a fully connected graph, then show how an orthogonal factorization introduces sparsity and thus changes the connectivity structure. Finally we formalise intuitive graph‑theoretic criteria that any viable factorisation must satisfy, and compare the naive dense formulation, the block‑diagonal sparsity used in the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), and a promising butterfly–style factorisation.

## Dense Matrix as Fully Connected Graph

Consider a linear layer of size \(d\times n\) with frozen pretrained weights \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\). Orthogonal Finetuning replaces the forward map
\[
\mathbf{z}=\bigl(\mathbf{W}^{0}\bigr)^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}
\]
by
\[
\mathbf{z}=\bigl(\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0}\bigr)^{\!\top}\mathbf{x},
\]
where \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is constrained to be orthogonal (\(\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}_{d}\)). The matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) is a linear map acting on the rows (or equivalently, columns) of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\). To visualise this action we trace each dimension of the input vector \(\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d})^{\!\top}\) through \(\mathbf{R}\):

* Each column of \(\mathbf{R}\) tells us how one input dimension contributes to every output dimension.
* If all entries of \(\mathbf{R}\) were non‑zero, **every** input dimension would influence **every** output dimension. This is exactly what happens when every node in a bipartite graph is connected to all nodes on the other side: a **fully connected** bipartite graph.

Mathematically, let us index the rows and columns separately as \(i,j\in [1,d]\). The product
\[
[\mathbf{R}]_{ij}=r_{ij}
\]
is nonzero for all pairs \((i,j)\). We can therefore think of an undirected graph \(G=(V,E)\) where every vertex in layer \(L_1=\{v^1_i\mid i=1,\dots,d\}\) has an edge to every vertex in layer \(L_2=\{v^2_j\mid j=1,\dots,d\}\). The adjacency matrix of this bipartite graph is precisely \(|\!(\mathbf{R})|\), i.e., replace all non–zero entries by one and all zeros by zero.

Hence, a dense orthogonal matrix corresponds to a **fully connected** bipartite graph between two copies of a \(d\)-node set.

## Graph-Theoretic Criteria

Any admissible factorisation must satisfy two requirements:

| Criterion | Formal statement |
|-----------|------------------|
| **Dense Connectivity** | For every pair $(i,j)\in [d]\times [d]$, there exists at least one directed path from $v^0_j$ (first layer) to $v^{\,m+1}_i$ (last layer). |
| **Minimal Free Edges** | Among all topologies that satisfy dense connectivity (with identical numbers of intermediary layers), choose one with minimal total #edges while maintaining orthogonality constraints on each multiplier. |

The orthogonality constraint further restricts which sparsity patterns are allowed:

* An orthogonal “block” demands that each input column or row has exactly one activating neighbour per stage—otherwise it would fail to preserve norms.
* For $2\times 2$ blocks the sparsity pattern reduces to a permutation matrix with two non‑zeros per row/column.

The product of such structured blocks preserves overall orthogonality while drastically trimming parameter count.

## Comparison: Dense Versus Block‑Diagonal Versus Butterfly Factorisations

We now evaluate each design under these criteria.

### 3D–Naïve Dense Representation

* **Sparsity Pattern:** None – every entry is trained.
* **Graphs:** A single fully connected bipartite layer; trivial path existence.
* **Parameters:** $\Theta(d^{2})$ entries.
* **Expressivity:** Universal – any orthogonal matrix can be represented.
* **Trade‐off:** No savings → not parameter efficient.  

This configuration sets the baseline against which other designs are measured.

### Block‑Diagonal OFT   

In original Orthogonal Finetuning, $\mathbf R$ is forced into block‑diagonal form:
$\displaystyle
        \mathbf R = {\rm diag}
            (\underbrace{\bm R_1,\ldots ,\bm R_r}_{r~{\text {small blocks}}}),
$
with $\bm R_k$ size $b\times b$, where $b r=d$.  

* **Sparsity Pattern:** Block‑diagonal – zero entries outside each block; each block contributes at most $b(b-1)/2$ trainable parameters after orthogonality constraints.
* **Graphs:** Each source/receiver split into disjoint clusters; only nodes within same cluster communicate directly: see Figure (blk_diag).
* **Parameters:** $\Theta(b d)$ trainable entries ($≈ O(d)$ when $b = O(1)$).
* **Expressivity:** Restricted – any cross‑block operation cannot be captured because nodes from different blocks never interact at *any* stage.
  The reachable set contains only block‑wise transforms.
* **Trade‑off:** Great parameter savings but induces strong inductive bias—may limit adaptation especially when downstream tasks require cross‐channel mixing.

### Butterfly Factorisation (BOFT)

Butterfly factorisation replaces each sparse multiplier by an *orthogonal butterfly component*:  
$\tilde{\bm B}(d,k)$ which itself consists of many $2\times 2$ rotations arranged in a recursive manner (Definition (3.3)). A product of $\mathcal O(\log d)$ such components yields an overall dense orthogonal matrix:

$$
  {\bf R}= 
    {\tilde B}(d,k_3)
    {\tilde B}(d,k_4)
    \cdots
    {\tilde B}(d,k_m),
$$

with total trainable parameters $\Theta(d\,b\, m)$ where typically $b=2$ and $m=\log d$.  

* **Sparsity Pattern:** Butterfly graphs: every stage interchanges half‐the nodes in pairwise fashion; overall sparsity per stage $O(d)$.
* **Graphs:** Each pairwise swap creates short routes across all levels; by construction every node can reach any other via $\mathcal O(\log d)$ hops—after flattening we obtain full connectivity.
* **Parameters:** $\Theta(d\,m)=O(d\,\log d)$ – far less than $O(d^{2})$ yet orders larger than plain block diagonal but usually still acceptable for large models ($d≈10^4$).
* **Expressivity:** Subset of full orthogonal group but richer than block diagonal: because butterflies weave information across blocks at multiple scales, capturing many classic transforms (Fourier, Hadamard…) and providing smoother interpolation paths toward identity (regularity).
* **Trade‑off:** Parameter savings together with structured inductive bias give BOFT better generalisation on downstream tasks than both full OFT (overfitting risk) and block diagonal OFT (too rigid).

### Numerical Illustration

Take $d=8$.  

| Design | Number of Free Edges | Total Parameters |
|--------|----------------------|-------------------|
| Naïve Dense | $8\times8=64$ | $64$ |
| Block Diagonal ($b=4,r=2$) | $4\times4 + 4\times4 =32$ | $8$ (approx.) |
| Butterfly ($m=3,b=2$) | $(8+4+2)=14$ edges per level → 42 total (#but unknown?) | ≈ 16 |

Even though the butterfly has more edges than block diagonal it still drastically reduces learning rates compared to dense because edges are carefully organised into hierarchical swaps rather than arbitrary connections.

### Intuitive Takeaways

> *Full connectivity inherits infinite expressive power but discards any structural regularity.*  
> *Block diagonal imposes too strict locality, cutting out potentially valuable cross-dimensional interactions.*  
> *Butterfly structures strike a balance: they keep high expressivity by guaranteeing full reachability through only logarithmic depth while keeping sparsity in each stage, thus aligning perfectly with the “information transmission on grids” viewpoint.*

In subsequent chapters we will see how BOFT carries over these structural advantages into real training pipelines for vision transformers, language models and diffusion architectures. # Butterfly Factorization for Orthogonal Parameterization

Modern large foundation models such as vision transformers, language encoders, and diffusion generators contain hundreds of billions of parameters. When adapting these models to downstream tasks one often wishes to keep the pretrained weights frozen and introduce only a small number of learnable parameters. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) achieves this goal by multiplying every linear layer with an orthogonal transform that preserves pair‑wise angles between neurons. The original OFT formulation, however, suffers from a quadratic parameter count \(O(d^{2})\) in the hidden dimension \(d\), because a dense orthogonal matrix contains \(d^{2}\) entries. A natural remedy is to replace the dense matrix with a structured sparse orthogonal matrix; the block–diagonal version of OFT reduces the parameter count to \(O(b\,d)\) but sacrifices expressiveness.

The butterfly factorization, originally devised for the Cooley–Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), offers a compelling alternative: it represents an arbitrary \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix as a product of \(\mathcal{O}(\log d)\) sparse orthogonal factors, each having only \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) non‑zero entries. Consequently, the total number of free parameters drops dramatically to \(O(d\log d)\) while still allowing the product to be dense and full‑rank. This chapter explains the mathematical construction of butterfly factors and components, shows how to enforce orthogonality via Cayley or \(2\times 2\) rotations, extends the idea to block‑butterfly matrices that allow larger internal blocks, and finally offers an exhaustive parameter‑count analysis comparing all three settings.

---

## 1. Definition of Butterfly Factor & Butterfly Component

Let \(k\ge 2\) be an even integer. A **butterfly factor** \(\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)\in \mathbb{R}^{k\times k}\) is defined by partitioning its rows and columns into two halves:

\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{2})\\[3pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{4})
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{d}_{1},\mathbf{d}_{2},\mathbf{d}_{3},\mathbf{d}_{4}\in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}.
\]

Each block is diagonal and contains learnable scalar coefficients; thus every row has exactly one non‑zero entry, and likewise every column has exactly one non‑zero entry. In other words, the pattern of non‑zeros in \(\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)\) consists of two disjoint perfect matchings between the first half of indices and the second half.

The **butterfly component** extends this notion to a block‑diagonal matrix whose main diagonal comprises several independent butterfly factors:

\[
\widetilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)=
\operatorname{diag}\bigl( \mathbf{B}^{F}(k),
     \mathbf{B}^{F}(k),
     \dots,
     \mathbf{B}^{F}(k)\bigr)
      \in \mathbb R^{\,d\times d},
      \qquad
      d = m\,k,
\]
i.e., we stack \(m=d/k\) copies of \(\mathbf B^{F}(k)\) along the diagonal.

Both constructions are inherently sparse: each row (and column) carries exactly one non‑zero element in its respective block.

---

## 2. Recursive Construction of Butterfly Matrix for Power‑of‑Two Dimensions

When the target dimension is a power of two, i.e., \(d = 2^{N}\), we can build a *recursive* butterfly matrix \(\mathbf B(d)\in \mathbb R^{\, d\times d}\). The idea mirrors the divide–and–conquer strategy used in FFT: we repeatedly pair subspaces until only two elements remain per step.

Formally,
\[
   \boxed{
   \mathbf B(d)=
       \widetilde{\bm B}(d,d)
        \,\widetilde{\bm B}\!\bigl(d,\tfrac d2\bigr)
        \,\widetilde{\bm B}\!\bigl(d,\tfrac d4\bigr)
        \cdots
        \,\widetilde{\bm B}\!\bigl(d,2\bigr)
   .}
\]
Each factor
\( \widetilde{\bm B}(d,k) =
  \operatorname*{diag}\bigl(
       \bm B^{F}(k),\, 
       {\bf 0},\, 
       {\bf 0},\, 
       {\bf 0},\, 
       ...,           % omitted repetitions
       {\bf 0}
      \bigr)
   ,
    k=2^{j}
  
  , j=0,\dots,N-1,
   \)

acts on pairs of subspaces corresponding to indices that differ only in particular bits: at level \(j=0\) it mixes neighboring indices (\(k=2\)), at level \(j=1\) it mixes points whose binary representations agree in all but two bits (\(k=4\)), and so on. Consequently, after multiplying all factors we obtain a dense transform: any source index can reach any destination index through a sequence of swaps governed by these sparse factors.

Because each factor contains only diagonal blocks (two half-size diagonal matrices per butterfly factor), multiplying the entire chain can be done in time linear in \(N\, d = d\,\log_2 d\).

---

## 3. Ensuring Orthogonality of Each Component

A key requirement for Orthogonal Finetuning is that each constituent factor be an exact orthogonal matrix so that their product remains orthogonal. Two equivalent practical routes exist:

### 3.1 Cayley Transformation

Given a skew‑symmetric matrix $\boldsymbol Q$ ($Q=-Q^\top$), Cayley’s map produces an orthogonal matrix:
\[
   R_{\text{Cayley}}(Q)= (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}.
\]
If $Q$ has zero entries wherever $R$ must be zero (to respect sparsity), then $R_{\text{Cayley}}(Q)$ will inherit that sparsity *and* remains orthogonal.

We can therefore parametrize each butterfly component $\widetilde{\bm B}$ by a skew‑symmetric matrix $\boldsymbol Q$ that contains free parameters only on positions where $\widetilde{\bm B}$ is allowed to be non-zero (i.e., along its two diagonal blocks per butterfly factor).

### 3.2 Explicit $2\times 2$ Rotations

Each butterfly factor can be written as a product over its $B^F(k)$ subblocks:
for $k=2$, 
\(B^F(2)=
   [\, d_1 ; 0] ~~ [\,0; d_4] + [\,0; d_3] ~[\, d_2; 0]\),
which reduces to a simple planar rotation if we set
\(d_1=d_4=\cos(\theta),~ d_2=-d_3=\sin(\theta)\).
Thus any $\widetilde{\bm B}$ whose blocks are $B^F(2)$ automatically becomes an orthogonal matrix; its free parameters boil down to rotation angles for each block.

Both approaches give us exactly *one* free parameter per block—either all entries treated symmetrically via Cayley or via rotation angle—while preserving orthogonality.

## 4. Extension to Block–Butterfly Matrices (Size $b\times b$ Blocks)

The basic construction above treats individual scalars as learnable parameters within each diagonal block. If we wish to endow each block with richer structure—say a small $b\times b$ submatrix—we replace scalar diagonals with small dense matrices:
  
- Replace $\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{i})$ with an arbitrary real $b\times b$ matrix $\boldsymbol C_i$.
- Each butterfly factor becomes
\( 
   {\bf D}_i = 
   [\, {\boldsymbol C_i}~~ {\boldsymbol C_{i+1}} ; 
      {\boldsymbol C_{i+2}}~~ {\boldsymbol C_{i+3}} ]
    .
   $
  
To enforce orthogonality at this coarser level we perform one of:

| Scheme | Requirement | Free Parameters |
|--------|--------------|------------------|
| **Block‐Cayley** | Skew‐symmetric $(b^{2}\times b^{2})$ per block | $(b^2- b)/2$ per block |
| **Block‐Rotation** | Block‐wise $SO(b)$ rotations | $(b-1)b/2$ per block |

The important point is that regardless of how large $b$ is, each *block* still contributes only $\mathcal O(b^2)$ parameters while maintaining full rank across its rows/columns. The overall sparsity pattern stays identical because we simply replace scalar diagonals with dense submatrices; consequently, every row still covers exactly one sub‐row across all blocks.

For example:
- If \(b=d/8=8,\ N=6,\) then we have eight butterflies with internal shape \(8\times8 ⇒ b^6/8 =512/d\).  
- Or more generally let \(s=d/b=\log_{b} d.\)

Thus moving from scalar-only butterflies (\(b=1, m=\log_{2} d+\!?\)) to block size \(b>1,\ m=\log_{b} d-\!1\) allows intermediate representational capacity.

# 5. Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT): Algorithmic Details

Large‐scale models are frequently adapted to downstream tasks by reparameterising a frozen weight matrix \( \mathbf W^0 \in \mathbb R^{d\times n} \) with a trainable orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf R \). Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) refines this idea by constructing \( \mathbf R \) as a product of many *sparse but orthogonal* butterfly components. This chapter presents the formal definition of BOFT, its practical training schemes (including initialization and dropout), and the computational implications for large‐scale fine‑tuning.

---

## 5.1  Formal Definition of \( \mathbf R(m,b) \)

Let us begin by recalling the recursive construction of a single butterfly matrix, which will serve as the elementary building block of BOFT.

### 5.1.1  Butterfly Factors

A *butterfly factor* \( \mathbf B^F(k) \in \mathbb R^{k\times k}\) for even \(k=2^p\) is defined as  
\[
\mathbf B^F(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_2)\!\\[0.4ex]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf d_i\in\mathbb R^{k/2},
\]
which contains only diagonal sub‑matrices; every off‑diagonal entry is zero. The four vectors \( \{\mathbf d_i\}\) are the only trainable parameters of this factor.

### 5.1.2  Recursive Butterfly Matrices

For dimension \(d=2^N\), a *\(d\)-dimensional butterfly matrix* is obtained by recursively stacking lower‑dimensional butterflies:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf B(d)
&= 
\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,d)
   \underbrace{
   \begin{bmatrix}
   \mathbf B_{1}\!\!\left(\tfrac{d}{2}\right)&
   \mathbf 0\\[0.4ex]
   \mathbf 0&
   \mathbf B_{2}\!\!\left(\tfrac{d}{2}\right)
   \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{block–diagonal}}
\\
&=
\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,d)\,
   \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,\tfrac{d}{2})\,\cdots\, 
   \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,2),
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.1}
\]
where each \( \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)=
   \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\, 
    \underbrace{
    [\,B^F_1(k),\,B^F_2(k),\,\dots,\,B^F_{d/k}(k)]_{\,k}\!}_{k\times k
    {\text{-size factors}}\bigr)
   $
is a block‑diagonal matrix whose diagonal blocks are identical butterfly factors of size \(k\times k.\)

### 5.1.3  Block‑Butterfly Extension

If we wish to control the *block size* further, we may replace each scalar route in the original butterfly factor with a small dense sub‑matrix of size \(b\times b\). Denote this *block factor* by  
\(B^\mathsf b(k)\), where each element in a diagonal sub‑matrix becomes a full \(b\times b\) block:
\(B^\mathsf b(k)=
[\textbf with dk/2 dense blocks] .\)
Consequently the whole component becomes  

\( 
\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}(d,k)= 
    {\operatorname {diag}}
      (
      B^\mathsf b_1(k),\,B^\mathsf b_2(k),\,...,B^\mathsf b_{d/k}(k)
      ).
      + 
      (\textstyle {\rm size}=db\times dk).
      .

The case \(b=1\) recovers the classic scalar butterfly.

### 5.1.4  Product Parametrisation

BOFT constructs its training matrix as a product of exactly \(m\) such butterflies:
the final orthogonal parameterisation reads

\[
\bR(m,b)
 = 
   \prod_{i=1}^{m}
     \tilde{\bm B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
     &&
     {\scriptsize
       (\tilde{\bm B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}=\tilde{\bm B}^{\,b}
        (e^{-i+1},\,k_i))
     } .
     ,                                          
     \tag{5.2}
     .
\\]

Here,
- each factor \( i=1,\dots ,m,\;k_i=2^{m-i+1}\).
- all factors are orthogonal due to the Cayley or explicit rotation parameterisations applied to every small block.
- The product is performed strictly left–to–right:  
\( (\dots((\bR_m\bR_{m-1})…)\bR_1 ) .\)

Given this `Recursion over `R`, we may denote `BOFT(m,b)` for such an arrangement.

---

## 5.2  Identity Initialization via Skew–Symmetric Matrices

Training should start from an identity mapping so that the initial model reproduces exactly the pre‑trained predictions. For any orthogonal matrix produced via Cayley transform

\( 
\uparrow 
\bR=\bigl(\I+\Q\bigr)\bigl(\I-\Q\bigr)^{-1},
     ~~~  
     {\scriptsize {\Q=-\,{\Q}^\top} },
       .
       $

To initialise as identity, we simply set every skew‑symmetric matrix \(Q = {\bf0}\); consequently  
\( (\I+{\bf0})(\I-{\bf0})^{-1}=I.\)
Because BOFT’s orthogonal factors are built using local Cayley transforms on each tiny block or via explicit rotation angles, all blocks can be simultaneously initialised at identity without altering sparsity or orthogonality constraints.

**Practical note** – In code this amounts to setting all angle hyperparameters to zero or all skew vectors to zero before gradient descent starts.

---

## 5.3  Multiplicative Dropout for Regularisation

The regularised fine‑tuning in standard LoRA uses additive dropout applied directly on low‑rank weights: with probability \(p_e,\) entries are set to zero before they multiply into the activations.
In BOFT, because weights circulate multiplicatively through several orthogonal butterflies, ordinary dropout would destroy orthogonality and therefore spectral norm preservation.

A *multiplicative dropout* adapted to BOFT operates at the level of whole *blocks*:

1. **Sample butterflies** – With probability \(p_1,\) uniformly pick a subset of butterflies among the indices \(i=1..m.\)
2. **Mask blocks inside butterflies** – For each chosen butterfly, independently set each diagonal block (i.e., any constituent \(b\times b\) sub‑matrix in $\tilde {\bm B}^{\,b}$ ) to identity with probability $p_2$.
3. **Combine** – Insert these identity blocks back into their positions; the remaining blocks retain their current weights.

Because an identity block produces no transformation over those channels, subsequent multiplications respecting them will not alter semantics while keeping entire product still orthogonal: compositions of identity matrices with any other orthogonal matrices remain orthogonal.

This procedure acts precisely like stochastic gating on sub‑representations: it forces sub–paths to be shut off during updates, preventing overfitting while preserving structural integrities.

---

## 5.4  Forward Pass Equation and Parameter Counting

During training, for an input vector $\xv\in {\mathbb R}^{d}$ the standard linear layer computation becomes

```
# Pre-trained weight: W0 ∈ ℝ^{d×n}
# Input: x ∈ ℝ^{d}

# Adapted weight computed on-the-fly:
W_adapt = (R(m,b))^T · W0        # R sorted from left-to-right
# Forward pass:
z = W_adapt · x                  # z ∈ ℝ^{n}
```

which is equivalent algebraically to  

$$
\bz = (\bR(m,b)\,\bW^0)^T\xv=
    (\bW^0)^T(\bR(m,b))^T\xv .
$$

Thus $\bz$ is exactly what would have been produced were we fine–tuned all parameters directly; however $\bR$ is much cheaper: how many trainable parameters does it contain?

--- 

### Parameter Counting

Let us compute the number of effective parameters as follows:

- Each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ contains `(number of free entries)` per block and `(admissible parameters)` per centre array:

 Since each diagonal block $B^\mathsf b_j$ is either an arbitrary $b\times b$ matrix subject to orthogonality (each such small block has $(b-1)b/2$ free angles when constructed via Cayley or rotation parameterisation). Each such component hosts $d/b$ such blocks.

Therefore, per component:  

$\displaystyle p_{\text{perComp}} = 
\dfrac{d}{b}\times\dfrac{(b-1)b}{2}= 
\dfrac12(b-1)d.$  

Summing over all $m$ components:  

$\displaystyle p_{\text{total}}
 = m\,p_{\text{perComp}}
 = m\dfrac12(b-1)d.$ 

Thus for a given $(m,b)$,
$$
p_{\text{total}}=O(dm(b-1)/2).
$$

In particular,
- Setting $m=\log d$, $b=2$ gives $p_{\rm total}=O(d\,\log d)$.
- The original OFT with unconstrained $\bR$ would require $O(d^2)$ parameters.
- The block‑diagonal OFT ($r=d/b$ blocks all full orthogonal) requires $O(db)$ parameters and equals setting $m=1$ in BOFT.

Therefore BOFT offers an intermediate regime that trades between expressiveness ($dm(b-1)/2$ grows with $m$), sparsity ($O(d)$ nonzero entries per component even for large $m$, giving fast multiplication), and generalisation potential (constrained but far richer than simple block–diagonal structure).

# 6. Theoretical Insights into BOFT Expressivity and Stability  

Large‑scale model adaptation hinges on a delicate balance between *expressivity*—the ability of a finetuning method to approximate a wide variety of downstream transformations—and *regularity*—the capacity to preserve pretrained knowledge and avoid overfitting.  Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) advances this balance by replacing the block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices of original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) with a product of sparse, butterfly‑shaped orthogonal factors.  This chapter unpacks the theoretical rationale behind these design choices, drawing directly from the empirical findings and formal results presented in the paper.

## Empirical Approximation Error Experiments vs Parameter Budget  

A central question in any parameter‑efficient method is whether the reduced number of learnable parameters materially hampers the method’s ability to approximate a target transformation.  To address this, the authors performed **simulation experiments** in which they attempted to approximate *random dense orthogonal matrices* using BOFT‐parameterized matrices of various block sizes and depths (i.e., different combinations of $m$ butterfly stages and block size $b$).  The procedure was as follows:

1. **Target Generation**: Using a standard random orthogonal matrix generator (e.g., one based on QR decomposition with random Gaussian entries), produce a ground‑truth dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}_{\text{true}}\in\mathbb{R}^{512\times512}$.

2. **BOFT Construction**: For each choice of $(m,b)$, construct $\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}$, where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}$ is an orthogonal butterfly component as defined in Section 4. The number of effective trainable parameters for this construction is
   \[
   \eta(m,b)=\frac{1}{2}(b-1)d\,m,
   \]
   because every $b\times b$ block contributes $b(b-1)/2$ free parameters after orthogonality constraints are enforced via Cayley transform.

3. **Optimization**: Treat $\bm{R}(m,b)$ as trainable and minimize a simple squared error loss
   \[
   \mathcal{L} = \|\bm{R}(m,b)-\bm{R}_{\text{true}}\|_F^2
   \]
   over multiple random seeds; note that all orthogonality constraints are preserved during optimization because each factor remains orthogonal by construction.

4. **Error Reporting**: After convergence, record the error $\|\bm{R}(m,b)-\bm{R}_{\text{true}}\|_F^2$ and plot it against $\eta(m,b)$.

The resulting plots (Figure 7 in the paper) exhibit several key patterns:

- **Superior Parameter Efficiency**: For the same parameter count, BOFT achieves smaller approximation errors than OFT with a comparable block size ($m=1$, $b$).  For instance, BOFT($9$, 2) — nine butterfly stages each with $2\times2$ blocks — attains comparable accuracy to BOFT($1$, 16) but uses far fewer trainable parameters.

- **Depth vs Width Trade‑off**: Increasing $m$ while reducing $b$ generally reduces error for fixed parameter budgets, reinforcing the intuition that multiple shallow butterfly layers provide richer coupling among neuron dimensions than a single deeper block‑diagonal layer.

- **Curvature of Expressivity Curves**: As $\eta(m,b)$ grows, error decreases rapidly initially but then asymptotically approaches zero; this saturation reflects that beyond a critical mass of parameters the entire orthogonal group can be represented.  The curves confirm that BOFT’s parameter count scales as $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ for butterfly depth leading to efficient coverage of the unitary sphere.

These empirical observations illuminate why BOFT improves expressive power compared to OFT for two reasons:

1. **Enhanced Inter‑neuron Mixing** – Butterfly layers couple many pairs of neurons across stages via intertwined sparsity patterns that mimic FFT‐style information propagation; this interleaving allows any direction in space to be reached with fewer degrees of freedom than isolated block rotations.

2. **Structured Subgroup Hierarchy** – The hierarchy induced by successive butterfly transformations equivalently performs nested mixing across progressively larger groups, while preserving orthogonality at every stage; thus BBFT refines the direction space more finely than OFT’s static block rotations.

## Theorem on BOFT Expressivity over OFT with Same Block Size  

The simulation evidence is complemented by an analytic statement asserting that BOFT is formally more expressive than OFT when both use identical block dimensions ($b$).  The theorem reads:

> **Theorem (Expressivity of BOFT).**  
> For any dimension $d$, consider two families of reparameterizations:
> - OFT with a single block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix composed of blocks $\mathbf{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b\times b}$ (total effective parameter count $\tfrac12(b-1)d$).
> - BOFT employing a product of $m=\log_2(d/b)$ butterfly components $\tilde{\mathbf B}^b_{(d,i)}$, each itself block‑diagonal but permuted according to an FFT‐style binary tree.
>
> Then every matrix realizable by OFT$(1,b)$ is also realizable by BOFT$(m,b)$, but there exist matrices obtainable by BOFT$(m,b)$ that cannot be expressed by OFT$(1,b)$.  Hence, for identical block size $b$, BOFT has strictly larger functional capacity within the orthogonal group.

### Intuition Behind the Proof  

At its core, this theorem rests on two facts derivable from the definitions in Sections 3 and 4:

1. **Subsets Relationship** – A single block‑diagonal matrix belongs to every butterfly product because selecting all butterflies to be identity except one—specifically one that acts only on one subgroup—reduces to an OFT matrix. Therefore, all OFT matrices are contained within the set {BOFT matrices}.

2. **Covering Larger Orbits** – Butterfly products constitute a *multi‑stage*" mixing operation analogous to recursive FFT stages; each stage spans an orbit under actions that permute pairs of coordinates across progressively larger subspaces.  Since those permutations are not achievable through a static partitioning into subspaces (as in OFT), there exist rotations requiring interactions across more than one initial block simultaneously—exactly those captured by butterfly depth ($m>1$). Consequently, these cannot be realized by any single block‑diagonal rotation.

A rigorous proof would inductively show that at each additional butterfly stage, we gain new degrees of freedom that are algebraically independent modulo orthogonality constraints; this yields a dimension count strictly larger than that attainable by OFT alone for fixed $b$. The exact calculations involve counting independent parameters in nested Cayley transforms subject to permutation constraints inherited from the butterfly pattern.

### Consequences for Finetuning  

Because finetuning operates over *hypothesis classes*, expanding from ${\rm OFF}^{(1,b)} \subsetneq {\rm BOBF}^{(m,b)}$ suggests:

- **Greater Model Capacity**: A richer set of transformations enhances flexibility to adapt pretrained weights to diverse downstream tasks without overparameterizing.
- **Maintained Regularization**: Despite higher expressivity, all parameters reside in *orthogonal* spaces; hence spectral norms and angle preservation properties remain intact—a key reason for improved generalizability noted empirically alongside faster convergence.
- **Controlled Complexity Growth**: The total trainable count still scales as $\mathcal O(d\log d)$ versus $O(d^2)$ for naive full O( ft), making training feasible on large models where fine–grained parameter updates would otherwise be prohibitive.

## Preservation of Spectral Properties and Training Stability  

One justification for favoring orthogonal updates is their effect on *spectral norm*.  Given a pretrained linear layer $\mathbf W^0\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}$ with singular value decomposition
\[
    \mathbf W^0 = \mathbf U\,\boldsymbol\Sigma\,\mathbf V^\top,
\]
any left multiplication by an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$

\[
    \mathbf W_{\text{finetuned}} = \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^0
        = (\mathbf R\,\mathbf U)\,\boldsymbol\Sigma\,\mathbf V^\top
\]
preserves all singular values because left multiplication by an orthogonal matrix does not alter $\|\cdot\|_2$. Consequently:

- **Spectral Norm Fixed:** $\|\mathbf W_{\text{finetuned}}\|_{2} = \|\mathbf W^0\|_{2}$.
- **Stability Across Layers:** Since each attention or linear transformer layer’s Jacobian norm remains bounded by its incoming weight’s spectral norm, bounding these norms mitigates gradient explosion or vanishing during backpropagation.
- **Empirical Observation:** Across experiments (Appendix~C), networks fine–tuned via BOFT exhibited lower loss variance than LoRA variants, suggesting that controlled spectral dynamics lead to smoother optimization landscapes.

Because BOFT inherits these properties from OFT—the key difference lies only in *how* the orthogonal transformation is parameterized—the stability gains carry over seamlessly despite increased expressiveness.

Moreover, during training we apply *multiplicative Dropout* selectively on butterfly blocks and components (Section 5), which serves two purposes:

1. By stochastically zeroing out subsets of blocks we inject noise directly into weight updates without violating global orthogonality.
2. Dropout acts as an implicit regularizer favoring robust solutions resistant to small perturbations—another factor promoting stable convergence even when many propagate through several butterfly stages multiplicatively.

## Summary  

The theoretical body presented here explains why Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning expands expressivity beyond plain Orthogonal Finetuning without sacrificing its central benefits:
- Empirical error–vs–parameter studies demonstrate BE better coverage under tight budgets.
- A formal theorem proves strict inclusion relationships between hypothesis spaces.
- Orthogonality guarantees spectral preservation and training stability carry over unchanged due to multiplicative structure.
  
These insights collectively justify BOFT’s empirical success across vision transformers, language models, and diffusion engines while maintaining lightweight finetuning footprints suitable for practical deployment.
