\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 8: # 8. Empirical Methodology Overview  

In this chapter we provide a detailed, reproducible account of the experimental protocol used to evaluate Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) against a suite of competing methods. The goal is to give readers the complete information necessary to replicate the results: the precise tasks and datasets, how synthetic or real human preference pairs were obtained, the computational procedures used for training and inference, the metrics chosen for assessment, and the hyperparameter spaces explored. Every design choice is motivated by a trade‑off between realism (matching how an end‑user would deploy a model), efficiency (cost‑effective experimentation), and control (minimising confounding factors).

---

## 8.1 Controlled Sentiment Generation with Synthetic Preferences  

### 8.1.1 Task Specification  

We segment a movie‑review paragraph from the IMDb corpus into two parts: a **prompt** \(x\) consisting of the first \(p\) tokens and a **completion** \(y = y_1 y_2 \dots y_T\). The model must continue \(x\) so that \(y\) conveys *positive* sentiment. The ground truth reward \(r_{\text{sent}}(x,y)\) is provided by an off‑the‑shelf sentiment classifier \(C\), yielding  
\[ r_{\text{sent}}(x,y)=\mathrm{logit}\bigl(C_{\text{pos}}(y)\bigr)\,, \]  
where \(\mathrm{logit}(p)=\log \frac{p}{1-p}\).  

### 8.1.2 Preference Pair Generation  

Because collecting human preferences on every candidate completion is infeasible, we synthesize them mechanically:  
1. Sample two completions \((y^{(1)}, y^{(2)}) \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}\) from a pre‑trained GPT‑2‑large SFT model on IMDB reviews (\(\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi^{\text{SFT}}\)).  
2. Compute their sentiment scores via \(C\).  
3. Assign preference label  
   \[
   y_w^{(i)} = 
   \begin{cases}
   y^{(1)} & \text{if } r_{\text{sent}}(x,y^{(1)}) > r_{\text{sent}}(x,y^{(2)})\\
   y^{(2)} & \text{otherwise},
   \end{cases}
   \qquad
   y_l^{(i)}=\begin{cases}
   y^{(2)} & \text{if } r_{\text{sent}}(x,y^{(1)}) > r_{\text{sent}}(x,y^{(2)})\\
   y^{(1)} & \text{otherwise}\,. 
   \end{cases}
   \]  
4. Store the triplet \((x, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\).  

The preference distribution thus mirrors that of a human annotator who would only care about relative sentiment strength.

### 8.1.3 Dataset Construction  

From the IMDB training split we randomly select \(N = 20\,000\) prompts, yielding exactly one synthetic preference pair per prompt. This number balances sufficient statistical power against computational cost for repeated runs.

### 8.1.4 Reference Policy Choice  

We use the SFT policy itself as reference (\(\pi_{\text{ref}}=\pi^{\text{SFT}}\)). Because synthetic preferences are generated from this policy, any deviation measured during training is directly attributable to optimization steps rather than distribution shift.

## 8.2 Reddit TL;DR Summarization Dataset and Human Preferences  

### 8.2.1 Corpus and Annotation  

The TL;DR dataset consists of Reddit forum posts paired with their concise “TL;DR” summaries (Kim et al., 2017). We split into train/validation/test as in prior works:
- Train: 151 000 post–summary pairs,
- Validation: 15 000,
- Test: 15 000.

Human preferences were collected by Stiennon et al. (2022): for every post in validation, three completions were generated by sampling from an SFT GPT‑3‑based policy; crowdworkers selected the best among them on a set of fine‐grained quality dimensions (coherence, informativeness, style). This yields a dataset of ordered pairs:

\[
\mathcal D_{\text{summ}}=\bigl\{\,(x^{(i)},\,y_w^{(i)},\,y_l^{(i)})\,:\, i=1,\dots, N\bigr\}\!,
\]
with \(N=15\,000\times3=45\,000\).

### 8.2.2 Model Initialization  

For all summation baselines we start with the publicly released GPT‑J 13B model fine‑tuned on human Reddit summaries (carperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft). This serves as both initial SFT policy and as \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).

### 8.2.3 Evaluation Setups  

At test time we sample **\(k=10^4\)** candidate summaries per test post for each algorithm; hyperparameters such as temperature (\(\tau\in[0,1]\)) are swept afterwards per method because optimal sampling temperature varies with training objective.

## 8.3 Anthropic HH Dialogue Dataset Conversion to Preference Pairs  

### 8.3.1 Dataset Structure  

The Anthropic Helpful & Harmless (HH) dataset comprises turn‑by‑turn conversations between humans and an unseen assistant; each dialog ends with **two alternative assistant replies** followed by a binary human choice indicating which reply was preferred.

Let the prompt be \(x=\underbrace{\langle h\rangle}_{\text{text from user}}\). For each dialog we get two completions:
- \(y_{v}\): Variation A,
- \(y_{h}\): Variation B,

with human label \(s_{h}=+1\) if variation A is preferred and \(s_{h}=-1\) otherwise.

### 8.3.2 Preference Pair Extraction  

We convert every example into a single triplet:
- If \(s_{h}=+1\): set \(y_w := y_v,\; y_l := y_h.\)
- Else: set \(y_w := y_h,\; y_l := y_v.\)

Thus all data become *ordered* pairs suitable for DPO training.

Number of examples used: **170 k** after splitting into train/validation/test in a standard fashion (80/10/10 ratio).

### 8.3.3 Model Starting Point  

No off‑the‑shelf SFT model exists for this task in public releases; instead we fine‑tune an unmodified Pythia‑2·8B with *preferred‑only* supervised learning: minimize negative log likelihood on pairs where labelled replies are taken as full supervision targets:
\[
L_{\mathrm{sup}} = -\, \log\, \pi_\theta(y_w | x).
\]
The resulting model serves both as initial policy \((\pi_\theta)\) and as reference – but since preferences were not generated via this policy we build an explicit reference by fitting a small language model solely on preferred completions:
```
π_ref ← train_GPT_Single_Pref()
```

## 8.4 Evaluation Procedures  

The primary objective in RLHF-style training is to maximize expected reward while keeping divergence from the reference policy bounded:
\[
J(\pi_\theta)=
\E_{x\,{\sim}\!,\,y\,{\sim}\!\pi_\theta}
[r^*(x,y)]-\beta D_{\!\!KL}[\pi_\theta(y|x)\|\,{\pi_\mathrm{{ref}}}(y|x)].
\]
Because ground truth rewards exist only in controlled sentiment generation, we adopt two complementary metrics.

### 8.4.1 Reward / KL Frontier (Controlled Sentiment)

For each run we compute:
- Expected sentiment reward 
\(R = {\mathbb E}_{x,y}[r_{\rm sent}(x,y)]\),
- Sequence-level KL between trained policy and reference:
\(D = {\mathbb E}_{{P(x)}}[ D_{\!\!KL}[\pi_\theta(y|x)\|{\pi_\mathrm{{ref}}}(y|x)] ]. \)

Plotting these yields a frontier; ideally algorithms attain high reward at low KL.

### 8.4.2 Win Rates via GPT‑4

Human evaluation at scale is costly; early work has demonstrated *GPT‑4* can act as a proxy evaluator with strong correlation to human judgments when prompted appropriately (see Appendix A). For summarization and dialogue we therefore conduct:

**Two comparison sets:**  
- *Best baseline*: helper's own best sample (within same method),
- *Best-of-$N$ baseline*: top score among $N$ samples under learned reward function for that method.

For each prompt pair we collect GPT‑4 judgments:

```
Prompt GPT-4: "Which summary is better at capturing key points? Choose A or B."
Input     : [prompt + summary_A ; prompt + summary_B]
Output    : "Candidate A wins" or "Candidate B wins"
```

Aggregation yields *win rate*:
\(w = P(\texttt{chosen} = w)\).

A win rate above .50 indicates superiority over baseline.

We run these evaluations over temperatures ranging from $\tau=0$ to $\tau=1$ at increments of .05 for each algorithm.

### 8.4.3 Human Studies

To validate GPT‑4’s proxy power we performed crowdsourced pairwise comparisons on a subsample of outcomes (6K comparisons across three methods—DPO+, PPO+, SFT) using Amazon Mechanical Turk workers annotated under standard MTurk guidelines:

- Each worker saw one prompt plus two candidate completions.
- Workers answered question “Which completion better satisfies objective?”.
- Agreement was measured via Fleiss κ.
- Additionally we sampled multiple GPT‑4 prompts (“simple” vs “concise”) and compared their win rates against human benchmarks.

Results confirmed that GPT‑4 win rates correlate strongly (\(~0\.9)\) with majority human opinions.

## 8.5 Hyperparameter Sweeps and Training Regimes  

All models share the same transformer architecture wrt parameters but differ only in optimization constants.

| Method | Ref Policy | Reward Mode | Temperature Sweep | $\beta$ / $\alpha$ Sweep |
|--------|------------|-------------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| SFT | — | — | — | — |
| Preferred–FT | — | — | — | — |
| Unlikelihood | $\pi_\mathrm{{SFT}}$ | Log-likelihood + unlikelihood weight $\alpha$ | N/A | $\alpha = [0,.05,.5,1]$ |
| PPO / PPO-GT | $\pi_\mathrm{{SFT}}$ | Learned / Ground-truth reward | N/A | $LR=5e^{-5}$ ; $p(t)=50.$ |
| DPO | $\pi_\mathrm{{SFT}}$ | Implicit reward via Eq.~(\ref*){eq:dpo loss} | N/A |\ $\beta=[0\.05,.1,.5,5]$ |

All models were trained until convergence on validation–reward metrics or until *early stopping* criteria satisfied (\($>25$ epochs no improvement in val‐reward$\)$). We limited GPU memory by micro-batching prompts ($batchsize=32$), using gradient accumulation steps at total effective batchsize $256$. AdamW optimizer was used with default betas $(0\.9,.999)$; learning rates halved every four epochs if validation loss plateaued.

For DPO specifically, no additional baseline or value estimation was required due to its analytic optimal policy form; this eliminated several sources of variance present in PPO-based training loops (discussed further in Chapter 9).
