\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 3: # 3. Mathematical Preliminaries and Preference Models  

Large‑scale language models are able to capture broad world knowledge, yet steering them toward desirable behavior requires a principled way of specifying what “desirable” means.  In practice, this desideratum is provided as *human preferences*: annotators are shown two completions of a prompt and are asked to pick the one they find better.  The entire RL‑HF pipeline can be rewritten in mathematical form once we translate these binary judgments into a probability model, optimize a reward function consistent with that model, and finally solve a constrained reinforcement‑learning problem that balances reward maximization with a distance penalty between the new policy and the original unsupervised model.  

In what follows we lay out the basic mathematical ingredients that underlie these steps:  
1) the Bradley–Terry model for expressing pairwise preference probabilities,  
2) the logistic loss used to learn a scalar reward function from these preferences,  
3) the KL‑regularized objective that couples the learned reward to policy improvement, and  
4) an analytic derivation of the optimal policy directly in terms of the reward.  Throughout we keep notation as close as possible to that of the paper in order to preserve a faithful link between theory and practice.

---

## Bradley–Terry Model for Pairwise Preferences

The Bradley–Terry (BT) model is a classic choice‑probability distribution for ranking data.  In our setting, each data point consists of a *prompt* $x$ (a partial context) and two *completions* $y_{1}$ and $y_{2}$.  Human annotators observe $(x,y_{1})$ and $(x,y_{2})$ and declare one as preferred, denoted $y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x$.  The BT model assumes that there exists a latent scalar **reward function** $r^{*}(x,y)$ such that higher rewards imply stronger preference.  Conditional on this reward, the probability that $y_{1}$ beats $y_{2}$ is given by  

\begin{equation}\label{eq:bt}
    p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2} \mid x)=
    \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)}%
         {\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)} .
\end{equation}

Because only differences of rewards matter in Eq.~\eqref{eq:bt}, adding any function depending solely on $x$ leaves the probability unchanged; this is an important source of under‑identification discussed later.

## Logistic Loss for Reward Learning

Given a static dataset of labeled pairs

\[
\mathcal{D}=\Bigl\{(x^{(i)},\,y_w^{(i)},\,y_l^{(i)})\Bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]

where $y_w$ is the *winning* (preferred) completion and $y_l$ the *losing* one, we can learn an approximate reward model
$r_{\phi}(x,y)$ by maximising likelihood under the BT form.  By reparameterising with
$\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$,  

\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_loss}
   \mathcal{L}_{R}(r_{\phi},\mathcal{D})
   =-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}
        \Bigl[\log\, \sigma\!\bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_w)-r_{\phi}(x,y_l)\bigr)
              \Bigr].
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:reward_loss} is precisely a binary cross‐entropy loss applied to the difference of predicted rewards.  In practice $r_{\phi}$ is implemented by attaching a linear head on top of a transformer encoder that ingests $(x,y)$; training proceeds exactly as in standard supervised learning.

Note that any constant shift in $r_{\phi}$ leaves $\mathcal{L}_{R}$ unchanged because it cancels out inside $\sigma(\cdot)$.  The same shift invariance carries over to any other BT–compatible loss formulation.

---

## KL‑Constrained Policy Optimization Objective

Once we have a reward evaluator \( r_{\phi} \), we turn to policy optimisation.  The initial unsupervised language model – or its late supervised fine–tuned variant – provides us with a *reference policy* \( \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) \).  Directly maximising expected reward risks catastrophic drift; consequently we restrict policy updates by penalising their divergence from \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \).  

The general objective takes the form

\begin{equation}\label{eq:rlhf_obj}
   J(\pi_{\theta})
   = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal D,\,
           y\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}
          \bigl[\,r_{\phi}(x,y)\bigr]
     - \beta\, 
      D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\;\|\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr] ,
\end{equation}

where \( \beta>0 \) modulates how far we are allowed to move from \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \).  
The KL term can be interpreted as an information constraint: it measures how many bits on average it would cost to describe draws from \( \pi_{\theta} \) instead of from \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \).

Directly optimising Eq.~\eqref{eq:rlhf_obj} is difficult because language generation is discrete; therefore traditional RLHF pipelines use stochastic gradient estimation (REINFORCE), on top of which they apply proximal methods such as PPO.

---

## Deriving the Optimal Policy from a Given Reward Function

A key observation – central to Direct Preference Optimization – is that when we optimise Eq.~\eqref{eq:rlhf_obj}, **the optimal policy has an explicit closed form** in terms of any given reward function \( r(x,y) \).  

### Derivation Outline

\[

      \sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)
             \exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac 1{\beta}\, r(x,y)\Bigr).
      \tag{A}

Because human annotators furnish only pairwise preferences, not full expectations under (unknown) rewards or policies, we must link Eq.~(\ref{eq:mapping_to_reward}) back into observable quantities.                β·

Thus, if we denote by $\hat r_\theta(x , y )
= β· log (π_θ(y|x)/π_ref(y|x))$,
the probability that humans prefer candidate $y_1$ over candidate $y_2$
is exactly

<blockquote>
<p>
\( p^*( y_1 ⪰ y_2 | x )
 = σ(
            β· log[π_\theta(y_2|x)/π_\text{{reg}}(y_2|x)]
          −β· log[π_\theta(y_1|x)/π_\text{{reg}}(y_1|x)]
 ...).
</p>

Consequently we may **directly train** $\theta$ by maximizing likelihood over observed preference data:

```math
ℒ_DPO(π_θ ; π_ref)
= − E_{ ( x , y_w , y_l )∈𝒟 }
    [ log σ (
             β·log[ πθ(y w | x)/π_ref(y w | x)]
           −β·log[ πθ(y l | x)/π_ref(y l | x)]
         ) ] .
```

This simple binary cross‑entropy loss has exactly the same functional form as Eq.~(\ref{eq:reward_loss}), but it now operates on logits derived from relative probabilities instead of raw scores.  
Importantly it obviates:
* The need to explicitly train $r_φ$, so no separate reward model exists;
* Any Monte Carlo sampling required in PPO or REINFORCE; and
* Hyperparameters associated with value‑function baselines or KL clipping schedules beyond $\beta$, which directly controls conservativeness.

---

### Why This Works Intuitively

A single iteration of gradient descent on $\mathcal L_\mathrm DPO$ pushes up $\log π_\theta(y_w|x)$ while pushing down $\log π_\theta(y_l|x)$, weighted by $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))$.  
If at some step $\hat r_\theta$ already ranks $y_w ≻ y_l$, then σ≈0 and there is almost no update—rendering the process self‑normalising.  
When $\hat r_\theta$ mis‑orders them ($σ≈½$), updates are strongest; thus errors drive corrections more aggressively than correct predictions do drive reinforcement—an elegant incarnation of “weighting by mistake”.

All operations remain deterministic differentiable because logits are fully differentiable functions of $\theta$: backpropagation propagates through every term automatically.
