\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 14: # 14. Limitations, Open Questions & Future Directions

Large language models (LLMs) can be steered by human preferences, but the rapid success of *Direct Preference Optimization* (DPO) has also highlighted a number of practical and theoretical gaps. In this chapter we first review the empirical limitations that emerged in the experiments reported for DPO, then we formulate a research agenda that spans algorithmic robustness, scalability and evaluation. Each sub‑topic is treated as a mini‑research proposal: we articulate the problem statement, why it matters for DPO and RLHF at large, and sketch possible lines of investigation.

---

## 14.1 Out‑of‑Distribution Generalization – A Depth Study

### 14.1.1 Problem Statement
DPO was evaluated on three downstream tasks (sentiment control, summarization, dialogue) with relatively modest domain shifts from the pre‑training data—benchmark datasets such as IMDb reviews or Reddit posts. Yet LLMs are routinely deployed on writings that differ substantially from their training corpora (e.g., legal documents, medical reports, scientific literature). The two‑point newsletter experiment in Section 5.5 (CNN/DailyMail news articles) provided preliminary evidence that DPO generalizes reasonably well under a weak distribution shift, but detailed analysis remains scarce.

### 14.1.2 Why It Matters
If a policy is over‑fitted to the distribution of prompts seen during preference collection, its KL constraint will not regularize it properly when presented with unseen prompts––the safety tax in RLHF can quietly vanish. Moreover, bias can accumulate differently across domains: a policy that rarely consults the reference policy on one domain may still produce unexpected hallucinations on another.

### 14.1.3 Research Directions
1. **Benchmark Construction** – Assemble multi‑domain preference datasets (e.g., synthetic legal digests vs. Reddit posts; medical discharge summaries vs.\ news). For each domain generate pairwise preferences using back‑edged oracle rewards (e.g., expert annotations or high‐confidence rule‑based metrics).  
2. **Quantitative Metrics** – Measure *domain shift stability* by computing KL divergence between DPO and PPO policies across domains, and correlating with *win‑rate drop* relative to oracle reviewers on each domain.  
3. **Domain Adaptation Experiments** – Test whether fine‑tuning on a small set of *unlabeled* prompts from a target domain improves performance (see next section).  
4. **Causality Analysis** – Use counterfactual intervention frameworks (e.g., ALEPH analysis) to distinguish performance drops due to encoder drift vs.\ decoder bias.

## 14.2 Self‑Labeling with Unlabeled Prompts

### 14.2.1 Problem Statement
Current DPO pipelines rely on an explicit preference dataset \(\mathcal{D}=\{x^{(i)},y_w^{(i)},y_l^{(i)}\}\), which is expensive to collect at scale: every prompt needs a pairwise comparison from humans or an oracle. Yet large unsupervised corpora provide a vast reservoir of unlabeled prompts \(\mathcal{U}\).

### 14.2.2 Why It Matters
If DPO could exploit unlabeled data via *self‑labeling*, we could dramatically reduce annotation cost and potentially allow continual learning as new prompts arrive.

### 14.2.3 Research Directions
1. **Pseudo‑Preference Generation** – Let a provisional policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) sample two completions \((y_1,y_2)\) for each unlabeled prompt \(x\in\mathcal{U}\). Use an auxiliary *quality function* \(q(y)\) (e.g., perplexity + heuristic sentiment score) to decide which completion should be considered “preferred.”  
2. **Iterative Self‑Supervision** – Alternate between training DPO on the pseudo‑preferences and updating \(\pi_{\theta}\). Analyze whether the KL constraint suppresses drift toward degenerate solutions within this loop.
3. **Curriculum Design** – Start with easy prompts where \(q(y)\) is highly informative; gradually introduce harder prompts as confidence grows.
4. **Theoretical Guarantees** – Explore whether DPO admits any convergence guarantees when preferences are noisy or biased; relate to noisy label learning literature.

## 14.3 Diagnostics for Reward Over‑Optimization

### 14.3.1 Problem Statement
Both RLHF and DPO risk maximizing the learned reward at the expense of other desirable attributes—hallucinations, repetitiveness, factual inaccuracy—because the reward shape is learned from limited human preferences.

### 14.3.2 Why It Matters
Over‑optimization can manifest as mode collapse or an increase in *spurious reward hacking*. For safety-critical deployments (medical diagnosis, code synthesis), this is unacceptable.

### 14.3.3 Research Directions
1. **Score Distributions Analysis** – Track the entropy of log probabilities under \(\pi_{\theta}\) versus \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). Sudden entropy loss may signal mode collapse.
2. **Reward Gap Monitoring** – Compute \(\mathbb{E}[r_{\phi}]\) vs.\ \(\mathbb{E}[\hat r_{\theta}]\) across epochs; large increases in implicit reward without corresponding gains in human preference win rate are warning signs.
3. **Adversarial Prompt Injection** – Create prompts designed to trigger known failure modes and evaluate if DPO’s KL constraint still prevents catastrophic outputs.
4. **Differentiable Oracle Comparison** – When possible, compare against an independent oracle (e.g., GPT-4 or expert annotator) post hoc; quantify *Oracle Miss Rate* as an over‑optimization metric.
5. **Ablation on Importance Weighting** – Systematically vary the coefficient applied to \(\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})\); investigate whether heavier weighting correlates with increased hallucination rates.

## 14.4 Scaling Experiments Beyond Ten Billion Parameters

### 14.4.1 Problem Statement
All experiments reported used models up to 6B parameters due to compute constraints; large enterprise models (13B–175B) both have richer behaviors and present significant optimization challenges.

### 14.4.2 Why It Matters
Scaling laws suggest diminishing returns when increasing model size without corresponding increases in data quality or algorithmic fidelity; verifying that DPO scales lawfully confirms its practical viability.
