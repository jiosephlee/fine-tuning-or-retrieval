Chapter 10: # 10 Performance on Real Preference Data Sets

In this chapter we move from toy settings to the realistic environments in which large language models are actually fine‑tuned by practitioners: **summarization** and **single‑turn dialogue** on real, crowd‑source or human‑annotated preference data.  We show that Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) not only matches the performance of reinforcement‑learning–based approaches such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) but does so with a *fraction* of the compute, while remaining robust to the choice of sampling temperature at inference time.  For each task we examine the trade‑off between *temperature* and *win rate*, and we compare against a “Best‑of‑N” baseline that samples many trajectories and picks the best according to an independently trained reward model.  

The chapter is organized around four core empirical observations that underpin DPO’s practical value:

1. **Summarization win rates versus temperature curves.**  
2. **Dialogue win rates versus temperature curves.**  
3. **Best‑of‑N baseline comparisons.**  
4. **Robustness to sampling temperature relative to PPO.**

Below we elaborate on each point, providing detailed experimental methodology, quantitative results, and an intuitive explanation of why DPO attains such strong performance.

## 10.1 Summarization Win Rates Versus Temperature Curves

### Experimental Setup

The summarization experiments use the public Reddit TL;DR dataset, consisting of forum posts paired with succinct humans–written summaries.  A pretrained GPT‑J model (6 B parameters) is first fine‑tuned by supervised learning (SFT) on human‐written completions from a separate but similarly‑trained SFT checkpoint named *openai_summarize_tldr_sft*.  The resulting SFT model serves as both the *reference policy* (\(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)) for DPO and PPO, and as a source of candidates for the Best‑of‑N baseline.

Human preferences are obtained from *stiennon2022learning*, which contains pairwise judgments over outputs produced by a distinct SFT model; each pair \((y_{w}, y_{l})\) is labelled with which summary the human prefers under prompt \(x\).  Because the preference set is collected on a different base model, we fit an *implicit* reward via DPO’s weighted cross‑entropy loss using \(\beta=1\).  PPO uses a surrogate objective with five PPO hyperparameter sweeps, and the reward function used in PPO is the same as that implicitly learned by DPO (i.e., they share \(r_{\phi}\)).  

For inference, we sample tokens from each policy with temperature \(\tau \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1\}\).  We generate \(K=50\) samples per prompt and compute win rates using GPT‑4 as a proxy for human evaluation (see Section 9 for justification).  The “winning” completion is defined as one that GPT‑4 deems higher quality relative to the reference completion supplied by the dataset.

### Results

Table 10.1 presents win rates for each method at every temperature:

| Method | τ=0 | τ=0.25 | τ=0.5 | τ=0.75 | τ=1 |
|--------|------|--------|-------|--------|-----|
| SFT    | 53 % | 55 %   | 58 % | 61 %   | 62 % |
| PPO    | 57 % | 61 %   | 64 % | 66 %   | 65 % |
| DPO    | 61 % | 68 %   | 71 % | 72 %   | 70 % |
| Best–of-128| –  | 76 %  | 78 % | 79 %   | 80 % |

> **Key observation:**  
> DPO attains the highest win rate at every temperature except τ=1 (where it remains competitive), whereas PPO lags by roughly five percentage points across all temperatures.

Plotting these results yields bell-shaped curves where all methods peak near τ≈0–0.5; however DPO’s curve is shifted upwards by about +7%. The sharpness of DPO’s curve indicates that its outputs are consistently high quality even when sampling aggressively (τ=0.75), whereas PPO’s curve drops when temperature rises beyond τ≈0.5.

### Discussion

The superior win rate of DPO can be understood through its intrinsic balance between two forces: (1) maximizing expected reward according to the learned preference distribution; (2) constraining deviation from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) via KL regularisation embedded in its objective \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}\). Since this balance exists *within* each gradient step—no post-hoc reweighting or clipping occurs—DPO learns a policy that naturally produces higher-quality outputs without over-exploring improbable sequences.

Critically, because DPO’s loss weight depends on an implicit reward estimate \(\hat r_{\theta}\), it down-weights updates that would overly shift probability mass away from high-probability regions of \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). This prevents mode collapse or excessive reliance on low-probability tokens, explaining why its win rates do not deteriorate even when temperature increases.

## 10.2 Dialogue Win Rates Versus Temperature Curves

### Experimental Setup

For dialogue we use Anthropic’s Helpful and Harmless collection (~170k dialogues). Each transcript ends with two responses from an unknown large LM; humans then label which response they prefer under active user query \(x\). Since no pre-trained SFT checkpoint exists for this task, we start from a Pythia‑2.8B base model and train a “Preferred-FT” baseline by supervising on just the preferred completions:  
\[
\pi_{\text{ref}} = \argmax_{\pi}
\mathbb{E}_{x,y_w}\bigl[\log \pi(y_w\mid x)\bigr].
\]
This Preferred-FT serves as both reference policy for DPO/PPO and as a neutral evaluator for Best‑of‑N.

The preference dataset comprises ~120k pairs spanning diverse topics (astrophysics queries, relationship advice, etc.). Training proceeds exactly as in summarization: we set \(\beta=0.5\), perform gradient updates until convergence (≈15k updates), then evaluate each policy over held-out prompts.

Inference uses sampling temperatures \(\tau = {0,\;0.25,\;0.5,\;0.75}\). For GPT‑4 evaluation we query it with prompt “Which response is more helpful/harmless?” against both policy output and reference completion; rapid batching ensures efficient scoring.

### Results

Table 10.2 shows win rates per method:

| Method      | τ=0   | τ=0.25 | τ=0.5    |
|-------------|-------|--------|----------|
| Preferred-FT| 60 %  | —      | —        |
| PPO         | 63 %  | **67 %**| **68 %** |
| DPO         | **66 %**| **70 %**| **72 %** |

> **Key point:** Beyond temperature zero everything else favors DPO: it consistently beats PPO by ~3–5 percentage points across all temperatures.

Notably, the difference between DPO at τ=0 and With Best–of-128 remains negligible (~+2%), indicating that most gains come from improved policy rather than costlier post-induction inference.

### Discussion

Resting upon TCP-like “Conservative Pseudo RL”, DPO adapts more aggressively to user subobjective without sacrificing safety constraints enforced during training: dropping tokens where reinterpretations would contradict human preferences leads instantly to higher quality dialogue artifacts while maintaining overall KL budget.

PPO requires repeated sampling inside its update loop—a costly operation penalised when scaling up token length or number of training steps—whereas DPO operates purely with deterministic gradients and log-likelihoods on offline pairs: allowing quick throughput even on resource-limited GPUs.

## 10.3 Best-of-N Baseline Comparisons

The Best-of-N strategy evaluates all policies in terms of how well they rank when coupled with an externally trained reward function:

1. Sample \(K\) completions (\(K = 32,\;128,\;256,\;512\)) per prompt.
2. Score each sample using reward \(r_{\phi}\) trained independently from the preference data.
3. Pick the highest-scoring sample for each prompt.
4. Compute win rate versus reference completions in test set.

Because this approach lasts many steps at inference time (exponential growth of cost), it is often championed only in academic settings where re-training budgets are negligible.

For both summarization and dialogue experiments we run Best-of-N across several \(K\). Figures showing cumulative win rate vs \(K\) reveal diminishing returns after \(K≈128\). In summarization: Best-of-128 reaches ~80 %, slightly above both PPO and DPO but rivaled only by computationally expensive tuning with higher temperatures or longer beam passes which are incompatible with small budgets.

In dialogue: Best-of-128 achieves ~75 %. Although slightly higher than pure DPO or PPO at similar compute budgets during training, best-of-N demands GPU usage during inference many times longer than simple single-sample generation—a major bottleneck in production pipelines dealing with milliseconds latency constraints typical in conversational AI services.

## 10.4 Robustness to Sampling Temperature Relative to PPO

An important practical question concerns how sensitive performance is to *sampling temperature*, especially when deploying models into real-world services where tunable decoding parameters are often limited or fixed due to latency considerations.

To check robustness we compare final win rates across temperatures after full training:

- **PPO:** Medium reliability; e.g., moving from τ=small≈0 (greedy decoding) to τ≈1 leads to >12 percentage point drop in summarization work.
- **DPO:** Significantly flatter curve: change from τ=small→τ≈1 yields only ≈4–6 percentage pusidual diffission.
- **Best-of-N:** Independant of input temperature because sampling strategy decouples generation without weight adjustments—obviously unrealistic for most platforms due to runtime overhead discussed earlier.

### Conclusion

This chapter demonstrates that direct.preference optimisation is not just a theoretical curiosity but provides an *implementation-ready alternative* capable of scaling up—even within conventional CPU/GPU budgets—to high-capacity language models used in modern NLP pipelines over real preference data sets like Reddit TL;DR summarisation or Anthropic dialogues with complex human thresholds.
We confirm three main properties that make it attractive:

- **State-of-the-art performance:** Wins balance beyond already-public RLHF baselines.
- **Computational lightness:** No RL loops needed during fine-tuning; inference evaluation requires only single pass per sample.
- **Temperature robustness:** Quintessential advantage requiring minimal runtime tuning sensitivity compared with normative RLHF schemes such as PPO leveraging stochastic RL gradients with high variance.”

These results strengthen our conviction that the philosophy underlying Direct Preference Optimization holds tangible promise for open-source practitioners who cannot afford elaborate reinforcement learning pipelines yet still want safe-and-surface-aligned language model generation.”
