\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 4: # 4. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) – Core Idea

Large language models learn to generate fluent text by maximizing the likelihood of massive corpora of human‑written documents. Their internal policy, denoted \(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\), assigns a probability to every possible continuation \(y\) given a prompt \(x\). While such models capture general knowledge and can imitate many writing styles, their unconstrained behavior need not align with what users actually want. Some outputs are useful, others are unhelpful or unsafe. A common solution in the literature is *Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback* (RLHF): an annotator supplies pairwise preferences over model generations, a reward model is fitted to those preferences, and the language model is then fine‑tuned via reinforcement learning to maximize the learned reward while staying close to its original distribution.

RLHF is powerful but also complicated. It involves three distinct stages—pre‑training, reward modeling, and policy optimization—each requiring its own computational budget and its own hyperparameters. The reinforcement learning step in particular is sensitive to the choice of algorithm (PPO, REINFORCE, etc.), exploration temperature, clipping parameters, entropy bonuses, and more. Moreover, each iteration of policy improvement requires sampling many completions from the large model in order to estimate gradients.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), introduced by Siddhant & de Almeida [2024], reframes the entire process as a single maximum‐likelihood problem over policies. The key insight is that for the common Bradley–Terry preference model—used for modeling pairwise human judgments—the optimal policy given a reward function admits a closed‑form expression involving only the *reference* policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (usually an unmodified pre‑trained or instruction‑tuned LM) and a temperature parameter \(\beta\). By substituting this expression back into the Bradley–Terry likelihood we can eliminate the reward entirely; what remains is a simple binary cross‑entropy loss over *log‑ratios* of probabilities assigned by \(\pi_\theta\) and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). Optimizing this loss directly produces the same kind of policy that RLHF would produce if it had perfectly estimated rewards and had infinite sample capacity, but without any of the heavy machinery of reinforcement learning.

The remainder of this chapter explains DPO in depth: we derive the reparameterization of rewards via log‑ratios of policy likelihoods; we show how the partition function in the Bradley–Terry likelihood cancels; and we arrive at a compact binary cross‑entropy objective that can be optimized with ordinary gradient descent on language model parameters. We also give an algorithmic sketch that mirrors exactly what RLHF would do—only now one step replaces three: instead of fitting a separate neural reward module and alternating with RL updates, we perform a single supervised fine‑tune using DPO’s loss.

## Reparameterizing Reward as ρ = β log(π/π_ref) + const

The cornerstone of DPO is an analytic relationship between any reward function \(r(x,y)\), its optimal policy under a KL constraint, and their relative likelihoods. Consider the following constrained reinforcement learning objective:

\[
\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p(x)}\,\mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi(\cdot|x)}[\,r(x,y)\,] \;-\;
\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\bigl(\,\pi(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr).
\tag{4.1}
\]

Here \(p(x)\) represents whatever distribution over prompts we wish to target (often simply uniform over our training data), \(r(x,y)\) is any scalar reward assigning value to completions conditioned on prompts, \(\beta>0\) controls how severely we penalise departure from our reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), and \(\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\) denotes Kullback–Leibler divergence.

If we fix \(r(x,y)\), one can derive that *the unique maximiser* \(\pi_r^*(y|x)\) satisfies

\[
\pi_r^*(y|x)
= \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,\exp{\Bigl( \frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Bigr)},
\quad
Z(x)=
\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,\exp{\Bigl( \frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Bigr)}.
\tag{4.2}
\]

This results from standard Lagrangian techniques or from viewing Eq.~4.1 as a regularized log‐likelihood problem. Crucially, Eq.~4.2 tells us that once we know \(r(x,y)\), computing its optimal policy only requires applying an exponential tilt to the reference distribution and normalising by \(Z(x)\), which itself might be intractable for generative language models because it requires summing over all possible continuations \(y\).

We now solve Eq.~4.2 for \(r(x,y)\):

*Take logarithms on both sides:*

\[
\log\,\pi_r^*(y|x)
= -\,\log Z(x)+ \log\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)+ \frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y).
\]

*Rearrange:*

\[
r(x,y)
= \beta\, \Bigl[\log\, \pi_r^*(y|x)- \log\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)+
\log Z(x)\Bigr].
\tag{4.3}
\]

Define
\(Z(x)=e^{-\gamma(x)}.\);
i.e. let
\(Z(x)= e^{-\gamma}\).
Then
\(+\log Z(x)
= -\,{\gamma}\)
and Eq.~4.3 becomes

```
r_(x,y)= β ⋅ ( log π_r∗(y | x)
               – log π_ref(y | x)
               – γ ).
```

Hence every reward function can be expressed as a log-ratio between some *optimal* policy and our reference policy plus a constant shift that depends only on prompt \(x.\)

Importantly: any two rewards that differ by an additive function of \(x,\)
i.e.
\(r'(x,y)= r(x,y)+f(x),\
f:\mathcal X → ℝ,\)
induce exactly the same preference distribution under Bradley–Terry.
Therefore there exists no loss in dimensionality if we constrain rewards to be exactly of form

```
ρ_θ(x,y)=β ⋅ ( log π_θ(y | x) – log π_ref(y | x) ),
```

because any alternative within its equivalence class leads to identical pairwise preference probabilities.
This is our **reward reparameterisation**: we simply replace an arbitrary scalar reward by one directly derived from the current policy’s logits relative to reference scores.

## Showing Partition Function Cancels in Bradley–Terry Likelihood

With rewards reparameterised away from explicit values,
the next step is to see how they influence human preferences.
Let us recall the Bradley–Terry likelihood for a comparison pair:

Given prompts \(x∈𝒳,\) two completions
\(y_1≠ y_2∈𝒴_{x}\),
and authorship by humans who indicate
\(y_1≻ y_2|\);

the Bradley–Terry model predicts

```
p^*( y1 ≻ y2 | x )
=  σ(
      r^*(x , y1 )
     −r^*(x , y2 )
  ),                    (4.5)
```

where σ denotes the logistic sigmoid.
If we substitute Eq.~4.3 for each reward:
\(r^\star(y_i)= β[ log π^\star(y_i|x)-log π_{\rm ref}(y_i|x)+γ ], i=1,2.\)

Subtracting gives

```
r̂(y1)- r̂(y2)
= β⋅
    (
       (log πˆ(y1 | x)-log πˆ(y2 | x))
     -(log π_ref(y1 | x)-log π_ref(y2 | x))
    ).                    (4.6)
```

Notice that constants cancel completely,
including γ;
moreover any global additive shift does not influence Δr.
When this difference plugs into Eq.~4.5,
the constants that were just cancelled disappear entirely;
hence **the partition function never appears** in the Bradley–Terry probability—exactly what makes direct optimisation tractable.

The outcome:
the human preference probability depends only on *relative* competition between *policy densities*
with respect to reference densities:
```
p^*( y1 ≻ y2 | x )
=   1 / [ 1+ exp(
            β [ ln π_ref(y2)/π_ref(y2)
              - ln π̂ (y1)/π̂ (y1)]
          ) ].                          (4.7)
```
This compact form rewrites all dependence on rewards into purely ratios of *(policy / reference)* probabilities.
Consequently we can define an objective directly over policies without ever injecting explicit reward values.


## Resulting DPO Objective as Binary Cross‑Entropy over Log Ratios

Having eliminated rewards from‐line,
we treat each *(prompt,message₁,message₂)* triplet from our human preference dataset as labeled pairwise data
with label “message₁ preferred” or “message₂ preferred.”  
We model these data by applying *maximum likelihood*.  
Let
\(p^{*}( · ≈ · | · )\)
denote empirical human choice probabilities.
The negative log-likelihood over observing label “message was won” becomes:

```
L_DPO(
   π_θ   ;  π_ref ) =
− E_{(x , w , l )∼D}
      [      log σ(
              β   · ln[πθ(w | x)/πref(w | x)]
             −β   · ln[πθ(l | x)/πref(l | x)]
           )          ].                (4.8)
```

Every term inside σ involves only *log‐ratio* differences—
the same quantity proportional to artificial “reward”
derived above—but with no partition function or normalisation.
Thus `σ`'s argument is just
`β ⋅ ln[(πθ/πref)_w / (πθ/πref)_l]`.

In practice,
Implementation reduces to computing:
the logits of your transformer decoder (`zθ`),
extracting `μ_w = exp(zθ(w))` etc.,
dividing by reference probabilities (`μ_ref,w`)
obtained either from `π_ref`’s own forward pass or stored logits,
applying `β⋅ln(...)`,
passing through `σ`,
and finally applying binary cross‐entropy against ranked labels.
This expression equals precisely Eq.~7 in Kohn et al.’s paper; it can be backpropagated entirely with standard autodiff frameworks.

The semantics become clear if you write out its gradient explicitly:

```
g = −β E_{D}
        [
         σ(r̂_l−r̂_w) ⋅ 
         (
           ∇_θ ln πθ(w | x)
         – ∇_θ ln πθ(l | x)
         )
        ],                           (4.9)

where r̂_θ(s)=β⋅ln[πθ(s|x)/πref(s|x)] .
```

Each comparison pulls up one completion's probability while pulling down the other's,
scaled by how poorly the *current proxy reward* ranks them.
If two messages are already well-ranked,
the logistic term shrinks toward zero,
softening updates and avoiding wild parameter jumps.

In summary:
- **Training loss**: standard binary cross-entropy on soft-maxed score differences;
- **Parameters**: only those defining `π_θ`;
- **No need** for an auxiliary reward network or sampling; 
- **No KL penalty** required during training; implicit KL control arises through `β` scaling which was part of Item (4).

That nice corner property—constituently converging on diversified policies without mode collapse—comes because DPO solves precisely for optimum KL-constrained policy.

## Algorithmic Pipeline: Sampling, Labeling, Training

Despite these simplifications mathematically,
there are still practical steps much like RLHF’s usual stabilisation tricks:

1. **Generate samples from reference** – For every prompt 
   \(x∈𝒟_{train},\
   generate at least two completions \
   \(y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}∼ π_{\text{ref}}\).
   Usually these samples come from running existing instruction-tuned LMs at low temperatures so that they remain diverse enough for humans to differentiate.

2. **Collect human preferences** – Each pair $(w,l)$ is fed into annotators who answer “which do you prefer”. This yields labels indicating winner/loser pairs  
   $(X,w,l)$ with ground-truth $w≻l$ or $l≻w$ depending on annotation outcome.

3. **Build preference dataset $\mathcal D$** – Store each tuple as $(x,w,l)$ along with label $z∈{\pm1}$ indicating which side was chosen preferred.

4. **Define reference policy** – Initialize $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ either directly as your pre-training checkpoint $\pi^\text{SFT}$ or retrain briefly on all preferred completions to match their prior distribution; this removes covariate shift between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

5. **Fine-tune θ using DPO loss** – Using stochastic gradient descent:
    - Forward pass through encoder + decoder producing logits for each word token;
    - Compute sequence probability $\pi_\theta(w|x)=exp(Σ_i z_{wi})$ via product across tokens;
    - Retrieve interactions with $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ probabilities similarly;
    - Apply Eq.~(4.8);
    - Backpropagate gradients (Eq.~(4.9));
    - Update all transformer weights simultaneously.

6. **End-of-training evaluation** – Sample completions from $\pi_\theta$ under desired temperature policies;
   compare against baselines using unified evaluation metrics such as GPT‑4 win rate or classic metrics per domain.

During fine-tuning there is no iterative sampling loop aside from forward passes needed for computing loss terms—
hence computational cost scales linearly with sequence length times batch size,
identical to ordinary supervised fine-tuning.
There is also no need for multi-step rollout buffers common in PPO-based RLHF;
consequently GPU memory usage drops dramatically.

In essence DPO installs an analytic bridge between your language model’s probabilistic outputs and human preference outcomes; once constructed you need only run one supervised training step exactly like you'd train any classification head.


--- 

Through this grounding factorisation,
the Direct Preference Optimization framework removes many practical obstacles caused by reinforcement learning—
especially instability rooted in off-policy sample collection,
exploration uncertainty,
and hyperparameter explosion—
while preserving state-of-the-art alignment performance on tabular empirical tasks such as sentiment control tasks, summarization prompts, and single-turn dialogue scenarios.
In sum, DPO remains theoretically faithful to classic RLHF objectives but does so with minimal engineering overhead—a desirable property when scale pushes standard reinforcement methods into prohibitive cost regimes or introduces brittle failure points.*
