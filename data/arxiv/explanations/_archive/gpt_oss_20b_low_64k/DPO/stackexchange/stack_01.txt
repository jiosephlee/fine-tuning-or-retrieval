\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How does the KL‑constrained optimal policy in DPO differ from the standard REINFORCE policy used in PPO?
Question:
The paper derives the optimal policy for a reward function with a KL constraint as 
$$\pi_r(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr).$$
I understand this is a soft‑max reweighting of the reference policy, but I am confused how this differs from the actor‑critic style updates used in PPO. Does the KL term guarantee the same behaviour? Also, why is the partition function $Z(x)$ problematic to estimate, and why does DPO avoid it altogether?
Answer:
**Answer**

The KL‑constrained objective that appears in every RLHF pipeline is  

$$
\max_{\pi_\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal D,\; y\sim \pi_\theta}\!\!\bigl[r_\phi(x,y)\bigr]\;-\;\beta 
\,D_{\!KL}\!\bigl[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,
 \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr].
$$
\tag{1}

PPO (and other actor–critic methods) solve a *surrogate* of this problem by
sampling trajectories from the current policy, estimating the reward
advantage, and clipping the probability‑ratio update so that each step does
not move the policy too far from the previous one.  
The policy update therefore looks like a REINFORCE gradient,

$$
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y|x)\;
 \bigl(r_\phi(x,y)-b(x)\bigr),
$$

where \(b(x)\) is a learned value estimate or a scalar baseline that
approximates the (unknown) partition function  
$$
\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)e^{r_\phi(x,y)/\beta}.
$$
Because this quantity is not known analytically and must be estimated
either by Monte‑Carlo sampling or by a learned critic, PPO typically has
high‑variance gradients and needs careful tuning of clipping, learning
rates, and reward scaling.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### The closed‑form optimal policy

For any fixed reward function \(r(x,y)\), Eq. (1) has an *exact*
optimiser given by a soft‑max reweighting of the reference policy:

$$
\pi_r(y|x)=
\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,e^{r(x,y)/\beta}}
       {Z(x)}, 
        \qquad 
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,e^{r(x,y)/\beta}.
$$
\tag{2}

This is nothing more than an exponential tilt of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$;
the KL term guarantees that the tilted distribution never strays far
from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  The only obstacle to using (2) directly is
the *partition function* $Z(x)$.  Computing it would require summing
over every possible response $y$—an impossible task for language,
since there are astronomically many completions.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Why solving for the partition function is hard

The partition function plays two roles:

1. **Normalisation** – it makes $\pi_r(\cdot|x)$a proper probability
   distribution.
2. **Recalibration** – it absorbs any global offset in the reward.
   Two reward functions that differ only by a constant additive term
   yield exactly the same policy (they belong to the same equivalence
   class defined in § 5.1 of the paper).  Because of this equivalence,
   training can safely ignore any unknown additive constant.

In RLHF we therefore normally *concentrate* on learning $r_\phi(x,y)$
and let PPO implicit all these constants via its critic or baseline.
However, baselines are noisy, making training unstable.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### How DPO sidesteps $Z(x)$

DPO replaces the explicit reward $r_\phi(x,y)$ with an *implicit*
reward that is expressed directly in terms of the policy we want to learn:

$$
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=
\beta\,\log 
 \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} .
$$
\tag{3}

Substituting (3) into the Bradley–Terry preference model yields

$$
p^*(y_1\succeq y_2|x)=
\sigma\!\Bigl(
  \beta\, \log 
  \frac{\pi^*(y_2|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2|x)}
-\beta\, \log 
  \frac{\pi^*(y_1|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1|x)}
  \Bigr),
  $$
with $\sigma$ denoting the logistic function.
The partition function has vanished completely because it cancels
between numerator and denominator.  
Training therefore reduces to maximizing a *straightforward likelihood*
over observed preference pairs:

$$
{\cal L}_{DPO}(\theta)
= -\,{\mathbb E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)} 
   \log\sigma(
     \beta\,\log 
       \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
     - 
     \beta\,\log 
       \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
    ).
$$

Equation (5) is a single‑head cross‑entropy loss; there is no need for
importance sampling or bootstrapped value estimates.  
Because we learn directly the terms that appear in (4), we automatically
enforce that each update moves towards higher‑likelihood preferred outputs
and lower‑likelihood dispreferred ones while respecting the KL bound set by
the hyperparameter $\beta$.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### What does DPO do compared with PPO?

* **Same objective:** both optimise (1).  The difference is only in how
  they *parameterise* and *estimate* its optimum.
* **PPO:** samples trajectories → estimates advantage → clips ratio →
  changes policy via actor–critic gradients.  Requires baselines,
  respects KL only implicitly, and can collapse to mode‑collapse or get stuck if the critic is poor.
* **DPO:** turns optimisation into a deterministic supervised problem –
  no sampling required during training, no critic needed, no baselines.
  The KL constraint becomes an *explicit weight* (the factor of β)
  inside every gradient step.

Empirically (Fig. 4 of the paper), DPO achieves higher rewards at lower KL divergence than PPO, even when PPO receives ground‑truth rewards.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Summary

- The KL‑constrained optimal policy in Eq. (2) is a softmax reweighting of  
  the reference policy; it guarantees closeness to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ but needs a partition function $Z(x)$.
- Estimating $Z(x)$ would require summing over all possible generations,
  which is impossible for language models.
- DPO eliminates $Z(x)$ by re‑parameterising the reward in terms of the *policy itself*, producing an analytic likelihood objective (Eq. 5).
- Unlike PPO’s REINFORCE/actor‑critic updates—which rely on noisy baselines to approximate $Z(x)$—DPO uses pure supervised optimisation with no sampling during training, leading to greater stability and efficiency while still respecting the same KL constraint.

Thus, DPO achieves what PPO does through reinforcement learning but does so entirely deterministically and without having to estimate or approximate any partition function.
