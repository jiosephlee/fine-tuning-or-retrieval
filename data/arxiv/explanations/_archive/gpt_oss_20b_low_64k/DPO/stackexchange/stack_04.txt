\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How is a reference policy π_ref chosen when no SFT model exists?
Question:
When working with datasets where no supervised fine‑tuned model is available (e.g., Anthropic helpful‑harmless dialogue), the paper proposes initializing $\,π_{	ext{ref}}$ by maximizing likelihood over preferred completions. How does this differ from simply using a vanilla pre‑trained LM, and why is it important for mitigating distribution shift? Are there scenarios where this strategy might fail?
Answer:
**Choosing a Reference Policy When No SFT Exists**

In a typical RLHF pipeline the reference policy \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \) is the supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) model, \( \pi^{\text{SFT}} \).  
When an SFT model is unavailable—e.g., in the Anthropic “helpful‑harmless” dialogue corpus—Section 4 of the paper prescribes a lightweight alternative:  
\[
\pi_{\text{ref}}
  =\arg\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w)\sim\mathcal D}
          \bigl[\log \pi(y_w\mid x)\bigr]
\tag{1}
\]
i.e. a language model trained only on the *preferred* completions in the preference dataset.

---

### Why this is **not** the same as using a vanilla pretrained LM  

1. **Distribution Alignment.**  
   The preference pairs \((x,y_w,y_l)\) are generated from a reference distribution that ideally matches the *true* future use‑case. A generic pretrained LM has often seen a very different token distribution, especially in in‑domain jargon or constrained response styles. Fine‑tuning on \(y_w\) pulls the model towards *the same support that the preference judgments were made over*, reducing the likelihood that unseen choices will be “out‑of‑distribution” for the implicit reward.

2. **KL Regularisation Target.**  
   DPO’s objective (eq (9) in the paper) contains a KL penalty against \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \). If \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \) is far from the true reference policy, this penalty may push the final policy toward an irrelevant region of probability mass, weakening alignment with human preferences. Using a vanilla LM would make that penalty encode an arbitrary prior rather than one grounded in actual human choices.

3. **Weighting of Preference Examples.**  
   In DPO each term’s contribution is scaled by  
   \( \sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)) \), where
   \( \hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} \).
   If \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \) assigns very low probability to many training completions (as can happen with a vanilla LM on out‑of‑domain text), these terms become numerically unstable and gradients may vanish or explode. A refitted model that already assigns reasonable mass to preferred strings stabilises training.

---

### Why it matters for mitigating distribution shift

The *reference shift* problem arises when the reward model has been trained on samples drawn from a policy that differs markedly from the one being optimized (see Section 3 of the paper). With no SFT, using a refitted-policy guarantees that:

- The initial sampling distribution for generating comparison pairs comes from *the same* task focus as human annotators observed.
- During fine‑tuning, \( \pi_\theta \) is encouraged only to strays that remain plausible according to this more realistic baseline.
- Resulting policies stay within the support where the learned reward (or implicit reward via DPO) is trustworthy.

Without such alignment, a vanilla LM could drift toward tokens or tokens sequences unlikely to appear in the preference data, leading to mis‑estimated rewards and degraded user experience.

---

### When might this strategy fail?

1. **Sparse Preference Labels.**  
   If few human preferences cover an input space, maximising likelihood over only preferred completions will overfit to those examples, producing an overly narrow reference model that misrepresents true variability.

2. **Highly Biased Preferences.**  
   When human annotators consistently choose very similar responses (e.g., short blunt answers), \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \) collapses to a deterministic mode and offers little room for exploration; DPO will then suffer severe mode collapse.

3. **Meaningful Diversity Required Later.**  
   Some downstream tasks benefit from diverse sampling (e.g., creative generation). Training \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \) solely on lucky outcomes removes diversity and may hamper later fine‑tuning stages.

4. **Large Drop in Tutoring Domain Shift** – If annotations are collected on prompts generated by one language model but users will interact with another (different architecture or tokenizer), even a refitted policy can still be mismatched enough to cause an unseen domain shift.

In such scenarios one might:
- Use background unlabeled prompts from the target domain and mix them into training,
- Reweight or regularise training data,
- Or revert to training an explicit root LM via supervised learning on available high‑quality demonstrations before performing DPO.

---

#### Bottom line

Replacing a missing SFT with a maximised‑likelihood policy trained only on preferred completions gives DPO a realistic baseline that keeps KL regularisation meaningful and stabilises gradient signals under real human preference distributions—a small but crucial step for preventing drift and ensuring that subsequent optimisation aligns with what humans actually want.)
