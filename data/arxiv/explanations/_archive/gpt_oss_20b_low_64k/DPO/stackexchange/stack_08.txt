### "Unlikelihood" vs. DPO: What causes unlikelihood training to degenerate when $eta=0$?
Question:
The paper mentions that removing the weight term (i.e., setting $eta=0$) leads to degeneracy as shown in Appendix Table X. Why does unlikelihood training collapse in this scenario? Is it because it maximises $
abla igl[
abla 	ext{log} π(y_w)-
abla 	ext{log} π(y_l)igr]$ without any check on whether the model already prefers $y_w$? Any intuition for why square‐error style penalties fail here?
Answer:
Unlikelihood training (also known as “lowest‑likelihood” or “negative log‑likelihood” on the dispreferred output) is a popular loss that has been applied to language‑model fine‑tuning for a long time.  In the context of preference data it takes the form  

\[
\mathcal L_{\text{UL}}(\theta)=
-\mathbf E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\Big[
\eta\,\log \pi_\theta(y_w|x)
+(1-\eta)\,\bigl(-\log \pi_\theta(y_l|x)\bigr)\Big]\,,
\]

where $\eta\in[0,1]$ is a weighting factor that controls how much emphasis we put on the preferred completion $y_w$ versus the dispreferred completion $y_l$.  If we set $\eta=0$, the loss reduces to pure *unlikelihood* of $y_l$:

\[
\mathcal L_{\text{UL}}^{\eta=0}(\theta)=
-\mathbf E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}[-\,\log \pi_\theta(y_l|x)]
=\mathbf E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}[\log \pi_\theta(y_l|x)] .
\]

The gradient of this loss is simply

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal L_{\text{UL}}^{\eta=0}
= - \mathbf E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l|x)] .
\]

Thus every update blindly pushes the probability mass away from **every** dispreferred completion in the training set, **regardless** of whether the model already assigns it a low probability.  Two closely related problems arise:

1. **“Fake” counter‑examples** – The pairwise preference data assumes that $y_w$ is preferred because *relative to* $y_l$ it scores higher on an (unknown) human reward.  When we ignore $y_w$, we do not enforce that the model should *increase* the likelihood of $y_w$.  A sample whose probability is already low could be further collapsed to zero, while a sample that was once slightly better may be flattened together with many unrelated outputs.  The model ends up learning a very flat distribution that assigns almost equal probability to everything except a few 98 % “undesired” tokens.

2. **No self‑regularisation** – In DPO and PPO, the policy update is weighted by how badly the current model misorders the pair:  
   $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))\,(\nabla \log y_w - \nabla \log y_l)$.  
   The term involving $y_w$ prevents runaway collapse: if the model already prefers $y_w>y_l$, its contribution vanishes, leaving only the gradient that pushes $y_l$ down.  When this weight is missing (the case $\eta=0$), there is no check on the ordering, so any datapoint that contains a low‑probability $y_l$ gets penalised increasingly even if it was already well ordered.

Because of these two effects, unlikelihood training with $\eta=0$ often ends up in an *entropy‑minimising trap*.  The model learns to drive all probabilities toward zero except for a handful of tokens that appear rarely in $y_l$-examples (sometimes merely due to random noise).  The result is a collapsed policy that paradoxically assigns high probability to some *unpreferred* completions and very low probability to most other tokens – exactly what Table X of the DPO paper shows as “degeneracy”.

Why do “square‑error style penalties” fail as well?  
A traditional least‑squares penalty would minimise  
$(\pi_\theta(y_w|x)-p^*)^2 + (\pi_\theta(y_l|x)-q^*)^2$ where $p^*,q^*\notin[0,1]$.  
If we set $p^*=1,q^*=0$ (our naïve encoding of preference), the gradients push both probabilities linearly toward these hard targets, ignoring any *relative* structure in the data.  As soon as any single token’s probability falls outside a narrow band around its target value, its gradient saturates and disappears, leaving everything else untouched.  In practice this again causes mode collapse or huge swings in token probabilities because no mechanism tells us when we have already achieved enough separation between two responses.

> **Bottom line** – Unlikelihood collapses when $\eta=0$ because it removes any incentive to keep or raise probabilities for the preferred outcome and removes an importance weight that protects against over‑penalising already well‑ordered pairs.  The resulting objective behaves like “flatten everything but push certain tokens down”, which quickly destroys useful probabilistic structure and produces degenerate models.

These phenomena are precisely what are observed empirically in Appendix Table X: when $\beta=0/1/\dotsc$ but with no positive weight on $y_w$, generated samples have *utterly bland*, low‑diversity sequences that bear little resemblance to either member of their training pairs.  Introducing a small but non–zero weight on $y_w$ (or using DPO’s importance weight instead of raw likelihoods) restores stability and yields well‑behaved language models that honour human preferences.
