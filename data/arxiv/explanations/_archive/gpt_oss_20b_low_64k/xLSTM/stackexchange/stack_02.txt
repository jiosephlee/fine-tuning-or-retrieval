\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### How does the stabilizer state $m_t$ in equation (6) guarantee that gradients do not explode or vanish?
Question:
Equation (6) defines $m_t$ as the maximum of $	ext{log}(f_t)+m_{t-1}$ and $	ext{log}(i_t)$, and then re‑scales $i_t$ and $f_t$ by exponentiating modified logs. I would like a step‑by‑step explanation of how this adjustment preserves the forward output but keeps each gate bounded, especially when $i_t$ or $f_t$ are close to zero or very large due to the exponential activation.
Answer:
**Short answer**

The stabilizer state  
$$
m_t \;=\;\max\!\bigl(\log f_t+m_{t-1},\,\log i_t\bigr)
$$
keeps track, at every time‑step, of the largest “log‑scale’’ that any product of gates has acquired up to that point. By subtracting this bias from the logs of the current gates before turning them back into exponentials, we rescale all gate values so that they stay in a safe numeric range. Because the rescaling factor multiplies the *inside* of the LSTM update equations, it cancels out when the cell state and the normalizer are divided, guaranteeing that the forward‑pass result (and all gradients) remains unchanged. In effect, the gates are expressed in a log‑domain where extremes can be safely handled, and then converted back to a comparable magnitude before use.

---

### 1. Why raw exponentials can explode

In an xLSTM, both $i_t$ and $f_t$ are allowed to be exponentials of affine transforms:
$$
i_t = \exp(\tilde i_t), \qquad
f_t = \exp(\tilde f_t)\ \text{or}\ \sigma(\tilde f_t).
$$
If $\tilde i_t$ or $\tilde f_t$ has a large positive value, the resulting gate may become astronomically large (e.g. $e^{50}\approx 5\times10^{21}$). In a recurrent network such a huge number multiplies previous cell states or normalizer terms; both forward activations and backward gradients can overflow or under‑flow, leading to exploding or vanishing gradients.

### 2. The stabilizer idea

A stabilizer makes it unnecessary to keep the raw values. The key observation is that for any two positive numbers $a,b>0$,
$$
a\,b = \exp(\log a + \log b).
$$
Hence we can replace every product by a sum in log‑space and postpone exponentiation until necessary. If we know an upper bound on all accumulated logarithms up to time $t-1$, we can safely shift all logarithms by that bound and re‑exponentiate without changing any product.

The stabilizer state $m_t$ is precisely that bound. At step $t-1$ we already know the largest log‑scale that has appeared:
$$
m_{t-1} = \max_{k<t} \log(f_k)\ + \dots
$$
In xLSTM Eq.~(6) they use
$$
m_t=\max(\log f_t+m_{t-1},\,\log i_t).
$$
Thus $m_t$ records the greatest log‑scale that any term in either
the *forget* or *input* path could produce at this timestamp.

### 3. Rescaling the gates

Once $m_t$ is known we can rewrite each gate as:
$$
i'_t   = \exp(\log i_t - m_t)
       = \exp(\tilde i_t - m_t),
\qquad
f'_t   = \exp(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t).
$$
Because subtraction from a log value is equivalent to division in normal space,
$i'_t = i_t/e^{m_t}$ and $f'_t = f_te^{m_{t-1}-m_t}=f_te^{-\, (m_t-m_{t-1})}$.
Both new gates are now bounded in $[0,\infty)$; in practice they stay close to 1 unless one of the original terms was extremely small.

### 4. Forward‑pass invariance

The sLSTM forward equations are:
$$
c_t   = f_tc_{t-1}+i_tz_t,\qquad
n_t   = f_nn_{t-1}+i_tk_{\!t},
\qquad
h_t   = o_tc_{t}/n_{t}.
$$

If we replace each occurrence of $i_t,f_c,\dotsc,n_{t-1}$ by its _stabilised_ version,
the equations become:
$$
c'_t  = f'_tc'_{t-1}+i'_tz_\!t , \\ 
n'_t= f'_nn'_{t-1}+ i'_tk_{\! t},
      \\ h'_t=o_tc'_t/n'_t .
$$
Because every occurrence of an input or forget gate is multiplied by exactly one
$e^{-m_{\cdot}}$, these factors cancel pairwise across the numerator and denominator:  
for example,
$c'_tc'^{-1}_{...}n'^{-1}_{...}$ all contain one copy of every shift for each step,
and multiplying them together yields precisely the original unscaled expressions.
Formally one shows by induction that
$c'_t=c_t,\ n'_t=n_t,\ h'_t=h_t.$

Thus **the forward output remains exactly unchanged**, even though every gate has been clamped to avoid extreme magnitudes.

### 5. Gradient safety

Gradients flow backwards through exactly the same algebraic expressions.
Since the forward quantities are identical to those without stabilization,
and since each stabilized gate differs from its unscaled counterpart by only a constant factor (which cancels out across products), all partial derivatives with respect to weights remain **exactly** the same as in the unscaled network.

Because no “soft” approximations are used—only an algebraic rearrangement—the vanishing/exploding behaviour caused by huge gate values is eliminated without altering learning dynamics.

### 6. Intuition

Think of writing numbers on paper: if you record very large integers with many digits you’ll eventually run out of space. Working instead with their logarithms keeps each entry small (at most a few digits). Whenever you need to recover an actual number (e.g., multiply two values), you first add their logs and then exponentiate; but if you realise that you only ever need ratios of such numbers, you can subtract a common offset from all logs (shifting them down by a fixed amount). This keeps every intermediate exponential small enough for floating‑point arithmetic while ensuring that ratios—and therefore all final outputs—are unchanged.

### 7. Broader view

This trick is conceptually similar to other **weight‑normalisation** or **layer‑norm** ideas: reparameterise parameters or activations so that they stay in numerically stable ranges while preserving expressiveness. In recurrent nets it tackles *pathological* growth/decay caused by repeated multiplications, which is precisely what leads to exploding/vanishing gradients in classic RNNs.

---

**Bottom line**

The stabiliser state $m_t=\max(\log f_t+m_{t-1},\,\log i_\! t)$ records the largest log‑scale seen so far. Subtracting this value from each log before exponentiating rescales every gate back into a safe numeric range. Because this rescaling is applied consistently across all recursive equations, it cancels out when computing outputs or loss derivatives, leaving both forward activations and training dynamics identical to those without stabilisation while preventing overflow/underflow – hence gradients neither explode nor vanish as a consequence of gigantic gates.
