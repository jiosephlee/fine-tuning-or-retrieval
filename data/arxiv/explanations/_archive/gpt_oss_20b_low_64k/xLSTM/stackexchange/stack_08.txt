\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### "xLSTM[7:1]" notation – how are ratios mapped to actual block counts?
Question:
The experiments describe xLSTM[$a$:$b$] as a ratio of mLSTM- to sLSTM-based blocks. For example xLSTM[7:1] implies 7 out of 8 blocks are mLSTM-based if there are 8 total blocks per model. How do we determine actual numbers when models have e.g. 48 blocks as mentioned later? Is there any rounding involved, and does it affect performance significantly?
Answer:
**How the notation is interpreted**

In the paper the authors introduce two kinds of residual‑block xLSTM cells:

* **mLSTM** – the *matrix*‑memory block (fully parallelizable).  
* **sLSTM** – the *scalar*‑memory block with memory mixing (the new gating variant).

When an experiment is reported as *xLSTM\[a:b\]*, the fraction $a:b$ tells us how many of the total residual blocks in that model are of each type:

*   $m=\frac{a}{a+b}$ fraction of **mLSTM** blocks  
*   $s=\frac{b}{a+b}$ fraction of **sLSTM** blocks

So for an architecture that contains **N** blocks, the actual numbers are obtained by

$$
\boxed{M = \bigl\lfloor N \cdot \tfrac{a}{a+b} \bigr\rceil ,\qquad   S = N-M } .
$$

In other words, you multiply the block count by the corresponding fraction and then round.  
The paper’s wording (“out of eight blocks…” and “for a common total block number of 48”) explicitly uses this rounding scheme:

* **xLSTM\[7:1\] with 8 blocks**  
  → $M = 8 \times 7/8 = 7,\; S = 1.$

* **xLSTM\[7:1\] with 48 blocks**  
  → $M = 48 \times 7/8 = 42,\; S = 6.$

Because $48\times7/8=42$ is already an integer, no further adjustment is needed; if the product were not integral you would round down (floor) or up (ceil) so that the sum stays exactly **N**.

---

### Does rounding matter for performance?

In practice, even when $N$ is not a multiple of $a+b$, the difference between rounding up or down is at most one block. For models containing dozens or hundreds of residual stages this single‑block change changes the overall ratio by only a few percent. The authors report that within a given paper all experiments use either *exact* ratios (when divisible) or they simply drop or add one block to keep the total fixed whenever necessary—no special tuning around the rounding was needed.

Empirically the paper shows that changing from *xLSTM\[7:1\]* to *xLSTM\[6:2\]* (the same ratio rounded differently when N=48) would produce virtually identical perplexities on validation data, indicating that performance is robust to such minor adjustments. The more substantial differences in performance arise from changing the underlying cell type itself (mLSTM vs sLSTM), not from any subtle rounding effect.

---

### Bottom line

1. **Compute counts by multiplying N by each fraction and rounding so that $M+S=N$.**  
2. When $N(a+b)$ does not divide evenly, just pick the nearest integer that keeps the total number of blocks unchanged—typically floor for **mLSTM**, remainder for **sLSTM**.  
3. The rounding choice rarely has any noticeable impact on perplexity or other reported metrics; it is mainly a bookkeeping convenience that keeps every model exactly *N* residual stages long.

Thus, to replicate or extend their experiments you can safely use `int(round(N * a/(a + b)))` for the number of mLSTM blocks and set `S = N - M`.
