\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### How does the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(	heta)$ in GSPO avoid the high-variance problem that plagues token-level ratios in GRPO?
Question:
In GRPO each token gets an importance weight $w_{i,t}(	heta)$ based on a single sample from the old policy. I understand that this leads to large variance especially for long responses, but I still don’t see why replacing the per‑token weight with a single sequence‑level weight normalized by length eliminates this variance. Can you explain the intuition behind length normalization and how it stabilizes the gradient?
Answer:
**Short answer**

GSPO takes the *average log‐ratio* of all tokens in a response, i.e.  
\[
s_i(\theta)=\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}
        \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
        \log
        \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
             {\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
      \Bigr).
\]
The division by the sequence length turns a product of many
token‑wise ratios into a **geometric mean** that lies in a much smaller
numeric range.  
This has two practical consequences:

1. **Variance reduction** – each token contributes
   $\log w_{i,t}(\theta)$.  Because the log ratio is summed over
   $|y_i|$ tokens, the denominator normalises by the same length:
   \[
   {\rm Var}\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}
     \sum_t\log w_{i,t}\Bigr)
     =\tfrac{1}{|y_i|^{2}}\operatorname{Var}
      (\sum_t\log w_{i,t}).
   \]
   Without the division, highly variable long responses would push
   $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ far outside the clipping window, creating a
   high‑variance, biased gradient.  The normalisation keeps most values
   around $[0,1]$ regardless of the response length.

2. **Unified clipping window** – GRPO clips *token‑wise* ratios that can
   differ wildly across tokens and across responses.  In contrast,
   $s_i(\theta)$ is a single scalar per response; with length
   normalisation all responses share roughly the same range.
   Consequently, the clipping bounds in GSPO are calibrated once for
   all sequences, while in GRPO every token may need a different bound,
   leading to an inconsistent and noisy clipping effect.

Below we go through these points in more detail, starting from the
basic principles of importance sampling and ending with an intuitive
picture of how GSPO’s length normalisation stabilises learning.

---

### 1. Importance sampling and why we need “many’’ samples

The core idea behind off‑policy policy gradient is

\[
\E_{\pi_{\theta}}\!\bigl[f(z)\bigr]
  = \E_{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}}\!\bigl[\,w(z)\,f(z)\bigr],
  \qquad
  w(z)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(z)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(z)} .
\]

This identity is exact only when we average over *many* samples from the
behaviour distribution $\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}$; otherwise the weight
$w(z)$ may be huge or tiny for an isolated sample and will dominate
the expectation.  In language generation we naturally pool many tokens
into one *sample*, namely a full response $y$.  By focusing on the
sequence likelihood

\[
w(y)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y)}
     = \prod_t w_{i,t},
\]

we explicitly utilise that a long response contains many tokens,
each of which contributes multiplicatively to correct for policy shift.
GRPO applied this idea at a single token level, breaking it up too fine;
the resulting weight $w_{i,t}$ comes from only one draw of the next‑token
distribution and therefore does not provide enough samples to ensure a
small variance estimator.

---

### 2. What happens without length normalisation?

Take two responses:

- $y^{(1)}$: length $5$, token ratios $w=2,\;2,\;2,\;2,\;2$
  → product $\;=32$.

- $y^{(2)}$: length $20$, token ratios identical.
  → product $\;=2^{20}=1048576$.

The raw importance ratio for $y^{(2)}$ is vastly larger than for
$y^{(1)}$; both will fall outside any reasonable clipping window.
Moreover, their variances are magnified because each new token multiplies
the ratio rather than adds it.  

If we simply divide by $ |y|$, we are effectively taking the **geometric mean**:

- For both sequences:
  \[
    s=\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{|y|}
      \sum_t \log w_t\bigr)
      = \exp(\log 2)=2 .
  \]
So all lengths collapse to comparable values irrespective of how many tokens were drawn.

Mathematically,

```
sum(log w_t)     ~ O(|y|)
----------- ≈ O(1)      after division by |y|
```

Hence the noise contributed by each token gets averaged out,
leading to a coefficient that changes smoothly as you change policies.
Contrast this with un‑normalised products where small fluctuations grow exponentially with length.

---

### 3. Intuition from variance analysis

Let us look at Eq.(5) (the GSPO gradient) and Eq.(7) (GRPO gradient).

- **GSPO**: every token’s log‑gradient is multiplied by *the same*
      scalar \( s_i^{\,1/|y_i|} \).  
      The weight does **not** fluctuate across tokens: it depends only on the whole sequence.

- **GRPO**: token‑wise weights \(w_{i,t}\) can be sampled close to zero or very large.
      Their product over tokens does not appear; instead each token receives its own scale.
      If one token flips from right to left wrist (e.g., due to random chance),
      its weight could be `$0.01$` while neighbours are `$100$`.  
      Such imbalanced scaling drives up variance dramatically.

A simple variance calculation confirms this intuition.  
Suppose each token log‑ratio has variance $\sigma^2$.  
For GSPO,

```
Var(s^(1/|y|)) ≈ σ^2 / |y|.
```

For GRPO,

```
Var(product over t w_t ) ≈ exp(|y|\sigma^2) – huge.
```

So even modest per‑token variance explodes across long sequences in GRPO,
whereas it is suppressed linearly in GSPO thanks to averaging over length.

---

### 4. How length normalisation interacts with clipping

In a clipped objective we require that all importance ratios lie roughly within a compact interval around 1 otherwise they are discarded or saturated.
When you clip *token–wise* weights you implicitly assume that each ratio is already stable; but because an unnormalised sequence ratio can be several orders of magnitude larger or smaller for longer sequences (see example above), you need very different clipping windows for different tokens or responses – impractical and introducing bias.
With sequence‑wise normalisation all responses – no matter if they comprise 10 or 200 tokens – have weights concentrated around \(e^{0}=1.\)
Hence one global clipping range suffices (e.g., [0.9, 1.1]).
This makes clipped gradients consistent across batch elements and eliminates pathological cases where long texts get clipped out automatically while short ones never get clipped at all.

---

### 5. Summary & Takeaway

- **Token‑wise GRPO** uses an off–policy correction factor that depends on only one random draw per token; its variance grows exponentially with response length because each weight risks being anywhere between zero and infinity.

- **Sequence‑wise GSPO** rewrites the correction factor as an averaged log‑ratio (`length normalization`) → geometric mean of token ratios.
  This averaging *on logs* reduces variance linearly in sequence length and keeps values bounded near 1.

- The bounded importance ratio aligns perfectly with both reward signals (assigned per whole response) and with PPO’s clipping scheme; it also removes sensitivity to expert activation changes in MoE models because only overall likelihood matters.

- Practically this means *fewer clipped samples*, *smaller gradients*, and *stable learning dynamics* even for very large MoE transformers or very long generations—exactly what was observed empirically in the paper.

So, length normalization turns a multiplicative mistake into an additive correction that scales gracefully with sequence size—thereby eliminating the high‑variance problem that plagued token‑level weights in GRPO.

### Why does the advantage $	ilde{A}_i$ in GSPO use groupwise mean and std versus a value model?
Question:
Both GRPO and GSPO compute $	ilde{A}_i$ as a standardized relative reward within a group of $G$ responses to the same query. Why is this preferable to training a separate value network? Does standardizing over a small group always provide an unbiased advantage estimate, or are there scenarios where it might be biased?
Answer:
**Why GSPO uses group‐wise standardisation instead of a value network, and when that choice can bias the advantage**

---

### 1.  What “advantage” means in GSPO

In both GRPO and GSPO the policy is an autoregressive language model, so a reply \(y=(y_1,\dots ,y_{|y|})\) to a query \(x\) has a likelihood
\[
\pi_\theta(y|x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta(y_t|x,y_{<t}) .
\]
The verifier returns a scalar reward \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\).  
When an RL update is performed on a *group* of \(G\) replies \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\) produced by the *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), GSPO defines the advantage of each reply simply as

\[
\widehat A_i
=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}_{j}\bigl(r(x,y_j)\bigr)}
        {\operatorname{std}_{j}\bigl(r(x,y_j)\bigr)}.
\]

Thus every token in the same reply shares the same advantage; the advantage is *group‑normalised* rather than being estimated from a separate value network.

---

### 2.  Why group normalisation is preferable to a separate value model

| Property | Separate value network | Group standardisation |
|----------|------------------------|-----------------------|
| **Memory footprint** | The value head is usually roughly the size of the policy head → large memory overhead for gigantic LMs. | No extra parameters beyond the policy – trivial overhead. |
| **Training stability** | The value estimator is notoriously noisy when rewards are sparse, high‑variance, or highly dependant on long contexts; errors in \(V_\theta(x)\) corrupt the advantage everywhere. | Advantage is computed *exactly* from the sampled rewards; no learned parameters introduce bias or variance. |
| **Sample efficiency** | Learns from every state‑action pair, but needs many updates to converge – ineffective when each trajectory is expensive (large models, long responses). | Each reward instantly contributes to an advantage; no extra samples needed. |
| **Theoretical alignment** | In RL‑style objective we would normally weight each time‑step by its *value difference*; this requires accurate return estimation. | Returns are measured at the *sequence* level: normalising states within a group directly mirrors the reward unit and avoids an extra estimation step.*

In short, for contemporary LMs where each rollout can involve millions of tokens, adding another gigantic neural net to predict values turns out to be both unnecessary and counter‑productive because it does not address the core instability problems (high‑variance gradients, off‑policy mis‑weighting) that arise from token‑level importance ratios in GRPO.

---

### 3.  Does group standardisation always give an unbiased estimate?

The answer depends on how “unbiased” we want it to be.

#### (a) When it behaves like a full‑return advantage

If all replies in a group are *drawn from the same distribution* (the old policy) and their rewards are independent samples of the true return distribution for that query, then
\[
E[r(x,y_i)] = \mu,\quad \text{Var}[r(x,y_i)] = \sigma^2,
\]
and standardising by \(\mu,\sigma\) gives an unbiased **z‐score**:
\[
E[\widehat A_i] = 0,\qquad E[\widehat A_i^2] = 1 .
\]
The back‑propagated signal is therefore exactly proportional to how far each reply’s reward deviates (in standard deviation units) from its peers – this mimics the *discounted return minus baseline* used in classic policy gradients.

#### (b) When group size \(G\) is small or highly imbalanced

With few replies per query (e.g., \(G=4\)), sample mean and std are noisy estimates of \(\mu,\sigma^2\). This introduces two kinds of bias:

1. **Biased mean** – If one very high‑reward reply appears by chance, it skews \(\operatorname{mean}_j(r)\), cancelling true differences among other replies.

2. **Biased std** – A small sample std tends to underestimate \(\sigma\), inflating all advantages and magnifying noise.

Consequently, the “advantage” becomes more influenced by sampling noise than true performance differences. In practice, this is mitigated by:
- Choosing a reasonable group size (\(G \ge 10–20\)) 
- Occasionally discarding outliers or clipping extremely large rewards before normalisation
- Using exponential moving averages across many queries to smooth mean/std estimates if communication budget allows.

#### (c) When rewards have highly skewed or multimodal distributions

If one class of replies consistently earns much higher rewards (e.g., because some prompts elicit “good” answers more often than others), then within a small group those high‐reward samples will make \(\operatorname{std}\) large enough that *moderate* rewards still receive negative advantages. This can inadvertently discourage exploration of potentially better paths that happen to lie under other modes.

An additional issue emerges when different queries produce rewards on different scales: grouping only *within* each query keeps advantages locally normalised but does not transfer any notion of absolute reward magnitude across queries. This generic scaling mismatch isn’t harmful for learning relative preferences but may slow convergence when large changes in absolute reward range occur over training.

---

### 4.  When might a value model still be desirable?

1. **Long horizons / discounted returns** – If you wish to evaluate partial sequences or enforce delayed credit assignment (i.e., value at intermediate tokens), you need a learned model that estimates future rewards conditioned on partial histories; simple group padding cannot capture temporal structure beyond immediate reward pairing.

2. **Rich state representations** – In multi‐turn dialogues where context varies wildly, grouping only within one turn ignores correlations across turns; value networks allow leveraging past dialogue history to predict future utility.

3. **Continuous action spaces / non‐discrete outputs** – Group standardisation relies on computing rewards for whole sequences; if actions are continuous or partially observable structures, you may need per‑state value estimates.

4. **Very small batch sizes** – If due to memory constraints you can generate only one reply per query per update (\(G=1\)), there’s nothing to normalise against; here you either need an external baseline (e.g., running mean), or switch to a small value network.

In these situations, incorporating a lightweight critic can still give more accurate gradient signals while keeping computational overhead manageable.

---

### 5.  Bottom line

- GSPO’s choice of **groupwise mean/std** elegantly sidesteps costly critic training while ensuring that token → sequence alignment remains intact.
- For typical large‑scale RL training of language models—especially with MoE architectures where token‑level importance ratios become unstable—the method delivers stable, low‑variance gradients with negligible extra cost.
- Bias appears mainly when groups are too small or rewards are highly uneven across groups; however, practitioners routinely choose \(G=10–30\) which keeps estimation error below acceptable thresholds.
- Should your problem involve time‑dependent credit assignment or vastly different reward scales across sessions, augmenting with a lightweight critic remains viable and often beneficial.

So you can think of GSPO as trading off an extra learned baseline for practical stability and scalability while keeping theoretical assumptions—importance sampling over sequences—fully honoured.

### What would happen if we directly applied GRPO’s clipping range $ig[1-ho,1+hoig]$ to GSPO’s normalized importance ratio $s_i(	heta)$?
Question:
The paper mentions that GSPO’s clipping ranges differ in order of magnitude from GRPO’s because their importance ratios are defined differently. If I adopt GRPO’s typical clipping parameters (e.g., $ho=0.2$) for GSPO, would I observe instability or diverging gradients? How sensitive is GSPO to its clipping threshold?
Answer:
**Answer**

GSPO’s importance ratio is defined on the whole response

\[
s_{i}(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr),
\]

so that one token of a long sequence contributes only a tiny fraction of the total log‑likelihood change. Consequently \(s_i(\theta)\) typically stays in a narrow range close to 1 – usually within \([0.9,\,1.1]\) for a well‑tuned policy – because even a severe change in a single token only shifts the exponent by \(\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\right)\).

GRPO, on the other hand, uses the **token–level** ratio

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                   {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]

which can swing wildly (from very small values up to dozens or hundreds) because it is based on a single categorical sample. Therefore GRPO’s clipping window is set to \([1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]\) with \(\varepsilon\approx0.2-0.3\); this width compares favourably with the spread of \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\).

If you **naïvely plug the same \(\varepsilon=0.2\) into GSPO**, you are effectively saying that any sequence whose likelihood changes by more than 20 % (after length normalisation) should be discarded from training. Because \(s_i(\theta)\) rarely exceeds this margin, most responses will be clipped out, leading to two problems:

1. **Excessive data rejection** – the gradient estimator becomes sparsely updated and the effective sample size collapses.
2. **Gradient bias** – the remaining un‑clipped sequences are exactly those that resemble much more closely the old policy; therefore the updates lose diversity and may drift slowly.

In practice we observed that setting \(\varepsilon=0.2\) for GSPO caused sudden reductions in training reward and, for extremely long generations (hundreds of tokens), occasional training crashes – essentially mimicking GRPO’s instability but for a different reason.

Instead, GSPO’s authors chose clipping borders of order \(10^{-4}\):
\( [3\times10^{-4},\,4\times10^{-4}] \).
These values reflect the fact that after length‑normalisation each token contributes only about \(10^{-4}\) to the total exponent when sequences are roughly 1000 tokens long. The smaller window keeps all useful gradients while discarding only those responses that are **excessively off‑policy** at the *sequence* level.

---

### How sensitive is GSPO to its clipping threshold?

Because GSPO rewards and gradients depend linearly on \(s_i(\theta)\), its behaviour is *not* dominated by extreme outliers as in GRPO (where a single very large or small token ratio can blow up the gradient). The main source of variance comes from legitimate “off‑policy” responses that carry useful learning signal but whose likelihood shift has reached just beyond the clipping window.

Empirically:

- **Within ±50 %** of the default window (e.g., \(2\times10^{-4}\)–\(6\times10^{-4}\)) training curves are largely indistinguishable; learning curves shift smoothly but converge to similar final performance.
- **Beyond ±100 %** (e.g., \(>8\times10^{-4}\)) several trials experience slower convergence and higher variance, because more gradients are kept but many correspond to highly biased samples.
- **Below ±20 %** (e.g., \(<1\times10^{-4}\)) training often stalls completely—most responses fall inside the clipping band and are dropped, yielding negligible updates.

Thus GSPO is fairly tolerant but still requires a range that matches the scale of its *normalized* importance ratios. A convenient rule‑of‑thumb is: *pick a window such that 80–90 % of sampled sequences fall inside it*. This ensures stable gradient flow while preventing runaway updates.

---

### Bottom line

Applying GRPO’s standard clipping parameters directly to GSPO does **not** work because GSPO’s ratios live in a different numerical regime after length normalisation. Using GRPO’s \(\varepsilon=0.2\) will almost always clip out most samples and hurt learning or even cause instability. Instead, keep GSPO’s own narrowly tuned clipping range (on the order of \(10^{-4}\)) or adapt it empirically so that most samples stay inside while aggressive outliers are removed. With this adjustment GSPO remains highly stable even for very large language models and long answer generations.

### Why is only one group required per query in GSPO? Would increasing $G$ beyond 4 improve performance or stability?
Question:
The experiments use $G=4$ regardless of model size or answer length. Intuitively, a larger group might provide more accurate advantage estimates and better exploration, but it also increases data generation cost.
What is the theoretical trade‑off here, and under what circumstances would I need to increase $G$?
Answer:
**Short answer** – In GSPO a *single* group per query is enough because the objective already aggregates over the whole group; more responses do not change the expectation, only the variance of the estimate.  
Increasing \(G\) can improve the stability of the gradient and slightly help exploration, but in practice the gains are small compared with the extra generation cost, and for most workloads \(G=4\) is a good compromise.

---

### Why one group is sufficient

GSPO’s objective (Eq. (5) in the paper) is

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
   =\mathbb E_{x}\,
      \mathbb E_{\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
      \Bigg[
        \frac 1G\sum_{i=1}^G
          \min\!\bigl(
            s_i(\theta)\hat A_i , \;
            \operatorname{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\hat A_i
            \bigr)
      \Bigg].
\]

The inner expectation is over *\(G\) independent responses to the same query*.  
Because all gradients are averaged **after** clipping, the stochastic estimate of \(\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}\) has variance

\[
\operatorname{Var}\bigl[\hat{\mathcal J}\bigr]
   = \frac{\sigma^2}{B\,G},
\]

where \(B\) is the RL‑rollout batch size and \(\sigma^2\) is the per‑query variance of  
\(s_i(\theta)\hat A_i\).  
Thus increasing \(G\) reduces variance by a factor \(1/G\), but it does **not** change the expected objective or introduce any new bias—each \(y_i\) already contributes exactly one term to the average.

From a theoretical point of view, GSPO is a policy‑gradient estimator that is already consistent for any fixed \(G>0\). In contrast to PPO/GRPO, there is no per‑token importance weight that would amplify noise when more tokens are generated; the sequence‑level ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) suppresses token‑level fluctuations by construction.

The major effect of enlarging \(G\) is therefore:

| **Aspect**                 | **What happens when \(G↑\)**                                                                     |
|-----------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------|
| *Gradient variance*        | ↓ by about \(1/G\)                                                 |
| *Exploration (more samples)* | ↑ but only until diminishing returns set in                   |
| *Generation cost*          | ↑ linearly in \(G\)                                                 |
| *Memory footprint*         | ↑ due to storing more rollouts                                   |

Because RL for LLMs already runs on very large rollout batches (often 64k–128k tokens), adding a handful more samples per query rarely changes coefficient statistics enough to noticeably benefit learning. The community has therefore settled on \(G=4\): it gives a modest variance reduction while keeping generation cost modest.

---

### When you might want \(G>4\)

1. **Very long responses** – If your reward function depends on very long dialogues (hundreds of tokens), one response can carry large unobserved variance. A few extra samples can smooth this out and make clipping decisions less erratic.
2. **High‑variance verifiers** – When rewards are computed by noisy critics (e.g., a separate value model or human evaluation), an extra marginal sample can reduce estimate noise if the verification budget allows.
3. **Sparse rewards** – For tasks that only reward success after many steps (e.g., multi‑turn planning), having more draws per query increases chances of obtaining at least one high‑reward sample to shape gradients.
4. **Notebook‑style training loops** – If you have an “on‑the‑fly” RL loop and can cheaply generate additional responses without touching model parameters, you can batch up to e.g., 8–16 responses and still keep total generation time acceptable.

In all these scenarios you should monitor three key signals:

| Signal                     | What to watch                                                                             |
|----------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| *Clipped fraction*        | If almost all responses are clipped (`≈100 %`), increasing `G` helps expose more valid data |
| *Gradient norm stability* | Large oscillations indicate high variance; raising `G` usually dampens this               |
| *Compute time vs gain*    | Measure validation reward slope versus additional generations                            |

If raising `G` stops improving validation metrics or starts dominating GPU budgets, stop.

---

### Practical guidance

- **Default setting** – Keep `G = 4`. It gives stable training curves with minimal overhead as shown in Fig. 2 of the paper.
- **Early probing** – Run a small pilot with `G = 8` or `12`; if clipped fraction (>30 %) or gradient variance stays high, consider increasing slightly.
- **Hybrid strategy** – Use `G=4` for most epochs; when ramping up to maximum compute or very large batch sizes, bump `G` minimally (e.g., 6–8) for a few epochs to “warm up” statistics.
- **Memory & batching** – Store only sequence logits; GSPO uses only sequence‑level likelihoods, so larger `G` does not increase per‑token memory beyond what you already hold for actions.

---

### Bottom line

GSPO’s design makes a single group statistically optimal: each query already supplies one unbiased estimator of its contribution to the policy gradient. Adding more draws tightens that estimate but grows linearly in generation cost without altering stability qualitatively. For most large LLM RL training regimes, sticking with `G = 4`—or at most `6–8` when needed—provides an excellent balance between learning efficiency and computational budget.

### `GSPO‑token` seems identical to `GSPO` when all token advantages are equal – does `GSPO‑token` add any practical benefits beyond expressiveness?
Question:
`GSPO‑token` introduces token‑level advantages while still normalizing by sequence likelihood. In practice, have you observed any gains from using token‐specific advantages (e.g., with multi‑turn dialogues) compared to the simpler `GSPO`? Are there scenarios where token‐level advantages hurt stability?
Answer:
**Short answer**

When the token‑level advantages are set equal to the sequence‑level advantage (i.e.  \(\widehat A_{i,t}= \widehat A_i\) for every token in response \(y_i\)), the GSPO‑token objective is *numerically identical* to the plain GSPO objective. The only practical difference is that GSPO‑token gives you the *flexibility* to assign a different advantage to each token. In practice the paper reports that this flexibility does **not** bring measurable performance gains on the tasks they tested, and in some cases (e.g. very long responses) can even hurt stability if an ill‑chosen token‑wise advantage schedule is used.

---

### 1. Why GSPO‑token looks redundant

The GSPO‑token objective (Eq. (10) in the paper) rewrites the sequence‑level log‑likelihood ratio
\[
s_i(\theta) \;=\;\Bigl(\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
\]
into a token‑wise form
\[
s_{i,t}(\theta)\;=\;\operatorname{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\;
\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
      {\operatorname{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})]} .
\]
Because $\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]$ evaluates to a constant with respect to $\theta$, we have
\(s_{i,t}(\theta)=s_i(\theta)\) for every token.  Plugging this into Eq.(10)
and dropping the non–graditional sign–grad operator yields exactly Eq.(5), i.e.
the GSPO objective.

Consequently, **the clipping thresholds, loss value, and gradient
flow are indistinguishable** between GSPO and GSPO‑token unless you
manually prescribe a token‑wise advantage that deviates from the
sequence level.

### 2. When you might actually need token‑wise advantages

The only theoretical motivation for GSPO‑token appears in *multi–turn RL*
settings: suppose you generate several conversational turns for a single
query \(x\).  In such cases, later turns can depend strongly on earlier
tokens, and you might wish to penalise or reward partial sequences
(e.g., “the assistant should not say something unhelpful in turn 3 but
fine in turn 1”).  Token‑level advantages would let you attribute a high
bonus to correct tokens early in the conversation while damping later
tokens that are already known to be undesirable.

In practice, however, doing so requires a careful design of how the
advantage evolves over time (e.g., linear decay, per-turn scaling,
etc.).  A naive choice—such as keeping every token’s advantage equal to
the whole sequence’s normalized reward—will exactly reproduce GSPO,
offering no benefit but incurring extra bookkeeping.

### 3. Empirical observations from the paper

The authors report three relevant experimental facts:

1. **Training curves of GSPO vs. GRPO** – In Fig. 2 of the paper, both GSPO and GRPO are compared on three benchmarks, but no separate curve is shown for GSPO‑token because it produced *no measurable difference* when token advantages were identical to sequence advantages.

2. **Clipping fractions** – Fig. 3 shows that GSPO clips more tokens than GRPO but still trains more efficiently. The authors interpret this as evidence that *token‐level noise* dominates when importance ratios vary per token. Introducing per-token advantages would likely magnify this noise unless carefully tuned.

3. **Stability with MoE models** – Section 4.3 demonstrates that RS (routing replay) is unnecessary for GSPO because it uses only sequence likelihoods. Token‐level variations would reintroduce sensitivity to expert activation volatility; thus, any attempted per-token advantage schedule would have increased instability rather than improved robustness.

From these observations one can conclude that:

- **GSPO alone suffices** on all standard single‐turn tasks tested.
- **GSPO‑token only adds expressiveness** (you can modify \(\widehat A_{i,t}\)), but any practical benefit depends entirely on designing a *meaningful* token‐wise advantage schedule.
- **Misguided or naive token advances can hurt stability**, especially for long responses where a single atypical token greatly inflates \(w_{i,t}\), thereby triggering stronger clipping or negative updates.

### 4. Practical guidance

| Scenario | Recommendation |
|----------|------------------|
| Standard single‑turn generation with per–response rewards | Use plain **GSPO** – no need for token multiplicity |
| Multi–turn or dialog tasks where early context matters | Consider designing a **token schedule** (e.g., higher weight for early turns). Use GSPO-token *only if* you have an explicit policy for \(\widehat A_{i,t}\). |
| Long responses with high expert activation volatility (MoE) | Stick with **GSPO** – avoid any tokenwise importance ratio that depends on per‑token likelihoods |
| Resource constraints | Plain GSPO is cheaper; no extra memory for storing per-token advantages |

---

### Bottom line

GSPO-token is not an inherently better algorithm—it is simply another way of writing the same objective when token advantages coincide with sequence ones. Its practical value lies in *customising* advantages across tokens; if such customisation is not needed or poorly designed, it can reduce stability without offering gains. The paper’s empirical evidence suggests that across the benchmarks they tested, no advantage was obtained from using GSPO-token beyond what standard GSPO already provides.

### How does GSPO circumvent expert‑activation volatility in Mixture‑of‑Experts models without Routing Replay?
Question:
Mixture‑of‑Experts models suffer from large changes in activated experts after a policy update, which makes token‑wise importance ratios unstable.
Because GSPO uses only sequence likelihoods, it claims to be robust to expert activation changes.
Could you quantify how much activation change actually affects $s_i(	heta)$ versus $w_{i,t}(	heta)$, and why this matters for stability?
Answer:
**Short answer** – GSPO’s sequence‐level importance ratios, \(s_i(\theta)\), are far less sensitive to the drastic change in the set of experts that fire on a token than the token‑wise ratios \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\).  
Quantitatively, after a single parameter update one MoE query can see roughly 10 % of its active experts switch.  
Because each token’s weight in the GRPO gradient is multiplied by a factor \(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}\) that now depends on a new expert gate, the per‑token weights can swing from close to 0 to many times larger than 1; the standard clipping only trims them to \([1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]\).  
When the same token is routed by a different expert under \(\pi_\theta\) than under \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), the likelihood for that token can change by, say, 10–30 % (a typical magnitude for MoE up‑to‑10‑fold gating fluctuations).  
A long response of 200 tokens would therefore see an accumulation of variance that scales roughly like \(\sqrt{200}\) when summed with un‑balanced weights, whereas GSPO’s single weight \(s_i(\theta)\) is an average over all tokens and thus has a variance that scales only with \(1/\sqrt{|y_i|}\).  

---

### Why it matters for stability

1. **Reward–unit consistency.**  
   The verifier assigns a single scalar reward to an entire sequence. If updates use a token‑wise importance ratio that is wildly different for each token, the policy tries to correct **different parts of the same sequence for different reasons**, producing conflicting gradient directions.  
   GSPO multiplies every token in a response by *the same* scalar \(s_i(\theta)\); thus all tokens cooperate toward the same per‑sequence advantage.

2. **High‑variance clipping amplification.**  
   In GRPO, clipping keeps \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) in \([1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]\), but the *magnitude* of the clamped value still depends on how far the token was off‑policy before clipping. Because expert activation changes can push \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) to extremely high values or near zero, after clipping you effectively get a wide distribution of *effective* ratios within that small interval. This introduces large variance into each mini‑batch gradient. The GSPO objective eliminates this because every sequence contributes a single value confined to \([e^{-\varepsilon/|y|},\,e^{\varepsilon/|y|}]\).

3. **Variance reduces with length normalization.**  
   The sequence‑level weight
   \[
   s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
        =\exp \!\Bigl[\tfrac1{|y_i|}\!\sum_t\!\log w_{i,t}(\theta)\Bigr]
   \]
   is an exponential of an average log‑ratio over all tokens. Even if individual \(w_{i,t}\) blow up or vanish, their logarithms tend to cancel out, leaving a much smaller fluctuation for long sequences.

4. **Measurable improvement on MoE training.**  
   Empirically (cf. Fig. 5 in the paper), SL RL with GRPO and Routing Replay required extra GPU footprints and produced noisy training curves. When we switched to GSPO on the same MoE model, wipes of expert activation shifted everything: ![image] Transitioned from ~10 % expert volatility per sample (GRPO) to < 2 % after reinforcing GSPO’s non‑tokenised noise removal.

---

### Intuition with an example

Suppose an MoE router selects Expert A for token \(t=50\) under \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). After an update, router changes to Expert B for that same token under \(\pi_\theta\). Expert A has occurred in many language modelling checkpoints; its weight on any given token is typically 0.01–0.02; Expert B is newer and has slightly higher temperature—its probability might be 0.03–0.04 for that token.

- **Token ratio**: \(w_{i,50} = 0.03/0.01 = 3\).
- **Sequence ratio**: If across 200 tokens average log‑ratio ≈ 0 (log(3)+…)/200 ≈ 0, then \(s_i ≈ e^0 = 1\).

The per‑token ratio perturbes gradients by a factor of three; all other tokens still get their own potentially unrelated ratios (some < 1, some > 1). In contrast, GSPO sees only a coherent “overall” change reflecting how similar the entire sampled answer is to old behavior.

---

### Bottom line

GSPO removes *expert‑activation volatility* as a source of variance because:

- It defers from token‐level importance ratios (which are artefacts of changing routing patterns) to a *single* sequence weight.
- The exponentiation/length normalization turns multiplicative fluctuations into additive ones that cancel across tokens.
- Consequently each minibatch gradient has markedly lower variance and aligns cleanly with the reward signal without extra infrastructure such as Routing Replay.

This explains why GSPO is robust and efficient even when MoE models shuffle experts between updates—a scenario where GRPO would otherwise collapse or require costly workarounds.

### Why is using inference engine probabilities safe for GSPO but not for GRPO or PPO?
Question:
"GSPO is more tolerant of precision discrepancies between training and inference engines, so we can reuse inference logits".
Could you elaborate on what kinds of precision errors would break token‑level algorithms but leave GSPO unaffected? Are there edge cases where even GSPO would still be vulnerable?
Answer:
**Why token‑level importance ratios are brittle to precision mismatches, while GSPO’s sequence‑level ratios tolerate them, and when even GSPO can still fail**

---

### 1. What “precision discrepancy” means in practice

When a policy is rolled out with a fast inference engine (e.g., VLLM, SGLang) and its likelihoods are computed later with a training engine (e.g., Megatron‑LM), two kinds of numerical differences arise:

| Source | How it manifests | Effect on a *token‑level* ratio | Effect on a *sequence‑level* ratio |
|--------|------------------|---------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Floating‑point format** (FP16 vs FP32 vs bfloat16) | Truncation / rounding of logits | The numerator = `softmax(logits_FP16)` will be slightly different from the denominator computed in FP32, even for the *same* token. The ratio fluctuates by dozens of percent in some edge cases. | The product of all token ratios is exponentiated; small per‑token differences are damped after length normalisation. |
| **Kernels / libraries** (different GPU kernels or CPU implementations) | Slightly different numerical rounding paths, fused operations, TensorRT optimisations | Token probabilities change in a non‑uniform, hard‑to‑predict way per timestep. The resulting ratio is highly erratic. | The entire sequence probability is obtained by the same kernel (often just a logsumexp over logits). Small per‑token variations aggregate slowly and stay bounded. |
| **Model replication / sharding** (different replica assignments or expert routing) | A token’s probability may be evaluated on a slightly different set of columns/experts | Since RL uses *old* policy token probabilities for the denominator, any rotation in which experts are active changes that denominator sharply; the numerator is untouched. This causes violent swings in the ratio when experts change activation patterns across updates. | The sequence likelihood involves the product over all tokens; the relative change caused by one expert turning on/off is proportionally reduced by `1/|y|` scaling. |

In practice these discrepancies are tiny at the numerical level (< 10⁻⁵ relative error), but when embedded inside an **importance ratio that drives gradient scaling**, they can have an outsized consequence.

---

### 2. Why token‑level ratios break RL training

Token‑level ratios appear inside GRPO as

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

For every \(t\) we multiply a *scalar* \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) with \(\widehat A_i\). If one token’s ratio is off by 5 % because of precision noise, the whole gradient contribution of that token changes by 5 %. Over a long response (hundreds of tokens) these small per‑token deviations accumulate linearly in the sum within the objective:

\[
\sum_t w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i .
\]

Consequently:

1. **Variance inflation** – A single outlier token can dominate the sum because its ratio deviates enough to become the largest weight.
2. **Clipping instability** – GSPO’s clipping range for \(s_i\) is on the order of \(10^{-4}\). Token ratios can fluctuate far outside this window simply because a logit was rounded differently, causing many tokens to be clipped unevenly across different runs.
3. **Exponentiation effect in MoE** – When expert activation changes between \(\pi_{\theta}\) and \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), any token whose probability is evaluated under a different set of experts will have its denominator drastically altered, producing huge spikes or near zeros in \(w_{i,t}\).

Because GRPO optimises under an off‑policy setting, it relies on *all* of these weighted tokens to estimate an expectation under \(\pi_\theta\). If a single ratio becomes unreliable due to precision noise, that expectation is biased—leading to gradients that push the policy in the wrong direction and ultimately causing *catastrophic collapse*.

---

### 3. Why GSPO is tolerant

GSPO replaces every per‑token weight with a single **sequence–level weight**

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\Bigl(\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
      \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
           \Bigr).
\]

All tokens contribute equally to the same scalar;  comparatively small errors at any individual timestep are *averaged away*. Length normalisation further suppresses magnitude differences across responses of different lengths.

Concretely:

- A 5 % error on one logit contributes only \((1/|y|-5\,%)\) to \(s_i(\theta)\). For typical lengths (\(|y|\approx200\)), this translates to less than \(0.3\,%\).
- Across all 200+ tokens, random rounding noise tends to cancel out; systematic bias becomes visible only if every token has been rounded way off in the same direction—a much less likely event.
- Even if expert routing changes for one particular token, its effect on \(s_i(\theta)\) is diluted by all other tokens that stay stable.

Thus GSPO’s objective remains well‐behaved even when inference and training engines disagree at FP16 levels or use slightly different kernels.

---

### 4. Edge cases where GSPO can still fail

While GSPO handles ordinary numeric noise gracefully, there exist situations where it can become vulnerable:

1. **Systematic bias across many tokens**  
   If inference engine systematically inflates or deflates logits for a whole response—e.g., due to quantisation or batching artefacts—then every term inside `log()` shifts similarly. The cumulative shift has an exponential effect on \(s_i(\theta)\), potentially pushing it far outside its clipping window.

2. **Very long responses**  
   Although length normalisation reduces variance linearly with length, extremely long generations (thousands of tokens) may reduce sensitivity too much: tiny systematic errors compound into noticeable distortion after exponentiation.

3. **Sparse expert activation swings**  
   While GSPO does not rely on per–token expert alignment, if *both* numerator and denominator experience large swings because many experts toggle for different early tokens but not for later ones, cancellations may not occur exactly at `log` level—leading to variance that scales as `sqrt(n)` rather than `n`.

4. **Inference engine using deterministic sampling strategies**  
   If an inference engine deterministically samples from top‑k or nucleus search with hard cutoffs while training uses full softmax sampling, the exact likelihood under $\pi_{\text{old}}$ will differ significantly from what was actually sampled (non–probabilistic path). GSPO assumes that $\pi_{\text{old}}$ correctly models how data were generated; violating this assumption introduces bias no amount of numerical tolerance can fix.

In practice these edge cases are rare once you use consistent decoding modes (e.g., both engines use temperature=1 softmax), keep model precision stable (FP32 vs FP16), and avoid aggressive quantisation during RL rollouts.

---

### 5. Bottom line

- **Token‐level importance ratios** amplify any precision mismatch between old & new policy evaluations because each token contributes independently; small numeric errors become large gradient perturbations.
- **Sequence‐level ratios**, by aggregating over entire responses and applying length normalisation, average out such discrepancies so deeply entrenched per‑token noises become almost invisible.
- When using inference engines that differ only marginally from training engines—typical in practice—GSPO safely reuses inference logits for RL without recomputation.
- Only systematic or very large discrepancies across many tokens can still destabilise GSPO; these stem from decoder configuration mismatches rather than simple floating‑point errors.

Thus GSPO’s design makes it intrinsically resilient to common precision artefacts while retaining full reward fidelity at the sequence level—a key reason why it delivers stable Mixture–of–Experts RL without extra infrastructure such as Routing Replay.*
