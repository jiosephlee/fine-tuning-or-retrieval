Chapter 10: ## Chapter 10 Experimental Evaluation

The experimental section of this work is devoted to illustrating how **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** remedies the instability issues that plague contemporary RL‑based fine‑tuning of large language models, and how it brings practical benefits for Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) training and infrastructure design.  All experiments reported below are run on the same **cold‑start baseline**: the Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base model, which is a 30 billion‑parameter dense transformer that has been further pre‑trained on the A3B dictionary and already demonstrates competitive zero‑shot performance on many generation tasks.  We therefore conduct a *policy update* from this checkpoint by applying RL over a large set of carefully curated prompts (the query set \(\mathcal{D}\)), following the procedure described in Section 4 of the main paper.

> **Experimental Framework**  
>  - **Hardware**: 128 A6000 GPUs, each providing 48 GB memory; data pipeline distributed on two CPU nodes for prompt streaming.  
>  - **Software**: Megatron‑LMI v2.4 for training, SGLang v0.9 for inference; all parameters identical except for the algorithm and clipping hyper‑parameters.  
>  - **Token Budget**: Each RL epoch consists of \(1\times10^{6}\) generated tokens, grouped into batches of size \(1\,024\) sequences and then subdivided into four mini‑batches for gradient accumulation.

Below we reconstruct the pipeline, analyse training curves, benchmark performance metrics, depict our MoE experiments, and discuss practical gains in RL infrastructure.

---

### 10.1 Cold‑Start Fine‑Tuning Pipeline

The process begins with the **cold‑start baseline** (`Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base`) which is held fixed during initial data collection. From the pre‑trained checkpoint we generate a *rollout dataset* by sampling responses to each query in \(\mathcal{D}\) using greedy decoding with temperature \(1.0\). This yields a pool of \(G=8\) responses per query to form a conditional *group* as required by both GRPO and GSPO.

The rollout dataset \(\mathcal{R}\) comprises pairs \((x,y)\), with \(x\in\mathcal{D}\) and \(y\in\{y_{1},\dots,y_{G}\}\). To fit within memory constraints we store only the generated logits for each token of each response (so that we can recompute likelihoods later under updated policies without re‐generation), along with their verifier reward \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\).

During RL training we perform:

1. **Policy Update**: For each mini‐batch we compute sequence‐level importance ratios \(s_i(\theta)\) (GSPO) or token‐level ratios \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) (GRPO), clip them according to section 10.2, compute normalized advantages \(\widehat{A}_i\), and perform a single Adam step with learning rate \(2\times10^{-5}\).
2. **Evaluation Loop**: Every $500$ steps we evaluate two sets:
   - **Sampled Generation Metrics**: Pass@1 on AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces Elo after $8$ random samples per query.
   - **Reward Signal Monitoring**: Expected terminal reward over the batch.

This pipeline repeats for 200 epochs or until convergence is reached.

### 10.2 Clipping Range Settings for GSPO vs GRPO

Clipping is central to *proximal* policy optimization; it bounds how far new policies may stray from their old counterparts so as to keep estimation variance low. Because GSPO uses a *sequence‐level* importance ratio \(s_i(\theta)\), its numeric range is very different from GRPO’s token‐level ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\). Table 10.1 summarises the ranges chosen for our experiments.

| Algorithm | Operation | Ratio | Left Clip | Right Clip | Notes |
|-----------|-----------|-------|-----------|------------|-------|
| GRPO | Token | \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) | 0.80 | 1.07 | Tuned manually; retains moderate clipping |
| GSPO | Sequence | \(s_i(\theta)\)   | \(3\times10^{-4}\) | \(4\times10^{-4}\) | Very tight due to length scaling |

These settings were selected after a coarse hyper‑parameter sweep up to $0.5$ epochs; GSPO’s ratios shrink almost linearly with sequence length unless length normalized (exponent \(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\)). As noted in Section 5 of the main paper, widening or tightening these ranges hardly affected overall training curve shape but shifted the fraction of clipped responses – see §10.4.

#### Observations

### 10.4 Performance Metric Curves Across Benchmarks

The benefit of stable reward signals translates into *objective performance* measured over mainstream benchmarks (see main Table 5).

#### AIME’24 Pass@1

Figure 10.3 plots Pass@1 against cumulative compute (GPU-days). GSPO achieves an average Pass@1 of $82\,\%$ after $80$ GPU-days versus $70\,\%$ for GRPO when running under identical budget conditions.

#### LiveCodeBench Pass@1

LiveCodeBench evaluates code generation correctness via compilation tests and runtime evaluations on ten open problems per prompt. GSPO consistently surpasses GRPO by ~12 pp across all checkpoints; see Figure 10.4 illustrating rapid gains particularly between epochs 60–120 where GRPO stalls due to an abundance of irrelevant code patterns discovered early in training.

#### CodeForces Elo Rating

This Elo metric aggregates success rates across more than thirty million difficulty levels from CodeForces contests, converted into relative skill scores by policy ranking against standard LLM baselines.\(^*\)

GSPO’s performance climbs steadily from Elo 950 at baseline to Elo 1450 after full training; meanwhile GRPO plateaus at Elo 1150 before briefly dipping below baseline due to misaligned gradient updates.

> \(^*) The Elo conversion uses pairwise win rates against MiniLM-J152-v2 and GPT-NeoX-20B baselines across five difficulty tiers.\)

These curves illustrate not just stability but also *superior sample efficiency*: e.g., GSPO attains half the Pass@1 achieved by GRPO using only $30\,\%$ more communication overhead per epoch because it avoids recomputing token likelihoods frequently.

### 10.5 MoE Training with/without Routing Replay

Mixture-of-Experts backbones introduce sparsity that can cause highly variable expert activation patterns when moving between successive policies – a phenomenon dubbed *expert volatility*. Section 6 showed how Routing Replay restores stability by forcing new policies to partner with old routing decisions during importance weight computation.

#### Experimental Setup

| Technique | Routing Replay? |
|-----------|----------------|
| GRPO baseline | yes |
| GSPO baseline | no |

All other factors identical: same dataset size (128k prompts), batch size, learning rate etc.; MoE depth fixed at 48 layers; weight compression ratio set to $16\times$ as in production models.

#### Results

Figure 10.5 juxtaposes clipped fraction statistics while measuring accuracy over time:

![Figure 10.5 – Clipped Fractions & Accuracy](placeholder)

Key takeaways:

- With Routing Replay, GPT‑style MoE absl remains stable (*clipped fraction ≈20%*) but demands extra memory (~6 GB per worker) due to duplicated expert caching.
- Without Routing Replay – i.e., via GSPO – clipped fraction jumps initially (~45%) but converges quickly; importantly *accuracy plateaus* no later than epoch 80 while remaining within acceptable variances.
- The computational advantage is substantial: RP adds ~15% extra GPU time per epoch purely for bookkeeping; GSPO eliminates it as it never needs legacy routing tables during evaluation density steps.

#### Analysis

Under Grpo’s token–wise weighting scheme, expert outages produce disjoint changes in authorship probabilities across tokens making importance ratio estimates useless—we saw empirically unacceptable variances early when entire sequences involved different experts than during rollout generating expectations ($Δ(e)=0∼12 %$). GSPO’s sequence rating sidesteps this because it aggregates probabilities across tokens—expert shifts cancel out when normalising by sequence length—as detailed in Section 5’s gradient analysis (§5→§7).

### 10.6 Simplification of RL Infrastructure

RL pipelines traditionally suffer from precision mismatches between training engines (`Megatron`) – which perform microoperation calculations in BF16 precision – and inference engines (`SGLang`, `vLLM`) – which use larger grid tensors or INT8 quantised kernels for speed dissemination.\(^*\)

Because GSOP uses only sequence-level likelihoods (\(s_i(\theta)=\exp[\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \log w_{i,t}]\)), deviations due to half precision rounding are amortised over sequence tokens—the error scales multiplicatively but shrinks relative to token-level errors which compound linearly.\(^†\)

Experimental verification shows that reusing inference engine logit probabilities directly reduces GPU kernel stalls by **24%**, eliminating necessity of an intermediate “recompute under old policy” step entirely—an advantage invisible in GRP where tokenwise probability mismatches drive huge divergence because any single missable value corrupts entire gradient term.

In multi-turn interaction setups this manifests as far lower latency per round (~85ms instead ~128ms). Figure 10.sim demonstrates this latency reduction when switching RL inference from `Megatron` recompute mode to `SGLang` directly under GSOP pipeline—highlighting real world gain for production deployments.

> **Practical Takeaway:** For any MoE or dense large LLM requiring RL fine-tuning at scale (>20B parameters), employing GSOP removes two major engineering bottlenecks—routing replay overhead and costly likelihood realignment—while strengthening model robustness against catastrophic collapse observed under GROP.

### Concluding Remarks on Experiments

Across every monitored dimension—reward stability, speed-to-release metrics on AIME'24/LiveCodeBench/Codeforces Elo grids, MoE<|reserved_200686|>ent stability sans routing replay, and runtime acceleration—we observed unambiguous evidence that Group Sequence Policy Optimization provides a more faithful learning signal aligned with global rewards than its token-level counterpart GrpO. The experimental design faithfully adheres to institutional controls laid out in Chapter 8 so that differences are attributable solely to algorithmic choice rather than downstream engineering hacks.
