# Why Token‑Level Pitfalls Hurt Big‑Llama: The GSPO Solution Explained

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being fine‑tuned with reinforcement learning (RL) to align them with human preferences, solve hard reasoning tasks, or build new foundation models such as the Qwen series.  In practice, however, RL training on gigantic models is notoriously fragile.  A single crack in the off‑policy correction can lead to a system‑wide collapse that is impossible to recover from.  The source of the problem turns out to be surprisingly simple: **token‑level importance ratios**.

In this post we unpack why those per‑token corrections go wrong, how they wreak havoc on training dynamics, and how the newly proposed *Group Sequence Policy Optimization* (GSPO) fixes the issue by moving the importance ratio from token to sequence.  If you’ve run into broken checkpoints or perplexing stability bugs while training large LLMs, read on – GSPO may be exactly what you need.

---

## 1. Quick Primer: RL for Language Models

Before diving into the math, let’s recall the setting we’re operating in:

| Item | Symbol | What it means |
|------|--------|---------------|
| Query | \(x\) | An instruction or prompt |
| Response | \(y = y_1,y_2,\dots ,y_{|y|}\) | Tokens generated by our model |
| Policy | \(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\) | Autoregressive language model parameterized by \(\theta\) |
| Reward | \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) | Score given by a verifier (e.g., a human or a learned reward model) |

The goal of policy optimisation is to maximise the expected reward over all queries and responses:
\[
\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\:y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}[\,r(x,y)\,].
\]
Because we cannot sample from the *current* policy \(\pi_\theta\) after each update (we only have samples from the *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\)), RL algorithms use *importance sampling* to correct for this distribution shift.

---

## 2. How PPO and GRPO Handle Off‑Policy Corrections

### PPO – Token‑wise Clipping

The most widely used RL technique in LLMs is **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**. PPO starts from a set of responses \(\{y_t^{(i)}\}\) sampled with an old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). For each token \(y_t^{(i)}\), PPO calculates an importance ratio

\[
w_t^{(i)} = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}, y_{<t}^{(i)})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}, y_{<t}^{(i)})},
\]

and uses it to weight the gradient:
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{PPO}} \;\propto\; w_t\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t).
\]
PPO clips these ratios into a small band around one (\([1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]\)) so that updates remain conservative.

### GRPO – Group Relative Importance

GRPO was designed specifically for RL over huge models where maintaining a separate value network is costly. It sidesteps value estimates by forming a *group* of \(G\) responses for each query and using their rewards to compute **group advantages**:

\[
\hat A_i = \frac{ r(x,y_i)-\mu_r}{\sigma_r}, \qquad
w_{i,t} = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{<t})}.
\]

Every token in a response carries the same advantage \(\hat A_i\), but individual tokens still receive an individual importance ratio \(w_{i,t}\).

---

## 3. The Core Mistake in Token‑Level Ratios

Why are token‑level importance ratios problematic?

### 3.1 Misapplied Importance Sampling

**Importance sampling** says that if we want to estimate an expectation under a target distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\), we can re‑weight samples drawn from a behavior distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\):

> \[
> \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)]
> = 
> \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[\, 
>   \frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}
>   f(z)
>   \right].
> \]

The key here is that **the weight must be computed on *samples drawn from multiple draws** of \(z~{\tiny{\rm bii}}\)**. In language generation, tokens are sampled singularly: at time step \(t-1\), we already fixed everything up to that point and sample just one next token from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x,y_{<t})\).

Therefore, using the ratio
\(w_{t} = {\displaystyle {\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t)}}}\)
as an importance correction for only *that token* is mathematically *without filaments*: it does not correct any expectation because no averaging over many draws happens.

### 3.2 High Variance and Accumulation

Because each token shares a single ratio value derived from one draw, its variance can be huge – especially when model size grows and outputs become longer:

- Each ratio fluctuates wildly.
- The policy updates multiply these ratios across time steps.
- The product of many high‑variance terms shakes out into extreme values.

This variance gets multiplied further when we clip around a narrow band (\([1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon]\)). Tokens whose true likelihood under the current policy is much different than under the old policy get aggressively clipped; those whose weights fall into the clipping band get scaled linearly. In effect, we lose most of our gradients for long sequences because almost every token falls outside its narrow band.

The combined effect? **Gradients become very noisy** and unreliable, especially for long responses typical of complex reasoning tasks. Over many update steps this noise accumulates into catastrophic model collapse: the learned policy diverges so rapidly that no amount of checkpointing or hyper‑parameter tweaking brings it back.

---

## 4. Why Does This Hurt Big LLaMa?

Large MoE LLaMA variants like Big LaLLa possess two important traits that amplify this problem:

1. **Long-Response Generation** – Scientific proofs or code generation routinely exceed several hundred tokens.
2. **MoE Sparsity** – At each tokenize step only a small subset of experts participate; tiny changes in activation patterns drastically alter local likelihoods.

Both factors increase per‑token variance:

- Longer sequences mean more tokens contribute uncorrected noisy gradients.
- Expert activation volatility makes token-level ratios even less stable because switching experts flips local probabilities drastically.

Trying to mitigate this with tricks like *Routing Replay* (caching old expert activations and reusing them during importance ratio computation) adds overhead and complexity but only nudges stability forward—still not enough for safe scaling.

## 6. Why GSPO Stays In Control

### • Reduced Variance  

By collapsing many noisy per-token ratios into one aggregated value per response, variance shrinks dramatically: you’re no longer multiplying hundreds of uncertain scalars but aggregating them via exponentiation followed by root extraction.



### • Clipping Becomes Meaningful  

Clipping at sequence level ensures we temper sequences that deviate too much from what was historically seen while preserving gradients for “reasonable” sequences — regardless of length.



### • Merges Stable Training & MoE Complexity  

Since GSPO never relies on per-token expert activations (they’re hidden inside sequence likelihood), MoE activation flips no longer hit our importance weights directly — no special techniques like Routing Replay are needed.

## 8️⃣ Real Gains 🚀

In practice, researchers applying GSPO observed:

- **Stable training curves** even on tens‑of‑billions parameter MoE models.
- **Higher compute efficiency** – Fewer responses needed to reach a target performance compared to GRPO.
- A *radical simplification* of infrastructure: no need for Routing Replay or complex expert handling strategies.
- Even better final performance on benchmarks like AIME’24 and CodeForces Elo Rating when applied after large‐scale RL runs.


---


## 🎯 Bottom Line

Token‐level importance ratios sound innocent enough—but they mask a deep mathematical flaw when used on autoregressive language models: you’re trying to perform importance sampling with only one draw per time step! The consequence is gargantuan variance that blows up over long sequences and even worse for sparse MoE activations.

Group Sequence Policy Optimization turns this script upside down: by defining an importance ratio at **sequence level**, clipping entire responses rather than individual tokens, and aligning advantages exactly like rewards do so, GSPO eliminates high variance noise while keeping all desired RL properties intact.

If you’re training big LLMs and have seen “model collapse” during RL or outrageous instability when scaling up MoEs, give GSPO a try – it might just be your ticket out of these pitfalls.
