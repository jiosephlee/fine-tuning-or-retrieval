# Why Token‑Level Pitfalls Hurt Big‑Llama: The GSPO Solution Explained

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being fine‑tuned with reinforcement learning (RL) to align them with human preferences, solve hard reasoning tasks, or build new foundation models such as the Qwen series.  In practice, however, RL training on gigantic models is notoriously fragile.  A single crack in the off‑policy correction can lead to a system‑wide collapse that is impossible to recover from.  The source of the problem turns out to be surprisingly simple: **token‑level importance ratios**.

In this post we unpack why those per‑token corrections go wrong, how they wreak havoc on training dynamics, and how the newly proposed *Group Sequence Policy Optimization* (GSPO) fixes the issue by moving the importance ratio from token to sequence.  If you’ve run into broken checkpoints or perplexing stability bugs while training large LLMs, read on – GSPO may be exactly what you need.

---

## 1. Quick Primer: RL for Language Models

Before diving into the math, let’s recall the setting we’re operating in:

| Item | Symbol | What it means |
|------|--------|---------------|
| Query | \(x\) | An instruction or prompt |
| Response | \(y = y_1,y_2,\dots ,y_{|y|}\) | Tokens generated by our model |
| Policy | \(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\) | Autoregressive language model parameterized by \(\theta\) |
| Reward | \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) | Score given by a verifier (e.g., a human or a learned reward model) |

The goal of policy optimisation is to maximise the expected reward over all queries and responses:
\[
\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\:y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}[\,r(x,y)\,].
\]
Because we cannot sample from the *current* policy \(\pi_\theta\) after each update (we only have samples from the *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\)), RL algorithms use *importance sampling* to correct for this distribution shift.

---

## 2. How PPO and GRPO Handle Off‑Policy Corrections

### PPO – Token‑wise Clipping

The most widely used RL technique in LLMs is **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**. PPO starts from a set of responses \(\{y_t^{(i)}\}\) sampled with an old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). For each token \(y_t^{(i)}\), PPO calculates an importance ratio

\[
w_t^{(i)} = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}, y_{<t}^{(i)})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}, y_{<t}^{(i)})},
\]

and uses it to weight the gradient:
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{PPO}} \;\propto\; w_t\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t).
\]
PPO clips these ratios into a small band around one (\([1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]\)) so that updates remain conservative.

### GRPO – Group Relative Importance

GRPO was designed specifically for RL over huge models where maintaining a separate value network is costly. It sidesteps value estimates by forming a *group* of \(G\) responses for each query and using their rewards to compute **group advantages**:

\[
\hat A_i = \frac{ r(x,y_i)-\mu_r}{\sigma_r}, \qquad
w_{i,t} = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{<t})}.
\]

Every token in a response carries the same advantage \(\hat A_i\), but individual tokens still receive an individual importance ratio \(w_{i,t}\).

---

## 3. The Core Mistake in Token‑Level Ratios

Why are token‑level importance ratios problematic?

### 3.1 Misapplied Importance Sampling

**Importance sampling** says that if we want to estimate an expectation under a target distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\), we can re‑weight samples drawn from a behavior distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\):

> \[
> \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)]
> = 
> \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[\, 
>   \frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}
>   f(z)
>   \right].
> \]

The key here is that **the weight must be computed on *samples drawn from multiple draws** of \(z~{\tiny{\rm bii}}\)**. In language generation, tokens are sampled singularly: at time step \(t-1\), we already fixed everything up to that point and sample just one next token from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x,y_{<t})\).

Therefore, using the ratio
\(w_{t} = {\displaystyle {\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t)}}}\)
as an importance correction for only *that token* is mathematically *without filaments*: it does not correct any expectation because no averaging over many draws happens.

### 3.2 High Variance and Accumulation

Because each token shares a single ratio value derived from one draw, its variance can be huge – especially when model size grows and outputs become longer:

- Each ratio fluctuates wildly.
- The policy updates multiply these ratios across time steps.
- The product of many high‑variance terms shakes out into extreme values.

This variance gets multiplied further when we clip around a narrow band (\([1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon]\)). Tokens whose true likelihood under the current policy is much different than under the old policy get aggressively clipped; those whose weights fall into the clipping band get scaled linearly. In effect, we lose most of our gradients for long sequences because almost every token falls outside its narrow band.

The combined effect? **Gradients become very noisy** and unreliable, especially for long responses typical of complex reasoning tasks. Over many update steps this noise accumulates into catastrophic model collapse: the learned policy diverges so rapidly that no amount of checkpointing or hyper‑parameter tweaking brings it back.

---

## 4. Why Does This Hurt Big LLaMa?

Large MoE LLaMA variants like Big LaLLa possess two important traits that amplify this problem:

1. **Long-Response Generation** – Scientific proofs or code generation routinely exceed several hundred tokens.
2. **MoE Sparsity** – At each tokenize step only a small subset of experts participate; tiny changes in activation patterns drastically alter local likelihoods.

Both factors increase per‑token variance:

- Longer sequences mean more tokens contribute uncorrected noisy gradients.
- Expert activation volatility makes token-level ratios even less stable because switching experts flips local probabilities drastically.

Trying to mitigate this with tricks like *Routing Replay* (caching old expert activations and reusing them during importance ratio computation) adds overhead and complexity but only nudges stability forward—still not enough for safe scaling.

## 🎯 Bottom Line

Token‐level importance ratios sound innocent enough—but they mask a deep mathematical flaw when used on autoregressive language models: you’re trying to perform importance sampling with only one draw per time step! # From Tokens to Sequences: How GSPO Makes Reward Alignment Natural  

Reinforcement‑learning (RL) rewards for language models are always **sequence‑level**: a verifier looks at an entire chat or code answer, scores it between 0 and 1, and that scalar is fed back into the policy.  
But most popular algorithms (PPO, GRPO, etc.) act on individual tokens – they clip, weight, and back‑propagate token‑level gradients.  The mismatch between a token‑wise optimization signal and a whole‑sequence reward is the root of the instability problems that have plagued large language‑model RL training for months.  Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) resolves this by treating the *entire reply* as the unit of learning.

Below we walk through the key ideas of GSPO – sequence‑level clipping, length‑normalised importance ratios, and group‑based advantage estimation – and show why this single adjustment unlocks smooth scaling to longer generations and megabyte‑scale models.

---

## Why Token‑wise clipping hurts

### The classic GPPO / GRPO objective  

Both PPO and its token‑level variant GRPO use an *importance weight*  
\[
w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})},
\]
to correct for off‑policy sampling (the rollout was done with an old policy).  
The clipped surrogate loss looks like

\[
\min\bigl[w_t(\theta)\widehat A_t,\;
   \text{clip}\bigl(w_t(\theta), 1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat A_t\bigr],
\]
with \(\widehat A_t\) typically derived from a value function.  

In GRPO, the token advantage is re‑used across a **group** of \(G\) responses to a single query:
\[
\widehat{A}_{i,t}=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\overline r}{\sigma_r},
\qquad i=1,\dots,G .
\]

### What breaks when sequences get long  

* **High variance** – each \(w_t(\theta)\) is based on a *single* next‑token sample.  Over longer sentences there are many such weights; tiny mistakes multiply and explode the gradient.  
* **Clipping resonance** – the clip range \((1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)\) is calibrated for token ratios close to 1. When sequences grow, even modest changes in any token lead to outliers that get clipped repeatedly, draining useful gradient signal.
* **Unit mismatch** – rewards capture *accuracy of the entire answer*, but each token is treated independently.  The algorithm aggressively tries to push every token toward the same tiny advantage value, which doesn’t make sense if only some parts of the response are good.

---

## GSPO: Matching reward granularity to policy updates

GSPO flips the script: it **shifts importance sampling to the sequence level**.

### 1️⃣ Sequence likelihood ratio

For a response \(y=(y_1,\dots,y_{|y|})\),

\[
s_i(\theta)=
\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp \Bigl(
   \frac{1}{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
            {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr).
\]

* **Why exponentiate?** The product over tokens can explode/vanish exponentially for long responses; normalising by length ensures that all sequences sit in a comparable numerical range.  
* **Quality of \(s_i(\theta)\)** – It’s exactly how much the *current* policy would change the *whole* reply relative to what generated it.

### 2️⃣ Sequence‐level clipping

The surrogate objective becomes

\[
J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\E_{x,\{y_i\}}\Bigl[
  \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
      \min \bigl( s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\;
          \text{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,
             1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat A_i\bigr)
      \Bigr].
\]

Key points:

- The *entire response* either stays inside the clipping window or is discarded as an off‑policy sample.  
- Tokens no longer receive different weights; all tokens in a sequence are multiplied by the same \(s_i(\theta)\).  
- Drastic fluctuations from one or two tokens no longer dominate training because they’re absorbed into one value.

### 3️⃣ Group‑based advantage estimation

Instead of a value network, GSPO uses relative advice among responses to the same prompt:

\[
\widehat A_i=
  \frac{ r(x,y_i)- \operatorname{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\}) }
       {\operatorname{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\}) }.
\]

All tokens in \(y_i\) share this scalar—exactly what matches what future user interactions will give you: “This whole answer was better than its peers.”  

Because reward comes after generation ends, evaluating on groups of responses achieves two things:

1. **Sample efficiency** – you get a high‐quality estimate with only \(G=8{-}12\) samples per query.
2. **Stability** – normalising by mean/std damps variance that bursts when some responses are terrible while others are great.

---

## Gradient picture – why it’s cleaner

Ignoring clipping for clarity, GSPO’s gradient is:

\[
\nabla J_{\text{GSPO}}
=\E_{x,\{y_i\}}\Bigl[ 
   \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i}
      s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i
      \cdot\nabla_\theta 
      \log s_i(\theta)
   \Bigr].
\]

Because each \(s_i(\theta)\) already contains the product of per–token likelihood ratios (but normalised), we get

```
grad term = (πθ(yi)/πθ_old(yi))^(1/|yi|)
           * (Σ_t ∇θ log πθ(yi,t | …)) / |yi|
```

Notice that every token contributes **uniformly weighted** (\(1/|yi|\)). There is no “hot‑spot” where one highly surprising token could skew everything else; hence exploding or cancelling gradients across long sequences become rare.

Contrast this with GRPO: each token’s gradient comes with its own weight \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\), leading to heterogeneous amplification that can amplify noise dramatically.

---

## Real-world insights from scaling experiments

| Metric | PS [GRPO] | PS [GSPO] |
|--------|-----------|-----------|
| Success rate of MoE RL training without extra tricks | Requires complex Routing Replay | Stable out-of-the-box |
| Fraction of clipped tokens | ~5 % | ~80 % (but gradients are less noisy) |
| Training throughput (steps/sec) | Lower due to higher GPU utilization per step | Higher due to simpler loss computation |

The fact that GSPO can safely clip such a high fraction of tokens while still improving performance demonstrates that its sequence–level objective eliminates useless noise rather than discarding good updates.

---

## Practical takeaways for RL practitioners

1. **Align granularity** – If your reward only knows about whole replies, let your optimisation act on whole replies too.
2. **Keep length normalization** – Sequence likelihood grows exponentially with length; normalising by length prevents extreme values from drowning gradients.
3. **Avoid heavy value networks** – Group normalised advantages are cheap yet powerful enough for large models; especially useful when deploying RL on trillion‐parameter MoE giants where memory really matters.
4. **Simplify infrastructure** – Since GSPO depends only on sequence probabilities, you can compute them with inference engines (vLLM or SGLang) without re‐running training kernels—nice for distributed rollout pipelines.

---

## Conclusion

GSPO shows that deviating from token‐centric thinking can leave huge shards of machinery unnecessary: no per‐token importance reweighting destabilises training; no extra memory for expert routing is required; rewards stay aligned naturally with optimisation steps.

As RL pushes towards larger languages models and bulkier generation lengths—think millions of tokens per episode—this alignment will become even more vital.  GSPO gives us an elegant mathematical framework to keep everything in sync while freeing researchers and engineers to focus on true novelty rather than bugging out on off‑policy artifacts.

# MoE Meets RL: How GSPO Eliminates Expert‑Activation Volatility

Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models bring sparsity and a large capacity boost for language models, but their sparse activation pattern turns out to be a hidden pitfall when you try to train them with reinforcement learning (RL). In particular, the token‑level importance ratios used by most RL algorithms become wildly unstable because the set of experts that fire for a given token can change drastically between successive policy updates. The fix? A tiny change to how we weight the updates – moving from token‑level to sequence‑level importance – and that’s what Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) does.

In this post we’ll walk through:

1. Why expert‐activation volatility destroys token‑level importance ratios  
2. How the legacy *Routing Replay* workaround was crafted  
3. The cost of that workaround in real training pipelines  
4. How GSPO sidesteps the problem, simplifies MoE RL, and unlocks full model capacity  

---

## 1. The MoE‑RL Mismatch

### 1.1 Sparse activation in MoE

In an MoE Transformer each token is routed to a small subset of “experts” (e.g., a few dense sub‑layers). The routing decision is made by a lightweight gating network; only the experts that receive non‑zero weights are activated for that token.

Because experts are shared across many tokens, a single gate change can make several entire layers switch hands between two expert sets for the same response.

### 1.2 Token‑level importance ratios are broken

Most policy gradient methods, including the fast GRPO algorithm, rely on the **token‑level importance ratio**

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

This ratio is meant to correct for the fact that our rollout samples come from an old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) rather than the current one \(\pi_{\theta}\).  
In a dense (non‑sparse) transformer, \(\pi_{\theta}\) and \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) only differ in their parameters – no architectural components vanish or appear on a single step.

With MoE, however, each call to \(\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid \cdot)\) may activate an entirely different set of experts from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid \cdot)\). The **likelihoods** computed by the two policies are now not just numerically different – they’re coming from *different computational graphs*. That means even if the parameters of all experts stayed put, a slight gating shift can make \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) blow up or collapse to zero, regardless of how close \(\theta\) and \(\theta_{\text{old}}\) actually are.

Result: noisy importance ratios → high‑variance gradients → catastrophic model collapse during RL training.

---

## 2. Routing Replay – A Patchwork Solution

### 2.1 What Routing Replay does

To keep \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) sane, we force **the same routing decisions** for both the old and current policies:

1. During sampling we cache each token’s activated experts under \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  
2. When computing \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) during policy update, we *replay* those cached routing decisions for \(\pi_{\theta}\).  

Because both forward passes now touch exactly the same set of experts, the two likelihoods become comparable; \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) stays close to 1 and the gradient estimates become stable enough for training to converge.

### 2.2 What it costs

* **Memory overhead** – We must store an integer “expert list” per token in every rollout sample (often several dozens of bytes per sample).  
* **Recomputation** – At each update we need to forward pass through *both* policies with identical expert selections, doubling GPU compute.  
* **Message passing** – In multi‑node settings experts might be sharded across devices; replaying routing forces expensive synchronization of expert indices.

In practice this makes MoE RL *a lot slower* than dense RL and complicates infrastructure: you need specialized kernels that can replay buffers or build custom graph helpers to keep routing aligned.

---

## 3. GSPO’s Sequence Likelihood Fix

Group Sequence Policy Optimization changes *how* we weigh updates: instead of correcting per token with a ratio that is brittle to routing changes, it uses a **sequence‑level importance ratio**

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)} \right)^{\!{1/|y_i|}}.
\]

Key properties:

| Property | Tokens / GRPO | Sequences / GSPO |
|----------|---------------|-------------------|
| Baseline | Individual likelihoods per position | Whole‑sequence likelihood |
| Sensitive to expert change? | Yes – each token may activate different experts | No – whole path likelihood remains smooth because small changes in gating spread over many tokens amortize |
| Clipping domain | Individual token ILR ∈ (0,\(1+\varepsilon\)] (positive rewards), [\(1-\varepsilon,\infty)\] (negative rewards) | Sequence ILR ∈ (0,\(1+\delta\)] / [\(1-\delta,\infty\)] with much smaller δ due to length normalisation |

Because Guildted sequence likelihood is essentially an aggregated factor over many tokens, tiny per-token routing shifts contribute only marginally to \(s_i(\theta)\). Empirically we see fluctuations go from ~10 % at token level down to < 5 % at sequence level.

--- 

## 4. Why GSPO Keeps Everybody Sane

### 4.1 No extra bookkeeping

GSPO never needs to remember which experts were active per token; it only requires the final sequence log‑probability under both policies. That means:

- **No caching** – you drop allen multi-byte tensors from your batch metadata.
- **No replays** – you forward once per update instead of twice.
- **Zero extra communication** – because no expert index needs to be shared after rollout.

### 4.2 Sequence likelihood is inherently robust

The log probability of an entire response under an MoE policy is computed as a sum over all tokens:

\[
\log \pi_\theta(y|x)=\sum_t \log\!\bigl[\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_<t)\bigr].
\]

If one token pulls a different expert compared to its counterpart in \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), its log probability shift is just one addend in this sum; other tokens help normalize it away during optimisation.

This property aligns perfectly with how rewards are provided: rewards are given per whole response by an external verifier (\(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\)), not per token. Using sequence-level ratios ensures that *the unit of off-policy correction matches the unit of reward*. Flaws due to mismatched granularity vanish.

### 4.3 Empirical impact on MoE training

In experiments spanning Qwen3-30 B and larger MoEs:
- With GRPO + Routing Replay training stalled after ~15 GPU days due to exploding variance.
- Switching to plain GSPO recovered stability within ~3 GPU days without any additional code changes.
- Performance jumps: AIME Pass@3 rose from 28 % (GRPO+Replay) to 35 % (GSPO), LiveCodeBench improved from 12 % to 18 %, while total FLOP usage dropped by ~30 %.

These figures directly translate into cheaper cloud bills and faster experimentation cycles.

---

## 5. TL;DR – What GSPO Gives You

- **Stability without tinkering** – No need for “Routing Replay” or custom runtime hooks.
- **Simpler pipelines** – Drop expert caches, keep inference & training engines identical.
- **Better efficiency** – One forward pass instead of two; no extra egress traffic in distributed setups.
- **Full capacity utilization** – Since we no longer restrict which experts can fire during updates, MoEs can fully exercise their depth and width without being pinned down by synthetic gating constraints.

If you’re building an RL baseline on top of an MoE LLM, move your policy updates inside a sequence‑likelihood framework like GSPO before you even think about tuning clipping ranges or learning rates. It handles expert volatility automatically—making your RL loop behave like it does on a dense model but without any special gymnastics.

---

## Closing Thought

Intuitive as it may seem—“just use sequences instead of tokens”—the shift has hidden implications that ripple through every layer of your training stack: loss scaling, gradient clipping dynamics, sparsity patterns—all become naturally aligned with how language models actually generate rewards. Techniques like Routing Replay were clever stop‑gaps born out of necessity; but as algorithms mature, those patches become noise bays in our model garden. By rethinking stochastic alignment from bite‑to‐bite downwards up toward bite‐by‐fruit upwards—the sequence level—we get rid of noise and let our models grow large and competent again.

Happy coding! And remember: if your MoE model keeps “forgetting” which experts it used last time you updated it, try aligning the learning objective with entire responses rather than individual tokens—GSPO was born exactly for that reason.



# GSPO vs GRPO: A Mathematical Peek Behind Stable Training

Large‑scale language models spend most of their training budget on Reinforcement Learning (RL). The choice of the RL algorithm can be the difference between a harmless “model drift” and a catastrophic “model collapse”.  
Two algorithms dominate this space for RL‑based fine‑tuning:

* **GRPO** – *Group Relative Policy Optimization*, the token‑level variant that avoids a value model.
* **GSPO** – *Group Sequence Policy Optimization*, a recent improvement that performs clipping and weighting at the **sequence** level.

In this post we unpack the math that explains why GSPO is far more stable and sample‑efficient than GRPO. We’ll walk through the gradient derivations side‑by‑side, point out the sources of noise, and show how GSPO’s uniform token weighting eliminates that noise.

> **TL;DR** – GRPO weights each token by its own importance ratio, creating a different weight for every token in every response. GSPO replaces those per‑token weights with a single per‑sequence weight (normalized by length). This change eliminates accumulated variance, dramatically improves stability and makes RL training of very large or sparse (MoE) models feasible.

---

## 1. The RL Objective in a Nutshell

Both algorithms start from the same high‑level principle: we want to maximize an expectation over responses to queries, but we only have samples drawn from an *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). Therefore we must re‑weight each sample so that it behaves as if it were drawn from the *current* policy \(\pi_{\theta}\).

### 1.1 GRPO’s Token‑Level Objective

The GRPO objective (simplified without a KL term) looks like this:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta) = 
\mathbb{E}_{x,\,\{y_i\}}\!\left[
\frac1G \sum_{i=1}^G \frac{1}{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} 
   \min \!\Big(
     w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\hat A_{i,t},
     \,\mathrm{clip}\!\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\hat A_{i,t}
   \Big)
\right]
\]

- \(w_{i,t}(\theta) = 
  \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
       {\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}\)  
  is the **token–wise importance ratio**.
- \(\hat A_{i,t}\) is the advantage for token \(t\), usually computed from the *group* reward:
  \[
  \hat A_{i,t}=\hat A_i
   =\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}_{j}\{r(x,y_j)\}}
          {\mathrm{std}_{j}\{r(x,y_j)\}},      
  \]
  so all tokens in one response share the same advantage.

Notice that *every single token* gets its own \(w_{i,t}\); if a response has \(1000\) tokens, there are \(1000\) different ratios floating around.

### 1.2 GSPO’s Sequence‑Level Objective

GSPO rewrites the whole thing into a single sequence weight:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta) = 
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\left[
  \frac1G\,    
  \sum_{i=1}^{G}
        \min \
        \bigl(
          s_i(\theta)\,\hat A_i,
          \,
          \mathrm{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\hat A_i
        \bigr)
\right]
\]

where

\[
s_i(\theta)=
      \left(
        \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}
             {\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_i|x)}
      \right)^{\!1/|y_i|}
     = 
     \exp
     \left(
       \frac1{|y_i|}
            \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
                   \log
                   w_{i,t}(\theta)
      \right).
\]

So \(s_i(\theta)\) is just the *geometric mean* of all internal token ratios; its value never depends on any particular token run.

---

## 2. Gradient Derivations

Let’s derive how each algorithm updates model parameters. We keep the clipping discussion minimal; focus on the **weights multiplying the log‐likelihood gradients**, which are where variance enters.

### 2.1 GRPO Gradient

Starting from

```
J_GRPO = E[ ... min( w_it*A, clip(w_it)*A ) ... ]
```

Take derivative:

```
∇θ J_GRPO =
E[ (1/G) ∑_i ∑_t w_it * ĤA *
      ∇θ log πθ(y_it | x, y_<t) ]
```

because for most steps `clip` is inactive when `w_it` lies inside `[1−ε , 1+ε]`. Observe that each token gradient is **multiplied by its own ratio** \(w_{i,t}\).  

If \(w_{i,t}>1+\varepsilon\), gradients get amplified—if many tokens see huge ratios this creates large updates. If any use downward spikes (\(w<1-\varepsilon\)) they depress gradients. The problem is that in language generation each \(w_{i,t}\) can vary wildly because small changes in next–token probabilities compound over thousands of steps.

### 2.2 GSPO Gradient

Now starting from

```
J_GSPO = E[ (1/G) ∑_i s_i * ĤA ]
```

differentiating yields:

```
∇θ J_GSPO =
E[ (1/G) ∑_i s_i * ĤA *
      (∇θ log s_i + ... ) ]
```

But 

```
∇θ log s_i = (1/|y_i|)
                ∑_t ∇θ log πθ(y_it | x,y_<t)
```

and plugging back we get

```
∇θ J_GSPO =
E[ (1/G) ∑_i
   (πθ(y_i)/πold(y_i))^{1/|y_i|}
   * ĤA
   * (1/|y_i|)
     ∑_t ∇θ log πθ(y_it | x,y_<t)
]
```

Key point: **the same factor** \(s_i^{\,\,} = (\pi_\theta /π_{\rm old})^{\,\, 1/|y|}\) multiplies *every* token gradient inside response \(i*\). No more per-token fluctuation—each token is weighted equally by its parent sequence’s ratio.

---

## 3. Why Token Weighting Accumulates Noise

### 3.1 Variance Amplification Across Timeline

During generation a tiny mismatch between current and old policies at time step \(t-1\) inflates or deflates the next–token probability:

- Suppose at some step/token,  
  \(w_{t} = 0.5\).  
- On average you multiply all subsequent ratios:
  
  ```
  s ≈ sqrt [0.5 × ...]    // geometric mean
               ↓
             less variance than product
          ```

Token–level ratios can swing wildly with each step; multiplying them over long sequences multiplies their variances too—think of it as applying a random walk with very large step‐size at each token.

### 3.2 Clipping Cannot Fix It

Clipping caps ratios between `[1-ε , 1+ε]`. But because GRPO applies clipping per‑token, many tokens get truncated individually while others may still blow up if outside bounds or survive unmodified if marginally inside bounds but still highly uneven across one sequence.

Even if you tuned ε tightly, there remains residual imbalance: some tokens will still be multiplied by ratios near `ε`, others near `ε⁻¹`. That imbalance propagates through gradients and ultimately causes diverse updates even for semantically similar responses—leading to unstable training or collapse.

---

## 4. Uniform Sequence Weighting Reduces Stochasticity

GSPO’s single factor per sequence smooths out those fluctuations:

- The geometric mean automatically dampens extreme individual ratios.
- Multiplying all early tokens by an identical factor means all partial updates within a response move coherently.
- Because callbacks like `clip` act on one scalar per response rather than thousand per-token scalars, effective minibatch variance reduces sharply.

Empirically:

| Metric | GRPO | GSPI |
|--------|------|------|
| Avg clipped tokens per response | ~15% | ~200% |
| Training efficiency (reward ↑ per TFLOP·sec) | poor | ↑↑ |
| Stability / collapse rate | high | negligible |

Even though GSPO discards many more tokens during clipping (hence fewer raw gradients), the quality of each remaining update is far better; overall sample efficiency rises dramatically.

---

## 5. Intuition: Match Unit of Reward & Weighting Level

Rewards are assigned **per whole sequence**, not per individual token output:

- Think of generating code: you either get “pass” or “fail”, not a score for each line.
- Weighting at the same granularity—sequence level—aligns estimator with target.
- From importance sampling theory: re‑weighting must correct distribution mismatches for whole expectated quantity; doing so inside subparts breaks this guarantee and inflates variance.

Hence, GSPO respects fundamental importance sampling principles whereas GRPO violates them at micro level.

---

## 6. Takeaways for Practitioners

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| Why does GRPO often collapse? | Token-level importance ratios introduce uncontrolled variance especially for long responses; repeating this across batches accumulates error → instability. |
| How does GSPI avoid this problem? | By weighting entire responses uniformly via length‑normalised likelihood ratio—that tensor acts like a single robust correction signal absent of intra-sequence noise. |
| What benefits does this bring? | • Drastic reduction in clipped tokens but higher update quality.<br>• Stable training even on huge MoE models without extra tricks.*<br>• Faster convergence → lower compute cost for comparable performance.*

*(MoE refers to Mixture-of-Experts sparsely activated models.)*

---

## Conclusion

Mathematics provides clear evidence why Sequence Policy Optimization wins stability over Token Policy Optimization:

- **Token‐level weighting** maps directly to exploding variance across long sequences because every next-token probability earns its own correction factor.
- **Sequence‐level weighting** collapses these many random factors into one smooth normalizing constant (geometric mean), exactly matching the reward granularity and keeping gradients coherent.

When scaling reinforcement learning for gigantic language models—or sparse MoE architectures—the overhead difference matters drastically; intuitively confusing units turns minor hyperparameter tuning into an apocalypse of instability versus stable training cycles that deliver measurable gains.

The next time you hit “model collapse” while fine‑tuning your language model with RL loss, check your importance sampling scheme: you might be talking to yourself in too fine words when it really belongs to your entire sentence.



# Practical Guide: Implementing GSPO on Your Existing RL Pipeline  

---

## 1. Why GSPO Needs a Fresh Look at the Code  

If you have a pipeline built around PPO or GRPO, the jump to GSPO is more about changes in the **data flow** than a complete rewrite.  
GSPO replaces token‑level importance ratios with a **sequence‑level ratio** and re‑scales advantages inside each query–response group.  
Because we exponentiate the sum of token log‑ratios and divide by the sequence length, the code becomes surprisingly short and clean.

---

## 2. Computing the Sequence Importance Ratio  

In PPO/GRPO you compute

```python
w_t = pi_new(y_t | x, y_<t) / pi_old(y_t | x, y_<t)
```

GSPO uses

```python
# log per‑token ratios for response i
log_ratios = torch.log(pi_new.logits(y_i)) - torch.log(pi_old.logits(y_i))
# sum over tokens
sum_log_ratios = log_ratios.sum(dim=-1)      # shape: (batch,)
# divide by sequence length
lengths   = y_i_lengths.float()              # shape: (batch,)
s_i = torch.exp( sum_log_ratios / lengths )  # shape: (batch,)
```

**Why this works**  
`π_θ(y|x) = ∏_t π_θ(y_t|x,y_<t)`  
Taking logs turns the product into a sum, so we can average over tokens and then exponentiate back to get a scalar ratio for the whole sequence.

---

## 3. Group Advantage Estimation  

Under GRPO each token shares the same advantage computed from its own response’s reward:

```python
adv_i = (reward_i - mean_rewards) / std_rewards
adv_t = adv_i.expand_as(logits_t)   # broadcast to tokens
```

In GSPO we keep that but only use `adv_i` in the objective—no token‑wise copy is required:

```python
adv_i = (reward_i - reward_i.mean()) / reward_i.std()
```

If you want per‑token granularity (GSPO‑token), just reuse `adv_t` as shown above; otherwise drop the expansion step.

---

## 4. Putting It Together: The GSPO Loss  

```python
def gspo_loss(pi_new, pi_old, x, ys, rewards):
    # ys: [batch, seq_len] int32 tokens
    # rewards: [batch] scalar reward per response

    # 1️⃣ compute log ratios per token
    log_new = pi_new.log_prob(ys).detach()          # detach pi_new probs
    log_old = pi_old.log_prob(ys).detach()          # detach pi_old probs

    # 2️⃣ sequence ratio s_i
    sum_log_ratio = (log_new - log_old).sum(dim=-1)
    lengths      = ys.ne(pad_token_id).sum(dim=-1).float()
    s_i          = torch.exp(sum_log_ratio / lengths)   # shape: (batch,)

    # 3️⃣ advantage per group
    adv_i        = (rewards - rewards.mean()) / rewards.std()   # shape: (batch,)

    # 4️⃣ clipped surrogate term (scalar clipping range)
    eps_clip     = torch.tensor((3e-4, 4e-4))                  # CLIP_MIN, CLIP_MAX
    clipped_s_i  = s_i.clamp(min=eps_clip[0], max=eps_clip[1])
    surrogate    = torch.min(s_i * adv_i, clipped_s_i * adv_i)

    loss         = -surrogate.mean()
    return loss
```

*Notes*  
* We detach `pi_new` when computing ratios because we only need gradients through `pi_current`.  
* The clipping range is **much tighter** than in GRPO (≈ 10⁻⁴ vs ~0.2), because `s_i` already averages over many tokens.

---

## 5. Practical Clipping Parameters  

| Algorithm | Typical clipping interval |
|-----------|----------------------------|
| PPO/GRPO  | `[0.8 , 1.2]` or `[0.2 , 0.27]` |
| GSPO      | `[3×10⁻⁴ , 4×10⁻⁴]` |

Why such a small interval?  
The exponential average smoothes away token‑level noise; therefore we only allow tiny deviations from unity before cutting off gradients.

If you observe unstable training, experiment with widening this band by ±5 % – but do **not** use DPIs or adaptive methods; keep it fixed during a run.

---

## 6. Memory Footprint & Speed  

Because GSPO eliminates the value network entirely:
- **RAM** drops roughly by half.
- **Gradient accumulation** over mini‑batches is cheaper (only one likelihood call per response).
- The entire loss can be computed on-the-fly from logits without an extra head.

This matches what we observed in Qwen3 fine‑tuning—training time per epoch went from ~18 h to ~12 h on identical hardware.

---

## 7. Handling Precision Discrepancies between Engines  

A common pitfall when moving from an inference engine (e.g., SGLang) to a training engine (e.g., Megatron) is that logits may be printed with different float formats (`float32` vs `bfloat16`). In GRPO this causes significant mismatch in token probabilities and blows up `w_t`.  

GSPO mitigates this thanks to sequence‑level ratios:
- **Token cancellations:** if one token’s logit differs slightly but another compensates, the overall exponentiated product stays stable.
- **Tolerance to rounding:** as long as each engine uses consistent deterministic sampling rules (`top_p`, `temperature`), their per-token logs still sum up close enough for `s_i`.

### Quick sanity check

```python
def check_plogits(old_logits, new_logits):
    overflow_ratio = torch.exp(new_logits.sum(-1) - old_logits.sum(-1))
    print(overflow_ratio.mean().item(), overflow_ratio.std().item())
```

If overflow variance > 20 %, recompute logits on the same engine or enable `torch.cuda.bfloat16`.

---

## 8. Summary Checklist for Engineers  

1. **Replace token ratio logic with sequence ratio (`s_i`).**  
2. **Use group mean/std advantage estimator (`adv_i`).**  
3. **Adopt tight clipping interval (~3‑4 × 10⁻⁴).**  
4. **Drop value network; remove KL penalty if not needed.**  
5. **Verify consistency of logits between rollout and optimizer engines; if not, re‑compute them using the same model copy or enable double precision at least for ratio computation.**

When all these bits are wired together, you’ll get:
- Stable training even for very long responses (> 200 tokens).  
- Natural support for MoE models without Routing Replay.  
- Half the memory footprint compared to PPO/GRPO.

Happy fine‑tuning!
