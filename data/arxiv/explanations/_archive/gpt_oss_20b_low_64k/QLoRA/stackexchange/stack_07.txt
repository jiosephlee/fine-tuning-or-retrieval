\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### `k‐bit` QLoRA vs `k‐bit` Full Finetuning: Why do we recover full precision after adapter tuning?
Question:
Table 5 shows that performing only adapter tuning on a fully quantized (NF4) base restores performance equal to full 16‑bit fine‐tuning. Intuitively I thought significant information loss could not be recovered by training only a few new parameters. How can low‐rank adapters compensate for such heavy quantization errors across all layers?
Answer:
**Why a small low‑rank update can “undo’’ the noise introduced by 4‑bit quantization**

The claim in Table 5—that a vanilla 4‑bit NF4 LLaMA base trained with *only* LoRA adapters can match the full‑precision 16‑bit fine‑tuning performance—seems at first glance surprising because a 4‑bit representation can incur large round‑off and clipping errors, especially for the outliers (the very few very large weights that dominate a transformer layer).  
The key point is that these errors are *additive*, not multiplicative: each weight matrix is stored as  

\[
\mathbf{W}\approx \tilde{\mathbf{W}}+ \mathbf{E},
\]
where \(\tilde{\mathbf{W}}\) is the 4‑bit quantized version and \(\mathbf{E}\) is the reconstruction error (the difference between the real FP32 value and its quantized surrogate). The forward pass through a transformer block is essentially a series of matrix multiplications. If we treat these errors as an effective “bias” added to each projection, the model’s response will be perturbed in a systematic way.

LoRA inserts, for every linear layer \(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}\), an additive term
\[
\Delta = s\,\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2 ,
\]
where \(\mathbf{L}_1\in\mathbb R^{h\times r}\) and \(\mathbf{L}_2\in\mathbb R^{r\times o}\) are *trainable* and \(s\) is a scalar learned during fine‑tuning.  
Because \(\Delta\) has the same shape as \(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}\) (i.e., it feeds into the same downstream layers), it can substitute for whatever change is needed to counteract the quantization error \(\mathbf{E}\). In other words, the rank‑\(r\) correction space supplied by LoRA can be seen as an *explicit (learned) approximation of the error tensor*.

---

### What makes this approximation powerful

1. **Quantization error is low–dimensional in practice**  
   Empirical studies on transformer weights have shown that most of the variance in a weight matrix lies in its leading singular vectors—exactly what low‑rank matrix factorisations capture. Even though NF4 introduces discretisation noise into every element, that noise is largely *aligned* with the same directions that contain most of the signal power. Therefore a rank‑\(r=4\) or \(r=8\) projection per layer already has enough capacity to represent, or at least closely approximate, \(\mathbf{E}\).

2. **Adapters are placed everywhere**  
   The experiments underline that putting LoRA adapters on every attention projection and MLP layer (rather than just a handful of them) markedly improves performance. Each layer can correct its own local quantisation noise without affecting other layers, so we do not have to hope that one small set of corrections propagates across the whole network.

3. **Full‑backward gradients through frozen weights**  
   Although the base weights stay frozen in 16‑bit precision during training, gradients are still back‑propagated through them when updating an adapter:
   \[
   \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{L}_i}
        = s\, \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial (\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W})}
          }_{\text{computed via dequantised }\tilde{\mathbf W}}
          \,\frac{\partial (\mathbf X\,\tilde{\mathbf W})}{\partial \tilde{\mathbf W}}\,
          \frac{\partial \tilde{\mathbf W}}{\partial w},
   \]
   where all intermediate quantities are evaluated in BF16 precision thanks to *double dequantisation*. Consequently, the optimiser “sees” an accurate gradient signal coming from the real FP32 weight matrix; it only learns how much extra activation to add at each layer via the adapter.

---

### Intuitive analogy

Think of a smooth road that has been roughened by pavement chips (the quantisation error). A thin, flexible “correction strip” (the LoRA sheet) can be laid over every segment of road so that drivers feel no difference compared to driving on pristine pavement. The strip does not need to mirror every single chip exactly; it only has to reproduce enough curvature to give the same overall driving experience. Similarly, LoRA does not need to correct every noisy weight; it simply adjusts activations so that downstream layers perceive an almost identical effective weight matrix.

---

### Why full reset isn’t required

Unlike conventional fine‑tuning where we solve for new weight values globally, LoRA keeps most of the network fixed and only tunes a tiny subset whose purpose is explicitly additive correction. Because the base model already encodes all linguistic structure in its frozen weights, only subtle adjustments are needed after quantisation: precisely what LoRA provides.

When LoRA was initially proposed (Hu et al., 2021), it was shown that training 0.2 % or less of all parameters could recover most gains from full training for 7–13 B models in FP16. QLoRA extends this observation by proving empirically (Table 5) that *the same proportion* of parameters suffices even when those 99 %+ of weights live in NF4 format and carry significant quantisation noise – because that noise is constrained enough for low‐rank additions to neutralise it.

---

### Bottom line

- Quantisation produces an approximate representation but leaves the network’s expressive power largely intact.
- Low‑rank adapters placed throughout provide a learnable residual that can compensate for (and often exactly cancel out) these approximations.
- Backpropagation through frozen 16‑bit weights gives high‐quality gradients for those tiny residuals.
- Empirically, this combination reproduces full 16‑bit fine‑tuning quality while using far fewer trainable parameters and dramatically less GPU memory.

Thus Table 5’s claim rests on solid theory: quantisation error behaves like an *additive*, *low‐rank* perturbation, which cheap adapters can recover efficiently.
