\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### How does QLoRA handle back‑propagation through frozen quantized weights?
Question:
The update rule for LoRA involves computing $rac{ackslashpartial 	extbf{X}}{ackslashpartial 	extbf{W}}$ during adapter training. Since $	extbf{W}$ is stored in NF4, we must first dequantize it to BF16 before gradient computation. Does this dequantization introduce any approximation error during backpropagation? Does QLoRA store any intermediate activations related to $	extbf{W}$ that might affect memory usage or compute time?
Answer:
**Short‑answer**  
In QLoRA the 4‑bit weights are stored in *NormalFloat (NF4)* and *Quantized* as normalised integers with per‑block scales. During a forward or backward step the tensor is **dequantised only once** to bf16 (the computation dtype). The dequantisation step is a look‑up of the integer value multiplied by its block‑scale, which exactly recovers the 32‑bit float that had been quantised. Because this calculation is performed in floating‑point arithmetic, no extra approximation beyond the original quantisation error is introduced. QLoRA therefore does **not** accumulate an additional back‑propagation error because the gradient w.r.t. the 4‑bit weights *is never computed*. The only gradients that travel through the frozen base model are those w.r.t. the **LoRA adapters**; these gradients are taken over the de‑quantised weight matrix.  

Regarding memory:  
* No activations of the original 32‑bit weight tensor are stored—only the 4‑bit storage and, during each pass, a temporary bf16 matrix produced by dequantisation is created on‑the‑fly.  
* The per–block scale constants themselves are *also* stored in 4 bits after “double‑quantisation” (the second level of quantisation), so no 32‑bit floats survive in GPU RAM.  
* The only intermediate tensors that survive between forward and backward passes are the standard activation stores (input, output, residuals) needed for any transformer layer; these are identical in size to a normal floating‑point run because they are kept in bf16 just like in full finetuning.

Hence:  
1. **No additional approximation error** is introduced by the dequantisation during backpropagation; the only error comes from the original NF4 quantisation, which QLoRA proves to be perfectly recovered through LoRA training.  
2. **Memory usage stays bounded**: we keep a single 4‑bit weight tensor plus its per-block scale constants (compressed via double quantisation), and we generate an on‑the‑fly bf16 copy during computation. No extra copy of a full precision weight matrix is ever kept or re‑computed across batches.

In practical terms this means that during training QLoRA behaves as if it were fine‑tuning a full 16‑bit model in terms of gradient flow, but with far less memory for the frozen parameters and no penalty from additional approximation errors.
