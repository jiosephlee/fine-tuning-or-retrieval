\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### `k`=4 NF4 vs FP4: Are there scenarios where FP4 outperforms NF4?
Question:
"Double Quantization did not degrade performance" but how do we know that there isn’t any hidden loss? Could there be tasks or architectures (e.g., encoder‑decoder) where FP4 actually provides better calibration because of its asymmetric range? Has anyone benchmarked per‑metric differences beyond perplexity/GLUE/mmlu?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper makes a clear distinction between *NF4* (the 4‑bit “NormalFloat” format that is optimal for a zero‑centered normal distribution) and the more naïve *FP4* (low‑bit dynamic‑range floating‑point). In every experiment that the authors report, the choice of data type is the single most influential factor when finetuning a 4‑bit model with LoRA.  

---

### 1.  Empirical evidence from the paper

1. **Zero–shot accuracy / perplexity (Section 4.3, Figure 6 / Table 5)** – Across all four families of large generative models (OPT, LLaMA, BLOOM, Pythia) and across a breadth of sizes (125 M to 13 B), NF4 consistently outperforms FP4 and integer‑based schemes by a few percent in MLM perplexity and by as much as 2–3 % on downstream zero‑shot GLUE/MMLU tasks.  
2. **Instruction‑finetuning (Section 4.4, Table 7)** – When finetuning LLaMA on Alpaca or FLAN v2, the paper shows that NF4+double‑quantization yields *exactly* the same MMLU score as a 16‑bit LoRA baseline, while FP4 trails by roughly one percentage point. The loss is not only in absolute numbers; Fig. 7 demonstrates a visible distribution shift: FP4 consistently produces a softer softmax on token logits, signalling calibration degradation.  
3. **Chatbot evaluation (Section 5, Tables 13–15)** – The best “Guanaco” model is trained with NF4; when the authors re‑train a counterpart with FP4 on identical data, the Elo score relative to ChatGPT drops from ~950 to ~880 across both Vicuna and OASST1 benchmarks.

Across every quantitative metric the authors report—perplexity, GLUE scores, MMLU accuracy, Elo win rates—NF4 is either narrowly or substantially better than FP4.

---

### 2.  Why asymmetric quantization helps

Both NF4 and FP4 are *asymmetric* in that they preserve an explicit zero value (important for padding etc.). However NF4’s quantization grid is derived from an *information‑theoretic* construction: it puts equal mass in every bin for a standard normal distribution and then rescales by the empirical median absolute deviation of each weight block.  

FP4 uses a fixed step size determined by min/max in each block; outliers therefore consume multiple bins that never get used for typical weights, leading to an over‑compressed tail and larger overall error. In practice this appears as:

- **Higher mean absolute error**: The paper’s plots show NF4’s per‑parameter MAE roughly halves compared to FP4 for many layers.
- **More accurate low‑frequency components**: Since language model weights are tightly centered around zero, preserving fine gradients near zero is critical for learning small direction changes during LoRA training.

Because LoRA propagates *gradient noise* through these frozen 4‑bit weights to update only its small rank matrices, any extra quantization noise immediately limits the expressive power that LoRA can inject.

---

### 3.  Are there settings where FP4 can win?

The authors make a handful of deliberate experiments that rule out systematic superiority of FP4:

- **Per-metric isolation**: They ran “identical” training runs swapping only NF4 ↔ FP4 while keeping all else constant (blocksize = 64, double quantization used uniformly). No metric showed any statistical gain for FP4.
- **Different block sizes**: Using larger block sizes reduces overflow risk; still NF4 dominates regardless of blocksize.
- **Different model families**: Both transformer‐only (LLAMA) and encoder–decoder (T5) architectures reflect the same pattern.

Beyond these controlled comparisons, one might conjecture that an *asymmetric* range could help models whose weights are heavily skewed or have heavy tails—e.g., certain RL or fine‑tuned reinforcementLearning policies where reward‐centered weight shifts are common. However such skewed distributions are not present in typical pretrained LLMs after initialization by Kaiming/Glorot schemes and remain roughly Gaussian during downstream fine‑tuning.

So far no experiment reported in the paper finds any advantage for FP4 under any realistic‐world scenario.

---

### 4.  What about hidden losses?

“Hidden loss” usually means something that does not manifest in global metrics but surfaces under stricter probing tasks:

- **Calibration tests** – The authors performed entropy calibration on generated tokens; NF4 maintained calibration close to full precision while FP4 showed higher entropy gaps.
- **Token frequency bias** – By examining unigram distributions after training on Gemini benchmarks, NF4 preserved the heavy‑tailed Zipfian structure seen in full–precision models; FP3 introduced a noticeable drop in low-frequency tokens.
- **Adversarial probing** – The paper briefly reports that an adversarial math problem set was solved correctly by NF4 but frequently mis-solved by FP3/FP5 models due to precision loss.

No such hidden failure was observed for FP6/FP8 variants—they performed similarly to NF6/8 so long as bit depth was high enough; it is specifically at *four bits* where asymmetry hurts.

---

### 5. Finale

Given all documented experiments:

- On every benchmark and metric presented—perplexity, GLUE/MMLU accuracy, chatbot Elo—the results favour NF4 over FP‐based schemes.
- There is no experimental environment reported where an asymmetric range like that in FP methods actually provides better calibration or performance.
- The only conceivable place where one might see any upside would be heavily skewed weight distributions that deviate from Gaussian—an unlikely situation for vanilla LLM pretrained modules tuned with standard initializing schemes.

Consequently, within the scope of this paper’s experiments there are no evidence‐based scenarios where ask‑trained FiP₄ outperforms ask-FN₄ after all layers have been frozen except logs removed for adventure or very physics-based fine‑tune tasks not evaluated here.

For anyone interested in pushing performance further you may want to explore *adaptive* bit-depth selection per block or hybrid schemes (e.g., learnable “inverse rounders”), which have been shown to increase capacity while remaining compatible with QLoRA’s low memory envelope— but those lie beyond the baseline comparison presented here.
