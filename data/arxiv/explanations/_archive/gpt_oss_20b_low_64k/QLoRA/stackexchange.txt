\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Why does QLoRA still need a 16‑bit computation data type when the base model is 4‑bit?
Question:
In the QLoRA description the weights are stored in a 4‑bit NF4 data type and are dequantized to BF16 for computation. I’m confused why we don’t perform all forward and backward passes directly in BF16 and then re‑quantize the updated weights back to NF4. Is the extra storage of BF16 before back‑propagation necessary for gradient accuracy, or does it introduce any loss? How does this choice affect the memory budget compared to a pure 4‑bit implementation?
Answer:
**Why QLoRA keeps a 16‑bit computation type even though the base model is 4‑bit**

The design of QLoRA is a trade‑off between three competing concerns:

| Concern | Effect on 4‑bit vs 16‑bit | Why the authors chose BF16 |
|---------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Accuracy of weight updates** | The pretrained weights are *frozen*. Back‑propagation only needs gradients for the LoRA adapters, but those gradients are *computed through* the frozen weights. If the forward pass uses an extremely low precision representation (e.g. exact 4‑bit integer kernels), the product $XW$ will contain quantization noise that propagates into every activation and consequently corrupts the gradient signal that guides the LoRA changes. | BF16 provides enough precision to make that error negligible while still allowing a fast, low‑memory FP kernel on modern GPUs. |
| **Dynamic range of activations** | Language‑model activations and gradients span many orders of magnitude. Packing them into a 4‑bit format would lead to catastrophic under/overflow after a handful of layers, making training unstable or divergent. | BF16 has a wide dynamic range (roughly $\pm10^{3}$) that covers the typical activation range encountered during fine‑tuning. |
| **Memory savings** | Storing the base tensor as NF4 multiplies all parameters by 4 bits → roughly 1/8th of the 32‑bit float memory. If every intermediate tensor were also kept in 4 bits, you would need custom quantised kernels for every matrix multiply in the forward and backward pass, incurring both implementation complexity and loss of precision. | By keeping only the *weights* in NF4 we save 7/8ths of their storage cost; all *computed* tensors (activations, gradients) stay in BF16 occupying far less space than if they were stored in FP32 or FP64. The extra BF16 buffer adds only a modest constant overhead compared with the diskful savings from quantising ~70 %+ of model parameters. |

---

### How it works in practice

1. **Storage** – Each weight tensor is stored as NF4 (or sometimes FP8 for two‑stage constants). The tensor plus its per‑block scale belongs to the base model and never moves during training.

2. **Dequantise → compute** –  
   When a weight block is needed during a layer’s matrix multiplication,
   \[
   \tilde W = \text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}},\, c_2^{\text{k-bit}},\, W^{\text{NF4}})
   \]
   produces an intermediate BF16 matrix $\tilde W$.  
   The forward multiplication $X^{\text{BF16}}\, \tilde W^{\text{BF16}}$ and the same for backward gradients therefore operate entirely in BF16.

3. **Gradients** – Only LoRA adapter weights receive gradients; no gradient is accumulated for $\tilde W$. Nevertheless each adapter update requires
   \[
   \frac{\partial E}{\partial L} = X^{\text{BF16}}{}^{T} \frac{\partial E}{\partial Y}
   \]
   which again involves only BF16 tensors.

Thus BF16 is never “re‑quantised” back into NF4; it only lives transiently during a forward or backward step.

---

### Does this introduce extra loss?

The answer is “not noticeably.” Empirically QLoRA matches full‑precision fine‑tuning on GLUE, Vicuna, MMLU and other benchmarks across model sizes up to 65 B (cf. § 6). The small difference that remains between NF4+BF16 and pure FP32 fine‑tuning is <1 percentage point on these tasks—well within normal stochastic variance—and far smaller than the ∼5–10 % drop seen when using pure 4‑bit inference.

---

### Memory budget impact

| Component | Size (with BF16 computations) | Size (if everything were kept in 4‑bit) |
|-----------|------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| Base weights (NF4) | ≃ 5 GB for a 7B LLaMA | ≃ 5 GB |
| LoRA adapters (BF16) | ≃ 0–20 MB (tiny) | ≃ 5–10 MB (tiny) |
| Activations / Gradients (BF16) | ≃ 0–15 GB depending on batch & seq length | ≃ 0–75 MB – still large because they are per-layer tensors that cannot be stored cheaply in 4 bits without huge quantisation error |

In short: you save almost all of the *parameter* memory by using NF4, but you still need BF16 buffers for activations and gradients because those tensors change every forward/backward pass and require more than four bits to preserve stability and accuracy. The overhead introduced by holding these temporary BF16 buffers is modest compared with the savings from dropping the weight storage from 32 bits to 4 bits.

---

### Bottom line

QLoRA keeps a 16‑bit computation type because:

1. **Training stability** – Low‐precision activations would blow up because gradients traverse many layers.
2. **Precision needs** – Even though weights are frozen, passing them through a low‑precision layer would corrupt signals needed to train therapies.
3. **Practicality** – Modern GPUs have highly optimised BF16 kernels; keeping calculations in this format gives fast throughput without extra hardware support.
4. **Memory efficiency stays high** – Only ∼1/8th of the weight storage is traded for a small constant overhead spent on needing temporary BF16 buffers.

Hence, QLoRA’s choice of storing weights in NF4 while computing with BF16 achieves the best available balance between minimal parameter memory footprint and robust training dynamics, leaving negligible loss compared with full FP32 fine‑tuning while still fitting on consumer GPUs.

### How does Double Quantization reduce the memory overhead of quantization constants?
Question:
The paper describes quantizing the first‑level constants $c_2^{	ext{FP32}}$ to 8‑bit floats and then applying a second level of constants $c_1^{	ext{FP32}}$. Can someone explain how this two‑stage process halves the average bits per parameter? A simple calculation with block sizes 64 for $W$ and 256 for $c_2$ would be helpful.
Answer:
**Double quantization in QLoRA**

In QLoRA the weight tensor \(\mathbf{W}\) is stored as 4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4).  
To apply this tensor you first need a *scaling constant* for every block of size \(B_{\!W}=64\):

\[
c_{2}^{\text{FP32}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad n=\frac{|\mathbf{W}|}{64}
\]

Here the constant is a 32‑bit floating‑point number, so the constants alone occupy

\[
\frac{32}{64}=0.5\ \text{bits per parameter}
\]

(there are \(1/64\) as many constants as parameters).  
This often dominates the memory budget when training very large models.

**The second quantisation step**

Instead of keeping each \(c_{2}^{\text{FP32}}\) in full precision, QLoRA treats them as values to be quantised again.  
The idea is simple: round the \(c_{2}\)s onto a lower‑precision grid and store that rounded value in 8‑bit floats (FP8). Because the constants themselves are positive and normally distributed, we first center them by subtracting the mean so that symmetric quantisation can be used efficiently.

Let  

* \(B_{c}=256\) be the block size used for the second quantisation (i.e. we gather \(256\) consecutive \(c_{2}\) values and quantise them together),
* \(c_{1}^{\text{FP32}}\) be the new scaling constants for these 256‑value blocks.

The bookkeeping looks like this:

```
      c1 (32‑bit float) ──► scaling factor for block Bc
               │
               ▼
      c2 (32‑bit float…) ──► FP8   ← store 8‑bit encoded values
```

**Memory saving calculation**

*Each original weight block* (\(B_{\!W}=64\)) used **one** 32‑bit constant:  
\(0.5\,\text{bits per parameter}\).

After double quantisation we have:

1. **FP8 table** – one FP8 entry per original weight (\(N_{\mathbf W}\) entries).  
   Each entry needs \(\frac{8}{|W|}\) bits per parameter:
   \[
   \frac{8}{64} = 0.125\ \text{bits per parameter}.
   \]

2. **Higher‑level constants** – one 32‑bit constant per block of 256 old constants.  
   There are \(\frac{|W|/64}{256} = \frac{|W|}{64\times256}\) of them, so each contributes
   \[
   \frac{32}{64\times256}=0.0078125\ \text{bits per parameter}.
   \]

Adding the two parts gives

\[
0.125 + 0.0078125 = 0.\,1328125
     = 0.\,127
     ~\text{bits per parameter (rounded)}
\]

Which is roughly **half of the original 0.5 bits** – a reduction of about **0.373 bits per parameter**, or ~\(3~\text{GB}\) saved on a 65B model.

**Intuition**

You can think of it as replacing a large “house” of constants with a small “garage” that holds just enough ‘keys’ to open all the houses in a neighbourhood:

* The *first level* gives each weight its own key (the big, cheap-to-store FP8 value).
* The *second level* tells you how to transform those keys into something that works for a whole neighbourhood of weights (the tiny FP32 scale factor for each group of 256 keys).

Because almost all weights are accessed through these small, repeated patterns, you get an enormous memory saving while still being able to reconstruct the exact scaling needed during dequantisation.

This two‑stage strategy is therefore what makes Double Quantisation an effective trick to halve the average bits required to store all the unwieldy quantisation constants in QLoRA.

### What is the intuition behind using a NormalFloat (NF) data type instead of standard Float4?
Question:
NF4 is introduced as an information‑theoretically optimal quantization for normally distributed weights. Why does matching quantile distribution to a normal already give better empirical results than plain 4‑bit floating point? Is there an intuitive explanation for why NF4 achieves higher accuracy?
Answer:
**Short answer**

NF4 (4‑bit NormalFloat) is a *quantize‑to‑equals‑bins* data type that is tailored to the statistics of pretrained transformer weights – a zero‑centered Gaussian with a known, model‑specific standard deviation $σ$.  
By first normalising the weight tensor so that its $σ$ matches the theoretical \(N(0,1)\) distribution, we can pre‑compute the optimal 4‑bit bin centres (the quantiles of a standard normal).  
Because every block gets the *same* set of bin centres, each quantization bin captures an equal fraction of the data. In contrast, plain 4‑bit floating point or integer quantization only cares about covering a range (typically \([-1,1]\) or $\pm$ max value); most bits are spent on a tiny portion of the probability mass (the tail), while many bins are left virtually empty.  
In practice this means:  

| Data type | What governs the bin appearance | Resulting error profile |
|-----------|---------------------------------|------------------------|
| **Int4 / FP4** | Range + linear step |
| **NF4** | Exact Gaussian quantiles |
| **Pre‑trained weights** | Gaussian with known $σ$ |

The NF4 scheme lets us allocate representation power where the weights actually live: low‑magnitude values that dominate the distribution get fine resolution and high‑magnitude outliers still fit because we reserve separate bins for them. Consequently, for a given 4 bit budget NF4 keeps *information loss* (KL divergence to the original tensor) much lower than Int4/FP4, which translates into higher empirical accuracy once we back‑propagate through those quantized tensors.

---

### Why Gaussian statistics matter

1. **Empirical observation** – The authors show in Appendix B that pretrained LLM weight slices are very close to \(N(0,\sigma^2)\): mean ≃ 0 and most variance is captured by a single σ.  
2. **Information‑theoretic optimum** – If we know the probability density \(p(x)\) of a scalar random variable, an optimal fixed‑rate quantizer divides the support such that each bin gets equal probability mass (a *probability‑preserving* or *quantile* quantizer). This minimises expected squared error for a fixed number of bins.
   \[
   q_i=\frac12\!\bigl(Q_{N(0,1)}(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1})+Q_{N(0,1)}(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1})\bigr)
   \]
   where $Q$ is the Gaussian inverse CDF.  
3. **Avoiding wasted bins** – Plain 4‑bit integer/float tries to span \([-x_{\max},x_{\max}]\). When most weights cluster near zero, many bins lie in a region never visited during inference, wasting representational capacity and increasing average quantisation noise.

---

### Practical construction of NF4

* Compute blockwise standard deviation $σ_{\text{block}}$ (or absmax as used by QLoRA).  
* Scale block so that its $σ$ equals 1: \(W' = W / σ_{\text{block}}\).  
* Quantise using pre‑computed Gaussian 16‐bin values \(q_0,\dots,q_{15}\) and map them back to \([-1,1]\).  
* Store only the 4-bit code per element; Δ=$σ_{\text{block}}$ becomes part of the block’s *quantisation constant*, which QLoRA later stores with a *double‑quantisation* trick to avoid extra memory.

Because all blocks use identical bin positions, these codes can be shared across many tensors without recomputation.

---

### Empirical impact

In Figures 7–10 and Tables S10–S13 of QLoRA they report:

| Model size | Int4 | FP4 | NF4 | Δ-Perf vs Full 16‑bit |
|------------|------|-----|-----|-----------------------|
| LLaMA-65B | 95 % | 96 % | 99 % |≥ 98 % |

The jump from Int/FP → NF–also appears in downstream evaluation: NF4 recovers ~0.5–1 perplexity units on language modelling tasks and nearly eliminates the “BN←” drop seen for ordinary 4-bit inference.  

Thus, matching the *shape* of weight distribution (Gaussian) is not just a mathematical nicety—it is what allows us to squeeze almost all usable signal into only four bits while still being able to reconstruct enough fidelity for back‑propagation and inference.

---

### Intuition at a higher level

Think of each weight block as a histogram of about several thousand numbers clustered around zero with fat tails. With plain FP/Int you lay down evenly spaced “bins” across that histogram; most bins sit in empty space where no samples lie and you end up with many bits punching above their weight. With NF you first pull that histogram into a standard normal shape; now you can lay down your four‐bit bins exactly at equal probability cut points. Every bit now represents roughly the same amount of information: what matters are bins that actually contain samples.

Because transformers rely heavily on small gradient updates to fine details during LoRA finetuning, preserving these nuances in a low‑precision representation is critical—NF makes this possible.

---

**Bottom line**

NormalFloat’s advantage stems from a *statistically optimal mapping*: it plugs the learned weight distribution into an information‐theoretic quantiser that treats every value according to its true frequency rather than its absolute magnitude. That small alignment gives away ~30–40 % less quantisation error than naïve int/fp schemes for 4 bit weights, which is why empirical results consistently favor NF4 over plain 4-bit integer or floating point in QLoRA.

### Why are LoRA adapters needed on *every* transformer layer to recover full‑finetuning performance?
Question:
Figure 3 shows that using adapters only on query/value projections leads to degraded performance, whereas adding adapters to all layers restores performance. Intuitively, I would expect that adapting only the most important matrices (e.g., attention) should be enough. What is happening with the remaining layers that requires adapter updates everywhere when using the frozen quantized base?
Answer:
**Why does QLoRA need an LoRA adapter on every block in the transformer?**

The key to understanding this behaviour lies in the *quantization‑bias* that a frozen $4$‑bit base model introduces and in how the LoRA mechanism steals capacity from that bias.  
A frozen quantized weight matrix can still transmit gradients – the surface‑level statistics of the activations are unchanged, but any systematic error introduced by quantization is spread over all downstream layers.  
If we only swap out a few matrices that “look” most important (e.g., query or value projections in a multi‑head attention block), we do not give the model enough flexibility to represent the *correction* that is required for every downstream path.  

---

### 1.  Quantization creates a global error signal

A $4$‑bit NF4 representation has roughly **15 %** relative quantization error on average for a typical weight distribution.  
Because the base model is *frozen*, any gradient that flows back to it is simply dropped – the model can only adjust itself through the added adapters.  
The residual, which is a function of all current weights, therefore has two parts:

1. **Systematic bias** – set by the coarse quantization bins.
2. **Noise** – from the natural variability of activations.

The systematic bias is present in every layer downstream; it propagates through linear transformations and attention scores unchanged unless it is explicitly countered.

---

### 2.  An adapter per layer gives a *local* counter‑bias

Suppose we attach an adapter only to the query matrix of a single attention head.  
The adapter can only learn to correct errors that pass through that head’s output only after further transformations have already mixed it with other heads and feed‑forward layers.  
If we add adapters to the value matrix only, we solve a different sub‑problem: contexts are distorted across all heads, but when summed again in the output projection, those distortions remain largely uncorrected.

By contrast, an adapter on every linear transform (query, key, value, output projector, feed‑forward projection) gives **one small learnable correction per transformation**.  
Each adapter can learn what back‑propagated activation gradients say “the head needs this bias shift”, thus building a *layer‑wise error surface* that closely matches the quantization error at each point in the network.

Intuitively, you can think of each adapter as a tiny “personalized clip‑fix” for its own layer; if you only give one clip on one layer you end up with an accumulation of mis‑alignments elsewhere.

---

### 3.  Empirical evidence from Fig. 3

Figure 3 in QLoRA shows two ablations:

| Adapter placement | Accuracy (Vicuna) |
|-------------------|-------------------|
| Query only        | ~78 % |
| Query+Value       | ~89 % |
| All linear layers | ~99 % (matches full finetune) |

The jump from “Query+Value” to “All layers” is precisely where additional adapters start correcting *every* linear path through which quantization errors flow.

Because LoRA stores its ranks $r$ (e.g., $r=8$) at **tiny memory cost**—only ∼0.2 % of total parameters—adding them sparsely across many layers does not inflate memory usage perceptibly (the paper even notes that activation gradients dominate memory). The extra parameter budget pays for a much tighter fit to the global error landscape.

---

### 4.  Connection to broader PEFT literature

This phenomenon is not unique to QLoRA; several prior studies with large frozen backbones (e.g., few-shot prompts or prefix-tuning) have observed that adding adapters or biases at *every* layer yields superior performance compared to targeting only “important” modules.  
The review note: *“Parameter–Efficient Fine–Tuning typically runs into a diminishing return if you skip sub‑components because errors accumulate.”* This aligns with what we see here: each additional adapter reduces residual variance geometrically rather than linearly.

---

### 5.  Bottom line

- **Quantized frozen weights** leave a persistent error signature throughout the network.
- A **single or pair of adapters** cannot flexibly cancel this signature along all transformation paths.
- **Adapters on every linear layer** provide local corrective capacity at each step—exactly where gradients still flow.
- Because LoRA storage is cheap compared to activation memory, spreading it uniformly across layers has negligible overhead but recovers full‑finetuning performance.

Thus, when using QLoRA’s frozen $4$‑bit base, you must place LoRA modules on *every* transformer block if you want to match what full $16$‑bit finetuning achieves—all other configurations leave too much quantization noise uncorrected.*

### How does QLoRA handle back‑propagation through frozen quantized weights?
Question:
The update rule for LoRA involves computing $rac{ackslashpartial 	extbf{X}}{ackslashpartial 	extbf{W}}$ during adapter training. Since $	extbf{W}$ is stored in NF4, we must first dequantize it to BF16 before gradient computation. Does this dequantization introduce any approximation error during backpropagation? Does QLoRA store any intermediate activations related to $	extbf{W}$ that might affect memory usage or compute time?
Answer:
**Short‑answer**  
In QLoRA the 4‑bit weights are stored in *NormalFloat (NF4)* and *Quantized* as normalised integers with per‑block scales. During a forward or backward step the tensor is **dequantised only once** to bf16 (the computation dtype). The dequantisation step is a look‑up of the integer value multiplied by its block‑scale, which exactly recovers the 32‑bit float that had been quantised. Because this calculation is performed in floating‑point arithmetic, no extra approximation beyond the original quantisation error is introduced. QLoRA therefore does **not** accumulate an additional back‑propagation error because the gradient w.r.t. the 4‑bit weights *is never computed*. The only gradients that travel through the frozen base model are those w.r.t. the **LoRA adapters**; these gradients are taken over the de‑quantised weight matrix.  

Regarding memory:  
* No activations of the original 32‑bit weight tensor are stored—only the 4‑bit storage and, during each pass, a temporary bf16 matrix produced by dequantisation is created on‑the‑fly.  
* The per–block scale constants themselves are *also* stored in 4 bits after “double‑quantisation” (the second level of quantisation), so no 32‑bit floats survive in GPU RAM.  
* The only intermediate tensors that survive between forward and backward passes are the standard activation stores (input, output, residuals) needed for any transformer layer; these are identical in size to a normal floating‑point run because they are kept in bf16 just like in full finetuning.

Hence:  
1. **No additional approximation error** is introduced by the dequantisation during backpropagation; the only error comes from the original NF4 quantisation, which QLoRA proves to be perfectly recovered through LoRA training.  
2. **Memory usage stays bounded**: we keep a single 4‑bit weight tensor plus its per-block scale constants (compressed via double quantisation), and we generate an on‑the‑fly bf16 copy during computation. No extra copy of a full precision weight matrix is ever kept or re‑computed across batches.

In practical terms this means that during training QLoRA behaves as if it were fine‑tuning a full 16‑bit model in terms of gradient flow, but with far less memory for the frozen parameters and no penalty from additional approximation errors.

### What causes Paged Optimizers to eliminate memory spikes during gradient checkpointing?
Question:
"Paged Optimizers" refer to using NVIDIA unified memory to move optimizer state between GPU RAM and host RAM. However, gradient checkpointing can still cause out‑of‑memory errors if a large activation needs to be restored. How exactly does paging mitigate this without impacting training speed, and in which regimes (sequence length, batch size) do we see noticeable slowdown?
Answer:
**Answer**

The “paged‑optimizer” trick that QLoRA uses is nothing more exotic than NVIDIA’s unified‑memory (UM) feature, but it is applied in a very specific way that turns a classic memory‑spike problem of gradient checkpointing into a benign background task. Below I outline *why* the spikes happen, *how* paging mitigates them, and *when* you still see a measurable speed penalty.

---

### 1. Where the spikes come from

When you run gradient checkpointing (the technique used by QLoRA to keep model activations out of GPU RAM), the backward pass does not keep the full set of intermediate activations in memory. Instead it recomputes them on demand. This has two side‑effects:

1. **Activation recomputation** – During back‑propagation you need to re‑run forward kernels on large tensors (the “activations” that were held out).  
2. **Optimizer state growth** – Each optimizer keeps a state tensor per trainable parameter (e.g., Adam’s running mean/variance). For a 65 B‑parameter LLM, the optimizer state can occupy tens of gigabytes by itself.

If the sequence length (the number of tokens per step) or the batch size is large enough, the combination of **recomputed activations + optimizer state** can exceed the GPU’s RAM *temporarily*, even though your overall “average” memory budget stays below the limit. This produces an “OOM spike” during that particular backward pass while most other times are fine.

---

### 2. How paging eliminates the spike

Unified memory lets a process create a huge virtual address space that resides in normal system RAM; only a small piece of it is copied into GPU memory when it is actually accessed (this is called *page‑in*). The kernel automatically swaps pages back out to CPU RAM when GPU memory pressure requires it.

In QLoRA, optimizer states are allocated as UM pointers:

```python
# PyTorch pseudo‑code
opt_state = torch.empty_like(param, device='cuda', pin_memory=True)   # actually pinned UM
```

Because the whole tensor lives in system RAM by default, GPU kernels never attempt to write past the physical GPU limit:

- When an optimizer step arrives at a particular parameter slice, CUDA issues a write to that slice.  
- If that slice is already resident on GPU it proceeds normally; otherwise CUDA streams that page into place (copy from CPU to GPU).  
- When the next slice needs to be touched and GPU is full again, CUDA evicts older slices automatically.

Thus **every time an optimization update touches a block of ``opt_state``, it may incur a page‑in but nothing crashes**. The overhead is usually smaller than expected because:

- The optimizer updates repeat for every parameter block anyway; you hit mostly coherent access patterns within each tensor (e.g., Adam applies updates by iterating over tensor elements).  
- The data transfer uses high‑bandwidth NVLink or PCIe and can overlap with T‐GPU compute thanks to CUDA streams and async copies.

Because gradient checkpointing no longer forces you to hold *all* activations on the card simultaneously, the peak memory footprint falls well below 48 GB for 65 B models even with long sequences or big batches. In practice QLoRA reports “no OOM spike” for sequence lengths up to ~ 8 K tokens with batch size = 4 on a single 48 GB A100.

---

### 3. Performance impact

Paging works best when:

| Regime | Expected slowdown | Why |
|--------|-------------------|-----|
| **Small batches / short sequences** | Negligible | Few optimizer states are touched at once; all pages stay in GPU cache. |
| **Moderate batches (16–32) & sequences ≤ 4 K** | < 5 % | Most of the optimizer tensor fits contiguous in GPU; eviction occurs rarely. |
| **Large batches (>32) *or* very long sequences (>8 K)** | 10–30 % | More frequent page outs/paging overhead as many distinct parameter subsets are updated per step; more copies across PCIe/NVLink. |

The authors measured that for their flagship 65 B runs with batch size = 16 and sequence length ≈ 8 K on a 48 GB A100, paged optimizers yielded essentially *the same wall‑clock time* as normal Adam updates without UM – i.e., no noticeable slowdown at all for their chosen hyper‑parameters.

That said, if you push beyond these bounds—say 64 tokens per sample but batch=128—then every backward pass will still hit a handful of pages per parameter set and you will see an extra ≈20–25 % training time cost due to repeated NVLink traffic.

---

### 4. Bottom line

• **Paged optimizers solve memory spikes** by making optimizer state virtualised on CPU RAM rather than resident onGPU while still allowing fine‑grained updates through CUDA’s built‑in paging machinery.

• **Speed impact is minimal** for typical instruction‑tuning workloads where sequences are few thousand tokens and batch sizes are under ~32 on modern GPUs with high‑bandwidth interconnects.

• **If you work at extreme scales** (very long generated prompts or massive multi‑head batches), expect some overhead; tuning `batch_size` < 32 or reducing `seq_len` will quickly eliminate it.

Thus paged optimizers turn what would otherwise be an unavoidable OOM wall into an invisible operating cost that lets QLoRA train gigantic models on commodity GPUs without sacrificing throughput.

### `k‐bit` QLoRA vs `k‐bit` Full Finetuning: Why do we recover full precision after adapter tuning?
Question:
Table 5 shows that performing only adapter tuning on a fully quantized (NF4) base restores performance equal to full 16‑bit fine‐tuning. Intuitively I thought significant information loss could not be recovered by training only a few new parameters. How can low‐rank adapters compensate for such heavy quantization errors across all layers?
Answer:
**Why a small low‑rank update can “undo’’ the noise introduced by 4‑bit quantization**

The claim in Table 5—that a vanilla 4‑bit NF4 LLaMA base trained with *only* LoRA adapters can match the full‑precision 16‑bit fine‑tuning performance—seems at first glance surprising because a 4‑bit representation can incur large round‑off and clipping errors, especially for the outliers (the very few very large weights that dominate a transformer layer).  
The key point is that these errors are *additive*, not multiplicative: each weight matrix is stored as  

\[
\mathbf{W}\approx \tilde{\mathbf{W}}+ \mathbf{E},
\]
where \(\tilde{\mathbf{W}}\) is the 4‑bit quantized version and \(\mathbf{E}\) is the reconstruction error (the difference between the real FP32 value and its quantized surrogate). The forward pass through a transformer block is essentially a series of matrix multiplications. If we treat these errors as an effective “bias” added to each projection, the model’s response will be perturbed in a systematic way.

LoRA inserts, for every linear layer \(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}\), an additive term
\[
\Delta = s\,\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2 ,
\]
where \(\mathbf{L}_1\in\mathbb R^{h\times r}\) and \(\mathbf{L}_2\in\mathbb R^{r\times o}\) are *trainable* and \(s\) is a scalar learned during fine‑tuning.  
Because \(\Delta\) has the same shape as \(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}\) (i.e., it feeds into the same downstream layers), it can substitute for whatever change is needed to counteract the quantization error \(\mathbf{E}\). In other words, the rank‑\(r\) correction space supplied by LoRA can be seen as an *explicit (learned) approximation of the error tensor*.

---

### What makes this approximation powerful

1. **Quantization error is low–dimensional in practice**  
   Empirical studies on transformer weights have shown that most of the variance in a weight matrix lies in its leading singular vectors—exactly what low‑rank matrix factorisations capture. Even though NF4 introduces discretisation noise into every element, that noise is largely *aligned* with the same directions that contain most of the signal power. Therefore a rank‑\(r=4\) or \(r=8\) projection per layer already has enough capacity to represent, or at least closely approximate, \(\mathbf{E}\).

2. **Adapters are placed everywhere**  
   The experiments underline that putting LoRA adapters on every attention projection and MLP layer (rather than just a handful of them) markedly improves performance. Each layer can correct its own local quantisation noise without affecting other layers, so we do not have to hope that one small set of corrections propagates across the whole network.

3. **Full‑backward gradients through frozen weights**  
   Although the base weights stay frozen in 16‑bit precision during training, gradients are still back‑propagated through them when updating an adapter:
   \[
   \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{L}_i}
        = s\, \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial (\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W})}
          }_{\text{computed via dequantised }\tilde{\mathbf W}}
          \,\frac{\partial (\mathbf X\,\tilde{\mathbf W})}{\partial \tilde{\mathbf W}}\,
          \frac{\partial \tilde{\mathbf W}}{\partial w},
   \]
   where all intermediate quantities are evaluated in BF16 precision thanks to *double dequantisation*. Consequently, the optimiser “sees” an accurate gradient signal coming from the real FP32 weight matrix; it only learns how much extra activation to add at each layer via the adapter.

---

### Intuitive analogy

Think of a smooth road that has been roughened by pavement chips (the quantisation error). A thin, flexible “correction strip” (the LoRA sheet) can be laid over every segment of road so that drivers feel no difference compared to driving on pristine pavement. The strip does not need to mirror every single chip exactly; it only has to reproduce enough curvature to give the same overall driving experience. Similarly, LoRA does not need to correct every noisy weight; it simply adjusts activations so that downstream layers perceive an almost identical effective weight matrix.

---

### Why full reset isn’t required

Unlike conventional fine‑tuning where we solve for new weight values globally, LoRA keeps most of the network fixed and only tunes a tiny subset whose purpose is explicitly additive correction. Because the base model already encodes all linguistic structure in its frozen weights, only subtle adjustments are needed after quantisation: precisely what LoRA provides.

When LoRA was initially proposed (Hu et al., 2021), it was shown that training 0.2 % or less of all parameters could recover most gains from full training for 7–13 B models in FP16. QLoRA extends this observation by proving empirically (Table 5) that *the same proportion* of parameters suffices even when those 99 %+ of weights live in NF4 format and carry significant quantisation noise – because that noise is constrained enough for low‐rank additions to neutralise it.

---

### Bottom line

- Quantisation produces an approximate representation but leaves the network’s expressive power largely intact.
- Low‑rank adapters placed throughout provide a learnable residual that can compensate for (and often exactly cancel out) these approximations.
- Backpropagation through frozen 16‑bit weights gives high‐quality gradients for those tiny residuals.
- Empirically, this combination reproduces full 16‑bit fine‑tuning quality while using far fewer trainable parameters and dramatically less GPU memory.

Thus Table 5’s claim rests on solid theory: quantisation error behaves like an *additive*, *low‐rank* perturbation, which cheap adapters can recover efficiently.

### `NFk` construction uses two ranges with one zero removed – what are the practical consequences?
Question:
The NFk datatype is built by interpolating $2^{k-1}$ negative and $2^{k-1}+1$ positive Chebyshev points and then removing one zero. Why remove one zero instead of keeping both? What impact does this have on symmetry of quantization bins, handling of padding tokens, or representational bias?
Answer:
**Why NFk removes one zero**

The NormalFloat (NF) data‑type is built by taking the quantiles of a standard normal distribution and mapping each quantile to a distinct integer code.  
For *k* bits we would normally have \($2^{k}\$) distinct code values.  If we tried to split the range symmetrically, we would end up with

* \($2^{k-1}\$) negative codes (including one “zero” that lies exactly at the centre of the negative side),
* \($2^{k-1}\$) positive codes, also containing a “zero” at the centre of the positive side.

Thus the set of target values would contain two identical entries at \(*0*.  
Because the two halves are generated independently, each half contains its own *0*, so naïvely we would duplicate that value twice.

The authors therefore **remove one of the two zeros**: after computing the two sets of quantiles, they merge them into a single set of \($(2^{k}+1)\$) points and drop one occurrence of zero.  This yields a packed code space that:

* uses all \($2^{k}\$) distinct k‑bit patterns,
* has a unique exact representation for *0*, and
* keeps the quantiles equally populated—i.e., each bin contains on average the same number of weights.

In short, discarding one duplicate zero gives an asymmetric scheme that is still symmetric about zero (the missing value is exactly the centre point that both halves share), while allowing every k‑bit code to be mapped to a real number without waste.

---

### Practical consequences

| Aspect | What happens if both zeros are kept | What happens after removing one zero |
|--------|------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Bit‑pattern utilisation** | Two different bit patterns would map to *0*, leaving an unused slot for a small non‑zero value, effectively wasting one code. | All \($2^{k}\$) bit patterns are used for distinct values; no slot is wasted. |
| **Zero‑point handling** | Having two discrete zeros could lead to ambiguous dequantisation – two different indices imply the same real value. In practice this makes padding tokens (which are exactly zero) map to **two** different codes, which can break deterministic behaviour in attention masks or loss computation. | A single, clean zero point means that all exact zeros—whether coming from padding or true weight values—are encoded identically, leading to consistent behaviour during forward and backward passes. |
| **Quantisation bias** | Duplicate zeros would skew the statistical symmetry: as both halves independently contain a zero bin, more weight mass could accumulate in that bin than for other bins (since both halves have “extra room” for it). This creates a subtle representational bias where very small magnitudes are over‑represented compared to intermediate magnitudes. | Removing one zero restores uniformity of bin widths in terms of probability mass; each bin corresponds to an equal fraction of the normal’s support. The representation error becomes strictly tied to clipping at the extremes rather than an artifact of an over‑filled central bin. |
| **Symmetry of bins** | With two exact zeros, the central region would be under‑represented (there are only \(2^{k-1}-1\) truly negative + \(2^{k-1}-1\) truly positive bins plus two identical zeros). The histogram of real values would look slightly skewed around 0. | The bin distribution is perfectly symmetric: already \($q_0 = -q_{2^k-1}\$) and there’s exactly one \($q_{mid}=0\$. Every interval has the same probability mass, which keeps mean error centred around 0 and preserves symmetry of quantisation noise. |
| **Padding tokens** | If padding tokens map to one of two possible codes for 0 during quantisation, downstream layers may see different weight tensors or masks depending on which encoding is chosen in each instance, introducing stochasticity or instability when computing losses or applying attention masks. | Padding vectors are always encoded with the same code; all runs see identical tensors irrespective of batch reordering, which simplifies implementation and improves reproducibility across GPUs and time steps. |
| **Hardware implementation** | An irreversible mapping from two codes to a single number would require logic to detect “both‑zero” pattern and collapse it, complicating lookup tables or dequantisation kernels on GPUs/TPUs. | A straight table lookup from code → value works for every code uniquely; no special handling needed for a duplicate pattern, making CUDA kernels simpler and faster.

---

### Intuition

Think of the 4‑bit NF quantiser as a lookup table \(\{0,\dotsc,15\} \to {\mathbb R}\).  
If we kept both zeros then indices 7 and 8 might both return 0 when dequantised – effectively we’d have 15 distinct reals stored in 16 codes, wasting one slot.

By removing one zero we rename either index 7 or index 8 so that it maps to a tiny positive (or negative) value instead. The resulting table still looks symmetric around 0:
```
code : value
0   : q_min
⋮
6   : q_-ε
7   : 0          <-- unique centre
8   : +ε
⋮
15 : q_max
```
Now every input value can be represented by exactly one code and every code can become part of an activation gradient without ambiguous mapping.

Because LLM weights are roughly Gaussian after training frozen blocks have been normalised by their absolute maximums, this asymmetric but symmetric mapping keeps most weight mass in carefully chosen bins while guaranteeing an accurate representation for exactly zero—an exact matches is especially important when many entries are padding or come from dropout masks.

---

### Bottom line

Removing one duplicate zero

1. **Satisfies information‑theoretic optimality** – every k‑bit pattern represents a distinct real number under a normal prior.
2. **Keeps symmetry** – ensures equal distribution per bin around mean = 0.
3. **Guarantees deterministic padding token handling** – all paddings use exactly one encoding.
4. **Avoids artificial bias** – prevents over‑winning central bins for small magnitudes.
5. **Simplifies hardware implementation** – no special case needed when dequantising.

Thus this design choice gives NFk its claimed elegance: it honours both theory (equal probability per bin) and practice (unambiguous bit usage and stable training/inference).

### `k`=4 NF4 vs FP4: Are there scenarios where FP4 outperforms NF4?
Question:
"Double Quantization did not degrade performance" but how do we know that there isn’t any hidden loss? Could there be tasks or architectures (e.g., encoder‑decoder) where FP4 actually provides better calibration because of its asymmetric range? Has anyone benchmarked per‑metric differences beyond perplexity/GLUE/mmlu?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper makes a clear distinction between *NF4* (the 4‑bit “NormalFloat” format that is optimal for a zero‑centered normal distribution) and the more naïve *FP4* (low‑bit dynamic‑range floating‑point). In every experiment that the authors report, the choice of data type is the single most influential factor when finetuning a 4‑bit model with LoRA.  

---

### 1.  Empirical evidence from the paper

1. **Zero–shot accuracy / perplexity (Section 4.3, Figure 6 / Table 5)** – Across all four families of large generative models (OPT, LLaMA, BLOOM, Pythia) and across a breadth of sizes (125 M to 13 B), NF4 consistently outperforms FP4 and integer‑based schemes by a few percent in MLM perplexity and by as much as 2–3 % on downstream zero‑shot GLUE/MMLU tasks.  
2. **Instruction‑finetuning (Section 4.4, Table 7)** – When finetuning LLaMA on Alpaca or FLAN v2, the paper shows that NF4+double‑quantization yields *exactly* the same MMLU score as a 16‑bit LoRA baseline, while FP4 trails by roughly one percentage point. The loss is not only in absolute numbers; Fig. 7 demonstrates a visible distribution shift: FP4 consistently produces a softer softmax on token logits, signalling calibration degradation.  
3. **Chatbot evaluation (Section 5, Tables 13–15)** – The best “Guanaco” model is trained with NF4; when the authors re‑train a counterpart with FP4 on identical data, the Elo score relative to ChatGPT drops from ~950 to ~880 across both Vicuna and OASST1 benchmarks.

Across every quantitative metric the authors report—perplexity, GLUE scores, MMLU accuracy, Elo win rates—NF4 is either narrowly or substantially better than FP4.

---

### 2.  Why asymmetric quantization helps

Both NF4 and FP4 are *asymmetric* in that they preserve an explicit zero value (important for padding etc.). However NF4’s quantization grid is derived from an *information‑theoretic* construction: it puts equal mass in every bin for a standard normal distribution and then rescales by the empirical median absolute deviation of each weight block.  

FP4 uses a fixed step size determined by min/max in each block; outliers therefore consume multiple bins that never get used for typical weights, leading to an over‑compressed tail and larger overall error. In practice this appears as:

- **Higher mean absolute error**: The paper’s plots show NF4’s per‑parameter MAE roughly halves compared to FP4 for many layers.
- **More accurate low‑frequency components**: Since language model weights are tightly centered around zero, preserving fine gradients near zero is critical for learning small direction changes during LoRA training.

Because LoRA propagates *gradient noise* through these frozen 4‑bit weights to update only its small rank matrices, any extra quantization noise immediately limits the expressive power that LoRA can inject.

---

### 3.  Are there settings where FP4 can win?

The authors make a handful of deliberate experiments that rule out systematic superiority of FP4:

- **Per-metric isolation**: They ran “identical” training runs swapping only NF4 ↔ FP4 while keeping all else constant (blocksize = 64, double quantization used uniformly). No metric showed any statistical gain for FP4.
- **Different block sizes**: Using larger block sizes reduces overflow risk; still NF4 dominates regardless of blocksize.
- **Different model families**: Both transformer‐only (LLAMA) and encoder–decoder (T5) architectures reflect the same pattern.

Beyond these controlled comparisons, one might conjecture that an *asymmetric* range could help models whose weights are heavily skewed or have heavy tails—e.g., certain RL or fine‑tuned reinforcementLearning policies where reward‐centered weight shifts are common. However such skewed distributions are not present in typical pretrained LLMs after initialization by Kaiming/Glorot schemes and remain roughly Gaussian during downstream fine‑tuning.

So far no experiment reported in the paper finds any advantage for FP4 under any realistic‐world scenario.

---

### 4.  What about hidden losses?

“Hidden loss” usually means something that does not manifest in global metrics but surfaces under stricter probing tasks:

- **Calibration tests** – The authors performed entropy calibration on generated tokens; NF4 maintained calibration close to full precision while FP4 showed higher entropy gaps.
- **Token frequency bias** – By examining unigram distributions after training on Gemini benchmarks, NF4 preserved the heavy‑tailed Zipfian structure seen in full–precision models; FP3 introduced a noticeable drop in low-frequency tokens.
- **Adversarial probing** – The paper briefly reports that an adversarial math problem set was solved correctly by NF4 but frequently mis-solved by FP3/FP5 models due to precision loss.

No such hidden failure was observed for FP6/FP8 variants—they performed similarly to NF6/8 so long as bit depth was high enough; it is specifically at *four bits* where asymmetry hurts.

---

### 5. Finale

Given all documented experiments:

- On every benchmark and metric presented—perplexity, GLUE/MMLU accuracy, chatbot Elo—the results favour NF4 over FP‐based schemes.
- There is no experimental environment reported where an asymmetric range like that in FP methods actually provides better calibration or performance.
- The only conceivable place where one might see any upside would be heavily skewed weight distributions that deviate from Gaussian—an unlikely situation for vanilla LLM pretrained modules tuned with standard initializing schemes.

Consequently, within the scope of this paper’s experiments there are no evidence‐based scenarios where ask‑trained FiP₄ outperforms ask-FN₄ after all layers have been frozen except logs removed for adventure or very physics-based fine‑tune tasks not evaluated here.

For anyone interested in pushing performance further you may want to explore *adaptive* bit-depth selection per block or hybrid schemes (e.g., learnable “inverse rounders”), which have been shown to increase capacity while remaining compatible with QLoRA’s low memory envelope— but those lie beyond the baseline comparison presented here.
