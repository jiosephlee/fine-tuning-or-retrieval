\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Unleashing Math Power: How DeepSeekMath’s 120 B Web Corpus Turns the Internet Into a Knowledge Base

For journalists, engineers, and data‑engineering students alike, the headline “Large‑Language Models Still Hanker for Math” paints a familiar picture: GPT‑4, Gemini‑Ultra, and their peers can write prose but struggle with step‑by‑step proofs or grading algebraic calculations.  
DeepSeekMath flips that narrative by showing that *everything* you need to learn math is already on the web – if you know how to find it.

Below we unpack the data‑collection pipeline that turned Common Crawl into a 120 billion‑token **math corpus**, explore why web data beats arXiv, and highlight the clever decontamination that keeps benchmarks unbiased.

---

## 1. The Challenge: Pulling Math Out of the Noise

Common Crawl is a treasure trove: every month it packs 240 TB of HTML crawled from around the globe. The raw volume looks impressive, but two problems loom:

1. **Signal vs. Noise** – most pages contain no math at all; even pages that do use inline LaTeX can be noisy or irrelevant.
2. **Benchmark Contamination** – large LLMs can overfit on evaluation datasets (e.g., GSM8K, MATH). If the training set contains exact passages from those problems, the score story becomes meaningless.

To extract clean math while keeping diversity high, DeepSeekMath built an *iterative selector* and filtered pipeline.

---

## 2. FastText Selector – The First Filter

FastText is a lightweight word‑embedding model that also works as a text classifier. The steps were:

1. **Seed Corpus** – OpenWebMath (13.6 B tokens) served as ground truth positives.
2. **Negative Sample** – 500 k random HTML pages from Common Crawl ensured the model learned what *isn’t* math.
3. **Training** – With 256‑dimembeds, 3‑gram vocabulary and 3 epochs, FastText scored every page in a 40 B token subset of Common Crawl.
4. **Top‑K Selection** – Pages with scores above a threshold were taken into the next round.

After the first run, only ~40 B tokens survived—still a huge fraction of webpages but many promising math pages were missing because the positive example set was too narrow.

---

## 3. Iterative Enrichment Loop

To expand coverage without overfitting:

1. **Domain Analysis** – Each URL’s *base domain* (e.g., `mathoverflow.net`) was examined for its “math density” (percentage of pages hit by the first selector). Domains >10 % were flagged as *math domains*.
2. **Manual Spot‑Check** – Domain leads (like `mathoverflow.net/questions`) were checked manually; missing pages were added back into the seed set.
3. **Retraining** – The FastText model saw an enlarged positive set and retrained; it could now pick up previously missed sections such as forum threads or lecture notes.
4. **Repeat** – The cycle ran four times total. After each round we measured training performance on growing token budgets (40B → 80B → 120B → 160B) to decide when to stop.

Result: after four rounds we harvested **35.5 M URLs → ~120 B tokens**, roughly seven times larger than Minerva’s web collection and nine times larger than OpenWebMath.

---

## 4. Multi‑Lingual Advantage

Unlike many earlier corpora built primarily from arXiv PDFs (mostly English), DeepSeekMath’s webpage basis naturally includes content in dozens of languages – English and Chinese together account for >70 % of tokens; dozens of others fill out the rest.

Why does this help?

- **Language Diversity Improves Generalization** – When Meta-based models are fine‑tuned on monolingual data, they often underperform on underrepresented languages.
- **Metric Gap Closure** – Chinese benchmarks (CMATH, Gaokao-MathQA) showed performance rises of up to +12 % relative to English‑only pre‑training corpora.
- **Practical Impact** – DeepSeekMath base outperforms larger closed models on Chinese Math benchmarks because it never had to “learn” Chinese through translation or synthetic data.

---

## 5. Contamination Prevention: Keeping Benchmarks Clean

Any claim about beating MATH or GSM8K requires absolute certainty that no part of those datasets leaked into training:

1. **Exact N-Gram Blocking** – Any text fragment containing a ten‑word exact match with any benchmark passage was excised.
2. **Shorter Bad Words** – For shorter passages (<10 words) we performed three‑gram exact matches; if found, entire page dropped.
3. **Full Page Rejection** – In rare cases an entire webpage consisted solely of benchmark content (e.g., downloaded question archives); those went outright.

The result? Even after crawling all four iterations, only <0.01 % of pages were rejected purely due to contamination—so almost all math power came from fresh internet content.

---

## 6. Why Web Data Becomes Superior to arXiv Papers

ArXiv is undeniably rich with advanced mathematics—but:

- It is heavily dominated by LaTeX formatted research papers that often include dense symbolic notation rather than step‑by‑step reasoning.
- Preprocessing arXiv files preserves macros and author commentary that confuses tokenizers or create overly technical instructions.
- When trained exclusively on arXiv material (even in cleaned Form like `ArXiv-RedPajama`), evaluations on quantitative problems did not improve; sometimes they degraded in class tests such as GSM8K or MATH.

In contrast:

- Web pages such as educational blogs, Q&A sites (`math.stackexchange.com`), online textbooks (`openstax.org`) naturally provide *solutions* written in plain English plus occasionally added code snippets or diagrams.
- The web dataset is inherently multilingual: non-English problem statements appear verbatim for local audiences—an essential factor for models working globally.
- A web corpus can be immediately filtered for contamination; arXiv PDFs cannot be trivially split into clean chunks without laborious manual review.

Thus, building a large‐scale math corpus directly from public web traffic yields higher quality step‑by‑step solutions —the kind LLMs learn to rediscover themselves during downstream fine‐tuning—without relying on symbolic equation templates alone.

## 7. Resulting Impact: From Numbers to Real Math Power

A quick look at numbers highlights the payoff:

| Model | GSM8K (CoT) | MATH Open​ | CMATH |
|------|-------------|------------|-------|
| Mistral 7B | ~30 % | ~9 % | ~18 % |
| OpenAI GPT‑4 | ~90 % | ~75 % | — |
| DeepSeekMath Base 7B | ~36 % | ~36 % | ~46 % |
| DeepSeekMath–Instruct 7B | ~44 % | **~50 %** | ~58 % |

After instruction fine‑tuning this base model with hundreds of thousands of human–annotated solution pairs (both chain‐of‐thought and program‐of‐thought), DeepSeekMath reaches a *competition–level* score on MATH —the closest open‐source achievement to GPT‑4 and Gemini Ultra so far —all without external toolkits or ensemble voting.

---

## 8. Takeaway for Researchers & Practitioners

1. **Start Where Everyone Else Reads** – If you want a high‑quality math dataset, crawl public webpages rather than rely solely on arXiv PDFs.
2. **Iterate Your Selector** – A single fastText pass is too narrow; iteratively enrich your seed until you hit diminishing returns (~90–95 % coverage).
3. **Language Matters** – Including multilingual content can dramatically lift benchmark scores across non-English datasets without extra curation effort.
4. **Leak Detection Is Critical** – Even a small contamination can inflate scores; strict ngram filtering is inexpensive but indispensable.

With these principles someone else could follow suit—reproduce DeepSeekMath’s performance with less compute and data than most competing closed models—and potentially beat them across more mathematics challenges in the future.
