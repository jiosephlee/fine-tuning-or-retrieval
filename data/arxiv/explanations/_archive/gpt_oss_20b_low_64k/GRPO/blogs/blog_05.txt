\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Beyond Numbers: Why Web Pages Beat ArXiv for Mathematical LLM Pre‑Training

Modern large language models (LLMs) are no longer polite chatbots; they are high‑street math tutors that can solve algebra, calculus, and even formal geometry proofs on demand.  For that to happen, the model has to see a *lot* of math‑heavy text during pre‑training – everything from textbook expositions to StackExchange answers to research papers.  The obvious place to look is arXiv: it is a treasure trove of peer‑reviewed theoretical work, often written in dense LaTeX.  Yet recent work on **DeepSeekMath** shows that a carefully curated web‑crawl corpus outgrows arXiv by a wide margin.  This post digs into why that is so, unpacking the differences in data pipelines, token distribution, and the unintended biases that lurk inside arXiv.

## 1. The two camps: MathPile/RedPajama vs DeepSeekMath

| Data | Source | Tokens (≈) | Governance | Training focus |
|------|--------|------------|-----------|----------------|
| **MathPile** | Textbooks + Wikipedia + ProofWiki + CommonCrawl + arXiv + StackExchange | 8.9 B | Mixed quality; heuristics for filtering |
| **RedPajama** (arXiv subset) | Skribbling from arXiv LaTeΧ files (all but preambles/comments/macro sections) | 28 B | Strict removal of boilerplate |
| **DeepSeekMath** | FastText‑filtered CommonCrawl web pages (≈ 120 B math tokens) | 120 B | Curated via multi‑step fastText classifier and manual annotation |

> **Bottom line:** Even though RedPajama dedicates more than three times the token count of MathPile to arXiv material, both fail to match the sheer volume and diversity of the DeepSeekMath corpus.

## 2. Token quality in three different pipelines

### 2.1 MathPile’s weighted mix

MathPile samples heavily from arXiv (over 85 % of tokens), padded with textbooks and Wikipedia.  The pipeline applies regex‑based filtering – for example, it drops any text containing more than six consecutive equation tokens or excessive LaTeX macros.  While this keeps the training data tidy, it also excises many *typical* pedagogical passages that are rife with clear step‑by‑step reasoning – exactly what you want LLMs to learn.

### 2.2 RedPajama’s “clean” but shallow paperwork

RedPajama focuses exclusively on arXiv manuscripts, stripping them of LaTeX fragments that are not part of the main text.  This leaves a large chunk of technical proofs (e.g., “Lemma 3.4” followed by a compact proof).  These proofs are precise but terse; they rarely contain motivational language or rich examples that help the model learn how humans actually *explain* math.

### 2.3 DeepSeekMath’s web‑crowd source

DeepSeekMath starts with OpenWebMath – a hand‑picked set of high‑quality math pages – and trains a fastText classifier to pull in new pages from CommonCrawl with similar stylistic fingerprints (math heavy punctuation, symbols like `$\sqrt{ }$`, `$\int$`, frequent use of terms such as “derivative”, “proof”, etc.).  The pipeline has four key steps:

1. **Seed selection** – hundreds of thousands of positive examples improve the classifier iteratively.
2. **Near–Duplication pruning** – removes near‑identical pages found across multiple domains.
3. **Benchmark decontamination** – any sub‑string overlap with existing evaluation datasets is excised.
4. **Language balancing** – retains English, Chinese, and other languages in proportion to their real traffic.

The outcome is a gigantic, multilingual corpus where every page contains *concentrated* mathematical insight, yet is embedded in natural language exposition typical of blogs or community forums.

## 3. Three reasons Web Pages Surpass ArXiv

### A) Natural language richness

ArXiv papers are written with an academic audience in mind: concise sentences peppered with dense notation and only occasional explanatory narratives. By contrast, community‐hosted math forums feature everyday conversational language (“I think” or “what about” etc.), side remarks (“thanks for the hint”), and more verbose reasoning steps. When an LLM sees such patterns during training, it learns *how* to phrase explanations in plain English—a critical skill for generating step‑by‑step CoT solutions.

### B) Structural variety

Web pages cover everything from short code snippets answering a question (`def factorial(n): …`) to long blog posts describing historical development or applications of a theorem. This structural heterogeneity forces the model to generalize across disparate contexts (code vs prose), fostering better transfer ability when performing program‐of‐thought reasoning later in fine–tuning.

### C) Free from academic gatekeeping

ArXiv imposes its own style guide: equations must be presented in counter‑aligned blocks; citations follow a particular LaTeX macro style; authors use \verb|\begin{theorem}| constructs that don't map easily to natural language flow. The DeepSeekMath pipeline freely adopts whatever formatting appears on CommonCrawl – sometimes even Markdown headers like `### Proof` – giving the model exposure to more diverse tokenization cues that surface during inference.

## 4. The impact on downstream tasks

When trained on the DeepSeekMath corpus (120 B tokens), even a modestly sized `7B` model (“DeepSeekMath-Base”) beats GPT‑4 on several public benchmarks:

| Benchmark | Size | DeepSeekMath-Base (%) | GPT-4 / Gemini Ultra (%) |
|-----------|------|----------------------|--------------------------|
| GSM8K     | 7B   | 88·2 %               | 91·7 %                   |
| MATH      | 7B   | 51·7 %               | ~55–60 %                 |

In comparison, models trained solely on MathPile or RedPajama *match* or *fall below* smaller published models like Mistral–7B or InternLM2–20B when it comes to self‐contained CoT solutions without tool invocation.

## 5. Future hybrid strategies

While web data currently dominates performance gains, it isn’t without pitfalls:

- **Noise & duplication** – even after near–deduplication, generic sites can host boilerplate templates.
- **Bias toward popular domains** – if most scraped pages come from StackOverflow jokes rather than rigorous proofs.

A promising hybrid approach could combine:

1. **Web‑derived mathematics** for general reasoning flair.
2. **Lean scientific papers** focused on breakthrough theory but subjected to a stricter human sanity check.
3. **Interactive datasets** (e.g., notebooks from Kaggle or Colab) that couple code execution and narrative explanation.

Aligning these sources under an RL loop (e.g., Group Relative Policy Optimization) could balance learning signals from both high‐volume web content and deeply technical literature.

## 6. Takeaways for practitioners

- Don’t assume “academic depth = training quality.”  
- Use large‑scale web crawling combined with intelligent filtering; histories show this yields better stepwise reasoning than heavy reliance on arXiv alone.
- Validate downstream performance on *both* English and non‑English benchmarks; multilingual arithmetic often benefits from broader source diversity.
- Future LLMs will likely blend web corpora with curated science texts under reinforcement learning regimes that reward correct reasoning at each token step.

In short: When building high-performing math LLMs today, your attention wall should be pointing at giant volumes of well-selected web pages—learn how the community talks about numbers—and not just at black‐box PDFs dutifully submission into preprint servers. Webness proves that *how* we encounter mathematics matters as much as *what* we encounter it about.
