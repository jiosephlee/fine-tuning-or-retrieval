\title{Blogs about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

# The New Scaling Law: When 1‑Bit Models Beat 16‑Bit Counterparts

Large Language Models (LLMs) have become the benchmark for what can be achieved with deep neural networks, but their rapid growth in size is also inflaming concerns about compute cost, energy use, and ecological impact. A recently published study challenges the conventional wisdom that higher precision is always better by showing a new **1‑bit scaling law**: a modest 3‑billion‐parameter model compressed to ternary weights outperforms a 70‑billion‑parameter full‑precision (FP16) LLM in both speed and memory, while matching or exceeding its accuracy.

In this post we walk through those surprising results, explain the intuition behind why 1‑bit models can scale better, and discuss what this could mean for the future of LLM research.

## 1. What’s Inside a 1‑bit LLM?

The family of **BitNet** models replaces every standard `nn.Linear` layer in a Transformer with a **BitLinear** block that uses only three possible weight values: –1, 0, or +1.  
The extra “0” gives a *tiny* extra bit of representational power relative to pure binary weights; the resulting *1.58‑bit* representation is written as:

```
W̃ = RoundClip( W / (γ + ε), -1, 1 )
γ = (1 / nm) ∑|Wij|
```

so each weight is first scaled by the average absolute value of its matrix, then rounded to the nearest integer in {–1, 0, +1}.  
Activations live in 8 bits, which reduces bandwidth dramatically but still stay expressive enough for modern attention mechanisms.

The architecture overall follows the tried‑and‑true *LLaMA* design: no biases, RMSNorm instead of LayerNorm, SwiGLU activations, and rotary embeddings. This synergy lets BitNet stay compatible with popular open‑source toolchains (HuggingFace 🤗 Transformers, vLLM, llama.cpp).

## 2. The Scaling Experiment

### Training Setup

| Model | Size | Precision | Tokens Trained |
|-------|------|-----------|----------------|
| Baseline | 70 B | FP16 (full) | Standard Llama training |
| BitNet b1.58 | up to 70 B | ternary weights + INT8 activations | Same token count as baseline |

Both families were pre‑trained on the same data (RedPajama, ~100 billion tokens where applicable) and evaluated on a battery of zero‑shot benchmarks (ARC‑Easy/Challenge, Winogrande, PIQA, etc.) as well as perplexity on WikiText2/C4.

### Key Findings

- **Perplexity Match at Tiny Size**  
  A *3 B* BitNet b1.58 achieves perplexity comparable to an *FP16* LLaMA model of the same size and even holds up against larger FP16 baselines.

- **Zero‑shot Superiority at Medium Size**  
  A *3.9 B* BitNet outperforms an *FP16* 3 B counterpart on average zero‑shot accuracy while using ~30 % less GPU memory and running ~40 % faster per token.

- **Unprecedented Pareto Improvement at Huge Scale**  
  At *13 B*, *30 B*, and *70 B*, the BitNet variants are consistently faster (up to ×4 speedup), consume less memory (up to ×3 reduction), and consume far less energy (up to ×70 savings in matrix multiply FLOPs). The most striking outcome is that a **3 B BitNet beats a 70 B FP16** model in both throughput and energy consumption while delivering equivalent or better accuracy.

In other words, scaling up precision does not linearly improve performance; instead, as you increase model size under fixed memory budgets or latency constraints it may be cheaper—and sometimes better—to fall back to lower precision.

## 3. Why Does This Happen?

### A New Computation Paradigm

At first glance you might think “less precision should hurt.” But most of an LLM’s cost lies in matrix multiplication—tight loops of floating point multiply–add instructions. When all weights are ternary:

- Multiplications collapse to simple sign flips → **no true multiplier needed**.
- Whole operations become integer addition → **drastically lower energy per operation** on CMOS chips.
- The latency measured on GPUs actually increases because floating point math is heavily optimized; but integer math fits better into predictable execution pipelines.

Hence speed improvements come from cheaper arithmetic rather than algorithmic changes.

### Memory Advantage Drives Latency Gains

Weights live in just three levels instead of 16 bits. For a *large* model where most time is spent shuttling dense layers from host DRAM into on‑chip SRAM/VRAM:

- Bandwidth pressure drops proportionally with weight dimension.
- The same storage can now hold far more parameters → larger virtual model partitions fit entirely on one chip.
- Latency goes down because fewer DRAM fetches are needed during inference – especially critical for long contexts where KV caches would otherwise dominate memory traffic.

### Energy Efficiency Amplifies With Scale

Energy modeling shows that integer addition costs ~0.06 pJ per instruction versus ~0.4–0.5 pJ for FP16 multiply–add pairs on a modern process node (7 nm). The BitNet budget wins by more than an order of magnitude—especially as the proportion of linear layers grows with scale.

## 4. Implications for Future LLM Design

### A Fresh Scaling Law

Remember how original scaling laws related loss/perplexity to parameter count? Here we see an additional dimension: **precision vs cost-efficiency tradeoff**. Empirically:

```
13 B BitNet ≈ Latency / Energy ≈ FP16 3 B
30 B BitNet ≈ Latency / Energy ≈ FP16 7 B
70 B BitNet ≈ Latency / Energy ≈ FP16 13 B
```

Thus you can get the same practical resource headline figures by replacing much larger full‐precision models with smaller ternary ones.

### Design Guidelines for Tomorrow’s Models

1. **Prioritize Model Architectures Friendly to Low Precision** – Vanilseq transformers already work well; attention still needs careful scaling but no radical changes are required beyond quantization-aware training.
2. **Use Knowledge Distillation or Mixed Precision Fine Tuning** – To keep downstream tasks accurate we can fine‐tune ternary weights while protecting critical heads via residual float layers.
3. **Revisit Mixed-Precision Training Pipelines** – Custom backward passes can maintain higher precision gradients while forward passes stay ternary; this balances stability with efficiency.
4. **Explore Hardware Co-design** – Designers should target integer units that specifically accommodate sign–zero sign patterns; e.g., bespoke low-power accumulators or block‐RAM-friendly accumulators.
5. **Apply to MoE & Long Sequence Architectures** – Both are memory bound; shrinking weights shrinks expert footprints dramatically and eases communication overhead.

### Edge & Mobile Democratization

A ‘small’ but powerfully accurate ternary LLM can fit on an edge TPU or even ARM Cortex CPU without sacrificing responsiveness—a game changer for personalized assistants that must run offline while staying energy conscious.

## 5. Open Questions & Next Steps

- **Can we push below “ternary”?** Zero-point-free custom activation scales suggest further compressions—maybe even binary-with-two resistive states or per‑token dynamic scaling.
- **What about non-transformer backbones?** Vision transformers have already benefited from binarized layers; would quaternized language backbones hit similar gains?
- **Long Contexts & KV Caching** — Lowering activation precision from FP32 → INT8 doubles feasible memory budgets—we’re looking at even longer contexts without swap tricks.
- **Hardware Realization Speedups** — The pencil-and-paper analysis shows huge potential but building chips that exploit these signed/zero patterns efficiently will be imperative.

The takeaway? Precision need not be monotonically increasing for better results—and indeed it might be *the opposite*. By embracing half–bit representations and new quantization recipes like those in BitNet b1.58 we unlock energy‐efficient compute bodies that scale well enough to replace humongous full‐precision models without falling apart on accuracy or latency.

Stay tuned—future LLM papers may very well adopt this new paradigm as we search for greener ways to power AI at scale.
