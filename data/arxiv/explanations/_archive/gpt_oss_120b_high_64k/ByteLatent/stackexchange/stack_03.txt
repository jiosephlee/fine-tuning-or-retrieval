\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

### How does the average patch size affect both training context length and inference FLOP budget?
Question:
The paper mentions that longer patches reduce inference FLOPs because the latent transformer runs fewer steps, yet they also adjust the byte sequence length so that the expected number of bytes per batch stays constant. Can someone clarify how changing average patch size $\bar{p}$ impacts (a) the effective context length seen by the global transformer and (b) the total FLOPs per byte as defined in Equation (2) in Section 4.3?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
When the average patch size $\ bar⁠p$ grows we keep the *number of raw bytes* that enter a batch essentially fixed (the paper explicitly equalises “bytes‑per‑batch” across experiments). Because a “patch” now contains more bytes, the same $n_{\text​{ctx}}$ bytes are represented by fewer patches  

$$
n_p=\frac{\,n_{\text​{ctx}}}{\,\ bar⁠p}\quad(\text​{in expectation}),
$$  

and therefore  

*the global latent transformer sees a shorter contextual window measured in patches*  
($\,n_p$ tokens instead of the previous $\,n_{\text​{ctx}}/\hspace{-0.3em}\bigl(\hspace{-0.3em}\text​{old }\:\ bar⁠p\bigr)$). In practice this means that at any generation step the block‑causal attention mask covers only $\,n_p$ previous latent states rather than a larger set, even though the underlying byte‑level history is unchanged.  

*The total flop cost per byte drops roughly proportionally to $1/\ bar⁠p$.*
Equation (2) gives the full flop count for one forward–backward pass
