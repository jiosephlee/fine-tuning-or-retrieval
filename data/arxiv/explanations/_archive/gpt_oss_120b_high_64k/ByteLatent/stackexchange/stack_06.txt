\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

### How is Bits‑Per‑Byte (BPB) computed from cross‑entropy loss, and why is it preferred over perplexity for byte models?
Question:
In Section 4.2 BPB is defined as $\text{BPB}(x)=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\mathbf{x})}{\ln(2) \cdot n_{\text{bytes}}}$. I understand this normalizes cross‑entropy by number of bytes, but I'm unclear about the conversion factor $\ln(2)$. Is this simply converting nats to bits? Also, does BPB allow direct comparison between tokenized LLMs (with different vocabulary sizes) and byte LLMs?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the paper the authors use **Bits‑Per‑Byte (BP / B)** as their main language‑modeling quality metric instead of the more familiar *perplexity*.  
The definition they give in § 4.2  

\[
\operatorname{BPB}(x)=
   \frac{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CE}}(\mathbf{x})}
        {\ln(2)\;n_{\text{bytes}}}
   \tag{1}
\]

is just a change of units applied to the ordinary cross‑entropy loss.  
Below I explain each part of (1), why the factor $\ln(2)$ appears, and why this metric lets us compare a byte‑level model with a sub‑word/tokenizer‑based model.

---

### 1️⃣ From cross‑entropy (nats) to bits  

When an LLM is trained it usually minimizes the **cross‑entropy**

\[
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CE}}(\mathbf{x})=
   -%
   \sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\log p_{\!\theta}(x_i\,|\,x_{<i}),
   \tag{2}
\]

where *log* denotes the natural logarithm $\ln$.  
Because $\ln$ is used, the loss has units of **nats** (natural units of information).

In information theory we often want the loss expressed in **bits**, i.e.\ using a base‑2 logarithm:

\[
-\sum_i \log_{2} p_{\!\theta}(x_i\,|\,x_{<i})
=
-\frac{1}{\ln(2)}
       \sum_i \ln p_{\!\theta}(x_i\,|\,x_{<i})
=
\frac{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CE}}}{\ln(2)} .
\]

Thus *dividing* by $\ln(2)$ simply converts *nats* → *bits*.  
The numerator in (1) therefore equals the total number of **bits** required (according to the model) to encode the whole sequence $\mathbf{x}$.

---

### 2️⃣ Normalising per input byte  

A language model may be evaluated on sequences whose length is measured in **tokens**, **characters**, or **bytes**.  
If we want a *per‑unit* quantity that does not depend on how many prediction steps were taken, we divide by the number of elementary symbols that actually exist in the data—in this work those symbols are **bytes**:

\[
n_{\text{bytes}}
   = \text{length of }\mathbf{x}\text{ measured in raw bytes}.
\]

Hence  

\[
\operatorname{BPB}(x)
   =
   \frac{\displaystyle
         -\sum_i \log_{2} p_{\!\theta}(x_i\,|\,x_{<i})}
        {\displaystyle n_{\text{bytes}}},
   =
   \frac{\displaystyle
         -\sum_i \log_{2} p_{\!\theta}(x_i\,|\,x_{<i})}
        {\displaystyle n_{\#bytes}},
\]

or equivalently  

\[
\operatorname{BPB}(x)
   =
   \frac{\displaystyle
         -\sum_i \log_{2} p_{\!\theta}(x_i\,|\,x_{<i})}
        {\displaystyle n_{\text{bytes}}},
   =
   \frac{-\sum_i \log_{2} p_{\!\theta}(x_i\,|\,x_{<i})}
        {n_{\#bytes}}\!,
\]

where $n_{\#bytes}$ denotes “the number of bytes”.  
Intuitively, *BPB tells us how many binary digits, on average, our model would have to spend to describe each original byte*—exactly the quantity used in data compression theory.

---

### 3️⃣ Why “bits per byte’’ is preferred over perplexity for byte models  

|                     | Definition                                                            | Unit                              |
|---------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| Perplexity          | $\displaystyle PPL = e^{\,\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CE}}}$                | “effective branching factor’’ per *model step* |
| Bits‑Per‑Byte       | $\displaystyle BPB = 
      \frac{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{CE}}}{(\ln 2)\,n_{\text{bytes}}}$          | “bits’’ per *raw input symbol*               |

* Dependency on tokenizer – **strong** for perplexity (one step ≡ one *token*, so changing vocab size changes PPL even if prediction quality stays unchanged); **none** for BP/B (always measured per *byte*, irrespective of whether predictions were made at token granularity or at byte granularity).  
* Upper bound – no simple absolute bound for perplexity; BP/B ≤ 8 bits/byte (because a uniform distribution over 256 possible bytes has entropy $\log_₂ 256 = 8$).

Because a perplexity value depends on how many symbols the model predicts, comparing two models that use different vocabularies is misleading: a larger BPE vocabulary yields fewer prediction steps (lower PPL) even though its intrinsic ability to predict the next piece of information may be unchanged. In contrast, BP/B abstracts away from *how many steps* were taken and evaluates *how well* a model compresses the *real data* itself—the raw byte stream.

The paper stresses this point in § 4.3 (“Bits‑Per‑Byte…a tokenizer independent version of perplexity”). By reporting BP/B they obtain an absolute scale that can be interpreted like a compression ratio:
*8 bits/byte would be random data; 1–3 bits/byte indicates strong predictive structure.*

---

### 4️⃣ Comparing tokenized LLMs with byte‑level LLMs using BP/B  

If a tokenised model outputs a probability distribution over its finite vocabulary $\mathcal V_{\texttt{tok}}$, we can still compute BP/B provided we know how those tokens map back onto underlying bytes:

1️⃣ **During evaluation** we feed the same raw byte sequence into both models.  

2️⃣ For a tokenizer‐based model we look at each *token* it predicts, retrieve its exact byte string (the tokenizer is deterministic), and assign that whole string the same probability mass given to the token.  

3️⃣ The contribution of that prediction to the total bit count is then  

   \[
   -\,\log_{2}p(\text{token})
      = -%
        \sum_{b∈\text{token}}
           \log_{2}p(b~|~\text{previous context}),
   \]

   where each constituent byte inherits the token’s probability.  

4️⃣ Summing over all predictions and finally dividing by the total number of bytes yields exactly formula (1).

Because step 3 does not depend on how many tokens were used—only on how many *bytes* those tokens covered—the resulting BP/B value is comparable across any pair of models, irrespective of vocabularies or patching strategies.

The paper follows this procedure implicitly: they train both families on exactly the same raw data and evaluate them with Eq.~(1). Consequently their scaling curves (“BPB vs FLOPs”) can be plotted on a single axis even though one curve belongs to an ordinary BPE architecture (Llama 3) and another belongs to BLT’s dynamic patches.

---

### 5️⃣ Summary  

* The factor $\boldsymbol{\ln(2)}$ simply turns a natural‐log loss (nats) into a base‐two log loss (bits); dividing by $n_{\#bytes}$ then gives an average “bits required per original byte”.  
* BP/B measures **information density** rather than an abstract branching factor and therefore has an intuitive upper bound (8 bits/byte) and lower bound close to zero for highly predictable text.  
* Because it normalises with respect to raw bytes instead of tokens or patches, **BP/B enables fair head‑to‑head comparison between any two language models**, regardless of whether they use a large BPE vocabulary or operate directly on dynamically sized patches.  
* In practice one computes it exactly as Eq.~(1): take whatever cross‑entropy loss your training code reports (typically in nats), divide by $\ln(2)$ and then by the number of bytes processed. This yields a single scalar that reflects both modelling quality and compression efficiency—precisely what § 4.3 of the BLT paper aims to capture.
