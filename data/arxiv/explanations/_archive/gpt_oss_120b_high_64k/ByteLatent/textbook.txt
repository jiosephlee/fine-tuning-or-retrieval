# Foundations of Large Language Models and Tokenization

## The Transformer Encoder–Decoder Paradigm  

The dominant neural architecture behind modern large language models (LLMs) is the **Transformer** \citep{vaswani2017attention}, which consists of alternating self‑attention layers and feed‑forward networks. In its most general formulation a Transformer can be arranged either as an encoder–decoder pair (as originally proposed) or as a decoder‑only model that generates text autoregressively.  

In the **Byte Latent Transformer (BLT)** framework described in this work there are three distinct Transformer blocks:

1. **Local Encoder** – a lightweight Transformer whose purpose is to ingest raw byte embeddings and produce *patch* representations.
2. **Latent Global Transformer** – a deep autoregressive decoder‑only Transformer whose attention mask is *block‑causal*: each patch may attend only to patches at its own position or earlier positions within the same document.
3. **Local Decoder** – another lightweight Transformer that maps latent patch embeddings back to raw byte predictions.

All three modules share architectural ingredients such as multi‑head attention with RoPE positional embeddings \citep{RoPe}, SwiGLU activation functions \citep{swiglu}, RMSNorm \citep{RMSNorm}, and Flash/Flex attention implementations for efficient execution (\autoref{fig:arch_high_level}). By separating computation into *local* byte‑level processing and a *global* latent processing stage, BLT mirrors the classic encoder–decoder division while still operating on raw byte streams rather than pre‑tokenized symbols.

## Autoregressive Language Modeling Objectives  

LLMs are trained by asking them to predict the next discrete element given all previously observed elements—a task formalized as **autoregressive language modeling**. For a sequence of symbols \(x_{1}, x_{2},\dots ,x_{n}\) the model defines a conditional distribution  

\[
p(x_{i}\mid \mathbf{x}_{<i})\,,\qquad \mathbf{x}_{<i} = \{x_{1},\dots ,x_{i-1}\}.
\]

Training minimizes the cross‑entropy loss  

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} = -\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p(x_{i}\mid \mathbf{x}_{<i}) .
\]

When operating on bytes rather than tokens this objective becomes *next‑byte prediction*. In BLT an auxiliary small byte‑level language model provides estimates  

\[
p_{e}(x_i=v\mid \mathbf{x}_{<i})
\]

that are later used to compute entropies \(H(x_i)\) for dynamic patch formation (see Section “Entropy Patching”). The same cross‑entropy formulation underlies both pretraining on billions of bytes and downstream fine‑tuning on classification or generation benchmarks.



## Why Textual Data Must Be Discretized  

Neural networks accept numerical tensors as inputs; therefore raw textual streams must be transformed into a discrete numerical representation before feeding them into a model. Traditional approaches employ **tokenization**, which groups one or more raw bytes into a fixed lexical unit called a *token*. Each token index selects an embedding vector from an embedding matrix, producing a continuous representation suitable for subsequent transformer layers.

The necessity for discretization arises because:

* **Finite Vocabulary:** An embedding matrix can only contain a finite number of rows—each row corresponds to one member of the vocabulary.
* **Efficient Indexing:** Directly mapping arbitrary byte sequences without grouping would require an embedding matrix with \(256^{k}\) rows for any patch length \(k\), which is infeasible.
* **Model‑Level Inductive Bias:** Grouping bytes enforces an inductive bias about what constitutes meaningful linguistic chunks (words, subwords), shaping how information flows through attention mechanisms.

BLT departs from traditional tokenizers by discarding any *predefined* vocabulary whatsoever: it learns *latent* patch embeddings dynamically via its local encoder and decoder networks while still preserving the requirement of discrete indexing at each step.

## Common Sub‑Word Tokenizers  

While BLT demonstrates that fixed‑vocabulary tokenizers are unnecessary for high‑performance LLMs, most contemporary models—including Llama 3—still rely on **sub‑word tokenizers** such as **Byte‑Pair Encoding (BPE)** \citep{gage1994new,sennrich-etal-2016-neural}. A BPE tokenizer iteratively merges the most frequent adjacent byte pairs until reaching a predetermined vocabulary size. The resulting vocabulary consists of strings ranging from individual characters up to multi‑character fragments.

Other widely adopted sub‑word methods include **Unigram** segmentation and **WordPiece**, both of which also construct finite vocabularies by optimizing statistical criteria over training corpora. Although these variants do not appear explicitly in our empirical study, they share the central property of imposing a *static* mapping from original byte sequences to token indices prior to model training.


## Vocabulary Size Versus Sequence Length Trade‑offs  

A key design choice when selecting a tokenizer is determining its vocabulary size \(|\mathcal{V}|\). This choice directly influences two competing aspects:

1. **Average Token Length:** Larger vocabularies tend to contain longer multi‑byte entries, thereby reducing the number of tokens needed to represent a given amount of text.
2. **Embedding Table Dimensionality:** Increasing \(|\mathcal{V}|\) enlarges the embedding matrix—each extra entry consumes memory proportional to its hidden dimension \(h\).

Consequently there exists an intrinsic trade‑off between **compute per inference step** (which grows with embedding table size) and **number of inference steps** (which shrinks as average token length grows). BLT sidesteps this dilemma by allowing arbitrarily sized *patches* that are determined dynamically at runtime rather than being constrained by a fixed vocabulary count.



## Impact of Tokenizer Design on Training Dynamics  

Because every transformer step processes one discrete unit—whether it be a token or an adaptive patch—the tokenizer dictates how much computation each part of an input receives during training:

* With **fixed tokens**, every step receives identical compute regardless of how predictable its content is. As noted in our analysis ("Tokenization-based llms allocate the same amount of compute to every token"), this uniform allocation leads to inefficiencies when many tokens correspond to low‑entropy predictions (e.g., whitespace or common suffixes).
* In contrast, BLT’s ***entropy patching*** identifies high‑uncertainty regions using next‑byte entropy estimates \(H(x_i)\). When entropy exceeds either an absolute threshold \(\theta_g\) or a relative increase \(\theta_r\),

  \[
  H(x_t) > \theta_g
  \quad
  \text{or}
  \quad
  H(x_t)-H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r,
  \]

  a new patch boundary is inserted, causing the global latent transformer to process fewer steps overall while dedicating more capacity precisely where predictions are hard.
* Empirically this dynamic allocation yields up to “50 % fewer flops at inference” compared with BPE baselines (\S “scaling”), demonstrating that even when total FLOPs are held constant across models, allocating them adaptively leads to superior downstream performance.
* Moreover, because patches can vary in size across languages and domains without re‑training a tokenizer, BLT mitigates “multilingual inequity” observed in fixed vocabularies (\citet{liang2023xlm}).

These observations highlight how tokenizer design cascades through data preprocessing, model architecture choices, and ultimately influences efficiency curves observed during large‐scale pretraining.\footnote{Our FLOP estimation follows Chinchilla’s formulation~\citep{hoffmann2022training}, counting feed‑forward operations plus query/key/value projections while treating embedding lookups as zero FLOPs.}

## Limitations Inherent to a Fixed Discrete Vocabulary  

Fixed vocabularies impose several constraints that become pronounced at scale:

* **Domain/Modality Sensitivity:** Since tokens encode heuristics about word boundaries learned from pretraining data (\citet{dagangetting}), models may struggle when presented with out‐of‐distribution modalities such as code or scientific formulas where common tokens do not capture essential structure.
* **Sensitivity to Input Noise:** Tokenizations tend to break under character perturbations because any alteration may produce unseen byte patterns leading either to out-of-vocabulary tokens or drastically different segmentations (\S “Robustness”).
* **Lack of Orthographic Knowledge:** Tokens hide underlying character composition; consequently models lose direct access to fine-grained spelling information (\citet{edman2024cute}).
* **Multilingual Inequity:** A single finite set cannot equally cover all scripts; low‐resource languages receive fewer unique tokens per byte → poorer representation capacity (\citet{liang2023xlm}, \citet{petrov2024language}).

These limitations motivate research directions like BLT’s “patches” which maintain full byte visibility while still enabling hierarchical processing.

By contrasting traditional sub‑word tokenizers with BLT’s adaptive patching mechanism we see both why discretization remains indispensable and how moving away from static vocabularies can unlock important gains in efficiency, robustness, and multilingual fairness—all central themes explored throughout the remainder of this text.

Motivation for Byte‑Level Modeling  

Large language models conventionally begin their processing pipeline by converting raw textual data into a sequence of *tokens*.  
A tokenizer groups adjacent bytes according to heuristic rules learned from a finite training corpus and then maps each group onto an entry in a pre‑determined vocabulary.  
While this strategy has been remarkably successful for many natural–language tasks, recent work—including the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT)—has identified several systematic shortcomings that become especially pronounced when models are scaled toward billions of parameters and trillions of training bytes.  
These shortcomings arise because a fixed vocabulary encodes strong prior assumptions about how information should be compressed, assumptions that do not always align with the true complexity of the data.  
Consequently, important dimensions such as domain diversity, spelling variation, orthographic detail, and multilingual equity are compromised.  
The following sections explore each of these dimensions in depth, tracing their origins back to tokenization‐based designs and motivating why a raw‑byte approach such as BLT offers a principled alternative.



# Domain / Modality Sensitivity of Sub‑Word Vocabularies  

Sub‑word tokenizers are typically trained on a single monolingual or multilingual corpus that reflects certain domains more heavily than others (e.g., web text versus scientific literature).  
Because the tokenizer learns which byte sequences should be merged into a single token based on frequency statistics alone, it implicitly favors patterns that dominate the training data while assigning distinct tokens—or even completely novel segmentations—to rarer patterns found in specialized modalities such as source code, mathematical notation, or biomedical abstracts.  

When a model trained on such a tokenizer encounters text from an underrepresented modality, it often produces an abundance of short or fragmented tokens that increase the effective sequence length dramatically.  
Each additional token incurs extra feed‑forward network computations—a cost that dominates large transformer models regardless of how efficiently attention is implemented \citep{dubey2024llama}.  
Thus the model expends comparable compute on easy predictions (e.g., common English words) while starving hard predictions that occur at domain boundaries (e.g., symbols in code).  

BLT eliminates this source of bias by eschewing any pre‑computed grouping rule altogether.  
Instead of fixing “where” along the byte stream computation should be applied, BLT determines patch boundaries adaptively based on *local prediction entropy* (see Section \ref{section:dyn-patch}), thereby allocating more transformer steps precisely where the data proves most information‑dense regardless of domain.



# Inability to Handle Out‑of‑Vocabulary Tokens  

A central limitation of traditional tokenizers stems from their finite vocabulary size.  
If an incoming byte string cannot be assembled from existing tokens, the tokenizer falls back on fallback strategies such as breaking the string into many smaller units or inserting a generic “unknown” symbol.  
Both strategies inflate the number of steps required for processing while simultaneously discarding useful statistical regularities present at the character level—for example morphological cues hidden inside rare word forms or newly minted slang terms.\  

From an information–theoretic standpoint this loss manifests as unnecessary entropy spikes: predicting an unseen token forces the downstream language model to assign probability mass over an expanded set without guidance from its internal representations.^[The softmax over an enlarged output space becomes harder to optimize.]  
In contrast, raw–byte modelling never encounters “unknown” symbols because every possible value belongs inherently to the byte alphabet $\mathcal{V}=\{0,\dots ,255\}$.  
Every byte can be predicted directly, preserving granularity even for neologisms or exotic Unicode glyphs.\footnote{Actual Unicode code points are encoded via UTF‑8 byte sequences; BLT operates uniformly over these sequences without special handling.*}   
Consequently BLT sidesteps OOV issues entirely while retaining full expressive power.



# Fragility to Spelling Errors and Input Noise  

Because a tokenizer’s segmentation decisions rely heavily on exact character matches, minor perturbations such as typographical errors or case changes often yield dramatically different token sequences.  
A single misplaced letter can split what would otherwise be a single meaningful token into several unrelated ones—or force insertion of multiple “unknown” markers—thereby altering both input length and semantic context.\footnote{Empirical results reported in Section \ref{sec:robustness} show up to 8 percentage‐point drops for BPE models under various corruption schemes whereas BLT remains comparatively stable.*}  

Such fragility hinders robustness in real–world deployments where user‐generated text frequently contains misspellings, OCR errors, or adversarial noise.  
By modeling bytes directly, BLT treats every corruption merely as another pattern within its continuous probability distribution $p_{e}(x_i|\mathbf{x}_{<i})$.  
Since BLT’s encoder processes raw byte streams without assuming any higher‐level grouping beforehand, small perturbations affect only localized regions rather than reconfiguring entire token boundaries across the whole sentence.



# Loss of Orthographic / Phonological Information  

Sub‑word tokens aggregate multiple characters into single discrete symbols; during training this aggregation obscures direct access to lower‐level orthographic patterns such as repeated graphemes (“ss”, “tt”), suffixes (“–tion”), or character‐level phonological cues (e.g., silent letters).  
Consequently models must infer these regularities indirectly through statistical co‑occurrences among whole tokens—a process that requires substantially larger datasets and parameter budgets.\footnote{CITE EDMAN 2024 CUTE demonstrates considerable gains when models retain explicit grapheme information.*}  

A raw–byte architecture preserves every individual character throughout both encoding and decoding stages via its local encoder $\mathcal{E}$ and decoder $\mathcal{D}$ modules (Section \ref{section:local}).  
The inclusion of hash n‑gram embeddings further enriches each byte’s representation with contextual lexical fragments ranging from trigrams up through octagrams.^[Equation (5) defines how $g_{i,n}$ maps via $E^{hash}_n$].  
As a result BLT learns direct mappings between grapheme sequences and phoneme sequences—a capability highlighted by its superior performance on Grapheme–to–Phoneme benchmarks (Section \ref{sec:robustness}).  



# Multilingual Bias Introduced by Language‑Specific Token Splits  

Tokenizers trained on multilingual corpora allocate vocabulary capacity unevenly across languages because frequent languages dominate frequency counts while low‐resource languages receive fewer dedicated symbols.\citep{liang2023xlm,petrov2024language}  
This imbalance inflates average token lengths for underrepresented scripts (often exceeding five bytes per token) while compressing high‑resource scripts into shorter tokens.\footnote{Llama 3’s average token size grew modestly from 3.7 bytes → 4.4 bytes after expanding its vocabulary—a change limited precisely because enlarging one language’s vocabulary inevitably harms another.*}  

When such biased segmentations feed into downstream transformers, low‐resource languages suffer both higher computational cost (more tokens per sentence) and weaker lexical representations (fewer dedicated embeddings).  
Raw–byte processing treats every script uniformly—as opposed sequences over $\mathcal {V}$—thereby removing any systematic preference built into BPE merges.\citep{limisiewicz2024myte}   
The resulting *multilingual equity* appears empirically in improved low‑resource machine translation scores reported for BLT relative to BPE baselines (Section \ref{sec:robustness}).  



# Historical Attempts at Character/Byte Models and Why They Struggled at Scale  

Early neural language models operated directly on characters or bytes using recurrent architectures such as LSTMs \citep{sutskever2011generating,mikolov2012subword}.   
While these systems avoided fixed vocabularies outright, their sequential nature limited parallelism and made training on massive corpora prohibitive.\footnote{Later hierarchical LSTM approaches added boundary detectors \citep{chung2019hierarchical}, yet still faced scaling bottlenecks.*}  

The advent of transformers amplified interest in character‐level modeling because self–attention admits full parallel computation across positions.\citep{vaswani2017attention}  
However naïve transformer implementations suffer quadratically growing attention costs $\mathcal O(n^2)$ w.r.t.\ sequence length $n$.  Since operating on bytes multiplies $n$ roughly threefold compared with word/sub‐word inputs (\textit{i.e.,} average word ≈ 4 bytes), even modest corpora become computationally expensive.\citep{xue2022byt5}   
Researchers therefore introduced several mitigations:\ 

* **Efficient attention kernels** – sliding window attention \citep{el2020characterbert}, FlashAttention \citep{dao2022flashattention}, FlexAttention \citep{flexattention}. These reduce memory pressure but do not eliminate feed‑forward costs which dominate large transformers regardless of attention design (§ 1).  

* **Hybrid architectures** – CANINE employed local convolutional downsampling followed by a deep global transformer \citep{clark2022canine}; MegaByte introduced fixed strided patches harvested into latent vectors \citep{yu2023megabyte}. While these designs curtailed sequence lengths enough to train models up to 1 B parameters within reasonable flops budgets—and even surpassed some BPE baselines under very low data regimes—they still lagged behind state‐of‐the‐art tokenizer models when scaling beyond hundreds of millions parameters​‡[Fig 1 left].  

* **Alternative recurrence** – Mamba·Byte leveraged recurrence instead of attention work < 350 M parameters \citep{wang2024mambabyte}, achieving competitive bits-per-byte yet still relied on static compression schemes rather than fully dynamic grouping.'

Collectively these works demonstrated feasibility yet underscored two persistent challenges:
(1) **Dynamic allocation** – static patching schemes cannot respond adaptively when local information density spikes;
(2) **Scalable representation learning** – preserving fine detail without overwhelming flop budgets remained elusive.



# Key Open Questions That Byte Latent Transformer Aims to Answer  

BLT was conceived precisely around the unresolved issues highlighted above.  Its design raises—and seeks answers—to several fundamental research questions:

1. **Can raw byte inputs achieve parity with sophisticated tokenizer pipelines at large scales?**   
   Prior attempts attained parity only at modest sizes (< 1 B) or under generous data regimes; BLT’s experiments up through 8 B parameters and 4 T training bytes demonstrate parity against Llama 3 while spending up&nbsp;$\leqslant$ 50 % fewer inference flops (§ 5).

2. **How should computation be allocated dynamically according to local predictability?**   
   By computing next–byte entropies $H(x_i)=\!\sum_{v\in \mathcal V}\! p_e(v|\mathbf{x}_{<i})\,\log p_e(v|\mathbf{x}_{<i})$ with an auxiliary small LM ($p_e$), BLT defines *entropy patching* rules that place patch boundaries either when $H(x_t)>\theta_g$ (global threshold) or when $H(x_t)-H(x_{t-1})>\theta_r$ (approximate monotonicity).   This yields longer patches over predictable stretches (“…Mozart”) and shorter patches where uncertainty spikes (“Who composed …?”), directly mapping information density onto transformer steps (§ 2).

3. **Is it possible simultaneously to grow model capacity *and* increase average patch size without exceeding an inference flop budget?**   
   Because each latent transformer step processes one patch irrespective of its internal byte count (§ 3), expanding patches reduces step count while freeing FLOPs that can be reinvested into deeper global layers ($l_{\mathcal G}$).  Fixed–inference scaling experiments confirm steeper performance curves when patch size grows from ≈ 4½ bytes → 8 bytes (§ 5).

4. **Do raw bytes restore lost linguistic signals such as orthography and phonology while enhancing robustness?**   
   Empirical evaluation demonstrates superior performance on noisy HellaSwag variants (+8 points), grapheme–to–phoneme translation (+5% absolute), CUTE character manipulation (+25 points),   and low-resource machine translation (+2 BLEU relative gain).   These gains substantiate that preserving fine‐grained byte streams improves downstream reasoning tasks often illserved by token abstractions (§ 7).

5. **Can existing pretrained BPE models be converted efficiently into byte‐level counterparts?**   
   By initializing BLT’s global transformer $\mathcal G$ from Llama 3 weights while learning lightweight encoder/decoder modules (`distillation' strategy § 10), BLT inherits high‐level syntactic knowledge yet retains bytewise robustness—a promising direction toward “byte‑ifying” massive pretrained checkpoints without retraining from scratch.


Together these questions outline both practical engineering challenges (efficient cross‑attention implementation,\S~3) and theoretical considerations regarding scaling laws unique to raw‐byte architectures (§ 5). 
Addressing them validates whether removing static vocabularies truly unlocks new dimensions—computational efficiency, robustness across domains and languages—and reshapes future research trajectories beyond conventional tokenizer dependence.

From Bytes to Patches – The Patching Paradigm  

# Patch as a Formal Function $f_{p}$  

In a byte‑latent language model the raw input is a contiguous stream of bytes  
\[
\mathbf{x}= \{x_{i}\mid i=1,\dots ,n\},
\]  
where each $x_{i}\in\mathcal{V}$ is an element of the 256‑byte vocabulary.  The central operation of the **patching paradigm** is to convert this long sequence into a shorter sequence of **patches**  
\[
\mathbf{p}= \{p_{j}\mid j=1,\dots ,m\},\qquad m<n .
\]  
A *patch* $p_{j}$ is itself a contiguous subsequence of $\mathbf{x}$.  Formally, a *patching function* $f_{p}$ maps every position $i$ to a binary indicator  
\[
c_{i}\in\{0,1\},
\]  
where $c_{i}=1$ signals that $x_{i}$ starts a new patch and $c_{i}=0$ means that $x_{i}$ continues the current patch.  By scanning $\mathbf{x}$ once and emitting a new patch whenever $c_{i}=1$, $f_{p}$ produces the ordered list $\mathbf{p}$.  In this view, the length $m$ of the patch sequence is precisely the number of **Transformer steps** required by the global model—each step processes one latent patch representation rather than an individual byte.  Consequently, the **average patch size**  
\[
\overline{s}\;=\;\frac{n}{m}
\]  
is the principal lever controlling computational expense: larger $\overline{s}$ yields fewer global steps and therefore fewer floating‑point operations (flops).  

# Strided Patching: Fixed $K$‑Byte Strides  

The most elementary instantiation of $f_{p}$ is *strided* or *static* patching, in which every patch contains exactly $K$ bytes irrespective of content:
\[
p_{j}= \{x_{(j-1)K+1},\,x_{(j-1)K+2},\,\dots ,\,x_{jK}\}.
\]  
Because $K$ is fixed, one can immediately compute the number of global steps as $m = \lceil n/K\rceil$, granting precise control over flop budgets during both training and inference.  This simplicity makes strided patching attractive for rapid prototyping and for experiments that require an explicit relationship between average patch size and computational cost.  

However, strided grouping discards any notion of *information density*.  A byte that follows whitespace in source code may be extremely predictable (e.g., another space), yet it still consumes an entire global step if it lands alone inside a stride.  Conversely, dense informational regions—such as mathematical formulas or rare named entities—receive only one step for every $K$ bytes even when many more predictions are required.  Moreover, identical substrings can be split differently when they cross stride boundaries, leading to inconsistent representations across contexts.  These drawbacks motivate more adaptive strategies that allow the model itself to allocate compute where it matters most.  

# Space Patching: Using Whitespace and Space‑Like Bytes  

A modestly more informed deterministic scheme is *space padding*, often called **space patching**.  The idea is to treat every *space‑like* byte as a natural boundary marker:
> A *space‑like* byte is any byte that is **not** a Latin character, digit, or UTF‑8 continuation byte; each resulting patch must contain at least one non–space‑like byte.

Formally, let $\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}$ denote the set of space‑like bytes.  The patching rule then declares $c_{i}=1$ if either $x_{i}\in\mathcal{S}$ **or** if $x_{i-1}\in\mathcal{S}$.  As a result each word (or other linguistic unit delimited by spaces) becomes its own patch:
\[
p_{j}= \text{bytes between two consecutive space‑like delimiters}.
\]  

Space patching has two immediate benefits:

1. **Consistent linguistic granularity** – because words are always segmented identically regardless of surrounding context, the model can reuse learned representations across occurrences.
2. **Targeted allocation of flops** – predictions that follow a space are typically high‑entropy (e.g., the first character of a new word), so dedicating one global step per word ensures that these hard decisions receive the full capacity of the latent transformer.

Nevertheless, space padding suffers from limited flexibility:
* it cannot gracefully accommodate languages that lack whitespace (e.g., Chinese or Thai) or domains where whitespace does not align with information density (e.g., source code where operators matter);
* it offers no mechanism for varying $\overline{s}$ beyond what is dictated by natural word lengths;
* long strings without spaces would be forced into overly large patches that could degrade efficiency.

Thus while space patching improves over naïve striding by injecting an elementary data‐driven cue—namely “the next token starts after a space”—it still falls short when truly adaptive allocation is desired.

# Incremental vs Non‑Incremental Segmentation  

When designing any segmentation procedure it is crucial to consider whether it can be applied *on‑the‑fly* during generation, without peeking ahead at future bytes.  A segmentation function $f_{p}$ is said to be **incremental** if its decision at position $i$ depends solely on the prefix $\mathbf{x}_{<i}=\{x_{1},...,x_{i-1}\}$.  Formally,
\[
f_{p}(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) \;=\; f_{p}(\mathbf{x})_{\;<i},
\tag{1}
\]
meaning that processing more input never retroactively changes earlier decisions about where patches begin.

Both strided and space patching are incremental:
* In striding, whether index $i$ starts a new patch follows a simple arithmetic test ($i\bmod K = 1$) which depends only on $i$.
* In space padding, whether $x_i$ starts a new patch depends purely on whether $x_i$ belongs to $\mathcal{S}$—a property observable from the prefix alone.

In contrast, most widely used subword tokenizers such as Byte‐Pair Encoding (**BPE**) violate incrementality.  The same prefix may tokenize differently depending on which characters follow because BPE greedily merges frequent byte pairs based on global statistics rather than local predictability.  Consequently,
\[
f_{\text{BPE}}(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) \neq f_{\text{BPE}}(\mathbf{x})_{\;<i}
\]
in general.  This behavior impedes seamless generation: at generation time we cannot know whether we have reached a token boundary without examining future bytes (or inserting an explicit delimiter token).  The authors point out that adding a dedicated delimiter token would enforce incrementality but would also strictly increase sequence length—negating some efficiency gains afforded by patches.

# Comparison Between Patches and Traditional BPE Tokens  

The distinction between **patches** and **BPE tokens** is twofold:

1\. **Vocabulary versus Dynamism** —  
   *Tokens* are drawn from a **finite**, pre‑computed vocabulary determined before training (e.g., Llama 3’s 128 K token types).  Each token corresponds to one row in an embedding table and one column in an output projection matrix—both fixed for the life of the model.  
   *Patches*, by definition, have **no fixed vocabulary**.  A segment may consist of any arbitrary byte subsequence; its latent representation is produced on demand by lightweight encoder modules (§ 5), typically via cross‑attention pooling over raw byte embeddings plus optional n‑gram hash embeddings.

2\. **Compute–Vocab Tradeoff** —  
   Enlarging a tokenizer’s vocabulary usually leads to longer average tokens (more bytes per token) because more frequent multi‑byte strings become single units—but at the same time inflates the embedding table and output head dimensions proportionally.  This creates an almost zero‑sum tradeoff: saving flops by processing fewer tokens forces us to spend more parameters on I/O layers.
   
   With patches there is no such tradeoff: we may increase $\overline{s}$ arbitrarily (e.g., from 4 bytes per patch up to 8 bytes per patch) without modifying any embedding lookup tables—the encoder still only sees raw bytes individually before pooling them into latent vectors.  Therefore longer patches reduce global Transformer flops while leaving local encoder/decoder flops essentially unchanged.

3\. **Direct Access to Byte Features** —  
   Because tokens are opaque symbols once mapped through the tokenizer—information about their constituent bytes is lost—the downstream model never sees raw character patterns directly unless additional auxiliary mechanisms are introduced. In contrast, patches preserve direct byte-level context inside each latent representation via cross‑attention over embedded bytes (and optional hash n‑grams). This preservation explains empirical gains in robustness and orthographic reasoning observed in BLT models (§ 7).

# Why Average Patch Size Controls Compute Cost  

The overarching cost driver in both training and inference for BLT lies in how many times we invoke its large **latent transformer** $\mathcal G$, which operates at the granularity of patches rather than individual bytes.  If we denote:

* $n$ – total number of bytes processed,
* $m$ – corresponding number of patches,
* $\overline{s}=n/m$ – average patch size,

then each forward pass through $\mathcal G$ consumes roughly
\[
\text{FLOPs}_{\mathcal G}\;\approx \;\bigl(\text{per–step FLOPs}\bigr)\times m,
\]
where “per–step FLOPs” depend only on hidden dimensionality and layer depth but not on input length directly.   Hence halving $m$ (by doubling $\overline{s}$) halves this dominant component of total computation.

The remaining components—the local encoder ($\mathcal E$), local decoder ($\mathcal D$), and cross‑attention blocks—are largely proportional to the raw byte count because they attend within small windows ($w_{\mathcal E},w_{\mathcal D}$) or process each byte exactly once for pooling purposes. Their contribution grows linearly with $n$, but because their hidden dimensions ($h_{\mathcal E},h_{\mathcal D}$) are deliberately kept much smaller than those of $\mathcal G$, their overall flop share stays modest even for long contexts.

Thus **average patch size acts as an efficient knob**:
* Larger patches → fewer global steps → lower overall FLOPs while preserving model capacity,
* Smaller patches → finer granularity → potentially higher fidelity predictions at increased cost.

In practice BLT exploits this knob by allowing different datasets or training regimes to set different target $\overline{s}$ values via entropy‐based dynamic patching (§ 3), thereby achieving controlled tradeoffs between speed and accuracy without altering any architectural hyperparameters other than those governing locality.
