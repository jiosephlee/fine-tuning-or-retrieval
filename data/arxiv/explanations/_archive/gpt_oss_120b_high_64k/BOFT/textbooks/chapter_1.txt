Chapter 1: Foundations of Parameter‑Efficient Adaptation for Foundation Models  

# Evolution of Foundation Models and Resource Constraints  

The past several years have witnessed an unprecedented surge in the scale and capability of neural networks that serve as **foundation models**—large, pretrained systems that can be repurposed for a wide variety of downstream tasks. Two emblematic examples illustrate this trend. First, large language models such as ChatGPT \cite{brown2020language,chen2021evaluating} build upon predecessors like GPT‑3, whose publicly reported size reaches roughly $175$ billion parameters \cite{brown2020language}. Second, generative vision systems exemplified by Stable Diffusion \cite{rombach2022high} employ deep diffusion processes together with massive transformer backbones to produce high‑fidelity images conditioned on text prompts.  

These successes are tightly coupled to ever larger model capacities. As shown by the progression from modestly sized recurrent networks to multi‑billion‑parameter transformers, performance improvements on benchmarks often correlate with increases both in depth (more transformer layers) and width (larger hidden dimensions). However, each step up in scale brings an exponential growth in **parameter count**—the total number of learnable scalars that define the model’s behavior—and consequently an explosion in computational requirements during pretraining.  

Training such a model from scratch entails three intertwined resource demands:

1. **Compute** – The number of floating‑point operations grows roughly as $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for a dense weight matrix of dimension $d$, leading to petaflop‑scale workloads when $d$ reaches several tens or hundreds of thousands.
2. **Memory** – Storing activation maps for back‑propagation across dozens of layers multiplies memory consumption roughly linearly with $d$ but also requires auxiliary buffers proportional to batch size.
3. **Energy** – The cumulative power draw over weeks or months of GPU/TPU time translates into significant carbon footprints and financial costs that exceed the budgets of most academic laboratories and many industry teams.

Collectively these constraints render “training from scratch” an impractical strategy for most practitioners who wish simply to leverage an existing foundation model for a new task (e.g., sentiment classification on a modest text corpus or object detection on a domain‑specific image set). Consequently, research has shifted toward **efficient adaptation** techniques that reuse pretrained weights while limiting additional computational burden.

# Why Full‑Model Training Is Prohibitive  

To appreciate quantitatively why naïvely fine‑tuning every parameter is often untenable, consider a linear layer within a transformer that maps an input $\mathbf{x}\in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ to an output $\mathbf{z}\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ via $\mathbf{z}= \mathbf{W}^{\top}\!\mathbf{x}$ with weight matrix $\mathbf{W}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. If $d=n=4096$, just this single layer contains $d\times n = 16\,\text{M}$ parameters; replicating such layers across dozens of transformer blocks yields hundreds of millions of trainable values per model component alone. Scaling up further—to $d=n=16384$, which is typical for state‑of‑the‑art vision transformers—produces $\sim 268$ M parameters per layer.  

When every entry must be updated during fine‑tuning, two consequences jeopardize feasibility:

* **Parameter budget** – Updating all entries means storing separate gradient tensors and optimizer states (often topically three times larger than the weight tensor). The memory footprint can quickly exceed device limits even after gradient checkpointing.
* **Optimization difficulty** – Large numbers of free variables increase the risk of overfitting on modest downstream datasets because the adaptation stage lacks sufficient supervisory signal relative to degrees of freedom being adjusted.
* **Training time** – Each optimization step now performs dense matrix multiplication with $d\times n$ multiplications per forward/backward pass. Even when using mixed precision or hardware acceleration, wall‑clock time scales linearly with total parameter count.

These obstacles motivate **parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning (PEFT)** methods which deliberately constrain how many weights are altered during adaptation while striving to preserve downstream performance comparable—or even superior—to naive full fine‑tuning.

# Parameter‑Efficient Fine‑Tuning Paradigms  

Researchers have converged on three canonical families for PEFT, each embodying a distinct design philosophy regarding *where* adaptivity is injected into an otherwise frozen foundation model.

## Model Fine‑Tuning  

In its most straightforward incarnation, **model fine‑tuning** leaves the architecture untouched but updates only a selected subset—or sometimes all—of the existing weight matrices using gradient descent. This approach inherits all structural inductive biases baked into the pretrained model without introducing any extra components at inference time; thus *no additional latency* arises when deploying the adapted system—a property highlighted repeatedly throughout recent literature \cite{hulora2022,qiu2023controlling}.  

Beyond naive selection heuristics such as using a small learning rate \cite{raffel2020exploring}, more principled variants have been explored that respect geometric relationships among neurons. One notable example is **Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning (OFT)** \cite{qiu2023controlling}, which constrains each transformed layer by a trainable orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. Because orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles between column vectors (“neurons”), OFT provably maintains relational information originally encoded during pretraining—a desirable regularity fostering better generalization \cite{liu2018learning,qiu2023controlling}.  

From a parameter perspective, representing $\mathbf{R}$ densely would require $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$ trainable scalars—clearly at odds with efficiency goals when $d$ is large. The original OFT therefore introduced a **block‐diagonal** sparsity pattern:
\[
\mathbf{R} = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{R}_{r})\,,\qquad 
\mathbf{R}_{i}\in \mathbb{R}^{b\times b},\quad br=d,
\]
which reduces trainable count proportionally to $bd$, yet imposes an artificial grouping constraint on neuron dimensions.\footnote{The aforementioned constraint may limit expressiveness because dimensions belonging to distinct blocks never interact under $\mathbf{R}$.}

Nevertheless, model fine‑tuning remains attractive because it does not alter runtime computation graph—every forward pass still executes precisely the same sequence of matrix multiplications defined by the original backbone.\footnote{The cost overhead appears only during training where multiple sparse orthogonal matrices are multiplied at each step if richer factorizations are employed; after adaptation they can be absorbed into the frozen weights without extra inference cost—a point emphasized later when discussing Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT).}

## Adapter Modules  

A second family augments each layer with **adapter modules**, small neural subcomponents inserted between existing transformations while leaving pretrained weights frozen \cite{houlsby2019parameter,pfeiffer2020adapterfusion}. Concretely, after computing $\mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{W}^{\top}\!\mathbf{x}$ one passes $\mathbf{x}'$ through an adapter consisting typically of a down‐projection $\mathbf{W}_{\downarrow}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}\times r}$ followed by an up‐projection $\mathbf{W}_{\uparrow}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\text{out}}}$ where $r \ll d_{\text{in}}, d_{\text{out}}$. Because $r$ is intentionally tiny—often only a few percent of hidden dimension—the total extra trainable parameters per adapter are roughly $\mathcal( rd )$, far lower than updating all elements of $\mathbf W$.  

Adapter approaches introduce ***additional computational steps*** during inference—a small amount relative to full matrix multiplication but nonetheless nonzero—so they entail modest latency increments compared with pure model fine‑tuning. Nevertheless they offer greater flexibility than block-diagonal orthogonal transforms because adapters can learn arbitrary linear functions within their bottleneck subspace.

## Prompt Tuning  

A third line exploits **prompt tuning**, wherein task‐specific information is encoded as learnable *prefix tokens* attached directly to input sequences before feeding them through the frozen backbone \cite{li2021prefix,lester2021power}. In language models this amounts to prepending $p$ virtual token embeddings $\mathbf P\in\mathbb R^{p\times d_{\text{model}}}$ that are optimized while all internal weights remain untouched:
\[
[\texttt{\small <prompt>}_1,\dots,\texttt{\small <prompt>}_p,\texttt{\small <input>}] 
\xrightarrow{\;\textnormal{\fontsize}{10}{10}\selectfont  \texttt{\small backbone}}\;
\texttt{\small output}.
\]
Because only embedding vectors are trained—their total count scales as $p\, d_{\text{{model}}}$—the parameter budget can be minuscule even when $p$ is small (\eg~10–20 tokens). Importantly this scheme imposes *no change* on any weight matrices or activation pathways during forward passes; thus it satisfies zero‐latency requirements completely.\footnote{The tradeoff is limited expressive power since all adaptation must be mediated through modifications at the input embedding level rather than directly adjusting deeper transformations.}

---  
These three paradigms constitute complementary corners of design space: *model fine‑tuning* emphasizes minimal runtime alteration at potential cost in trainable size; *adapter modules* enrich expressive capacity while incurring modest latency; *prompt tuning* offers ultra‐lightweight adaptivity but may suffer expressive constraints for complex downstream tasks.\footnote{All three families continue to inspire hybrid designs—e.g., combining adapters with prompt embeddings—to balance tradeoffs further~\cite{sung2021training}.}

# Desiderata for Parameter–Efficient Fine–Tuning Methods  

Effective PEFT strategies ought not only to reduce raw parameter count but also satisfy broader practical criteria derived from real-world deployment constraints:

## Low Parameter Budget  

A primary goal is minimizing *the number of free variables* introduced during adaptation relative to total model size $\lvert \Theta\rvert$. If only $k$ out of $\lvert \Theta\rvert$ parameters are learnable ($k\ll \lvert \Theta\rvert$), both memory allocation for optimizer states and required storage for checkpointing shrink dramatically:
\[
k = \mathcal O(d) \quad
    (\textit {vs.\ }   k = \mathcal O(d^{2}) \textit {for a dense orthogonal update}).
\]
Methods such as block–diagonal OFT achieve this reduction by partitioning dimensions into blocks ($b$ per block gives roughly $bd$ free scalars), while adapter modules rely on rank-$r$ projections yielding approximately $2rd$ free scalars per layer—both substantially less than naïve full fine-tuning’s order-$d^{2}$ cost.\footnote {In practice $b,r,$ or $p$ are chosen via hyperparameter search respecting downstream data volume and hardware limits.}

## No Inference Latency Increase  

From an application standpoint it is often essential that deployment speed remain unchanged after adaptation—especially when serving real-time user queries or processing high-throughput streams (\eg~online translation services). Model fine-tuning uniquely guarantees *zero additional compute*: once training finishes one can simply replace each original weight matrix $\mathbf W^{0}$ by its adapted counterpart $(\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})$, fusing any learned orthogonal transformation into preexisting tensors so that inference proceeds exactly as before.\footnote {Adapter modules incur extra matrix multiplications inside every forward pass but typically add less than 5 % overhead due to their low dimensionality; prompt tuning incurs none because only input embeddings differ—but does require concatenating extra token vectors at runtime.)}

## Good Generalization 

Parameter efficiency should also translate into robust performance on previously unseen inputs within the target domain. Maintaining *knowledge preservation*—i.e., ensuring that adaptations do not catastrophically overwrite useful representations learned during pretraining—is crucial here.\footnote {A recurring observation across recent work is that imposing structural regularities such as orthogonality preserves spectral properties (\eg~singular values) thereby stabilizing gradients and improving transferability~\cite{miyato2018spectral}.} 

In OFT this principle manifests through angle preservation: if neurons before adaptation are represented by column vectors $\{\boldsymbol w_{i}^{0}\}$ then applying an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$ guarantees
\[
\langle\, \boldsymbol w_i^{0},\, \boldsymbol w_j^{0}\,\rangle
= 
\langle\, \mathbf R\, \boldsymbol w_i^{0},\, 
          \mathbf R\, \boldsymbol w_j^{0}\,\rangle,
\]
so relational geometry remains intact throughout training—a property empirically linked with superior convergence rates and reduced overfitting.\cite{sachdeva2018}
Adapters implicitly maintain original representations by restricting updates onto low-rank residuals which often act like gentle nudges rather than wholesale rewrites.\footnote {Similarly prompt tuning respects base internals entirely because it never touches hidden weights directly.)}

Overall, successful PEFT designs strike an optimal balance across these desiderata:
* **Compactness** prevents resource bottlenecks,
* **Latency neutrality** preserves deployment viability,
* **Regularization via structured transformations** fosters transference from massive pretraining reservoirs onto specific downstream domains.



---  

In summary, modern foundation models possess staggering expressive power due chiefly to their enormous parameter counts—a virtue accompanied by prohibitive training costs whose magnitude has spurred intense research into *parameter-efficient adaptation*. By surveying three principal families—model fine-tuning (including orthogonal strategies such as OFT), adapter modules, and prompt tuning—we lay groundwork for deeper investigations into how structural constraints (e.g., sparsity patterns forming butterfly factorizations) can further shrink parameter budgets without compromised accuracy or speed—a theme pursued rigorously in subsequent chapters devoted to Orthogonal Butterfly Fine-Tuning (BOFT).\footnote {The remainder of this textbook explores precisely these ideas: constructing dense orthogonal transformations from products of sparse matrices via butterfly factorizations, analyzing their expressivity under graph‐theoretic “information transmission” lenses, and presenting empirical evidence across vision transformers, language models, and diffusion generators.\hfill ∎}
