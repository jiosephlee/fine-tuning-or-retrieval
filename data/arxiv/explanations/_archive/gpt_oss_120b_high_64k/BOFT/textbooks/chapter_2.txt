\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 2: Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Geometry and Motivation  

In modern deep learning, large pretrained “foundation” models are adapted to downstream tasks by updating only a small part of their parameters.  A central challenge is to modify these parameters while preserving the knowledge already captured during pretraining.  **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** addresses this challenge by restricting the adaptation to an *orthogonal* transformation applied uniformly to all neurons in a layer.  The following sections develop the geometric intuition behind OFT, formalize its mathematical underpinnings, describe how orthogonality is enforced during optimization, explain why zero‑initialization stabilizes training, and summarize the empirical advantages reported by the original OFT work.

---

## Angle Preservation Between Neurons as a Principled Regularizer  

A linear layer in a neural network can be written as a weight matrix  
\[
\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
\]  
where each column \(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) corresponds to one output neuron (or feature) of that layer.  The *relational geometry* among neurons is encoded by the pairwise inner products  
\[
\langle \mathbf{w}^{0}_{i},\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j}\rangle 
   \;=\;\big(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\big)^{\!\top}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j},
\]  
or equivalently by the angles  
\[
\theta_{ij}
   \;=\;\arccos\frac{\langle \mathbf{w}^{0}_{i},\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j}\rangle}
                         {\|\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\|\,\|\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j}\|}.
\]  

During pretraining these angles capture semantic relationships: neurons whose weight vectors point in similar directions respond similarly to inputs, while those whose vectors are nearly orthogonal encode distinct features.  If finetuning were to arbitrarily distort these angles, the pretrained knowledge could be lost or corrupted, leading to poor generalization on new data.

A **principled regularizer** therefore aims to keep all pairwise angles unchanged while still allowing the layer to adapt to task‑specific signals.  Orthogonal transformations satisfy exactly this requirement because an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) obeys  
\[
\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}_{d}.
\]  
Multiplying every column vector by the same \(\mathbf{R}\) yields transformed columns \(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{i}= \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\).  Their inner product satisfies  
\[
\langle \tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{i}, \tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{j} \rangle
   = (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i})^{\!\top}(\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j})
   = (\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i})^{\!\top} \underbrace{\big(\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}\big)}_{\!\mathbf I}
      \,\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j}
   = \langle \mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}, \mathbf{w}^{0}_{j} \rangle,
\]  
and consequently every angle \(\theta_{ij}\) remains exactly the same.  Thus an *orthogonal finetuning* step implements an **angle‑preserving regularizer** without needing any explicit penalty term—the geometry is enforced automatically by the algebraic property of orthogonality.

## Mathematical Formulation: Orthogonal Multiplicative Update  

The central idea of OFT is to reparameterize the *new* weight matrix of a layer as a product of a learnable orthogonal matrix and the frozen pretrained matrix:
\[
\boxed{\;\;\tilde{\mathbf W}= \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0}\;\;}
   \qquad 
   \text{with }\;\; 
   \mathbf R^\!\top \mathbf R = \mathbf I_{d}, 
   \;\;
   \mathbf R \in\mathbb {R}^{d\times d}.
\]  

During a forward pass on an input vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb {R}^{d}\), the output becomes
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf z 
      &= (\tilde{\mathbf W})^{\!\top} \,\mathbf{x}
       = (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top} \,\mathbf{x}
        = (\mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top} (\mathbf R^{\!\top} \,\boldsymbol{x})
        .
\end{aligned}
\]  
Because only \(\mathbf R\) changes during finetuning while \(\mathbf W^{0}\) stays fixed, all updates are confined to a *single* orthogonal transformation that rotates (or reflects) the representation space learned by pretraining.  This multiplicative update differs from additive low‑rank methods such as LoRA; instead of adding new directions it *re‑orients* existing ones while preserving lengths and inner products among them.

When every layer of a deep model adopts this reparameterization, the entire network undergoes a sequence of coordinated rotations that adapt its behavior without destroying its original geometric structure.

# Angle Preservation Between Neurons as a Principled Regularizer  

Consider a linear layer whose pretrained weight matrix is denoted  

\[
\mathbf W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n},
\]  

where each column $\mathbf w^{0}_{i}\in\mathbb R^{d}$ corresponds to one output neuron (or feature).  The relational geometry among these neurons is captured by their pairwise inner products $\langle \mathbf w^{0}_{i},\mathbf w^{0}_{j}\rangle$ or equivalently by the angles  

\[
\theta_{ij}= 
      \arccos
      \frac{
          \langle \mathbf w^{0}_{i},\,\mathbf w^{0}_{j}\rangle
      }{
          \|\mathbf w^{0}_{i}\|\,\|\mathbf w^{0}_{j}\|
      } .
\]  

During pretraining these angles encode semantic similarities: neurons whose weight vectors point in similar directions tend to respond similarly to inputs, whereas nearly orthogonal vectors encode complementary information.  When fine‑tuning a model for a downstream task we would like to retain this relational structure because it embodies most of the knowledge learned from massive upstream data.  A principled regularizer therefore aims **to keep every pairwise angle unchanged** while still allowing task‑specific adaptation.

An *orthogonal* transformation provides exactly this guarantee.  If $\displaystyle
    \mathbf R^\top    \!\!  	extbf I_{d}$,
then for any two columns $\b w_i^0$ and $\b w_j^0$

\[
(\b R\b w_i^0)^\top (\b R\b w_j^0)
       = (\b w_i^0)^\top\b R^\top\b R\b w_j^0
       = (\b w_i^0)^\top\b w_j^0,
\]

so their inner product—and consequently their angle—remains identical after transformation.  By applying **the same** orthogonal matrix $\b R$ simultaneously to every column, OFT forces all pairwise angles between neurons to stay exactly as they were after pretraining.  In this sense angle preservation acts as an implicit regularizer embedded directly into the model’s parameterisation rather than being added as an auxiliary loss term.

The core idea of Orthogonal Finetuning is to re‑parameterise each weight matrix with an *orthogonal left‑multiplication*:  

\[
\fbox{$
    \tilde{\b W}=\,\b R\,\,{\b W}^{\,0},
    \
    {\rm where }\;
    {\b R}^\top {\b R}= {\bf I}_d,
    \
    {\b R}\in\rbb f ^ {d\times d}
$}.
\\[4pt]

During inference or forward propagation an input vector $\b x∈ℝ^{d}$ produces

```
z =
(tilde_W)^⊤ x
=
(RW⁰)^⊤ x
=
(W⁰)^⊤(Rᵀx)
```

Thus only $\b R$ changes during fine‑tuning while $\b W⁰$ remains frozen.  The entire adaptation process reduces to learning **a single rotation (or reflection) of the representation space** per layer rather than altering every individual entry of $\b W⁰$.  Compared with additive low–rank approaches such as LoRA—which introduce new directions—the multiplicative form preserves both lengths and mutual orientations of all pretrained weight vectors.


# Cayley Transform for Enforcing Orthogonality During Training  

Optimising directly over matrices constrained by $\b R^\top\b R={\bf I}_d$ is inconvenient because stochastic gradient descent does not naturally respect such manifolds.  The original OFT sidesteps this difficulty by employing **the Cayley transform**, which maps any skew‑symmetric matrix $\b Q$ onto an orthogonal matrix:

```math
\b R 
      = (\bf I +  	extbf Q)\bigl( {\bf I} -  	extbf Q\bigr)^{-1},
      \
      {\rm where }\;
      {\bf Q}= -{\bf Q}^\top .
```

Because every skew‑symmetric $\b Q$ has $\tfrac12 d(d-1)$ independent entries, optimisation can be performed on these unconstrained parameters using ordinary gradient descent.  After each gradient step one simply recomputes $\b R$ via the above formula; by construction $ \b R $ remains perfectly orthogonal regardless of how $\b Q$ changes.  No projection onto an orthogonal manifold or penalty term is required, which makes optimisation both **exact** (the constraint never drifts) and **efficient** (the computation involves only standard matrix operations).


# Zero‑Initialization via Identity Matrix and Its Effect on Stability  

Fine‑tuning large pretrained models typically starts from parameters that already perform well on many inputs.  To avoid perturbing this good initialisation unnecessarily many PEFT methods initialise their trainable components so that they contribute nothing at time zero—for example LoRA sets its low‑rank update matrices to zero.  

In OFT the natural analogue is **identity initialisation** of the orthogonal component:

* Set all entries of $\b Q$ to zero before training.
* Consequently $( {\bf I}+{\bf Q})( {\bf I}-{\bf Q})^{-1}= {\bf I}_d$, i.e., $\b R$ starts as the identity matrix.
* Hence $\tilde{\b W}= {\bf I}_d\,{\b W}^{\,0}= {\b W}^{\,0}$ at epoch zero.

This design ensures that at the outset there is *exactly* no change from the pretrained network, thereby preserving pretraining performance before any gradient information accumulates.  Because subsequent updates are performed through a smooth manifold (the space of orthogonal matrices), even relatively large learning rates produce gradual rotations rather than abrupt jumps in parameter space, which empirically leads to **more stable optimisation**—a phenomenon emphasized throughout the original OFT experiments.


# Empirical Benefits Reported in Prior OFT Work  

The authors of OFT evaluated this geometric framework on several demanding adaptation tasks:

* **Text‑to‑image diffusion models** – OFT was applied when users wanted fine‑grained control over generated images.  In these experiments OFT achieved *stronger generalisation* across unseen prompts compared with additive low‑rank baselines such as LoRA.
* **Training stability** – Across multiple runs OFT displayed *consistent convergence*, exhibiting fewer spikes or divergences during early epochs when using typical learning rates for large models.
* **Preservation of pretrained knowledge** – By maintaining exact pairwise angles among neurons throughout fine‑tuning, OFT retained more of the semantic structure encoded during upstream training, which translated into higher downstream task accuracy at comparable parameter budgets.
* **Parameter budget** – Although plain OFT employs dense $d\times d$ orthogonal matrices—hence more trainable parameters than low‑rank approaches—the authors demonstrated that even its block‑diagonal variant already outperforms LoRA while remaining practical for very large layers when computational resources permit.

Collectively these findings suggest that adopting an angle‑preserving *orthogonal* transformation provides a **principled route** toward adapting massive foundation models without sacrificing either stability or downstream performance.


---  

In summary, Orthogonal Finetuning leverages a fundamental geometric property—preservation of inter‑neuron angles—to construct an elegant regulariser built directly into model parameters via an orthogonal left multiplication.  The Cayley transform guarantees exact feasibility during optimisation, while identity initialisation secures smooth entry into training dynamics.  Empirically this construction leads to robust adaptation across vision and language domains, positioning OFT as a conceptually simple yet powerful alternative within today’s landscape of parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning techniques.
