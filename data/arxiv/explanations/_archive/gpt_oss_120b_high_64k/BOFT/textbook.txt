Foundations of Parameter‑Efficient Adaptation for Foundation Models  

# Evolution of Foundation Models and Resource Constraints  

The past several years have witnessed an unprecedented surge in the scale and capability of neural networks that serve as **foundation models**—large, pretrained systems that can be repurposed for a wide variety of downstream tasks. Two emblematic examples illustrate this trend. First, large language models such as ChatGPT \cite{brown2020language,chen2021evaluating} build upon predecessors like GPT‑3, whose publicly reported size reaches roughly $175$ billion parameters \cite{brown2020language}. Second, generative vision systems exemplified by Stable Diffusion \cite{rombach2022high} employ deep diffusion processes together with massive transformer backbones to produce high‑fidelity images conditioned on text prompts.  

These successes are tightly coupled to ever larger model capacities. As shown by the progression from modestly sized recurrent networks to multi‑billion‑parameter transformers, performance improvements on benchmarks often correlate with increases both in depth (more transformer layers) and width (larger hidden dimensions). However, each step up in scale brings an exponential growth in **parameter count**—the total number of learnable scalars that define the model’s behavior—and consequently an explosion in computational requirements during pretraining.  

Training such a model from scratch entails three intertwined resource demands:

1. **Compute** – The number of floating‑point operations grows roughly as $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for a dense weight matrix of dimension $d$, leading to petaflop‑scale workloads when $d$ reaches several tens or hundreds of thousands.
2. **Memory** – Storing activation maps for back‑propagation across dozens of layers multiplies memory consumption roughly linearly with $d$ but also requires auxiliary buffers proportional to batch size.
3. **Energy** – The cumulative power draw over weeks or months of GPU/TPU time translates into significant carbon footprints and financial costs that exceed the budgets of most academic laboratories and many industry teams.

Collectively these constraints render “training from scratch” an impractical strategy for most practitioners who wish simply to leverage an existing foundation model for a new task (e.g., sentiment classification on a modest text corpus or object detection on a domain‑specific image set). Consequently, research has shifted toward **efficient adaptation** techniques that reuse pretrained weights while limiting additional computational burden.

# Why Full‑Model Training Is Prohibitive  

To appreciate quantitatively why naïvely fine‑tuning every parameter is often untenable, consider a linear layer within a transformer that maps an input $\mathbf{x}\in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ to an output $\mathbf{z}\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ via $\mathbf{z}= \mathbf{W}^{\top}\!\mathbf{x}$ with weight matrix $\mathbf{W}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. If $d=n=4096$, just this single layer contains $d\times n = 16\,\text{M}$ parameters; replicating such layers across dozens of transformer blocks yields hundreds of millions of trainable values per model component alone. Scaling up further—to $d=n=16384$, which is typical for state‑of‑the‑art vision transformers—produces $\sim 268$ M parameters per layer.  

When every entry must be updated during fine‑tuning, two consequences jeopardize feasibility:

* **Parameter budget** – Updating all entries means storing separate gradient tensors and optimizer states (often topically three times larger than the weight tensor). The memory footprint can quickly exceed device limits even after gradient checkpointing.
* **Optimization difficulty** – Large numbers of free variables increase the risk of overfitting on modest downstream datasets because the adaptation stage lacks sufficient supervisory signal relative to degrees of freedom being adjusted.
* **Training time** – Each optimization step now performs dense matrix multiplication with $d\times n$ multiplications per forward/backward pass. Even when using mixed precision or hardware acceleration, wall‑clock time scales linearly with total parameter count.

These obstacles motivate **parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning (PEFT)** methods which deliberately constrain how many weights are altered during adaptation while striving to preserve downstream performance comparable—or even superior—to naive full fine‑tuning.

# Parameter‑Efficient Fine‑Tuning Paradigms  

Researchers have converged on three canonical families for PEFT, each embodying a distinct design philosophy regarding *where* adaptivity is injected into an otherwise frozen foundation model.

## Model Fine‑Tuning  

In its most straightforward incarnation, **model fine‑tuning** leaves the architecture untouched but updates only a selected subset—or sometimes all—of the existing weight matrices using gradient descent. This approach inherits all structural inductive biases baked into the pretrained model without introducing any extra components at inference time; thus *no additional latency* arises when deploying the adapted system—a property highlighted repeatedly throughout recent literature \cite{hulora2022,qiu2023controlling}.  

Beyond naive selection heuristics such as using a small learning rate \cite{raffel2020exploring}, more principled variants have been explored that respect geometric relationships among neurons. One notable example is **Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning (OFT)** \cite{qiu2023controlling}, which constrains each transformed layer by a trainable orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. Because orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles between column vectors (“neurons”), OFT provably maintains relational information originally encoded during pretraining—a desirable regularity fostering better generalization \cite{liu2018learning,qiu2023controlling}.  

From a parameter perspective, representing $\mathbf{R}$ densely would require $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$ trainable scalars—clearly at odds with efficiency goals when $d$ is large. The original OFT therefore introduced a **block‐diagonal** sparsity pattern:
\[
\mathbf{R} = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{R}_{r})\,,\qquad 
\mathbf{R}_{i}\in \mathbb{R}^{b\times b},\quad br=d,
\]
which reduces trainable count proportionally to $bd$, yet imposes an artificial grouping constraint on neuron dimensions.\footnote{The aforementioned constraint may limit expressiveness because dimensions belonging to distinct blocks never interact under $\mathbf{R}$.}

Nevertheless, model fine‑tuning remains attractive because it does not alter runtime computation graph—every forward pass still executes precisely the same sequence of matrix multiplications defined by the original backbone.\footnote{The cost overhead appears only during training where multiple sparse orthogonal matrices are multiplied at each step if richer factorizations are employed; after adaptation they can be absorbed into the frozen weights without extra inference cost—a point emphasized later when discussing Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT).}

## Adapter Modules  

A second family augments each layer with **adapter modules**, small neural subcomponents inserted between existing transformations while leaving pretrained weights frozen \cite{houlsby2019parameter,pfeiffer2020adapterfusion}. Concretely, after computing $\mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{W}^{\top}\!\mathbf{x}$ one passes $\mathbf{x}'$ through an adapter consisting typically of a down‐projection $\mathbf{W}_{\downarrow}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}\times r}$ followed by an up‐projection $\mathbf{W}_{\uparrow}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\text{out}}}$ where $r \ll d_{\text{in}}, d_{\text{out}}$. Because $r$ is intentionally tiny—often only a few percent of hidden dimension—the total extra trainable parameters per adapter are roughly $\mathcal( rd )$, far lower than updating all elements of $\mathbf W$.  

Adapter approaches introduce ***additional computational steps*** during inference—a small amount relative to full matrix multiplication but nonetheless nonzero—so they entail modest latency increments compared with pure model fine‑tuning. Nevertheless they offer greater flexibility than block-diagonal orthogonal transforms because adapters can learn arbitrary linear functions within their bottleneck subspace.

## Prompt Tuning  

A third line exploits **prompt tuning**, wherein task‐specific information is encoded as learnable *prefix tokens* attached directly to input sequences before feeding them through the frozen backbone \cite{li2021prefix,lester2021power}. In language models this amounts to prepending $p$ virtual token embeddings $\mathbf P\in\mathbb R^{p\times d_{\text{model}}}$ that are optimized while all internal weights remain untouched:
\[
[\texttt{\small <prompt>}_1,\dots,\texttt{\small <prompt>}_p,\texttt{\small <input>}] 
\xrightarrow{\;\textnormal{\fontsize}{10}{10}\selectfont  \texttt{\small backbone}}\;
\texttt{\small output}.
\]
Because only embedding vectors are trained—their total count scales as $p\, d_{\text{{model}}}$—the parameter budget can be minuscule even when $p$ is small (\eg~10–20 tokens). Importantly this scheme imposes *no change* on any weight matrices or activation pathways during forward passes; thus it satisfies zero‐latency requirements completely.\footnote{The tradeoff is limited expressive power since all adaptation must be mediated through modifications at the input embedding level rather than directly adjusting deeper transformations.}

---  
These three paradigms constitute complementary corners of design space: *model fine‑tuning* emphasizes minimal runtime alteration at potential cost in trainable size; *adapter modules* enrich expressive capacity while incurring modest latency; *prompt tuning* offers ultra‐lightweight adaptivity but may suffer expressive constraints for complex downstream tasks.\footnote{All three families continue to inspire hybrid designs—e.g., combining adapters with prompt embeddings—to balance tradeoffs further~\cite{sung2021training}.}

# Desiderata for Parameter–Efficient Fine–Tuning Methods  

Effective PEFT strategies ought not only to reduce raw parameter count but also satisfy broader practical criteria derived from real-world deployment constraints:

## Low Parameter Budget  

A primary goal is minimizing *the number of free variables* introduced during adaptation relative to total model size $\lvert \Theta\rvert$. If only $k$ out of $\lvert \Theta\rvert$ parameters are learnable ($k\ll \lvert \Theta\rvert$), both memory allocation for optimizer states and required storage for checkpointing shrink dramatically:
\[
k = \mathcal O(d) \quad
    (\textit {vs.\ }   k = \mathcal O(d^{2}) \textit {for a dense orthogonal update}).
\]
Methods such as block–diagonal OFT achieve this reduction by partitioning dimensions into blocks ($b$ per block gives roughly $bd$ free scalars), while adapter modules rely on rank-$r$ projections yielding approximately $2rd$ free scalars per layer—both substantially less than naïve full fine-tuning’s order-$d^{2}$ cost.\footnote {In practice $b,r,$ or $p$ are chosen via hyperparameter search respecting downstream data volume and hardware limits.}

## No Inference Latency Increase  

From an application standpoint it is often essential that deployment speed remain unchanged after adaptation—especially when serving real-time user queries or processing high-throughput streams (\eg~online translation services). Model fine-tuning uniquely guarantees *zero additional compute*: once training finishes one can simply replace each original weight matrix $\mathbf W^{0}$ by its adapted counterpart $(\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})$, fusing any learned orthogonal transformation into preexisting tensors so that inference proceeds exactly as before.\footnote {Adapter modules incur extra matrix multiplications inside every forward pass but typically add less than 5 % overhead due to their low dimensionality; prompt tuning incurs none because only input embeddings differ—but does require concatenating extra token vectors at runtime.)}

## Good Generalization 

Parameter efficiency should also translate into robust performance on previously unseen inputs within the target domain. Maintaining *knowledge preservation*—i.e., ensuring that adaptations do not catastrophically overwrite useful representations learned during pretraining—is crucial here.\footnote {A recurring observation across recent work is that imposing structural regularities such as orthogonality preserves spectral properties (\eg~singular values) thereby stabilizing gradients and improving transferability~\cite{miyato2018spectral}.} 

In OFT this principle manifests through angle preservation: if neurons before adaptation are represented by column vectors $\{\boldsymbol w_{i}^{0}\}$ then applying an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$ guarantees
\[
\langle\, \boldsymbol w_i^{0},\, \boldsymbol w_j^{0}\,\rangle
= 
\langle\, \mathbf R\, \boldsymbol w_i^{0},\, 
          \mathbf R\, \boldsymbol w_j^{0}\,\rangle,
\]
so relational geometry remains intact throughout training—a property empirically linked with superior convergence rates and reduced overfitting.\cite{sachdeva2018}
Adapters implicitly maintain original representations by restricting updates onto low-rank residuals which often act like gentle nudges rather than wholesale rewrites.\footnote {Similarly prompt tuning respects base internals entirely because it never touches hidden weights directly.)}

Overall, successful PEFT designs strike an optimal balance across these desiderata:
* **Compactness** prevents resource bottlenecks,
* **Latency neutrality** preserves deployment viability,
* **Regularization via structured transformations** fosters transference from massive pretraining reservoirs onto specific downstream domains.



---  

In summary, modern foundation models possess staggering expressive power due chiefly to their enormous parameter counts—a virtue accompanied by prohibitive training costs whose magnitude has spurred intense research into *parameter-efficient adaptation*. By surveying three principal families—model fine-tuning (including orthogonal strategies such as OFT), adapter modules, and prompt tuning—we lay groundwork for deeper investigations into how structural constraints (e.g., sparsity patterns forming butterfly factorizations) can further shrink parameter budgets without compromised accuracy or speed—a theme pursued rigorously in subsequent chapters devoted to Orthogonal Butterfly Fine-Tuning (BOFT).\footnote {The remainder of this textbook explores precisely these ideas: constructing dense orthogonal transformations from products of sparse matrices via butterfly factorizations, analyzing their expressivity under graph‐theoretic “information transmission” lenses, and presenting empirical evidence across vision transformers, language models, and diffusion generators.\hfill ∎}

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Geometry and Motivation  

In modern deep learning, large pretrained “foundation” models are adapted to downstream tasks by updating only a small part of their parameters.  A central challenge is to modify these parameters while preserving the knowledge already captured during pretraining.  **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** addresses this challenge by restricting the adaptation to an *orthogonal* transformation applied uniformly to all neurons in a layer.  The following sections develop the geometric intuition behind OFT, formalize its mathematical underpinnings, describe how orthogonality is enforced during optimization, explain why zero‑initialization stabilizes training, and summarize the empirical advantages reported by the original OFT work.

---

## Angle Preservation Between Neurons as a Principled Regularizer  

A linear layer in a neural network can be written as a weight matrix  
\[
\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
\]  
where each column \(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) corresponds to one output neuron (or feature) of that layer.  The *relational geometry* among neurons is encoded by the pairwise inner products  
\[
\langle \mathbf{w}^{0}_{i},\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j}\rangle 
   \;=\;\big(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\big)^{\!\top}\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j},
\]  
or equivalently by the angles  
\[
\theta_{ij}
   \;=\;\arccos\frac{\langle \mathbf{w}^{0}_{i},\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j}\rangle}
                         {\|\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\|\,\|\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j}\|}.
\]  

During pretraining these angles capture semantic relationships: neurons whose weight vectors point in similar directions respond similarly to inputs, while those whose vectors are nearly orthogonal encode distinct features.  If finetuning were to arbitrarily distort these angles, the pretrained knowledge could be lost or corrupted, leading to poor generalization on new data.

A **principled regularizer** therefore aims to keep all pairwise angles unchanged while still allowing the layer to adapt to task‑specific signals.  Orthogonal transformations satisfy exactly this requirement because an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) obeys  
\[
\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}_{d}.
\]  
Multiplying every column vector by the same \(\mathbf{R}\) yields transformed columns \(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{i}= \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\).  Their inner product satisfies  
\[
\langle \tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{i}, \tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{j} \rangle
   = (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i})^{\!\top}(\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j})
   = (\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i})^{\!\top} \underbrace{\big(\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}\big)}_{\!\mathbf I}
      \,\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j}
   = \langle \mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}, \mathbf{w}^{0}_{j} \rangle,
\]  
and consequently every angle \(\theta_{ij}\) remains exactly the same.  Thus an *orthogonal finetuning* step implements an **angle‑preserving regularizer** without needing any explicit penalty term—the geometry is enforced automatically by the algebraic property of orthogonality.

---

## Mathematical Formulation: Orthogonal Multiplicative Update  

The central idea of OFT is to reparameterize the *new* weight matrix of a layer as a product of a learnable orthogonal matrix and the frozen pretrained matrix:
\[
\boxed{\;\;\tilde{\mathbf W}= \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0}\;\;}
   \qquad 
   \text{with }\;\; 
   \mathbf R^\!\top \mathbf R = \mathbf I_{d}, 
   \;\;
   \mathbf R \in\mathbb {R}^{d\times d}.
\]  

During a forward pass on an input vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb {R}^{d}\), the output becomes
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf z 
      &= (\tilde{\mathbf W})^{\!\top} \,\mathbf{x}
       = (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top} \,\mathbf{x}
        = (\mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top} (\mathbf R^{\!\top} \,\boldsymbol{x})
        .
\end{aligned}
\]  
Because only \(\mathbf R\) changes during finetuning while \(\mathbf W^{0}\) stays fixed, all updates are confined to a *single* orthogonal transformation that rotates (or reflects) the representation space learned by pretraining.  This multiplicative update differs from additive low‑rank methods such as LoRA; instead of adding new directions it *re‑orients* existing ones while preserving lengths and inner products among them.

When every layer of a deep model adopts this reparameterization, the entire network undergoes a sequence of coordinated rotations that adapt its behavior without destroying its original geometric structure.

# Angle Preservation Between Neurons as a Principled Regularizer  

Consider a linear layer whose pretrained weight matrix is denoted  

\[
\mathbf W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n},
\]  

where each column $\mathbf w^{0}_{i}\in\mathbb R^{d}$ corresponds to one output neuron (or feature).  The relational geometry among these neurons is captured by their pairwise inner products $\langle \mathbf w^{0}_{i},\mathbf w^{0}_{j}\rangle$ or equivalently by the angles  

\[
\theta_{ij}= 
      \arccos
      \frac{
          \langle \mathbf w^{0}_{i},\,\mathbf w^{0}_{j}\rangle
      }{
          \|\mathbf w^{0}_{i}\|\,\|\mathbf w^{0}_{j}\|
      } .
\]  

During pretraining these angles encode semantic similarities: neurons whose weight vectors point in similar directions tend to respond similarly to inputs, whereas nearly orthogonal vectors encode complementary information.  When fine‑tuning a model for a downstream task we would like to retain this relational structure because it embodies most of the knowledge learned from massive upstream data.  A principled regularizer therefore aims **to keep every pairwise angle unchanged** while still allowing task‑specific adaptation.

An *orthogonal* transformation provides exactly this guarantee.  If $\displaystyle
    \mathbf R^\top    \!\!  	extbf I_{d}$,
then for any two columns $\b w_i^0$ and $\b w_j^0$

\[
(\b R\b w_i^0)^\top (\b R\b w_j^0)
       = (\b w_i^0)^\top\b R^\top\b R\b w_j^0
       = (\b w_i^0)^\top\b w_j^0,
\]

so their inner product—and consequently their angle—remains identical after transformation.  By applying **the same** orthogonal matrix $\b R$ simultaneously to every column, OFT forces all pairwise angles between neurons to stay exactly as they were after pretraining.  In this sense angle preservation acts as an implicit regularizer embedded directly into the model’s parameterisation rather than being added as an auxiliary loss term.

The core idea of Orthogonal Finetuning is to re‑parameterise each weight matrix with an *orthogonal left‑multiplication*:  

\[
\fbox{$
    \tilde{\b W}=\,\b R\,\,{\b W}^{\,0},
    \
    {\rm where }\;
    {\b R}^\top {\b R}= {\bf I}_d,
    \
    {\b R}\in\rbb f ^ {d\times d}
$}.
\\[4pt]
```Margin note omitted```

During inference or forward propagation an input vector $\b x∈ℝ^{d}$ produces

```
z =
(tilde_W)^⊤ x
=
(RW⁰)^⊤ x
=
(W⁰)^⊤(Rᵀx)
```

Thus only $\b R$ changes during fine‑tuning while $\b W⁰$ remains frozen.  The entire adaptation process reduces to learning **a single rotation (or reflection) of the representation space** per layer rather than altering every individual entry of $\b W⁰$.  Compared with additive low–rank approaches such as LoRA—which introduce new directions—the multiplicative form preserves both lengths and mutual orientations of all pretrained weight vectors.


# Cayley Transform for Enforcing Orthogonality During Training  

Optimising directly over matrices constrained by $\b R^\top\b R={\bf I}_d$ is inconvenient because stochastic gradient descent does not naturally respect such manifolds.  The original OFT sidesteps this difficulty by employing **the Cayley transform**, which maps any skew‑symmetric matrix $\b Q$ onto an orthogonal matrix:

```math
\b R 
      = (\bf I +  	extbf Q)\bigl( {\bf I} -  	extbf Q\bigr)^{-1},
      \
      {\rm where }\;
      {\bf Q}= -{\bf Q}^\top .
```

Because every skew‑symmetric $\b Q$ has $\tfrac12 d(d-1)$ independent entries, optimisation can be performed on these unconstrained parameters using ordinary gradient descent.  After each gradient step one simply recomputes $\b R$ via the above formula; by construction $ \b R $ remains perfectly orthogonal regardless of how $\b Q$ changes.  No projection onto an orthogonal manifold or penalty term is required, which makes optimisation both **exact** (the constraint never drifts) and **efficient** (the computation involves only standard matrix operations).


# Zero‑Initialization via Identity Matrix and Its Effect on Stability  

Fine‑tuning large pretrained models typically starts from parameters that already perform well on many inputs.  To avoid perturbing this good initialisation unnecessarily many PEFT methods initialise their trainable components so that they contribute nothing at time zero—for example LoRA sets its low‑rank update matrices to zero.  

In OFT the natural analogue is **identity initialisation** of the orthogonal component:

* Set all entries of $\b Q$ to zero before training.
* Consequently $( {\bf I}+{\bf Q})( {\bf I}-{\bf Q})^{-1}= {\bf I}_d$, i.e., $\b R$ starts as the identity matrix.
* Hence $\tilde{\b W}= {\bf I}_d\,{\b W}^{\,0}= {\b W}^{\,0}$ at epoch zero.

This design ensures that at the outset there is *exactly* no change from the pretrained network, thereby preserving pretraining performance before any gradient information accumulates.  Because subsequent updates are performed through a smooth manifold (the space of orthogonal matrices), even relatively large learning rates produce gradual rotations rather than abrupt jumps in parameter space, which empirically leads to **more stable optimisation**—a phenomenon emphasized throughout the original OFT experiments.


# Empirical Benefits Reported in Prior OFT Work  

The authors of OFT evaluated this geometric framework on several demanding adaptation tasks:

* **Text‑to‑image diffusion models** – OFT was applied when users wanted fine‑grained control over generated images.  In these experiments OFT achieved *stronger generalisation* across unseen prompts compared with additive low‑rank baselines such as LoRA.
* **Training stability** – Across multiple runs OFT displayed *consistent convergence*, exhibiting fewer spikes or divergences during early epochs when using typical learning rates for large models.
* **Preservation of pretrained knowledge** – By maintaining exact pairwise angles among neurons throughout fine‑tuning, OFT retained more of the semantic structure encoded during upstream training, which translated into higher downstream task accuracy at comparable parameter budgets.
* **Parameter budget** – Although plain OFT employs dense $d\times d$ orthogonal matrices—hence more trainable parameters than low‑rank approaches—the authors demonstrated that even its block‑diagonal variant already outperforms LoRA while remaining practical for very large layers when computational resources permit.

Collectively these findings suggest that adopting an angle‑preserving *orthogonal* transformation provides a **principled route** toward adapting massive foundation models without sacrificing either stability or downstream performance.


---  

In summary, Orthogonal Finetuning leverages a fundamental geometric property—preservation of inter‑neuron angles—to construct an elegant regulariser built directly into model parameters via an orthogonal left multiplication.  The Cayley transform guarantees exact feasibility during optimisation, while identity initialisation secures smooth entry into training dynamics.  Empirically this construction leads to robust adaptation across vision and language domains, positioning OFT as a conceptually simple yet powerful alternative within today’s landscape of parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning techniques.

Limits of Conventional OFT Designs
===================================

In order to appreciate the innovations that later chapters will present, it is essential first to understand why the original **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** framework incurs a heavy parameter cost and how its most common remedy—imposing a block‑diagonal structure—trades expressive power for efficiency.  This section dissects these trade‑offs from both a geometric and an algorithmic perspective, giving the reader a clear intuition for what a “dense” orthogonal transformation buys and why its naïve implementation is impractical for today’s large foundation models.

## Dense versus Block‑Diagonal Orthogonal Matrices

The core idea of OFT is to preserve **pairwise angles** between neurons when adapting a pretrained linear layer.  Consider a pretrained weight matrix  
\[
\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
\]
where each column \(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) corresponds to one neuron’s input‑side parameters.  During finetuning OFT replaces the forward computation  

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}
\qquad (\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})
\]

with  

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf{x},
\]

where \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is constrained to be **orthogonal** (\(\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}_{d}\)).  An orthogonal matrix implements a rotation or reflection of the ambient space; consequently every inner product \(\langle \mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}, \mathbf{w}^{0}_{j}\rangle\) – and hence every angle between two neurons – remains unchanged after multiplication by \(\mathbf{R}\).  From a geometric standpoint this means that the knowledge encoded in the pretraining phase stays intact while still allowing the model to reorient its feature space toward the downstream task.

A **dense** orthogonal matrix has no enforced zeros: all \(d^{2}\) entries may be non‑zero (subject only to the orthogonality constraints).  Such a matrix can rotate any direction into any other direction, providing maximal flexibility for adapting the pretrained features.  In contrast, a **block‑diagonal** orthogonal matrix restricts rotation to within predetermined subspaces; the matrix consists of several smaller orthogonal blocks placed along its diagonal and zeros elsewhere:

\[
\mathbf{R}= \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_{1},\,\mathbf{R}_{2},\,\dotsc ,\,\mathbf{R}_{r}),
\qquad
\mathbf{R}_{i}\in \mathbb{R}^{b\times b},
\]
with \(b r = d.\)  Each \(\mathbf{R}_{i}\) only mixes dimensions that belong to its own block, leaving all cross‑block interactions untouched.

Intuitively, a dense \(\mathbf{R}\) can be thought of as a “global” reshape of the feature space, whereas a block‑diagonal \(\mathbf{R}\) behaves like many independent “local” reshapes running in parallel.  The former offers complete expressivity at the price of many learnable numbers; the latter offers drastic parameter savings but at the cost of severely limiting how information flows between groups of dimensions.


## Parameter Scaling \(O(d^{2})\) for Full Orthogonal Matrices

To parametrize an arbitrary orthogonal matrix one typically adopts a *Cayley transform*:

\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\qquad
\mathbf{Q}^\top = -\,\mathbf{Q}.
\]

The skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) contains all degrees of freedom of \(\mathbf{R}\).  A real \(d\times d\) skew‑symmetric matrix has exactly  

\[
|\, \{\,(i,j)\mid 1 \le i < j \le d\,\} | = \frac{d(d-1)}{2}
\]

independent entries (each off‑diagonal pair contributes one parameter while diagonal entries are forced to zero).  Hence learning an unrestricted orthogonal transformation costs  

\[
O(d^{2})
=
O(d(d-1)/2)
=
O(d^{2})
\]

trainable parameters for **each** finetuned linear layer.

When foundation models contain layers with widths ranging from several hundred up to tens of thousands, this quadratic dependence quickly becomes prohibitive: storing even a single dense orthogonal matrix may exceed GPU memory limits, and back‑propagating through hundreds of such matrices would dramatically increase training time.  Consequently, without any reduction technique OFT cannot be applied to modern large‑scale networks in a practical manner.


## Block‑Diagonal OFT: Sparsity Pattern and Reduction to \(O(b\,d)\) Parameters

The most direct way to shrink the parameter budget is to impose sparsity patterns on \(\mathbf{R}\).  The simplest such pattern—adopted in the original OFT formulation—is **block diagonal**.  By dividing the input dimension \(d\) into \(r=d/b\) equal parts and allowing each part only its own orthogonal transformation \(\mathbf{R}_{i}\), one replaces one massive \(d\times d\) rotation by many smaller rotations:

* Each submatrix \(\mathbf{R}_{i}\in \mathbb{R}^{b\times b}\) still requires  
\(b(b-1)/2 = O(b^{2})\) parameters via its own Cayley transform.
* Since there are \(r=d/b\) blocks, the total number of trainable scalar values becomes  

  \[
  r\,\frac{b(b-1)}{2}
   = \frac{d}{b}\,\frac{b(b-1)}{'2}
   = \frac{{d}(b-1)}{'2}
   = O(b\,d).
  \]

Thus when we set \(b \ll d,\) e.g., \(b=16,\ d=1024,\) we shrink from roughly one million parameters down to roughly sixteen thousand—a reduction by two orders of magnitude.

## Inductive Bias Introduced by Fixed Block Partitioning

A *bias* in machine learning refers not just to an asymmetry in data but also to constraints imposed by model architecture that shape which functions can be represented efficiently.  
In block‑diagonal OFT three intertwined sources of inductive bias arise:

1. **Locality Bias** – By construction each dimension group interacts only with dimensions belonging to the same group. If useful information resides across groups (as it often does in vision or language representations where features are highly entangled), those interactions must pass through indirect pathways such as deeper layers or additional residual connections, potentially slowing convergence or limiting final performance.
   
2. **Index‑Based Grouping Bias** – The groups are formed based purely on their numerical indices rather than any learned similarity between dimensions. This arbitrary partitioning may split semantically related features into different blocks or lump unrelated ones together, forcing the model either not to correlate them at all or to rely on other layers that may already be saturated.
   
3. **Expressivity Limitation** – As an extreme case consider trying to approximate a classic linear transform such as the discrete Fourier transform (DFT). The DFT mixes all frequencies globally and cannot be expressed exactly by any finite block‑diagonal product because every output coefficient depends on every input coefficient. Consequently any attempt using only block‑diagonal rotations will inevitably incur approximation error no matter how large we make \(b<D.\)

From an information‐theoretic view these biases reduce *effective capacity*: they restrict what kinds of rotations can be represented without spending additional parameters elsewhere (e.g., adding many more blocks or deeper fine‐tuning stages). Empirically this manifests as slower training or lower downstream accuracy compared with full OFT when sufficient computational budget is available.


## Motivation for a Dense Yet Parameter‑Efficient Orthogonal Representation

The observations above motivate seeking an architectural compromise that preserves **global mixing**—the hallmark advantage of dense rotations—while keeping *parameter counts* well below quadratic growth.  An ideal solution would satisfy three desiderata identified implicitly by the authors:

| Desideratum | Intuition |
|---|---|
| **Dense Connectivity** | Every input dimension should ultimately influence every output dimension so that global relationships can be learned directly. |
| **Parameter Economy** | Total trainable numbers should scale close­to linearly with \(d,\) ideally \(O(d\,{\rm polylog}(d)),\) rather than quadratically. |
| **Orthogonality Preservation** | The transformation must remain strictly orthogonal throughout training so that pairwise angles—and thus pretraining knowledge—are retained without extra regularization tricks.|

Achieving dense connectivity without storing all \(d^{2}\) entries necessitates *factorizing* the target dense rotation into a product of several sparse factors—a strategy reminiscent of how fast Fourier transforms compress computation via butterfly patterns. Each sparse factor contributes only \(O(d)\) nonzero entries but collectively they propagate information across every pair of dimensions after enough successive multiplications.

The key insight is that rather than imposing *hard* zero patterns at training time (as in block diagonal), one can encode *structured* zero patterns that are mathematically guaranteed—by construction—to yield full connectivity after composition while still requiring far fewer independent scalar values than storing an arbitrarily dense matrix outright.

This line of reasoning sets up subsequent chapters where such factorized structures (specifically butterfly factorizations) are introduced formally as “Orthogonal Butterfly” (**BOFT**) methods that achieve precisely this dense yet parameter-efficient goal.


--- 

In summary, conventional OFT designs suffer from an inherent tension: using an unrestricted dense orthogonal matrix guarantees maximal expressive power but incurs prohibitive quadratic parameter costs; replacing it with a naive block‑diagonal sparsity saves parameters linearly but injects rigid inductive biases that limit global information flow and hamper performance on tasks requiring cross‑dimensional interaction. Understanding these limits motivates exploring structured factorizations—like butterfly matrices—that retain global mixing while dramatically curtailing trainable degrees of freedom—a theme explored in depth in later sections.

Information‑Transmission View of Orthogonal Matrix Factorizations  

# Grid Representation with $d$ Source Nodes → $d$ Target Nodes over $m\!+\!1$ Layers  

When we seek to replace a dense orthogonal transformation by a product of several sparse factors, it is useful to imagine the computation as a flow of information across a layered graph.  Consider a linear layer of dimension $d$ that we would like to transform by an orthogonal matrix  
\[
\bm{R}\;=\;\bm{B}_{m}\,\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots \bm{B}_{2}\,\bm{B}_{1},
\]  
where each factor $\bm{B}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is sparse and will be learned during finetuning.  The product can be visualised as a grid consisting of $m+1$ vertical levels (or “columns’’) and $d$ horizontal positions (“nodes’’) at each level.  The leftmost column contains the **source nodes**—the original coordinates of the input features—while the rightmost column contains the **target nodes**, i.e., the coordinates after applying the full transformation $\bm{R}$.  

At level $0$ we place a copy of each source node indexed by $j\in\{1,\dots,d\}$.  At level $i\;(1\le i\le m)$ we place another copy of the $d$ nodes; these represent intermediate representations after multiplication by $\bm{B}_{i}$.  Finally, at level $m$ we have the $d$ target nodes after all factors have been applied.  Traversing from left to right corresponds exactly to sequential left‑multiplication by $\bm{B}_{1},\,\bm{B}_{2},\dots,\bm{B}_{m}$.  

Because every factor is linear, information can travel only along directed edges that link a node at level $i-1$ to a node at level $i$.  The overall layout therefore forms a **grid‑structured directed acyclic graph** whose connectivity is dictated by the sparsity patterns of the matrices $\{\bm{B}_{i}\}$ (see Figure \ref{fig:info_trans} for an illustrative example with $d=4$, $m=5$).  This graphical picture provides an intuitive scaffold for reasoning about how many parameters are truly needed to realise any dense orthogonal matrix.



# Edges ↔ Non‑Zero Entries in Factor Matrices $\boldsymbol B_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol B_{m}$  

In linear algebra, an entry $[\bm B_i]_{pq}=0$ means that component $p$ receives no contribution from component $q$ during multiplication by $\bm B_i$.  In the graph view this translates directly into *absence* of an edge from node $q$ at level $i-1$ to node $p$ at level $i$. Conversely, any non‑zero entry corresponds to a *directed edge* carrying information from one level’s node to another’s.  Hence **the set of all directed edges across all levels is identical to the union of all non‑zero positions** in the factor matrices:
\[
E \;=\;\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\bigl\{\,(p,q)\mid [\bm B_i]_{pq}\neq0\,\bigr\}.
\]  

Because each factor is square ($d\times d$), there are at most $d^{2}$ possible directed connections per layer.  When every entry were non‑zero we would obtain a fully connected bipartite graph between successive layers—a case that corresponds precisely to using a single dense matrix $\bm R$, i.e., $m=1$, $\bm B_{1}=\bm R$.  In practice we deliberately enforce sparsity so that only a small subset of those potential connections remains trainable; those surviving connections become our **free edges**, i.e., parameters we must learn during finetuning.



# Dense Connectivity Requirement and Minimal Free Edges Principle  

Two desiderata emerge naturally from the information‑transmission perspective:

1. **Dense connectivity** – For the final product $\bm R=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\bm B_i$ to be dense (no zero entry), every source node must possess *at least one directed path* that reaches **every** target node.  In graph terms this means that for any pair $(s,t)$ with source index $s\in[1,d]$ and target index $t\in[1,d]$, there exists a sequence $(s=v_0,v_1,\dots,v_m=t)$ such that each successive pair $(v_{k-1},v_k)$ belongs to an edge set contributed by $\bm B_k$.  If some pair lacks such a path then the corresponding entry $(t,s)$ in $\bm R$ will inevitably be zero, violating density.

2. **Minimal free edges** – Among all edge configurations satisfying dense connectivity we wish to minimise the total number of *trainable* connections (i.e., those not forced by structural constraints).  A smaller number of free edges translates directly into fewer learnable parameters and thus higher parameter efficiency—a primary goal when adapting gigantic foundation models.

Together these conditions define an optimisation problem over graph topologies: find a set of sparsity patterns for $\{\bm B_i\}$ such that every source can broadcast its signal throughout all levels using as few transmissible links as possible.



# Implications of Orthogonality on Edge Constraints  

Orthogonality imposes additional algebraic constraints on each factor matrix beyond mere sparsity.  By definition an orthogonal matrix satisfies  
\[
\bm B_i^\top \,\bm B_i = \bm I_d,
\]  
which immediately forces two crucial properties:

* **Full rank** – Each $\bm B_i$ must be nonsingular, meaning it establishes a bijection between its input and output nodes.  

* **Exactly $d** distinct “mandatory’’ connections per layer – To achieve full rank while preserving orthogonality, every row must contain exactly one non‑zero entry whose magnitude equals one (up to sign), and similarly each column must contain exactly one such entry.  In graphical terms this enforces a *perfect matching* between nodes at consecutive levels: there are precisely $d$ directed edges whose weights are fixed (\(\pm1\)) solely by necessity, not by learning choice.

Because these mandatory \(d\) edges are completely determined by orthogonality—they cannot be altered without breaking invertibility—they are conventionally **excluded from our count** of free parameters.  The remaining entries may still be zero or learnable non‑zero values; their pattern constitutes the *degrees of freedom* we can exploit for information transmission.

Orthogonality also introduces **dependency among optional edges**.  For instance consider a \(2\times2\) orthogonal block:
\[
\begin{bmatrix}
a & b\\
c & d
\end{bmatrix},
\quad
a^{2}+c^{2}=b^{2}+d^{2}=1,
\quad 
ab+cd=0.
\]
If we set \(a=0,\) then orthonormality forces \(c=\pm1,\) which in turn mandates \(b=0,\) leading \(d=\pm1.\) Thus forcing a zero at any position automatically determines zeros elsewhere—a phenomenon clearly visible when visualising edge patterns in Figure \ref{fig:blk_diag}. Consequently any feasible set of free edges must respect these algebraic couplings: removing one transmissible connection may automatically remove another due purely to orthogonal constraints.



# Comparison of Naïve Dense Factorization versus Structured Factorizations  

A naïve approach would dispense with intermediate layers altogether and learn a single dense orthogonal matrix \(\bm R.\) In graph language this corresponds directly to connecting every source node to every target node using \(d^{2}-d = d(d-1)\) *free* directed edges (the diagonal entries belong to mandatory matches).   For moderate dimensions this leads quickly toward quadratic parameter budgets—e.g., when \(d=4,\) there are \(12\) free parameters required just for density.

Structured factorizations achieve dramatic savings by spreading connectivity across multiple sparse layers while preserving overall density through paths rather than direct links:

* **Block–diagonal OFT** – The original OFT imposes that each factor \(\bm R = \operatorname{diag}(\!\bm R_{1},\,\dots,\!\bm R_{r})\) consist exclusively of independent blocks \(\bm R_{i}\in \mathbb R^{b\times b}\) where \(br=d.\) Graphically this restricts communication within disjoint “clusters’’ so no cross‑cluster paths exist; consequently many source–target pairs become unreachable unless \(b=d.\) While dramatically reducing parameters (\(\mathcal O(bd)\)), this design sacrifices expressive power because dense connectivity cannot be realised unless each block spans all dimensions—precisely what defeats parameter efficiency.

* **Butterfly factorization** – Inspired by Cooley–Tukey’s fast Fourier transform topology, butterfly graphs connect nodes across levels using patterned sparse matrices known as butterfly factors.   A butterfly construction generates exactly \(\mathcal O(d\,\log d)\) free edges while guaranteeing that every source can reach every target through logarithmic‑length paths.   For example Figure \ref{fig:info_trans} shows an explicit construction with five factors ($m=5$) achieving dense output using only ten free edges—already fewer than what would be needed by any single dense factor (twelve for $d=4$).   Importantly these ten free connections obey orthogonality because each butterfly component can itself be rendered orthogonal via Cayley transforms applied on its small blocks (\(2\times2,\) or more generally \(b\times b.\))

Thus moving from naïve density (\(\mathcal O(d^{2})\)) toward structured sparse decompositions yields two key benefits:
  
* A substantial reduction in trainable parameters – from quadratic down to near‐linear (\(\mathcal O(d\,log d)\)).  
* Retention—or even enhancement—of expressive capability because information may still flow freely via multi‑step pathways mandated by graphs such as butterflies or other well‑designed topologies.


Together these observations justify recasting Orthogonal Finetuning as an **information transmission problem** on layered graphs: By engineering sparsity patterns that obey both dense connectivity and minimal free-edge principles under algebraic ortho­gonal constraints, one can obtain compact yet powerful parameterizations suitable for adapting massive foundation models without sacrificing performance.



---  

In summary, representing an orthogonal matrix factorisation as information flow across a grid clarifies why certain sparsity patterns succeed while others fail.  The mapping “edge ⇔ non‑zero entry’’ makes it easy to reason about required connections; dense connectivity guarantees that no destination remains blind; minimal free‑edge designs minimise learnable parameters; and orthogonality imposes exact matchings plus subtle dependencies among admissible edge sets.  This framework not only explains why block‑diagonal OFT struggles with expressivity but also illuminates why butterfly factorizations—by virtue of their carefully crafted sparse graphs—achieve remarkable parameter efficiency without losing density, paving the way for practical large‑model adaptation techniques such as BOFT introduced later in the paper.

# Butterfly Structures from Fast Fourier Transform

The butterfly structure that lies at the heart of the Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT) is a powerful tool for constructing dense orthogonal transformations while keeping the number of trainable parameters modest. In this chapter we develop an intuition for why butterfly factorizations are ideally suited for **information‑efficient transmission** across a layer, we formalize their algebraic construction, and we survey how these matrices have been employed in machine‑learning contexts—most notably as a building block for the **Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning** (BOFT) method. Throughout we stay faithful to the definitions and observations presented in the original research article.

---

## Recap of Radix‑2 Cooley–Tukey Algorithm

The discrete Fourier transform (DFT) maps a length‑$N$ signal $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ to its frequency representation $\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$. A naïve matrix multiplication would require $\mathcal{O}(N^{2})$ operations. The **radix‑2 Cooley–Tukey algorithm** reduces this cost dramatically by recursively splitting an $N$‑point DFT into two $(N/2)$‑point DFTs. Concretely, if $N=2^{L}$ is a power of two, one writes

\[
\hat{\mathbf{x}}[k]
=
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\;e^{-2\pi i kn/N}
=
\sum_{n=0}^{N/2-1}
\bigl(x[2n]+e^{-2\pi i k/N}\;x[2n+1]\bigr)\,
e^{-2\pi i k n/(N/2)} .
\]

The first term inside the parentheses mixes each pair of even and odd samples via a **butterfly operation**, while the outer sum is itself a DFT of half the size. By repeatedly applying this decomposition $L=\log_{2} N$ times, one obtains a factorization of the original DFT matrix into $L$ sparse factors. Each factor couples only pairs of indices that differ in a single binary digit – exactly the pattern depicted by a classic **butterfly graph**. The algorithm therefore achieves an overall computational complexity of $\mathcal{O}(N\log N)$ because each stage contributes only $\mathcal{O}(N)$ elementary additions and multiplications.

From a conceptual standpoint this recursion emphasises two crucial ideas:

1. **Local mixing** – at every stage only a small subset (typically two entries) exchange information.
2. **Global propagation** – after $\log_{2} N$ stages every output coordinate has indirectly received contributions from all input coordinates because information has been passed along a binary tree‑like network.

These two ideas will reappear when we reinterpret butterfly factorizations as dense information pathways within neural networks.

---

## Definition of Butterfly Factor $\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)$ and Recursive Construction of $\mathbf{B}(d)$

To translate the FFT’s computational graph into a matrix language suitable for neural‑network finetuning, the paper introduces a **butterfly factor** parameterised by a dimension $k$ that is itself a power of two:

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{2})\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{4})
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^{k\times k}.
}
\tag{1}
\]

Here each $\mathbf{d}_{i}\in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}$ is an arbitrary vector that fills the diagonal blocks with scalar entries. When all entries are set to $1$, $\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)$ reduces to the binary connectivity pattern used in a single FFT stage: every row contains precisely two non‑zero entries – one per half‑column – thus establishing exactly one directed edge from each source node at level $i$ to two target nodes at level $i+1$.

The full **butterfly matrix** $\mathbf{B}(d)\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ for $d=2^{N}$ is built recursively by stacking such factors at multiple scales:

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{B}(d)
=
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d)\,
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{B}_{1}\!\!\left(\tfrac d 2\right) & \mathbf0\\[4pt]
\mathbf0 & \mathbf{B}_{2}\!\!\left(\tfrac d 2\right)
\end{bmatrix}
=
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d)\,\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\!\left(d,\tfrac d 2\right)\,\cdots\, 
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2).
}
\tag{2}
\]

In this expression:

* $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)$ denotes a **butterfly component**, namely a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are copies of $\mathbf B^{F}(\cdot)$ with block size $k$:
  \[
  \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)=
  \operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\,\underbrace{\mathbf B_{1}^{F}(k),\, \dots,\,
  \mathbf B_{d/k}^{F}(k)}_{d/k~\, \text{blocks}}\,\bigr).
  \]
* $\mathbf B_{1}(\tfrac d 2)$ and $\mathbf B_{2}(\tfrac d 2)$ are themselves butterfly matrices defined on half the dimension.
* The recursion terminates when $k=2$, where each component reduces to a $2\times 2$ block that can be interpreted as a rotation in a two‑dimensional subspace.

Thus $\mathbf B(d)$ is explicitly written as a product of $\log_2 d$ sparse matrices, each having exactly $d$ non‑zero entries (one per row). The sparse pattern at successive levels reflects precisely the connections described by an FFT butterfly graph.

---

## Graphical Interpretation: $\mathcal O(d\,\log d)$ Edges Achieve Full Connectivity

The paper proposes an **information‑transmission viewpoint**: consider $d$ source nodes (the columns of an input vector) at level $0$, and $d$ destination nodes at level $m$, where $m$ denotes the number of factor matrices used in a factorisation
$\displaystyle \mathbf R = \mathbf B_m\, \mathbf B_{m-1}\cdots \mathbf B_1$.
Each intermediate level contains $d$ nodes as well; an edge from node $j$ at level $\ell-1$ to node $i$ at level $\ell$ exists if entry $(i,j)$ in $\mathbf B_\ell$ is non‑zero. The whole product therefore describes how information propagates from sources to sinks through **directed edges across adjacent levels**.

For an arbitrary dense matrix $\mathbf R$, achieving full connectivity would require all possible $d^2 - d$ edges (the diagonal entries correspond to trivial self‑loops that do not need learning). A naïve dense factorization thus incurs
$\Theta(d^{\,2})$
trainable parameters—precisely what makes traditional Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) parameter‑inefficient when using unrestricted orthogonal matrices.

The butterfly construction dramatically reduces this count while still guaranteeing:
* **Dense connectivity** – every source node can reach every sink node after traversing all layers.
* **Sparsity optimality** – each factor contributes exactly $d$ edges (one per row), amounting to 
$\displaystyle m\,d = \mathcal O(d\,\log d)$
edges when we pick 
$m=\log_2 d$
as dictated by Eq.~(2).

Figure 3 in the source material visualizes such a graph for $d=8$. The picture shows that after only three stages ($m=\log_28=3$), any node on the leftmost column can route its signal to any node on the rightmost column via successive pairwise exchanges defined by the butterfly factors. Hence with only 
$\mathcal O(d\,\log d)$ trainable scalar values—the diagonal entries inside each factor—we realize every possible dense orthogonal transformation required for finetuning applications.

## Historical Uses of Butterfly Matrices in Machine Learning

Although invented initially as an algorithmic shortcut for computing DFTs, butterfly matrices have found several influential roles across machine learning:

| Domain | Representative Use | Insight |
|--------|-------------------|---------|
| **Fast linear transforms** | Parameterising structured linear layers as products of butterfly components (e.g., DAO 2019; Chen 2022) | Allows learning customized linear maps that retain fast multiplication speed |
| **Stability of recurrent networks** | Embedding butterfly blocks within RNN transition matrices (Jing 2017) | Orthogonal parts prevent exploding/vanishing gradients |
| **Kernel approximation** | Approximating shift‑invariant kernels via randomized butterfly matrices (Munkhoeva 2018) | Leverages fast multiplication for scalable kernel methods |
| **Sparse training & network compression** | Using butterfly factorizations to impose data-sparse patterns on learned weight matrices (Parker 1995; DAO 2020) | Sparse factors reduce storage while preserving expressive power |

These precedents demonstrate that butterfly structures are not merely computational tricks but also provide *regularizing inductive biases*: their sparsity pattern mirrors many classical transforms—Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), Hadamard transform, discrete cosine transform—each known for efficiently mixing information globally after only logarithmic depth. Consequently they have become natural candidates for *parameter-efficient* reparameterizations like BOFT where we wish to retain rich expressive capacity without paying quadratic parameter costs.

---

## Key Properties Relevant to Orthogonal Parameterisation

When applying butterflies within Orthogonal Finetuning we must satisfy strict algebraic constraints: each factor must be orthogonal so that their product remains an element of the orthogonal group $\mathcal O(d)$. The paper enumerates several properties that make butterflies especially suitable:

1. **Local Orthogonality via Cayley Transform**  
   Each elementary block—particularly those with size $k=2$—can be rendered orthogonal using the Cayley parameterisation:
   \[
   \mathbf R=(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1},
   \qquad
   \mathbf Q^\top = -\,\mathbf Q .
   \]
   For a $2\times 2$ block this reduces to an ordinary rotation angle; higher‐dimensional blocks ($b>1$) are formed by embedding multiple such rotations into larger submatrices while preserving overall orthogonality.

| Factor size | Non‑zero entries per row | Trainable parameters |
|-------------|--------------------------|----------------------|
| $k=2$, block size $b=1$ | 1 | $(b-1)/2 =0$, i.e., identity initialization |
| General $(m,b)$ BOFT | $(b-1)/2\,dm$ effective scalars | $\displaystyle \frac12(b-1)d\,m = \mathcal O(d\,m)$ |

   Because each completed block contributes only $(b-1)/2$d free scalar variables (the remaining degrees are fixed by orthogonality), BOFT achieves
   \[
   |\Theta_{\text{trainable}}|\;=\;\frac12(b-1)d\,m .
   \]
   Choosing {\small$m=\log_{{ } }(d/b)+ {\rm const}$} yields
   $
   |\Theta|\;
    =%
    \mathcal O(d\,\log d)
   $
   parameters—substantially fewer than OFT’s quadratic budget even when OFT opts for dense blocks ($b=d$).

2. **Identity Initialization Guarantees Zero Perturbation**  
   All butterfly components may start as identity matrices simply by initializing their underlying skew–symmetric matrices $\mathbf Q$ as zero. Consequently,
   \[
   \bm R(m,b)=
   \prod_{\ell=1}^{m}\tilde{\bm B}^{\,b}_{(d,\ell)} = \bm I_d ,
   \]
   replicating OFT’s “start close to pretrained” philosophy without sacrificing expressive potential later on.

3. **Structured Expressivity Beyond Block Diagonal OFT**  
   Theorem 4 from the article asserts:
   
   > *BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size.*  
   
   Specifically, composing multiple alternating products
   $
      \bm B_{i,j}(d)\bm B_{i,j}^{\top}(d)
   $
   over indices $(i,j)$ yields any element of $\mathcal O(d)$. In practice, modest depths—
     e.g., BOFT$(6,4)$ versus OFT$(1,16)$—already approximate random dense orthogonal matrices far more accurately while using fewer free parameters (see Figure 5). Thus BOFT balances *expressivity* against *regularity*: it retains strong inductive bias from its structured sparsity yet can still span large swaths of the orthogonal group when needed.

4. **Preservation of Spectral Norms & Training Stability**  
   Multiplying an existing pretrained weight matrix $\bm W^{0}$ on its left by an orthogonal matrix does not alter singular values:
   $
      (\bm R\bW^{0})^\top (\bm R\bW^{0})
      = (\bW^{0})^\top\bW^{0}.
    $
    Hence both OFT and BOFT inherit spectral norm preservation—an attribute linked empirically with stable optimisation and better generalisation in deep networks (\cite{krylov2006review}, although specific citation omitted per instruction).

5. **Compatibility with Multiplicative Dropout**  
   Because BOFT’s transformation is multiplicative rather than additive like LoRA’s low‑rank updates, dropout must be applied *to whole components*. The paper proposes selecting randomly:
    * some fraction $p_{1}$ of entire butterfly components;
    * within each selected component some fraction $p_{2}$ of its diagonal blocks;
     
    These selected parts are replaced by identity operators during training iterations—a “multiplicative dropout” mechanism that mitigates overfitting while respecting orthogonality constraints throughout training epochs.

Collectively these properties make butterflies an ideal vehicle for constructing dense orthogonal transformations under tight parameter budgets—a central requirement when fine‑tuning massive foundation models where naïve full‐matrix optimisation would be prohibitive both in storage and compute.

### Summary

The Cooley–Tukey radix‑2 FFT illuminates how local pairwise exchanges can be cascaded logarithmically to achieve global mixing—a principle captured formally by *butterfly factorizations*. By defining elementary factors $\displaystyle\bm B^{F}(k)$ and recursively assembling them into $\displaystyle\bm B(d)$, we obtain:
* A deterministic sparsity pattern involving only $\mathcal O(d\,\log d)$ nonzero entries.
* A clear graphical model wherein every source node can reach every sink node after traversing all layers.
* A mathematically tractable way to enforce orthogonality via per‐block Cayley parametrisation.
* Proven expressive superiority over naïve block-diagonal orthogonal finetuning while retaining strong regularising inductive bias stemming from classic linear transforms (DFT, Hadamard etc.).

These insights underpin BOFT—the novel “Orthogonal Butterfly” finetuning framework presented in the research article—showing how one can efficiently adapt huge vision transformers, language models and diffusion models without incurring substantial inference overhead or sacrificing training stability. The remainder of this textbook will later explore how BOFT integrates into full model pipelines and compares empirically against other PEFT techniques such as LoRA and adapter tuning.
