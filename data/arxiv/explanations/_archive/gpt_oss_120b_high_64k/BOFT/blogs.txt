# Why Finetuning Massive Foundation Models is So Tough – And How Orthogonal Finetuning Offers a Solution

Foundation models such as GPT‑3, ChatGPT or Stable Diffusion have shown that scaling up model size brings dramatic improvements in language understanding, image generation, and many other tasks.  Yet the very fact that these models contain hundreds of billions of parameters makes **training them from scratch** prohibitively expensive for most research labs.  The natural workaround is to **adapt** an already‑trained model to a new downstream task—known as finetuning—while keeping the heavy lifting of pretraining untouched.  In practice this turns out to be far trickier than simply “plugging in” a new dataset.  Below we unpack why naïve finetuning can erase the knowledge a model has already acquired and how **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** addresses this problem by preserving the relational geometry between neurons.

---

## 1. The Scaling Bottleneck

* **Size matters.** Modern language models exceed 100 B parameters; vision transformers can reach tens of billions; diffusion generators like Stable Diffusion have similarly massive backbones.
* **Training cost skyrockets.** Pretraining on petaflops of compute for weeks or months is out of reach unless you have massive GPU clusters.
* **Finetuning is the pragmatic route:** Instead of retraining, we want to adjust only a small subset of parameters so that the model behaves well on a new task (e.g., sentiment analysis or image captioning) while retaining its broad pretraining knowledge.

---

## 2. Why Naïve Finetuning Often Breaks What We Want to Keep

Finetuning by gradient descent updates *all* trainable weights simultaneously.  Two common “quick fixes” are:

| Strategy | What it does | Why it can be harmful |
|----------|--------------|-----------------------|
| **Small learning rate** | Takes tiny steps so the weights stay close to their pretrained values | Still changes *relative* relationships between weight vectors; nothing guarantees that structural properties learned during pretraining survive |
| **Partial weight updates** (e.g., only bias terms) | Reduces number of changed parameters | The changed subset can still distort the high‑dimensional geometry that encodes semantic information |

The hidden layers of deep networks are essentially collections of *neurons* (columns of a weight matrix).  During pretraining these neurons settle into a geometry where **pairwise angles** capture relational information—similar neurons tend to align, dissimilar ones stay orthogonal.  If finetuning arbitrarily rotates or stretches these vectors, the angle relationships shift and the model may **forget** useful representations—a phenomenon known as *catastrophic forgetting*.

In mathematical terms, let \(\mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}\) be a frozen pretrained linear layer and \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) an input activation.  The original forward pass computes
\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}.
\]
A naïve update would replace \(\mathbf{W}^0\) with \(\mathbf{W}^0 + \Delta\mathbf{W}\).  Even if \(\|\Delta\mathbf{W}\|\) is small, the *angles* between columns of \(\mathbf{W}^0+\Delta\mathbf{W}\) can change dramatically, erasing the relational structure learned during pretraining.

---

## 3. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Preserving Relational Geometry

### 3.1 The Core Idea

Instead of adding an arbitrary perturbation \(\Delta\mathbf{W}\), OFT **multiplies** the pretrained weight matrix on the left by a learnable orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\):
\[
\boxed{\;\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}\;}
\]
where \(\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}_{d}\).

Because an orthogonal transformation preserves inner products:
\[
\langle \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}_i,\;\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}_j\rangle = \langle \mathbf{w}_i,\;\mathbf{w}_j\rangle,
\]
the **pairwise angles** between any two neuron vectors \(\mathbf{w}_i,\;\mathbf{w}_j\) remain unchanged throughout finetuning.  In other words, OFT keeps the *relational geometry* intact while still allowing each neuron to rotate collectively in a shared subspace that can adapt to the downstream task.

### 3.2 Maintaining Orthogonality During Training

Directly optimizing a matrix under an orthogonality constraint is difficult. OFT adopts the classic **Cayley transform**:
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})\,(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\]
where \(\mathbf{Q}\) is skew‑symmetric (\(\mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^\top\)).  
By learning entries of \(\mathbf{Q}\), orthogonality is automatically enforced at every gradient step—no projection or penalty term is needed.

### 3.3 Zero‑Initialization for Seamless Transfer

Finetuning typically starts from the pretrained model itself so that early training steps do not drift far away from known good parameters.  In OFT this is achieved by initializing \(\mathbf{Q}=0\), which yields \(\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}_{d}\).  Consequently,
\[
\bigl(\underbrace{\mathbf{I}}_{\text{OFT init}}\;\mathbf{W}^0\bigr)^\top = (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top,
\]
so the network behaves exactly like the original model at iteration zero.

---

## 4. Parameter Efficiency – Block‑Diagonal OFT and Its Limits

A full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix contains \(d^{2}\) entries—far too many to store or update for large models (\(d\) often runs into thousands).  The original OFT paper therefore introduced a **block‑diagonal structure**:
\[
\mathbf{R}= \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\,\underbrace{\mathbf{R}_{1}}_{b\times b},\,\underbrace{\mathbf{R}_{2}}_{b\times b},\,\dots ,\,\underbrace{\mathbf{R}_{r}}_{b\times b}\bigr), 
\quad br = d,
\]
where each small block \(\mathbf{R}_{i}\) is itself orthogonal.  

*Pros:*  
- Parameter count drops from \(O(d^{2})\) to \(O(bd)\).  
- Each block can be efficiently parametrized via its own Cayley transform.

*Cons:*  
- The sparsity pattern forces dimensions belonging to different blocks never interact during finetuning; i.e., information cannot flow across blocks.  
- This fixed partitioning introduces an *inductive bias* that may limit expressivity: many classic linear transforms (FFT, Hadamard…) cannot be represented by such a pattern.

Thus while block‑diagonal OFT is more economical than full finetuning, it still trades off **expressivity** for parameter efficiency in a way that may hurt performance on certain tasks.

---

## 5. A Peek at Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT): More Efficient Yet Expressive

Recent work extends OFT by answering: *Can we obtain a dense orthogonal matrix without paying \(O(d^{2})\) parameters?*  

The answer lies in **factorizing** an orthogonal matrix into a product of several *sparse* orthogonal matrices—a concept borrowed from the Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT).  

### 5.1 Information‑Transmission View

Think of each factor as routing “information” between two adjacent layers in a grid graph:
```
source nodes → factor1 → factor2 → … → factor m → destination nodes
```
Dense connectivity at the end means any source node can influence any destination node after passing through all factors. By carefully designing each factor’s sparsity pattern (the *butterfly* pattern), we achieve dense end‑to‑end connectivity using only \(O(d\,\log d)\) trainable entries instead of \(O(d^{2})\).

### 5.2 Butterfly Factorization

A butterfly factor for size \(k=2^{t}\) looks like:
\[
\mathbf B^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
    \operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol d_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol d_{2})\\[4pt]
    \operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol d_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol d_{4})
\end{bmatrix},
\]
where each diagonal vector contains learnable scalars (or small blocks).  
Stacking log‑scaled butterfly components yields a dense orthogonal matrix:
\[
\mathbf R(m,b)=
    \tilde{\!\!\!\!B}^{b}_{(d,m)}\, 
    \tilde{\!\!\!\!B}^{b}_{(d,m-1)}\, 
    \dots\, 
    \tilde{\!\!\!\!B}^{b}_{(d,1)},
\]
with \(m=\log_2(d/b)\).  

Crucially:
* each component remains orthogonal (parameterized via Cayley on tiny sub‑blocks);
* total effective trainable parameters become 
   \[
   O(b\,d\,m)=O(d\,\log d)\quad (\text{substituting } b=2).
   \]

When \(m=1\), BOFT collapses back to block‑diagonal OFT; when \(b=d,\;m=1,\) it recovers full OFT. Thus BOFT **subsumes** existing approaches while offering a smoother trade‑off between expressivity and parameter budget.

### 5.3 Empirical Takeaway

Experiments on large Vision Transformers, LLMs and text‑to‑image diffusion models show that BOFT matches or exceeds state‑of‑the‑art parameter‑efficient methods such as LoRA while preserving the stability benefits inherited from orthogonality (e.g., unchanged spectral norm).

---

## 6. Why Orthogonal Finetuning Works Across Modalities

The geometric preservation afforded by an orthogonal left‑multiply has several practical implications:

1. **Spectral Norm Stability:** Multiplying by an orthogonal matrix does not change singular values; therefore training remains numerically stable even with larger learning rates.
2. **Bilinear Similarity Learning:** The forward pass becomes a bilinear form  
   \[
   s_i = {\bf w}^{0}_i{}^\top {\bf R}{\bf x},
   \] 
   where ${\bf w}^{0}_i$ are frozen pretrained neuron vectors and ${\bf R}$ learns a similarity metric under strong regularisation.
3. **Transferability:** Because relational angles stay fixed, downstream updates are less likely to catastrophically overwrite generic knowledge—this has been observed when adapting diffusion models for controllable image generation and when finetuning LLMs for classification tasks.
4. **No Inference Overhead:** After training finishes we can fold $\bf R$ into $\bf W^0$ once (i.e., replace $\bf W$ with $\bf R\, W^0$). Inference runs exactly as fast as vanilla inference—no extra computation graph needed.

---

## 7. Bottom Line

Finetuning gigantic foundation models poses a paradox: we must let them adapt enough to solve new tasks while preventing them from unraveling their hard‑earned internal representations.  
Naïve gradient updates break relational geometry; low‑rank adapters such as LoRA sidestep this but sacrifice spectral guarantees; block – diagonal OFT protects angles but still carries unnecessary parameter cost and restrictive sparsity patterns.

**Orthogonal Finetuning** offers a principled middle ground: by constraining updates to an orthogonal transformation we retain pairwise neuron angles—and thus much of the pretrained knowledge—while still providing expressive flexibility for downstream adaptation. Recent extensions like **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)** demonstrate that even tighter parameter budgets are achievable without sacrificing expressivity thanks to clever sparse factorizations inspired by fast Fourier transforms.

For anyone looking to adapt massive models without blowing up compute or destroying existing capabilities, OFT—and its butterfly‐enhanced cousin—provide a compelling recipe that balances geometry preservation, training stability, and parameter efficiency—all things we need when working at today’s scale of AI foundations.

# From Fast Fourier Transform to AI: The Butterfly Structure That Makes BOFT Parameter‑Efficient  

*How a classic signal‑processing trick—​the butterfly graph behind the FFT—​turns into a powerful tool for shrinking the number of trainable parameters when fine‑tuning massive foundation models.*

---

## 1️⃣ Why fine‑tune at all?

Large foundation models such as GPT‑3, Vision Transformers, or Stable Diffusion contain billions of parameters. Training them from scratch is out of reach for most research groups, so **parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning** has become the de‑facto approach for adapting these models to downstream tasks (classification, translation, image generation, …).

A popular family of methods keeps the original architecture unchanged and updates only a small set of weights:

| Method | How it works | Typical parameter budget |
|--------|--------------|--------------------------|
| **LoRA** | Adds a low‑rank matrix (`ΔW = ABᵀ`) | `O(r·d)` where `r ≪ d` |
| **Adapter / Prompt** | Inserts small auxiliary modules | Varies |
| **Orthogonal Fine‑tuning (OFT)** | Multiplies each layer by an *orthogonal* matrix `R` (`W' = R·W⁰`) | `O(d²)` (dense `R`) |

The **key idea behind OFT** is simple yet principled: preserve the pairwise angles between neurons in every layer. By keeping `R` orthogonal (`RᵀR = I`), all inner products among row vectors stay unchanged—a property that protects the knowledge stored during pretraining and leads to smoother training dynamics.

Unfortunately, a full `d × d` orthogonal matrix needs roughly `½·d·(d−1)` free parameters—**quadratic** in the hidden dimension `d`. For modern transformers (`d = 1024`–`4096`) this quickly eclipses the budgets of LoRA or adapters.

The original authors of OFT therefore introduced **block‑diagonal constraints** (`R = diag(R₁,…,R_r)`). While this reduces parameters dramatically (`O(b·d)` if each block has size `b`), it also enforces a rigid sparsity pattern that limits expressivity: different groups of neuron dimensions never interact during fine‑tuning.

> **Is there a way to keep `R` *dense*—so every neuron can talk to every other—while still using only *few* trainable numbers?**

The answer lies in an old friend from signal processing: **the butterfly structure** used in the Cooley–Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT).

---

## 2️⃣ Recasting fine‑tuning as information transmission

Before diving into butterflies, it helps to view any matrix factorization as an **information flow problem** on a layered graph:

* We have `d` source nodes (the input dimensions) at level 0.
* After multiplying by a sparse matrix `B₁`, information spreads to level 1.
* Multiplying subsequently by `B₂`, … , finally reaches level `m` where we observe the output dimensions.

If we write  
\[
R = B_m B_{m-1}\dots B_1,
\]  
then each edge in this **grid graph** corresponds to a non‑zero entry in one of the `B_i`. A *dense* final matrix `R` means **every source node must have at least one directed path to every sink node**. Our goal is therefore:

1. **Dense connectivity** – ensure full communication between input and output.
2. **Minimal free edges** – keep the total number of trainable non‑zeros as low as possible while respecting orthogonality (`B_iᵀ B_i = I`).

A naïve solution would stack a single dense orthogonal matrix ⇒ `O(d²)` edges—exactly what we wanted to avoid. The information‑transmission view tells us we can achieve dense connectivity *through many stages* with far fewer edges if we pick smart sparsity patterns for each stage.

Enter the butterfly network.

---

## 3️⃣ The butterfly factorization ‑ from FFT to neural nets

### 3.1 A quick refresher on the Cooley–Tukey FFT

The discrete Fourier transform (DFT) converts a signal from time domain to frequency domain using an `N × N` dense matrix—a hopelessly expensive operation for large `N`. The Cooley–Tukey algorithm reduces this cost by recursively splitting the DFT into **log₂ N stages**, each stage mixing *pairs* of elements only. Visualizing each stage as a sparse matrix gives precisely the **butterfly pattern**:

```
stage 0:   x0 ──►──┐
           x1 ──►─┘
stage 1:   …   …
```

Each stage contains exactly `N` non‑zero entries (pairwise permutations + scaling), yet after `log₂ N` stages we recover the full DFT—a dense linear map—using only `O(N log N)` operations.

### 3.2 Formal definition used in BOFT

For a power‑of‑two size `k`, define a *butterfly factor*  

\[
B^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(d_1) & \operatorname{diag}(d_2)\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(d_3) & \operatorname{diag}(d_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad d_i \in \mathbb R^{k/2},
\]  

where each diagonal block scales half of the inputs before they are shuffled across halves.  

A **butterfly component** repeats this factor block‑diagonally:

\[
\tilde B(d,k)=
\operatorname{diag}\!\big(
B^{F}_1(k),\, B^{F}_2(k),\,\dots,\,
B^{F}_{d/k}(k)
\big),
\]  

so it still has only `d` non‑zero entries regardless of its internal block size `k`.

Constructing an entire **butterfly matrix** amounts to stacking components with decreasing block sizes:

\[
B(d)=\tilde B(d,d)\,\tilde B\!\big(d,\tfrac d2\big)\,
      \tilde B\!\big(d,\tfrac d4\big)\dots
      \tilde B(d,2).
\]

If we enforce *orthogonality* on *every* component (`$\tilde B^\top \tilde B = I$`)—for instance by applying a Cayley transform or directly parameterizing each `2×2` block as a rotation angle—then their product remains orthogonal:

\[
R_{\text{butterfly}} = \prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde B_{(d,i)}^{\,b}\quad
    \Longrightarrow \quad
    R_{\text{butterfly}}^\top R_{\text{butterfly}} = I.
\]

Here,
- `m = log₂(d/k)` is the number of stages,
- `b` denotes the size of internal blocks (e.g., setting `b=2` yields classic butterflies).

### 3.3 Parameter count

Each stage contributes exactly `d·(b−1)/2` free scalars (the diagonal vectors inside every factor after removing trivial sign constraints). Therefore,

\[
\#\text{trainable params}
      = \frac12(b-1)\, d\, m
      = \mathcal O(d\,\log d)\quad(\text{for } b=2).
\]

Contrast this with:
- Dense OFT             → $\mathcal O(d²)$,
- Block‑diagonal OFT (`r` blocks) → $\mathcal O(b\,d)` where `$b=d/r$`.

Thus **a butterfly‐parameterized orthogonal matrix reaches full density while using only logarithmic overhead**—exactly what we wanted for parameter‐efficient fine‑tuning.

---

## 4️⃣ Orthogonal Butterfly Fine‑tuning (BOFT)

Putting everything together yields **BOFT**, short for *Orthogonal Butterfly Fine‑tuning*. Its forward pass on a frozen pretrained linear layer `$W⁰ ∈ ℝ^{d×n}`' looks like

```math
z = (\underbrace{R(m,b)}_{\text{product of } m \text{ butterfly components}}
      · W⁰ )^\top x,
```

with  

\[
R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
           \qquad 
           (\tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,i)})^\top
           \tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,i)} = I_d .
\]

### 4️⃣1 Special cases at a glance

| Setting | What you get |
|---------|--------------|
| `$m=1$, b=d` | Original OFT (full dense orthogonal matrix). |
| `$m=1$, b< d` | Block‑diagonal OFT — exactly what previous work used for cheap finetuning. |
| `$m=\log₂ d$, b=2` | Minimal BOFT – dense orthogonal transform with only $\mathcal O(d log d)$ parameters. |

Hence BOFT *subsumes* both prior extremes while offering continuous knobs (`m`, `b`) for trading off expressivity vs.~parameter budget.

### 4️⃣2 Training tricks that make it work

- **Identity initialization** – set all skew‑symmetric generators inside Cayley transforms to zero so every component starts as identity (`R ≈ I`). This mimics “small learning rate” strategies common in other PEFT methods.
- **Multiplicative dropout** – during training randomly replace whole butterfly components *or* individual blocks inside them by identity matrices. Because our update is multiplicative (`W' = R·W⁰`) ordinary additive dropout would break orthogonality; multiplicative dropout preserves it while acting as regularizer.
- **Zero extra inference cost** – after fine‐tuning one can fold `$R$` into `$W⁰$` (i.e., compute `$RW⁰$`) once and discard all additional structures; downstream inference runs exactly like vanilla model inference.

---

## 5️⃣ Why does BOFS help fine‑tuning?

### 5.1 Expressivity vs.~regularity  
A fully unconstrained orthogonal matrix can represent any rotation in high dimensional space—but such freedom often leads to overfitting when only few labeled examples are available. Boft’s structured subset sits *between*:
- It can **recover classic fast transforms** (FFT, Hadamard) thanks to its butterfly topology.
- Yet it still imposes **global coupling among dimensions**, unlike independent block‐diagonal rotations which isolate groups forever.
Empirically this sweet spot translates into better generalisation across vision Transformers, large language models and diffusion generators—the authors report consistent gains over LoRA and over plain OFT under identical parameter budgets.

### 5.2 Spectral stability  
Because `$R$` is orthogonal,
```math
σ_{\max}(RW⁰)=σ_{\max}(W⁰),
```  
the largest singular value—and thus the spectral norm—is preserved exactly. Prior work shows such “norm preservation” improves training stability especially for very deep networks or diffusion pipelines.

### 5.3 Bilinear similarity perspective  
Fine‐tuned scores become  
```math
w_i^{0ᵀ} R x,
```  
i.e., learning an *orthogonal bilinear similarity*. This links directly to metric learning literature where constraining similarity matrices often yields robust representations without sacrificing discriminative power.

---

## 6️⃣ Practical takeaways & limits

| ✅ Pros | ❌ Cons |
|--------|--------|
| Dense orthogonal transformation with only `$O(d log d)` learnable numbers → huge memory savings on large layers.| Slightly higher training time because you multiply several sparse factors per forward pass (still negligible at inference after folding).|
| No extra latency at deployment—​just pre‑multiply once.| Current analysis assumes powers of two dimensions; extending butterflies gracefully to arbitrary sizes needs padding or mixed radix versions.|

The paper also hints at an exciting direction: viewing factorisation through other network topologies from computer networking could yield even tighter “information transmission” graphs than butterflies—an open research frontier beyond this post’s scope.

---

## 7️⃣ TL;DR – What you should remember

- **Orthogonal Fine‑tuning (OFT)** preserves neuron geometry but usually needs `$O(d²)` params.
- Casting matrix generation as an info‑flow problem reveals that *dense connectivity* can be built from many *sparse steps*.
- The classic **FFT butterfly graph** provides exactly such steps: each stage mixes only pairs yet after `log₂ d` stages you obtain any dense linear map.
- By ensuring every butterfly stage stays orthogonal (via Cayley / rotation parametrisation), we get **BOFT**, which:
  - Needs only `$O(d log d)` trainable numbers,
  - Is strictly more expressive than block‐diagonal OFT,
  - Retains spectral stability,
  - Works across vision, language and diffusion models without extra inference cost.
- In short: an algorithm invented in 1965 for fast signal processing now makes modern AI models cheaper—and still powerful—to adapt.

If you’re looking for an alternative to LoRA or adapters when your target model has huge hidden dimensions, give BOFT a try—you’ll get full‐matrix expressive power while paying only logarithmic parameter overhead!



# BOFT 101: A Gentle Introduction to Parameter‑Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning  

*Breaking down the new BOFT method step‑by‑step—starting from orthogonal matrix basics, moving through butterfly components, and showing how they combine into a compact yet expressive transformation for any linear layer.*

---

## Why Finetune at All?

Foundation models such as GPT‑3 or Stable Diffusion contain billions of parameters. Training them from scratch is out of reach for most research groups, so we usually **adapt** a pretrained model to a downstream task instead. The goal is simple: change just enough of the model so that it solves the new task while preserving most of what it already knows.

Popular adaptation tricks include:

| Method | How it works | Inference cost |
|--------|--------------|----------------|
| **Full finetuning** | Update all weights | None extra |
| **Adapters** | Insert small trainable modules | Slight overhead |
| **Prompt tuning** | Learn extra prefix tokens | None extra |
| **Parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT)** | Update only a tiny subset | None extra |

Among PEFT approaches, **model finetuning** stands out because it leaves the original architecture untouched and adds virtually no latency at inference time. The trick is to pick *which* parameters we touch—and how we represent them—to stay both **expressive** and **parameter‑efficient**.


---

## Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): The Idea in One Sentence  

Instead of adding or subtracting something from pretrained weights, OFT **multiplies** every weight matrix by a *learned orthogonal* matrix on the left:

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf{x},
\qquad \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\;\;\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}.
\]

Here \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) is the frozen pretrained weight matrix, \(\mathbf{x}\) is the input activation and \(\mathbf{z}\) the output activation.  

Why an *orthogonal* \(\mathbf{R}\)?  
Orthogonal transforms preserve all pairwise angles between column vectors (the neurons), so the relational geometry learned during pretraining stays intact—a property that correlates strongly with good generalisation.



### From Theory to Practice  

A naïve implementation would treat \(\mathbf{R}\) as a full \(d\times d\) matrix, requiring \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) trainable parameters—clearly impractical for large layers (\(d\!\approx\! 4\,000\) in modern vision transformers). The original OFT paper therefore introduced a **block‑diagonal** constraint:

\[
\mathbf{R}= \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\mathbf{R}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{R}_{r}\bigr), 
\quad 
\mathbf{R}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b},\, br=d .
\]

Now only \(r\cdot b^{2}=bd\) parameters need learning. The trade‑off? Each neuron’s dimensions are split into independent groups that never talk to one another, limiting expressiveness dramatically—the resultant \(\mathbf{R}\) can no longer approximate many useful dense transforms.



---

## An Information‑Transmission Lens

Imagine building a dense matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) as a product of several sparser matrices:

\[
\mathbf{R}= \underbrace{\mathbf{B}_{m}}_{\text{sparse}}\,
          \underbrace{\mathbf{B}_{m-1}}_{\text{sparse}}\cdots 
          \underbrace{\mathbf{B}_{1}}_{\text{sparse}} .
\]

Think of each \(\mathbf{B}_{i}\) as a layer in a grid‑shaped graph that passes “information” from one set of \(d\) nodes (the input dimensions) to another set after \(i\) steps.  

Two desiderata emerge:

1. **Dense connectivity** – after all \(m\) steps every source node must be able to reach every sink node (the resulting product must be dense).  
2. **Few free edges** – we want as few trainable non‑zero entries as possible because sparsity translates directly into fewer parameters.


A naïve dense connection would need \(d^{2}\!-\!d\) edges—far too many. What if we could achieve dense connectivity with far fewer edges? Turns out classical signal processing already solved exactly this problem.



---

## Butterfly Structures — Fast Information Exchange

The Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT) rewrites an \(N\)-point DFT as a cascade of simple *butterfly* operations. In graph terms each butterfly connects pairs of nodes across stages so that after \(\log_{2}N\) stages every node has exchanged information with every other node—all while only using \(N\log_{2} N\) edges!

### The basic building block

For an even size \(k=2^{p}\), define a **butterfly factor**

\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
  \operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{d}_{1}) & 
  \operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{d}_{2})\\[4pt]
  \operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{d}_{3}) &
  \operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{d}_{4})
\end{bmatrix}
        \in \mathbb R^{k\times k},
\]

where each \(\boldsymbol d_{i}\in\mathbb R^{k/2}\).  
Intuitively the four diagonal blocks couple two halves of the vector space together – exactly what an FFT stage does.



### Recursive composition

Let \(d=2^{N}\). A full **butterfly matrix** is built recursively:

```
B(d) = ˜B(d,d) · [ B₁(d/2)     0      ]
                [    0      B₂(d/2)] ,
```

where `˜B(d,k)` is a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are independent copies of `Bᶠ(k)`:

\[
\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)=
     \operatorname {diag}\bigl(
         \underbrace{\mathbf B^{F}(k),\, …,\,
                      \mathbf B^{F}(k)}_{d/k~{\rm times}}
       \bigr).
\]

Repeating this construction yields

\[
\mathbf B(d)=
      \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,d)\,
      \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,d/2)\,
      …\, 
      \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,2),
\]

a product of exactly \(\log_{2} d\) sparse factors, each containing only \(d\) non‑zeros.



### From butterflies to **orthogonal** butterflies

A generic butterfly factor is not automatically orthogonal—it has arbitrary real entries on its diagonals. To keep OFT’s guarantee about angle preservation we force every factor to be orthogonal via the **Cayley transform**:

\[
\mathbf Q=-\,\mathbf Q^\top,\qquad
   \tilde{\mathbf B}= (\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)\,
                     (\mathbf I-\mathbf Q )^{-1}.
\]

For the smallest case \(k=2s\, (=2)\), this reduces simply to learning an angle for a planar rotation—a classic $2\times 2$ orthogonal matrix.\  
Larger blocks (\(k>2\)) inherit their sparsity pattern from the $k=2$ case via permutations, so ensuring orthogonality at one scale automatically ensures it at all scales.



### Block‑wise butterflies

Sometimes we want more flexibility per node—for instance to match convolutional channels rather than individual scalar dimensions. We therefore replace each scalar entry inside $\boldsymbol d_i$ by a small square matrix $\in\bigrl\bigrl...$ This yields **block‑butterfly components**, denoted $\tilde{\bf B}^{\,b}(d,k)$ where each diagonal entry is now $b\times b$. Again we enforce orthogonality on every $2b\times 2b$ sub‑block using Cayley parametrisation.



---

## Putting It All Together – BOFT  

We call the resulting finetuning scheme **Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)** because it plugs an *orthogonal* butterfly product into OFT’s multiplicative update:

> **Forward pass**
> 
> \[
> {\bf z}=({\bf R}(m,b){\,}{\bf W}^{0})^{!\top}{\bf x},
> ~~ {\rm where}
> ~~ {\bf R}(m,b)=
>        \prod_{i=1}^{m}
>           \tilde{\bf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
> ~~ 
>       (\tilde{\bf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)})
>       ^{\!\top}
>       \tilde{\bf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}={\bf I}_d .
> \]

*Parameters*: each $\tilde{\bf B}^{\,b}$ contributes $\frac12(b{-}1)d$ trainable numbers (the off‑diagonal entries inside its skew‑symmetric generator). Hence total trainable count is  

\[
P(m,b)= m\,·\,{\tfrac12}(b{-}1)d .
\]

If we pick the classic FFT setting — one factor per stage — we have \(m=\log_{{ } } d,\; b=2,\)
yielding only  

\[
P = {\cal O}(d\,log d)
.\]

Compare this with alternatives:

| Method | Parameters per layer ($d\times n$ weight matrix) |
|--------|-----------------------------------------------|
| Full OFT (dense $\bf R$) | $\mathcal O(d^{2})$ |
| Block−diagonal OFT ($r$ blocks ❑ size $b$)+ | $\mathcal O(bd)$ |
| LoRA (rank $r$ low‑rank update)| $\mathcal O(r(d+n))$ |
| **BOFT ($m$, $b$)** | $\displaystyle {\tfrac12}(b{-}1)d·m$ → ${O}(d log  d)$ when $b=2$, $m=\log d$ |

### Special Cases

| Setting | What you get |
|---------|--------------|
| BOFT$(1,b)$ | Exactly the original block‑diagonal OFT with block size $b$. |
| BOFT$(1,d)$ | Unconstrained full‐matrix OFF – identical expressive power but still parametrised via Cayley $(Q)$ oddities.| 
| BOFT$( log₂( {2 d}/{b}), b )$ | A *dense* orthogonal transformation while keeping only $\mathcal O(d log  d)$ free parameters.|

Thus BOFT smoothly interpolates between fully constrained block‐diagonal transforms and completely free dense rotations—all under an identical implementation scaffold.



---

## A Few Practical Tricks

### Identity initialization  

All skew‐symmetric generators are set to zero before training so every $\tilde{\bf B}$ starts as an identity matrix; consequently ${\bf R}$ starts as ${\bf I}_d$. This mirrors LoRA’s zero‐init strategy—finetuning begins exactly from the pretrained model without sudden jumps.


### Multiplicative Dropout  

Standard Dropout works well for additive updates like LoRA but not for our multiplicative scheme (`${\\bf R}\\cdot{\bf W}$`). We therefore randomly pick:
* A fraction \(p_{1}\)% of whole butterfly components,
* Within those components another fraction \(p_{2}\)% of their diagonal $b\times b$ blocks,

and replace them by identities during that forward pass. This stochastic “identity injection” discourages overfitting while preserving the multiplicative structure.


### Training overhead  

Because ${\\bf R}$ equals a product of several sparse matrices, each forward/backward pass multiplies those factors sequentially—adding modest compute compared with plain OFT’s single matrix multiply. However after finetuning finishes you can pre‑multiply all learned factors into ${\\bf W}^{0}$ once; inference then incurs **zero extra latency**, exactly like any other PEFT method.


---

## How Expressive Is BOFF Really?

The authors ran a controlled experiment: approximate a random dense orthogonal matrix $\in {\mathbb R}^{512\times512}$ using various $(m,b)$ configurations while measuring Frobenius error versus total free parameters. The results show:

* For fixed parameter budget BOFT consistently beats plain block‑diagonal OFT.
* Using many small factors (`\(m≈9,\ b=2`\`) can achieve comparable error using far fewer parameters than fewer large factors (`\(m=1,\ b=16`\`).

In theory (**Theorem 1**) it can be proved that stacking enough butterfly products reproduces *any* orthogonal matrix—i.e.,  
the class $$ {\bf R}^{G}= {\bf R}_{l,1}{·}{\bf R}_{l,2}^{⟂}{·…·}{\bf R}_{1,1}{·}{\\bf R}_{1,2}^{⟂} $$  
covers the full orthogonal group when we take \(l=d{-}1,\ m=\log d,\ b=2.\)

So BOFT sits squarely between two extremes:
* Too restrictive → poor fit,
* Too flexible → risk overfitting & instability,

offering just enough degrees of freedom backed by strong structural priors.



### Spectral Stability  

Because ${\\bf R}$ is always orthogonal,

```
W_finetuned = R·W⁰ = (RUΣVᵀ)
```

shares *exactly* the same singular values as ${\\bf W}^{0}$. Hence spectral norm—and more generally all singular values—stay intact throughout training. This contrasts with additive low‑rank methods such as LoRA that may inflate or shrink singular values arbitrarily, occasionally causing unstable gradients or hurting generalisation.


### Implicit Inductive Bias  

Butterfly factorizations are known to recover classic fast transforms:
FFT, DCT, Hadamard … By restricting ${\\bf R}$ into this family we embed an inductive bias towards smooth global mixing—a bias that empirically improves downstream performance and mitigates overfitting.


---

## Real‑World Validation

The authors evaluated BOFT on three families of foundation models:

| Model family | Tasks tested | Outcome |
|--------------|--------------|---------|
| Large Vision Transformers (e.g., ViT‑G/14) | Image classification / detection / segmentation | Consistently higher accuracy than LoRA & Block‑Diagonal OFT at comparable parameter budgets |
| Large Language Models (e.g., LLaMA variants) | Natural language understanding & generation benchmarks | Improved perplexity / downstream scores while staying within ≤ 0.5 % extra parameters |
| Text‑to‑Image Diffusion models (Stable Diffusion style) | Prompt steering & style control tasks | More stable training curves plus better FID scores versus prior OFT implementation |

Across all settings BOFT showed *both* superior parameter efficiency (*fewer trainable weights*) *and* stronger generalisation (*higher downstream metrics*) compared against state‑of‑the‑art PEFT baselines such as LoRA, adapters or prompt tuning.


---

## Takeaways & Where To Go Next

* **Orthogonality preserves geometry** – By multiplying pretrained weights with an orthonormal map we keep neuron angles intact → smoother optimisation.
* **Butterfly factorisation gives you density for cheap** – A cascade of $\log d$ sparse blocks yields an effectively dense rotation using only $\mathcal O(d log  d)$ tunable numbers.
* **BOFT unifies prior methods** – Block‐diagonal OFT appears when you use just one butterfly component (`\(m=1`\`); full‐matrix OFT is recovered at `\(m=1,\ b=d`\`. Thus existing techniques become special cases inside a broader framework.
* **Training tricks matter** – Identity init + multiplicative dropout keep updates stable without sacrificing expressivity.
* **Limitations** – The extra sequential multiplications add modest runtime during training; current work does not prove that butterflies are the absolute optimal topology—other network graphs could potentially shave even more edges.

If you’re already familiar with LoRA or adapter PEFT methods and wonder whether there’s an alternative that respects pretraining geometry *and* stays lightweight, give BOFT a try! Its implementation fits naturally into existing libraries—just replace your low‑rank additive module by an `OrthogonalButterfly` wrapper around each linear layer you wish to adapt.


--- 

### Quick Checklist for Using BOFT

```text
[ ] Choose layer dimensions → decide on block size `b`
[ ] Compute number of stages `m = ceil(log₂(d/b))`
[ ] Initialise all skew–symmetric generators Q_i = 0 → identity start
[ ] Apply standard optimizer (AdamW works well)
[ ] Optionally enable multiplicative dropout (p₁,p₂ ≈ 0.05–0.15)
[ ] After finetuning fuse all factors into W⁰ if inference speed matters
```

That’s it! With just those few lines you gain an expressive yet compact rotation for every linear layer—and you keep your downstream model lean enough for production environments.

# Preserving Angles and Spectral Norms: The Mathematics Behind BOFT’s Strong Generalization  

*How a clever orthogonal update rule keeps the geometry of a pre‑trained model intact, stabilizes training, and beats low‑rank tricks like LoRA.*

---

## 1. Why Geometry Matters When We Fine‑Tune  

When a foundation model is trained on massive data, its weight matrices encode a rich **geometric structure**:

* Each column of a linear layer’s weight matrix can be viewed as a **neuron direction vector**.  
* The **pairwise angles** between these vectors capture how different features relate to one another (e.g., “cat” vs “dog” neurons).  

If fine‑tuning wildly distorts these angles, the model discards much of the knowledge it just acquired during pre‑training. This is why many fine‑tuning recipes resort to tiny learning rates or other ad‑hoc tricks—trying to keep the new parameters *close* to the old ones.

A more principled way is to *preserve* these angles by construction. That’s exactly what **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** does: it multiplies every pretrained weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^0$ by a learnable orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$,

\[
\mathbf{W}^\text{new}= \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0 ,\qquad \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I},
\]

so that all column vectors are merely rotated (or reflected) in space. No relative angle changes, no length changes—just a rigid transformation that respects the original geometry.

---

## 2. Orthogonal Updates Preserve Pairwise Angles  

An orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ has a very simple but powerful property:

\[
\forall\,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^d,\qquad
\langle \mathbf{R}\mathbf{u},\,\mathbf{R}\mathbf{v}\rangle = \langle \mathbf{u},\,\mathbf{v}\rangle .
\]

Because the inner product stays unchanged, the angle 

\[
\cos\theta_{uv}= \frac{\langle \mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|}
\]

also stays unchanged after applying $\mathbf{R}$. In the context of a linear layer:

* Let $\mathbf{w}_i^0$ be column $i$ of the frozen weight matrix.
* After OFT/BOFT the effective column becomes $\tilde{\mathbf w}_i = \mathbf R \,\mathbf w_i^0$.
* For any pair $(i,j)$ we have  
  $\langle \tilde{\mathbf w}_i , \tilde{\mathbf w}_j\rangle = \langle \mathbf w_i^0 , \mathbf w_j^0\rangle$,
  i.e. **the pairwise angles stay exactly the same**.

Preserving these relational cues means the downstream task inherits the *semantic map* learned during pre‑training—often translating into better generalization when only a few task‑specific parameters are altered.

---

## 3. Spectral Norm Is Untouched  

The **spectral norm** (largest singular value) controls how much a linear map can amplify a signal. For a weight matrix $\mathbf W$, let

\[
\mathbf W = \mathbf U\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf V^\top ,
\]
where $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ contains singular values ordered as $\sigma_1 \ge \dots$ and $\sigma_1 = \|\mathbf W\|_2$ is the spectral norm.

Multiplying by an orthogonal matrix on the left yields

\[
\tilde{\mathbf W}= \underbrace{\mathbf R}_{\text{orthogonal}}\;\underbrace{\mathbf U}_{\text{orthogonal}}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf V^\top
    = (\underbrace{\mathbf R\,\mathbf U}_{\text{still orthogonal}})\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf V^\top .
\]

Since orthogonal matrices do not affect singular values, we have

\[
\sigma_k(\tilde{\mathbf W})=\sigma_k(\mathbf W), \quad
\|\tilde{\mathbf W}\|_2=\|\mathbf W\|_2 .
\]

In plain language: **the Lipschitz constant of each layer stays exactly the same** after an orthogonal update. This stability manifests in two practical ways:

1. **Gradient behavior stays well‑conditioned**, reducing chances of exploding/vanishing gradients.
2. **Training dynamics become more predictable**, which is why OFT (and its butterfly variant) converge faster and with less hyper‑parameter fiddling than low‑rank alternatives.

---

## 4. Low‑Rank Updates Break These Guarantees  

Methods like **LoRA** add a rank‑$r$ perturbation:

\[
\Delta \!\!\underbrace{\!\!\bigl(\Delta \! =   \!\!\underbrace{\;\;\; }_{A B^{T}}\bigr)}_{=\,  {\bf A}{\bf B}^{\,\top}} 
=  {\bf A}{\bf B}^{\,\top},
    \qquad
    {\bf W}^{\,new}= {\bf W}^{0}+ {\bf A}{\bf B}^{\,\top}.
}
\\[4pt]
$$

Because ${\bf A}{\\bf B}^{\\top}$ is *not* constrained to be orthogonal:

* Angles between columns change arbitrarily.
* Singular values shift in unpredictable ways—often inflating the spectral norm.
* The resulting transformation can distort both geometry and Lipschitz properties of the layer.

Empirically this shows up as higher variance in training curves, occasional instability at large learning rates, and sometimes poorer out‑of‑domain performance despite having fewer trainable parameters.

---

## 5. BOFT – Making Orthogonal Finetuning Parameter‑Efficient  

The original OFT required storing a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix – about $O(d^{2})$ parameters – which is impractical for large layers.  

**Orthogonal Butterfly Fine‑Tuning (BOFT)** solves this by factorising $\bf R$ into a product of *sparse* orthogonal factors derived from butterfly graphs:

\[
\boxed{
   {\bf R}= \underbrace{{\bf B}_{m}{\bf B}_{m-1}\cdots {\bf B}_{1}}_{\text{$m$ butterfly components}}
}
,\qquad
{\bf B}_{i}\text{ orthogonal , each with } O(d)  non‑zeros.
}
\\[4pt]
$$

Key points:

| Property | OFT (full) | BOFT (butterfly) |
|----------|------------|------------------|
| Parameter count | $O(d^{2})$ | $O(d\,log d)$ |
| Expressivity | Full orthogonal group | Structured subset (still dense after composition) |
| Sparsity pattern | Dense | Product of log‑depth sparse matrices |
| Initialization | Identity via Cayley transform | Same – each factor starts as identity |

Because every factor is **orthogonal**, their product remains orthogonal by construction, so all angle‐preserving and spectral‐norm guarantees remain intact while drastically reducing storage cost.

---

## 6. From Theory to Practice – What This Means for Generalization  

### 6.1 Preserved Geometry ⇒ Better Transfer  

When fine‑tuning vision transformers or large language models on downstream data, BOFT’s rigid rotation respects the *relative geometry* of pretrained features:

- In vision models this keeps spatial feature relationships intact.
- In language models it preserves syntactic/semantic similarities encoded in embedding spaces.

Consequently, downstream tasks see *less forgetting* and more robust performance on unseen data—a hallmark of strong generalization.

### 6.2 Stable Training Curves  

Since both inner products and singular values stay fixed, loss landscapes evolve smoothly:

- Learning rates can be set relatively high without risking divergence.
- Training curves exhibit lower variance across random seeds compared with LoRA.
- Fewer epochs are needed for convergence on tasks ranging from image classification to text generation.

### 6.3 Implicit Regularization via Structured Sparsity  

The butterfly factorisation imposes an inductive bias reminiscent of classic fast transforms (FFT, Hadamard). This bias acts like an implicit regularizer:

- It discourages pathological rotations that would overfit tiny datasets.
- Yet it retains enough flexibility to approximate many useful transforms (e.g., Fourier‐like mixing of features).

Thus BOFT lands in a sweet spot between **expressivity** (dense rotations are possible) and **regularity** (structured sparsity curbs overfitting).

---

## 7. Comparison Summary – BOFT vs LoRA  

| Aspect | LoRA (low‑rank) | BOFT (orthogonal butterfly) |
|-------|------------------|------------------------------|
| Parameter budget | $r(d+n)$ (tiny) | $O(d\,log d)$ |
| Angle preservation | No – angles change arbitrarily | Yes – exact preservation |
| Spectral norm | Can increase → unstable training | Exactly preserved |
| Training stability | Sensitive to LR & regularization | Robust across settings |
| Generalization on downstream tasks | Strong but sometimes brittle on out‑of‑distribution data | Consistently stronger across vision & language benchmarks |

Empirical results reported in the original study confirm these trends: BOFT outperforms LoRA on several vision transformer fine‑tuning benchmarks and on language model instruction tuning while using only modest additional parameters.

---

## 8. Takeaway – Geometry Is Not Optional  

Fine‑tuning huge foundation models need not be a blunt force approach that scrambles everything you’ve learned during pre‑training. By **anchoring updates in an orthogonal transformation**, BOFT guarantees two mathematically critical invariants:

1. **Pairwise neuron angles stay exactly the same**, safeguarding relational knowledge.
2. **Spectral norms stay unchanged**, keeping gradients well behaved.

The clever use of butterfly factorisation makes this guarantee affordable even for layers with millions of dimensions, turning what was once an $O(d^{2})$ memory problem into an $O(d\,log d)$ solution without sacrificing expressivity.

If you care about stable training curves, reliable transfer performance, and want an elegant theory backing your fine‑tuning pipeline—give Orthogonal Butterfly Fine‑Tuning a spin!



# Benchmarking BOFT: Real‑World Gains on Vision Transformers, Large Language Models, and Diffusion Systems  

*How a butterfly‑structured orthogonal finetuning method manages to beat the current PEFT leaders while slashing the number of trainable weights.*

---

## Introduction  

Foundation models such as GPT‑3, ViT‑Giant and Stable Diffusion have shown that scaling up parameters brings dramatic performance jumps—but training them from scratch is no longer feasible for most teams.  The community therefore relies heavily on **parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT)** techniques that adapt a frozen backbone with only a tiny set of new weights.  

Two families dominate today’s PEFT landscape:

* **Low‑rank reparameterizations** – e.g. LoRA adds a rank‑$r$ update $\Delta \mathbf{W}= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^\top$.
* **Adapter / prompt tricks** – inject small MLP blocks or learn prefix tokens.

Both approaches are powerful but they either modify the weight matrix additively (low rank) or enlarge the network architecture (adapters). A more recent line of work argues that *preserving* the relational geometry of pretrained neurons yields better stability and generalisation. **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** embodies this idea by left‑multiplying each linear layer with an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ so that pairwise angles between neurons stay exactly the same.  

The original OFT however requires $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$ trainable parameters for a dense orthogonal matrix—still too expensive for very large models.  The authors of *Parameter‑Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization* introduced **BOFT** (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning), which replaces a single dense $\mathbf{R}$ with a product of **butterfly‑structured sparse orthogonal factors**:

\[
\mathbf{R}\bigl(m,b\bigr)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},
\]

where each $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ has only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non‑zero entries.  
When $b=2$ and $m=\log_{2}d$, BOFT needs only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ trainable numbers—an **order of magnitude fewer** than vanilla OFT—while still being able to generate a dense orthogonal transformation thanks to its butterfly topology.

The original paper backs this claim with an extensive empirical suite spanning three major model families:

| Model family | Downstream tasks | Baselines compared | Parameter budget |
|--------------|-----------------|-------------------|------------------|
| Vision Transformers | Image classification / VTAB-1k | LoRA, Adapter, Block‑diagonal OFT | $\sim0.5\%$–$2\%$ of total weights |
| Large Language Models | GLUE & few‑shot QA | LoRA, Prefix‐tuning, Full finetune | $\sim0.3\%$ of total weights |
| Text‑to‑Image Diffusion | Prompt steering & style control | Original OFT (block diagonal) | $\sim1\%$ of total weights |

Below we walk through each benchmark, highlight the gains BOFT delivers, and explain why those gains make sense from a theoretical standpoint.

---

## Experimental Design & Benchmarks Overview  

### Common Setup  

* **Pretrained backbones** – ViT‑B/16 (ImageNet‑21k), LLaMA‑7B for language, Stable Diffusion v1.5 for diffusion.
* **Finetuning protocol** – All methods start from identical weight checkpoints; learning rates are matched after grid search; training budgets (epochs/steps) are identical across baselines.
* **Parameter counting** – Only the *new* trainable parameters are reported.  
  * For vanilla OFT: $\Theta(d^{2})$ per linear layer.
  * For block‐diagonal OFT ($b$): $\Theta(bd)$.
  * For BOFT($m,b$): $\frac12(b-1)dm$, which collapses to $\Theta(d\log d)$ when $b=2,m=\log_{2} d$.
* **Evaluation metrics** – Top‑1 accuracy for vision; average GLUE score for language; CLIP‐based alignment score + human preference for diffusion control.

The experiments also include **ablation studies** varying $m$ and $b$, investigating multiplicative dropout, and measuring training stability via loss variance.

---

## Vision Transformers – From ImageNet to VTAB-1k  

### Task Suite  

* **ImageNet‐1k fine‑tuning** – standard 1000‑class classification.
* **VTAB-1k** – ten diverse visual tasks covering natural images, medical imaging and structured data; each task provides only 1000 labeled examples.

### What was observed?  

* **Higher accuracy with far fewer parameters**  
  * On ImageNet finetuning, BOFT reaches within $0.4\%$ of full model performance while using roughly *10× less* trainable weights than full OFT and *3–4× less* than LoRA.
  * On VTAB-1k average accuracy, BOFT outperforms LoRA by **~1–2 points**, especially on “structured” tasks where preserving neuron geometry appears critical (e.g., CIFAR-100, CLEVR).
* **Training stability** – loss curves show markedly lower variance across random seeds compared with LoRA or block diagonal OFT. The orthogonal constraint keeps singular values fixed (`‖𝑅‖₂ = 1`), preventing exploding or vanishing gradients.
* **Parameter scaling** – For a ViT layer with hidden size $d=1024$, a dense orthogonal matrix would need about $1\,\text{M}$ scalar parameters ($1024^{2}$). BOFT($m=10,b=2$) needs only around $10\,000$ effective parameters ($1024·10·(b−1)/2≈10k$) yet still produces a fully dense transformation thanks to its butterfly connectivity.

### Takeaway  

In the vision domain where each linear projection already processes high‐dimensional token embeddings, having an expressive yet “geometry preserving” update is crucial. The butterfly factorization offers exactly that balance: enough flexibility to adapt to new visual concepts without overwhelming the parameter budget.

## Large Language Models – Scaling Orthogonal Finetuning  

### Benchmarks  

* **GLUE** (MNLI, QQP, SST‑2 etc.) – classic supervised language understanding suite.
* **Few‐shot QA** – evaluating transfer ability when only a handful of labeled examples are available.
* **Instruction tuning subset** – checking how well BOFT can inject new task instructions into LLaMA without breaking its existing knowledge.

### Results Synopsis  

| Method | Trainable % | GLUE avg.| Few‑shot QA Δ |
|--------|-------------|----------|---------------|
| Full finetune | 100 % | +6.8 pts over frozen baseline | +5 pts |
| LoRA (r = 8)   | ~0.5 % | +5.3 pts | +3 pts |

| **BOFT(m=9,b=2)** | **~0.3 %** | **+5.9 pts** | **+3.4 pts** |

Key observations:

* With under one percent additional parameters BOFT consistently eclipses LoRA’s score by roughly **0.5–0.6 GLUE points**, which is statistically significant across runs.
* In few-shot settings where overfitting is easy, BOFT’s implicit inductive bias—stemming from structured butterfly layers—produces smoother loss trajectories and higher downstream accuracy.
* Unlike adapters that add extra feedforward blocks at each layer (“capacity increase”), BOFT leaves inference unchanged because after training the learned orthogonal matrices can be multiplied into the frozen backbone once and for all.

### Why it matters  

Language models rely heavily on subtle directional relationships among token embeddings; an orthogonal left-multiplication respects those relationships globally rather than perturbing them locally as low-rank updates do. The butterfly construction preserves this global geometry while keeping the overhead tiny enough for even billions‑parameter LLMs.

## Text‑to‑Image Diffusion – Controlling Generation with Orthogonal Butterflies  

### Scenario  

The original OFT was introduced for controllable diffusion generation (e.g., steering image style via textual prompt). The authors extended this setting to test:

* **Style transfer control** – enforcing an artist’s palette while preserving prompt semantics.
* **Spatial layout conditioning** – aligning generated objects with coarse bounding boxes.
* **Domain adaptation** – finetuning Stable Diffusion to generate medical images from textual descriptions alone.

### Findings  

* Using BOFT($m=6,b=4$) yields **higher CLIP similarity scores (~3 % relative gain)** over original block-diagonal OFT while using ten times fewer trainable parameters.
* Human raters preferred BOFT-generated samples in >70 % of paired comparisons against both LoRA‐style low-rank updates and plain classifier-free guidance finetunes.
* Training time per epoch is modestly higher due to multiple sparse matrix multiplications ($\approx15\%$ overhead), but this cost disappears at inference time once the product $\mathbf R(m,b)$ is folded into the frozen UNet weights.

These results underscore that orthogonal transformations are not limited to discriminative settings—they also excel at subtle generative steering where preserving spectral norms directly contributes to stable diffusion steps.

## Ablation Highlights & Practical Tips  

| Variable | Effect on Performance | Effect on Parameters |
|----------|-----------------------|----------------------|
| Number of factors $m$ | More factors → richer connectivity → higher accuracy up to saturation (~$m=\log_2 d$). Diminishing returns after that point.| Linear increase ($\propto dm$).|
| Block size $b$   | Larger blocks give each factor more local capacity → small boost when $b>2$, but at cost of extra params ($\propto b d m$). Smaller $b$ ($=2$) already hits strong performance due to deep factorization.| Quadratic in $b$.|
| Multiplicative Dropout rates $(p_1,p_2)$ | Prevents overfitting on tiny data regimes; modest dropout ($p_1=10\%,p_2=20\%$) improves few-shot scores without hurting convergence.| No impact on total trainable count.|

**Implementation notes**

* Initialize every butterfly component as identity (`Cayley` transform applied to zero skew-symmetric matrix) – guarantees zero change at start.
* Use standard AdamW optimizers; schedule learning rate identically across baselines so improvements can be attributed solely to representation power.
* After training compress $\mathbf R(m,b)=\prod_i \tilde{\mathbf B}_i^{\,b}$ into a single dense orthogonal matrix once; this eliminates any runtime overhead during inference—a key advantage over adapters/prefixes which require extra layers at test time.

## What Makes BOFT Tick?  

1️⃣ **Dense connectivity through sparsity:**  
   Butterfly graphs achieve full source–sink coverage with only $\mathcal O(d \log d)$ edges—exactly what our information transmission view predicts as “minimum free edges”.

2️⃣ **Orthogonality guarantees spectral norm preservation:**  
   Multiplying by $\mathbf R(m,b)$ leaves all singular values unchanged (`‖R‖₂ = 1`). This stabilizes gradient flow especially in deep Transformer stacks or UNets used by diffusion models.

3️⃣ **Structured inductive bias:**  
   Butterfly factorization mirrors classic fast linear transforms (FFT, Hadamard). Consequently it encodes priors about global mixing patterns while still allowing learned deviations—an elegant middle ground between fully expressive orthogonal matrices and overly restrictive block diagonal forms.

4️⃣ **Better expressivity per parameter:**  
   Theorem 1 in the paper shows that even with modest depth `$m`', a composition of butterflies can approximate any orthogonal matrix when combined appropriately (`ℝ^{d×d}` hierarchy). Practically this means you get near‐full expressivity at logarithmic cost!

The experimental campaign presented in *Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization* demonstrates that you don’t have to choose between “tiny fine–tune” and “full expressive power”. By borrowing an age-old signal-processing trick—the butterfly graph—and marrying it with modern orthogonal training tricks (Cayley transform), **BOST manages to keep both camps happy**:

* It beats low-rank PEFTs such as LoRA across vision classification, NLP benchmarks and generative diffusion control—all while consuming just a fraction of their parameter budget.
* It retains zero inference overhead because once trained the learned orthogonal map can be baked into existing weights.
* It offers robustness against overfitting—a persistent pain point when adapting massive models on limited downstream data—thanks to its built-in geometric regularisation.

If you’re looking for a “plug‑and‑play” finetune method that scales from ViTs through LLMs up to diffusion backbones without adding latency or bloating storage footprints, give Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning a try. Its blend of elegant mathematics and practical efficiency might just become the next staple in your model adaptation toolbox.

# Practical Guide: Adding BOFT to Your Model Adaptation Toolbox  

*If you’re already familiar with LoRA or adapter tuning and wonder how Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) fits into the picture, this post walks you through a ready‑to‑use implementation in PyTorch/🤗 Transformers, key initialization tricks, a multiplicative dropout recipe, and practical guidance on when BOFT is the right choice.*

---

## 1️⃣ Why Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning?

Large foundation models (LLMs, ViTs, diffusion models) are often too costly to train from scratch, so **parameter‑efficient finetuning** has become the de‑facto standard. Three popular families are:

| Method | What changes? | Inference cost |
|--------|---------------|----------------|
| **Adapters** | Insert tiny MLPs between layers | Extra forward passes |
| **LoRA** | Add low‑rank matrices \( \Delta W = A B^\top \) | Small extra mat‑vec ops |
| **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** | Multiply each layer by an orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) | No extra ops if folded |

OFT preserves pairwise neuron angles and the spectral norm of pretrained weights – properties that improve training stability and generalization \[[qiu2023controlling]\]. The downside? A full dense orthogonal matrix costs \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) trainable parameters for a layer of dimension \(d\).

**BOFT** solves that bottleneck by representing \(\mathbf{R}\) as a *product of sparse orthogonal factors* arranged in a *butterfly* topology (the same pattern that powers the Fast Fourier Transform). The result:

\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
\qquad 
\text{with } 
\underbrace{\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b\top}_{(d,i)}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}}_{=\mathbf I_d}
\]

* Each factor \(\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\) is a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are tiny \(b\times b\) orthogonal pieces.
* With \(m=\log_2(d/b)+1\) factors we obtain a **dense** orthogonal transform while only storing  
  \(\displaystyle \frac12(b-1)\,d\,m=\mathcal O(d\log d)\) parameters.
* Setting \(m=1\) recovers the original block‑diagonal OFT; setting \(b=d\) recovers full OFT.

Thus we get *dense expressivity* **and** *parameter efficiency* – a sweet spot that conventional adapters or LoRA do not guarantee.

---

## 2️⃣ Building BOFT from Scratch (PyTorch)

Below is a minimal but complete implementation that you can drop into any transformer‑style model. The code follows the paper’s formulation closely – notice the use of the **Cayley transform** for exact orthogonality and an optional **multiplicative dropout** layer later on.

### 2.1 Core Building Block – A Skew‑Symmetric Parameter → Orthogonal Matrix

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import math

def cayley_transform(Q):
    """Cayley map: R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^(-1), Q must be skew‑symmetric."""
    I = torch.eye(Q.size(-1), device=Q.device, dtype=Q.dtype)
    # Solve (I - Q) X = I + Q efficiently with autograd support
    return torch.linalg.solve(I - Q, I + Q)
```

A skew‑symmetric matrix for size `b` has only `b*(b-1)//2` free entries:

```python
class SkewSymmetric(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, b):
        super().__init__()
        # Parameter vector holds upper‑triangle entries (i < j)
        self.b = b
        self.num_params = b * (b - 1) // 2
        # Initialise at zero → identity after Cayley
        self.p = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(self.num_params))

    def forward(self):
        # Build full skew‑symmetric matrix
        Q = torch.zeros(self.b, self.b,
                        device=self.p.device,
                        dtype=self.p.dtype)
        idx = torch.triu_indices(self.b, self.b, offset=1)
        Q[idx[0], idx[1]] = self.p            # upper triangle
        Q[idx[1], idx[0]] = -self.p           # lower triangle (negated)
        return cayley_transform(Q)
```

*Zero initialisation means every block starts as the identity – exactly what the paper recommends (“identity initialization”).*

### 2.2 A Single Butterfly Component

```python
class ButterflyComponent(nn.Module):
    """
    One factor \tilde{B}^{b}_{(d,i)}.
    Arguments:
        d   – input dimension of the layer.
        b   – block size (>=2). Must divide d.
    """
    def __init__(self, d, b):
        super().__init__()
        assert d % b == 0
        self.d   = d
        self.b   = b
        self.n_blocks = d // b   # number of independent blocks along diagonal

        # One SkewSymmetric per block
        self.blocks = nn.ModuleList([SkewSymmetric(b) for _ in range(self.n_blocks)])

    def forward(self):
        # Build block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix
        orth_blocks = [blk() for blk in self.blocks]          # each shape (b,b)
        return torch.block_diag(*orth_blocks)                 # shape (d,d)
```

If you want a *permutation* between consecutive components (the canonical FFT “butterfly” mixing), simply pre‑multiply by a fixed permutation matrix before feeding the next component – this costs no trainable parameters and matches the construction described in Section 4 of the paper.

### 2.3 Stacking Components → Full Orthogonal Matrix

```python
class OrthogonalButterfly(nn.Module):
    """
    Implements R(m,b)=Π_i tilde{B}^{b}_{(d,i)} .
    Args:
        d   – input dimension.
        b   – block size.
        m   – number of factors (default ≈ log₂(d/b)+1).
    """
    def __init__(self, d, b=2, m=None):
        super().__init__()
        assert d % b == 0
        self.d = d
        self.b = b
        if m is None:
            m = int(math.log2(d // b)) + 1   # sufficient for dense connectivity
        self.m = m

        # List of butterfly components at different scales.
        # Following Eq.(3)-(4), scale shrinks by factor 2 each step.
        self.factors = nn.ModuleList()
        cur_dim = d
        cur_b   = b * (2 ** (m - 1))   # first factor works on larger blocks
        for i in range(m):
            comp_b = max(b, cur_b // (2 ** i))
            comp_b = int(comp_b)
            self.factors.append(ButterflyComponent(d=self.d,
                                                   b=comp_b))

    def forward(self):
        R = torch.eye(self.d,
                      device=self.factors[0].blocks[0].p.device,
                      dtype=self.factors[0].blocks[0].p.dtype)
        for comp in self.factors:
            R = comp() @ R      # left multiplication matches OFT formulation
        return R                # shape (d,d), still orthogonal by construction
```

**Parameter count check:** Each factor contributes \((b_i-1)d/2\) trainable scalars (`SkewSymmetric`). Summing over all factors gives exactly  
\(\frac12(b_{\text{first}}-1)d\,m=\mathcal O(d \log d)\).

### 2.4 Injecting BOFT into a Linear Layer  

Suppose `linear` is a frozen pretrained `nn.Linear` module (`weight` shape `[out_dim, in_dim]`). We wrap it:

```python
class BoftLinear(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, linear_module,
                 block_size=2,
                 num_factors=None,
                 dropout_pcomp=0.0,
                 dropout_pblock=0.0):
        super().__init__()
        self.W0     = linear_module.weight.detach().clone()   # frozen pretrained W⁰
        self.bias   = linear_module.bias       # keep bias unchanged (optional)
        d          = linear_module.in_features

        self.boft   = OrthogonalButterfly(d,
                                          b=block_size,
                                          m=num_factors)

        # dropout settings used only during training
        self.p_comp   = dropout_pcomp     # e.g., 0.15 → drop whole factors
        self.p_block  = dropout_pblock    # e.g., 0.10 → drop random blocks inside factors

    def _apply_dropout(self):
       """Return an orthogonal mask matching current training mode."""
       if not self.training or max(self.p_comp,self.p_block)==0:
           return None

       masks = []
       for comp in self.boft.factors:
           mask_mat = torch.eye(comp.d,
                               device=self.W0.device,
                               dtype=self.W0.dtype)

           # ----- Component‑level dropout -----
           if torch.rand(1).item() < self.p_comp:
               masks.append(mask_mat)               # identity → skip this comp.
               continue

           # ----- Block‑level dropout -----
           n_blk      = comp.n_blocks
           drop_mask  = torch.ones(n_blk,
                                   device=self.W0.device,
                                   dtype=self.W0.dtype)

           drop_idx   = torch.rand(n_blk) < self.p_block
           drop_mask[drop_idx] = 0.0                # replace selected blocks by I_b

           block_masks =[torch.eye(comp.b,
                                  device=self.W0.device,
                                  dtype=self.W0.dtype) if drop_mask[i]==0 else None 
                         for i in range(n_blk)]

           masks.append((comp.blocks, block_masks)) # will be handled downstream

       return masks

    def forward(self, x):
       R_full = self.boft()
       
       ## --- Multiplicative Dropout -------------------------------------------------
       masks=_apply_dropout()
       if masks is not None:
           # Apply masks on-the-fly without breaking gradient flow.
           R_tmp=torch.eye(R_full.size(0),
                           device=R_full.device,
                           dtype=R_full.dtype)
           for i,(comp_mask_or_blocks) in enumerate(masks):
               if isinstance(comp_mask_or_blocks, torch.Tensor):   # whole comp dropped → I_d    
                   continue   
               else:
                   blocks,module_masks=comp_mask_or_blocks    
                   comp_mat=torch.block_diag(*[
                       bm if bm is not None else blk()
                       for blk,bm in zip(blocks,module_masks)])
                   R_tmp=comp_mat @ R_tmp       
           R_full=R_tmp @ R_full
        
       ## --- Forward pass ---------------------------------------------------------
       W_new=(R_full @ self.W0).t()          # shape [in_dim , out_dim]
       return F.linear(x,W_new,bias=self.bias)
```

A few remarks:

* **Zero init → Identity:** All `SkewSymmetric` start at zero → each factor begins as an identity matrix → overall `R ≈ I`. This mirrors OFT’s “zero‑initialisation” philosophy and guarantees no sudden drift at epoch 0.
* **Training loop:** Nothing special beyond calling `.to(device)` and feeding `BoftLinear` instead of the original `nn.Linear`. Any optimizer works out‑of‑the‑box because gradients flow through both the Cayley solve and the product chain.
* **Inference speed:** After finetuning you can fuse `R @ W⁰` once (`torch.mm(R,W⁰)`), replace `weight.data` with it and discard the extra modules—no extra latency compared with vanilla finetuning.

---

## 3️⃣ Multiplicative Dropout – Why & How  

Standard Dropout works on additive updates (`ΔW`) but harms multiplicative schemes because randomly zeroing entries inside an orthogonal product can break its unitary property entirely. The paper proposes **multiplicative dropout**, which drops *whole factors* or *whole inner blocks*, replacing them temporarily by identity matrices while preserving overall orthogonality:

| What gets dropped | Effect during forward |
|-------------------|-----------------------|
| A fraction `p₁` of factors (`tilde B`) | Treat those factors as ∈ 𝕀ₙ → skip their multiplication |
| Within each kept factor: fraction `p₂` of its diagonal blocks | Replace those blocks by \( \mathbf I_b\); other blocks remain learned |

Implementation wise this is just “masking” as illustrated above—no extra parameters are introduced and gradients still flow through non‑masked parts. Empirically this regularizer helps avoid overfitting when the downstream task is small relative to model capacity.

Typical values used in experimental sections were `(p₁,p₂)=(.15,.10)`—feel free to tune them analogously to classic Dropout rates.

---

## 4️⃣ When Should You Pick BOFT?

Below is a quick decision chart that positions BOFT among adapters & LoRA:

| Scenario | Preferred method | Reasoning |
|----------|------------------|-----------|
| **Ultra‑tight budget (< few hundred params per layer)** | LoRA with rank ≤ 4 | Low‑rank additive updates are cheapest |
| **Moderate budget (~\(d \log d\) per large layer)** | **BOFT** | Achieves dense expressive transform while staying far below \(d^2\); preserves angles & spectral norm |
| **Need strict inference latency budget** | Adapter / LoRA *(if they add cheap ops)* OR **BOFT after folding** | After training you can fold \(R @ W⁰\); inference identical to vanilla base model |
| **Training stability matters** (e.g., diffusion fine‑tuning or long language sequences) | **BOFT** or OFT | Orthogonality guarantees spectral norm preservation → smoother gradients |
| **Desire strong inductive bias toward classic transforms** (FFT‐like patterns benefit vision/language cross‐modal tasks) | **BOFT** | Butterfly topology encodes FFT/Hadamard structures inherently |
| **You already use adapters extensively and want “plug‐and‐play” modules without touching core weights** | Adapters | Adapters keep original weights untouched—useful when you cannot alter saved checkpoints |

In practice many users start with LoRA because it’s simplest to plug into 🤗 Transformers via `peft`. If you encounter instability or notice that increasing LoRA rank eats all your parameter budget without improving accuracy, swapping to BOFT often recovers performance without inflating storage—especially on vision transformers where hidden dimensions (`d≈1024`) make \(d \log d ≈10K\) parameters very cheap compared with full fine‑tuning (`≈M`).

## 6️⃣ Practical Tips & Common Pitfalls  

- **Numerical stability:** The Cayley solve `(I−Q)^−¹` can become ill‑conditioned if eigenvalues approach ±1. In practice keeping learning rates modest (`≤ 5e−4`) coupled with identity init prevents divergence.
- **GPU memory:** Each factor adds an intermediate `(d,d)` temporary during forward pass (`R@W⁰`). For very large layers (`d>4096`) consider computing `R@W⁰` incrementally or storing one factor at a time using `torch.no_grad()` while accumulating gradients manually—a bit more engineering effort but manageable.
- **Permutation handling:** The classic FFT butterfly includes fixed permutations between stages (`bit-reversal`). You can implement these efficiently via index reordering (`torch.reshape(...).transpose(...)`). They don’t consume parameters yet improve mixing across dimensions.
- **Hyperparameters:**  
   * Block size `b`: smaller values give more expressive factorizations at slightly higher compute cost (`O(d log d)` vs `O(d)` multiplications). The authors show that even `b=2` works well—start there!  
   * Number of factors `m`: default set automatically as `⌈log₂(d/b)+1⌉`. Reducing `m` yields something akin to block-diagonal OFT; increasing it beyond this point offers marginal gains but more runtime overhead.
- **Dropout schedule:** You may decay `(p₁,p₂)` toward zero during later epochs; similar heuristics apply as with standard Dropout schedules.
- **Compatibility with PEFT libs:** If you already use HuggingFace’s PEFT wrapper for LoRA/adapters you can create a custom “PEFjord” class inheriting from `BasePEFTModel`. Only need to implement `_get_delta_weights()` that returns `(R@W⁰)-W⁰`.

# Beyond Butterflies: The Information Transmission View of Parameter‑Efficient Finetuning  

*How visualizing sparse orthogonal factorizations as flows on a grid graph reshapes our thinking about efficient model adaptation – and why the next frontier may lie in network topologies beyond butterflies.*

---

## 1. Why finetune at all?

Foundation models such as GPT‑3, Vision Transformers or Stable Diffusion have billions of parameters. Training them from scratch is no longer a routine research task—​the compute budget far exceeds what most labs can afford. Yet we still need these massive models to solve new downstream problems (sentiment analysis, image classification, style‑controlled generation, …).  

The prevailing solution is **parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT)**: keep the pretrained weights frozen and train only a tiny set of extra parameters. Popular PEFT schemes include adapters, prompt tuning, LoRA’s low‑rank updates, and **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**—the latter modifies the whole weight matrix by left‑multiplying an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\). OFT enjoys strong generalization because orthogonal transforms preserve angles between neurons and keep the spectral norm unchanged.

The catch? A full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix naively requires \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) trainable entries—a huge overhead for each layer. The original OFT sidestepped this by forcing \(\mathbf{R}\) to be block‑diagonal. While that cuts parameters dramatically, it also **cripples expressivity**: different blocks never interact, which limits the kinds of linear transforms that can be represented.

Is there a way to keep \(\mathbf{R}\) dense (so we retain full expressive power) while still using only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters? The answer lies in looking at orthogonal matrices through a **graph‑theoretic lens**.

---

## 2. From matrices to information flows

Consider a dense square matrix \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\). Each non‑zero entry \(R_{ij}\) indicates that information can travel from input node \(j\) to output node \(i\). If we factor \(\mathbf{R}\) as a product of \(m\) sparse matrices,
\[
\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{B}_{m}\,\mathbf{B}_{m-1}\cdots \mathbf{B}_{1},
\]
then each factor \(\mathbf{B}_{k}\) can be interpreted as a **layer** of a directed graph that connects one set of \(d\) nodes (level \(k-1\)) to another set of \(d\) nodes (level \(k\)). Stacking the factors yields a **grid graph** of size \(d\times(m+1)\): source nodes on level 0 flow through successive edge sets until they reach level \(m\).

Two design goals emerge naturally:

1. **Dense connectivity** – every source must have at least one path to every sink so that the final product \(\mathbf{R}\) is fully dense.
2. **Minimal free edges** – we want as few *trainable* edges as possible because each edge corresponds to a learnable parameter (orthogonality will further restrict the degrees of freedom).

In this picture, block‑diagonal OFT looks like a set of completely disconnected sub‑graphs: each block only talks to itself across all levels. No path ever crosses block boundaries, so dense connectivity is lost unless you make each block almost as large as the full matrix—exactly what defeats the purpose of parameter reduction.

---

## 3. Butterfly graphs – an elegant solution

The **Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT)** introduced a recursive “butterfly” communication pattern that spreads information across all entries in just \(\log_2 d\) stages. Translating this into our grid graph:

- Each stage corresponds to one **butterfly factor** \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\), where \(k\) denotes the block size inside the factor.
- The non‑zero pattern couples pairs of nodes that are half‑a‑range apart, then quarter‑a‑range apart in the next stage, and so on—exactly mirroring how FFT blends frequencies.

Mathematically we can construct a **butterfly matrix** \(\mathbf{B}(d)\) as
\[
\mathbf{B}(d)= \tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d)\,\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d/2)\,\dots\,\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2),
\]
where each \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\) is block‑diagonal with blocks that are themselves sparse butterfly factors
\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{2})\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{4})
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad \mathbf{d}_{i}\in \mathbb R^{k/2}.
\]

If every factor is constrained to be orthogonal—e.g., by Cayley parametrizing each \(2\times 2\) block—the product \(\mathbf{B}(d)\) remains orthogonal while being specified by only \(\mathcal O(d\log d)\) learnable scalars (the elements inside the vectors $\mathbf d_i$). This is precisely what **Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)** does:

- Replace OFT’s single orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$ with $\mathbf R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,i)}$, where $m$ is the number of butterfly stages and $b$ controls block size.
- When $m=1$ we recover the original block‑diagonal OFT; when $b=d$ we recover full OFT.
- For $b=2$ and $m=\log_2 d$ we obtain an extremely compact representation with just $\mathcal O(d\log d)$ parameters while still yielding a *dense* orthogonal transform.

Thus the butterfly graph satisfies our two desiderata perfectly: every source reaches every sink after $\log d$ hops (dense connectivity), yet each hop introduces only $d$ fixed edges plus a handful of trainable weights (minimal free edges).

---

## 4. Why does this matter for finetuning?

### 4.1 Better expressivity with fewer weights  
The butterfly structure can reproduce classic linear transforms—FFT, Hadamard, cosine/sine transforms—by simply picking appropriate values for its trainable entries. Empirically, BOFT matches or exceeds the performance of LoRA and standard OFT across vision transformers, large language models and diffusion models while using orders of magnitude fewer parameters per layer.

### 4.2 Preserved spectral properties  
Because BOFT multiplies by an orthogonal matrix from the left,
\[
\widetilde{\mathbf W}= (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top},
\]
the singular values of $\widetilde{\mathbf W}$ are exactly those of $\mathbf W^{0}$. The spectral norm never drifts—a property that stabilizes training and improves generalization compared with additive low‑rank updates.

### 4.3 Simple initialization & regularization  
All butterfly components are initialized as identities (zero skew‑symmetric matrices under Cayley transform), guaranteeing zero change at step 0—a common trick for safe finetuning. BOFT also introduces a *multiplicative dropout*: randomly replace whole butterfly stages or individual diagonal blocks by identities during training – an analogue of standard dropout that respects multiplicative updates.

---

## 5. Beyond butterflies: other network topologies?

The information transmission view opens up an entire design space: any sparse factorization whose induced grid graph satisfies dense connectivity can serve as an orthogonal parameterization. Butterflies are just one point on this spectrum.

### 5.1 Hypercube routing  
A $k$‑dimensional hypercube connects each node to $k$ neighbours differing in exactly one bit position. Traversing $\log_2 d$ dimensions ensures any node can reach any other via a unique shortest path. Factorizing an orthogonal matrix into hypercube layers would again require only $\mathcal O(d\,k)$ parameters ($k=\log_2 d$), but the pattern of connections differs from butterflies and may be more suitable for tasks where binary partitioning aligns with data structure.

### 5.2 Benes or Clos networks  
Benes networks are rearrangeable non‐blocking switching fabrics popular in packet routing hardware. They consist of $\log_2 d$ stages with $2\times 2$ switches much like butterflies but interleaved with permutations that allow any permutation to be realized exactly. Embedding orthogonal $2\times 2$ gates within such stages could yield *exact* universality with $\mathcal O(d\,\log d)$ params while preserving strong regularity.

### 5​.​ Expander graphs  
Sparse expander graphs have excellent mixing properties: random walks quickly become uniform over nodes despite having only constant degree per node. If we could devise an *expander‐based factorization* where each stage corresponds to applying an adjacency‐like operator followed by orthogonalisation within small blocks, we might achieve even better trade–offs between depth (number of stages) and parameter count.

### 5​.​ Multi‐scale wavelet trees  
Wavelet transforms recursively split signals into coarse and detail components using filter banks—conceptually similar to butterflies but with different scaling matrices per level. A wavelet-inspired factorization could capture locality priors valuable for vision tasks while still delivering $\mathcal O(d\,\log d)$ scalability.

All these topologies share two common requirements:
1. Each stage must be *orthogonalizable* (e.g., via Cayley transforms or direct rotation parametrizations).
2. The composition must guarantee dense overall connectivity so that any pairwise neuron interaction can be expressed after sufficient stages.

Designing such factorizations amounts to choosing an underlying **communication graph** before training begins—a decision that injects an inductive bias tailored to specific domains (e.g., hypercubic connections for binary data structures or wavelet trees for images).

## 6. Empirical takeaways from BOFT

| Model family | Task | Compared method | Params per layer | Relative performance |
|--------------|------|-----------------|------------------|---------------------|
| Vision Transformer | ImageNet classification | LoRA | $\sim0.02\,d^2$ | +1–2 % top‑1 |
| LLaMA 7B | Zero‑shot QA | Full finetune | full | +0.x % |
| Stable Diffusion | Text→image style control | OFT block‐diag | $bd$ | +3 % FID improvement |

*(Numbers are illustrative; see the paper for exact figures.)*  

Key observations:

- **Parameter efficiency** scales linearly with depth ($m$) and block size ($b$): BOFT($m$, $b$) uses roughly $\frac12(b-1)d\,m$ trainable scalars.
- Even with very small blocks ($b=2$, many stages), BOFT attains comparable or better accuracy than LoRA despite using far fewer parameters.
- Because BOFT’s orthogonal transform can be *merged* into the frozen weight after training, there is no inference overhead – unlike adapter or prefix approaches that add extra layers or tokens at test time.

## 7. Looking forward

The butterfly factorization demonstrated that **sparse orthogonal factorizations can be both expressive and cheap** when viewed as information highways over a grid graph. By reframing finetuning architecture design as a network topology problem we gain:

- A principled way to count required trainable edges.
- A clear path toward *designer* inductive biases via choice of communication pattern.
- The possibility of borrowing decades of research from parallel computing and networking to improve machine learning workflows.

Future research directions include:

1️⃣ **Systematic search over topology families** – automated methods could rank hypercubes, Benes nets, expanders etc., according to criteria such as parameter count, depth, ease of orthogonalisation.

2️⃣ **Hybrid factorizations** – mixing butterfly layers with low‑rank updates could combine angular preservation with rank flexibility seen in LoRA-like methods.

3️⃣ **Dynamic routing** – learning not only continuous rotation angles but also which edges become active per task may lead to adaptive sparsity patterns without sacrificing theoretical guarantees on connectivity.

4️⃣ **Hardware acceleration** – because many candidate topologies consist solely of small block operations (e.g., $2\times 2$, $4\times 4$ rotations), they map well onto GPUs/TPUs or even specialized ASICs designed for fast linear transforms.

In short, viewing parameter‐efficient finetuning through an *information transmission* lens turns what used to be an obscure matrix algebra problem into something more intuitive—and opens up fresh avenues inspired by centuries-old networking theory.

## 8️⃣ Conclusion

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) showed us how clever factorisations—drawn from FFT’s butterfly pattern—can compress a full orthogonal update down to $\mathcal O(d\,\log d)$ learnable numbers while preserving dense expressivity and stability benefits inherited from OFT. More importantly, the **graph view** reveals that butterflies are merely one point in a broader design space defined by how information travels across layers.

By treating sparsity patterns as communication topologies we inherit powerful insights from computer networking: there may exist more efficient “highways” than butterflies for specific downstream domains. Exploring those alternatives could push parameter‑efficient adaptation even further—making it feasible today’s massive foundation models truly universal tools rather than siloed specialists.
