\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why does left‑multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserve the spectral norm of $
 W^{0}$?
Question:
The authors claim that both OFT and BOFT keep $\,$	extstyle ||
 W^{0}||_{2}$=||
 R
 W^{0}||_{2}$ because $
 R$ is orthogonal. Could someone provide a short proof using singular value decomposition? Also, during training $
 R$ may deviate slightly from perfect orthogonality – does this affect spectral norm preservation?
Answer:
Both Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its butterfly variant (BOFT) keep the spectral norm of a pretrained weight matrix $W^{0}$ unchanged after the update  

$$
W_{\text{new}} = R\,W^{0},
$$  

because the learned matrix $R$ is orthogonal, i.e.\ $R^{\!\top}R=I$.  
The reason can be seen most cleanly through the singular‑value decomposition (SVD).

Let the SVD of the frozen weight be  

$$
W^{0}=U\,\Sigma\,V^{\!\top},
$$  

where $U\in\bigr(\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\bigr)$ and $V\nobreak=\bigr(\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\bigr)$ are orthogonal and  
$\displaystyle 
   \Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{\min(d,n)})
$
contains the singular values $\sigma_{i}\ge 0$.  
Multiplying on the left by $R$ gives  

$$
RW^{0}=R\,U\,\Sigma\,V^{\!\top}= (RU)\,\Sigma\,V^{\!\top}.
$$  

Since both $R$ and $U$ are orthogonal, their product $RU$ is also an orthogonal matrix. Therefore the SVD of $RW^{0}$ is simply  

$$
RW^{0}
   = \underbrace{(RU)}_{\text{orthogonal}}\;\Sigma\,V^{\!\top},
$$  

so **the singular values remain exactly the diagonal entries of $\Sigma$.**  
The spectral norm—by definition the largest singular value—is therefore  

$$
\bigl\lVert RW^{0}\bigr\rVert_{2}
   = \max_{i}\sigma_{i}
   = \bigl\lVert W^{0}\bigr\rVert_{2},
$$  

which proves that left‑multiplication by an orthogonal matrix preserves the spectral norm.

An equivalent, perhaps more intuitive argument uses the definition of the operator norm: for any unit vector $x\nobreakin\bigr(\mathbb{R}^{n}\bigr)$,

$$
\bigr\lVert RW^{0}x\bigr\rVert_{2}
   = \bigr\lVert R(W^{0}x)\bigr\rVert_{2}
   = \bigr\lVert W^{0}x\bigr\rVert_{2},
$$

because an orthogonal transformation does not change Euclidean length. Taking a supremum over all unit‑norm inputs yields exactly the same conclusion as above.

### What if $R$ drifts away from perfect orthogonality?

During training we parametrize $R$ with a Cayley transform (or with a product of butterfly components that are each enforced to be orthogonal). In exact arithmetic this guarantees $R^{-\!\!,T}R=I$. In floating‑point arithmetic however a small residual may appear, so we can write  

$$
R^{-\!\!,T} R = I + \Delta,
\qquad
\sideset{}{_{} }\norm{\Delta}_{2}=:\varepsilon,
$$  

with a tiny $\varepsilon>0$. For any unit vector $x$,  

$$
(1-\varepsilon)\,\bigl(x^\top(W^{0})^\top(I+\Delta)\,W^{0}x\bigr)
   \;\le\
   x^\top(R(W^{0}))^\top RW{x}
   =
   x^\top(W^{۰})^\TOP(I+\Delta)\,W¹{x}
   \;\le\
(1+\varepsilon)\,\bigl(x^\T(W¹)^⊤ W¹ x\bigr).
$$  

Taking square roots and then a supremum gives  

$$
(1-\varepsilon ) ^{\frac12}\,\bigl\Appellate ‖ W⁰ ‖ _₂ 
    ≤ ‖  R  W⁰ ‖ _₂ 
    ≤ (1+ε ) ^{\frac12 }\;\bigl‖  W⁰ ‖ _₂ .
$$  

Hence a deviation from exact orthogonality perturbs the spectral norm only by a factor roughly equal to $(1±½ε )$. In practice, with well‑conditioned parametrisations such as Cayley or butterfly components one typically has $\varepsilon<10^{-6}$, so **the change in $\|\cdot\rVert_{2}$ is negligible** and does not harm training stability.


If one wishes to eliminate even this tiny drift, a post‑training projection onto the Stiefel manifold—e.g.\ via QR or polar decomposition—restores exact orthogonality without affecting downstream performance, because it leaves all singular values unchanged.


---

In summary, an orthogonal left‑multiplication leaves every singular value untouched, which directly explains why OFT and BOFT preserve the spectral norm of $W⁰$. Small numerical violations of orthogonality only cause minute bounded changes in that norm; they can be safely ignored or corrected with standard re‑orthogonalisation if desired. This property underlies the improved training stability and generalisation reported for both OFT and its butterfly‑efficient variant BOFT.
