\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How should one choose $(m,b)$ per layer versus using a global configuration across layers?
Question:
Different layers have different hidden dimensions ($d$). The paper presents BOFT$(m,b)$ where $m$ controls depth of factorisation and $b$ controls block size. In practice should we keep $(m,b)$ identical for all layers, or allocate larger budgets (larger $m$, smaller $b$) to deeper layers? What trade‑offs have been observed?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
In the experiments reported in the BOFT paper the authors kept a **single pair $(m,b)$** for every linear layer and obtained state​of​the​art results with very little tuning. This works because the butterfly factorisation is already highly expressive even when $m$ is modest, and because the number of trainable parameters in a layer grows **linearly** with its hidden dimension $d$

$$
#\,{\rm params}_{ {\rm layer} }=\frac{(b-1)}{2}\,d\, m .
\xrightarrow{\rm (1)}
$$

If you would like to redistribute capacity among layers you can do so by solving (1) under a **per‑layer budget** $C$

$$
{
   \frac{(b_{i}-1)}{2}\,
   d_{i}\,
   m_{i}=C
}\xrightarrow{\rm (2)}
$$

where $d_{i}$ is the hidden size of layer $i$. In practice this usually means  

* keep $m_{i}=m$ constant (so every layer suffers the same extra $O(m)$ multiply‑adds),  
* choose a **smaller block size** $b_{i}$ for very wide layers ($d_{i}$ large) so that their total number of trained parameters does not explode,  
* optionally increase $m_{i}$ for upper‑most transformer blocks when you want them to be slightly more expressive – those blocks tend to be most important for downstream tasks.  

The trade‑offs implied by $(1)$–$(2)$ are exactly what the authors observed in their controlled experiments  

| Change                               | Effect on expressivity                                                                                                                                          | Effect on training cost                                                                                                                                                                   |
|--------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Increase $m$\*** (more butterfly factors)     | Reduces the approximation error of a dense orthogonal matrix dramatically when $b$\ is small – see Figure&nbsp;5 where&nbsp;BOFT$(9,\,2)$ outperforms BOFT$(1,\,16)$ despite using far fewer parameters. | Each extra factor adds one sparse matrix–vector multiplication whose cost is $\mathcal O(d)$; therefore wall–clock time grows roughly linearly with&nbsp;$m$.                                 |
| **Decrease $b$\*** (smaller blocks)            | Gives finer–grained mixing between dimensions inside every factor, which again lowers the error for a fixed total number of parameters (the curve “$(b=4)$’’ lies left of “$(b=16)$’’ in Figure&nbsp;5).               | Parameter count scales with $(b-1)$; halving&nbsp;$b$\ roughly halves the number of learned entries while keeping the same&nbsp;$m$.                                                            |
| **Keep both large** ($\,b\simeq d,\; m=1$\*) | Degenerates into the original OFT block–diagonal scheme; expressive power is limited and many downstream tasks suffer a noticeable drop in accuracy.                                        | Minimal extra compute (only one sparse matrix), but also minimal benefit over standard OFT.                                                                                                  |

From these observations two practical guidelines emerge  

1️⃣ **Pick a modest depth** $m=\log_{2}(d/b)$ so that after multiplying all factors you obtain a *dense* orthogonal matrix. This choice corresponds exactly to using a full butterfly network $(\,\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,2),\,\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,4),\,…,\,\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,d)\,)$. In all experiments this setting gave the best accuracy‑per‑parameter trade‐off while keeping inference latency identical to OFT (the orthogonal product can be folded into the frozen weight after finetuning).  

2️⃣ **Use a small base block size** $b=4$ or even $b=2$\*** whenever you can afford a slightly larger $m$\***; it yields far better approximation quality than enlarging~$b$. If memory is tight for very wide layers you may raise $b$\*** locally just enough to respect your overall parameter budget—this is precisely what equation $(2)$ prescribes.  

### Why most users stick with a *global* $(m,b)$  

* **Implementation simplicity:** All transformer blocks share exactly the same sequence of butterfly components, which lets us pre‐allocate contiguous GPU buffers once and reuse them throughout forward/backward passes.  
* **Stable training:** The multiplicative dropout introduced in §3.3 assumes an identical dropout mask distribution over all components; having heterogeneous numbers of components would require custom masks per layer and was not studied.  
* **Empirical sufficiency:** The ablation tables (Appendix&nbsp;B.4) show that moving from BOFT$(\,\log_{2} d,\!4\,)$ to an “adaptive’’ schedule changes validation accuracy by less than 0.3 % while increasing code complexity considerably.  

### When an *layer‑wise* schedule can be useful  

If your downstream task contains very few labelled examples or your hardware allows noticeable memory headroom, allocating **more factors** ($\,m_{i}\!\uparrow$\*) or **smaller blocks** ($\,b_{i}\!\downarrow$\*) to deeper transformer blocks often yields modest gains—especially on language models where top–most attention heads are known to carry most task–specific information. Conversely, early convolutional stages of vision transformers typically have small hidden sizes ($\,d\simeq256$\*); there it is perfectly reasonable to set~$m_{i}=1$\*** and pick a relatively large~$b_{i}$, thus keeping those stages ultra‐lightweight without harming overall performance.  

### Summary checklist  

* Compute per‐layer target $\#\,{\rm params}=C_{\rm total}/L_{\rm layers}$.  
* Solve $(\,b_{i}-1\,)\, d_{i}\, m_{i}/2=C_{\rm target}$.  
* Keep $m_{i}= m_{\rm global}$ unless you deliberately want extra capacity at specific depths—then raise only those $m_{i}$.  
* Choose ≤ smallest feasible $b_{i}$ ; typical values are $b=4$ (good balance) or $b=2$\*** when you have generous compute budget.)  
* Verify that after plugging $(\,m_{i}, b_{i}\,)​into   
  $\displaystyle   
     {\bf R}(m,b)=
     \prod_{j=1}^{\, m}
         \tilde{\bf B}^{\, b}_{(\,d,j)}   
   $
  you still obtain an orthogonal matrix (the Cayley transform guarantees this automatically).
