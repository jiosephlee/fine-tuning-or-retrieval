\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### What is required for identity initialization of all skew‑symmetric matrices in multiple butterfly components?
Question:
Each component uses Cayley transform with its own skew‑symmetric generator $
 Q_{j}$. To start from an overall identity transformation we need all products equal identity initially. Is it sufficient simply to set every $
 Q_{j}=0$, or do we also need special handling of permutations within each butterfly component?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes – initializing every skew‑symmetric generator $Q_{j}=0$ makes every Cayley‑parameterised butterfly component equal to the identity matrix, and therefore their product is also the identity. No extra manipulation of the fixed permutations that appear in the butterfly sparsity pattern is required, provided you use the construction described in the paper.

---

### Why $Q_{j}=0$ gives an identity component  

Each butterfly component $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ is parametrised by a Cayley transform

$$
\mathbf R_{i}= (\mathbf I + \mathbf Q_{i})(\mathbf I - \mathbf Q_{i})^{-1},
        \qquad
        \mathbf Q_{i}^\top = -\mathbf Q_{i}.
$$

If we set $\mathbf Q_{i}= \mathbf 0$,

$$
\mathbf R_{i}= (\mathbf I + \mathbf 0)(\mathbf I - \mathbf 0)^{-1}
           = \mathbf I\,,
$$

so every learnable block inside this component collapses to a $2b\times 2b$ (or $2\times 2$) rotation with angle $0^{\circ}$. Because a zero matrix is both symmetric and skew‑symmetric, it satisfies the Cayley requirement automatically. Consequently

$$
\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}=\mathbf I_{d}
        \quad\text{for all } i .
$$

The full orthogonal transformation used by BOFT,

$$
\mathbf R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
$$

is therefore an explicit product of identities and consequently equals $\mathbf I_{d}$.

---

### What about the “permutations’’ inside a butterfly factor?  

A butterfly factor $\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)$ is defined as a **block‑diagonal** matrix whose blocks have a fixed sparsity pattern (the “butterfly” connection pattern). Those patterns are *static* – they determine **which** entries may become non‑zero, but they do not contribute any numerical values by themselves. When we initialise all trainable parameters inside each block so that their rotation matrices are identities, every block becomes

```
[ 1   0 ;
  0   1 ]
```

(and similarly for larger $b\times b$ blocks). Placing such blocks on the prescribed sparse locations yields an overall matrix that has ones exactly on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere, i.e. precisely $\displaystyle \!\!\,\!\!\,\!\!\;\!\;\!\;\,\!_{{}}_{{}}\;\;\;\;\,\!_{\!}{ }\!\displaystyle {\!\mathbbsubscript D}{ }$, precisely $\mathsf I_d$.  

Thus there is no hidden “permutation’’ that survives after setting $Q_j=0$. The only case where an additional handling would be needed is if one inserted an *explicit* permutation matrix $P$ outside the parametrised blocks (e.g. writing  
$R=\!P\,(\text{Cayley}(Q))\,P^{!\top}$). In the construction used in BOFT no such external $P$ appears; any re‑ordering required by the Cooley–Tukey structure is already baked into the sparsity layout and does not alter numerical values when all blocks are identities.

---

### Intuition  

Think of each component as a collection of tiny two‑dimensional rotors placed on a wiring diagram shaped like a butterfly. The diagram tells us *where* rotors sit but not *how* they turn. Setting all rotation angles to zero means every rotor lets its two wires pass straight through without turning them—exactly what an identity map does. Because every rotor starts idle, even after wiring several stages together nothing has been changed; information flows unchanged from input to output.

---

### Practical take‑away  

When implementing BOFT:

1. Initialise every learnable vector that defines a $2\times 2$ (or $2b\times 2b$) block’s Cayley transform to **zero**.
2. Do **not** modify any masks or hard‑coded connections – they remain as defined by the butterfly factorisation.
3. Verify that after initialisation ``torch.allclose(R(m,b), torch.eye(d))`` holds true; this sanity check confirms that your code respects the theory.

With these steps you obtain exactly the same pretrained model before finetuning begins, satisfying the desired “zero‑update’’ requirement without any extra tricks concerning permutations.
