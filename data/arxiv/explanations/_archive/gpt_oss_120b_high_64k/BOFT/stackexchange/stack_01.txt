**Practical consequences for training stability**

1. **Spectral norm invariance** – Let  

   $$
      {{W}}^ ⁰={U }\Sigma V ^ ⊤ .
   $$   

   Then   

   $$
      {{R}}{{W}}^⁰=(RU ) ΣV ⊤,
   $$   

   because $R$ is orthogonal, $RU$ remains orthogonal,
   so all singular values (and therefore the spectral norm) stay
   exactly as in the pretrained model.
   
2. **Gradient geometry** – The Jacobian of the forward map under OFT contains an extra factor~$\boldsymbol R$
   on each side but its operator norm equals one,
   therefore gradient magnitudes are essentially unchanged from pretraining.
   
3️⃣ **Zero‑initialisation consistency** – Both methods initialise their trainable part
as identity/zero so that at step \( 0 )\the network reproduces pretrained behaviour.
Because OFT’s update stays on an orthogonal manifold,
the geometry cannot drift far away unless many degrees–of–freedom are exercised,
acting as an implicit regulariser.



### Generalisation implications

Preserving pairwise angles means relational information encoded during pretraining,
often interpreted as semantic similarity,
remains intact.
When downstream tasks merely reshape these relationships,
an orthogonal transformation suffices without destroying latent structure that would otherwise aid generalisation.



### Trade‑offs and parameter efficiency

* **Parameter count** – A dense orthogonal matrix needs $\mathcal O(d^2)$ parameters,
  larger than a rank‑$r$ LoRA update ($ℴ(r(d+n))$).
  Block‑diagonal or butterfly factorizations reduce this cost;
  BOFT achieves $\mathcal O(d log d)$ whilst retaining dense connectivity.
* **Expressivity** – OFT rotates all outputs jointly;
  per‑neuron alterations beyond this type demand larger blocks or depth.
  Low‑rank LoRA can modify individual directions independently,
  yielding higher expressivity at expense of geometric distortion.
* **Inductive bias** – Rotational bias imposes an implicit metric constraint
  ($ĸᵢ⋅𝐱↦ℎ(Rᵀĸᵢ)$), beneficial when retention of relational structure matters.



### TL;DR

* **OFT** multiplies a pretrained weight matrix on the left by an orthogonal matrix $\boldsymbol R$, i.e.\ it *rotates* every output neuron identically.
  Rotations preserve inner products and norms,
  so pairwise neuron angles stay unchanged.
* **LoRA** adds a low‑rank product $\Delta\bW=AB^\top$, i.e.\ it *translates*
  each neuron independently;
  this translation generally alters lengths and angles unless $AB^\top$
  happens to be negligible.
* Because OFT leaves singular values untouched and keeps gradient magnitudes identical
  to pretraining, training tends to be numerically stable
  and less sensitive to learning‑rate choices.
* Preserving geometric relations leads to better generalisation—
  downstream tasks benefit from retained semantic structure—
  whereas LoRA’s flexibility can sometimes degrade stability
  or cause over‑fitting if not carefully regularised.
* The main drawback of raw OFT is parameter cost;
  BOFT mitigates this via butterfly factorisation,
  achieving dense rotations with only $\mathcal O(d log d)$ trainable parameters—a compromise between LoRA’s efficiency
  and OFT’s geometric fidelity.
