# Why Finetuning Massive Foundation Models is So Tough – And How Orthogonal Finetuning Offers a Solution

Foundation models such as GPT‑3, ChatGPT or Stable Diffusion have shown that scaling up model size brings dramatic improvements in language understanding, image generation, and many other tasks.  Yet the very fact that these models contain hundreds of billions of parameters makes **training them from scratch** prohibitively expensive for most research labs.  The natural workaround is to **adapt** an already‑trained model to a new downstream task—known as finetuning—while keeping the heavy lifting of pretraining untouched.  In practice this turns out to be far trickier than simply “plugging in” a new dataset.  Below we unpack why naïve finetuning can erase the knowledge a model has already acquired and how **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** addresses this problem by preserving the relational geometry between neurons.

---

## 1. The Scaling Bottleneck

* **Size matters.** Modern language models exceed 100 B parameters; vision transformers can reach tens of billions; diffusion generators like Stable Diffusion have similarly massive backbones.
* **Training cost skyrockets.** Pretraining on petaflops of compute for weeks or months is out of reach unless you have massive GPU clusters.
* **Finetuning is the pragmatic route:** Instead of retraining, we want to adjust only a small subset of parameters so that the model behaves well on a new task (e.g., sentiment analysis or image captioning) while retaining its broad pretraining knowledge.

---

## 2. Why Naïve Finetuning Often Breaks What We Want to Keep

Finetuning by gradient descent updates *all* trainable weights simultaneously.  Two common “quick fixes” are:

| Strategy | What it does | Why it can be harmful |
|----------|--------------|-----------------------|
| **Small learning rate** | Takes tiny steps so the weights stay close to their pretrained values | Still changes *relative* relationships between weight vectors; nothing guarantees that structural properties learned during pretraining survive |
| **Partial weight updates** (e.g., only bias terms) | Reduces number of changed parameters | The changed subset can still distort the high‑dimensional geometry that encodes semantic information |

The hidden layers of deep networks are essentially collections of *neurons* (columns of a weight matrix).  During pretraining these neurons settle into a geometry where **pairwise angles** capture relational information—similar neurons tend to align, dissimilar ones stay orthogonal.  If finetuning arbitrarily rotates or stretches these vectors, the angle relationships shift and the model may **forget** useful representations—a phenomenon known as *catastrophic forgetting*.

In mathematical terms, let \(\mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}\) be a frozen pretrained linear layer and \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) an input activation.  The original forward pass computes
\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}.
\]
A naïve update would replace \(\mathbf{W}^0\) with \(\mathbf{W}^0 + \Delta\mathbf{W}\).  Even if \(\|\Delta\mathbf{W}\|\) is small, the *angles* between columns of \(\mathbf{W}^0+\Delta\mathbf{W}\) can change dramatically, erasing the relational structure learned during pretraining.

---

## 3. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Preserving Relational Geometry

### 3.1 The Core Idea

Instead of adding an arbitrary perturbation \(\Delta\mathbf{W}\), OFT **multiplies** the pretrained weight matrix on the left by a learnable orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\):
\[
\boxed{\;\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}\;}
\]
where \(\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}_{d}\).

Because an orthogonal transformation preserves inner products:
\[
\langle \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}_i,\;\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}_j\rangle = \langle \mathbf{w}_i,\;\mathbf{w}_j\rangle,
\]
the **pairwise angles** between any two neuron vectors \(\mathbf{w}_i,\;\mathbf{w}_j\) remain unchanged throughout finetuning.  In other words, OFT keeps the *relational geometry* intact while still allowing each neuron to rotate collectively in a shared subspace that can adapt to the downstream task.

### 3.2 Maintaining Orthogonality During Training

Directly optimizing a matrix under an orthogonality constraint is difficult. OFT adopts the classic **Cayley transform**:
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})\,(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\]
where \(\mathbf{Q}\) is skew‑symmetric (\(\mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^\top\)).  
By learning entries of \(\mathbf{Q}\), orthogonality is automatically enforced at every gradient step—no projection or penalty term is needed.

### 3.3 Zero‑Initialization for Seamless Transfer

Finetuning typically starts from the pretrained model itself so that early training steps do not drift far away from known good parameters.  In OFT this is achieved by initializing \(\mathbf{Q}=0\), which yields \(\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}_{d}\).  Consequently,
\[
\bigl(\underbrace{\mathbf{I}}_{\text{OFT init}}\;\mathbf{W}^0\bigr)^\top = (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top,
\]
so the network behaves exactly like the original model at iteration zero.

---

## 4. Parameter Efficiency – Block‑Diagonal OFT and Its Limits

A full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix contains \(d^{2}\) entries—far too many to store or update for large models (\(d\) often runs into thousands).  The original OFT paper therefore introduced a **block‑diagonal structure**:
\[
\mathbf{R}= \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\,\underbrace{\mathbf{R}_{1}}_{b\times b},\,\underbrace{\mathbf{R}_{2}}_{b\times b},\,\dots ,\,\underbrace{\mathbf{R}_{r}}_{b\times b}\bigr), 
\quad br = d,
\]
where each small block \(\mathbf{R}_{i}\) is itself orthogonal.  

*Pros:*  
- Parameter count drops from \(O(d^{2})\) to \(O(bd)\).  
- Each block can be efficiently parametrized via its own Cayley transform.

*Cons:*  
- The sparsity pattern forces dimensions belonging to different blocks never interact during finetuning; i.e., information cannot flow across blocks.  
- This fixed partitioning introduces an *inductive bias* that may limit expressivity: many classic linear transforms (FFT, Hadamard…) cannot be represented by such a pattern.

Thus while block‑diagonal OFT is more economical than full finetuning, it still trades off **expressivity** for parameter efficiency in a way that may hurt performance on certain tasks.

---

## 5. A Peek at Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT): More Efficient Yet Expressive

Recent work extends OFT by answering: *Can we obtain a dense orthogonal matrix without paying \(O(d^{2})\) parameters?*  

The answer lies in **factorizing** an orthogonal matrix into a product of several *sparse* orthogonal matrices—a concept borrowed from the Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT).  

### 5.1 Information‑Transmission View

Think of each factor as routing “information” between two adjacent layers in a grid graph:
```
source nodes → factor1 → factor2 → … → factor m → destination nodes
```
Dense connectivity at the end means any source node can influence any destination node after passing through all factors. By carefully designing each factor’s sparsity pattern (the *butterfly* pattern), we achieve dense end‑to‑end connectivity using only \(O(d\,\log d)\) trainable entries instead of \(O(d^{2})\).

### 5.2 Butterfly Factorization

A butterfly factor for size \(k=2^{t}\) looks like:
\[
\mathbf B^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
    \operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol d_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol d_{2})\\[4pt]
    \operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol d_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol d_{4})
\end{bmatrix},
\]
where each diagonal vector contains learnable scalars (or small blocks).  
Stacking log‑scaled butterfly components yields a dense orthogonal matrix:
\[
\mathbf R(m,b)=
    \tilde{\!\!\!\!B}^{b}_{(d,m)}\, 
    \tilde{\!\!\!\!B}^{b}_{(d,m-1)}\, 
    \dots\, 
    \tilde{\!\!\!\!B}^{b}_{(d,1)},
\]
with \(m=\log_2(d/b)\).  

Crucially:
* each component remains orthogonal (parameterized via Cayley on tiny sub‑blocks);
* total effective trainable parameters become 
   \[
   O(b\,d\,m)=O(d\,\log d)\quad (\text{substituting } b=2).
   \]

When \(m=1\), BOFT collapses back to block‑diagonal OFT; when \(b=d,\;m=1,\) it recovers full OFT. Thus BOFT **subsumes** existing approaches while offering a smoother trade‑off between expressivity and parameter budget.

### 5.3 Empirical Takeaway

Experiments on large Vision Transformers, LLMs and text‑to‑image diffusion models show that BOFT matches or exceeds state‑of‑the‑art parameter‑efficient methods such as LoRA while preserving the stability benefits inherited from orthogonality (e.g., unchanged spectral norm).

---

## 6. Why Orthogonal Finetuning Works Across Modalities

The geometric preservation afforded by an orthogonal left‑multiply has several practical implications:

1. **Spectral Norm Stability:** Multiplying by an orthogonal matrix does not change singular values; therefore training remains numerically stable even with larger learning rates.
2. **Bilinear Similarity Learning:** The forward pass becomes a bilinear form  
   \[
   s_i = {\bf w}^{0}_i{}^\top {\bf R}{\bf x},
   \] 
   where ${\bf w}^{0}_i$ are frozen pretrained neuron vectors and ${\bf R}$ learns a similarity metric under strong regularisation.
3. **Transferability:** Because relational angles stay fixed, downstream updates are less likely to catastrophically overwrite generic knowledge—this has been observed when adapting diffusion models for controllable image generation and when finetuning LLMs for classification tasks.
4. **No Inference Overhead:** After training finishes we can fold $\bf R$ into $\bf W^0$ once (i.e., replace $\bf W$ with $\bf R\, W^0$). Inference runs exactly as fast as vanilla inference—no extra computation graph needed.

---

## 7. Bottom Line

Finetuning gigantic foundation models poses a paradox: we must let them adapt enough to solve new tasks while preventing them from unraveling their hard‑earned internal representations.  
Naïve gradient updates break relational geometry; low‑rank adapters such as LoRA sidestep this but sacrifice spectral guarantees; block – diagonal OFT protects angles but still carries unnecessary parameter cost and restrictive sparsity patterns.

**Orthogonal Finetuning** offers a principled middle ground: by constraining updates to an orthogonal transformation we retain pairwise neuron angles—and thus much of the pretrained knowledge—while still providing expressive flexibility for downstream adaptation. Recent extensions like **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)** demonstrate that even tighter parameter budgets are achievable without sacrificing expressivity thanks to clever sparse factorizations inspired by fast Fourier transforms.

For anyone looking to adapt massive models without blowing up compute or destroying existing capabilities, OFT—and its butterfly‐enhanced cousin—provide a compelling recipe that balances geometry preservation, training stability, and parameter efficiency—all things we need when working at today’s scale of AI foundations.
