\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

**Introduction to Mathematical Reasoning Challenges in Large Language Models**

# Why Mathematics Requires Precise Logical Chains  

Mathematics differs from most natural‑language tasks because its statements must obey strict logical rules rather than loosely inferred patterns. A typical algebra problem does not merely ask whether a sentence “sounds plausible”; it demands a sequence of deductions that follow from axioms, definitions, and previously derived results until a single numeric value or symbolic expression is obtained. When a language model attempts such a problem it must reproduce each inference step exactly as a human mathematician would write it—any slight deviation can invalidate the entire derivation because later steps rely on earlier ones being correct.

The importance of maintaining a coherent chain of thought is illustrated by modern prompting techniques such as chain‑of‑thought (CoT) prompting \citep{cot} and program‑of‑thought (PoT) prompting \citep{pot,pal}. In CoT prompting the model is encouraged to verbalize intermediate calculations (“First compute … Then simplify …”) before delivering the final answer, thereby making its reasoning trace visible both to evaluators and to downstream tools such as automatic graders. PoT prompting extends this idea by having the model generate executable code (for instance Python code that uses \texttt{sympy} or \texttt{math}) whose execution yields intermediate values before the final answer is reported \citep{pot,pal}. Both methods rely on a disciplined progression through logical steps; if any link in the chain is broken—say an arithmetic mistake or an incorrect algebraic manipulation—the final result will be wrong regardless of how fluent the surrounding language appears.

In addition to correctness, precision matters because mathematical expressions have an unambiguous syntax dictated by symbols such as “\(=\)”, “\(+\)”, “\(\int\)”, which do not tolerate paraphrasing without changing meaning much more than ordinary prose does. Thus large language models must learn representations that capture symbolic structure alongside natural linguistic patterns—a dual requirement seldom seen outside mathematics or programming domains.


# Differences Between Quantitative Reasoning and Formal Proof Tasks  

Quantitative reasoning problems are typically presented as word problems whose goal is to compute a numerical answer after performing several elementary operations (addition, subtraction, probability calculations, calculus manipulations). Benchmarks such as GSM8K \citep{gsm8k} and the competition‑level MATH dataset \citep{MATH} belong to this family: a model reads a textual description (“A box contains …”), carries out mental arithmetic or algebraic manipulation step‑by‑step, and finally outputs an integer or rational number that can be checked against a ground‑truth answer key.

Formal proof tasks sit higher up on the hierarchy of mathematical difficulty because they require transforming an informal statement into a rigorously verified theorem within a proof assistant’s logic system (e.g., Isabelle). In the miniF2F benchmark \citep{minif2f} each problem originates from an Olympiad‑level theorem; the model must generate a sequence of lemmas and tactic invocations that together constitute a machine‑checkable proof script \citep{dsp_proof}. Unlike quantitative tasks where success is judged solely by numerical equality, formal proofs are validated through automated theorem provers such as Sledgehammer \citep{sledgehammer}; even if every individual arithmetic step looks plausible in natural language, any missing logical justification will cause verification failure.

Consequently these two families demand different kinds of reasoning capacities from language models. Quantitative problems focus heavily on accurate arithmetic manipulations and plain English explanations of those calculations (often enhanced through CoT), whereas formal proof problems require familiarity with higher‑order logic syntax — the ability to encode definitions “\(∀x∈ℝ,\ f(x)=x^2\)” — and to orchestrate tactics that manipulate goals within a structured proof state.


# Key Public Benchmarks  

The community has coalesced around several widely used benchmarks that expose different aspects of mathematical competence:

* **GSM8K** evaluates elementary school–level quantitative reasoning through eight‑step word problems drawn from grade‑school curricula.
* **MATH** raises difficulty dramatically by featuring competition‑level problems spanning algebra, geometry, calculus, combinatorics, and number theory; it serves as a primary indicator of deep symbolic understanding.
* **SAT‑style Assessments** such as those derived from Llemma \citep{llemma} test multiple‑choice mathematics akin to standardized exams.
* **Open Courseware (“OCW Courses”)** provide university‑level problem sets that test sustained reasoning over longer proofs.
* **MMLU‑STEM** assesses broad scientific knowledge including mathematics via multiple choice questions drawn from college textbooks.
* **Chinese Benchmarks** broaden linguistic coverage: MGSM‑zh measures Chinese‐language quantitative reasoning; CMATH gauges Chinese competition problems; Gaokao‑MathCloze and Gaokao‑MathQA target China’s national university entrance examinations.
* **Formal Mathematics Benchmarks** such as miniF2F challenge models with informal‑to‑formal translation tasks requiring interaction with proof assistants like Isabelle \citep{isabelle}.

Each benchmark defines its own success metric—typically accuracy for multiple‐choice tasks or exact match for numeric answers—and together they span from straightforward calculation through intricate symbolic derivations.


# Performance Gap Between Proprietary Models and Open Models  

Despite rapid progress in large language modeling, there remains a pronounced disparity between closed‑source giants such as GPT‑4 \citep{gpt4} or Gemini‑Ultra \citep{gemini} and publicly available alternatives. Proprietary systems consistently dominate every benchmark mentioned above, often by margins exceeding ten percentage points on high‐stakes datasets like MATH.

Open‑source candidates—including generalist models such as Mistral 7B \citep{mistral}, Llemma 34B \citep{llemma}, Qwen 72B \citep{qwen}, or specialized efforts like WizardMath v1 \citep{wizardmath}—have historically lagged behind these closed systems by sizeable gaps both in raw accuracy and stability across domains (English versus Chinese). Even large multilingual bases trained on extensive internet corpora fail to match proprietary fine‐tuned variants due primarily to limited exposure to high‐quality mathematical texts during pretraining.

DeepSeekMath demonstrates that careful data engineering can dramatically narrow this divide without increasing parameter counts dramatically. The DeepSeekMath Base 7B model attains $64.2\%$ accuracy on GSM8K while achieving $36.2\%$ on competition‐level MATH—performance comparable to Minerva 540 B \citep{minerva}, which possesses roughly seventy–seven times more parameters than DeepSeekMath Base 7B despite being closed source.\footnote{Minerva builds upon PaLM \citep{palm} with additional mathematical fine‐tuning data.)} After instruction tuning its CoT capability reaches $46.8\%$ on MATH; following reinforcement learning via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) this figure rises further to $51.7\%$, matching—or even exceeding—the publicly reported scores of many proprietary contemporaries.\footnote{Self‐consistency over $64$ samples pushes this number up to $60.9\%$, illustrating additional gains obtainable through sampling strategies without external toolkits or voting schemes.)}

Thus while GPT‑4 still leads comfortably (\textit{e.g.,} surpassing $90\%$ on many datasets), DeepSeekMath narrows the gap enough that open collaboration becomes feasible: researchers can now study high‐performing mathematical reasoning without dependence on closed APIs.


# Motivation Behind Building an Open‑Source Math Specialist  

Two intertwined motivations drive the creation of an openly released mathematics specialist:

1️⃣ **Exploiting Public Web Data at Scale** – The authors show empirically that Common Crawl contains vast reservoirs of high‐quality mathematical exposition when filtered through a fastText classifier trained iteratively on seeds such as OpenWebMath \citep{openwebmath}. By employing four rounds of mining plus human annotation they assembled the DeepSeekMath Corpus comprising $120$ billion tokens—a volume roughly seven times larger than Minerva’s math web pages \citep{minerva} and nine times larger than OpenWebMath itself.\footnote{The corpus additionally includes substantial Chinese content, which directly benefits Chinese benchmark performance.)} This effort proves that carefully engineered public data pipelines can yield training material rivaling privileged corpora used by closed models.\footnote{In contrast experiments reveal arXiv papers alone contribute little improvement despite being common in other pretraining efforts~\citep{mathpile}.}

2️⃣ **Demonstrating That Model Size Is Not Solely Deterministic** – By initializing from a code‐oriented base (DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B) rather than a generic LLM family, developers observed substantial gains in both tool–augmented problem solving (“program‐of‐thought”) and pure CoT strategies—a finding that partially answers long–standing conjectures about whether code training enhances logical abilities.\footnote{Two‑stage training experiments confirm code exposure improves subsequent math learning while also mitigating catastrophic forgetting when mixed jointly.)} The resulting system reaches competitive scores across English & Chinese quantitative tests while preserving strong coding aptitude measured by HumanEval \citep{codex} / MBPP \citep{mbpp}. Moreover, reinforcement learning via GRPO—which discards separate value critics in favor of groupwise reward baselines—offers further improvement (+$5$\%-point boost on MATH) while drastically reducing memory consumption compared with conventional PPO.\footnote{GRPO replaces KL penalties with direct divergence regularization between policy and reference models.)}

Collectively these insights suggest that open research communities can achieve state‑of‑the‑art mathematical reasoning without privileged data or enormous parameter budgets so long as they focus on meticulous data curation, leverage code pretraining for structural awareness, and adopt efficient RL refinements like GRPO.\medskip

In sum, building an openly accessible math specialist serves both practical educational aims—providing powerful tutoring assistance across languages—and scientific goals—creating reproducible baselines for future work exploring data selection pipelines, curriculum design for mathematics pretraining, and more robust reinforcement learning methods.\newline

Foundations: Transformer Models, Pre‑training Paradigms and Mathematical Benchmarks  

# Transformers for language modeling – attention mechanism recap  

Large language models such as DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5 7B and its descendants (DeepSeekMath‑Base 7B and DeepSeekMath‑Instruct 7B) are built upon the decoder‑only **transformer** architecture introduced in prior work on sequence modeling. In this setting each input token is first mapped to a dense embedding vector and then processed through several stacked layers containing **multi‑head self‑attention** blocks followed by feed‑forward networks.  

Self‑attention allows every position \(t\) in a sequence of length \(T\) to compute a weighted sum over all previous positions \(< t\). Concretely, three linear projections generate “query”, “key”, and “value’’ vectors \(\mathbf{q}_t , \mathbf{k}_i , \mathbf{v}_i\). The affinity between query \(t\) and key \(i\) is measured by a scaled dot product \(\frac{\mathbf{q}_t^\top \mathbf{k}_i}{\sqrt{d}}\), where \(d\) denotes the head dimension. After applying a softmax across all keys the resulting attention weights determine how much information from each previous token contributes to the representation at position \(t\). Multiple heads run this computation in parallel, enabling the model to capture distinct patterns such as arithmetic relationships, algebraic substitution or logical deduction simultaneously. The outputs of all heads are concatenated and passed through a feed‑forward network before proceeding to the next layer.  

Because every layer attends over **all** preceding tokens, transformers naturally support long contexts—a property leveraged throughout DeepSeekMath’s training where a context window up to 4 000 tokens is routinely employed (see Section 3 “Training Setting”). This ability turns out to be essential when solving multi‑step mathematical problems that require remembering intermediate results generated many steps earlier.



# Next token prediction versus masked language modeling  

During **pre‑training** DeepSeekMath follows an **auto‑regressive next‑token prediction** objective rather than a masked language modeling scheme. Formally, given a prefix \(x_{<t}\) consisting of the first \(t{-}1\) tokens, the model maximizes 
\[
\log p_{\theta}\!\bigl(x_t\,|\,x_{<t}\bigr)
\]
for every position \(t\) in a document spanning up to 4 000 tokens. This objective matches the generation process used at inference time—all downstream tasks—including chain‑of‑thought answering or program synthesis are performed via left‑to‑right decoding until an end‐of‐sequence marker appears.  

By contrast, **masked language modeling** randomly replaces selected tokens by a special mask symbol and asks the network simultaneously to predict all masked positions conditioned on both left and right context (e.g., BERT style). While masked objectives have demonstrated success for understanding‐oriented tasks, they break the natural autoregressive dependency required for stepwise reasoning chains typical of mathematics problems, where each subsequent step must depend precisely on what has been written before. Consequently all experiments reported in this work—including those evaluating quality after reinforcement learning—are grounded exclusively in next‑token prediction training.



# Scaling laws: parameters versus tokens  

A central theme emerging from our experiments is that **data scale** can compensate for modest model size when targeting sophisticated mathematical reasoning.  

*Parameter scale alone does not guarantee performance.* The closed‑source Minerva 540 B model (\(540\times10^9\) parameters) builds upon PaLM 540 B while ingesting web mathematics data at massive scale – it nevertheless attains comparable scores to our **7 B** parameter base model after dedicated mathematical pre‑training (`DeepSeekMath‐Base 7B`). Concretely `DeepSeekMath‐Base` reaches $64.2\,\%$ accuracy on GSM8K and $36.2\,\%$ on competition­‐level MATH despite having roughly **1⁄77**th of Minerva’s parameters.\footnote{Results quoted from Section 3 “Training and Evaluating DeepSeekMath‐Base 7B”.}   

*Token quantity matters.* The `DeepSeekMath` corpus comprises **120 B** mathematics‐related tokens drawn from Common Crawl (≈ 40× more than Minerva’s web portion). Empirical curves plotted during corpus validation reveal that models trained on this very large corpus continue improving far beyond those trained repeatedly on smaller collections such as MathPile (\~ 9 B) or OpenWebMath (\~ 13 B). When exposure reaches 120 B math tokens (`DeepSeekMat h−Corpus`) performance curves are still rising at epoch 1 whereas baselines plateau after ≈ 50 B collated repetitions.\footnote{Derived from Figure 2 caption “DeepSeek‐LLM 1.3 B ... steeper learning curve”.}  

Thus **scaling law** insights gleaned here assert roughly linear gains with increasing *token* exposure once data quality remains high—even modestly sized decoders reap substantial benefits when fed tens of billions of carefully filtered mathematical examples.



# Role of code‑oriented pre‑training in downstream reasoning  

A distinctive design decision behind DeepSeekMath was initializing all stages from **DeepSeek‐Coder‐Base‐v1\.5 7B**, a model already exposed extensively to source code during its prior phase (§3 “Training & Evaluating”). Empirical ablations confirm three concrete effects:\footnote{All quantitative observations follow Section 4 “Lessons Learnt in Pre‑Training”.}

1️⃣ **Program‐aided solving improves dramatically after code exposure** – When evaluating program‐of‑thought prompting (`PoT`) on GSM8K and MATH, a model pretrained solely on mathematics already attains nontrivial scores; adding an initial stage consisting of **400 B** code tokens before mathematics raises those scores further⁠—the subsequent math phase (`150 B` math tokens) then pushes performance even higher.\footnote{Table 10 (“code–math”) summarises these jumps although exact numbers are omitted here for brevity}.  

2️⃣ **Pure numerical reasoning benefits even without tool use** – After two stages (code → mathematics), chain-of-thought accuracy on zero‑tool problems surpasses that obtained by direct mathematics‐only training (`150 B` math). This suggests that exposure to programming constructs—variables assignment, function calls, loop logic—enhances the model’s capacity for sequential logical manipulation required by arithmetic derivations.\footnote{Observed even before reinforcement learning was applied}.  

3️⃣ **Catastrophic forgetting mitigated via mixed one‑stage training** – When code and math data were interleaved during a single preprocessing pass (`400 B` code + `150 B` math tokens), coding ability measured on HumanEval/MBPP remained stable while program-aided solving retained much of its gain compared with clean two-stage schedules.\footnote{Table 11 (“code–math–general”) records this balance}.  

Collectively these findings support the hypothesis articulated early (§3): *Code training improves reasoning abilities*, at least within domains where algebraic manipulation mirrors programming constructs.



# Detailed description of benchmark families  

A suite of public benchmarks serves repeatedly throughout our evaluation pipeline; understanding their scope clarifies why certain design choices matter.\footnote{Benchmarks listed chiefly in Sections 3 and 4}.  

* **GSM8K** (\citep{gsm8k}) – A collection of grade–school arithmetic word problems designed for zero–shot chain-of-thought assessment (“quantitative reasoning”). Each instance requires several intermediate steps such as integer arithmetic, fractions handling or basic algebraic rearrangement before arriving at a final integer answer.\footnote{Our best post‐RL score reaches $88.2\,\%$ accuracy (§5 “RL”).}  

* **MATH** (\citep{MATH}) – The *competition­‐level* benchmark comprising contest problems sourced primarily from national Olympiads and college entrance examinations across algebra, geometry, combinatorics & calculus domains.\footnote{Each problem expects an extended symbolic derivation; baseline deep models typically achieve $<30\,\%$, whereas DeepSeekMath‐RL hits $51.7\,\%$.} Its difficulty surpasses GSM8K considerably due both to richer symbolic notation (e.g., summations) and deeper multi–step logical dependencies.\footnote{Self–consistency sampling over $64$ variants lifts performance further up to $60.9\,\%$.}  

* **CMATH** (\citep{wei2023cmath}) – A Mandarin counterpart covering Chinese K–12 curricula across $76$ sub–topics such as linear equations or quadratic factorisation.\footnote{Chinese benchmarks include MGSM\-zh along with Gaokao variants mentioned later}. Its multilingual nature enables us to test whether pre–training corpora contain non­English mathematical discourse—a property confirmed by superior scores when using DeepSeekMath’s multilingual web collection.\footnote{See Table 3 “corpora comparison”.}  

* **MiniF2F** (\citep{minif2f}) – A *formal theorem proving* benchmark that supplies short informal Olympiad statements together with human written informal proofs; models must generate *formal* Isabelle/HOL scripts (`miniF2F`) which an automated prover (`Sledgehammer`) then attempts to complete.\footnote{Our Base model demonstrates strong few-shot autoformalization per Table 6}. This task evaluates translation fidelity between natural-language mathematical exposition and machine-checkable proof terms—a capability distinct yet complementary to numerical problem solving.



# Metrics used across tasks (accuracy %, Pass@K / Maj@K)  

Evaluating mathematical competence requires diverse quantitative measures depending on whether answers are deterministic or stochastic.\footnote{Metric explanations appear mainly Sections 3 “Evaluation” and §5 “RL”.}

* **Raw accuracy (%)** – For datasets providing an exact answer key (e.g., GSM8K & MATH), correctness is assessed deterministically: an output matches if its final numeric value equals the gold solution after canonicalisation (removing whitespace/punctuation). Percentages reported throughout (“$51.7\,\%$” on MATH without tools or “$88.2\,\%$” on GSM8K after RL) directly reflect this metric.\footnote{Used also for multiple-choice exams like MMLU-STEM where correct option selection counts similarly}.  

* **Pass@K** – When generative sampling produces multiple candidate solutions per question (as employed during reinforcement learning), *Pass@K* quantifies whether *any* one among $K$ generated answers is correct:\[
   {\rm Pass}@K = \frac{\text{\#questions having at least one correct sample}}{\text{\#total questions}} .
   \]  
This metric captures raw diversity capacity—it rewards simply producing at least one right answer among many attempts regardless how improbable each variant individually might seem.\footnote{Figure 13 explores Pass@K behavior after RL}.  

* **Maj@K** – A stricter sibling metric takes a *majority vote*: among $K$ sampled responses one groups identical answers together and selects whichever occurs most frequently (`majority`). If this majority corresponds precisely to the gold answer then Maj@K registers success:\[
   {\rm Maj}@K = \frac{\text{\#questions whose majority answer matches gold}}{\text{\#total questions}} .
   \]  
Maj@K reflects *distributional alignment*: reinforcement learning aims not merely at occasional correctness but at reshaping probabilities so correct answers become dominant peaks.\footnote{Section 5 reports that RL increases Maj@K considerably while Pass@K stays roughly unchanged}.  

Finally,
* **Self-consistency / majority voting over many samples** – An operational variant employed during inference draws dozens ($64$) sampled completions per question using chain-of-thought prompting; taking either majority consensus or averaging numeric predictions yields notable boosts—for example moving MATH accuracy from $51.7\,\%$ (single sample) up to $60.9\,\%$ under self-consistency (\S1 Abstract).---These metrics together enable nuanced analysis ranging from deterministic scorecards (accuracy %) towards probabilistic robustness assessments critical when deploying LLMs for real-world mathematical assistance.
