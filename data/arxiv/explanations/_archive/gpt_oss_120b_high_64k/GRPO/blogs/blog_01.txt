\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Mining Math Knowledge from the Web: How DeepSeekMath Built a 120‑Billion‑Token Corpus  

Mathematical reasoning has become one of the toughest frontiers for large language models (LLMs).  
Open‑source models can still lag far behind proprietary giants such as GPT‑4 or Gemini Ultra—largely because they lack a massive, high‑quality source of “math text.”  

DeepSeekMath tackled this bottleneck head‑on by turning raw web data from **Common Crawl** into a **120 billion‑token** multilingual mathematics corpus. In this post we walk through their clever data‑mining pipeline step by step, showing how fastText classification, iterative domain filtering, de‑duplication, and careful benchmark de‑contamination turned billions of generic web pages into a goldmine for math reasoning.

---

## Why Start From the Open Web?

Most recent math‑focused LLMs rely heavily on curated sources such as arXiv papers or manually assembled problem sets. While valuable, those datasets are relatively small and heavily English‑centric. The authors observed that **Common Crawl**—a public snapshot of billions of webpages—contains far more mathematical text than previously exploited, provided you can sift the signal from the noise.

The challenge is twofold:

1. **Scale** – Common Crawl holds petabytes of HTML; we need a way to extract *only* the mathematically relevant fragments without drowning in unrelated content.  
2. **Quality & Safety** – The extracted data must be clean (no duplicate boilerplate), multilingual enough to help non‑English benchmarks, and free of exact test‐set leakage that would artificially inflate evaluation scores.

DeepSeekMath’s solution is an **iterative pipeline** that gradually widens its net while sharpening its precision.

---

## Step 1 – Seed Corpus & FastText Classifier

The pipeline starts with a small but reliable “seed” set:

| Seed resource | What it provides |
|--------------|------------------|
| **OpenWebMath** | A curated collection of high‑quality mathematical webpages (~500 k examples). |

Using this seed, the team trained a lightweight binary classifier with **fastText**, a word‑embedding based text classifier that runs quickly even on hundreds of millions of samples.

### Training details (kept simple)

* **Positive examples:** Randomly sample 500 k lines from OpenWebMath.  
* **Negative examples:** Randomly sample another 500 k webpages from Common Crawl that are *not* in the seed set.  
* **FastText hyperparameters:**  
  * Embedding dimension = 256  
  * Learning rate = 0.1  
  * Word n‑grams up to length 3  
  * Minimum word frequency = 3  
  * Training epochs = 3  

This produces a model that can assign each webpage a “math relevance score”—higher means more likely to contain genuine mathematical exposition or problem statements.

---

## Step 2 – Massive De‑duplication

Before firing the classifier over the entire crawl we need to shrink it dramatically:

* **URL‑based deduplication:** Remove identical URLs across different crawl snapshots.  
* **Near‑deduplication:** Detect near‑identical HTML bodies using locality-sensitive hashing and discard duplicates.

After this cleaning stage only **≈ 40 billion** distinct HTML pages remain—a tractable set for batch scoring with fastText.

---

## Step 3 – First Pass Retrieval

FastText scores every page in the deduplicated set and ranks them by relevance. The authors then experimented with several token budgets (40 B, 80 B, 120 B …) by pretraining a small model on each slice and measuring downstream performance on MATH/GSM8K later on.

**Result:** The sweet spot for the first iteration was keeping roughly the top **40 billion tokens** worth of webpages—that is, enough material to see concrete gains without overwhelming downstream training time.

At this point they already had a solid “first draft” containing many textbooks excerpts, forum discussions, lecture notes—mostly English but with some Chinese snippets tucked in as well.

---

## Step 4 – Iterative Expansion via Domain Mining

The initial classifier missed a lot of useful content because its positive set was still too narrow; many mathematically rich sites simply didn’t look like OpenWebMath in surface text features. To widen coverage they introduced an *iterative domain feedback loop*:

1️⃣ **Group pages by domain** – A domain is defined as all URLs sharing the same base host (e.g., `mathoverflow.net`).  

2️⃣ **Compute collection ratio per domain** – For each domain compute what percent of its pages were already captured in the previous pass.  

3️⃣ **Pick promising domains** – Any domain where >10 % of its pages were flagged as “math” is considered “math‐related”. Typical winners include `mathoverflow.net`, `stackexchange.com` subforums like `math.stackexchange.com`, university course sites (`ocw.mit.edu`), etc.

4️⃣ **Manual URL curation** – Human annotators skim these domains and tag specific URL patterns that lead to genuine mathematics content (e.g., `mathoverflow.net/questions/*`).  

5️⃣ **Enrich seed set** – All previously missed webpages matching these curated URLs are added to the positive pool.

6️⃣ **Retrain fastText** – With this richer positive set the classifier now learns new lexical cues (“proof”, “theorem”, LaTeX fragments) which lets it retrieve more hidden gems in the next round.

The loop repeats four times total:

| Iteration | New webpages added | Cumulative tokens |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| 1 | — | ≈ 40 B |
| 2 | millions more | ≈ 80–100 B |
| 3 | diminishing returns | ≈ 118 B |
| 4 | <2 % fresh content vs iteration 3 | ≈ 120 B |

After four passes they amassed **35.5 million** distinct math webpages—just enough to reach a final size of roughly **120 billion tokens**. By iteration 4 they noted that *≈98 %* of what could be found had already been captured, so they stopped further crawling.

---

## Step 5 – Multilingual Boost

Because Common Crawl mirrors global internet traffic, many domains naturally contain non‑English material—most prominently Chinese university lecture notes and forum threads (`zhihu.com`, `baidu.com` learning sections). The classifier doesn’t filter by language, so these streams survive alongside English texts automatically.

When evaluating downstream models on Chinese benchmark suites (CMATH, Gaokao-MathCloze), DeepSeekMath showed noticeable improvements over English‑only corpora—a clear sign that the multilingual component isn’t just incidental noise but genuine signal.

---

## Step 6 – Benchmark De‑contamination

A subtle but essential safeguard concerns *data leakage*: if portions of test sets appear verbatim in training data, reported scores become misleadingly high. DeepSeekMath applies a strict n‑gram filter:

* For every benchmark sentence (e.g., GSM8K problems), extract all contiguous **10‑grams** (sequences of ten words).  
* Scan every candidate webpage; if any exact match appears → discard that page completely.  
* For shorter benchmark strings (<10 grams) that are at least three words long, perform an exact match on those shorter n‑grams as well.

Thus no component longer than three words from any public test suite ends up inside the training pool—a practice aligned with prior LLM hygiene guidelines.

---

## Validating Corpus Quality

The authors didn’t assume “bigger = better.” They ran controlled experiments using a modest **1.3 billion‐parameter** LLM (DeepSeek‑LLM 1.3B) trained for exactly **150 billion tokens** on each candidate corpus:

| Corpus compared | Tokens | Relative size vs DeepSeekMath | Downstream performance† |
|----------------|--------|------------------------------|--------------------------|
| MathPile       | 8.9 B | ~0.07×                       | Lagging behind          |
| OpenWebMath    |13.6 B | ~0.11×                       | Moderate gains          |
| Proof-Pile‑2   |~42 B† | ~0.35×                       | Mixed results           |
| **DeepSeekMath**   |120 B   | —                              | Consistently best across eight benchmarks |

† Measured on few-shot chain‑of‑thought prompts over GSM8K, MATH, SAT, OCW Courses and multilingual tasks; all scores were higher for DeepSeekMath at comparable token budgets, confirming superior *average quality* together with better *learning curves*—the model keeps improving well beyond where other corpora saturate early.

---

## Impact on Model Performance

Using this corpus as part of continued pretraining for a **7 billion parameter** model yielded striking outcomes:

* On GSM8K it hit **64.2%**, beating Minerva’s massive 540 billion‐parameter baseline.
* On competition‐level MATH it achieved **36.2%**, again outpacing all open‐source peers and closing within ≈10% absolute accuracy of closed models like Gemini Ultra or GPT‑4 when later reinforced by RL techniques.
* Crucially, gains weren’t limited to English tasks—the same model also topped several Chinese exam benchmarks thanks to the multilingual portion harvested from Common Crawl.

These results demonstrate that *the number of parameters isn’t everything*: when you feed even a modestly sized model with focused high-quality data at scale, you can reach performance levels previously thought exclusive to billion‐parameter giants.

---

## Takeaways & Future Directions

1️⃣ **Public web data is rich enough for cutting–edge math LLMs**, provided you apply disciplined selection pipelines rather than naïve scraping.  
2️⃣ **FastText + iterative domain curation** offers a surprisingly cheap yet effective way to bootstrap large specialized corpora without needing massive human annotation budgets upfront.  
3️⃣ **Multilingual content comes “for free”** when you mine globally scoped crawls—this can substantially improve performance on non–English benchmarks without extra effort.\  
4️⃣ Rigorous **benchmark decontamination** remains non‐negotiable; simple n​-gram exclusion suffices for most public datasets.\  
5️⃣ Even after building such an extensive dataset there’s still room for improvement—particularly in geometry reasoning and theorem proving where the current corpus still underrepresents certain visual or formal proof styles.\  

The authors plan to keep refining their data pipeline—adding smarter URL classifiers, leveraging newer crawling snapshots, and exploring more sophisticated de‑duplication methods—to push both size and quality beyond the current “120 billion token” milestone.

---

### TL;DR

DeepSeekMath proved that publicly accessible web archives hold enough high‐quality mathematics text to train state-of-the-art reasoning models. By starting with a small seed set, training a fastText relevance classifier, iteratively expanding through domain analysis and manual URL vetting, massively deduplicating HTML pages, and scrubbing any overlap with test suites, they turned Common Crawl into a clean **120 billion token**, multilingual mathematics corpus—the backbone behind their powerful new LLM family.
