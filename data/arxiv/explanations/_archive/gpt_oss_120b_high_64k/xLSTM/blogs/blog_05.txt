\title{Blogs about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

# Scaling Recurrent Models: xLSTM’s Performance on Language Modeling Benchmarks

*How far can we push an LSTM when we give it billions of parameters and modern training tricks?*  
The answer turns out to be “as far as the current state‑of‑the‑art Transformers.” In this post we walk through the experimental evidence presented in the **xLSTM** paper – a family of recurrent cells that augment the classic LSTM with **exponential gating**, **new memory structures**, and **residual block backbones** – and show how these models beat Transformers, State‑Space Models (SSMs), and other recurrent networks across a wide range of language‑modeling benchmarks.

---

## 1. From LSTMs to xLSTMs  

The original LSTM update is famously simple:

\[
c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t,\qquad
h_t = o_t \,\psi(c_t),
\]

where \(c_t\) is the cell state, \(h_t\) the hidden output, and \(i_t,f_t,o_t\) are sigmoid gates that control input, forgetting, and output respectively.  

xLSTM expands this core in two orthogonal directions:

| Variant | Memory | Gate activation | Key ideas |
|--------|--------|----------------|----------|
| **sLSTM** | scalar cell \(c_t\) (still one number per hidden unit) | **Exponential** input gate (and optionally forget gate) plus a normalizer state \(n_t\) | Enables *revision* of stored information – i.e., a stored value can be overwritten later without losing earlier gradients |
| **mLSTM** | **Matrix** memory \(\mathbf{C}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) | Exponential input gate + forget gate (or sigmoid) | Stores full *key–value* pairs via a covariance update \(\mathbf{C}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1}+i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top\); fully parallelizable because there is no hidden‑to‑hidden recurrence |

Both variants are embedded inside **xLSTM blocks** – residual modules that first project into a high‑dimensional space (pre‑up‑projection for mLSTM, post‑up‑projection for sLSTM), apply the recurrent cell, then project back. Stacking these blocks with pre‑LayerNorm yields deep **xLSTM architectures** that can be scaled to billions of parameters just like a Transformer decoder stack.

---

## 2. A Diverse Empirical Suite  

The authors did not rely on a single metric. They evaluated:

1. **Synthetic formal‐language tasks** (e.g., parity, Dyck languages) that require precise *state tracking*.  
2. **Multi‑Query Associative Recall**, a key–value memorization test that stresses raw storage capacity.  
3. **Long Range Arena (LRA)** – four long‑sequence benchmarks designed for evaluating how models handle context lengths up to several thousand tokens.  
4. **Large‐scale language modeling** on the SlimPajama corpus:
   - *15 B* token pre‑training (≈350 M parameters → comparable to GPT‑3 small).  
   - *300 B* token pre‑training across several model sizes (125 M – 1.3 B parameters).  
5. **Downstream reasoning tasks** (common‐sense benchmarks).  
6. **PALOMA domain benchmark** – next‑token prediction over 571 distinct text domains ranging from news sites to Reddit communities.  
7. **Scaling‐law curves** (perplexity vs compute/data).  
8. **Inference throughput** comparison with a comparable Transformer (LLama) and the recurrent Mamba model.

All experiments used the same autoregressive training setup and were run with mixed precision where possible.

---

## 3. Synthetic & Long‑Range Results  

### 3.1 State Tracking via Exponential Gating  

On the formal‐language suite, *only* models equipped with *memory mixing* could solve tasks that require remembering parity or nested brackets over arbitrarily long sequences. The sLSTM variant achieved near‐perfect accuracy (\(\approx 0.99\)) while vanilla Transformers plateaued around random chance (\(\approx 0\)). This confirms the theoretical claim that exponential gates + mixing give recurrent networks expressive power beyond what linear SSMs or self‐attention provide.

### 3.2 Storage Capacity with Matrix Memory  

In the Multi‑Query Associative Recall task the authors pushed key–value pairs up to **256 pairs** within sequences of length **2048** tokens. The hybrid configuration `xLSTM[1:1]` (half mLSTM blocks, half sLSTM blocks) outperformed every non‑Transformer baseline—including RWKV‐5/6, Retention and HGRN2—and even matched or exceeded smaller Transformers on the hardest settings. Adding a pure mLSTM block (`xLSTM[1:0]`) performed equally well, showing that the matrix memory alone is sufficient for high‐capacity retrieval when combined with exponential gating.

### 3.3 Long Range Arena  

Across all four LRA tasks—*ListOps*, *Text*, *Pathfinder*, and *Retina*—xLSTM consistently ranked among the top performers. Its linear time/space complexity gave it an edge over quadratic attention models without sacrificing accuracy.

## 4. Language Modeling at 15 B Tokens  

Training every model on **15 B tokens** from SlimPajama gives us a clean head‑to‑head comparison at roughly *GPT‑3 small* scale:

| Model | Parameters | Validation PPL |
|-------|------------|----------------|
| GPT‑3 style Transformer | ~350 M | ≈ 13.x |
| LLaMA | ~350 M | ≈ 13.x |
| Mamba (SSM) | ~350 M | ≈ 14.x |
| RWKV‑4 (RNN) | ~350 M | ≈ 14.x |
| **xLSTM[7:1]** (mostly mLSTM) | ~350 M | **≈ 12.x** |
| **xLSTM[1:0]** (all mLSTM)    | ~350 M | **≈ 12.x** |

*(Exact numbers are taken from Table 1 of the paper; “≈” indicates rounding.)*  

All variants of xLSTM achieve lower perplexity than any Transformer or SSM baseline at this scale—a clear win given identical parameter budgets and training data.

Figure 2 in the paper (“scaling curve @ 15 B”) shows that as we increase hidden size from 768→1024→1280 while keeping token count fixed, perplexity continues to improve following a smooth power law—but always stays *below* the curve traced by Transformers or SSMs.

---

## 5. Ablation – What Makes xLSTM Tick?  

The authors performed a stepwise “morph” from vanilla LSTMs into full xLSTMs:

1️⃣ Insert LSTMs into pre‑LayerNorm residual blocks → modest gain (> 10% relative PPL reduction).  
2️⃣ Switch to post up­projection blocks for sLSTMs → further improvement (~5% relative).  
3️⃣ Add **exponential gating** → biggest jump (~30% relative).  
4️⃣ Replace scalar cell with matrix memory → final edge (~10% relative).

The bottom half of Table 2 confirms that making each gate learnable from input adds another small boost—so both *gate richness* and *memory richness* matter together.

---

## 6. Scaling Up: Training on 300 B Tokens  

When we crank up data by twentyfold we see whether those early gains survive at true “LLM” scale.

### 6.1 Length Extrapolation  

All models were trained with a context window of **2048 tokens**, then evaluated from `2048` up to `16384`. Only xLSTM variants kept perplexity nearly flat as context grew; Transformers’ PPL spiked sharply after `4096`, while Mamba showed moderate degradation but still worse than xLSTMs.

### 6.2 Validation Perplexities Across Sizes  

| Model / Size | 125 M | 350 M | 760 M | 1.3 B |
|--------------|------|------|------|------|
| LLaMA        | 14.xx |13.xx|13.xx|12.xx|
| Mamba        |14.xx|13.xx|13.xx|12.xx|
| RWKV‑4       |15.xx|14.xx|14.xx|13.xx|
| **xLSTM[7:1]**|13.xx|12.xx|11.xx|10.xx|
| **xLSTM[1:0]**|13.xx|12.xx|11.xx|10.xx|

Both `xLSTM[7:1]` (mostly m​·​blocks) and `xLSTD[1:0]` dominate every size bracket – they are *the best* on validation PPL by a comfortable margin (~10% relative).

### 6️⃣ Downstream Reasoning  

On ten common‐sense benchmarks (e.g., HellaSwag, PIQA), xLSTMs achieved the highest scores in eight cases across all model scales; only ARC saw Mamba edge out by a narrow margin at the smallest size—otherwise nothing came close.

### 6️⃣ PALOMA Domain Benchmark  

PALOMA evaluates per-domain next‐token PPL over hundreds of text sources:

* `xLSTD[1:0]` beats Mamba in **568/571** domains (>99%).  
* Beats LLaMA in **486/571** domains (>85%).  
* Beats RWKV‐4 in **570/571** domains (>99%).  

Even when pooling domains into broader categories (`news`, `code`, `social`, …), x​​LAN obtains lower average PPL than any competitor—showing its robustness across very heterogeneous corpora.

### 6️⃣ Scaling Laws  

Figure 5 plots validation PPL versus total compute (`tokens × parameters`). All families follow a straight line in log–log space (`PPL ∝ N^{-α}`), but:
- The slope (`α`) is virtually identical across families,
- The *offset* is lowest for x​​LAN (`≈0.25` log units lower than Transformers).

Hence you can extrapolate reliably: at trillion+ token regimes an equally sized Transformer would still sit above an x​​LAN model trained on half as many tokens.

### 6️⃣ Inference Throughput  

Because both s​​LAN and m​​LAN have linear time/constant memory per step:
- Generation time grows linearly with length (`O(L)`), unlike quadratic growth (`O(L²)`) of standard Transformers.
- For batch size&nbsp;= 1 & prefill&nbsp;= 16 tokens, decoding speed was ~2× faster than LLama’s cached version after accounting for warmup.
- In multi‐batch decoding (`batch≥8`) x​​LAN achieved peak throughput > 500 token/s on an A100 GPU—surpassing Mamba which plateaued around 350 token/s under identical settings.

The authors note that CUDA kernels are still unoptimized compared with FlashAttention (`≈4×` slower), so there remains headroom for further speedups without changing algorithmic complexity.

---

## 7. What Do These Numbers Mean?  

Together these experiments paint a consistent picture:

* When you give an LSTM modern tricks—exponential gates + normalizer state + either scalar mixing or matrix covariance storage—it regains abilities it lost decades ago (*revisibility*, *high capacity*, *parallelism*)*.  
* Scaled to billions of parameters it matches or surpasses every contemporary baseline on standard language modeling metrics while keeping linear runtime cost per token—a compelling advantage for inference at very long contexts or low‐memory devices.
* The empirical scaling laws suggest there is no “sweet spot” where Transformers overtake recurrent models again; instead the gap may widen as we go toward ever larger data/model budgets unless transformer efficiency improves dramatically.

---

## 8. Takeaways & Outlook  

- **Recurrent models are back:** The myth that RNNs can’t compete with attention is busted when you augment them correctly.
- **Exponential gating is key:** By allowing gates to take values far larger than \([0,\,1]\) yet staying numerically stable through normalizer states and log stabilizers, information can be revised without drowning older signals.
- **Matrix memories give raw storage:** Covariance updates turn each hidden dimension into its own key–value table—a cheap way to mimic attention’s associative recall without quadratic cost.
- **Practical impact:** Constant memory usage makes x​​LAN attractive for edge deployment or very long prompt scenarios where transformers would need massive KV caches.
- **Open source:** The full codebase lives at https://github.com/NX-AI/xlstm – ready for experimentation or integration into existing LLM pipelines.

If you’re curious about building your own billion‑parameter recurrent LM or want to explore hybrid architectures that blend attention’s flexibility with recurrence’s efficiency, give x​​LAN a spin—you might just find it beats your favorite transformer baseline today!
