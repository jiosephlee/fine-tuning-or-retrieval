Chapter 2: # 2. Foundations of Recurrent Neural Networks and Classic LSTMs  

In this chapter we develop a clear mathematical foundation for discrete‑time recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and then specialize this foundation to the Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) architecture as originally proposed in the early 1990s.  The presentation follows directly from the definitions and equations introduced in the “Extended Long Short‑Term Memory” paper, extending them with intuition that helps a first‑time learner understand why each term appears, how gradients propagate through time, and how various design choices affect expressive power and trainability.

## Recap of Discrete‑Time Recurrent Neural Networks  

A discrete‑time recurrent neural network processes an ordered sequence  
\[
\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\dots ,\mathbf{x}_{T}\},
\]  
where each element \(\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}}\) is presented at integer time step \(t=1,\dots ,T\).  
At every step the network maintains an internal hidden representation \(\mathbf{h}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{hid}}}\).  
The hallmark recurrence relation updates this hidden state by combining **two** sources:
the freshly supplied input vector and the **previous** hidden activation:
\[
\boxed{\;\;\mathbf{h}_{t}=g\!\bigl(\underbrace{\mathbf{W}_{x}\,\mathbf{x}_{t}}_{\text{input contribution}}
            +\underbrace{\mathbf{W}_{h}\,\mathbf{h}_{\,t-1}}_{\text{recurrent contribution}}
            +\mathbf{b}\bigr)\;}
\tag{2}
\]
Here \(g(\cdot)\) denotes a pointwise non‑linearity such as \(\sigma(\cdot)\) or \(\operatorname*{tanh}(\cdot)\);  
the learnable parameters consist of an *input* weight matrix \(\mathbf W_x\), a *recurrent* matrix \(\mathbf W_h\), and a bias vector \(\mathbf b\).  

Equation (2) captures precisely what is repeated throughout every subsequent inference step,
and it provides an explicit map from past context (\(\mathbf h_{\,t{-}1}\)) to future context (\(\mathbf h_{\, t}\)).
When unrolled over all steps this recurrence defines a directed computational graph whose depth equals
the sequence length \(T\).  

In classical vanilla RNNs all gates share a **single** hidden state that both
stores information **and** determines how new data influences that storage.
Because both operations rely on identical parameters,
the network quickly encounters numerical difficulties when learning long‑range dependencies,
as explained next.



## Gradient Flow Through Time Steps and the Vanishing/Exploding Problem  

Training an RNN amounts to minimising a loss function \(L=\sum_{k=1}^{T}\ell_k(y_k,\hat y_k)\)
by back‑propagation through time (BPTT).
During BPTT gradients propagate backwards over every recurrence,
and at time step \(k<t\) they appear multiplied by successive Jacobian factors:
\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf h_k}
=
\underbrace{\Bigl( 
          \prod_{i=k+1}^{T}
          \frac{\partial \mathbf h_i}{\partial 
                 \mathbf h_{\,i-1}}\Bigr)}_{\displaystyle 
          J_{k+1:T}}
      \frac{\partial L}{\partial 
            \mathbf h_T}.
      \tag{3}
\]
The Jacobian  
\(J_i=\partial {\bf h}_i/\partial {\bf h}_{\,i-1}\)
encodes how much a small perturbation at one time influences later activations.
If every eigenvalue magnitude satisfies 
\(||J_i||<1,
   ~ i= k+1,\dots,T,
   '
the product \(J_{k+1:T}\) decays exponentially with distance
\(T-k.\)
Consequently gradients vanish → learning stalls for long correlations.
Conversely,
if any eigenvalue magnitude exceeds one,
gradients grow exponentially → exploding updates cause numerical overflow.
Both phenomena were identified already by Hochreiter (1995)
as fundamental obstacles for learning sequences longer than a few dozen steps.



## …the Constant‑Error Carousel Concept  

Long Short‑Term Memory was proposed precisely to mitigate the vanishing–gradient issue.
Its central innovation—the *constant‑error carousel*—places an **additive**
memory variable \(c_t\nobreakspace=\nobreakspace f_t\,c_{\, t-1}+i_t\,z_t   \
one line"  

into which gradient contributions **flow unchanged** unless explicitly altered.
Consider only the term involving past memory:
taking derivative w.r.t.\!\(c_{\, t-1}\),
we obtain
\[
\dfrac{\partial c_t}{%
     		\vphantom{\big|}}
		 {\partial c_{\, t-1}}
     = f_t .
     \tag{4}
\]
Hence along a chain spanning many steps,
the total influence on \(c_k\) derives solely from products 
\(f_{k+1}f_{k+2}\dots f_T.\)
If forget gates are close to unity,
\(f_i\simeq 1,\)
the product remains near one:
gradients preserve their magnitude over arbitrarily long horizons.
If particular timesteps should erase stale content,
the associated forget gates can shrink toward zero,
temporarily reducing gradient flow;
this controlled decay replaces uncontrolled exponential decay
found in plain vanilla RNNs.
Thus the constant‑error carousel provides an *unimpeded corridor*
through which error signals propagate steadily—
capturing precisely why early works referred
to it as “the central idea” enabling long‑range learning.

## Common Architectural Variations  

While Equations (5)–(11) capture “vanilla” LSTMs,
researchers quickly explored extensions aimed at either enlarging storage capacity or providing extra decision signals.

### Vectorised Cell States  

Early work treated `cₜ` as a single real number.
Later formulations aggregated several scalar cells into a vector 
`\boldsymbol cₜ∈ℝ^{d_c}` .
All four gates operate elementwise on this vector;
meanwhile additional *recurrent matrixes* ${R}` couple distinct dimensions:
```
   {\boldsymbol c}_ [t] = f[t] ⊙ {\boldsymbol c}[ [t−   ]]
                     + i[t] ⊙ z[t] .
```
These coupling matrices enable information stored in one dimension
to influence updates elsewhere—a mechanism called *memory mixing*
that proved essential when tackling tasks requiring nuanced state tracking ([Greff et al., 2015]; see Appendix B).

### Peephole Connections  

A second family inserts direct pathways from the current
cell value `cₜ` back into every gate’s preactivation:
```
˜ iₜ ← … + pᵢ ⊙ c₍ₜ−¹〉 ,
˜ fₜ ← … + p_f ⊙ c₍ₜ−¹〉 ,
˜ oₜ ← … + p_o ⊙ c₍ₜ〉 .
```
Here `{pᵢ,p_f,p_o}` are learned vectors (“peepholes”).
By exposing exact memory magnitude when deciding whether
to write (`i`) or erase (`f`) information,
peepholes sharpen timing behavior especially on periodic tasks.
Although not part of Eq.(6)’s minimal specification,
most textbooks regard them among “common architectural variations”.

### Multi‑Head / Multi‐Cell Design  

Within `sLSTM`—a descendant introduced later—the authors allow several heads.
Each head contains its own collection of scalar memories;
mixing occurs *within* heads but never across heads.
Consequently sLSTMs attain a novel form ...

of parallelism reminiscent \
of recent multi‐head attention while retaining true recurrence.


These variants demonstrate how modest changes—adding extra linear terms or reconfiguring shape—
can dramatically alter representational capacity without abandoning Core&#160;«const­ant‐error Carousel» principle.




## LayerNorm Placement Conventions in Modern RNN Stacks  

Training very deep stacks (dozens or hundreds of layers) requires stabilising statistics across layers.
Two prevailing strategies exist concerning Layer Normalisation (`LN`):

### Pre‑LayerNorm  
`LN` is applied **before** any residual addition:
```
yᶦ = LN(fᶦ(xᶦ))         # feedforward/submodule  
outᶦ = xᶦ + yᶦ           # residual connection
```
The normalization therefore sees only freshly computed sublayer output,
insulating subsequent layers from internal covariate shifts.
The `xLSTM` paper reports that all large experiments (“pre‑LayerNorm residual backbones”) follow exactly this pattern,
mirroring practice adopted throughout today’s transformer‑based language models such as GPT‑3 or PaLM.
Pre‑LN has been shown empirically to enable deeper stacking without requiring fine‐grained learning‐rate schedules.

### Post‑LayerNorm  
An older convention places `LN` **after** adding the skip connection:
```
outᶦ = LN(xᶦ + yᶦ).
```
While mathematically equivalent under some conditions,
post‑LN normalises a sum containing both old activations (`xᶦ`) and fresh updates (`yᶦ`).  
When `yᶦ` has much larger magnitude than `xᶦ`,
post‑LN may amplify noise originating from earlier layers—a difficulty evident especially when scaling models past hundreds of millions parameters.



#### Why Modern Stacks Prefer Pre‑LN  

From an optimization viewpoint ``pre’’ normalisation guarantees that each submodule receives inputs whose mean≈0 and variance≈1 regardless of depth.
Consequently gradient magnitudes entering backward passes stay balanced throughout extremely deep networks such as those required for billions of parameters—the very setting explored by `xLSTM`.
Even though `mLSTM` eliminates explicit hidden‐to‐hidden mixes making parallelisation easier,
its designers still embed it inside pre‑LN blocks so that training dynamics remain comparable with contemporary transformer baselines.

In this chapter we built from first principles toward the canonical LSTM formulation:

* We began with generic discrete‐time recurrences (§ Recap…) highlighting how inputs and past states mingle via distinct learnable transforms.  
* We examined gradient propagation using BPTT (§ Gradient…) illustrating why naïve recurrences suffer exponential attenuation/extinction (“vanish”/“explode”).  
* We then introduced Hochreiter’s constant–error carousel (§ CCE…) which preserves gradients through an additive memory variable whilst still permitting selective forgetting via sigmoidal “forget” gates.  
* Afterwards we derived stepwise every equation comprising vanilla LSTMs (§ Full set…) making clear roles for candidate inputs, three multiplicative gates, squashing nonlinearity ψ, nonlinear activations φ–and their associated weight/bias families (`input vs recurrent`).
