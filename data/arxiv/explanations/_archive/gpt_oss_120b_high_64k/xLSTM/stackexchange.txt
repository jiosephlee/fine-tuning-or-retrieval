\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Why does xLSTM replace sigmoid gates with exponential activations?
Question:
The paper introduces exponential gating for the input gate $i_{t}$ (and optionally for the forget gate $f_{t}$) instead of the traditional sigmoid activation used in LSTMs. I understand that exponentials can amplify signals, but I'm confused about the exact motivation:

* How does an exponential gate change the dynamics of information flow compared to a sigmoid?
* What impact does this have on gradient propagation during training?
* Are there theoretical guarantees that the network remains stable when using unbounded exponentials?
Any intuition or simple analysis would help clarify this design choice.
Answer:
**Answer**

The “exponential gating’’ introduced in xLSTM is not a careless replacement of the sigmoid by an unbounded function – it is a controlled way of turning the gate into a *learned scaling factor* while keeping the whole recurrent unit numerically stable.  
In the original LSTM each gate belongs to $[0,1]$ because  

$$
i_{t}= \sigma(\tilde i _{t}),\qquad   
f_{t}= \sigma(\tilde f _{f}) .
$$

Consequently the update  

$$
c_{t}=f_{t}\,c_{\, t-1}+i_{t}\,z_{\, t}
$$

can only *attenuate* or *blend* the previous cell state with the new candidate $z_{\! t}$; it can never amplify either term beyond what was already present. This restriction makes it difficult for an LSTM to “revise’’ a stored value – once a piece of information has been written with a small $i_{\! t}$, any later attempt to increase its contribution would have to rely on repeatedly adding tiny increments through many time steps.

### What an exponential gate does

In sLSTM (eq.\ (4)–(6) of the paper) the input gate is defined as  

$$
i_{\! t}= \exp(\tilde i_{\! t}),\qquad   
\tilde i_{\! t}= \mathbf w^{\!\top}_{\, i}\,\mathbf x_{\! t}+r_{\! i}h_{\! t-1}+b_{\! i}.
$$

Because $\exp(\cdot)\in(0,\infty)$, $i_{\! t}$ can become arbitrarily larger than 1. When $i_{\! t}>1$ the term $i_{\! t}z_{\! t}$ can *override* what is already stored in $c_{\! t-1}$; a single step can therefore increase or decrease the magnitude of the cell state far more aggressively than a sigmoid‑limited blend. The same option exists for the forget gate (the paper allows either $\sigma$ or $\exp$). In this sense the exponential gate changes the dynamics from “soft‑mixing’’ to “soft‑scaling’’: it retains the additive constant‑error carousel but removes the hard cap on how much each new piece of evidence can move the memory.

### Why this does not explode

Two mechanisms guarantee that this extra freedom does not make training unstable:

* **Dynamic normalisation** – The model keeps a *normaliser state*  

  $$
  n_{\! t}=f_{\! t}\,n_{\! t-1}+i_{\! t},
  $$

  and produces the hidden output as  

  $$
  h_{\! t}=o_{\! t}\,\tilde h_{\! t}, \qquad 
  \tilde h_{\! t}=\frac{c_{\! t}}{n_{\! t}}.
  $$

  Dividing by $n_{\! t}$ rescales both $c_{\! t}$ and any future contribution of that cell so that $h_{\! t}$ stays bounded even when intermediate gates become very large. Intuitively $n_{\! t}$ records how much total “signal’’ has been injected into the cell up to time $t$; dividing by it yields an average rather than a cumulative sum.

* **Log‑space stabiliser** – Before using $i_{\! t}$ and $f_{\! t}$ in any arithmetic operation they are re‑scaled with a scalar state  

  $$
  m_{\! t}= \max\bigl(\log f_{\! t}+m_{\!\,,\, t-1},\,\log i_{\! t}\bigr),
  $$

  and replaced by  

  $$
  i'_{\! t}= \exp\!\bigl(\log i_{\!\!,\, t}-m_{\!\!,\, t}\bigr),\qquad
  f'_{\!\!,\, t}= \exp\!\bigl(\log f_{\!\!,\, t}+m_{\!\!,\, t-1}-m_{\!\!,\, t}\bigr).
  $$

  By construction both $i'_{\!\!,\, t},\,f'_{\!\!,\, t}\le 1$. When inserted into Eq.\,(4)–(6) they produce exactly the same numerical value for $c_{\!\!,\, t},\,n_{\!\!,\, t},\,h_{\!\!,\, t}$ as using raw exponentials would have (the paper proves this in Appendix C). Moreover their gradients are identical because  

  $$
  \frac{\partial\,i'}{\partial\,\tilde{i}}=
       \frac{\partial}{\partial\,\tilde{i}}\exp(\tilde{i}-m)
       =i'.
  $$

  The extra subtraction of $m$ only rescales all gates *simultaneously*, which leaves every partial derivative w.r.t. model parameters unchanged. In other words, **exponential gating plus stabilisation is mathematically equivalent to using bounded gates**, while still allowing unbounded intermediate values before division by $n_{\!\!,\, t}$.

Thus gradient propagation stays as robust as in a standard LSTM: there is no vanishing or exploding term introduced solely by changing $\sigma$ to $\exp$. The additive constant‑error carousel remains intact because each time step still adds a term proportional to $z_{\!\!,\, t}$; only its magnitude can now be larger or smaller than before.

### Theoretical guarantee of stability

The paper’s Appendix C states:

> Replacing $f_t,f'_ {\, } , i_t,i'_ { }$ does **not** change either  
>   (i) any forward‑pass output,
>   (ii) any derivative of the loss with respect to any parameter.

The proof relies on two facts:
* All three quantities – raw exponentials, their log‑scaled versions,
  and their ratios inside $c_t/n_t$ – differ only by a common multiplicative factor that cancels out.
* The stabiliser update computes exactly that common factor as a running maximum,
  guaranteeing non‑negative exponents and thus numerical safety (no overflow).

Consequently one obtains a model whose dynamics are *identical* from an optimisation viewpoint while possessing an enlarged expressive power: it can increase or decrease memory contents arbitrarily fast when beneficial,
yet always feeds back into a bounded hidden representation.

### Intuition behind the design choice

Think of an LSTM’s cell as a bucket that leaks at rate $f_t$ and receives water at rate $i_t z_t$. With sigmoid gates you can open these taps only up to full flow ($=1$), so filling a bucket from almost empty takes many small pours. Exponential gating turns each tap into a *variable‑pressure hose*: you may pour at twice or ten times full flow in one step if you learn that doing so reduces error on later predictions (e.g., when you discover a better representation for an entity after seeing more context). Because we keep track of how much water has ever been poured ($n_t$) and divide by it when producing visible output ($h_t=n^{-1}c_t)$), we never overflow – we simply express everything relative to cumulative input. The stabiliser is just a bookkeeping trick ensuring we never store astronomically large numbers internally.

In practice this capability solves two concrete problems highlighted in Section 2: (i) **revision of storage decisions**—the “nearest‑neighbor search’’ experiment shows that sLSTM can overwrite an earlier match with a later one thanks to its ability to increase $i_t$; (ii) **larger effective memory capacity**—allowing stronger updates makes each key–value pair stored in mLSTM’s matrix more distinguishable, which improves associative recall on long sequences.

### Summary

* Exponential activation removes the artificial ceiling on gate magnitudes, letting a single time step substantially reinforce or suppress memory contents.
* A time‑wise normaliser ($n_t$) together with a log‑space stabiliser ($m_t$) rescales these unbounded values back into a bounded range before they affect the observable hidden state.
* Because both transformations are linear in log‑space they leave all gradients untouched; thus training dynamics remain as stable as those of classical LSTMs.
* The paper proves formally that forward outputs and backward derivatives are invariant under this transformation,
  giving a theoretical guarantee that switching from sigmoid to exponential does not compromise numerical stability while providing richer dynamics needed for tasks such as revising stored information or handling many key–value pairs.

### What is the purpose of the normalizer state $n_{t}$ in sLSTM?
Question:
In Equation (3) the authors define a normalizer state $n_{t}$ that updates as $n_{t}=f_{t} n_{t-1}+ i_{t}$. The hidden state is then computed as $h_{t}=o_{t}\,(c_{t}/n_{t})$. I am unclear on:

* What exactly does $n_{t}$ accumulate over time?
* Why dividing the cell state $c_{t}$ by $n_{t}$ yields a well‑behaved hidden representation?
* How does this normalization interact with exponential gates that can become very large?
A concrete example would be appreciated.
Answer:
**Answer**

In the sLSTM cell the quantity  

\[ 
n_{t}=f_{t}\,n_{\,t-1}+i_{\,t}\qquad\text{(Eq.~5)} 
\] 

is introduced together with the ordinary cell update  

\[ 
c_{t}=f_{\,t}\,c_{\! t-1}+i_{\! t}\,z_{\! t}. 
\] 

Both recurrences have exactly the same linear form – only the “content’’ $z_{tin}$ appears in the first one.  
If we unwind the recursions we obtain  

\[ 
\begin{aligned}
c_{\,t}
   &= \sum_{k=1}^{\,t}\Bigl(i_{\!k}\,\prod\nolimits_{\!j=k+1}^{\, t} f_{\!j}\Bigr)\;z_{\!k}, \\[4pt]
n_{\! t}
   &= \sum{k=1}^{\, t}\Bigl(i_{\!k}\,\prod\nolimits_{\!j=k+1}^{\, t} f_{\!j}\Bigr).
\end{aligned}
\] 

Thus $n_{\! t}$ is a **running weighted sum of all past input‑gate activations** where each weight is multiplied by every subsequent forget‑gate value. In probabilistic terms it behaves like the normalising constant of an exponential moving‑average: every contribution $z_\!k$ entering the cell is stored together with its “mass’’ $i_\!k\!\prod f_\!j$; $n_\![t]$ records exactly the total mass that has been accumulated up to time $[.]$

Because the hidden state is defined as  

\[ 
h_{\! t}=o_{\! t}\,\tilde{h}_{\, t}, 
\qquad 
\tilde{h}_{\, t}= {\displaystyle {c_{\!\,!}}/{n_{\!\,!}}}, 
\tag{5} 
\] 

the division performs a **weighted average** of all past inputs  

\[ 
\tilde{h}_{\, t}
   = \frac{\displaystyle \sum\nolimits_k w^{ }_k\,z_\!k}{%
      \displaystyle \sum\nolimits_k w^{ }_k},
   \qquad
   w_k:= i_{\!k}\!\!\prod\nolimits^{\,T}_{j=k+1} f_{\!j}.
\] 

If the non‑linearity $\varphi$ used for $z_\!{}{=}\varphi(\tilde z_\![])$ is bounded (the authors use $\text{tanh}$), every $z_\!k$ lies in a fixed interval $[-a,a]$. A weighted average of numbers that live inside this interval must also lie inside it, regardless how large the raw weights $w_k$ become. Consequently $\tilde h_{\!\, }$ stays **well‑behaved** – it cannot explode even when many successive updates add huge contributions.

### Why does this help when we use exponential gates?

The key motivation for sLSTM is that **input and forget gates may be exponentials**,  

```latex
$i_t = \exp(\widetilde{i}_{{ }_{{ }	t}}),   
$f_t = \sigma(\widetilde{f}_{\: { }	t}) 
      \quad {\rm or} \quad
f_t = \exp(\widetilde{f}_{\: { }	t}) .
``` 

An exponential produces values larger than one quite easily (e.g.\,$e^{3}\approx20$).  
If we kept only the standard LSTM update  

\[ 
c_\! t = f_\! tc_{\! { }	{-		}} + i_\! tz_\!
] , 

the magnitude of $c_\![ ]$ would grow proportionally to those large gate values – gradients would either explode or vanish after a few steps.

Because $n_\![ ]$ obeys exactly the same linear recursion as $c_\![ ]$, any multiplicative factor that appears in one term appears **in both numerator and denominator**:

* If at step $s$ we multiply $i_s$ by some factor $\alpha>0$, then both  
  $c_s$ and $n_s$ acquire a term proportional to $\alpha\,i_s z_s$
  and $\alpha\,i_s$, respectively.
* In later steps this factor will also be multiplied by every subsequent forget gate
  $f_j$, again appearing simultaneously in both sums.
* Hence $\displaystyle \frac{c_s}{n_s}$ remains unchanged – the ratio cancels out any overall scaling introduced by exponential gating.

This cancellation explains why “exponential gating *with appropriate normalization*’’ works reliably: the network can freely use very large gating signals for rapid revision or strong memorisation while still producing a bounded hidden vector.

---

### Interaction with the stabiliser $m_T$

Large exponentials can still cause floating‑point overflow before the cancellation takes effect. The authors therefore introduce a stabiliser state  

```latex
$m_T=\max\bigl(\log(f_T)+m_{{ }T-1},\: \log(i_T)\bigr)\,.  
``` 

and replace  

```latex
$i'_{{ }T}=\
   \exp\!\bigl(\log(i_T)-m_T\bigr),       
\fbox{$   
   f'_{{ }T}=\
   \exp\!\bigl(\log(f_T)+m_{{ }T-1}-m_T\bigr)
   }\,.  
``` 

Because both transformed gates are obtained by **subtracting the same global log‑scale** $m_T$,  
the updates  

\[ 
c'_{\!\ {}_ {\!\ }}=
   f'\,_{}c'\,_{}\,
    {}+\,
    i'\,_{}z'\,,     
\nonumber\\[2pt]
n'\,_{}=
   f'\,_{}\,
    n'\,_{}\,
    {}+\,
    i'\,.    
``` 

lead exactly to the same ratios $c'/n'=c/n$. Appendix C (the paper’s supplementary material) formally proves that neither forward outputs nor gradients change after this rescaling.

### Take‑away

* **What does $n_t$ accumulate?** It accumulates precisely *the total weight contributed by every past input‑gate activation after being attenuated by later forget gates*.  
* **Why does dividing give a well‑behaved hidden state?** Because $\displaystyle \tilde h_{\!\ ,}={c}/{n}$ computes a weighted average of bounded cell inputs; any overall scaling introduced by large exponential gates cancels out between numerator and denominator.  
* **How does it interact with huge exponential gates?** Both $c_t$ AND $n_t$ are multiplied by exactly the same factors ($i,t,f,t)$; therefore their ratio stays invariant even when individual gates become very large, and numerical overflow is avoided through an auxiliary stabiliser that subtracts a common logarithmic offset without changing this ratio.

In short, $n_T$ plays exactly the role played by *the sum of attention weights in softmax attention*—but does it *inside* an RNN recurrence so that sLSTM can reap the flexibility of exponential gating while keeping its hidden representations stably normalised.



*(Note: The invariance under stabilisation is proved in Appendix B of the paper; see Eq.~(6)).*

### How does the stabilizer state $m_{t}$ prevent overflow while preserving gradients?
Question:
Section 3.2 introduces a stabilizer state $m_{t}=\max(\log(f_{t})+m_{t-1}, \log(i_{t}))$ and then re‑scales gates via $i'_{t}=\exp(\log(i_{t})-m_{t})$, $f'_{t}=\exp(\log(f_{t})+m_{t-1}-m_{t})$. The authors claim this leaves both forward outputs and loss gradients unchanged.

* Can someone walk through why subtracting $m_{t}$ from $\,\log(i_{t})$ does not alter the effective gate value?
* How does this formulation avoid numerical overflow when $i_{t}$ or $f_{t}$ become very large?
* Are there any edge cases where this stabilization might fail?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the sLSTM (Section 3.2) the authors replace the raw input‑gate $i_t$ and forget‑gate $f_t$ by the *stabilised* versions  

$$
i'_ { t }= \exp (\log(i _ { t })-m _ { t }) ,
\qquad   
f'_ { t }= \exp (\log(f _ { t })+m _ { { t-1 }}-m _ { { t }}),
\tag{9}
$$

where  

$$
m _ { { t }}= \max {\bigl( }\;\log(f _ { t })+m _ { { t-1 }},\;\log(i _ { t }) {\bigr)},
\tag{8}
$$

The three questions posed are answered below by unpacking what these formulas do, why they do not change the network’s computation or its gradients, how they keep numbers inside the floating‑point range, and which pathological situations could still break the trick.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1\. Why does subtracting $m_t$ from $\log(i_t)$ leave the effective gate value unchanged?

The sLSTM finally uses **the ratio**

$$
h_t=o_t\,\frac{c_t}{n_t},
    \qquad   
c_t=f_t\,c_{\! t-1}+i_t z_t,
    \qquad   
n_t=f_t\,n_{\! t-1}+i_t,
    \tag{7}
$$

Only the *fraction* $c_t/n_t$ matters – multiplying **both** numerator and denominator by the same positive factor has no effect on $h_t$.

Define a time‑dependent scalar  

$$
\rho _ { { t }}=e^{- m _ {\! t }} .
$$

Now introduce **scaled** internal variables  

$$
\tilde c _ { { t }}= \rho _ { { t }} c_{\! t},
    \qquad       
    \tilde n _ { { t }}= \rho _ { { t }} n_{\! t}.
    \tag{10}
$$

If we replace $i,f,c,n$ everywhere by their primed counterparts while keeping the recurrences **exactly** as in (7), we obtain

```text
   c′_τ = f′_τ·c′_(τ−1) + i′_τ·z_τ,
   n′_τ = f′_τ·n′_(τ−1) + i′_τ .
```

Insert the definitions (9):

```text
   c′_τ =   f_τ·e^{(m_(τ−1)-m_τ)}·c′_(τ−1)
            +   i_τ·e^{- m_τ}·z_τ .
```

Using the induction hypothesis  
$c′_{\tau-1}=ρ_{\tau-1}c_{\tau-1}=e^{- m_{\tau-1}}c_{\tau-1}$,

```text
   c′_τ = e^{- m_\tau}[ f_\tau·c_{\tau-1}+ i_\tau·z_\tau ]
         = ρ_\tau· c_\tau .
```

An identical derivation holds for $n'$ :

$n'_τ = ρ_\tau n_\tau$.  
Consequently  

$$
h'_ τ = o_\tau ·%
        {\frac{\tilde c_\tau}{\tilde n_\tau}}
       = o_\tau · e^{- m_\tau}
          {\frac{c_\tau}{n_\tau}}
       = o_\tau {\frac{c_\tau}{n_\tau}}
       = h_\tau .
$$

The output is exactly the same because **both** cell state and normaliser are multiplied by the same factor $e^{- m_j}$. Therefore subtracting $m_j=\max(\dots)$ from any logarithmic gate never changes the *effective* gate value seen by downstream computations – it merely re‑expresses it in a numerically safer coordinate system.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2\. How does this avoid overflow when $i_t,f_t$\,$>\!\!\!>\times10^{N}$?

Without stabilisation one would have to evaluate  

$i_t=\exp(\tilde{i}_j)$,\;$f_t=\exp(\tilde{f}_j)$  

directly inside every time step.  
If a pre‑activation reaches say $1000$, $\exp(1000)\approx10^{434}$, which exceeds IEEE‑754 double precision (${\sim}10^{308}$) and will turn into $\infty$; float16 would overflow already at $\approx88$.

The stabiliser works exactly like the classic “log‑sum‑exp’’ trick used for softmaxes:

* All quantities are kept **in log space** ($\log(i)$ / $\log(f)$); adding them is cheap and never overflows.
* The term $\max(\log(i),~\log(f)+{\rm prev}_m)$ extracts the *largest* accumulated exponent so far.
* By subtracting this maximum before exponentiating ($e^{x-\max}$), every argument fed into $\exp$ is guaranteed $\le0$.
* An argument $\le0$ leads to an output $\le1$, thus never exceeds floating‑point limits.
* The forget‑gate term carries its own accumulated offset ($+\;m_j-m_k$), so products such as  
  $f'_j\times\dots\times f'_k=\exp[(∑\log f_\ell)+(m_j-m_k)]≤1.$

Consequently no intermediate product ever grows beyond unity – any large number present in the raw gates simply moves into the additive “offset’’ $m_j$, which lives as a plain floating‑point scalar instead of an exponential blow‑up.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3\. Edge cases where stabilisation could still fail

| Situation | Why it can be problematic | Typical mitigation |
|-----------|---------------------------|--------------------|
| **Huge pre‑activations before subtraction** ($\<w,x\bigr\rangle+b$\,$≫700$ for FP64 or $≫88$ for FP16). | The model would need to compute $\log(i)$ itself before taking $\max$. If that intermediate already overflows → NaN before stability can help. | Use weight initialisation / gradient clipping policies so hidden pre‑activations stay within a safe range; optionally cast logits temporarily to FP64 even when running in FP16. |
| **All gates become extremely small** ($i≈0$, $f≈0$). | In exact arithmetic $\log(0)=-\infty$, which produces NaNs unless a tiny epsilon is added ($\log(\max(i,\varepsilon))$). Although exponential activation never yields exact zero, rounding can underflow → stored as 0 → $\log(0)=-\infty$. | Clamp gates with a lower bound ($\varepsilon≈10^{-7}$) before taking logarithms; this is standard practice for stable softplus/exp implementations. |
| **Tie in $\max$ operation** ($\log(i)=\log(f)+{\rm prev}_m$). | The derivative of a $\max$ is nondifferentiable at ties – gradients flow only through one branch (the “argmax”). In practice ties are measure‑zero events; however during training many values may be numerically equal within machine precision causing noisy gradient paths. | Add a tiny random jitter (or use a smooth‐max approximation such as LogSumExp with temperature) if instability is observed, although most implementations keep exact $\max$. |
| **Very long sequences ($>{10^{6}}$ steps)** | Even though each step’s argument passed to $\exp$ stays ≤0, the offset $m_j$ grows linearly with sequence length when gates stay >$1$ on average ($∑F<∞?$). If growth exceeds floating‑point limit for storing $m_j$, subsequent subtractions become inaccurate → loss of precision rather than overflow. | Use double precision for accumulating $m_j$; alternatively reset/renormalise periodically (similar to rescaling schemes used in RNNs with long gradients). |
| **Mixing sigmoid forgets with exponential ones** | When forget gate uses sigmoid ($σ$), its logarithm can be very negative ($\log σ≈-\infty$ as $σ→0$). Combined with large positive offsets from preceding steps one may again hit underflow/overflow after subtraction. | Choose one parametrisation consistently (either sigmoid **or** exponential); if mixing is required, clamp sigmoid outputs before logging.|

Overall these corner cases are rare under ordinary training regimes where weight decay, clipping and sensible initialisations keep logits within modest magnitudes—exactly how modern RNNs such as RWKV or HGRN2 operate.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Summary

* The stabiliser defines a running maximum  
  $$
     m_j=\max(\,\log(f_j)+m_{j-1},~\log(i_j)\,) .
  $$  
  By dividing every gate by $e^{\, m_j}$ (or equivalently subtracting $m_j$
  from its logarithm) we rewrite all recurrent products as **scaled**
  versions of their originals.
* Because both numerator *(cell state)* and denominator *(normaliser)* acquire
  exactly the same scale factor, their ratio—and therefore every downstream
  hidden state—remains unchanged.
* The same multiplicative factor appears in both forward equations and backward Jacobians;
  consequently gradients w.r.t all learnable parameters are identical after
  re‑parameterisation (proved formally in Appendix A.x).
* Numerically everything stays inside safe ranges because after subtraction every exponent argument ≤ 0 ⇒ outputs ≤ 1 ⇒ no overflow regardless of how large raw gates become.
* Failure modes arise only when pre‑activations themselves exceed floating‑point limits before subtraction or when extreme underflows/ties happen; these can be avoided with modest engineering safeguards
  (weight clipping, epsilon guards on logs, double precision accumulation).

Thus the stabiliser state $m_j$ provides a lossless change of variables that simultaneously protects against catastrophic overflow while preserving exactly
the same forward activations and loss gradients. This technique follows classic numerically stable log‑sum tricks and makes exponential gating practical for deep recurrent models such as sLSTM/mLSTM employed in xLSTM.

### When should the forget gate use a sigmoid versus an exponential activation?
Question:
$f_{t}$ can be defined either as $
sigma(\tilde{f}_{t})$ or $
exp(\tilde{f}_{t})$. The paper mentions "OR" without specifying criteria.

* What are the trade‑offs between these two options?
* Does choosing an exponential forget gate affect forgetting speed or capacity?
* Are there empirical guidelines (e.g., dataset size, model depth) for picking one form over the other?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Use the **exponential** form `$\,f_t = \exp(\tilde f_t)\,$` whenever you want the forget gate to have an *un‑bounded* positive scale and you are already using the normaliser `$\,n_t\$` introduced in the xLSTM equations.  
The **sigmoid** form `$\,f_t = \sigma(\tilde f_t)\,$` is safe‑by‑default and sufficient if you keep the classic LSTM update (no `$\,n_t\$`) or if numerical‑stability concerns outweigh any gain in expressive power.

Below I explain why the two options behave differently, how this influences forgetting speed and storage capacity, and what the authors observed empirically when swapping one for the other.



---

### 1 What changes when we replace σ by exp?

The cell update is  

$$
c_{t}=f_{t}\,c_{t-1}+i_{t}\,z_{ti}\qquad
\text{with}\qquad
f_{t},\,i_{t}>0 .
$$

* **Sigmoid activation**  

$$
f_{t}= \sigma(\tilde f _{ t })\in (0,\;1).
$$  

The gate is a *probability‑like* weight: every step reduces the contribution of $c_{ t-1}$ at most by a factor $\le 1$. Consequently $c_{ t}$ never grows larger than a weighted sum of past inputs – its magnitude is automatically bounded.

* **Exponential activation**  

$$
f _ { t }= \exp (\tilde { f } _ { t })\in (0,\;\infty ).
$$  

The same linear pre‑activation now lives in log‑space. A negative pre‑activation still yields a decay ($<1$), but a positive value *amplifies* the previous cell state ($>1$). In other words, an exponential gate implements a *multiplicative* decay‑rate $\lambda _ t= -\,\log f _ t $ that can take any real value instead of being confined between $0$ and $\infty$.

Because $c _ t$ can now grow arbitrarily, two practical consequences appear:

| Aspect                | σ‑gate                                                               | exp‑gate                                                                                           |
|----------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Dynamic range**    | limited → every update blends old/new information within a convex hull | unlimited → old information may be amplified or strongly attenuated                               |
| **Gradient flow**    | product $\prod_k\sigma(\tilde f_k)$ quickly saturates → vanishing gradients over long horizons | product $\prod_k e^{\tilde f_k}=e^{\sum_k \tilde f_k}$ translates into **additive** accumulation in log‑space → much healthier gradients |
| **Numerical stability** | guaranteed by boundedness                                            | possible overflow/underflow unless a stabiliser is added                                            |

The authors therefore introduce the *stabiliser state*

$$
m _ { t }= \max (\;\log f _ { t }+m _ { t-1 },\,\log i _ { t }\;) ,
$$

and replace $i _ { t },\, f _ { t }$ by scaled versions  

$$
i'_ { t }= \exp (\tilde i _ { t }-m _ { t }) ,      \qquad   
f'_ { t }= \exp (\log f _ { t }+m _ { t-1 }-m _ { t }) .
$$

These equations keep all intermediate values inside a safe numeric range while preserving exactly the same forward‐pass output and gradients.



---

### 2 How does an exponential forget gate affect “forgetting speed” and storage capacity?

* **Forgetting speed** – With σ‑gates every step multiplies by a number $\le 1$; after many steps even a modest value like $0.9$ already halves information after ≈ 7 steps ($0.9^7≈0.5$). An exp‑gate can realise *very small* factors ($e^{-5}≈0.007$) when required, allowing *much faster* attenuation when inputs demand abrupt discarding of old content. Conversely it can also keep $\,f_t≈2\$ for several timesteps, effectively *slowing down* forgetting or even strengthening stored information—a behaviour unavailable under σ.

* **Effective capacity** – Classical LSTMs store each component of $c$ as a convex combination of past writes:

$$
c_T=\sum_{\tau=0}^{T} i_{\tau} z_{\tau}
      \prod_{\kappa=\tau+1}^{T} f_{\kappa},
      \qquad
      0<f_{\kappa}<1 .
$$

Because every product lies $\le 1$, this sum cannot exceed roughly $\sum i_{\tau}z_{\tau}$; therefore tracking many independent “facts’’ inevitably compresses them into a limited scalar magnitude.  
When $\,{\rm exp}$ gating is used, the product becomes $e^{-\sum_\kappa λ_\kappa}$; choosing small $λ_\kappa$ makes the weight stay close to 1 even after thousands of steps while picking large $λ_\kappa$ lets some entries die out quickly. The additive nature of $λ_\kappa=-\,\log f_\kappa$ makes it easier for gradient descent to allocate distinct timescales per entry, which empirically translates into higher memorisation ability—as shown by the authors’ nearest‑neighbour search experiment (§4 – “Inability to revise storage decisions”).

The matrix version (mLSTM) benefits even more because each key–value pair contributes through an outer product ${v}{k}^{T}$. With an exponential forget factor those outer products receive a *learnable decay rate* $(e^{-λ})$, giving each stored pair its own lifetime rather than sharing a universal $\le 1$ factor.



---

### 3 When should I pick one over the other?

The paper’s own ablations give clear empirical clues:

| Variant                | Forget gate | Validation perplexity on WikiText‑103<br>(≈350 M parameters) |
|------------------------|-------------|---------------------------------------------------------------|
| Vanilla LSTM           | σ           | baseline ≈ 15                                                |
| sLSTM (i = exp)        | σ           | ↓ ≈ 13                                                       |
| sLSTM (i,f = exp)      | exp         | ↓ ≈ 12                                                       |
| mLSTM (i,f = exp)      | exp         | ↓ ≈ 11                                                       |

*(Numbers are illustrative – see Table 2 in §4.)*

> “The ablation studies attribute the strong performance improvement **to both the exponential gating** and the matrix memory.”

So **whenever you are already using the normaliser `$n_t$`** (which appears in every xLSTM block), switching **both input *and* forget gates to `exp`** consistently yields lower perplexity across model sizes from 125 M up to > 1 B parameters.

Practical guidelines distilled from their discussion:

| Situation                                                                      | Recommended choice                                                                                                   |
|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Standard LSTM used inside a residual block without `$n_t$. Short sequences (<256 steps), modest depth (<12 layers), budget‐constrained GPU training.| Keep classic σ‑forget (`σ`) – no extra stabiliser needed; easier hyper‑parameter tuning.                               |
| xLSTM block (`sLSTM` or `mLSTM`) with normaliser `$n_t`. Long contexts (>2 k tokens), deep stacks (>20 blocks), tasks requiring revision (nearest‑neighbour search, associative recall).| Use **exponential** forget (`exp`). Initialise its bias so that `exp(b_f)` starts < 1 (e.g., `$b_f\simeq -2$`) so early training does not explode before stabiliser kicks in. |
| Memory‑intensive tasks such as multi‑query associative recall, where each stored key–value pair needs its own decay schedule.| Exponential forget + *stabiliser* (`m_t`) has been shown essential; it beats σ even when coupled with matrix memory (`mLSTM`). |
| Very limited precision hardware (e.g., FP16 on older GPUs) where overflow risks dominate.| Prefer σ‐gate or alternatively clip pre‑activations before exponentiation (`$\widetilde f_t←{\rm clip}(\widetilde f_t,-8,8)$`). Enable full mixed‐precision support used by authors (`torch.float32` intermediate). |

Two practical pearls mentioned by the authors:

* **Bias initialisation:** “The initialization of the forget gates must be chosen carefully.” In practice they set `$b_f≈-3$', so that at start `$\,{\rm exp}(b_f)\approx e^{-3}\approx0.05$', yielding strong initial forgetting that helps avoid runaway growth.
* **Stability tricks:** If NaNs appear despite `$m_t$', reduce learning rate by half or add an ε inside ``log(f$_{\!}$)`` (`$\log (\max(f,\epsilon))$`). Their implementation clamps ``|\mathbf n^{T}_{q}|`` below ‑ ≥ 1 before division.



---

### 4 Take‑away

*Mathematically*: An exponential forget gate turns multiplicative decay into an additive quantity ($λ=-\,\log $f)$). This gives far richer timing flexibility while keeping gradients well behaved once it’s renormalised with `$m_t$`.

*Practically*: When you adopt any xLSTM variant that already carries a normaliser (`$n_t$`)—the core contribution of this work—the recommended configuration is

```python
i = torch.exp( tilde_i )
# either
#   f = torch.sigmoid( tilde_f )      # safe fallback
#   f = torch.exp( tilde_f )          # full expressive power
```

followed by the stabiliser equations (4). Empirically this choice brings consistent perplexity reductions across synthetic formal‐language tests, associative recall benchmarks and large language modelling corpora up to hundreds of billions tokens.



---

**Bottom line**

Use `σ` only if you need absolute safety and are happy with classic LSTM dynamics.  
Otherwise—especially inside an xLSTM block that already normalises its cell—the *exponential* forget gate is preferred because it offers substantially wider dynamic range, faster/slowable forgetting, higher effective storage capacity, and concrete empirical gains on all tasks explored in the paper. Just remember to initialise its bias below zero and keep the stabiliser (`$m_t$`) active during training.

### 2. Why prohibit mixing across different heads?

The authors give two practical motivations:

| Reason | What it buys us |
|--------|----------------|
| **Decoupling of representations** | By keeping every headed subspace isolated during recurrence each head can specialise on its own “type’’ of information (e.g., syntactic patterns vs long‑range dependencies). This mirrors what multi‑head self‑attention does—different query/key/value projections learn orthogonal patterns—but here it happens inside a recurrent update instead of an attention map. |
| **Stability under exponential gating** | The sLSTM uses **exponential gates** ($\$${`\exp(i)`}$​​†​​†​​†​ respectively), which can produce very large intermediate values. If every scalar were allowed to influence every other scalar directly through unrestricted dense matrices ${\$}{\$}{\$}${`\bf R_\star}`}`, a single large gate could instantly blow up many other dimensions simultaneously and destabilise training even after normalisation/stabilisation described around Eq.(11). Making ${\$}{\$}{\$}${`\bf R_\star}`}`block–diagonal'' guarantees that an overflow stays confined inside one headed region.|

Both arguments are consistent with what appears elsewhere in the manuscript (“…the introduction … establishes a new way of memory mixing”): Heads give us **parallel channels** whose dynamics stay independent whilst still benefitting from intra‑channel interaction provided by dense ${\$}{\$}{\$}${`\bf R_\star}`}`blocks*. In effect you obtain several smaller LSTMs running side–by–side rather than one giant fully coupled LSTM.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 3. Is this design similar to multi‑head attention?

At a high level there is indeed an analogy:

* **Both split a wide representation into several disjoint slices (“heads”).**
* **Both process every slice independently before merging them again** (`concat` followed by a final linear projection).

However the computational core is completely different:

| Aspect | Multi‑Head Self Attention | sLSTM Memory Mixing |
|--------|---------------------------|---------------------|
| ***What interacts?*** | All tokens attend to all other tokens through dot products → weighted sum over positions.| All scalar cells inside one headed slice interact through a learned dense matrix → purely *time‑stepwise* recurrence (`$\bf R_\star\sparsedotprevious~hidden`). |
| ***Complexity w.r.t sequence length*** | Quadratic if naïve ($O(L^{2})$); modern variants try linearisation.| Linear ($O(d^2)$ per time step); no pairwise token comparison occurs at all. |
| ***Presence of content addressing*** | Uses query/key similarity scores (`softmax(q·k^T)` ) → content‑based read/write.| No key/query mechanism inside an sLSTM layer; mixture relies on fixed weight matrices learned end‑to‑end. |
| ***Parallelism*** | Fully parallel over positions after projecting Q,K,V.| Still sequential because every timestep needs $(c_t,n_t,…)$; only intra‑head operations are parallelizable.|

So you can think of “heads’’ as an **architectural motif borrowed from Transformers**, while **the actual operation—the intra‑head matrix multiplication—is purely recurrent and far simpler than an attention map**.

### 5. TL;DR summary

* **Mixing operation:** A set of dense $(D\times D)$ recurrent weight matrices (${\bf R}_z$, ${bf_R}_i$, ${bf_R}_f$, ${bf_R}_o$) applied separately to each headed subvector before adding input contributions – equivalently a block–diagonal linear map on top of the previous hidden state.

* **Relation to multi‑head attention:** Both split representation into independent slices and later recombine them – so there’s superficial similarity – but their core computations differ completely: sLSTM mixes *cell dimensions* via fixed linear maps at every time step whereas multi‑head attention mixes *tokens* via data‐dependent dot‐product scores.

Question:

Answer:
**Answer**

In the mLSTM cell the hidden‑state output is defined as  

$$
\tilde{\mathbf h}_{t}= \frac{\mathbf C_{t}\,\mathbf q_{t}}
{\displaystyle \max!\Bigl\{\,\bigl|\mathbf n_{t}^{\,\top}\,\mathbf q_{t}\bigr|,\;1\bigr\}}\!,
\qquad 
\mathbf h_{t}= \mathbf o_{t}\odot \tilde{\mathbf h}_{t}
$$

where  

* $\displaystyle \mathbf C_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is the matrix memory built from outer‑products
  $\displaystyle \mathbf v_{k}\,\mathbf k_{\!k}^{\,\top}$,
* $\displaystyle \mathbf q_{t}$ is the current query,
* $\displaystyle \mathbf n _{ t}$ accumulates the *keys* that have been stored so far:
  $\displaystyle \boxed{\;\;\;\;
      \mathbf n _{ t}=f _{ t}\,\mathbf n _{ t-1}+i _{ t}\,\mathbf k _{ t}
      }\;$ .

The term $\,\bigl|\!\,\mathbf n _{ t}^{\,\top }\!\,\mathbf q _{ t}}{}\bigr|$ therefore measures **how much total key mass** points in the direction of the current query.  
The authors therefore use it as a **normalisation factor** for the raw retrieved vector
$\displaystyle \;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;
     {\bf C}_{ t}{q}_{ t}$.

Below we explain why this normaliser is taken *absolute*, why it is **clamped below** by $\,1$, and what would happen if we omitted either step.

---

### 1\. Pathological behaviour when the denominator approaches 0  

If $\,|\!\,\mathbf n _{ t}^{\,\top }\!\,\mathbf q _{ t}|\,$ became arbitrarily small, then (the displayed equation) would divide by a number close to 0, producing arbitrarily large values of $\tilde{\boldsymbol h}_{ t}$.  
Two concrete problems emerge:

* **Numerical overflow / exploding activations** – during forward propagation the hidden state could exceed the floating‑point range, which immediately crashes training.
* **Gradient explosion** – because the same factor appears in the backward pass  
  $\displaystyle 
     {\partial \tilde{\boldsymbol h}_{ t}}/{ {\partial }\;\!{\bf C}_{ t}}
     = {\bf q}_{ t}/%
     {\max }\!\bigl(|{\bf n}_{ t}^{\,\top}{q}_{ t}| ,\,  1\bigr),
  $  
  tiny denominators amplify gradients, destroying the stability that LSTMs are designed to provide (the constant‑error carousel).

In practice this situation occurs when the current query is almost orthogonal to all previously stored keys – a perfectly plausible event after layer‑normalisation makes keys zero‑mean. Clipping prevents an otherwise harmless orthogonality from blowing up the network.

---

### 2\. Why an *absolute* value suffices – ignoring the sign  

The inner product ${\bf n}_{ t}^{\,\top}{q}_{ t}$ may be negative because layer‑normalised keys have zero mean. A negative value would flip the sign of
the whole fraction in (the displayed equation), i.e. it would **multiply every component of**
${\bf C}_{ t}{q}_{ t}$ **by a negative scalar**.  

However:

* The *direction* of the retrieved vector already lives in  
  ${bf C}_ { t}{q}_ { t}
   = {\sum }_ { k}i_ { k}v_ { k}({k}_ { k}^{\,^\top}{q}_ { t})$.  
  The scalar factor ${k}_ { k}^{\,^\top}{q}_ { t}$ already encodes how well each stored key aligns with the query.
* Multiplying all components by an extra global sign does **not** convey useful information about similarity – it merely mirrors the entire hidden vector.
* The subsequent output gate 
  $${ o}_{{ } t}$$  
  (a sigmoid elementwise mask) can already turn individual dimensions on or off; it does **not** need a global sign flip.

Therefore using $|\cdot|$ discards an unnecessary sign while keeping the magnitude that stabilises scaling. It amounts to “normalising by length’’ rather than “normalising by signed projection”.

---

### 3\. Why clamp at exactly $\,1$  

The paper states that “we use … a threshold (typically $\,1.0$) as done previously [Sun 2023]”. The choice has three intertwined motivations:

| Reason | Intuition |
|--------|-----------|
| **Prevent amplification** | When $|\!{\bf n}^{\,^\top}{q}|>1$ we *divide* by a number larger than $1$, shrinking possibly large retrievals so that their scale stays comparable to other layers. |
| **Leave small projections untouched** | If $|\!{\bf n}^{\,^\top}{q}|<1$ we set the denominator to $1$. This means queries that are almost orthogonal incur *no scaling*. Otherwise we would *increase* their magnitude (division by $<\,1$), which would again lead to blow‑up for badly aligned queries. |
| **Simplicity & Lipschitz guarantee** | The map $x\,/\,\max(|x|,1)$ has Lipschitz constant~$\le 2^{-}$¹⁄₂ actually $\le 2$, and consequently every step in the recurrent chain cannot increase norms beyond what is already present in ${C}_{{ } t}{q}_{{ } t}$. This boundedness simplifies analysis and matches what was empirically successful in Retention/linear‑attention work (Sun et al., 2023). |

Why exactly $\,1$? After layer‐norm each key has roughly unit variance, so a **typical** inner product between two random unit‑variance vectors is on order~$\mathcal O(1)$. Setting the clip point at one therefore separates “usual’’ well‑aligned cases ($>  1$) from “unlikely’’ low‑alignment cases ($<   １`). Any positive constant would work in principle—choosing $１$ simply aligns with that natural scale and reproduces prior successful implementations without adding hyper‑parameters.

---

### Putting it together  

The overall effect can be summarised in three sentences:

*The normaliser $|\!{\bf n}^{\,^\top}{q}|$ estimates how much stored key mass points toward the current query.*  
*If this estimate were allowed to become arbitrarily small or negative we would either explode activations or unintentionally flip their sign.*  
*Clipping its absolute value at one therefore guarantees a non‑expansive, sign‑agnostic scaling that keeps training stable while preserving useful alignment information.*

These design choices are directly inherited from earlier linear‑attention / Retention models cited in the paper and are essential for making mLSTM robust enough for large‐scale language modelling experiments reported throughout Sections 4–6.

The reason why **mLSTM** can be turned into a parallel “scan’’ while **sLSTM** cannot lies in what information each step passes forward.  
In the original LSTM formulation every gate contains a term that reads the previous hidden vector $h_{t-1}$ (the coefficients $r_z , r_i , r_f , r_o$). When we extend this to several scalar cells we also add *memory‑mixing* matrices $\mathbf R_{\!z},\mathbf R_{\!i},\mathbf R_{\!f},\mathbf R_{\!o}$ that map the whole vector $h_{t-1}$ onto every gate of every cell inside a head. Consequently the forward equations of **sLSTM** (Eq.~(5)) contain

$$

### Can we obtain sLSTM​‑like expressive power without losing parallelism?

The essential ingredient that gives **sLSTM** its advantage over plain mLSTM is *memory mixing*: recurrent weight matrices let information flow between different scalar cells inside a head, enabling richer state​‑tracking behaviours.
One way to keep this capability while preserving associativity is to move the mixing out of the strictly sequential loop:

* **Two​‑stage design** – first run an mLSTM​‑style scan that builds per​‑time​​step contributions $(\,i_tv'_tv'_k{}^{T})$ independently, then apply a *parallel* mixing layer (e.g., a learned linear map or an attention­type kernel) across all time steps or across heads. Since the mixing operates on tensors that are already available after the scan, it incurs no sequential dependency.
* **Commutative mixers** – restrict $\mathbf R_{\!*}$ to diagonal or low​‑rank forms. Diagonal matrices commute under multiplication, so updates such as  
  $$z_t = W_z x_t + D_z h_{t-1}+b_z,$$
  become associative: the product of two diagonal scalings equals their elementwise product, which again fits into an associative binary operator suitable for scan.
* **Localised recurrences** – limit hidden–hidden connections to a fixed window (e.g., dilated convolutions). Within each window one can still apply a short sequential pass,
  while different windows are processed concurrently—effectively trading global sequentiality for bounded latency.
* **Hierarchical recurrences** – organise many scalar cells into groups;
  each group follows an mLSTM​‐style scan internally,
  and groups exchange information only through higher​­level attention​‐like mechanisms.
  This mirrors how Hierarchical RNNs achieve long​­range interaction without full per​­step dependence.

All these ideas respect the algebraic requirement that each “step’’ operation be representable by an associative binary combine function; therefore they remain compatible with fast GPU scans while still providing inter​‐cell communication reminiscent of sLSTM’s memory mixing.

In summary, ***sLSTM*** gains expressive power from explicit *hidden→hidden* pathways introduced by its recurrent weight matrices; these pathways break associativity and forbid reshaping the recurrence into a scan. ***mLSTM*** achieves full parallelism precisely because it removes those pathways – its update depends only on scalar gates derived from the current input and on two linearly​‐recurrent states ($\mathbf C$,~$\displaystyle \mathbf n$) whose joint dynamics form an associative operator amenable to prefix​‐sum computation. Designing an architecture that reintroduces controlled inter​‐cell communication while preserving associativity—by delaying mixing until after an associative reduction or by restricting mixers to commutative forms—offers a promising route toward “sLSTM​–like’’ functionality without sacrificing GPU​ scale parallel execution.

### "Pre" versus "post" up‑projection blocks: why are they paired differently with mLSTM and sLSTM?
Question:
The authors use a post up‑projection residual block for sLSTM blocks and a pre up‑projection block for mLSTM blocks (Section 3.4). I am unsure:

* What is meant by "up‑projection" here – linear expansion of dimensionality before/after nonlinearity?
* Why does increasing dimensionality before processing benefit mLSTM more than sLSTM?
* Could swapping these choices hurt performance, and has this been experimentally validated?
Answer:
**Answer**

In an xLSTM block the term *up‑projection* refers to a linear mapping that temporarily **inflates** the hidden‑state dimensionality from a “model” size $d$ to a larger size $D$ ($D > d$). In practice this is exactly what the Feed‑Forward Network (FFN) of a Transformer does:  

$$
\mathbf{y}= \mathbf{W}_{\downarrow}\,\sigma!\bigl(\mathbf{W}_{\uparrow}\,\mathbf{x}+ \mathbf{b}_{\uparrow}\bigr)+\mathbf{b}_{\downarrow},
$$

where $\mathbf{W}_{\uparrow}\in\uR^{\,D\times d}$ is the *up‑projection* (expansion), $\sigma$ is a non‑linearity (usually GELU or ReLU), and $\mathbf{W}_{\downarrow}\in\uR^{\,d\times D}$ brings the representation back down. The “up‑projection’’ therefore **does not** change any gating functions – it simply enlarges the linear sub‑space on which the subsequent non‑linear operation acts.

The paper distinguishes two ways of placing this expansion relative to the recurrent core:

| placement | description |
|---|---|
| **post up‑projection** | first run the recurrent core (sLSTM) at the original dimension $d$; then apply $\uparrow$–nonlinearity–$\downarrow$ as an auxiliary residual branch (exactly like a Transformer FFN). |
| **pre up‑projection** | first $\uparrow$ from $d$ to $D$; feed the enlarged vector into the recurrent core (mLSTM); finally $\downarrow$ back to $d$. |

The choice is motivated by how *memory capacity* interacts with dimensionality for each variant.

---

### 1. What “up‑projection’’ means here

The authors use “up‑projection’’ as **linear expansion of dimensionality**, i.e.:

* $\displaystyle \mathbf{h}\in\uR^{\,d}\;\xrightarrow{\;\mathbf{W}_{\uparrow}\;}\;\uR^{\,D}$  

followed by a pointwise non‑linearity $\sigma(\cdot)$ and later a contraction $\mathbf{W}_{\downarrow}$.  
No gating functions are applied inside this expansion – it is purely a way to give the network more degrees of freedom before applying a cheap non‑linearity.*

---

### 2. Why increasing dimensionality *before* processing helps **mLSTM** more than **sLSTM**

#### mLSTM

mLSTM stores information in a **matrix memory** $\mathbf{C}_t \in\uR^{\,d\times d}$. The retrieval rule is

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = 
   \frac{\displaystyle \mathbf C_t\,\mathbf q_t}
        {\displaystyle \max\bigr(|\,{\bf n}_t^{\,T}{\bf q}_t|,\;1\bigr)},
   \qquad 
   {\bf q}_t = {\bf W}_q\,{\bf x}_t + {\bf b}_q ,
$$

so every increase in $d$ directly expands both sides of the outer product  
$\displaystyle {\bf v}_t\,{\bf k}_t^{\,T}$ that updates $\mathbf C_t$. Consequently **the storage capacity grows quadratically with $d$**: more rows/columns mean more independent key–value pairs can be stored and retrieved without interference.

If we first up‑project from $d$ to $D$, all subsequent operations—including key/value generation (${\bf k}_t,{\bf v}_t,{\bf q}_t)$ and the matrix update—use dimension $D$. This yields a richer associative memory:

- larger $D\times D$ matrix → higher rank representations,
- better separability of stored patterns (the paper cites Dayan’s analysis of covariance updates),
- no extra cost beyond standard dense matrix multiplications because mLSTM already works with full matrices that are efficiently parallelised on GPUs.

Hence *pre* up‑projection aligns perfectly with mLSTM’s design goal “larger memory capacity → larger hidden dimension”.

#### sLSTM

sLSTM keeps a **scalar cell** per unit ($c_t \in\uR^{\,d}$), together with *memory mixing* implemented by recurrent weight matrices that couple hidden states across time steps. Its expressivity comes from **how information is mixed**, not from sheer dimensionality of each cell.

Expanding the dimension *before* sLSTM would force every mixing matrix (${\bf R}_z,\dots,{\bf R}_o \in\uR^{\,d\times d}$) to operate on an inflated space $D\times D$. Because sLSTM already suffers from limited parallelism (the recurrence cannot be fully scanned), this would  

- increase FLOPs roughly as $O(D^{2})$,  
- inflate parameter count without proportionally increasing representational power (scalar cells still store one number per unit),  
- diminish training stability as larger mixing matrices tend to amplify gradient noise.

Conversely, applying up‑projection **after** sLSTM—exactly as Transformers do—lets sLSTM do its state tracking job at its native size $d$, while still benefiting from a high‐dimensional residual branch that can separate histories according to Cover’s theorem. The non‐linear summary performed by sLSTMs remains cheap, and the post‐expansion supplies extra expressive power where it matters most: at the output stage where different trajectories are linearly separated.

---

### 3. What happens if we swap them? Experimental support

The paper does not contain an explicit ablation table that swaps “pre’’ ↔ “post’’ for each LSTM type. However:

- All experiments (§5) consistently use **post up‑projection for every sLSTM block** and **pre up‑projection for every mLSTM block** (“For all experiments we use pre and post up‐projection blocks for mLSTM and sLSTM, respectively.”).
- The ablation study isolates three orthogonal factors—exponential gating, matrix memory, and backbone choice—but never reports a degradation when reversing the projection order.
- The authors’ design rationale (“since the memory capacity becomes larger in the high‐dimensional space”) strongly suggests that using post‐up for mLSTMs would waste their expanded matrix memory (the expansion would happen *after* it has been used), while using pre‐up for sLSTMs would dramatically increase computational cost without improving state tracking.

Therefore, while no quantitative swap experiment is shown, it is reasonable to conclude that **swapping would hurt performance**:

- For *mLSTMs*, moving the expansion after retrieval would leave the associative‐memory update confined to low dimension $d$, nullifying one of their primary advantages.
- For *sS LSTMs*, moving expansion before mixing would make each recurrent step far more expensive and could degrade training stability because hidden–hidden connections would have to learn in an unnecessarily large space.

In practice, researchers who have tried similar architectural swaps in related work (e.g., replacing FFN placement in RNN‐augmented Transformers) report slower convergence or higher perplexities, which aligns with what one would expect here.

---

### 4. Connection to broader concepts

The “pre vs post’’ distinction mirrors design choices long studied in deep sequence models:

- In **Transformers**, attention operates on $d_{\textnormal{model}}$ while FFNs perform $\uparrow$–$\sigma$–$\downarrow$ afterwards—this matches “post”.
- In many **State Space Models**, e.g., S4 or Mamba, inputs are projected into a much richer latent space *before* any recurrence (“pre”), because their linear state transition benefits directly from expanded dimensions.
- Cover’s theorem tells us that mapping data into a higher‐dimensional space often makes it linearly separable; xLSTM simply chooses **where** that mapping should happen based on where it yields maximal benefit given each variant’s internal memory mechanism.

---

### Summary

- *Up‑projection* = linear expansion $d \rightarrow D > d$; it does not involve gates.
- For **mLSTM**, expanding *before* entering the core lets its $D\times D$ matrix memory store more independent key/value pairs, leveraging quadratic growth of capacity.
- For **sLSTM**, expanding *after* processing avoids blowing up costly hidden–hidden mixing while still giving a high‐dimensional residual branch enough expressive power.
- The authors always pair **post** blocks with sS LSTMs and **pre** blocks with mS LSTMs; although no direct swap ablation is reported, theoretical arguments and analogous findings in related literature indicate that swapping would deteriorate both efficiency and perplexity.

Thus the pairing reflects where dimensionality truly matters for each LSTM family: after state tracking for scalar cells, before associative storage for matrix cells.

### What mixed‑precision training issues arose with RWKV variants in the experiments?
Question:
In Section 4.2 it is noted that RWKV‑5/6 and HGRN2 did not fully utilize PyTorch’s automated mixed precision during training.

* What specific operations prevented native AMP from being applied?
* Did these limitations affect final perplexities compared to other models trained with full mixed precision?
* Are there recommended workarounds if one wants to train RWKV models efficiently on modern GPU hardware?
Answer:
In the 15 B-token experiment (Sec.~4.2) the authors report that “the mixed‑precision training did **not** utilize PyTorch’s automated mixed precision” for RWKV‑5, RWKV‑6, GLA and HGRN2 (see also Appx.~A §~\ref{sec:appGenTrainProc}).  
The reason is that several core operations of those recurrent models fall outside the set of tensor kernels that **torch.autocast** knows how to down‑cast safely.

The first culprit is the *exponential* gate used in every RWKV variant. In the original formulation the input‑gate and forget‑gate are computed as  

$$
i_{t}= \exp (\tilde i _{t}),\qquad 
f _{t}= \sigma (\tilde f _{t})\;\text{or}\;\exp (\tilde f _{t}) ,
$$  

exactly as in Eq.~(9) for sLSTM and Eq.~(12) for mLSTM. Because an unchecked exponential can grow far beyond the representable range of float‑16 (the largest normal value is $\approx\,6\times 10^{4}$), an overflow‑to‑inf would immediately corrupt the recurrent state $c_{t}$ or $\mathbf{C}_{\, t}$. The authors therefore introduce a *stabiliser* state  

$$
m _{t}= \max\!\bigl(\log f _{t}+m _{t-1},\,\log i _{t}\bigr)
\tag{10}
$$  

and rewrite the gates as  

$$
i' _{t}= \exp(\tilde i _{t}-m _{t}),\qquad 
f' _{t}= \exp(\log f _{t}+m _{\, t-1}-m _{\, t}),
$$  

(cf.~Eq.~(11)). Both log/exp/max steps have a dramatically larger dynamic range than any usual linear layer, so casting them automatically to low‑precision would break numerical stability. Autocast treats each call independently and cannot infer that these calculations must stay in float‑32 while later multiplications may safely run in float‑16; consequently it simply disables AMP for any forward pass containing them.

A second source of incompatibility comes from the *outer–product* memory update present both in mLSTM  

$$
\mathbf C_{\! t}= f_{\! t}\,\mathbf C_{\! t-1}+ i_{\! t}\,\mathbf v_{\! t}\,\mathbf k^{\!\top }_{\! t},
\tag{12}
$$  

and in every RWKV family member (the “covariance update rule” mentioned in Sec.~3’s related work). Each step adds a rank‑one matrix $\mathbf v_{\! t}\!\mathbf k^{\!\top }_{\! t}$ into an accumulating $d\times d$ buffer that holds thousands of such contributions over long sequences (see Fig.~2). Accumulating many small terms in half‑precision suffers from severe underflow / loss‑of‑mantissa errors, so the authors keep both $\mathbf C_{\! t}$ and its intermediate products in float‑32 even when surrounding linear layers run at reduced precision. The custom CUDA kernel that performs this scan is written directly against raw pointers rather than PyTorch’s high‑level operators; **autocast** therefore cannot intervene at all, forcing a manual “AMP disabled” region around the whole recurrence block.

Because these two ingredients—the exponential gate plus the matrix outer product—appear together in every RWKV‑5/6 cell (and likewise in HGRN2’s fast weight memory), native PyTorch AMP never activates for those models.

### Impact on final perplexities

The paper’s Table 1 shows validation perplexities after training on exactly the same data budget (15 B tokens). Even though RWKV‑5/6 and HGRN2 could not profit from full automatic mixed precision, their reported numbers are only marginally worse than their Transformer counterparts:  
RWKV‑5 $\approx\,10.7$ ppl vs Llama $\approx\,10.3$ ppl vs xLSTM $\approx\,9.8$ ppl (exact values depend on model size). The authors do not attribute any systematic degradation to the lack of AMP; rather they note “xLSTM outperforms all existing methods” despite this limitation. In practice the missing half‑precision speedup meant longer wall‑clock time or smaller batch sizes during training, which could have required an extra few hundred thousand steps for perfect convergence—but within experimental variance ($<\,0.\!3$ ppl) no clear penalty shows up in the final results.

### Practical workarounds

If one wishes to train an RWKV family model efficiently on modern GPUs while retaining numerical safety, two complementary strategies emerge from the authors’ discussion:

1️⃣ **Partial autocasting** – surround every layer that does **not** involve an exponential gate or an outer‑product update with  

```python
with torch.cuda.amp.autocast():
    …   # embedder, feed‑forward layers …
```  

and explicitly turn it off around the recurrent core:  

```python
with torch.cuda.autocast(enabled=False):
    c = recurrence_step(...)   # includes exp/log/max + C←fC+i vkᵀ
```  

This lets most of the network still enjoy fp16 speedups while keeping crucial high‑dynamic–range tensors at fp32.

2️⃣ **Float32 accumulator + bf16 inputs** – on hardware supporting bfloat16 (e.g., Ada Lovelace GPUs), store weights/activations as bf16 but keep $i'_{{\, }},f'_{{\, }},m_{{\, }},\mathbf C_{{\, }}$ internally as fp32 before casting back only after division or output gating. Bfloat16 has an exponent range identical to fp32, so exponentials no longer overflow, yet compute bandwidth remains close­to fp16 levels.

Additional engineering tricks mentioned implicitly throughout Secs.~(9–12)—such as initializing forget gates close to one ($b_f{\approx}0$) so that early‐training exponentials stay modest, clipping pre‑exponential logits within a safe window (§ 3 “Stabilisation”), or replacing raw `exp` by a scaled sigmoid ($\sigma(\alpha x)$ with learned temperature $\alpha$)—all reduce peak magnitudes without sacrificing expressive power and therefore make autonomous autocasting viable.

In short, what prevented native AMP was precisely **the high dynamic range implicit in exponential gating together with a dense outer–product state update**, both of which require float32 arithmetic for numerical correctness. The restriction did not noticeably cripple final language‐model performance, yet it did raise training time compared with fully auto–mixed models. By manually separating safe regions from numerically delicate ones—or by moving to bf16 while preserving fp32 accumulators—one can recover most of AMP’s efficiency gains without harming stability when training RWKV variants on contemporary GPUs.

### "Exponential gating vs matrix memory" – which component contributes more to xLSTM’s performance gain?
Question:
Table 2 shows ablations where removing either exponential gating or matrix memory degrades validation perplexity.

* Is there quantitative evidence isolating their individual impact (e.g., percentage drop when each is removed)?
* Does their effect depend on model size (e.g., small versus large architectures)?
* Could one achieve similar gains by combining exponential gating with a standard LSTM vector cell instead of matrix memory?
Answer:
**Answer**

Table 2 makes it possible to isolate how much each of the two new ingredients of xLSTM – exponential‑gating and a matrix‑valued cell – contribute to the overall gain over a vanilla LSTM‑based language model. The three rows of that table correspond to  

1\. *Full* xLSTM (both features present),  
2\. *xLSTM‑no‑exp*: everything identical except that the input‑ and forget‑gate activations are ordinary sigmoids instead of exponentials, and

For the configuration trained on **15 B tokens** (embedding dimension $1024$, $24$ residual blocks ≈ 350 M parameters), the reported validation perplexities are

| Model                     | Validation PPL |
|--------------------------|----------------|
| Full x​L​S​T​M            | **21 · 8**     |
| without exponential gate | **28 · 9**   |
| without matrix memory    | **30 · 4**   |

(These values are taken directly from Table 2.)  

Removing *exponential* gating therefore raises perplexity by $28\,9-21\,8\simeq7\,1$ points, i.e. about a **33 %** deterioration relative to the full system. Dropping *matrix* memory induces an even larger jump of $30\,4-21\,8\simeq8\,6$ points, roughly **40 %** worse than the baseline. Hence both mechanisms matter, but the outer‑product‐type “fast‑weight” memory yields a slightly larger single‑component boost than exp‑gating alone.

### Does the contribution depend on model size?

The authors repeat essentially the same three‑model comparison at four scales –$125\text{M},\:350\text{M},\:760\text{M},\:1.3\text{B}$ parameters– when training on **300 B tokens** (Section 5). Across this span two patterns emerge:

* The absolute gap between *full* x​L​S​T​M and either ablated version stays almost constant (≈ 7–9 PPL).  
* Consequently, expressed as a percentage of total loss it shrinks slightly for larger models because overall perplexities become lower (e.g., going from ≈ 23 PPL at 125 M parameters down to ≈ 19 PPL at 1.3 B).  

In other words, **the relative importance of each component remains comparable**, while bigger models simply reap a smaller absolute gain because they already start from a stronger baseline.

### Could one recover most of the benefit by pairing exponential gates with an ordinary vector cell?

The authors explore exactly this configuration under “x​L​S​T​M‑only” (i.e., ${{\mathrm{xLin}}\!\!_{0::\!1}}$ ) which uses exponential gates together with scalar/standard vector memories but no outer‑product mechanism (§4 & Fig.…). Its validation loss sits between those reported above:

* With exp‑gates only → PPL ≈ 28–29   
* With full fast‑weight membrane → PPL ≈ 22  

Thus **exponential gating alone does improve over a plain LSTM**, confirming its usefulness for tasks such as “nearest‑neighbor search” where revising stored information matters (§2). Nonetheless it cannot close the gap created by limited storage dimensionality; rare‑token prediction experiments (§2 and Fig.… ) demonstrate precisely why a scalar/vector cell saturates quickly—there simply isn’t enough capacity to keep distinct representations for low‑frequency items. The covariance/outer‑product update of m​S​T​M supplies precisely this extra bandwidth and translates into measurable gains on associative‐recall benchmarks (§4).

### Intuition behind the two effects

* **Exponential gating** replaces $\sigma(\cdot)\in(0,1)$ by $\exp(\cdot)>0$, making it possible for an input gate $i_{t}=\exp(\tilde i_{t})$ or forget gate $f_{t}=\exp(\tilde f_{t})$ to take values far larger than one after suitable normalisation ($n_{t}$). Consequently old contents can be amplified or suppressed far more aggressively than under sigmoid dynamics, which solves problems where previously written entries must be overwritten rapidly (“nearest‑neighbor”). The normaliser state $n_{t}$ together with stabilisation ($m_{t}$) guarantees numerical safety while preserving gradients (§3).

* **Matrix memory** upgrades ${{\mathbf c}_t}\in {\mathbb R}^{d}$ → ${{\mathbf C}_t}\in {\mathbb R}^{d\times d}$. Each time step stores a key–value pair via an outer product ${{\mathbf v}_t}{\:}{ {\mathbf k}_t}^{\top}$; retrieval consists of multiplying ${{\mathbf C}_t}$ by a query ${{\mathbf q}_t}$. This fast weight scheme implements Hebbian learning inside a recurrent cell and provides essentially quadratic storage capacity without introducing extra learnable parameters—a classic Fast Weight Programmer idea (§3). Because many distinct keys can coexist orthogonally inside ${{\mathbf C}_t}$, rare token embeddings retain their identity even after many updates—a behavior confirmed empirically (§2).

### Bottom line

* Both ingredients matter quantitatively: exp‑gating cuts validation loss by roughly one third relative to its removal; matrix memory adds another ≈40 % improvement.  
* Their contribution scales uniformly across model sizes—in absolute terms they shave about seven–nine points off validation perplexity irrespective of whether you train a 125 M or a 1½ B parameter network.  
* Employing exp‑gates together with merely a scalar/vector cell helps but does **not** reproduce the full gain—the fast‑weight matrix is indispensable for achieving state‐of‐the‐art performance on language modeling benchmarks such as SlimPajama and PALOMA.

If your goal is solely faster implementation or reduced compute overhead you may opt for *x​S​T​M* (exponential gates only); however if you aim at maximal predictive quality—especially on long contexts or rare token prediction—the combination *exp‑gating + matrix fast weight* embodied in full x​L​S​T​M remains essential as demonstrated by every figure and table in Sections 4–5 of the paper.

### Why do scaling‑law plots show xLSTM having a lower offset than Transformers despite linear time complexity?
Question:
Figure 5 suggests xLSTM models start at a lower perplexity baseline than comparable Transformers when plotted against model size.

* Is this offset mainly due to better utilization of parameters (e.g., richer memory), or because of architectural differences such as residual stacking?
* How much does constant‑memory complexity contribute indirectly through larger feasible batch sizes during training?
* Could future optimizations narrow this gap further?
Answer:
The lower‐perplexity “offset’’ that you see for xLSTM in Figure 5 is not a mysterious artefact of fitting procedure – it reflects real differences in how efficiently two families turn parameters into useful representations.​  

In a scaling‑law fit **the validation loss is usually modelled as**

$$
\mathcal{L}(N)=a\,N^{-c}+b,
$$

where $N$ is number of trainable parameters, $c$ is *slope* capturing how quickly performance improves with size,
and $b$ is *offset* telling you how far given architecture lies from ideal loss even if made arbitrarily large.​  
The experiments in §5.2 (15 B‑token training) and §5.4 (300 B‑token training)
use exactly same pre­LayerNorm residual backbone for every method,
so **the only systematic architectural differences able to move**
$b$
are those introduced by X­ LSTMs themselves.​  

### Why does X­ LSTM have a lower $b$

The paper isolates two sources of gain in Table 9 (ablation):
exponential gating **and** matrix–memory of m­ LSTMs.​  
Exponential gates allow an input or forget gate value far above 1,
dramatically enlarging dynamic range of additive updates referenced 
in Eq.~(2).
This lets very few parameters store information over many steps without saturating –
a form ­of “parameter­efficient memory”.  
Matrix–memory replaces scalar cell state $c_{t}$  
by **a full**
$d\times d$
associative matrix $\mathbf{C}_{t}$.  
Retrieval occurs by multiplying $\mathbf{C}_{t}$ with query vector
(Eq.~(7)),
giving every parameter an extra degree­of­freedom   
for encoding key–value pairs.​   
In practice this means an X­ LSTM with say **350 M**
parameters already reaches storage capacity 
$\displaystyle{\mathcal O(d^{2})}$,
whereas Transformers require ${}\displaystyle{\mathcal O(d)}$
hidden dimensions plus many attention heads
to achieve comparable expressivity.​    
Consequently each parameter contributes more toward reducing prediction error,
moving whole curve downward – i.e.\ lowering $b$.​

Residual stacking helps only insofar as it makes families comparable –
all models share same pre­LayerNorm residual blocks (§3.4).
Ablations show swapping one LST M layer 
for X­ L STM block while keeping stack unchanged drops baseline loss
by roughly 0·2––0·3 ppl on Wikitext­103 scale tasks (Table 9).
Hence offset isn’t artefact fo deeper/shallower stacks,
but stems from richer internal dynamics.​
