\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### "Pre" versus "post" up‑projection blocks: why are they paired differently with mLSTM and sLSTM?
Question:
The authors use a post up‑projection residual block for sLSTM blocks and a pre up‑projection block for mLSTM blocks (Section 3.4). I am unsure:

* What is meant by "up‑projection" here – linear expansion of dimensionality before/after nonlinearity?
* Why does increasing dimensionality before processing benefit mLSTM more than sLSTM?
* Could swapping these choices hurt performance, and has this been experimentally validated?
Answer:
**Answer**

In an xLSTM block the term *up‑projection* refers to a linear mapping that temporarily **inflates** the hidden‑state dimensionality from a “model” size $d$ to a larger size $D$ ($D > d$). In practice this is exactly what the Feed‑Forward Network (FFN) of a Transformer does:  

$$
\mathbf{y}= \mathbf{W}_{\downarrow}\,\sigma!\bigl(\mathbf{W}_{\uparrow}\,\mathbf{x}+ \mathbf{b}_{\uparrow}\bigr)+\mathbf{b}_{\downarrow},
$$

where $\mathbf{W}_{\uparrow}\in\uR^{\,D\times d}$ is the *up‑projection* (expansion), $\sigma$ is a non‑linearity (usually GELU or ReLU), and $\mathbf{W}_{\downarrow}\in\uR^{\,d\times D}$ brings the representation back down. The “up‑projection’’ therefore **does not** change any gating functions – it simply enlarges the linear sub‑space on which the subsequent non‑linear operation acts.

The paper distinguishes two ways of placing this expansion relative to the recurrent core:

| placement | description |
|---|---|
| **post up‑projection** | first run the recurrent core (sLSTM) at the original dimension $d$; then apply $\uparrow$–nonlinearity–$\downarrow$ as an auxiliary residual branch (exactly like a Transformer FFN). |
| **pre up‑projection** | first $\uparrow$ from $d$ to $D$; feed the enlarged vector into the recurrent core (mLSTM); finally $\downarrow$ back to $d$. |

The choice is motivated by how *memory capacity* interacts with dimensionality for each variant.

---

### 1. What “up‑projection’’ means here

The authors use “up‑projection’’ as **linear expansion of dimensionality**, i.e.:

* $\displaystyle \mathbf{h}\in\uR^{\,d}\;\xrightarrow{\;\mathbf{W}_{\uparrow}\;}\;\uR^{\,D}$  

followed by a pointwise non‑linearity $\sigma(\cdot)$ and later a contraction $\mathbf{W}_{\downarrow}$.  
No gating functions are applied inside this expansion – it is purely a way to give the network more degrees of freedom before applying a cheap non‑linearity.*

---

### 2. Why increasing dimensionality *before* processing helps **mLSTM** more than **sLSTM**

#### mLSTM

mLSTM stores information in a **matrix memory** $\mathbf{C}_t \in\uR^{\,d\times d}$. The retrieval rule is

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = 
   \frac{\displaystyle \mathbf C_t\,\mathbf q_t}
        {\displaystyle \max\bigr(|\,{\bf n}_t^{\,T}{\bf q}_t|,\;1\bigr)},
   \qquad 
   {\bf q}_t = {\bf W}_q\,{\bf x}_t + {\bf b}_q ,
$$

so every increase in $d$ directly expands both sides of the outer product  
$\displaystyle {\bf v}_t\,{\bf k}_t^{\,T}$ that updates $\mathbf C_t$. Consequently **the storage capacity grows quadratically with $d$**: more rows/columns mean more independent key–value pairs can be stored and retrieved without interference.

If we first up‑project from $d$ to $D$, all subsequent operations—including key/value generation (${\bf k}_t,{\bf v}_t,{\bf q}_t)$ and the matrix update—use dimension $D$. This yields a richer associative memory:

- larger $D\times D$ matrix → higher rank representations,
- better separability of stored patterns (the paper cites Dayan’s analysis of covariance updates),
- no extra cost beyond standard dense matrix multiplications because mLSTM already works with full matrices that are efficiently parallelised on GPUs.

Hence *pre* up‑projection aligns perfectly with mLSTM’s design goal “larger memory capacity → larger hidden dimension”.

#### sLSTM

sLSTM keeps a **scalar cell** per unit ($c_t \in\uR^{\,d}$), together with *memory mixing* implemented by recurrent weight matrices that couple hidden states across time steps. Its expressivity comes from **how information is mixed**, not from sheer dimensionality of each cell.

Expanding the dimension *before* sLSTM would force every mixing matrix (${\bf R}_z,\dots,{\bf R}_o \in\uR^{\,d\times d}$) to operate on an inflated space $D\times D$. Because sLSTM already suffers from limited parallelism (the recurrence cannot be fully scanned), this would  

- increase FLOPs roughly as $O(D^{2})$,  
- inflate parameter count without proportionally increasing representational power (scalar cells still store one number per unit),  
- diminish training stability as larger mixing matrices tend to amplify gradient noise.

Conversely, applying up‑projection **after** sLSTM—exactly as Transformers do—lets sLSTM do its state tracking job at its native size $d$, while still benefiting from a high‐dimensional residual branch that can separate histories according to Cover’s theorem. The non‐linear summary performed by sLSTMs remains cheap, and the post‐expansion supplies extra expressive power where it matters most: at the output stage where different trajectories are linearly separated.

---

### 3. What happens if we swap them? Experimental support

The paper does not contain an explicit ablation table that swaps “pre’’ ↔ “post’’ for each LSTM type. However:

- All experiments (§5) consistently use **post up‑projection for every sLSTM block** and **pre up‑projection for every mLSTM block** (“For all experiments we use pre and post up‐projection blocks for mLSTM and sLSTM, respectively.”).
- The ablation study isolates three orthogonal factors—exponential gating, matrix memory, and backbone choice—but never reports a degradation when reversing the projection order.
- The authors’ design rationale (“since the memory capacity becomes larger in the high‐dimensional space”) strongly suggests that using post‐up for mLSTMs would waste their expanded matrix memory (the expansion would happen *after* it has been used), while using pre‐up for sLSTMs would dramatically increase computational cost without improving state tracking.

Therefore, while no quantitative swap experiment is shown, it is reasonable to conclude that **swapping would hurt performance**:

- For *mLSTMs*, moving the expansion after retrieval would leave the associative‐memory update confined to low dimension $d$, nullifying one of their primary advantages.
- For *sS LSTMs*, moving expansion before mixing would make each recurrent step far more expensive and could degrade training stability because hidden–hidden connections would have to learn in an unnecessarily large space.

In practice, researchers who have tried similar architectural swaps in related work (e.g., replacing FFN placement in RNN‐augmented Transformers) report slower convergence or higher perplexities, which aligns with what one would expect here.

---

### 4. Connection to broader concepts

The “pre vs post’’ distinction mirrors design choices long studied in deep sequence models:

- In **Transformers**, attention operates on $d_{\textnormal{model}}$ while FFNs perform $\uparrow$–$\sigma$–$\downarrow$ afterwards—this matches “post”.
- In many **State Space Models**, e.g., S4 or Mamba, inputs are projected into a much richer latent space *before* any recurrence (“pre”), because their linear state transition benefits directly from expanded dimensions.
- Cover’s theorem tells us that mapping data into a higher‐dimensional space often makes it linearly separable; xLSTM simply chooses **where** that mapping should happen based on where it yields maximal benefit given each variant’s internal memory mechanism.

---

### Summary

- *Up‑projection* = linear expansion $d \rightarrow D > d$; it does not involve gates.
- For **mLSTM**, expanding *before* entering the core lets its $D\times D$ matrix memory store more independent key/value pairs, leveraging quadratic growth of capacity.
- For **sLSTM**, expanding *after* processing avoids blowing up costly hidden–hidden mixing while still giving a high‐dimensional residual branch enough expressive power.
- The authors always pair **post** blocks with sS LSTMs and **pre** blocks with mS LSTMs; although no direct swap ablation is reported, theoretical arguments and analogous findings in related literature indicate that swapping would deteriorate both efficiency and perplexity.

Thus the pairing reflects where dimensionality truly matters for each LSTM family: after state tracking for scalar cells, before associative storage for matrix cells.
