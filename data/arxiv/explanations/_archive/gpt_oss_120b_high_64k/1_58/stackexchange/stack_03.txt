\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### What role does the "absmean" scaling factor $\gamma$ play in the weight quantizer?
Question:
The paper defines

$$\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|,$$

and then computes ``RoundClip$(W / (\gamma + \epsilon), -\!\!\!

Why do they divide by *the average absolute value* rather than using other statistics such as ``max(|W|)`` or RMS?

I am also curious about how this choice influences:

* The sparsity pattern after rounding/clipping;
* Training stability across layers;
* The magnitude distribution fed into subsequent layers.
Answer:
In **BitNet b1.58** every learnable matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ is kept in full‑precision during training and only its **ternarised** version  

$$
\widetilde{W}= \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma+\varepsilon},\, -1,\, 1\bigr), 
\qquad 
\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|
$$  

is used for the forward‑pass (the gradient flows through $W$ via a straight‑through estimator).  
The scalar $\gamma$ is therefore a **layer‑wise scale** that maps a “typical’’ magnitude of the raw weights onto the interval $[-1,\, 1]$.  

---

### Why the *average* absolute value?

* **Robustness to outliers** – The maximum $\max|W|$ is dictated by a single extreme entry. If one weight grows unusually large (which happens frequently during early training), $\gamma$ would become too big and almost every other entry would be divided down below $0.5$; after rounding almost all elements would turn into $0$. This makes the sparsity pattern erratic and destabilises learning.  

* **Preserving the central mass** – The mean absolute value $\displaystyle \frac{1}{nm}\sum|W_{ij}|$ estimates the *typical* size of a weight under heavy‑tailed distributions that appear in deep nets. It ignores sign but captures scale without being dominated by rare large values, unlike an RMS  
$\displaystyle \sqrt{\frac{1}{nm}\sum W_{ij}^{2}}$, which up‑weights large outliers even more strongly.  

* **Simple and cheap** – Computing a sum of absolutes costs exactly the same order as any other statistic used during training and does not require a square‑root operation.  

Consequently $\gamma$ adapts smoothly as training progresses while keeping each layer’s effective gain in a reasonable range.  

---

### Influence on sparsity

After division by $\gamma$, an entry becomes zero iff  

$$
\Bigl|\frac{W_{ij}}{\gamma}\Bigr|<\tfrac12 .
$$  

Thus the **zero‑fraction** equals  

$$
p_0=\Pr(|W_{ij}|<0.5\,\gamma).
$$  

If $W_{ij}$ follows a symmetric distribution centred at zero (e.g., a Gaussian), about one third – two thirds of the elements survive the threshold depending on the exact shape – yielding a *moderate* sparsity that is similar from layer to layer and stable over training epochs. Using $\max|W|$ would make $p_0\simeq 1$ (almost all zeros), while an RMS would push $p_0$ closer to $0.9$; both extremes harm expressivity.  

This controlled density gives BitNet b1.58 its “feature‑filtering’’ capability: some connections are intentionally switched off ($0$), yet enough $\pm 1$ connections remain to transmit information efficiently.  

---

### Training stability across layers

Every linear layer receives inputs multiplied by $\widetilde{W}$. Because  

$$
\widetilde{W}= \gamma\,\operatorname{round}\!\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma}\Bigr),
$$  

the **effective gain** contributed by a layer is approximately $\gamma\,c_{\text q}$, where $c_{\text q}$ is the expected magnitude of $\operatorname{round}(\cdot)$ (roughly $\tfrac23$ for a symmetric distribution). Since $\gamma$ tracks the average size of $W$, this gain stays comparable from one layer to another even though the underlying full‑precision parameters may differ widely in scale during optimisation. In turn  

* gradients do not explode or vanish because the forward‑pass does not introduce sudden amplifications,
* optimisation hyper‑parameters (learning‑rate schedule, Adam β’s…) can be kept identical to those used for FP16 baselines,
* RMSNorm applied later further normalises activations, so any residual scale mismatch is removed before non‑linearities.  

Hence the abs‑mean normalisation acts like a lightweight **weight‑normalisation** that stabilises deep transformers trained with extreme quantisation.  

---

### Magnitude distribution seen by subsequent layers

After ternarisation each matrix behaves as  

$$
x^{(l+1)} = \widetilde{W}^{(l)}x^{(l)}
          = \gamma^{(l)}S^{(l)}x^{(l)},
          \qquad S^{(l)}_{ij}\in\{-1,0,+1\}.
$$  

The scalar $\gamma^{(l)}$ therefore replaces the original spread of weight magnitudes with a single gain term that mirrors their *average* size. Because this term varies only slowly during training, downstream layers receive activations whose variance stays close to what they would have seen with full‑precision weights—up to a constant factor that RMSNorm subsequently removes. In practice this means no extra loss‑scale tuning or learning‑rate warm‑up is required beyond what works for unquantised LLMs.  

---

### Summary

Using the **average absolute value** ($\gamma$) instead of max or RMS provides three practical benefits:

1️⃣ **Balanced sparsity** – It makes roughly half of the entries survive rounding, avoiding pathological densification or over‑pruning.  
2️⃣ **Robust layer‑wise scaling** – It captures typical weight magnitude while being insensitive to occasional outliers, keeping forward gains stable across depth and training steps.  
3️⃣ **Preserved activation statistics** – By normalising each matrix onto a common scale before ternarisation, activations entering later layers retain magnitudes comparable to those in full‑precision models, which together with RMSNorm guarantees stable optimisation without special tricks.  

These properties are precisely what allow BitNet b1\.58 to train from scratch at “only’’ $\pm 13/0~·~\gamma$ precision while matching FP16 performance at modest memory and compute cost.​
