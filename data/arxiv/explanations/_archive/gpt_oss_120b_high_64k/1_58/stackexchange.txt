\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why is a model with ternary ($-1$, $0$, $+1$) weights called a "1-bit" LLM?
Question:
The paper introduces *BitNet b1.58* where every parameter can take three discrete values ($-1$, $0$, $+1$). Yet throughout the text they refer to it as a "1-bit" LLM and claim an effective precision of roughly 1.58 bits per weight.

I was under the impression that one binary bit can only encode two states. How do the authors justify calling this architecture "1-bit"? Where does the number 1.58 come from mathematically, and does it reflect actual storage format or just an information‐theoretic average?
Answer:
**Answer**

The word *bit* in “*‑bit LLM*’’ does not refer strictly to how many physical binary cells are used to hold each weight.  
It is used as a shorthand for *how many bits of information* are required, on average, to describe the set of possible weight values after quantisation.  

---

### From two states to three states  

If a parameter can take only two equiprobable values (for example `+γ` or `–γ`) there are exactly  

$$
N=2\modelspace{}\Longrightarrow{}\log_{2}N=\log_{2}2=1 
$$  

binary decisions required. In other words each such weight carries **one bit** of information – this is what earlier *binary* networks such as BitNet called *“1‑bit”* because their kernels need only a sign test instead of a multiplication.  

BitNet (the model introduced in your paper) adds **zero** as a third possible value:

$$
W_{ij}\in\{-1,\;0,\;+1\}.
$$  

Now there are three equiprobable alternatives ($N=3$). By Shannon’s source–coding theorem an optimally encoded symbol drawn from three equally likely symbols needs  

$$
\boxed{\;\log_{2}{3}\approx1.58496;}
$$  

bits on average. The authors round this number to **≈ $1.58$** and therefore refer to their model as “*BitNet*” where the superscript denotes its *effective* precision.  

If the three symbols are not uniformly distributed (say $p_{+},p_{-},p_{0}$), the true entropy would be  

$$
H(W)= -\,\sum_{k\:in\:\\{- ,0,+\\}}p_k\,\log_{2}p_k,
$$  

which would be $\leqslant\,\log_{2}{3}$. In practice trained ternary models tend to have many zeros, so their *actual* entropy may be slightly smaller than $\log_{2}{3}$; however reporting $\approx1.58$ provides a convenient upper bound that matches measured model file sizes after modest compression.

---

### Why keep the label “‑bit’’?

The name **“BitNet’’** was coined when all weights were strictly binary (`±γ`).  
That design removed every floating‑point multiplication from matrix multiplications – they became plain integer additions plus occasional sign flips – which gives an *energy* advantage reminiscent of operating on literal **single‑bit** data streams.

Adding a third state (`0`) does **not** reintroduce multiplications:  
a zero simply contributes nothing in an inner product, while `±` still only require a sign‑test and an addition/subtraction step. Consequently the computational kernel is still *one‑sign‑bit* per non‑zero entry plus an optional zero mask. The authors therefore keep “$k$‑bit’’ in front of their family name while acknowledging that the *information content* per parameter has risen from exactly one bit to roughly $\log_{2}{3}$ bits.

---

### What does “≈ $1.58$ bits per weight’’ mean in practice?

If you were forced to store every ternary entry without any compression you would need at least **two raw bits** (e.g., one sign bit and one presence/mask bit). Real implementations typically pack eight signs into one byte and eight masks into another byte, yielding $2\times N/8=N/4$ bytes = **2 bits / weight** before any compression overhead such as padding or alignment.

When you then apply ordinary lossless compressors (run‑length coding for long stretches of zeros, Huffman coding across signs/masks, etc.) you can bring the *average* size down close to its entropy bound:

$$
{\rm file~size}
   \;\approx{}\;
   \frac{H(W)}{8}\times N
   =    \frac{\log_{2}{3}}{8}\times N
   \approx {\rm 0.197 }N~{\rm bytes},
$$

which corresponds precisely to **~$1.58\,{\rm bits}/{\rm param}$**—the figure quoted in the paper under “effective precision”.  
Thus the reported number reflects an *information‑theoretic average* rather than a literal hardware word width; it tells us how much memory would be needed if we could encode the ternary tensor perfectly efficiently.

---

### Summary

* A binary ($±$) quantisation needs exactly one bit ($\log_{2}{2}=1$).  
* Adding a third value (`0`) expands the alphabet to three symbols, whose optimal encoding requires $\displaystyle\rule[-4pt]{0pt}{14pt}\log_{2}{3}\simeq1.58$ bits per symbol on average—this is where **the number $1.58$** comes from.  
* The term **“‑bit LLM’’** survives from the original BitNet family because its computational core still uses only sign tests and additions; no floating‑point multiplications are required even with the extra zero state.  
* The quoted **$\displaystyle≈\,1.58\,{\rm bits/weight}$** describes an *average storage requirement*, derived from Shannon entropy assuming roughly uniform usage of `$-$`, `$0$, `$+$`. Actual memory layouts may allocate up to two raw bits per weight before compression, but after standard packing/compression they approach this information limit, which justifies calling it a *“low‑precision”, near‑one‑bit”* model despite having three discrete values.

### From the algorithm to GPU instructions

A CUDA kernel that implements **BitLinear** typically proceeds like this:

```cpp
// x : int8 activation vector   length N
// w : packed ternary weights   length N   (2‑bits per entry)
// out : int32 accumulator per output row

for (int row=tid.x; row<out_dim; row+=blockDim.x) {
    int32_t sum_pos = 0;
    int32_t sum_neg = 0;
    for (int j=0; j<N; ++j) {
        uint8_t packed = w[row*N/4 + j/4];        // four weights per byte
        uint8_t sub   = (packed >> (2*(j%4))) & 3; // extract two‑bit code

        // convention used in BitNet:
        //   sub==0 → -1 , sub==1 → 0 , sub==2 → +1   (or any equivalent)
        if (sub == /*code for +1*/) {
            sum_pos += static_cast<int>(x[j]);         // integer add
        } else if (sub == /*code for -1*/) {
            sum_neg += static_cast<int>(x[j]);         // integer add
        }
        // sub == code for 0 ⇒ nothing is done
    }
    out[row] = sum_pos - sum_neg;   // final subtraction also uses adder logic
}
```

Key points:

* **Packing** – each weight needs only two bits (`00`, `01`, `10`), so an entire row can be stored compactly in global memory and fetched with one load.
* **Branch‑free masks** – many implementations replace the `if`s above with bitwise masks (`mask_pos = -(sub==+code)` etc.) so that every thread executes the same instruction stream.
* **No multiplier instructions** – all arithmetic operations are `add.s32` or `add.s16` (or their fused‑add equivalents). The subtraction at the end is just an add of a negated value (`sub` → `add` on two’s complement).
* **Accumulation width** – inputs are int8 but accumulators are int32, exactly as done in standard GEMM kernels (`int8 × int8 → int32`). This avoids overflow while keeping each elementary operation cheap.



-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### Why this saves energy / time

On modern GPUs an FP16 multiply‑accumulate (`fma.rn.f16`) costs roughly an order of magnitude more energy than an INT8 add (`add.s8`). Because every weight in a BitLinear layer reduces to either “add” or “subtract”, the inner loop contains only the cheap `add` instructions described above. The reduction in arithmetic energy, together with the four‑fold compression of weights (2 bits instead of 16), explains why the paper reports *“almost no multiplication operations for matrix multiplication”*.



-----------------------------------------------------------------------

### Summary

* A ternary weight $\{-1,0,+1\}$ turns each scalar product into *conditional* adds: `$+·x$` becomes `$acc += x$`, `$-·x$` becomes `$acc -= x$`, and `$0·x$` does nothing.
* In practice this is realised by packing two‑bit codes for the three possible weights, loading them with vector instructions, generating positive/negative masks, and feeding selected activations into integer adders.
* Subtraction is just an addition of the two’s‑complement value, so no dedicated multiplier unit is required.
* The resulting kernel uses only integer additions (`add.s8`, `add.s16`, or `add.s32`) plus simple logic for masking—exactly what the BitLinear layer claims.

Thus a $2\times2$ ternary matrix multiplied by an activation vector needs only three integer adds/subtractions on hardware and no generic multiplications at all. This is why BitNet’s *BitLinear* can claim “floating‑point multiplications eliminated entirely”.

### What role does the "absmean" scaling factor $\gamma$ play in the weight quantizer?
Question:
The paper defines

$$\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|,$$

and then computes ``RoundClip$(W / (\gamma + \epsilon), -\!\!\!

Why do they divide by *the average absolute value* rather than using other statistics such as ``max(|W|)`` or RMS?

I am also curious about how this choice influences:

* The sparsity pattern after rounding/clipping;
* Training stability across layers;
* The magnitude distribution fed into subsequent layers.
Answer:
In **BitNet b1.58** every learnable matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ is kept in full‑precision during training and only its **ternarised** version  

$$
\widetilde{W}= \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma+\varepsilon},\, -1,\, 1\bigr), 
\qquad 
\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|
$$  

is used for the forward‑pass (the gradient flows through $W$ via a straight‑through estimator).  
The scalar $\gamma$ is therefore a **layer‑wise scale** that maps a “typical’’ magnitude of the raw weights onto the interval $[-1,\, 1]$.  

---

### Why the *average* absolute value?

* **Robustness to outliers** – The maximum $\max|W|$ is dictated by a single extreme entry. If one weight grows unusually large (which happens frequently during early training), $\gamma$ would become too big and almost every other entry would be divided down below $0.5$; after rounding almost all elements would turn into $0$. This makes the sparsity pattern erratic and destabilises learning.  

* **Preserving the central mass** – The mean absolute value $\displaystyle \frac{1}{nm}\sum|W_{ij}|$ estimates the *typical* size of a weight under heavy‑tailed distributions that appear in deep nets. It ignores sign but captures scale without being dominated by rare large values, unlike an RMS  
$\displaystyle \sqrt{\frac{1}{nm}\sum W_{ij}^{2}}$, which up‑weights large outliers even more strongly.  

* **Simple and cheap** – Computing a sum of absolutes costs exactly the same order as any other statistic used during training and does not require a square‑root operation.  

Consequently $\gamma$ adapts smoothly as training progresses while keeping each layer’s effective gain in a reasonable range.  

---

### Influence on sparsity

After division by $\gamma$, an entry becomes zero iff  

$$
\Bigl|\frac{W_{ij}}{\gamma}\Bigr|<\tfrac12 .
$$  

Thus the **zero‑fraction** equals  

$$
p_0=\Pr(|W_{ij}|<0.5\,\gamma).
$$  

If $W_{ij}$ follows a symmetric distribution centred at zero (e.g., a Gaussian), about one third – two thirds of the elements survive the threshold depending on the exact shape – yielding a *moderate* sparsity that is similar from layer to layer and stable over training epochs. Using $\max|W|$ would make $p_0\simeq 1$ (almost all zeros), while an RMS would push $p_0$ closer to $0.9$; both extremes harm expressivity.  

This controlled density gives BitNet b1.58 its “feature‑filtering’’ capability: some connections are intentionally switched off ($0$), yet enough $\pm 1$ connections remain to transmit information efficiently.  

---

### Training stability across layers

Every linear layer receives inputs multiplied by $\widetilde{W}$. Because  

$$
\widetilde{W}= \gamma\,\operatorname{round}\!\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma}\Bigr),
$$  

the **effective gain** contributed by a layer is approximately $\gamma\,c_{\text q}$, where $c_{\text q}$ is the expected magnitude of $\operatorname{round}(\cdot)$ (roughly $\tfrac23$ for a symmetric distribution). Since $\gamma$ tracks the average size of $W$, this gain stays comparable from one layer to another even though the underlying full‑precision parameters may differ widely in scale during optimisation. In turn  

* gradients do not explode or vanish because the forward‑pass does not introduce sudden amplifications,
* optimisation hyper‑parameters (learning‑rate schedule, Adam β’s…) can be kept identical to those used for FP16 baselines,
* RMSNorm applied later further normalises activations, so any residual scale mismatch is removed before non‑linearities.  

Hence the abs‑mean normalisation acts like a lightweight **weight‑normalisation** that stabilises deep transformers trained with extreme quantisation.  

---

### Magnitude distribution seen by subsequent layers

After ternarisation each matrix behaves as  

$$
x^{(l+1)} = \widetilde{W}^{(l)}x^{(l)}
          = \gamma^{(l)}S^{(l)}x^{(l)},
          \qquad S^{(l)}_{ij}\in\{-1,0,+1\}.
$$  

The scalar $\gamma^{(l)}$ therefore replaces the original spread of weight magnitudes with a single gain term that mirrors their *average* size. Because this term varies only slowly during training, downstream layers receive activations whose variance stays close to what they would have seen with full‑precision weights—up to a constant factor that RMSNorm subsequently removes. In practice this means no extra loss‑scale tuning or learning‑rate warm‑up is required beyond what works for unquantised LLMs.  

---

### Summary

Using the **average absolute value** ($\gamma$) instead of max or RMS provides three practical benefits:

1️⃣ **Balanced sparsity** – It makes roughly half of the entries survive rounding, avoiding pathological densification or over‑pruning.  
2️⃣ **Robust layer‑wise scaling** – It captures typical weight magnitude while being insensitive to occasional outliers, keeping forward gains stable across depth and training steps.  
3️⃣ **Preserved activation statistics** – By normalising each matrix onto a common scale before ternarisation, activations entering later layers retain magnitudes comparable to those in full‑precision models, which together with RMSNorm guarantees stable optimisation without special tricks.  

These properties are precisely what allow BitNet b1\.58 to train from scratch at “only’’ $\pm 13/0~·~\gamma$ precision while matching FP16 performance at modest memory and compute cost.​

### Why are activations scaled symmetrically to [$-Q_b$, $Q_b$] per token instead of asymmetrically [$0$, $Q_b$] with a zero‑point?
Question:
For activation quantisation the authors deviate from typical unsigned integer schemes by first centering each token’s activation range around zero:

*Activations* \(a\) → \(a' = \operatorname{round}\bigl(\frac{a}{s}\bigr)$

with $s$ chosen so that all values lie in $[-Q_b,Q_b]$. They claim this "gets rid of the zero‑point" while having negligible effect on performance.

Could anyone explain:
* The difference between symmetric vs asymmetric quantisation;
* Why removing an explicit zero‑point simplifies implementation;
* Whether any subtle accuracy loss occurs because dynamic range differs across tokens.
Answer:
The paper adopts the same “per‑token” activation quantiser as BitNet but changes one detail: instead of first shifting the activation range into an unsigned interval $[0,Q_{b}]$ and storing a separate **zero‑point** $Z$, they centre every token’s activations around 0 and map them into the signed interval $[{-}Q_{b},\,Q_{b}]$.  
To see why this matters we first have to distinguish the two usual families of quantisers.

---

### Symmetric vs asymmetric quantisation  

A **symmetric** quantiser assumes that the real‑valued tensor is roughly centred at zero.  
It uses a single scale factor  

$$
s=\frac{L}{Q_{b}},
\qquad L=\max_{i}|x_{i}|,
$$

and maps a value $x$ to an integer  

$$
q = \operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{x}{s}\right)\in\{-Q_{b},\,\dots ,\,Q_{b}\}.
$$

No offset is needed because $q=0$ already corresponds exactly to $x=0$.

An **asymmetric** quantiser allows a non‑zero minimum $a$ (for instance $a=0$ when all samples are non‑negative).  
It computes  

$$
s=\frac{b-a}{2^{k}-1},\qquad
Z = \operatorname{round}\!\Bigl(-\,\frac{a}{s}\Bigr),
$$

and produces  

$$
q = \operatorname{round}\!\Bigl(\frac{x}{s}\Bigr)+Z,
\qquad q\,{\in}[0,\,Q_{b}],
$$

where $Z$ is called the *zero‑point*.  In practice this means that the integer representation is unsigned (e.g.\ uint8).

Both variants are mathematically equivalent – you can always rewrite one as the other – but the choice determines what extra data has to be stored and what extra arithmetic must be performed during inference.

---

### Why “getting rid of the zero‑point’’ simplifies things  

In BitNet b1.58 the weight matrix is ternary $\{-1,\,0,\,+1\}$.  Multiplying a signed integer activation by a ternary weight collapses to either adding, subtracting or skipping a value:

```c
if w == +1 : out += a
if w == -1 : out -= a
```

When activations are stored as signed integers already centred on 0, this operation can be implemented with pure integer add/subtract instructions – exactly what modern GPUs/TPUs accelerate with INT8 $\times$ INT8 $\rightarrow$ INT32 kernels.  Introducing an unsigned activation would require **first** converting `a` back into a centred value by subtracting its zero‑point `Z`:

```c
a_centered = a_uint8 - Z
```

That subtraction has three consequences:

1. **Extra data path** – `Z` has either to be loaded from memory together with every activation tensor or kept in a register for an entire layer.  In per‑token quantisation `Z` would be different for every token, so it cannot be shared across many elements.
2. **Extra arithmetic** – every element of the GEMM needs an additional subtraction before the multiply‑add loop can start, breaking tight inner kernels that assume raw INT8 operands.
3‑**Hardware mismatch** – most SIMD/GEMM units have dedicated instructions for *signed* INT8 multiply‑add (e.g.\ `vpmaddubsw` on x86 or `sdot` on ARM).  Feeding them unsigned operands forces either an emulation path or an extra conversion step which hurts throughput and energy efficiency.

By enforcing symmetry we avoid all three overheads.  The only thing that still has to be kept per token is its **scale** $s_{t}=\,\max|a|/Q_{b}$, which is needed anyway for de‑quantising later or feeding back into subsequent layers (e.g.\ for RMSNorm).

---

### Does this hurt accuracy because each token gets its own range?  

Two things happen when we quantise **per token** symmetrically:

*The dynamic range adapts* – each token obtains its own scale $s_{t}$, so even if one position contains very large values while another contains tiny ones, both can use the full $[-Q_{b},\,Q_{b}]$ span without causing overflow elsewhere.  
*Half of the representable codes may stay unused* – if a particular activation distribution is strictly non‑negative (as after ReLU), mapping it onto $[-Q_{b},\,Q_{b}]$ leaves the negative side empty, effectively halving the resolution on the positive side.

In practice these effects turn out to be minor for transformers trained in the BitNet regime:

* The linear layers preceding most non‑linearities produce roughly zero‑mean outputs because they are followed by RMSNorm / LayerNorm, which normalises each token’s statistics.  Consequently most activation vectors contain both signs, so the symmetric interval is well utilised.
* Even when an activation becomes one‑sided (e.g., after SwiGLU’s gating), its magnitude is typically bounded by its counterpart branch; any loss of granularity caused by “wasting” negative codes is compensated by the high precision already offered by **8 bits**, which gives 256 distinct levels.
* The only possible source of degradation would be an outlier within a token that inflates $s_{t}$ and therefore coarsens most other values in that vector.  Empirically this occurs rarely enough that the authors report “negligible” impact on perplexity or downstream tasks.

Thus symmetry does not materially reduce representational power; rather it removes unnecessary bookkeeping while keeping enough effective resolution thanks to per‑token scaling and the natural balance introduced by normalisation layers.

---

### Bottom line  

In BitNet b1.58 activations are quantised as  

$$
q_i^{(t)}=\operatorname{round}\!\Bigl(\frac{a_i^{(t)}}{s_{t}}\Bigr),
        \qquad
        s_{t}=\frac{\displaystyle\,\max_j|a_j^{(t)}|}{Q_{b}},
$$

so every token $t$ lives in $[-Q_{b},\,Q_{b}]$.  
This *symmetric* scheme eliminates an explicit zero‑point, allowing raw signed INT8 values to flow straight into matrix‑multiply kernels that already assume centred data – a win for both software simplicity (no per‑token offset storage or subtraction) and hardware efficiency (pure add/subtract on ternary weights).  
Because transformer activations are roughly centred after normalisation, using a symmetric interval does not sacrifice notable accuracy; any theoretical loss due either to unused negative codes or occasional outliers is empirically negligible, as demonstrated by the paper’s experiments showing parity with FP16 baselines.​
