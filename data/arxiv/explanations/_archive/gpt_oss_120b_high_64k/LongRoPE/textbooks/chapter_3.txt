\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

Chapter 3: 3. Existing Approaches to Extending Context Windows  

# Fine‑tuning on Longer Sequences – Data Scarcity and Computational Cost  

A straightforward way to enlarge a language model’s usable context is to continue training it on sequences that are longer than the original window length $L$. When a model is exposed repeatedly to token positions beyond $L$, its internal parameters learn how to attend to and predict tokens situated at those novel indices. Recent work has demonstrated that this strategy can push the effective window size from a few thousand tokens up to roughly $128\,\text{k}$ tokens \cite{longlora,pi,yarn,zhang2024soaring,liu2023scaling}.  

However, two practical hurdles quickly become dominant once we aim far beyond $128\,\text{k}$ tokens. First, publicly available corpora contain only a limited supply of documents whose tokenized length exceeds $1\,\text{M}$. The longest ubiquitous benchmark datasets—PG‑19 \cite{pg19}, Proof‑pile \cite{proof-pile}, Books3 \cite{pile}—rarely provide enough examples for stable gradient estimates at such extreme lengths. Consequently the optimizer sees very few instances of the newest position indices during fine‑tuning, which hampers generalisation.  

Second, training transformers with quadratic attention costs $\mathcal O(L'^2)$ makes each step substantially more expensive as $L'$ grows. Extending from $4\,\text{k}$ to $256\,\text{k}$ already demands orders of magnitude more GPU memory and wall‑clock time than conventional pre‑training regimes; extending toward millions of tokens would require prohibitive numbers of GPU hours even when using efficient parallelism techniques \cite{cube}. These two constraints—the paucity of ultra‑long text data and the steep computational overhead—explain why prior fine‑tuning approaches have stalled around the $100\,\text{k}$ token mark.


# Position Interpolation (PI): Linear Scaling of Rotation Angles  

Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) encodes each token’s absolute index $n$ as a set of sinusoidal rotations applied dimensionwise \cite{rope}. For embedding dimension $d$, a single RoPE vector can be expressed as  

\[
\bigl[\cos(n\theta_0),\,\sin(n\theta_0),\,\cos(n\theta_1),\,\sin(n\theta_1),\,\dots,\,
\cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}),\,\sin(n\theta_{d/2-1})\bigr],
\tag{1}
\]  

where $\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}$ defines a decreasing frequency spectrum (the default base $\theta=10\,000$).  

When extending the context from $L$ tokens to a larger target length $L'$, Position Interpolation (PI) simply rescales every rotary angle by a uniform factor equal to the extension ratio  

\[
s=\frac{L'}{L}.
\]  

In practice this amounts to dividing every argument inside the cosine/sine functions by $\lambda=s$, yielding the unified formulation  

\[
[\cos(\tfrac{n}{\lambda}),\, \sin(\tfrac{n}{\lambda}),\, 
 \cos(\tfrac{n}{\lambda^{{1}}}),\, \sin(\tfrac{n}{\lambda^{{1}}}),\, 
 \dots,\,
 \cos(\tfrac{n}{\lambda^{d/2-1}}),\, \sin(\tfrac{n}{\lambda^{d/2-1}})].
\tag{2}
\]  

Because $\lambda$ is identical for all dimensions, PI compresses the entire spiral of positional codes into a tighter region of the unit circle. The resulting “crowded” representation makes it difficult for attention heads to discriminate nearby token indices—a problem that grows rapidly as $s$ increases. Empirically PI performs acceptably only for modest extensions (e.g., $s=2$); once $s$ reaches four or higher its perplexity degrades sharply.


# Neural Tangent Kernel–Based Scaling – Unequal Dimension‑wise Interpolation/Extrapolation  

The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) viewpoint treats RoPE’s sinusoidal components as Fourier features encoding the scalar input $n$ \cite{ntk,dynamicntk}. Information theory suggests that low‑frequency dimensions (\emph{i.e.,} those with large $\theta_i$) capture coarse positional structure while high‑frequency dimensions encode finer granularity. Consequently applying a single uniform rescale factor misallocates interpolation pressure across dimensions.  

NTK‑aware methods therefore assign a dimension‑dependent scale  

\[
\lambda_i = s^{\,i},
\]  

where $i$ indexes over half‑the embedding dimensions ($i = 0,\dots,d/2-1$). Smaller indices correspond to higher frequencies; they receive a smaller $\lambda_i$, meaning less compression (“interpolation”). Larger indices get larger $\lambda_i$, allowing more aggressive compression (“extrapolation”). This heterogeneous treatment spreads positional distortion more evenly across frequencies and mitigates crowding in the highest–frequency slots. A dynamic variant further adjusts $\lambda_i$ depending on the current sequence length during inference \cite{dynamicntk}, enabling modest extensions ($s \le 4$) without any additional fine‑tuning. Nonetheless even under NTK scaling the attainable non‑fine‑tuned stretch remains bounded well below a million tokens.


# YaRN Method – Three‑Group Frequency–Based Scaling Scheme  

YaRN builds upon both linear PI and NTK ideas by recognizing that not all frequency bands benefit equally from a single interpolation rule \cite{yarn}. It partitions the set of RoPE dimensions into three contiguous groups according to their intrinsic frequency:

* **High‑frequency group** – retains its original scale ($\lambda=1$, i.e., pure extrapolation).  
* **Mid‑frequency group** – adopts an NTK‐style power law $\lambda_i = s^{\,i}$, providing graded compression between high‐ and low‐frequency ends.  
* **Low‑frequency group** – follows pure linear PI ($\lambda=s$), applying uniform downscaling across these coarser components.

The exact boundaries between groups are chosen manually based on empirical validation experiments performed on each model family. By allocating a gentle treatment to high frequencies while compressing low frequencies more aggressively, YaRN achieves stronger performance than raw PI or static NTK when extending windows up through several tens of thousands of tokens. However because its grouping rule is handcrafted rather than data driven, it may miss optimal allocations for newer architectures or dramatically larger extensions.


# Limitations Observed at Extensions Beyond ≈ 128 k Tokens  

Empirical studies reported throughout the literature consistently indicate that none of the aforementioned schemes sustain quality past roughly one hundred thousand tokens. When evaluated on long‐form language modelling benchmarks such as PG‑19 or Proof‑Pile, models employing PI quickly suffer perplexity blowups after modest stretches; NTK continues flattening performance only up until about fourfold expansion ($L'≈16$ k when starting from $4$ k); YaRN’s three–group strategy begins deteriorating once it reaches contexts far larger than its designed operating range—for example its perplexity spikes already near $7$ k when attempting an $8$ k evaluation window in non‐fine‐tuned settings (c.f., Fig.\,\ref{fig:non-ft}). Consequently all prior works converge around an upper bound close to $128$ k tokens if they rely solely on positional interpolation together with limited fine‑tuning resources.


# Fundamental Obstacles Identified by the Authors  

The inability of existing approaches to breach the $128\,\text{k}$ limit stems from three intertwined challenges articulated in the introduction:

* **Catastrophic values from untrained positions**  
  When an LLM’s context window is enlarged dramatically—say from $4\,000$ tokens toward over one million—more than ninety percent of absolute indices have never been seen during pretraining. Those fresh positions lack calibrated rotary angles within RoPE’s learned spectrum, producing outliers (“catastrophic values”) that poison attention scores and impede convergence during subsequent fine‑tuning ⟨\(^{[1]}\).

* **Scarcity of ultra‑long training corpora**  
  Datasets containing passages longer than one thousand thousand tokens remain rare or synthetically constructed at great expense. Without abundant examples spanning the target length $L'$, gradient updates cannot reliably teach a model how best to exploit newly introduced positional codes ⟨\(^{[2]}\).

* **Attention crowding leading to degradation on short contexts**  
  Extending context dilutes each token’s contribution across an ever larger set of pairwise interactions; attention weights become spread thinly over millions of positions rather than concentrating around nearby neighbours crucial for short‐range reasoning ⟨\(^{[3]}\). This “attention crowding” manifests as noticeable drops in performance even when evaluating within the original ($4\,000$ ‑token) window after naïve interpolation has been applied.


Taken together, these obstacles explain why previous techniques plateau near \( \sim10^5 \) tokens despite methodological refinements such as NTK adaptations or Yu↑R​N’s three–group scheduling. Overcoming them requires both a more expressive way of reshaping RoPE across dimensions *and* an efficient strategy for progressively adapting models without demanding massive ultra‑long corpora—a motivation that drives the LongRoPE method introduced later in this textbook.
