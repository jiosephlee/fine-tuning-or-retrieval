\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

Chapter 4: 4. Empirical Analysis of Non‑Uniformities in Positional Interpolation  

# Preliminary  

Understanding how large language models encode the order of tokens is essential before delving into the empirical observations made by the authors. Modern transformer‑based language models typically rely on rotary position embeddings (RoPE) because they allow the attention mechanism itself to incorporate positional information without requiring an explicit additive bias. For a token placed at discrete position index \(n\), its RoPE vector can be expressed compactly as  

\[
\bigl[\cos (n\theta _0),\;\sin (n\theta _0),\;\cos (n\theta _1),\;\dots ,\;\cos \bigl(n\theta _{d/2-1}\bigr),\;\sin \bigl(n\theta _{d/2-1}\bigr)\bigr]\!,
\]  

where \(d\) denotes the hidden dimension of the model, each pair \((\cos ,\sin )\) corresponds to one two‑dimensional rotation plane, and \(\theta _i=\theta ^{-\,2i/d}\) specifies the rotation frequency associated with dimension \(i\). The scalar \(\theta\) is called the base value of RoPE; existing large language models adopt a default base of \(10\,000\).  

When researchers wish to enlarge a model’s context window from its original length \(L\) to a longer length \(L'\), they introduce an **extension ratio**  

\[
s=\frac{L'}{L}\!,
\]  

which quantifies how many times longer the new window is relative to the pretrained one. Extending the context window inevitably creates novel position indices that have never been seen during pretraining, which can destabilize training if handled naïvely. A common remedy is **positional interpolation**, i.e., modifying the angular frequencies so that newly introduced positions are effectively “compressed” into the range on which the model was originally trained.  

All recent positional‑interpolation schemes can be written under a unified formulation that rescales each rotation angle by a factor that may depend on both the RoPE dimension \(i\) and a global scaling function \(\lambda (\beta )^i\):  

\[
 
    \bigl[\cos \!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{\,\lambda (\beta )^{0}}\bigr),\, 
          \sin \!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{\,\lambda (\beta )^{0}}\bigr),
          \dots ,
          \sin \!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{\,\lambda (\beta )^{d/2 -1}}\bigr)\bigr]\!,
 
\]  

where we have introduced \(\beta=\theta ^{\,2/d}\) for convenience and where \(\lambda (\beta )^i\) denotes an effective rescaling factor applied to dimension \(i\). Different interpolation methods instantiate this factor differently:

* **Linear positional interpolation (PI)** simply sets a uniform rescaling factor equal to the extension ratio, i.e., \(\lambda = s\) for every dimension.
* **NTK‑based interpolation** draws inspiration from neural‑tangent‑kernel theory and applies a dimension‑dependent exponent so that lower‑frequency dimensions are stretched more than higher‑frequency ones; formally one may write \(\lambda = s^{\,i}\).
* **YaRN** partitions dimensions into three frequency groups—high frequency, medium frequency, low frequency—and assigns extrapolation (\(\lambda =1\)), NTK‑style scaling, or linear interpolation respectively for each group. The grouping itself is chosen empirically based on human intuition rather than an exhaustive search.

These three families illustrate a growing recognition that **non‑uniform treatment across RoPE dimensions** may help alleviate two fundamental problems caused by naïve linear compression: crowding of angular values when many new positions are forced into a limited angular interval, and catastrophic out‑of‑distribution values at far‑away positions that have never been visited during pretraining.



# Finding 1 – Variation Across Dimensions Requires Dimension‑Specific Rescaling Factors \(\lambda _i\)  

The first empirical insight arises from an exhaustive evolutionary search over possible choices of rescaling factors across individual RoPE dimensions. Instead of constraining all dimensions to share a single scalar factor—as PI does—or forcing them into three coarse groups—as YaRN does—the authors allowed each dimension \(i\) its own continuous factor \(\lambda _i\). The search objective was minimisation of language model perplexity on long documents drawn from validation subsets of PG19 and Proof‑Pile, without any additional fine‑tuning of model weights.  

When evaluated under this *dimensionally heterogeneous* regime, LLaMA2‑7B achieved substantially lower perplexities than any of the existing baselines. Results reported in Table 1 of the source material show that even at context lengths where vanilla PI already begins to falter ((8 k–16 k)), the searched configuration maintains smooth degradation curves rather than sudden spikes. Remarkably, YaRN—a method previously thought superior because it combines extrapolation with NTK‐style scaling—performed worse than plain PI on PG19 precisely because it fails to reach target lengths without further training; its perplexity rose sharply after roughly 7 k tokens despite being designed for longer windows.  

The success of per‑dimension optimisation stems from preserving **key frequency components** within RoPE that are most responsible for distinguishing nearby token positions. High‐frequency planes correspond mathematically to larger rotation rates (\(n/\beta ^i)\); applying an overly aggressive compression factor would cause their angular values at adjacent positions to become indistinguishable—a phenomenon often described as “crowding”. By assigning smaller rescaling values (closer to 1) for these critical planes while permitting larger values for low‐frequency planes—which encode coarse positional relationships—the search discovers an embedding geometry that remains expressive even after massive index stretching.


# Finding 2 – Early Token Positions Benefit From Reduced or No Interpolation (\(\hat n\))  

A second axis along which non‑uniformity manifests concerns **token position** rather than embedding dimension. Intuitively, earlier tokens in an input sequence exert disproportionate influence over subsequent attention computations because many attention heads allocate higher scores toward earlier contexts—a pattern documented in prior work such as Streaming LLM and LM‑Infinite . To test whether respecting this asymmetry improves long‐context performance, the authors introduced a threshold parameter \(\hat n\): all positions \(n< \hat n$ are left untouched by any rescaling (the original RoPE angles are retained), while positions \(n \geqslant \hat n$ receive the dimensionwise factors discovered via evolutionary search.\)

Through systematic ablations where \( \hat n ∈ \{0,\dots ,256\}\) was varied while keeping all other settings constant, two trends emerged clearly:

1. Retaining original rotary angles for any positive number of early tokens reduced perplexity compared with applying rescaling uniformly from token 0 onward.
2. The *optimal* value of \( \hat n$ depended on how far beyond the pretrained window one attempted to extend; longer extensions benefitted from larger thresholds because more early tokens needed protection against aggressive compression.


These observations validate the hypothesis that early positions serve as “anchors” within attention maps and should therefore escape aggressive positional distortion.


# Finding 3 – Proper Exploitation Improves Performance With And Without Fine‑Tuning  

Having established two distinct forms of non‑uniformity—dimensionwise scaling factors \(λ_i$\) and an initial-token safeguard parameter \(̂n$\)—the authors examined whether integrating both simultaneously yields tangible gains under both *non‑fine­tuned* inference and *fine‑tuned* training regimes.

In purely inference scenarios where no additional gradient updates are performed after altering RoPE geometry, simply applying the searched heterogeneous factors already delivered marked improvements over every baseline at context lengths up through 16 k tokens—the same ranges where prior works required costly fine‑tuning steps merely to remain functional. This demonstrates that well‐chosen non‑uniform rescaling alone can compensate for much of the information loss introduced by naive linear compression.



Fine­tuning experiments pushed this advantage further into truly extreme domains such as 64 k tokens. Here LLaMA2‐7B was fine­tuned for a modest number of steps while using the searched factors tailored originally for an 8× extension scenario (\(s=8\times)\). Table 3 illustrates that even after this additional optimisation step our method outperforms PI and YaRN both *before* any weight updates—as expected since it supplies a better starting point—and *after* training—thanks largely to reduced distributional shift between pretraining positions and their newly interpolated counterparts.


Thus “exploiting non‐uniformities” does not merely provide incremental gains; it reshapes how much extra computation is required at all—enabling profound extensions without any gradient updates while simultaneously offering stronger foundations when full fine‐tuning does become necessary.


# Experimental Protocol Used For Validation  

The empirical studies were conducted under a rigorous yet pragmatic evaluation pipeline designed solely around next-token prediction loss expressed via **perplexity**, which remains a standard measure of language model uncertainty over large corpora.



*Data selection.* For searching rescaling factors across dimensions—and later confirming their effectiveness—the authors sampled five random validation documents from PG19 . Each selected document was required to exceed strictly more than $L'$ tokens so that every extended position could be exercised during measurement.\footnote{PG19 consists primarily of books sourced from Project Gutenberg assembled specifically for evaluating long-context models; Proof-Pile contains professional legal texts used historically as challenging test material.\par} These short sample sets kept computational cost manageable while still reflecting realistic token distributions.\par

*Search objective.* During evolutionary optimisation (described more fully elsewhere), each candidate set of parameters $(λ_0,\dots ,λ_{d/2 -1},̂n)$ was applied directly onto a frozen LLM weight set before computing perplexity on those five documents.\par

*Fine­tuning regime.* When assessing how well non-uniform interpolations behave under weight adaptation, LLaMA2–7B was separately fine­tuned at sequences up to either 64 k tokens using standard AdamW optimisation settings reported in Sect.~4.A . The same five‐sample validation set served again as an evaluation checkpoint after each predefined training step count.\par

*Baselines.* In every comparison scenario three established baselines were included:\newline
– Linear PI ($λ=s$ universally);\\
– Dynamic NTK ($λ=s^{\,i}$);\\
– YaRN which employs three manually engineered groups.\newline
All baselines operated without further parameter updates during inference whenever possible.\par

*Metric reporting.* Perplexity values were averaged over all sampled documents without any additional smoothing or temperature scaling. When tabulated results appeared throughout Sec.~3 (“Findings”), lower values unequivocally indicated superior modelling fidelity.



Overall this protocol isolates *positional embedding* effects from confounding factors such as data variance or optimiser dynamics, giving credence particularly to conclusions about why uniform approaches degrade quickly.



# Why Uniform Scaling Fails At High Extension Ratios  

Uniform scaling compresses every rotary angle by exactly the same factor irrespective of its intrinsic frequency or its distance from sequence start.\par

Two intertwined phenomena explain why such naïve compression collapses once $s$ exceeds modest values (~ 4×):



*Crowding.* In RoPE geometry each two-dimensional rotation plane maps discrete token indices onto points along a unit circle according to angle $n/\beta ^i$. If all angles are divided uniformly by $s$, then points corresponding even to nearby token indices become tightly clustered together on this circle when $s$ becomes large enough—that is multiple distinct inputs map onto virtually indistinguishable vectors.\par This makes it extremely hard for downstream feedforward layers or attention heads to separate neighboring words—a problem dubbed “crowding” throughout earlier work.\par

*Out-of-distribution novelty.* Extending beyond training limits introduces *new* absolute position indices whose corresponding raw angles would fall far outside any range observed during pretraining (\(n > L$). Uniformly shrinking these novel angles does not restore their statistical properties because their distribution becomes heavily skewed toward low magnitude values while preserving relative ordering poorly.\par Consequently attention mechanisms encounter unforeseen query/key interactions leading frequently either  
    – Extremely small dot products producing vanishing gradients during subsequent fine­tuning rounds, or  
    – Excessively large dot products resulting in saturated softmax outputs which heavily bias hidden states toward early tokens.\par Both effects contribute antagonistically toward exploding loss spikes once more than roughly ninety percent of token positions become “new”, precisely what occurs when attempting raw extensions beyond $1000k$ tokens.



The empirical findings motivate respecting two orthogonal axes of variation:\newline
(i) Allocating smaller compressions $λ_i≈1$ toward higher–frequency planes preserves angular separation where it matters most;\newline
(ii) Leaving early‐position angles untouched shields crucial attention anchors.\newline
Together these adjustments prevent excessive crowding while keeping overall representation capacity sufficient even at theoretically extreme ratios such as \$512\times\$ extensions reported later in LongRoPE.



In summary, this comprehensive empirical investigation demonstrated concretely that **non-uniform treatment**—both across embedding dimensions via dedicated scalars \(λ_i\$`and across sequence location via threshold \\(\\hat n\\)—is indispensable when stretching rotary embeddings far beyond their native window sizes.
