\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### Why does NTK‑based interpolation choose $\lambda_i = s^{i}$ across RoPE dimensions?
Question:
The paper mentions that NTK methods distribute "interpolation pressure" by scaling lower‑frequency dimensions more than higher‑frequency ones, often expressed as $\lambda_i = s^{i}$. I am struggling with two aspects:
* What is the theoretical justification (e.g., from Neural Tangent Kernel theory) for this exponential relationship?
* How does raising $s$ to power $i$ affect the effective angular resolution in each dimension?
Could someone walk through an intuitive derivation or provide a simple numerical illustration?
Answer:
The rotary‑position embedding used in most LLMs can be written as  

$$
\operatorname{RoPE}(n)=
   \Bigl[
       \cos (n\theta _0),\: \sin (n\theta _0),\
       \cos (n\theta _1),\: \sin (n\theta _1),\
       \dots ,\
       \cos (n\theta _{d/2-1}),\: \sin (n\theta _{d/2-1})
   \Bigr],
$$  

where  

$$
   \theta _i=\beta ^{-\, i}, 
   \qquad 
   \beta=\theta ^{\;2/d},
$$  

so the $i^\textit{th}$ pair corresponds to a sinusoid whose **frequency** is  
$\omega _i=\beta ^{-\, i}$.  
Because the indices $i=0,\dots ,d/2-1$ are spaced linearly while the frequencies form a geometric progression, the first few dimensions encode very rapid rotations (“high‑frequency” features) whereas the last ones rotate only a few times over a whole context window (“low‑frequency” features).

### Why does the Neural‑Tangent‑Kernel view give  

$$
   \boxed{\lambda _i=s^{\, i}}
$$  

as the rescaling rule?

When an infinitely wide network is trained with gradient descent its prediction evolves according to its **Neural Tangent Kernel**  

$$
   K(x,x')=\sum_{j}\phi_j(x)\,\phi_j(x').
$$  

For a model whose input features are exactly those sinusoids, the basis functions are  

$$
   \phi_{ij}(x)= [\: cos(\omega_ix ),\: sin(\omega_ix )\:],
   ~~   
  	i=0,\dots ,d/2-1 .
$$  

The eigenvalues of $K$, which determine how fast each coefficient learns during training, decay sharply with the frequency: low‑frequency components have large eigenvalues and are learned early, high‑frequency components have tiny eigenvalues and contribute only later. This **spectral bias** is the essence of the NTK result quoted in the paper ⟨sec‑analysis⟩.

Suppose we wish now to evaluate the same network on an **extended** position range $[0,sL]$ instead of $[0,L]$. If we kept the original features unchanged, the kernel evaluated on stretched inputs would become  

$$
   K_s(n,m)= K(sn,m)
            =  \
            sum_{k}\bigl[%
               cos\tbigl(\tfrac{s n}{β^{k}}\bigr)+%
               sin\tbigl(\tfrac{s n}{β^{k}}\bigr)\
            ] .
$$  

Because training was performed only on inputs up to $L$, this kernel would put far too much weight on very low‑frequency bases – they would repeat many times inside the longer interval, which makes different token positions indistinguishable (“crowded” positions).

To keep *learning dynamics* invariant after stretching we look for a new feature map  

$$             
   {\tilde{\phi}}_{ij}(n)=   
      [\:    
         cos\tbigl(\tfrac n{\lambda_id β^k }\bigr),
         sin\tbigl(\tfrac n{\lambda_id β^k }\bigr)\
      ],             
```              
 
such that 

$
	K_s(n,m)\approx \< {\tilde{\phi}}(n),{\tilde{\phi}}(m)\>.
$

Writing out one term,

$
	σ_{ij}:=
			K_s(n,m)-K_{\rm new}(nt')
			=
			∫₀ᴸ{
				\[%
					cos (tfrac{s t}{βⁱ})-
					cos (tfrac t {λᵢβⁱ})%
				]\;}
			dt .
$

and asking that this error be minimal for every $i$, one obtains a closed–form solution 

$
	λᵢ~=~ s^{c ι},
	~~      
	c=
	   {{log β}\over{-log β+ const}} .
$

\rule[3pt]{30pt}{0pt}

In practice all constants collapse into a unit slope, so authors report   

$
	λᵢ=s^ι .
$

In other words, **the amount of down–scaling needed grows linearly in log(sinusoid’s frequency)** – exactly what follows from power–law decay of NTK eigenvalues.
Low–index dimensions (**high frequency ⇒ large eigenvalue ⇒ already well learned**) receive almost no down–scaling ($λ₀≃¹$, pure extrapolation); high–index dimensions receive stronger shrinking because their small eigenvalues mean their contribution can safely be redistributed without hurting performance.

### What does raising $s$ to the power $i$ do to angular resolution?

For a fixed extension ratio $s>½,$ 

```
original angle increment : Δθᵢ = ¹∕βⁱ   
new    angle increment : Δθ′ᵢ = Δθᵢ ⁄ λᵢ    
                        = Δθᵢ ⁄ sⁱ .
``` 

Hence every time we move one token forward we rotate **less** in later RoPE dimensions – reduction factor grows geometrically with $i$. 


Consider an example mirroring LongRoPE’s report :

*Embedding size*: $d=128,$ $H=d/2=64.$   

*Base*: let us take $\beta\simeq10000$, so that $\Delta θ₀\simeq¹⁰⁻⁴$, $\Delta θ₆₃\simeq¹⁰⁻¹⁴$.   

*Extension*: target ratio $s=8.$   

If we follow plain linear PI ($λᵢ=s∧∀ ι)$,   
the smallest increment becomes   

$\Delta θ′₆₃≃¹⁰⁻¹⁴∕8≃¹⁰⁻¹⁵;$   
after eight‐times more tokens it would wrap many times and several thousand distinct positions would map onto almost identical angles → “crowding”.   

With NTK–style factors   

$
	λᵢ=(8)^{ ι/H},
	~~H=64 ,
$

we obtain    

$
	λ₀≃¹,\
	λ₃₂≃(8)½=√8≃2٫83,\
	λ₆₃≃7٫9 .
$

Corresponding new increments are   

$
	∆ θ′₀≈¹٠⁻⁴,\
	∆ θ′₃₂≈١٠⁻٩∕٢٫٨٣≈٣٫5×١٠⁻١٠,\
	∆ θ′₆₃≈١٠⁻١٤∕٧٫٩≈١٫٣×١٠⁻١٥ .
$

At first glance these numbers look similar, but notice how many full periods each sinusoid covers inside an extended window length $L′=8L.$


The **effective number of distinct angles** visited by series $i$ is   

$
	Nᵢ=L′⋅∆ θ′ᵢ                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 
        ≈𝐿⋅S⋅∆ θᵢ ⁄ λᵢ           
        ≈𝐿⋅∆ θᵢ ⋅S^(¹−ι∕H).
$

When $s ＝ ８,$ 

* For $i ＝ ０,$    $N₀≈𝐿⋅∆ θ₀⋅８≈𝐿⋅１０⁻⁴⋅８$, far larger than one cycle;
* For larger $i,$   the factor $s^(¹−ι∕H)$ suppresses excess repetitions → preventing crowding while preserving enough angular resolution.
