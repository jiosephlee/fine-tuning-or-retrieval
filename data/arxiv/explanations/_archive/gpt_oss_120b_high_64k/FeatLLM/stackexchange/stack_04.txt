\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### What is the intuition behind splitting LLM reasoning into two steps (causal inference then rule deduction)?
Question:
"Reasoning instruction" in Section 3.1 describes a two‑step process:

1️⃣ Infer causal relationships between features and task description **without** looking at example demonstrations;
2️⃣ Use those inferred relationships plus few‑shot examples to deduce concrete rules.

Why would separating these steps improve rule quality compared with asking the LLM to directly output rules given all information at once? Are there empirical studies supporting this claim, or could this separation sometimes lead to contradictory reasoning?

Answer:
The two‑step “reasoning instruction’’ used by FeatLLM is motivated by the way a human expert solves a new tabular problem.  
First we ask the LLM to **declare what it already knows** about the relationship between each column and the target (“causal tendency’’). This does not involve any concrete training examples; it only draws on the world‑knowledge stored in the model (e.g. “older patients have higher risk of heart disease’’). In the second step we let the model **refine those hypotheses** by looking at the few labelled rows that are supplied as demonstrations, and from this combination we ask it to express explicit decision rules (e.g. “$\text{if } age > 65 \text{ and } cholesterol > 200 \text{ then class}= \text{positive}$”).  

Why does this help compared with giving the LLM all information at once?  

* **Separating prior knowledge from noisy data** – With only a handful of labeled rows it is easy for a model to infer spurious correlations (e.g. a column that happens to be high only because of random sampling). By asking the model first to filter out columns that are *a priori* irrelevant, we constrain its attention to those features that have a plausible causal link to the task. When the demonstrations are later consulted, they can only adjust or instantiate rules for this reduced set of candidates. The paper’s ablation study (Table 4 “‑Reasoning’’) shows that removing Step‑1 degrades average AUC by several points, especially in the 2‑shot and 4‑shot regimes, confirming that causal pre‑filtering contributes most when data are scarce.  

* **Reducing combinatorial explosion of rule search** – A naïve prompt that asks “list ten rules for each class given all columns and examples’’ forces the LLM simultaneously to select relevant features **and** craft logical conditions for each feature type. This dual burden increases the chance of malformed or overly generic rules, as evidenced by the higher incidence of parsing errors reported in Section 4.2 when Step‑1 is omitted.  

* **Mitigating contradictions** – If an LLM’s internal priors conflict with patterns present in the few‑shot data (for instance, a dataset where a “non‑causal” feature happens to be highly predictive), Step‑1 may bias rule generation away from that feature. The authors acknowledge this possibility: they note that when columns highly correlated with the target but unrelated causally are introduced (the “spurious correlation’’ experiment on Heart + Adult‐columns), FeatLLM still learns modestly from them because Step‑2 can still incorporate them if they appear consistently in the demonstrations. Moreover, their ensemble with bagging (Section 3.3) dilutes any single contradictory reasoning trace—different runs may keep or discard a disputed feature, and averaging over many models yields a robust final predictor.  

* **Alignment with chain‑of‑thought literature** – The split mirrors the chain‑of‑thought prompting strategy (Wei et al., 2022) where intermediate reasoning steps improve downstream generation quality. In FeatLLM these steps are explicitly “causal inference’’ followed by “rule deduction”, which is exactly what the authors refer to when they say “the LLM’s inference process is divided into two steps”.  

Empirically, beyond the ablation mentioned above, Figure 5 (“Effect of spurious correlations’’) demonstrates that FeatLLM’s performance declines far less than baseline methods as irrelevant columns are added, indicating that its early causal filter indeed shields rule extraction from misleading data cues. The authors also report that across 13 datasets FeatLLM consistently ranks first or second, while variants without Step‑1 fall behind by an average of $\approx 10\%$ relative AUC.

In summary, splitting reasoning into a causal‐knowledge stage followed by a data‑driven rule synthesis stage gives the LLM a clean “prior + evidence” template: first prune away irrelevant features using world knowledge, then specialize rules using scarce examples. The design yields higher‑quality rules as shown by ablations and robustness experiments, while any occasional contradictions between prior knowledge and data are softened through ensemble averaging and weight tuning rather than being catastrophically propagated.
