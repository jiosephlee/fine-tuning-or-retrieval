\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

# Taming Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) Models – No More Routing Replay Thanks to GSPO  

*How a simple change from token‑level to sequence‑level importance weighting makes large‑scale RL on sparsely activated language models stable and efficient.*

---

## Why reinforcement learning on MoE models has been hard  

Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures achieve massive parameter counts by activating only a tiny subset of “experts” per token. This sparsity gives us impressive capacity–efficiency trade‑offs, but it also introduces **expert‑activation volatility** when we try to fine‑tune the model with reinforcement learning (RL).  

In the context of **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**—the token‑level RL algorithm that avoids a value network—the importance weight for each token is  

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\bigr)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\bigr)} .
\]

Because an MoE model routes every token through a different set of experts, even a modest policy update can change the activated experts dramatically. Empirically we observed that after a single gradient step roughly **10 % of the experts** activated for a given token differ between $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}$} and $\pi_{\theta}$. When these fluctuating activations are plugged into $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, the resulting weights become extremely noisy, which quickly destabilises training and often leads to catastrophic model collapse.

### The ad‑hoc fix: Routing Replay  

To tame this noise researchers introduced **Routing Replay**. The idea is simple: cache the expert routing produced by $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ during rollout and *replay* those exact routes when evaluating $\pi_{\theta}$ so that both numerator and denominator in $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ see the same set of experts. While this restores stability in practice, it comes at a steep price:

* **Memory & communication overhead** – caching per‑token routing across large batches can dominate GPU memory.  
* **Capacity throttling** – forcing the new policy to reuse old routes limits how much the model can explore new expert combinations.  
* **Implementation complexity** – extra bookkeeping interferes with clean pipeline design and makes distributed training harder.

If only there were a way to avoid these heavy lifts altogether…

---

## The core insight: match reward granularity with off‑policy correction  

RL rewards in language generation are typically **sequence–level**: after producing an answer $y$ to a query $x$, we run a verifier $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$. The classic importance sampling identity tells us that correcting an off‑policy expectation requires a *single* ratio over whole samples:

\[
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\text{new}}}[f(y)] 
= \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\text{old}}}\!\Bigl[\frac{\pi_{\text{new}}(y)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y)}\,f(y)\Bigr].
\]

GRPO instead applies this identity at every token step—an inappropriate granularity when the reward lives at the end of the sequence. The result is high variance that compounds over long responses.

**Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** rectifies this mismatch by defining **one importance weight per generated response**:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{\!1/|y_i|}
          = \exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
               \log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                         {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr).
\]

The exponent $1/|y_i|$ performs **length normalization**, keeping $s_i(\theta)$ comparable across responses of different lengths and preventing a few tokens from dominating the ratio.

The **GSPO objective** then mirrors PPO’s clipped surrogate but operates on whole responses:

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G}
\Bigl[\tfrac1G \sum_{i=1}^{G}
   \min\bigl(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\,
            \operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon ,1+\varepsilon )\,\widehat A_i\bigr)
   \Bigr],
\]
where $\widehat A_i$ is the *group advantage* obtained by normalizing rewards across the $G$ responses generated for each query:

\[
\widehat A_i = \frac{
      r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\bigl(\!\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\bigr)}
      {\operatorname{std}\bigl(\!\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\bigr)} .
\]

Now the clipping decision is made on **entire sequences**, not individual tokens—exactly what we need when our feedback is sequence‐level.

---

## Why sequence‐level weighting neutralizes expert volatility  

Because $s_i(\theta)$ aggregates *all* token probabilities into one scalar before clipping, any individual token’s routing shift contributes only a tiny fraction (scaled by $1/|y_i|$). Even if 10 % of tokens switch experts after an update, their influence on the overall likelihood ratio is diluted by averaging across the whole sentence. Consequently:

* **No dependence on cached routes** – we can compute $\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)$ directly with the current forward pass; there is nothing special to preserve from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.
* **Stable importance ratios** – the distribution correction remains well behaved even when some tokens route through radically different experts.
* **Reduced variance** – by weighting all tokens equally before applying clipping (see Eq. (5) in the paper), GSPO eliminates the noisy token‐wise weight explosion that plagues GRPO.

Empirically this translates into smooth reward curves without any sign of collapse—even for deep MoE transformers such as Qwen3‑30B‐A3B where GRPO would otherwise diverge without Routing Replay.

---

## Experimental confirmation  

The authors fine‑tuned a cold‑start Qwen3‐30B‐A3B model under two setups:

| Setup | Clipping range | Requires Routing Replay? |
|------|----------------|---------------------------|
| GRPO | $[0.2,\ 0.27]$ | Yes |
| GSPO | $[3\times10^{-4},\,4\times10^{-4}]$ | No |

Training used four mini‑batches per rollout batch, a common practice for high GPU utilisation. The results were striking:

* **Training stability:** GSPO never exhibited catastrophic drops; reward curves rose monotonically throughout billions of tokens.
* **Sample efficiency:** With identical compute budgets and query sets, GSPO achieved higher final rewards on AIME’24 (Pass@1), LiveCodeBench (Pass@1), and CodeForces Elo than GRPO + Routing Replay.
* **Clipping behaviour:** Although GSPO clipped *more* responses overall—its fraction of clipped tokens was two orders of magnitude larger than GRPO—the resulting gradients were far less noisy because every clipped sample carried a coherent sequence‑level signal rather than an erratic token‑wise one.
* **MoE robustness:** No expert‐routing cache was needed; training converged smoothly despite obvious changes in per‑token expert assignments across updates.

These findings underpin why recent Qwen3 models attribute most of their RL performance gains to adopting GSPO.

---

## Side benefits: simpler infrastructure and lighter precision requirements  

Because GSPO only needs *sequence‐level likelihoods*, it tolerates modest numerical mismatches between training engines (e.g., Megatron) and inference engines (e.g., SGLang/vLLM). In practice we can feed directly the logits returned by an inference server into the clipping computation without recomputing log probabilities under the heavy training kernels—a boon for:

* **Partial rollout** or multi‑turn RL where latency matters,
* **Training–inference disaggregated pipelines**, and
* **Distributed settings** where moving large tensors back to the trainer would be costly.

All together this reduces engineering effort while preserving—or even improving—training throughput.

---

## Takeaway  

GSPO demonstrates that *the right granularity matters.* By aligning off‑policy correction with where rewards are actually given—at the level of whole generated sequences—we obtain an importance sampling estimator that is both theoretically sound and practically robust against MoE’s inherent activation volatility. The consequences are immediate:

1. **Routing Replay becomes obsolete** – no extra memory or communication overhead.
2. **Training stays stable even for deep sparse models**, enabling larger MoEs without extra tricks.
3. **RL pipelines shrink** – fewer moving parts mean fewer bugs and faster iteration cycles.
4. **Performance improves**, as shown on standard math/code benchmarks under identical compute budgets.

If you’re scaling RL on any sparsely activated LLM today—especially MoEs—switching from GRPO (or PPO) to GSPO could be all you need to unlock stable, efficient progress without resorting to ad hoc workarounds like routing replay.

---

### Looking ahead  

The authors envision GSPO as a foundational algorithmic block for future RL scaling efforts. As language models continue growing—in size *and* sparsity—sequence‑level policy optimization may become the default choice whenever rewards are global rather than local. We anticipate more open‐source implementations soon, which will make it easier for research labs and industry teams alike to reap these stability gains without reinventing complex replay mechanisms.
