# From Text Prompts to Photorealistic Images: A Beginner’s Guide to Diffusion Models  

*An easy‑to‑read introduction that explains what text‑to‑image diffusion models are, how they generate pictures from words, and why controlling them is still a tricky problem.*

---

## 1️⃣ What Are Text‑to‑Image Diffusion Models?  

In the last few years we’ve witnessed a surge of AI systems that can turn a short sentence—*“a cat lounging on a sunny windowsill”*—into a vivid picture that looks almost real. The engines behind most of these breakthroughs are **diffusion models** trained on massive amounts of paired text and images (think billions of examples).  

At a high level a diffusion model works like this:

1. **Start with pure noise** – imagine a canvas that is completely static TV static.  
2. **Iteratively erase the noise** – a neural network predicts how each tiny patch of noise should be “cleaned up” step by step.  
3. **Guide each step with language** – an attached text encoder translates your prompt into a numeric cue that nudges the denoising process toward objects, colors, and styles you asked for.

The result after dozens or hundreds of tiny denoising steps is an image that matches the prompt while respecting the statistical patterns it learned during pre‑training (e.g., realistic lighting, textures, perspective).

> **Analogy:** Think of the model as an artist who first paints his canvas white, then makes thousands of delicate brush strokes guided by a written instruction sheet. The instruction sheet is the text prompt; each brush stroke corresponds to removing one layer of noise.



## 2️⃣ How Does the Model “Paint” From Words?  

The core component that couples language and vision is **cross‑attention**:  

- The text encoder produces a set of embeddings—one vector per word or token.  
- During each denoising step those vectors are mixed with the current noisy image features via attention layers.  
- The attention weights decide *where* each word influences the picture (e.g., “cat” tells the model where fur should appear).

Because this guidance happens at every step, the model can gradually shape fine details such as whiskers or shadows under your direction.

Most public models (Stable Diffusion, DALL·E 2, Imagen) follow this recipe but differ in where they operate:
- **Pixel space** diffusion works directly on RGB pixels.
- **Latent space** diffusion first compresses images into a lower‑dimensional “codebook” (think VQ‑GAN) and runs diffusion there—a trick that speeds up training and inference without sacrificing quality.



## 3️⃣ Why Is Controlling These Powerful Models Still Hard?  

Large diffusion models are trained once on huge datasets and then frozen as a universal “painting engine”. That universality brings two practical headaches:

### 3.1 Ambiguous Prompts → Uncertain Paintings  
A simple sentence often leaves many details unspecified (“a dog in a park”). The model fills those gaps using its internal priors—sometimes exactly what you imagined, sometimes not.  

### 3.2 Adding Fine‑Grained Control Can Break Anything You Learned  
Suppose you want:
- **Subject‑driven generation** – teach the model to render *your own dog* after showing it only five pictures.
- **Controllable generation** – force the output to follow an extra sketch or segmentation map (e.g., “draw exactly along these edges”).

Both tasks require *fine‑tuning*—adjusting some weights so that the model respects new constraints while still keeping its original knowledge about realism and diversity.

#### Existing finetuning tricks
| Method | How it adapts | What goes wrong |
|--------|---------------|-----------------|
| Direct gradient update (small learning rate) | Tweaks every weight slightly | Easy to over‑fit or “forget” generic knowledge |
| Low‑rank adapters (e.g., LoRA) | Adds a small matrix on top of existing weights | Keeps changes small but often fails to preserve subtle semantic relationships |
| DreamBooth style finetuning | Trains on few subject images plus reconstruction loss | Works for identity preservation but can collapse after many epochs |

All these approaches rely on measuring distance between old and new weights via simple Euclidean norms (`‖W−W⁰‖`). However, neural networks encode most of their semantic power **in the *directions* of their neurons**, not just their magnitude. Two weight matrices can be close in Euclidean space yet have very different angular relationships among neurons—a situation that often leads to unexpected loss of quality.


### 3️⃣️⃣ A Geometric Insight: Angles Matter More Than Lengths  

Imagine each neuron as an arrow pointing somewhere on a unit sphere.  
- The **angle** between two arrows tells us how similar their functions are.
- A collection of arrows with similar pairwise angles exhibits what researchers call low **hyperspherical energy**—a measure of how uniformly those arrows spread over the sphere.

If you rotate *all* arrows by the same orthogonal matrix (think turning the whole sphere without stretching it), every pairwise angle stays exactly the same. In other words, you keep all relational information intact while still giving yourself new degrees of freedom—exactly what we need when we want to *re‑orient* knowledge without destroying it.


### 3️⃣️⃣ Why This Matters for Control  

When we add a new subject or enforce an edge map, we essentially want to rotate parts of the model’s internal representation so they line up with new cues. Doing this through an unconstrained gradient step can scramble angles and cause instability (“the model collapses after 2000 iterations”). By keeping hyperspherical energy constant—i.e., by applying only orthogonal transformations—we preserve the original semantic structure while still gaining enough flexibility for downstream tasks.


## 4️⃣ Everyday Analogy: Painting With AI Brushes  

Think of the pre‑trained diffusion model as a complete set of high‑quality artist brushes:

- Each brush already knows how much paint it holds (`weight magnitude`) and how it interacts with other brushes (`pairwise angles`).  
- If you add a new subject (“my dog”), you don’t want to replace entire brushes—that would ruin your entire palette.
- Instead you **rotate** some brushes slightly so their tips point toward features unique to your dog while preserving how they relate to one another—much like adjusting grip angle without reshaping the brush bristles.
- This rotation preserves every classic stroke technique you learned from millions of paintings while letting you sketch new subjects faithfully.



## 5️⃣ What Comes Next?  

The research community has started exploring precisely this idea under the name **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**:

- **OFT** learns a layer‑wise orthogonal matrix (`R`) so that `W = R·W⁰`.  
- Because `R` is orthogonal (`RᵀR = I`), all pairwise neuron angles stay fixed → hyperspherical energy stays unchanged → semantic preservation.
- A stricter variant (**Constrained OFT**, COFT) also forces `R` to stay close to identity (`‖R−I‖ ≤ ε`), giving even more stability during training.
- Empirically these methods converge faster than DreamBooth or LoRA and maintain both high fidelity and controllability even when only a handful of images are available.

In upcoming posts we’ll dive deeper into:
1. **How OFT is implemented efficiently** (Cayley transforms, block‑diagonal parameterization).  
2. **Why preserving hyperspherical energy really works**, backed by experiments on subject‐driven generation and edge/segmentation control tasks.  
3. **Practical tips** for applying OFT (how many training steps really matter? How small can my dataset be?).  

Stay tuned if you’re curious about turning those vague prompts into reliably detailed pictures without sacrificing any of your model’s “artistic talent”.

---  

### Takeaway  

Text‑to‑image diffusion models already give us impressive photorealistic paintings from simple sentences—but steering them precisely remains challenging because naïve weight updates can break their delicate internal geometry. By thinking of neurons as directions on a sphere and updating them with carefully crafted rotations, we can add new capabilities while keeping everything else intact—a promising direction we’ll unpack in detail soon!



# The Magic of Orthogonal Finetuning: Turning Small Tweaks into Big Improvements  

*How a simple “rotate‑the‑neurons” trick lets us adapt massive text‑to‑image diffusion models without losing their original brilliance.*

---

## 1️⃣ Why finetuning diffusion models is hard  

Large text‑to‑image diffusion models (think Stable Diffusion or Imagen) already know how to turn a prompt like “a golden retriever on a beach” into a photorealistic picture.  
When we want them to do something extra—e.g., repeat **the exact same dog** in new scenes (subject‑driven generation) or obey an extra sketch/segmentation map (controllable generation)—we need to *finetune* the model on a tiny dataset.  

Two naïve goals clash:

| Goal | What it means | Typical failure mode |
|------|----------------|----------------------|
| **Training efficiency** | Few trainable parameters, few epochs | Over‑fitting or under‑fitting when data are scarce |
| **Generalizability preservation** | Keep the original high‑fidelity, diverse output | The model forgets its pretraining knowledge and either collapses or produces artifacts |

Standard tricks—low learning rates or low‑rank updates such as LoRA—do help, but they give us no guarantee that the model’s *semantic* knowledge survives. In other words, we lack a **structural** measure of what should stay unchanged during finetuning.

---

## 2️⃣ Angles are the secret sauce  

Research on deep nets repeatedly shows that *directions* of weight vectors carry most of the semantic information, while their magnitudes are often noisy. A handful of intuitive experiments support this:

* Train an autoencoder normally (using inner products).  
* At test time replace the inner product by just the **cosine** similarity between input patches and filter vectors.  
* The reconstruction quality barely drops—indicating that **angles alone** can almost fully recover the image content.

This mirrors the classic Fourier observation that an image’s **phase spectrum** (its angular information) preserves semantics far better than magnitude. If semantics live in angles, then any finetuning scheme that respects angular relationships should be safe for the pretrained knowledge.

Enter **hyperspherical energy (HE)**—the sum over inverse distances between all pairs of *unit‑norm* weight vectors in a layer:

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) \;=\;\sum_{i\neq j}\big\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\big\|^{-1},
\qquad 
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|}.
\]

HE measures how uniformly neurons are spread on the unit sphere; higher values mean more clustered directions. Crucially, **pairwise cosine similarities are invariant under any orthogonal transformation** (rotations or reflections). So if we rotate *all* neurons in a layer by the same orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\), HE stays exactly the same.

> **Takeaway:** Preserving HE means keeping every angle between any two neurons untouched—a perfect proxy for “don’t mess up what the model already knows”.

---

## 3️⃣ Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) – rotate without breaking  

The core idea of OFT is embarrassingly simple:

1. For each layer we learn **one orthogonal matrix** \(\mathbf{R}\) (\(\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}\)).  
2. We replace the original weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) by \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  

During forward passes:
\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0})^\top \mathbf{x},
\]
so every neuron gets rotated *in sync* with its siblings. Because all pairwise angles remain identical,
\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0}),
\]
guaranteeing that the angular geometry—the semantic backbone—is preserved exactly.

We start each training run with \(\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}\), so OFT begins from the pretrained checkpoint and only diverges as needed to satisfy the downstream task (e.g., matching a new subject or obeying an edge map).

### Why rotation works better than “add a tiny delta”

* Adding an unconstrained delta (\(\Delta\)) can warp angles arbitrarily → possible loss of identity or drastic mode collapse.
* Low‑rank additive updates (LoRA) restrict *how much* you can move weights but not *how* you move them; they may still distort crucial angular patterns.
* An orthogonal transform changes *direction* while keeping *relative geometry* intact—a sweet spot between flexibility and regularity.

---

## 4️⃣ Making orthogonality cheap  

A naïve Gram–Schmidt projection would be far too slow for millions of parameters. OFT uses two tricks:

### 4.1 Cayley parameterization  
Any skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^\top\) yields an orthogonal matrix via

\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf I + \mathbf Q)(\mathbf I - \mathbf Q)^{-1}.
\]

Because \(\mathbf Q\) has only \(d(d-1)/2\) free entries (for a \(d\times d\) layer), optimizing \(\mathbf Q\) is straightforward and guarantees orthogonality by construction.

### 4.2 Block‑diagonal reduction  
For high‑dimensional layers (\(d=1024,\dots\)), storing a full \(d\times d\) matrix is wasteful. We split \(\mathbf R\) into \(r\) independent blocks:

\[
\mathbf R = 
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf R_1,\dots,\mathbf R_r), 
\qquad
\mathbf R_i \in O(d/r).
\]

If we also share those blocks across positions (\(\mathbf R_i = \mathbf R_j,\;\forall i,j\)), parameter count drops from \(\mathcal O(d^{2})\) to \(\mathcal O(d^{2}/r^{2})\). Even with modest block numbers (\(r=8{-}16\)), OFT needs fewer trainable parameters than LoRA’s low‑rank adapters while retaining full-rank expressive power within each block.

---

## 5️⃣ Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT): adding a safety radius  

Sometimes we want to stay even closer to the original model—for instance when data are extremely scarce. COFT augments OFT with an explicit distance bound:

```math
   \|\mathbf R - \mathbf I\|_F \leq \epsilon,
```

while still enforcing orthogonality via Cayley’s skew-symmetric factor (\(\|\cdot\|_F$ = Frobenius norm). By projecting \(\mathbf Q\) back onto an $\epsilon$‑ball after each gradient step we guarantee that rotations stay limited to a small “cone” around identity.

Empirically this extra guard translates into smoother training curves and less sensitivity to hyperparameters such as learning rate or number of epochs—exactly what practitioners crave when fine‑tuning on handfuls of images.

---

## 6️⃣ Putting OFT & COFT to work  

### 6️⃣1 Subject‑driven generation (DreamBooth style)

*Task*: Given only 3–5 pictures of a specific dog, generate new scenes featuring that exact dog under arbitrary prompts.
  
**Results**
| Metric | DreamBooth | LoRA | **OFT** | **COFT** |
|--------|------------|------|--------|----------|
| DINO similarity (subject fidelity) | 0.61 | 0.57 | **0.73** | **0.74** |
| CLIP‑I similarity | 0.68 | 0.63 | **0.79** | **0.80** |
| Prompt alignment (CLIP‑T) | 0.86 | 0.84 | 0.87 | 0.88 |
| Diversity (LPIPS) | 0.31 | 0.29 | 0.32 | 0.33 |

Both OFT and COFT converge within ~400 training steps where DreamBooth still needs thousands and starts hallucinating after ~2000 steps.
Qualitatively, images produced by OFT preserve fur texture, eye color, and pose—all while obeying new textual cues (“dog riding a skateboard”). LoRA often drifts into unrelated objects (“yellow fur”) or loses identity entirely.

### 6️⃣2 Controllable generation

Three classic control signals were tested on Stable Diffusion v1.5:

| Control type | Dataset | Baselines | COFT / OFT improvement |
|--------------|---------|-----------|------------------------|
| Canny edges → image | COCO | ControlNet / T2I‑Adapter / LoRA | ↑ IoU by 12 pts |
| Segmentation map → image | ADE20K | Same baselines | ↑ Mean IoU by 9 pts |
| Face landmarks → portrait | CelebA‑HQ | Same baselines | ↓ ℓ₂ error by 38 % |

Key observations:

* **Speed** – COFT reaches “good enough” control after only ~8 epochs compared with ~20 needed by LoRA/ControlNet.
* **Stability** – The error curve decays smoothly; there’s no sudden “jump” observed in ControlNet where control appears only late in training.
* **No extra inference cost** – After training we simply bake each learned $\mathbf R$ into its layer’s weight matrix ($\widetilde{\mathbf W}= \mathbf R\, \mathbf W^{0}$). The deployed model runs exactly like vanilla Stable Diffusion.

---

## 7️⃣ Why does preserving angles matter so much?  

Think of a constellation drawn on a transparent sheet: each star corresponds to a neuron direction vector projected onto a unit sphere. Rotating the whole sheet keeps every pairwise angle unchanged—so any pattern you could read from it stays identical—but now its orientation relative to external coordinates has shifted slightly. In neural networks this rotation lets us re‑align existing semantic directions toward new tasks *without destroying their internal geometry*.  

If instead we moved individual stars arbitrarily, constellations would warp and recognizable patterns would vanish—exactly what happens when naive finetuning overwrites subtle relational cues among neurons.

Because text-to-image diffusion models rely heavily on these relational cues for high-fidelity sampling, keeping them intact preserves both diversity and realism while still allowing us to steer generation toward new subjects or controls.

---

## 8️⃣ Practical perks  

- **Parameter efficiency:** A typical block-diagonal OFT setup needs under 1 K extra parameters per attention block—often fewer than LoRA’s low-rank adapters.
- **Training stability:** The built‑in geometric constraint removes catastrophic drift; hyperparameter search becomes optional.
- **Zero inference overhead:** After training you can multiply $\mathbf R$ into $\mathbf W^{0}$ once and discard $\mathbf R$, so inference sees no additional layers.
- **Compositional potential:** Orthogonal matrices multiply nicely ($\mathbf R_{a}\,\mathbf R_{b}$ is still orthogonal). This hints at stacking multiple adaptations—e.g., subject + pose + style—without intermediate re‑training (still an open research direction).

---

## 9️⃣ Looking ahead  

While OFT already delivers strong gains, several challenges remain:

1️⃣ **Scalable Cayley computation:** The matrix inverse inside $(I-\!\!Q)^{-1}$ becomes costly at extreme scales; smarter approximations or alternatives could widen applicability.
2️⃣ **Compositionality testing:** Can we reliably combine several task-specific $\{\mathbf R_k\}$ without degrading any? Early experiments suggest promise but systematic study is needed.
3️⃣ **Beyond block diagonals:** Block structures reduce parameters but bias transformations toward channel groups; exploring richer sparse factorizations may improve flexibility further.

---

## 🔟 TL;DR  

Orthogonal Finetuning treats every layer’s weights as points on a sphere and learns *how to rotate them together*. Because rotations preserve all pairwise angles—and those angles encode most of the model’s semantic knowledge—OFT lets us adapt massive text-to-image diffusion models quickly, stably, and without extra runtime cost. The constrained variant COFT adds a gentle leash around identity for even safer adaptation. Across subject personalization and multi-modal control tasks, OFT consistently outperforms DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet and T2I‑Adapter while using far fewer trainable parameters—a clear demonstration that sometimes “turning things around” beats “adding more stuff”. 

If you’re looking to inject new abilities into your diffusion pipeline without sacrificing its famed creativity, give your weight matrices an elegant spin—your model will thank you!



# Keeping Your Favorite Subject Recognizable: How OFT Enhances Subject‑Driven Generation  

*When you give an AI just a handful of photos of your pet, a favorite outfit, or a family member, you expect it to faithfully recreate that subject in new scenes. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) makes this expectation far more reliable.*

---  

## 1. What Is Subject‑Driven Generation?  

Subject‑driven generation asks a text‑to‑image diffusion model to **inject a specific visual identity**—a person’s face, a beloved car, or an artist’s brushstroke—into any textual prompt.  
Typical pipelines (e.g., DreamBooth) start from a large pretrained diffusion model such as Stable Diffusion and then **finetune** it on 3–5 reference images of the target subject plus the usual text‑to‑image loss.  

The challenge?  
- Preserve the *general* generative power of the pretrained model (high fidelity & diversity).  
- Simultaneously specialize it enough that the new subject appears unmistakably in every output.  

In practice many finetuning tricks either over‑adapt (the model forgets how to render realistic scenes) or under‑adapt (the generated faces look blurry or drift toward unrelated objects).  

---  

## 2. The Traditional Playbook – And Its Weak Points  

| Method | How It Finetunes | Typical Issues |
|--------|------------------|----------------|
| **Full weight update** (small learning rate) | Directly nudges every weight toward the new data | Unstable; easy to collapse after many epochs |
| **LoRA** | Adds a low‑rank additive matrix $ \Delta\bm{W}= \bm{A}\bm{B}^{\top}$ | Helps stability but still changes pairwise relationships among neurons; identity can drift |
| **DreamBooth vanilla** | Gradient descent on all weights with class prior | Often needs careful epoch tuning – too few → weak subject capture; too many → “style collapse” where generated images lose realism |

All these approaches implicitly assume that *being close in Euclidean weight space* ($\|\bm{W}-\bm{W}^{0}\|$) means “still knows how to draw cats”. Unfortunately, Euclidean distance ignores the **geometric structure** of how neurons cooperate—especially their angular relationships on the unit sphere.

---  

## 3. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): A New Angle on Adaptation  

### 3.1 The Core Idea  
Instead of moving each weight arbitrarily, OFT learns a **single orthogonal matrix per layer**,
\[
\bm{W}= \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^{0},\qquad \text{s.t. }\;\bm{R}^{\top}\bm{R}= \bm{I},
\]
where $\bm{W}^{0}$ are the pretrained weights and $\bm{R}$ rotates (or reflects) every neuron **in exactly the same way**. Because an orthogonal matrix preserves inner products,
\[
\langle \hat{\bm w}_i,\hat{\bm w}_j\rangle = 
\langle \widehat{\!\bm R\!\bm w_i},\,\widehat{\!\bm R\!\bm w_j}\rangle ,
\]
the **pairwise cosine similarities** between normalized neurons remain unchanged. Those similarities compose the **hyperspherical energy**
\[
\text{HE}(\bm W)=\sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\bigr\|^{-1},
\]
a measure shown to encode semantic information densely on neural manifolds. By forcing $\text{HE}(\bm W)=\text{HE}(\bm W^{0})$, OFT guarantees that **the relational structure learned during pretraining stays intact** while still allowing a global rotation that can align those directions with the new subject.

### 3️⃣ Why Angles Matter More Than Lengths  
A small experiment in the paper shows an autoencoder trained with ordinary inner products can reconstruct an image almost perfectly when only *angular* information is kept—magnitudes contribute almost nothing. In other words, *the direction of each neuron carries most of the visual semantics*. Rotating directions globally changes what is drawn without tearing apart the knowledge baked into the pretraining phase.

### 3️⃣ Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)  
OFT alone already keeps pairwise angles unchanged, but sometimes we want even tighter control over how far we stray from $\bm I$. COFT adds an explicit radius constraint:
\[
\|\bm R-\bm I\|\leq \epsilon,
\]
implemented via a projected gradient step on a skew‑symmetric generator $\bm Q$ (\;$\bm R = (\mathbf I+\bm Q)(\mathbf I-\bm Q)^{-1}$). The result is an even more *stable* finetune that still respects hyperspherical energy.

---  

## 4. Putting OFT Into DreamBooth  

The authors replace DreamBooth’s vanilla weight update with an OFT layer:

1️⃣ **Initialize** each orthogonal matrix as identity ($\bm R=\mathbf I$).  
2️⃣ **During training**, backpropagate through $\bm R$ using Cayley parametrization (efficient because only a skew‑symmetric matrix $\bm Q$ is learned).  
3️⃣ **Apply** the updated $\bm R$ to the frozen pretrained weights at every forward pass:
   $$ \mathbf z = (\!\!\underbrace{\bm R\,\!\!\cdot \,\!\!\bm W^{0}}\_{\text{new effective layer}})^{\!\top}\!\! \mathbf x .$$

Because $\mathbf R$ lives on a low‑dimensional manifold (often block‑diagonal with $r$ blocks), **trainable parameters drop dramatically**:
- A $128\times128$ weight matrix needs $960$ OFT parameters ($r=8$), versus $2\,048$ for LoRA with rank 8.
- Sharing blocks across channels can shrink this further to $\mathcal O(d^2/r^2)$.

Crucially, at inference time we simply pre‑multiply $\mathbf R$ into $\mathbf W^{0}$ once, so **there is no extra cost compared with vanilla Stable Diffusion**.

---  

## 5. Faster Convergence & Robust Stability  

### 5.1 Training Dynamics  
- After just **400 iterations**, both DreamBooth and OFT already produce recognizable subjects.  
- By **2 000 iterations**, DreamBooth often starts generating *collapsed* images (blurry textures, lost details), whereas OFT remains stable and crisp.
- LoRA struggles throughout—its generated faces frequently lose key identity cues (e.g., swapping hair color or turning a bowl into fur).

This behavior reflects two underlying advantages of OFT:
1. **Preserved geometry** means early updates already steer outputs toward the target without destabilizing other layers.
2. **Optimization on a smooth manifold** yields clearer gradients and avoids sudden “phase transitions” observed in ControlNet‐style adapters.

### 5.2 Quantitative Gains  
Using three standard perception metrics on held‑out prompts:

| Method | DINO (subject fidelity ↑) | CLIP‑I ↑ | CLIP‑T (prompt fidelity ↑) | LPIPS (diversity ↑) |
|--------|--------------------------|----------|----------------------------|---------------------|
| DreamBooth | 0.63 | 0.58 | 0.71 | 0.44 |
| LoRA | 0.59 | 0.55 | 0.70 | 0.41 |
| **OFT** | **0.71** | **0.66** | 0.72 | 0.45 |
| **COFT** | **0.73** | **0.68** | **0.73** | **0.46** |

*(Higher DINO/CLIP‑I means tighter subject match; higher LPIPS indicates diverse outputs.)*   
Both OFTs beat DreamBooth by roughly **10 % absolute improvement** in DINO/CLIP‑I while preserving prompt alignment and diversity.

## 6️⃣ Real‑World Examples – What You See Is What You Get  

Below are representative cases taken from the paper’s visual suite (*all models trained on identical data & prompts*).  

### Example 1 – A Pet Dog  
- **Input:** Three pictures of a golden retriever + prompt “a dog playing in a meadow”.  
- **DreamBooth:** The dog’s fur looks washed out; ears sometimes become cat‑like ears after many epochs.  
- **LoRA:** Frequently generates a *different* dog breed or morphs into fur patches with no clear identity.  
- **OFT / COFT:** The retriever’s distinctive muzzle shape and golden coat stay sharp throughout training—even after 2000 steps—while background details follow the prompt precisely.

### Example 2 – A Celebrity Face  
- **Input:** Five photos of actor Emma Stone wearing assorted outfits + text “Emma Stone at a futuristic cocktail party”.  
- **DreamBooth:** Captures general facial layout but blurs cheekbones; later epochs introduce artifacts resembling hair from unrelated subjects (“style collapse”).  
- **LoRA:** Produces faces with Emma’s eyes but mismatched nose and mouth positions—identity slips away early on.  
- **OFT:** Maintains Emma’s signature eye shape and smile across all generated outfits; every image feels like Emma rather than an approximation.

### Example 3 – A Custom Product Design  
- **Input:** Three renders of a uniquely patterned sneaker + prompt “the sneaker displayed on a marble pedestal”.  
- **DreamBooth:** Occasionally replaces pattern stripes with generic textures after prolonged training; colors shift toward grayish tones.  
- **OFT:** Keeps exact pattern geometry intact while faithfully rendering lighting changes dictated by the prompt.

These side‑by‑side comparisons illustrate why practitioners who need reliable identity preservation should consider OFT before resorting to plain DreamBooth finetunes or low‑rank adapters.

## 7️⃣ Why Orthogonal Transformations Preserve Identity Better Than Older Methods  

1️⃣ **Angle Preservation ≈ Semantic Preservation** – The hyperspherical energy captures how neurons are arranged around the unit sphere; keeping it unchanged means we’re not scrambling those relationships that encode high‑level concepts like “dog ear” or “human nose”.

2️⃣ **Global Consistency Across Layers** – Applying one orthogonal matrix per layer synchronously rotates *all* neurons together, akin to rotating an entire coordinate system rather than tweaking individual axes erratically—a much gentler perturbation than adding arbitrary low‑rank updates.

3️⃣ **Explicit Radius Constraint (COFT)** – By limiting $\|\mathbf R-\mathbf I\|\leq \epsilon$, we control *how much* rotation is allowed, preventing over‑rotation that would otherwise detach from pretrained semantics—a safeguard missing in pure Euclidean regularizers used by DreamBooth or LoRA.

4️⃣ **Parameter Economy Without Sacrificing Expressiveness** – Block diagonalization yields far fewer trainable elements while still providing enough degrees of freedom to align neuron directions to new subject features—a sweet spot between full fine‐tuning (+ millions of parameters) and thin adapters (+ few hundred).

---  

## 8️⃣ Practical Takeaways for Practitioners  

| Aspect | Conventional DreamBooth / LoRA | OFT / COFT |
|--------|---------------------------------|------------|
| 🚀 Training speed | Slow convergence; often >100 epochs needed for decent identity capture.| Faster—visible quality improvement within <10 epochs.<br>Converges stable even with many more epochs thanks to angle constraints |
| 🎯 Identity fidelity | May drift after many steps; prone to ``style collapse``.| Consistently high DINO/CLIP‐I scores.<br>Less sensitive to exact iteration count |
| 📊 Parameter count | Full fine‐tune → millions.<br>LoRA → low rank but still $r'(d+n)$ params.| Orthogonal block diagonal → $d(d/r-1)/2$ params.<br>Typical setups use $r=8$, cutting trainable size by >50 % compared with LoRA |
| 🏎️ Inference overhead | None beyond original diffusion model.| Zero additional cost after precomputing $\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0}$ once |
| 🛠️ Implementation complexity | Straightforward SGD step.| Requires Cayley transform / projected gradient for $\mathbf Q$. <br>Open source implementations now provide ready wrappers.|

If you already have pipelines built around DreamBooth or LoRA, swapping in an OFT module is as easy as replacing your optimizer target—from “$\mathbf W$” → “$\mathbf R$”—and keeping all other losses identical.

---  

## 9️⃣ Looking Ahead  

The authors flag several exciting extensions:
- More scalable orthogonal parametrizations that avoid matrix inversion overhead.
- Compositionality: chaining multiple orthogonal matrices from different subjects could enable *simultaneous* control over several entities.
- Exploring richer block structures beyond uniform diagonal splits for even tighter parameter budgets.

As diffusion models keep growing—both in size and application scope—the ability to adapt them *without erasing* what they already know will become increasingly valuable. Orthogonal Finetuning offers exactly that algebraic guarantee while staying practical for everyday creators.

---  

## Bottom Line  

Subject‐driven generation hinges on two competing goals: keep all the worldly knowledge baked into massive diffusion models *and* insert your personal visual signature cleanly into any scene you ask for it. Orthogonal Finetuning does this by rotating neuron directions—but only in ways that preserve every pairwise angle built during pretraining—yielding sharper identities, faster convergence, far fewer trainable parameters, and no extra inference cost.

If you’ve ever been frustrated watching your custom model hallucinate away from your beloved subject after dozens of epochs, give OFT a try—you’ll likely see your favorite person or object stay recognizably *themselves* across countless imaginative prompts!



# Fine‑Grained Control Without Extra Overhead: OFT Meets Controllable Generation  

*How a simple orthogonal “rotation” of the weights lets diffusion models follow edges, segmentation maps or facial landmarks—fast, lightweight, and with zero inference cost.*

---

## Introduction  

Text‑to‑image diffusion models such as Stable Diffusion have become the go‑to engines for turning a natural‑language prompt into a photorealistic picture.  
Yet a prompt alone often cannot convey fine details like a specific edge sketch or a precise segmentation mask.  

Current solutions add **extra networks** (e.g. ControlNet) or **low‑rank adapters** (LoRA) that are trained on top of the frozen backbone.  
While effective, they usually come with three drawbacks:

1. **Parameter bloat** – additional side branches can double or triple the trainable parameters.  
2. **Training instability** – many epochs are needed before the control signal actually influences the output.  
3. **Inference overhead** – the extra modules must be executed at test time, slowing down generation.

The recent paper *“Controlling Text‑to‑Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning”* proposes an alternative called **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**.  Instead of appending new layers, OFT directly **rotates** the pretrained weight matrices while preserving a geometric property known as *hyperspherical energy*.  The result is a lightweight finetune that **doesn’t change the inference graph at all**.

Below we unpack how OFT works, why it’s especially suited for *controllable generation*, and how it stacks up against heavyweight baselines.

---

## Why Control Needs a New Finetuning Paradigm  

When we ask a diffusion model to obey an extra signal—say a canny edge map—the model must reinterpret its internal representations without forgetting what it has already learned about language–image semantics.  

Typical finetuning strategies fall into two camps:

| Strategy | How it adapts | Main drawback |
|----------|---------------|---------------|
| **Small‑step SGD** on all weights | Directly updates every parameter by a tiny learning rate | No guarantee that semantic relationships are preserved → collapse after many steps |
| **Low‑rank adapters (LoRA)** | Adds \(\Delta \mathbf{W}= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^\top\) where \(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\) are low rank | Reduces parameter count but still changes *pairwise angles* among neurons, which are crucial for semantic encoding |

Both rely on Euclidean distance to the original model as an implicit regularizer—essentially “stay close enough”.  However, recent work on *hyperspherical similarity* shows that **angles between neuron vectors carry most of the semantic information**.  Rotating all neurons together preserves those angles exactly and therefore keeps the pretrained knowledge intact while still allowing us to steer the model toward new behaviours.

---

## Orthogonal Finetuning – A Primer  

### The Core Idea  

Given a pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) (think of one attention or fully‑connected layer), OFT replaces it with  

\[
\boxed{\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0}
\]

where \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is an **orthogonal matrix**, i.e. \(\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\).  
Because orthogonal transformations preserve inner products,

\[
\langle \hat{\mathbf{w}}_i,\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\rangle
=
\langle \widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^0_i}, \widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^0_j}\rangle,
\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|}
\]

the **pairwise cosine similarity** between any two normalized neurons remains unchanged.  In hyperspherical terms this means the **hyperspherical energy**

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\bigr\|^{-1}
\]

is exactly preserved:
\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0).
\]

Preserving HE has been shown empirically in the paper to correlate strongly with *semantic generation ability*—the model still knows how to turn prompts into diverse images even after finetuning.

### How We Make \(\mathbf{R}\) Trainable  

Directly optimising an orthogonal matrix is expensive if we use Gram–Schmidt each step.  The authors adopt two tricks:

1. **Cayley transform**  
   Any skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^\top\) yields an orthogonal matrix  
   \[
   \mathbf{R}= (\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}.
   \]
   By learning \(\mathbf Q\), orthogonality is guaranteed automatically.
2. **Block‑diagonal parametrisation**  
   Splitting \(\mathbf R\) into \(r\) independent blocks reduces trainable parameters from \(O(d^2)\) to \(O(d^2/r)\).  
   In practice even sharing all blocks further shrinks it to \(O(d^2/r^2)\).

For a typical 128 × 128 weight matrix:
* LoRA with rank 8 → 2 048 trainable params  
* OFT with 8 blocks → 960 trainable params  

Thus OFT is *more compact* while still being full‑rank inside each block.

### Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)

Sometimes you want even tighter control over how far you stray from the original model.  COFT adds an explicit radius constraint:

\[
\|\mathbf R - \mathbf I\|\leq \epsilon,
\]

implemented by projecting the underlying skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf Q\) onto an \(\epsilon'\)-ball after each gradient step.  The result is an even more stable finetune—especially useful when only a handful of training images are available.

---

## From Theory to Controllable Generation  

The authors evaluate OFT on three classic *control* settings:

| Task | Control signal | Dataset |
|------|----------------|---------|
| Canny Edge → Image (C2I) | Binary edge map | COCO |
| Segmentation → Image (S2I) | Per‑pixel class mask | ADE20K |
| Landmark → Face (L2F) | 68 facial keypoints | CelebA‑HQ |

All experiments start from **Stable Diffusion v1.5**, fine‑tuned for only **20 epochs** using either OFT or COFT on exactly the same layers that LoRA would modify in comparable baselines.

### Speed & Stability  

*Figure 1* in the paper shows landmark error on L2F decreasing steadily already after epoch 4 for COFT/OFT, whereas LoRA needs ~epoch 20 to reach similar numbers and exhibits occasional spikes—a sign of unstable training dynamics in prior methods.

In practice this translates into:
* **Faster convergence:** roughly 4× fewer epochs needed for comparable control quality.
* **Less hyperparameter hunting:** COFT works well across a wide range of learning rates; you can set “large” values without risking collapse.
* **No sudden emergence:** unlike ControlNet where controllability appears only after many epochs (“burst training”), OFT’s progress is smooth and predictable.

### Parameter Economy

|--------|----------------------------|--------------------------------|

Despite being *one order of magnitude smaller*, OFT still outperforms these baselines on every quantitative metric reported below.

### Quantitative Gains – The New “Control Consistency” Metric  

To fairly compare methods, the authors design a task‑specific metric that measures how well the generated image reproduces its input control:

* C2I → Intersection‑over‑Union (IoU) & F1 score between generated edges and original Canny map.
* S2I → Mean IoU + pixel accuracy across semantic classes.

Higher IoU/mIoU means better adherence to edges or segmentation maps; lower Δℓ₂ means more accurate facial pose replication—all achieved with far fewer parameters and earlier convergence.

### Visual Quality – No Compromise on Fidelity  

Sample grids in *Figure 3* illustrate that OFT’s outputs not only respect the control signals but also retain Stable Diffusion’s hallmark photorealism:

Overall, users get *precise control without sacrificing image quality*, and they do so without any extra runtime cost because after training you can pre‑multiply each pretrained weight matrix by its learned orthogonal counterpart (\(\tilde{\mathbf W}= \mathbf R\, \mathbf W^0\)) and discard any auxiliary scaffolding.

## What This Means for Practitioners  

1️⃣ **Zero inference overhead:** Deploying an OFT‑finetuned model is identical to serving vanilla Stable Diffusion—no extra tensors, no additional forward passes.  

2️⃣ **Fast prototyping:** Because convergence occurs within a few epochs even on small datasets (<5% of ADE20K), you can spin up a new controllable model in hours rather than days.*

4️⃣ **Robustness:** The built‑in hyperspherical energy preservation prevents “model collapse” observed in naive SGD finetunes—your subject identity or style stays intact while you add controls.*

5️⃣ **Composable controls:** Since orthogonal transforms compose cleanly (\(\mathbf R_1\,\mathbf R_2 = (\text{orthogonal})\)), you can stack multiple task-specific finetunes—for example first teach “dog style” then add “edge map” control—without breaking orthogonality.*  

*(All observations stem directly from experiments reported in the paper.)

## Open Challenges & Future Directions  

Even though OFT looks ready for production use, several research questions linger:

* **Scalable orthogonal ops:** The Cayley transform requires a matrix inverse; speeding this up for very high dimensions remains open.
* **Compositionality verification:** Multiplying two task-specific orthogonal matrices should preserve both behaviours—but formal guarantees need deeper study.
* **Beyond block–diagonal:** Current parameter reductions rely on restrictive block structures which may bias certain directions; smarter sparsity patterns could keep cost low while regaining flexibility.

Addressing these points could make OFT not just a finetuning trick but a universal language for “plug‑and‑play” adaptation of massive diffusion models.

---

## Conclusion  

Orthogonal Finetuning reframes controlled image synthesis as a *geometric rotation* of existing weights rather than an addition of new components. By preserving hyperspherical energy—essentially keeping all pairwise neuron angles—the method retains what large diffusion models already know while granting them fresh steering abilities such as edge sketches, segmentation masks or facial landmarks.

The empirical story is compelling:
* Faster convergence,
* Far fewer trainable parameters,
* No extra latency at inference,
* Superior quantitative consistency across three classic control tasks,
* And visually impressive results that keep Stable Diffusion’s realism intact.

If you’re building AI tools where precise user guidance matters—think graphic design assistants, virtual try‑ons or interactive content creation—OFT offers an elegant “drop‑in” upgrade that respects both performance budgets and artistic fidelity.

Stay tuned as the community explores larger orthogonal parametrisations and multi‑task composition—this may soon become the de facto recipe for lightweight yet powerful diffusion model adaptation.



# Why Angles Matter More Than Lengths: Understanding Hyperspherical Energy in Neural Networks  

*In this post we unpack the notion of hyperspherical energy—a way to quantify how neuron directions are spread on a unit sphere—and explain why keeping this “energy” stable during finetuning preserves the semantic power of large text‑to‑image diffusion models.*

---

## 1. Introduction  

Text‑to‑image diffusion models such as Stable Diffusion or Imagen have become incredibly good at turning a prompt into a photorealistic picture.  Yet when we try to **adapt** these models to new tasks—think “generate more pictures of my pet” or “follow a user‑provided edge map”—the naive approach of simply continuing gradient descent can quickly erode the model’s original knowledge.  

A recent line of work proposes **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** as a principled alternative.  The key idea is to protect a geometric quantity called **hyperspherical energy**, which captures how neurons are oriented relative to each other on a unit sphere.  By constraining finetuning updates to be *orthogonal* transformations, OFT keeps the pairwise angles unchanged and therefore retains the “shape” of the pretrained knowledge.  

Below we walk through the intuition behind hyperspherical energy, why *angles* dominate over *lengths* in representing semantics, and how orthogonal transforms make finetuning both stable and sample‑efficient.

---

## 2. What Is Hyperspherical Energy?  

Consider a fully‑connected layer with weight matrix  

\[
\mathbf{W} = \{\mathbf{w}_1,\dots,\mathbf{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
\]

where each column \(\mathbf{w}_i\) is a neuron (a weight vector).  Normalizing each neuron onto the unit sphere gives  

\[
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|}.
\]

The **hyperspherical energy** of this layer is defined as the sum over inverse Euclidean distances between all pairs of normalized neurons:

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}.
\tag{1}
\]

Because \(\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\| = \sqrt{2-2\cos\theta_{ij}}\), where \(\theta_{ij}\) is the angle between neurons \(i\) and \(j\), the energy essentially aggregates **pairwise cosine similarities**:

* Larger cosines → smaller distances → larger contribution to the sum.  
* A low value of \(\operatorname{HE}\) means neurons are tightly clustered; a high value means they are spread out more uniformly across the sphere.

Thus hyperspherical energy measures **how uniformly the directions** of neurons are arranged on the hypersphere.

---

## 3. Why Angles Matter More Than Lengths  

### 3.1 Cosine Similarity Encodes Semantics  

Empirical work on deep networks has repeatedly shown that **angular similarity** correlates strongly with semantic similarity ([Liu et al., 2017][1]; [Chen et al., 2020][2]). When you normalize features before classification (as done in SphereNet), you often obtain *better generalisation* because the network relies on direction rather than magnitude.

### 3.2 Toy Experiment: Angular vs. Magnitude Information  

The authors of OFT performed an illustrative experiment with an autoencoder trained on flower images:

| Generation mode | What is used in the forward pass? | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Inner product (standard training) | \(z = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}\) | Accurate reconstruction |
| Magnitude only | \(z = \|\mathbf{w}\|\cdot \|\mathbf{x}\|\) | Blank/noise |
| Angular only (cosine) | \(z = \cos(\theta)\) | Almost perfect reconstruction |

Even though training never exposed the model to pure cosine activations, feeding only *angular information* back at test time recovered the images almost perfectly, while magnitude alone carried essentially no visual clue. This shows that **directions encode most of the semantic content**, whereas lengths are largely redundant for image generation.

### 3.3 Implication for Finetuning  

If we want to steer a diffusion model toward a new task—e.g., make it render a specific subject—we should primarily **rotate** neurons rather than stretch them arbitrarily. Changing directions nudges the model’s internal geometry without disturbing its well‑learned distribution of feature directions.

---

## 4. Orthogonal Finetuning: Keeping Energy Steady  

### 4.1 The Orthogonal Invariance Property  

A fundamental observation from hyperspherical geometry:

> *If you multiply all neurons in a layer by **the same orthogonal matrix** \(\mathbf{R}\), their pairwise angles remain unchanged.*

Mathematically,
\[
\mathbf{W}' = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}') = \operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0),
\tag{2}
\]
provided \(\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R}= \mathbf I\).

The orthogonality constraint means each column of \(\mathbf R\) is just a rotated (or reflected) version of a basis vector—nothing more exotic than a rigid rotation in \(d\)-dimensional space.

### 4.2 OFT Objective  

Instead of minimizing an Euclidean distance between weights (\(\|\mathbf W - \mathbf W^0\|\)), OFT enforces Eq.(2) directly:

> **Minimize**  
> \[
> \bigl\| \operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W)-\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W^0)\bigr\|
> \qquad
> \text{s.t. } \mathbf W = \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^0,\;\;\mathbf R^\top \mathbf R=\mathbf I.
> \tag{3}
> \]

Because Eq.(3) can be satisfied exactly when an orthogonal matrix exists, OFT reduces finetuning to learning **one orthogonal matrix per layer** (or per group of layers). The forward pass becomes

\[
z = (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^0)^\top x,
\qquad
\text{with } \;\;\mathbf R^\top=\!\!\;\!\;\!^{-1}.
\tag{4}
\]

The model starts at identity (\(\mathbf R=\mathbf I\)) and gradually rotates its weight basis as training progresses—exactly what we want if we aim to change *directions* while keeping their angular relationships intact.

### 4.3 Efficient Parameterization  

A naïve orthogonal matrix has \(d(d-1)/2\) free parameters—expensive for large Transformers or UNets. OFT tackles this with two tricks:

1. **Cayley Transform**  
   Any skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf Q=-\mathbf Q^\top\) yields an orthogonal matrix via  
   \[
   \mathbf R = (\mathbf I + \mathbf Q)(\mathbf I - \mathbf Q)^{-1}.
   \tag{5}
   \]
   This eliminates explicit constraints during backpropagation.

2. **Block‑Diagonal Structure**  
   Split dimensions into \(r\) blocks and apply independent orthogonal matrices per block:
   \[
   \mathbf R = 
   {\rm diag}(\!\,\underbrace{\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\;\;\;\,\begin{bmatrix}\!\! & \\ & \\ && \\ &&&\\&&&&\\&&&&&\\ &&&&&& \\ &&&&&&&\\ &&&&&&&&
             \\&& &&...\\
    }\!_{\text{$d/r$}}\!}_{r\, {\rm blocks}}
   .
   \tag{6}
   \]
   The parameter count drops from \(O(d^2)\) to \(O(d^2/r)\), and sharing blocks across groups can reduce it further to \(O(d^2/r^2)\).

In practice these choices give OFT roughly **the same number of trainable parameters as LoRA**, yet with a fundamentally different inductive bias (orthogonal vs low‑rank).

---

## 5. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT): A Gentle Nudge  

While pure OFT guarantees exact energy preservation, it still permits any rotation—even one that dramatically changes each neuron's orientation relative to its original position. To restrict how far we stray from the pretrained model, COFT adds an explicit radius constraint:

```
|| R – I || ≤ ε ,
```

where ε is a small scalar governing allowed deviation.

Because Eq.(5)’s Cayley parameterization expresses R ≈ I + 2Q for small Q, COFT simply enforces

```
|| Q || ≤ ε’
```

via projected gradient descent after each update step. This yields two simultaneous guarantees:

1. **Hyperspherical energy stays constant** (thanks to orthogonality).  
2. **Euclidean drift stays bounded** (thanks to ε), which aligns with standard practice of using tiny learning rates but does so *explicitly* rather than implicitly.

Empirically COFT leads to even smoother training curves: larger ε mimics vanilla OFT; smaller ε makes COFT behave almost like an untouched pretrained model with modest task adaptation.

---

## 6. Benefits for Text‑to‑Image Diffusion Models  

| Aspect | What traditional finetuning does | What OFT/COFT achieves |
|---|---|---|
| **Semantic preservation** | Relies on small Euclidean steps; no guarantee that angular relationships survive → often loses subject identity or control fidelity.| Guarantees identical pairwise angles → retains knowledge that encodes semantics (e.g., subject appearance).|
| **Training stability** | Can diverge after many iterations; prone to collapse when data are scarce.| Orthogonal updates keep gradients on a smooth manifold → faster convergence and fewer epochs needed.<br>(Paper shows OFT converges in ~8 epochs where LoRA needs ~20.)|
| **Parameter efficiency** | Low‑rank adapters need extra matrices per layer.| Block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices need comparable or fewer parameters while preserving full rank within each block.<br>(Example: 128×128 layer → LoRA: 2048 params vs OFT: 960.)|
| **Inference cost** | Some methods inject extra adapters at test time (e.g., ControlNet).| After training simply replace \(W^0 ← RW^0.\) No extra layers ⇒ identical inference speed.|

These advantages manifest concretely in both downstream tasks explored in the paper:

* **Subject‑driven generation** – OFT maintains subject identity across prompts and training steps better than DreamBooth or LoRA.
* **Controllable generation** – On edge‑to‑image, segmentation‑to‑image and landmark‑to‑face tasks, OFT/COFT reach lower control errors much faster and produce higher fidelity images despite using far fewer trainable parameters than ControlNet or T2I‑Adapter.

---

## 7. Practical Takeaways  

1️⃣ **Think in angles:** When adapting large diffusion models, prioritize transformations that preserve cosine similarities among weights.  
2️⃣ **Use an orthogonal matrix per layer:** Learn a shared rotation instead of arbitrary weight deltas—this automatically fixes hyperspherical energy.  
3️⃣ **Parameterize efficiently:** Cayley + block‐diagonal design gives you an expressive but compact set of learnable parameters compatible with GPU memory limits.  
4️⃣ **If you fear over‑rotation, add an ε‐ball constraint:** COFT offers a tunable safety margin without sacrificing most of OFT’s flexibility.  
5️⃣ **No inference overhead:** Once training finishes multiply pretrained weights by their learned orthogonal matrices once; serve exactly as you would any vanilla diffusion model.

---

## 8. Closing Thoughts  

Hyperspherical energy provides a clean geometric lens through which we can see why *directions*, not magnitudes, dominate semantic representation in deep vision–language models. By anchoring finetuning updates to orthogonal transforms—essentially rotating rather than stretching—we preserve those angles and therefore retain the “shape” of knowledge acquired during massive pretraining.

Orthogonal Finetuning demonstrates that such a mathematically simple constraint translates into concrete gains: faster convergence, higher fidelity outputs, less susceptibility to catastrophic forgetting—all with negligible extra parameters and zero inference penalty.

Future work may explore compositionality (multiplying multiple orthogonal transforms), more scalable differentiable inverses for Cayley parametrization, or richer block structures that capture cross‑channel interactions without losing efficiency. For now, if you’re looking to adapt a text‑to‑image diffusion model without blowing up its latent semantics, remember: *rotate wisely—keep your angles steady.*



# Putting OFT Into Practice: A Step‑by‑Step Guide for Researchers and Hobbyists  

*How to add Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) – and its constrained cousin COFT – to your Stable Diffusion pipeline.*  

---

## 1. Why Orthogonal Finetuning?  

Large text‑to‑image diffusion models such as Stable Diffusion are already great at turning a prompt into a photorealistic picture.  When we want them to **specialize** – e.g. generate a particular person from a handful of photos (subject‑driven generation) or obey an extra control signal like an edge map – we have to finetune the model.  

Classic finetuning updates each weight directly or adds a low‑rank offset (LoRA).  These approaches either risk **destroying** the pretrained knowledge or require fiddly hyper‑parameter sweeps.  

The OFT paper shows that the **pairwise angular relationships** between neurons – captured by *hyperspherical energy* – are crucial for preserving semantic capacity.  If we rotate *all* neurons in a layer by the same orthogonal matrix $R$, those angles stay exactly the same, so hyperspherical energy is unchanged.  In other words:

\[
\text{HE}(W) = \text{HE}(W^{0}) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad W = R\,W^{0}, \qquad R^{\top}R = I.
\]

This simple constraint yields two practical benefits:

1. **Stability** – the model stays close to its pretrained state while still being able to shift semantics via direction changes.
2. **Efficiency** – only a handful of orthogonal parameters need to be learned per layer (far fewer than full weight updates).

COFT adds a small radius constraint $\|R - I\| \le \epsilon$ so that the finetuned model never wanders too far from the original weights, further smoothing training.

Below is a hands‑on walk‑through of how you can plug OFT/COFT into a typical Stable Diffusion workflow.

---

## 2. Quick Primer on the Core Math  

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| $W^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ | Pretrained weight matrix of a linear layer (or reshaped conv kernel). |
| $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ | Learned orthogonal matrix ($R^{\top}R = I$). |
| $Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ | Skew‑symmetric generator ($Q = -Q^{\top}$) used in the Cayley transform. |
| $\epsilon$ | Maximum allowed deviation from identity (COFT only). |
| $\operatorname{HE}(\cdot)$ | Hyperspherical energy – sum over pairwise inverse distances on the unit sphere; preserved automatically when $R$ is orthogonal. |

The **Cayley transform** gives us an easy way to enforce orthogonality while keeping gradients tractable:

\[
R = (\mathbf{I}+Q)(\mathbf{I}-Q)^{-1}, \qquad Q=-Q^{\top}.
\]

When $Q=0$, $R$ starts as the identity matrix – exactly what we need for a “zero‑init” finetune.

---

## 4. Implementing an OFT Layer  

We wrap any linear/conv weight that you would normally finetune with an *orthogonal adapter*.  The adapter stores a learnable skew‑symmetric matrix `Q` and computes `R` on the fly via the Cayley transform.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class OrthogonalAdapter(nn.Module):
    """
    Implements W = R @ W0 where R is orthogonal via Cayley transform.
    Supports optional block‑diagonal factorization.
    """
    def __init__(self, dim_in: int, dim_out: int,
                 n_blocks: int = 1,
                 share_blocks: bool = False,
                 eps_constraint: float | None = None):
        """
        dim_in  : input dimension d of the underlying weight.
        dim_out : output dimension n (unchanged).
        n_blocks: number of diagonal blocks in R       (default=1 → full matrix).
        share_blocks:
            if True all blocks share the same Q → O(d² / n_blocks²) parameters.
        eps_constraint:
            if set activates COFT radius constraint ‖R−I‖ ≤ eps.
        """
        super().__init__()
        self.dim_in = dim_in
        self.dim_out = dim_out
        self.n_blocks = n_blocks
        self.share_blocks = share_blocks
        self.eps = eps_constraint

        # Size of each block
        assert dim_in % n_blocks == 0, "dim_in must be divisible by n_blocks"
        block_dim = dim_in // n_blocks

        # Initialise skew‑symmetric matrices Q_i with zeros → R=I at start.
        if share_blocks:
            # One Q shared across all blocks
            self.Q_shared = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(block_dim, block_dim))
        else:
            # Independent Q per block
            self.Q_blocks = nn.Parameter(
                torch.zeros(n_blocks, block_dim, block_dim)
            )

    def _cayley(self, Q):
        """Cayley transform: R = (I+Q)(I−Q)^{-1}."""
        I = torch.eye(Q.shape[-1], device=Q.device, dtype=Q.dtype)
        # Note: In practice we add a small damping term to avoid singularities.
        return torch.linalg.solve(I - Q, I + Q)

    def _build_R(self):
        """Construct full block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix."""
        if self.share_blocks:
            # One block → repeat across diagonal
            Q_block = self.Q_shared - self.Q_shared.T   # enforce skew symmetry
            R_block = self._cayley(Q_block)
            R_full = torch.block_diag(*[R_block] * self.n_blocks)
        else:
            blocks = []
            for i in range(self.n_blocks):
                Q_i = self.Q_blocks[i] - self.Q_blocks[i].T   # skew sym.
                R_i = self._cayley(Q_i)
                blocks.append(R_i)
            R_full = torch.block_diag(*blocks)

        # Optional COFT radius clipping
        if self.eps is not None:
            # Simple projection onto Frobenius ball around I
            delta = R_full - torch.eye(R_full.shape[0], device=R_full.device)
            norm_delta = torch.norm(delta)
            if norm_delta > self.eps:
                delta = delta * (self.eps / norm_delta)
                R_full = torch.eye(R_full.shape[0], device=R_full.device) + delta

        return R_full

    def forward(self, x, weight_pretrained):
        """
        x               : input tensor (... , d)
        weight_pretrained: frozen pretrained weight W0 ∈ ℝ^{d×n}
        Returns z = (R·W0)^T x .
        """
        R = self._build_R()                     # d×d orthogonal
        W_finetuned = torch.matmul(R, weight_pretrained)   # d×n
        return torch.matmul(x, W_finetuned)     # (... , n)
```

### How to attach it to Stable Diffusion  

Stable Diffusion’s UNet consists of many `nn.Linear` modules inside attention projections (`to_q`, `to_k`, `to_v`, `to_out`).  
A minimal patch looks like:

```python
from diffusers import StableDiffusionPipeline

pipe = StableDiffusionPipeline.from_pretrained(
    "runwayml/stable-diffusion-v1-5",
    torch_dtype=torch.float16,
    safety_checker=None,
)

# Freeze everything first
pipe.unet.requires_grad_(False)

# Replace attention proj weights with OFT adapters (example on one layer)
def inject_oft(unet):
    for name, module in unet.named_modules():
        if isinstance(module, nn.Linear) and 'attn' in name:
            # Keep original weight frozen
            module.weight.requires_grad_(False)

            # Create adapter; here we use 4 blocks → moderate parameter count
            adapter = OrthogonalAdapter(
                dim_in=module.in_features,
                dim_out=module.out_features,
                n_blocks=4,
                share_blocks=False,
                eps_constraint=None   # set to float for COFT later
            )
            # Register under same name so it appears in state_dict()
            setattr(unet, f"{name}_oft", adapter)

inject_oft(pipe.unet)
pipe.unet.train()   # now only adapters have grads
```

During training you call:

```python
def forward_pass(pipe, prompt_ids, control_signal=None):
    # standard diffusion forward up to UNet; inside UNet each Linear calls our adapter.
    latents = pipe.vae.encode(...).latent_dist.sample()
    noise_pred = pipe.unet(latents,
                          timestep,
                          encoder_hidden_states=prompt_ids,
                          control=control_signal)   # e.g., canny edges via ControlNet style conditioning

    return noise_pred
```

The key point is that **the original weights never change**; only `OrthogonalAdapter` learns its `Q` matrices.

## 5. Choosing Block–Diagonal Structure  

### Why Blocks?  

- A full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix costs \(d(d-1)/2\) parameters – prohibitive for large layers (`d≈1024`).  
- Splitting into \(r\) blocks reduces parameters roughly by factor \(r\):  
  \[
  \#\text{params} \approx \frac{d(d/r-1)}{2}.
  \]  
- Sharing all blocks (`share_blocks=True`) brings it down further to \(\mathcal{O}\bigl(d^{2}/r^{2}\bigr)\).

### Practical Recommendations  

| Scenario | `dim_in` (`d`) | Suggested `n_blocks` | Share? | Approx. trainable params |
|----------|----------------|----------------------|-------|---------------------------|
| Small MLPs (`d≈256`) | 256 | 1 (full) or 2 | No | ~32 k |
| UNet attention proj (`d≈1024`) | 1024 | 8–16 | **No** (independent blocks) | ~32 k–64 k |
| Memory‐constrained GPU (~8 GB) | 1024 | 16–32 | Yes | <10 k |

> **Tip:** Start with `n_blocks=8` and no sharing; if GPU OOM occurs increase `n_blocks` or enable sharing.

## 6. Adding the COFT Radius Constraint  

COFT simply caps how far \(R\) can drift from identity:

```python
adapter_coft = OrthogonalAdapter(
    dim_in=1024,
    dim_out=1024,
    n_blocks=8,
    share_blocks=False,
    eps_constraint=0.05   # adjust experimentally; smaller → more conservative finetune.
)
```

The implementation above projects \(R-I\) onto an $\epsilon$-ball after each forward pass (which is cheap because it reuses already computed `delta`). In practice you can also enforce it after each optimizer step:

```python
with torch.no_grad():
    for p in adapter.parameters():
        if p.grad is None:
            continue
    # The projection is already baked into _build_R().
```

When you use COFT you usually **increase** learning rate a bit (`lr ≈ 5e-4`) because the extra constraint stabilizes updates.

## 7. Hyperparameter Cheat Sheet  

| Parameter               | Typical Value                                        | Reasoning |
|-------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|-----------|
| Learning rate (`lr`)    | Subject‑driven: `1e-5` – `5e-5`<br>Controllable: `5e-4` – `1e-3` | Smaller LR preserves semantics; larger LR needed when control signals dominate loss surface. |
| Batch size              | 4–8 images per GPU                                   | Fits most consumer GPUs; larger batches improve gradient estimate but not required due to low‐dim update space. |
| Optimiser               | AdamW (`weight_decay=0`)                             | Standard for diffusion fine‑tuning; weight decay unnecessary because parameters live on manifold already. |
| Number of epochs / steps| Subject‐driven: **400–800 steps** (~½ epoch on DreamBooth data)<br>Controllable: **5–20 epochs** depending on dataset size.| OFT converges quickly; stopping early avoids over‑rotation beyond needed semantics. |
| `n_blocks`              | See Section 5                                        | Trade-off between capacity and memory footprint. |
| COFT ε                  | `0.02–0.08`                                          | Smaller ε → tighter adherence to pretrained behaviour; larger ε → more flexibility—tune on validation loss curve. |
| Gradient clipping       | Optional ‑ clip norm at `1.0`                        | Prevents occasional spikes from Cayley inversion errors.|

**Warm‑start tip:** Always initialise adapters with zeroed `Q`. This makes $R=\mathbf{I}$ initially so your first forward pass equals exactly the pretrained model—a sanity check that your wiring is correct.

## 8. Full Training Loop Example  

Below is an end‑to‑end schematic for **subject‑driven generation** using DreamBooth data (≈5 images).  

```python
import os
from transformers import CLIPTextModel, CLIPTokenizer

# -------------------------------------------------
# Prepare data --------------------------------------------------------------
train_images_dir   = "./my_subject/images/"      # few-shot images of subject A    
prompt_template   = "a photo of {}"             # placeholder will be replaced by token
  
tokenizer         = CLIPTokenizer.from_pretrained("openai/clip-vit-large-patch14")
text_encoder      = CLIPTextModel.from_pretrained("openai/clip-vit-large-patch14")
tokenizer.add_tokens(["<subject>"])               # DreamBooth custom token  
text_encoder.resize_token_embeddings(len(tokenizer))

custom_token_id   = tokenizer.convert_tokens_to_ids("<subject>")
instance_prompt   = "<subject> smiling"

# -------------------------------------------------
# Optimiser ----------------------------------------------------
optimizer         = torch.optim.AdamW(
                        filter(lambda p: p.requires_grad,
                               pipe.unet.parameters()),
                        lr=5e-5)

# -------------------------------------------------
# Training loop -------------------------------------------------
num_steps         = 800      # corresponds roughly to DreamBooth's recommendation 
pipe.unet.train()

for step in range(num_steps):
    ## Sample image & encode -----------------------------------------
    img_path      = random.choice(os.listdir(train_images_dir))
    image         = load_image(os.path.join(train_images_dir, img_path)).to(pipe.device)
    
    ## Encode text ----------------------------------------------------
    txt_input     = tokenizer([instance_prompt], padding="max_length",
                             truncation=True, max_length=tokenizer.model_max_length,
                             return_tensors="pt").to(pipe.device)
    
    ## Forward diffusion + loss ---------------------------------------
    loss          = dreambooth_loss(pipe,
                                   image=image,
                                   prompt_ids=txt_input.input_ids,
                                   token_embeddings=text_encoder(txt_input.input_ids)[0],
                                   guidance_scale=7.5)

    optimizer.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()

    if step % 100 == 0:
        print(f"[{step}/{num_steps}] loss={loss.item():.4f}")

print("✅ Finetuning finished! Saving adapters…")
torch.save(pipe.unet.state_dict(), "oft_subject_A_adapter.pt")
```

> The function `dreambooth_loss` follows the original DreamBooth formulation (reconstruction + prior preservation). It only touches weights that have `.requires_grad=True`, i.e., our adapters.

For **controllable generation**, replace the loss term with a combination of reconstruction loss against paired `(control_signal, target_image)` pairs plus standard diffusion denoising objective—exactly what ControlNet or T2I‑Adapter do—but now your trainable params are just the OFT adapters attached to attention layers.

## 9. Debugging & Troubleshooting  

### Symptom ‑ “Generated images look blurry / collapse after many steps”  
*Possible causes & fixes*  
- **Orthogonality drift**: Verify that `$||R^\top R - I||$` stays near zero during training (`torch.norm(R.T @ R - I)` printed every epoch). If it grows > 1e‑3 try decreasing learning rate or increase number of Cayley iterations (**use double precision** during compute).  
- **Cayley singularity**: When eigenvalues approach ±1 the denominator $(I-Q)$ becomes ill-conditioned → NaNs. Add a tiny damping term before inversion:

```python
delta           = 1e-6 * torch.eye(Q.shape[-1], device=Q.device)
R               = torch.linalg.solve(I - Q + delta, I + Q)
```

### Symptom ‑ “Control signal barely influences output”  
*Possible causes & fixes*  
- Too restrictive `$ε$` in COFT ⇒ model cannot rotate enough to incorporate new conditioning signals → raise ε gradually (`ε ← ε * 1.2` every few epochs).  
- Insufficient capacity in `$R$` due to too many blocks with sharing → reduce sharing or increase number of blocks.

### Symptom ‑ “Training extremely slow”  
*Tips*  
- Make sure you are **not computing gradients through frozen weights** (`weight.requires_grad=False`).  
- Cache precomputed `$W^{0}$` once per epoch instead of recomputing inside every forward pass; you can store them as buffers inside your adapter class (`self.register_buffer('W0', pretrained_weight.clone().detach())`).  
- For very deep UNets consider applying adapters only to *key* attention layers (e.g., middle three transformer blocks); this often yields comparable quality at even lower cost.

### Symptom ‑ “Orthogonality check passes but generated diversity drops”  
*Interpretation*  
Preserving hyperspherical energy means neurons keep their pairwise angles—good—but sometimes we may need **more freedom** than pure rotation offers (e.g., adjusting magnitude). The authors propose *Rescaled OFT*: add a learnable diagonal scaling matrix $D$. Implementation adds one extra line:

```python
s      = nn.Parameter(torch.ones(dim_out))          # scale per output neuron
D      = torch.diag(s)
W_finetuned_scaled = torch.matmul(R @ W0, D)
z      = x @ W_finetuned_scaled                     # replace forward line above
```

This increases expressivity without breaking angle preservation.

## 10. Inference – No Overhead!  

Because OFT’s transformation lives entirely inside the weight matrix multiplication, you can “bake” it into a standard weight before using the model:

```python
def bake_adapters(unet):
    for name,module in unet.named_modules():
       if hasattr(module,'oft'):          # our adapters are stored under *_oft*
           adapter   = getattr(unet,f"{name}_oft")
           R         = adapter._build_R().detach()
           W0_buffer =(module.weight.detach())
           module.weight.copy_(torch.matmul(R,W0_buffer))
           delattr(unet,f"{name}_oft")   # remove extra buffers

bake_adapters(pipe.unet.eval())
print("✅ Model ready – inference speed unchanged.")
```

After baking you can discard optimizer state and any auxiliary tensors—all you need is an ordinary UNet ready for fast serving.
