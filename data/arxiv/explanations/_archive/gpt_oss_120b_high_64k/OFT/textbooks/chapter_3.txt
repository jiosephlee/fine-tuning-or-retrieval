\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 3: 3. Existing Finetuning Strategies and Their Shortcomings  

In order to adapt large text‑to‑image diffusion models to downstream tasks such as subject‑driven generation or controllable generation, researchers have explored a number of straightforward adaptation techniques.  Although each of these methods can achieve some degree of specialization, the original paper observes that they often suffer from either unstable training dynamics or a degradation of the pretrained semantic capabilities.  In this chapter we critically examine the most common approaches—plain gradient finetuning with a small learning rate, low‑rank additive updates (LoRA), auxiliary conditioning networks such as ControlNet and T2I‑Adapter, and the class‑specific prior preservation losses introduced by DreamBooth—highlighting both their underlying intuitions and their empirical shortcomings.

# Fine‑tuning with a Small Learning Rate  

The most direct way to adapt a pretrained diffusion model is to continue training its parameters $\bm{M}$ using stochastic gradient descent (or one of its variants) while initializing from the pretrained weights $\bm{M}^{0}$.  The update rule is typically written as  
\[
\bm{M} \leftarrow \bm{M} - \eta\,\nabla_{\bm{M}} \mathcal{L}(\bm{M}) ,
\]  
where $\eta$ denotes a **small learning rate** and $\mathcal{L}$ is the task–specific loss (for instance a reconstruction loss in DreamBooth).  By choosing $\eta$ very low—often orders of magnitude smaller than those used during pretraining—the optimizer is forced to stay close to $\bm{M}^{0}$ in Euclidean space, i.e. it implicitly seeks to minimize  
\[
\|\bm{M}-\bm{M}^{0}\| .
\]  

Intuitively this makes sense: if only a few examples are available for a downstream task, one would like to perturb the model only slightly so as not to erase its broad visual knowledge.  However, as emphasized in the paper, **Euclidean distance alone does not capture semantic structure**.  Two weight matrices that are close in $\ell_{2}$ norm may differ substantially in how their neurons interact pairwise on a unit hypersphere—a representation known as **hyperspherical similarity**.  Since diffusion models generate images by repeatedly applying linear transformations followed by non‑linearities across many layers, even modest Euclidean drift can inadvertently alter angles between neurons and thereby change how high‑level concepts are encoded.  

Empirically, plain finetuning with a small learning rate sometimes succeeds early in training but later **collapses** when more epochs are performed.  For example, Figure 1(b) of the original work shows that after roughly $2\,000$ iterations DreamBooth—a method built on plain gradient updates—produces unstable or degenerated samples despite starting from a well‑behaved point after $400$ iterations.  The instability stems from an implicit lack of any structural regularizer beyond the Euclidean proximity term; the optimizer may wander into regions where pairwise neuron relationships no longer reflect those learned during pretraining, causing a loss of fidelity and diversity.

# Low‑rank Weight Updates (LoRA) – Formulation and Intuition  

Low‑rank adaptation (LoRA) was introduced as an alternative to unrestricted gradient updates by constraining the form of the weight change.  Instead of updating each entry of $\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ directly, LoRA parameterizes the deviation as a product of two smaller matrices:
\[
\Delta\bm{W}= \bm{B}\bm{A},
\qquad
\text{with } 
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{r'\times d},\
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r'},
\]  
where $r'\ll \min(d,n)$ denotes the rank hyperparameter chosen by the practitioner.  The finetuned weight therefore takes the form
\[
\bm{W}= \bm{W}^{0}+ \Delta\bm{W}
        = \bm{W}^{0}+ \bm{B}\bm{A}.
\]  

The intuition behind LoRA is twofold. First, **parameter efficiency**: only $r'(d+n)$ scalar parameters need to be learned rather than $dn$, which dramatically reduces memory consumption when $d$ and $n$ are large (as is typical for diffusion models). Second, **regularization via low rank**: restricting $\Delta\bm{W}$ to lie in a low–dimensional subspace limits how far $\bm{W}$ can deviate from $\bm{W}^{0}$ while still allowing expressive modifications aligned with task‐specific directions.

From an algebraic perspective LoRA’s additive update can be interpreted as nudging each row vector (or column vector) of $\bm W^{0}$ along directions spanned by a few basis vectors defined by $\bm A$ and scaled jointly by rows of $\bm B$.  Because these basis vectors are shared across all neurons within a layer, LoRA preserves much of the original **pairwise geometry** among neurons—if $r'$ is very small—while still permitting meaningful adaptation (e.g., injecting new style cues).  

Nonetheless, LoRA does not directly control how **angles between neurons** evolve during training because its constraint focuses solely on rank rather than hyperspherical geometry.  Consequently several practical drawbacks emerge:

* Even though $\Delta\bm W$ has limited rank, it may nonetheless rotate neurons arbitrarily within that subspace, potentially breaking the “hyperspherical energy” that encodes semantic relationships.
* When $r'$ is chosen too large for stability reasons (“increase expressivity”), LoRA begins behaving like unrestricted finetuning: it can overly distort neuron directions while still satisfying its own low‑rank constraint.
* Empirical results reported in Section 4 demonstrate that LoRA frequently fails to preserve subject identity in DreamBooth‐style experiments (“the model completely loses subject identity” for certain prompts) despite converging quickly on training losses.

Thus while LoRA offers computational benefits relative to naïve gradient finetuning, it lacks an explicit mechanism for safeguarding **semantic preservation**, which becomes particularly critical when only few examples are available for downstream tasks.

# Extra Networks that Inject Control Signals  

Beyond modifying weights inside the pretrained diffusion backbone itself, another family of approaches augments the model with **auxiliary conditioning networks** designed specifically to ingest additional spatial or structural cues at inference time.  ControlNet \citep{zhang2023adding} and T2I‑Adapter \citep{mou2023t2i} exemplify this paradigm: both start from a frozen pretrained text‑to‑image diffusion model and attach extra trainable modules that consume an auxiliary signal—such as Canny edges, segmentation masks, or dense pose maps—and inject processed features into multiple layers of the diffusion pipeline.

Mathematically such methods define an extra function $\Phi_{\theta_{\text{aux}}}$ parameterized by auxiliary weights $\theta_{\textbf aux}}$, which maps an auxiliary input $\mathbf{s}$ into an embedding vector $\mathbf{s}_{l}$ at layer $l$.  This embedding is then combined additively or multiplicatively with the original latent representation before each denoising step:
\[
\mathbf{x}_{l}' = \mathbf{x}_{l}+ f_{l}\bigl(\Phi_{\theta_{\text aux}}(\mathbf{s}) ,\, \mathbf{x}_{l}\bigr),
\]  
where $f_{l}$ denotes a simple linear mixing operation specified per layer.  
Crucially, both ControlNet and T2I‑Adapter keep all **core diffusion weights frozen**, thereby avoiding any direct perturbation of $\{\boldsymbol W^{0}\}_{l}$ across layers.

The advantage of this approach is that it isolates task‐specific capacity within dedicated parameters; one can train on comparatively few paired examples without risking damage to general knowledge stored in $\boldsymbol W^{0}$.  Moreover it permits *test–time* flexibility because different control signals can be swapped without retraining an entire diffusion backbone.

However this strategy introduces several limitations directly relevant to stability and semantic fidelity:

1. **Additional inference overhead** – During sampling every forward pass must compute both the main diffusion step *and* pass through every auxiliary network branch(s), leading to higher latency compared with pure backbone evaluations.
2. **Limited expressivity for subtle semantic changes** – Because only an additive bias is injected while core weights remain untouched, achieving detailed subject manipulation (e.g., injecting a specific person’s face across varied backgrounds) can be difficult; empirical comparisons show that despite controlling Canny edges well enough for coarse shapes,
   ControlNet’s generated images sometimes lack fine–grained realism compared with OFT’s orthogonal transformations.
3. **Potential overfitting when data are scarce** – When only a handful of supervision pairs exist (as is typical for personalized generation), even modestly sized auxiliary networks may memorize training samples rather than learning robust mappings between control signals and visual semantics.
4. **No guarantee of preserving hyperspherical relationships** – Since auxiliary branches influence activations post hoc rather than transforming neuron directions themselves,
   they do not maintain pairwise angles among original weights,
   leaving open possibilities for subtle drift that harms long‑term generative diversity.

Thus although extra conditioning networks provide a clean modular approach,
their reliance on additional parameters at test time and lack of structural regularization prevents them from fully addressing stability concerns observed in practice.

# Class‐specific Prior Preservation Losses Used by DreamBooth  

DreamBooth represents another line of work that focuses on *preserving* specific semantic attributes while enabling new compositions via textual prompts \citep{ruiz2023dreambooth}.  Its training objective mixes two components:

1. A **reconstruction loss** encouraging generated samples conditioned on novel textual tokens (e.g., “[V]”) together with image‐level guidance from a few subject photographs;
2. A **class–specific prior preservation loss**, which penalizes divergence from images belonging to a broader class sharing visual characteristics unrelated to individual identity (for example general dog images when personalizing a particular dog).

Formally let $\mathcal L_{\text {recon}}$ denote pixel/feature reconstruction error between generated output $G(\mathbf p)$ under prompt $\mathbf p$ containing personalized tokens
and its reference image,
and let $\mathcal L_{\text {prior}}$ be defined over a set
$\{\tilde{\mathbf x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}$ drawn from the same generic class:
\[
\mathcal L_{\text {prior}} = 
    \frac1K\, \sum_{i=1}^{K}
    D_{\text {sim}}\bigl(G(\mathbf p_{\text {class}}),\, \tilde{\mathbf x}_{i}\bigr),
\]  
where $D_{\text {sim}}$ measures feature similarity via CLIP embeddings or other perceptual metrics,
and $\mathbf p_{\text {class}}$ denotes prompts describing generic class attributes without personalized tokens.
The total objective reads
\[
\mathcal L = 
    \lambda_{\text {recon}}\;\mathcal L_{\text {recon}}
    + 
    \lambda_{\text {prior}}\;\mathcal L_{\text {prior}},
\]  
with hyperparameters $(\lambda_{\text {recon}},\, \lambda_{\text {prior}})$ balancing fidelity against overfitting.\footnote{
The exact losses employed follow DreamBooth’s original implementation;
the present chapter does not add new terms beyond what appears in those references.
}

This formulation explicitly tries to keep generated images anchored within their broader feature manifold while allowing optional personalization through learned token embeddings added into
the text encoder’s vocabulary.
Consequently it mitigates catastrophic forgetting—an issue severe for naïve finetuning—but does so **only through direct supervision** rather than through structural invariants such as hyperspherical energy conservation.

Experimental findings described in Section 4 reveal that despite employing class‐preserving regularization,
DreamBooth still displays later instability:
after several thousand iterations newly minted subjects begin losing distinctive traits,
and artifacts appear when prompts request uncommon contexts (“yellow shirt” vs “yellow fur”).
These symptoms indicate that controlling Euclidean proximity via priors does not fully protect inter-neuron angular geometry,
which appears essential for long‑term semantic coherence according to later OFT analysis.

# Empirical Observations on Collapse or Loss of Identity  

Across all three families discussed above—plain small‐LR finetuning,
low‑rank adapters,
and auxiliary conditioning networks—the authors report consistent failure patterns when evaluating subject–driven generation or controllable generation tasks under realistic resource constraints:

* **Model collapse under prolonged training** – When fine‑tuned for many hundred updates,
the naïve gradient approach exhibits severe degradation manifesting as distorted textures,
mode collapse toward monochrome blobs,
or outright failure to generate any realistic picture.
This phenomenon was illustrated experimentally where after roughly $2\,000$ iterations DreamBooth’s outputs became “collapsed”.

* **Loss of subject identity** – In personalized generation experiments,
methods relying solely on additive updates either fail entirely or produce inaccurate variations.
For instance LO​RA often generates yellow fur instead of yellow shirts,
or “completely loses subject identity” when tasked with rendering an object placed inside a bowl.
Even though LO​RA’s rank restriction improves stability relative to bare gradients,
the resultant images sometimes bear little resemblance to target subjects because directional changes among neurons destroy key encoding patterns learned during pretraining.

* **Instability within conditional adapters** – While ControlNet retains overall visual quality thanks largely to its frozen backbone,
its reliance on added branches leads sometimes abrupt emergence of correct control signals only after many epochs;
before those points performance remains erratic.
T2I‑Adapter likewise displays slow convergence given limited data,
with quantitative metrics lagging behind both OFT variants even after comparable numbers of training epochs.

* **Insensitivity to iteration count for some methods vs OFT’s robustness** –
The paper emphasizes that OFT exhibits “insensitivity” towards fine‐grained scheduling:
even very large numbers ($>400$) iteration counts do not degrade output fidelity.
Conversely baseline techniques demand careful early stopping: otherwise they fall into poor local minima evidenced by drastic drops in DINO/CLIP-I metrics during later stages.
These empirically underscored weaknesses motivate introducing orthogonal constraints:
by preserving hyperspherical energy—the sum over inverse distances between normalized neurons—
the authors guarantee all pairwise angular relationships remain exactly unchanged under orthogonal transformation.
Consequently semantic information embedded across layers persists throughout finetuning leading directly to more stable convergence."

In summary, existing adaptation tactics either rely upon weak Euclidean proximity assumptions (plain finetuning),
impose only low‐rank structure without safeguarding neuron orientations (LoRA),
or delegate task specificity entirely onto extra modules while leaving underlying geometry untouched
(Auxiliary networks).
Moreover even sophisticated supervision such as class‐specific priors fails to prevent later degradation.
Through systematic experimental inspection these shortcomings become apparent across diverse downstream scenarios—
prompted me­thods suffer either instability or loss of subject fidelity,
thereby setting up motivation for Orthogonal Finetuning which explicitly addresses both robustness
and semantic preservation through mathematically provable invariance properties.
