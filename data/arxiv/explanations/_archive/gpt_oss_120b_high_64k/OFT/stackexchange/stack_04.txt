\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### What is the connection between phase preservation in Fourier transforms and orthogonal finetuning of neuron directions?
Question:
The authors draw an analogy between phase spectra (which retain semantic information) and neuron direction changes via orthogonal transformations. I find this analogy puzzling because:

1. In an image, phase encodes relative spatial shifts, while neuron directions are high‑dimensional weight vectors.
2. The Fourier analogy seems limited to linear operations.

Can anyone elaborate on how this intuition translates concretely into the diffusion model setting? Does changing neuron directions truly act like modifying phase while keeping magnitude constant?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper’s “Fourier‑phase” story is not meant to claim that a diffusion model literally stores images in the frequency domain.  
What it uses is a *structural* analogy that holds for **any** linear mapping – a convolution kernel, an attention matrix or a fully‑connected layer – once we write the mapping as a sum over *directions* (basis vectors) multiplied by *lengths* (scalars).  In a 2‑D Fourier transform those scalars are the **magnitudes** $|F(k)|$ and the **phases** $\theta(k)$; in a neural layer they are respectively  

* the **norms** $\|w_i\|$ of the weight vectors $w_i$ (how much total energy each neuron contributes),  
* the **angles** $\phi_i=\angle(w_i,x)$ between a neuron and an input $x$ (how that energy is aligned with the data).

---

### 1 Why the inner product makes phase–direction explicit

For a single neuron we have  

$$
z_i = w_i^{\!\top} x
     = \|w_i\|\;\|x\|\;\cos\!\bigl(\angle(w_i,x)\bigr).
$$

The cosine term depends only on the *relative orientation* between $w_i$ and $x$; it plays exactly the role of a **phase** term in a Fourier coefficient, where  

$$
F(k)=|F(k)|\,e^{j\,\theta(k)}
   \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
f(x)=\sum_k |F(k)|\,\cos(\theta(k)+2\,π\,k·x).
$$

If we keep $\|w_i\|$ fixed and rotate $w_i$, we change $\phi_i$ while leaving the “energy’’ $\|w_i\\|$ untouched – just as one can keep $|F(k)|$ fixed while altering $\theta(k)$.

---

### 2 Orthogonal matrices rotate *all* directions together

An orthogonal matrix $R∈O(d)$ satisfies $R^{⟂}R=I$.  
Multiplying every column (or row) of a weight matrix $W^{0}\inℝ^{d\times n}$ by the same $R$,  

$$
W=RW^{0},
$$

has three immediate consequences:

1. **Norm preservation** $\displaystyle \|Rw_{i}\|=\|w_{i}\|$.  
   This is exactly “keep the magnitude spectrum”.

2. **Angle preservation among neurons** $\displaystyle 
   ⟨Rw_{i},Rw_{j}⟩
   =⟨w_{i},w_{j}⟩$.  
   Hence every pairwise cosine similarity—and consequently the *hyperspherical energy*

   $$
   {\rm HE}(W)=
   \sum_{i≠j}\bigl\|\widehat{w}_{i}-\widehat{w}_{j}\bigr\}^{-1},
   \qquad
   \widehat{w}_{i}= {\frac{w_{i}}{\|w_{i}\|}},
   $$

   —remains unchanged. Preserving these angles mirrors preserving **relative phases** among Fourier coefficients.

3. **Invertibility** Since $R^{-1}=R^{⊤}$, no information is lost; we have merely changed the coordinate system in which neurons are expressed.

Thus an orthogonal fine‑tuning step amounts precisely to “changing only phase’’ while “leaving magnitude untouched”.

---

### 3 How this manifests inside a diffusion model

A Stable‑Diffusion UNet consists mainly of  

* Linear layers ($W^{0}x+b^{0}$),  
* Multi‑head self‑attention ($QK\,W^{V⊤}$),

both of which are built from inner products similar to $w^{⊤}x$.  
During denoising at timestep $t$,  

```python
h_t = f_{\theta}(z_t , c )
```

where $f_{\theta}$ contains many such linear maps.  
If we replace each map by its orthogonally transformed version $RW^{0}$, then for any intermediate activation vector $a_t$

```python
(RW^{0})ᵀ a_t = (W^{0})ᵀ (Rᵀ a_t),
```

so **the net effect equals rotating every activation before it meets the pre‑trained weights**. The rotation does not alter any singular values of those weights—those singular values are analogous to Fourier magnitudes—so overall *energy distribution* across channels stays exactly as before training started. What does change is how each channel aligns with the data, i.e. its effective “phase”.

Because diffusion sampling repeatedly feeds back activations through these same rotated linear maps, semantic aspects tied to those alignments can be steered (e.g., making a specific subject appear more often or obeying a control map), while the global statistical properties learned during pre‑training—textures, colours, overall realism—remain intact thanks to the preserved hyperspherical structure.

---

### 4 Why plain linear updates do not enjoy this property

Updating weights with ordinary SGD changes both norms ***and*** directions independently:

```python
W ← W − η ∇_W L .
```

Consequently  

* $\displaystyle \|w_{i}\|$ may drift far from its original value → “magnitude drift”.  
* Pairwise dot products $\displaystyle⟨w_{i},w_{j}⟩$ change → “phase drift”.

The Euclidean distance $\displaystyle \|W−W^{0}\|_F$ alone cannot capture whether we have destroyed those angular relationships, which—as experiments in the paper show—correlates tightly with loss of pretrained generation quality. Orthogonal fine‑tuning eliminates both drifts by construction, providing the stability observed empirically (fast convergence, no collapse).

---

### 5 Limits of the analogy

* **Fourier ↔ neural basis:** The analogy works because any linear operator can be written as a sum over an orthonormal basis (e.g., via SVD $W=UΣV^{⊤}$). The singular values play the role of magnitudes, whereas $U,V$ encode orientations—precisely what an orthogonal transformation reshapes.
* **Nonlinearities:** After ReLU / GeLU etc., strict separation into magnitude / phase no longer holds exactly, but these nonlinearities act pointwise, leaving the *directional bias* introduced by $R\,W^{0}$ largely intact.
* **Higher‑dimensional data:** “Phase encodes spatial shift’’ only for sinusoidal bases; for generic learned bases it encodes *relative alignment* with inputs, which is what matters for semantics in high‑dimensional feature spaces. Hence there is no mismatch—the notion of “phase’’ simply generalises from spatial translations to *any angular relationship* between data and filter.

---

### 6 Bottom line

Changing neuron directions through a **layer‑shared orthogonal matrix** does,
in precisely the same way as swapping only Fourier phases:

* it keeps every neuron's norm—the analogue of spectral *magnitude*—exactly as it was,
* it leaves all pairwise angles among neurons unchanged—analogous to preserving *relative phases*, which means that hyperspherical energy stays constant,
* it therefore retains most of the pretrained knowledge (global texture,
  colour statistics …) while providing enough freedom ($R$) to steer semantics toward downstream tasks (subject identity or control signals).

Consequently, orthogonal fine‑tuning can be viewed as “phase‑only’’ editing of a diffusion model’s linear operators—a principled way of separating semantic control from destructive alteration of learned generative priors.
