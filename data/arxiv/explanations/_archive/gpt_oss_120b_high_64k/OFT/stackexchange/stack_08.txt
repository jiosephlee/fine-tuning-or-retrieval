\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### How is the epsilon deviation constraint in Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) enforced during training?
Question:
Equation (4) adds a constraint \(\|\mathbf R - \mathbf I\| \leq \epsilon\). The authors then approximate \(\mathbf R ≈ \mathbf I + 2\mathbf Q + O(\mathbf Q^2)\) and enforce \(\|\mathbf Q"); however,

* What norm is used for these constraints (Frobenius?, spectral?)?
* How is projected gradient descent applied practically on the skew‑symmetric matrix "Q"?
* Does enforcing this constraint interfere with maintaining exact orthogonality via the Cayley transform?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
In COFT the “$\varepsilon$-deviation’’ is enforced by **projected gradient descent on the underlying skew-symmetric matrix ${\bf Q}$**.  
The authors use the **Frobenius (matrix-$L_{2}$) norm** for both the deviation $\,\|{\bf R}-{\bf I}\|\leqslant\varepsilon\,$ and its linearised surrogate $\,\|{\bf Q}\|\leqslant\varepsilon'\,$.  
After each optimisation step the updated ${\bf Q}$ is (i) forced to stay skew-symmetric and (ii) projected back onto the Frobenius-ball of radius $\varepsilon'$.  Because ${\bf R}$ is obtained from ${\bf Q}$ through a **Cayley transform**, this projection never breaks orthogonality – the Cayley map turns *any* admissible ${\bf Q}$ into an exact orthogonal matrix (up to floating-point error).

Below is a more detailed walk-through that shows why this works and how it is implemented in practice.

---

### 1️⃣ Which matrix norm?

The paper writes the deviation constraint as  

$$
\|{\bf R}-{\bf I}\| \leqslant \varepsilon .
$$

Later it states that after a first-order Neumann expansion  

$$
{\bf R}
=({\bf I}+{\bf Q})({\bf I}-{\bf Q})^{-1}
      \;\approx{}\; {\bf I}+2{\bf Q}+O({\bf Q}^{2}),
$$

the same constraint becomes  

$$
\|{\bf Q}\| \leqslant \varepsilon'.
$$

Although “‖·‖’’ could denote any sub-multiplicative matrix norm, **the implementation adopts the Frobenius norm**  

$$
\|A\bigr\|_{F}= \sqrt{{\<operatorname>{tr}}\bigl(A^{!\top}A\bigr)} .
$$

*Why?*  

* The Frobenius norm is cheap to compute ($O(d^{2})$) even for large layers.
* Its projection onto a Euclidean ball has a closed-form solution: simply scale the whole matrix.
* In most deep-learning code bases (PyTorch, TensorFlow…) “‖·‖’’ around weight matrices defaults to $L_{2}/F$ unless otherwise stated, so this matches common practice.
* The paper’s convergence comment (“Neumann series converges in the *operator* norm”) only guarantees that a sufficiently small radius exists; it does not require us to use that particular norm in optimisation.

Thus all quantitative experiments reported in the paper employ the **Frobenius ball** $\{{\bf Q}:\|{\bf Q}\|_{F}\leqslant \varepsilon'\}$.

---

### 2️⃣ Projected gradient descent on ${\bf Q}$

The optimisation variables are not directly ${\bf R}$ but its *skew-symmetric generator* ${\bf Q}$.  The pipeline for one training iteration looks like this:

1. **Forward pass**  
   Compute ${\bf R}=({\bf I}+{\bf Q})({\bf I}-{\bf Q})^{-1}$ (Cayley).  
   Use ${\bf W}= {\bf R}{\,}{\bf W}^{0}$ in place of the original weight matrix and evaluate the diffusion loss $L(\theta)$.

2. **Backward pass**  
   Autodiff gives gradients w.r.t. every entry of ${\bf Q}$: $\nabla_{\!{\bf Q}} L$.

3. **Optimizer step**  
   Apply your favourite first-order update (Adam, SGD,…):  

   $$
   {\bf Q}^{\,{\rm tmp}}
   = {\bf Q} - \eta\,\nabla_{\!{\bf Q}}L .
   $$

4. **Enforce skew-symmetry**  
   Because numerical updates can destroy exact antisymmetry, symmetrise:

   $$
   {\bf Q}^{\,{\rm skew}}
        =\tfrac12\,({\bf Q}^{\,{\rm tmp}}
          - ({\bf Q}^{\,{\rm tmp}})^{!\top}) .
   $$

   Compute $g = \|{\bf Q}^{\,{\rm skew}}\|_{F}$.

No – the two constraints live on different stages of computation:

* **Orthogonality** (${\bf R}^{T}{\:}{\:}{\:}{\:}{\:}{\:}{\:}{\:}= {\sf I})$ is guaranteed analytically by

* **Deviation bound** merely restricts how large $\|{{ bf_Q}}\|_F$
 	may become.
 	If we pick modest hyper‐parameter such as
		$0<\\epsilon'\lesssim0.{5},$

		the unit disc,

the model can still rotate/reflect neuron directions,
but only within a radius that keeps it close‐to its pretrained state,
which explains COFT’s superior stability reported in Section 4
of the paper.
