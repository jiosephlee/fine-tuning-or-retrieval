\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

# DPO vs. PPO: What Real‑World Experiments Reveal About Performance and Stability  

*An empirical look at Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) versus the classic PPO‑based RLHF pipeline across sentiment control, summarization, and single‑turn dialogue.*

---

## Introduction  

Fine‑tuning large language models (LLMs) so that they obey human preferences has become synonymous with **RL from Human Feedback** (RLHF). The de‑facto standard employs **proximal policy optimization** (PPO): first fit a reward model on pairwise human judgments, then run a reinforcement‑learning loop that pushes the LM toward high‑reward completions while staying close to its original “reference” policy through a KL penalty.

While effective, this pipeline is heavyweight:

* It needs *two* separate models (the LM and an auxiliary reward network).  
* Training requires *sampling* from the LM on every update step—costly for multi‑billion‑parameter models.  
* Hyperparameters such as KL targets, learning rates for the value head, and reward normalisation demand careful tuning.  
* In practice PPO can be unstable—policy collapse or high variance gradients are common complaints.

**Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** proposes a radical simplification: rewrite the RLHF objective so that **the policy itself encodes the reward**, allowing us to train directly with a binary cross‑entropy loss on preference pairs—no extra reward model, no RL loop.

Below we walk through what happens when this idea meets real data. Across three representative tasks—sentiment steering, Reddit TL;DR summarization, and single‑turn dialogue—we see that DPO matches or exceeds PPO’s quality while being far more stable and computationally lightweight.

---

## From RLHF to Direct Preference Optimization  

Traditional RLHF solves

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\;
\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_\theta}\!\big[r_\phi(x,y)\big]\;
-\;\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big),
\tag{1}
\]

where \(r_\phi\) is a learned reward model and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is usually an SFT checkpoint. The optimal policy under this KL‑constrained objective takes a closed form:

\[
\pi^*(y|x)=\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
            \exp\!\big(r(x,y)/\beta\big)}
               {Z(x)}, 
\qquad Z(x)=\sum_{y'} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)
            \exp\!\big(r(x,y')/\beta\big).
\tag{2}
\]

If we rearrange Eq. (2), **the reward can be expressed directly via the policy ratio**

\[
r(x,y)= \beta\,\log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta\,\log Z(x).
\tag{3}
\]

The Bradley–Terry preference model depends only on differences \(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\), so the partition term \(Z(x)\) cancels out when we plug Eq. (3) into

\[
p(y_1 \succ y_2 | x) = 
\sigma(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)).
\]

Consequently **the likelihood of human preferences can be written solely in terms of the policy** \(\pi_\theta\) and its reference \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). Optimizing this likelihood yields the **DPO loss**

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta)=-
    \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)} 
    \Big[
      \log\sigma
      \Big(
        \beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
        -
        \beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
      \Big)
    \Big]
}.
\tag{4}
\]

Training now reduces to **standard supervised learning** on binary labels—no sampling, no value network—while still solving exactly the same constrained optimization problem that PPO tackles.

## Benchmarks Under the Lens  

All experiments share a common pipeline:

1. **Collect preference pairs** *(prompt \(x$, two completions \(y_w$, \(y_l$), human label “\(y_w ≻ y_l$)*).  
2. **Fix** a reference LM (\(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)) – typically an SFT checkpoint trained on high‑quality data.  
3. **Minimize** Eq. (4) w.r.t.\(\theta.\)

The following sections report how this simple recipe performs against strong baselines—including full PPO implementations that learn an explicit reward model from exactly the same preference data.

### Controlled Sentiment Generation  

*Task*: Continue an IMDb review prefix such that the continuation carries **positive** sentiment.  
*Ground truth*: A sentiment classifier supplies an oracle reward used only for evaluation (not for training).

**Findings**

| Method | Reward (↑) | KL ⟂ Reference (↓) |
|--------|------------|-------------------|
| PPO + learned reward | ‑ | ‑ |
| PPO + ground‑truth reward *(PPO‑GT)* | — |
| **DPO** | **Strictly dominates both** | — |

Across eleven different hyperparameter settings (target KL ranging 3–12 for PPO; β ∈ {0.05,…,5} for DPO), *every* point on DPO’s frontier lies above all PPO points—meaning higher positive‐sentiment scores **for equal or lower KL divergence** from the reference policy. Even when PPO enjoys perfect knowledge of the true sentiment reward, it cannot catch up.

> *Takeaway*: The KL‐penalised tradeoff central to RLHF is captured perfectly by Eq. (4); DPO extracts it without ever running an RL algorithm.


### Summarization on Reddit TL;DR  

*Task*: Given a Reddit post (\(x\)), generate a concise TL;DR summary (\(y\)). Human annotators have already labeled pairs of candidate summaries as preferred/dispreferred (`Stiennon et al., 2022`).  

*Metric*: Win rate against reference TL;DR summaries as judged by GPT‑4 (validated later by humans).

**Results**

* At temperature 0 (the “greedy” regime)  
  * **DPO:** ≈ 61 % win rate  
  * **PPO:** ≈ 57 % win rate (best temperature = 0)  
* At higher temperatures (> 0.5) `PPO`’s performance degrades toward the base GPT‑J model, whereas **D PO remains robust**, showing only modest declines up to temperature 1.
* **Best‑of‑N** sampling—selecting the highest‐scoring among N candidates from the SFT model—still trails behind D PO’s single forward pass despite using many extra decode steps.


### Single‑Turn Dialogue with Anthropic HH  

*Task*: Produce helpful & harmless replies (`Helpfulness + Harmlessness` dataset). No dedicated SFT checkpoint existed; researchers first built a “reference” LM via Preferred‐FT on chosen completions only.

**Observations**

* **D PO** matches or exceeds the *Best‑of‑128* baseline—a method that samples dozens of replies per query and keeps only the top scorer from an external learned reward model.
* A publicly available **PPO‑trained HH model** could not be tuned to beat its base Pythia‑2.8B language model at any temperature—a stark contrast to D PO’s clear gains.
* Convergence curves show D PO reaches its peak performance after only a few thousand update steps, whereas PPO requires substantially longer training cycles.


### Out‐of‐Distribution Test on CNN/DailyMail  

To probe generalisation beyond its training distribution (`Reddit` → `News`), both models were evaluated on CNN/DailyMail articles using GPT‑4 win rates against gold summaries:

* **D PO** retained its advantage over PPO by a “significant margin” despite never seeing news articles during fine‑tuning.
* This suggests that learning directly from preferences does not sacrifice robustness under distribution shift—a common concern when removing an explicit reward network.


### Human Validation of GPT‑4 Evaluations  

Because most quantitative tables rely on GPT‑4 judgments, a small human study was conducted:

* Two prompting styles were tested (“simple” vs “concise”).  
* Human–GPT4 agreement rates were comparable to inter‐human agreement (≈ 70 % consistency).  
* Consequently GPT‑4 serves as a reliable proxy here, reinforcing confidence in all reported win rates.

## Why D PO Stays Stable When PPO Wobbles  

1️⃣ **Importance weighting built into the loss** – The gradient includes  
   \[
   \sigma(\hat r(y_l)-\hat r(y_w))
   = 
   \frac{1}{1+\exp[\hat r(y_w)-\hat r(y_l)]},
   \]
   where \(\hat r = \beta\, \log {\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}}}\).
   This factor automatically downweights examples already correctly ordered by the current policy and upweights those where it errs—preventing degenerate updates seen in naïve unlikelihood training.

2️⃣ **No value function / baseline needed** – In standard actor–critic approaches an estimated value baseline reduces variance but introduces another moving target that can destabilise learning if misestimated. Eq. (4) sidesteps this entirely because the KL term serves as an analytically exact regulariser rather than an estimated critic.

3️⃣ **Closed-form optimality** – By construction Eq. (2) guarantees that minimizing Eq. (4) yields *the exact optimal KL‑constrained policy*. There is no approximation gap between objective and optimizer—the source of many PPO pathologies such as premature clipping or clipping ratio collapse.

## Computational Footprint – Less Is More  

| Component | Traditional RLHF (PPO) | Direct Preference Optimization |
|-----------|-----------------------|--------------------------------|
| Reward network training | ✔︎ Separate phase → extra GPU hours | ✘ Not needed |
| Sampling during fine–tuning | ✔︎ Required each gradient step → high latency | ✘ No sampling |
| Hyperparameter search | KL target, clip range, value loss weight,… | Only β (often set once per task) |
| Training time (per epoch) | ≈ 2×–5× longer due to Monte Carlo rollouts & critic updates | ≈ 1× — just standard cross entropy passes |

Because Eq. (4) is just a binary classification loss over already collected pairs, existing pipelines can replace their RL stage with one line of code (`torch.nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss`) and continue using all familiar optimiser tricks (`AdamW`, learning-rate schedules). In practice this translates into *substantial GPU savings*, especially when scaling beyond 6B parameters—the authors demonstrate comparable results up through 6B models without any tuned tricks.

## Bottom Line – When Should You Reach For D PO?  

*If you already have—or can cheaply generate—a batch of *pairwise human judgments*, try D PO first.* It delivers:

* **Higher or equal quality** across sentiment steering, summarisation & dialogue,
* A clearer tradeoff curve between achieving desired behaviour (`reward`) and staying faithful to your base LM (`KL`),
* **Robustness** across decoding temperatures,
* Minimal engineering overhead (**no extra networks**, no sampling loops),
* Faster iteration cycles crucial when experimenting with new prompts or datasets.

PPO still has niche uses—for instance when you need *online* preference collection or want fine‐grained control over stochastic exploration policies—but for most offline alignment workflows where preferences are pre­collected, Direct Preference Optimization appears to be both simpler *and* stronger.


---

## Quick Takeaways  

- **Performance:** Across three real-world tasks D PO equals or beats state‐of‐the‐art PPO trained on identical preference data (+≈ 4 % gain in summarisation win rate).  
- **Stability:** The built-in weighting term prevents collapse; no separate value head nor baselines are required → smoother training curves & fewer hyperparameters.  
- **Efficiency:** Removes both reward model fitting and costly rollout generation → roughly half-to-one-quarter of GPU cost relative to conventional RLHF pipelines.  
- **Generalisation:** Outperforms PPO even on an unseen news domain, hinting at comparable OOD robustness despite ignoring an explicit reward function.  
- **Scalability:** Demonstrated up to 6B parameters with minimal tuning; theory suggests straightforward extension to much larger LMs.

In short, Direct Preference Optimization rewrites the script for alignment research: instead of “train a policy *against* a learned reward”, we now train “a policy *that *is​the​reward”. If you’re looking for a leaner path from human preference data to aligned language models, D PO is worth your attention today—and likely will become the default building block tomorrow.
