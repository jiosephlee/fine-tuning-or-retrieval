# The Math Made Easy: How DPO Derives an Optimal Policy in Closed Form  

*In this post we unpack the core mathematical trick behind Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). We’ll see how a seemingly complex reinforcement‑learning problem collapses into a simple binary‑cross‑entropy loss—no sampling, no extra reward network required.*

---

## 1. Why RLHF feels heavyweight  

Large language models (LLMs) are first trained on massive amounts of text without any notion of “good” or “bad” behaviour. To make them follow human preferences we usually do **RL from Human Feedback** (RLHF):

1. **Supervised fine‑tuning** produces a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (often called SFT).  
2. Human annotators compare two completions $y_1$ and $y_2$ for the same prompt $x$, providing a preference $y_w \succ y_l$.  
3. A **reward model** $r_{\phi}(x,y)$ is fit to these preferences using a Bradley–Terry likelihood.  
4. Finally a reinforcement‑learning algorithm (typically PPO) maximizes the expected reward while keeping the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$ close to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ via a KL penalty:

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\;\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \pi_{\theta}}\!\big[r_{\phi}(x,y)\big]}_{\text{reward}}
\;\;-\;\;\beta\,\underbrace{\operatorname{KL}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big)}_{\text{stay near reference}} .
\tag{1}
\]

Because $r_{\phi}$ lives on discrete sequences, we can’t differentiate through it directly; PPO therefore samples many continuations, estimates gradients with high variance, and needs careful hyper‑parameter tuning.  

**The big question:** *Can we solve (1) analytically instead of resorting to noisy RL?*  

---

## 2. A closed‑form solution for the KL‑constrained problem  

The constrained maximization problem above has been studied under “control as inference”. By introducing a Lagrange multiplier for the KL term and taking functional derivatives with respect to $\pi$, one obtains an exact optimal distribution:

\[
\boxed{\;
\pi^{*}(y|x)=
\frac{
 \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
 \exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\big)
}{
 Z(x)
}\;}
\tag{2}
\]

where  

* $Z(x)=\displaystyle \sum_{y'} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y')\big)$ normalises the probabilities for each prompt $x$.  

Equation (2) tells us **exactly** what the optimal policy looks like once we know the reward function $r$—no iterative RL needed.  

---

## 3. Turning rewards into policies  

We can rearrange (2) to express the unknown reward directly through the policies:

1. Take logs on both sides of (2):
   \[
   \log \pi^{*}(y|x)=
   \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)+
   \tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)- 
   \log Z(x).
   \]  
2. Solve for $r(x,y)$:
   \[
   r(x,y)=
   \beta\,\Big[\log\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
   +\log Z(x)\Big].
   \tag{3}
   \]

Notice that **$\log Z(x)$ depends only on the prompt** $x$, not on any particular completion $y$. This observation will be crucial because human preference models such as Bradley–Terry care only about *differences* of rewards between two answers:

\[
p^{*}(y_1 \succ y_2 | x)
=
\sigma\!\big(r^{*}(x,y_1)-r^{*}(x,y_2)\big),
\qquad 
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} .
\tag{4}
\]

If we plug (3) into the difference $r^{*}(x,y_1)-r^{*}(x,y_2)$,
the unwanted term $\log Z(x)$ cancels out:

\[
r^{*}(x,y_1)-r^{*}(x,y_2)
=
\beta\, 
      \Big(
      \log\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y_1|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1|x)}
      -
      \log\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y_2|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2|x)}
      \Big).
\]

Therefore the **human preference probability can be written purely in terms of policies**:

\[
p^{*}(y_1 \succ y_2 | x)
=
\sigma
    \Big(
    \beta\, 
    \big[
    \log\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y_2|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2|x)}
    -
    \log\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y_1|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1|x)}
    \big]
    \Big).
    \tag{5}
\]

Now we have eliminated any explicit reference to an unknown reward function or its partition function!

---

## 4. From pairwise preferences straight to a loss over $\boldsymbol{\theta}$  

Suppose our language model is parametrised as $\pi_\theta(y|x)$. Treating $(5)$ as a likelihood for observed human choices lets us write down a **binary cross‑entropy loss** that directly trains $\theta$:

\[
\boxed{
%
L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)
=
-
\,\E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim {\cal D}}\!
  \Big[
     \log\sigma
        \Big(
          \beta\, 
          \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
          -
          \beta\, 
          \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
        \Big)
  \Big]
%
}
\tag{6}
\]

where ${\cal D}$ contains triples “prompt $x$, preferred completion $y_w$, dispreferred completion $y_l$”.  

Key observations:

* The inner term is simply **the log‑odds ratio** between how much more likely our current model thinks `$y_w$` is compared with `$y_l$`, measured *relative* to the reference policy.
* The outer logistic $\sigma$ turns that log‑odds into a probability that matches human judgement.
* Optimising (6) is exactly what supervised binary classification does—hence **no reinforcement learning loop** is required.

---

## 5. What does the gradient actually do?  

Differentiating (6) with respect to $\theta$ yields

\[
\nabla_\theta L_{\text{DPO}}
=
-\beta\, 
\E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim {\cal D}}
     [
       s_{wl}\;
       (\nabla_\theta \!\log \pi_\theta(y_w|x)
        -   
       \nabla_\theta  \!\log      π_\theta(y_l|x))
     ],
\tag{7}
\]

with  

\[
s_{wl}= 
\sigma\( 
      {\hat r}_θ(x,y_l)-{\hat r}_θ(x,y_w)
      \),
  
{\hat r}_θ(x,y)=
β\, 
         ∗  ∖! ∖!∖!    \
         ∧   log \\!
                ∉   ..
                 …????

Well... Actually let’s clarify more gently:

Define an implicit “reward” induced by our current policy:

```
ŕθ(x,y) = β·log[ πθ(y│x) / π_ref(y│x) ] .
```

Then `s_{wl}` becomes `σ( ŕθ(x, y_l) – ŕθ(x, y_w) )`.  
Interpretation:

* If the model already assigns higher implicit reward to `$y_w$` (`ŕθ(w)>ŕθ(l)`), `s_{wl}` is close to zero → tiny update.
* If it gets the ordering wrong (`ŕθ(l)>ŕθ(w)`), `s_{wl}` ≈ 1 → strong push **up** `πθ(y_w)` and **down** `πθ(y_l)` .  
* The factor β scales how aggressively we move toward satisfying preferences while respecting the KL constraint encoded implicitly by using `π_ref` as baseline.

Thus DPO’s gradient automatically focuses learning effort where it matters most—exactly what PPO tries to achieve through high‑variance sampling and separate baselines—but does it analytically within one clean expectation.

---

## 6. Why this beats standard PPO—in theory  

Standard actor‑critic approaches approximate the same objective (1). They need:

* **Sampling** from $\pi_\theta$ at each step → expensive for large LMs.
* An estimate of $Z(x)$ or a value function baseline → noisy gradients and extra parameters.
* Careful tuning of learning rates, clipping thresholds, KL penalties etc.

DPO sidesteps all three by **absorbing** those components into a single supervised loss:

| Feature | PPO | DPO |
|---------|-----|-----|
| Reward model | Separate network trained first | Implicitly defined by policy |
| Sampling during training | Yes (rollouts) | No |
| KL control | Explicit penalty + clipping | Built into log‑ratio |
| Hyper‑parameter burden | High (λ​KL, clipping ε, learning rates…) | Only β (strength of KL anchor) |

Empirically this translates into faster convergence and more stable training—exactly what the paper reports across sentiment control, summarisation and dialogue tasks.

## 7. A one‑line training loop  

Putting everything together gives an almost trivial pipeline:

```python
# Pseudocode
π_ref = load_sft_model()                 # frozen reference LM
π_theta = copy_of(π_ref).requires_grad_(True)

optimizer = Adam(π_theta.parameters(), lr=…)

for batch in preference_dataset:          # each entry = (x, y_w, y_l)
    # Compute log probabilities under current and reference policies
    logp_ref_w = π_ref.logprob(x, y_w)
    logp_ref_l = π_ref.logprob(x, y_l)
    logp_cur_w = π_theta.logprob(x, y_w)
    logp_cur_l = π_theta.logprob(x, y_l)

    # Build DPO objective Eq.(6)
    logits = β * ((logp_cur_w - logp_ref_w) - (logp_cur_l - logp_ref_l))
    loss = -F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logits, torch.ones_like(logits))

    optimizer.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()
```

No rollouts, no auxiliary value head—just ordinary stochastic gradient descent on a binary classification loss!

---

## 8. Bottom line  

* **Closed‑form optimal policy:** Equation (2) tells us precisely how a KL‑constrained RL objective should look once we know any reward function.
* **Preference likelihood becomes binary cross entropy:** By reparameterising rewards as log‑ratios relative to a frozen reference LM we eliminate both the unknown partition function and any separate reward network.
* **Training simplifies dramatically:** We replace PPO’s expensive sampling loop with ordinary supervised learning over pairwise comparisons—still optimizing exactly the same objective that RLHF targets.
* **Performance holds up:** Experiments in the original DPO paper show matching or superior win rates on sentiment control, summarisation and single‑turn dialogue while being orders of magnitude cheaper to run.

In short, DPO shows that *your language model was already hiding its own reward function*. We just needed to ask it politely—by comparing its own output probabilities against a trusted baseline—and let binary cross entropy do the rest.
