\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why does DPO outperform PPO even when PPO has access to ground‑truth rewards (PPO‑GT)?
Question:
In Figure 2 (sentiment experiment), DPO dominates PPO‑GT on both reward and KL axes despite PPO‑GT using the true sentiment classifier as reward.
* Is this purely due to variance reduction from avoiding sampling in PPO?
* Does DPO benefit from an exact analytic gradient whereas PPO relies on REINFORCE style estimates?
* Could any hyper‑parameter tuning (e.g., learning rates, baselines) close this gap for PPO?
I’d like to understand which part of PPO’s pipeline causes its inefficiency relative to DPO.

Answer:
**Short answer:**  
DPO beats PPO‑GT because it turns the RLHF objective into an ordinary supervised‐learning problem whose gradients are *exact* and *low‑variance*. In contrast, PPO still has to estimate a policy‐gradient (the REINFORCE term) and an advantage (a value‐function baseline) from Monte‑Carlo samples, which introduces unavoidable stochastic error even when the reward itself is noise‑free. The extra variance together with the extra bias introduced by clipping / KL‑penalty surrogates makes PPO learn more slowly and settle at a worse reward–KL trade‑off. The authors verified this by sweeping a wide range of learning rates, KL targets, clip‑ε values and other hyper‑parameters; none of those settings erased the gap, indicating that the inefficiency is intrinsic to the actor–critic pipeline rather than a matter of poor tuning.

Below I unpack each of those points in more detail, following the reasoning given in the paper.



### 1. What both methods are optimizing  

Both algorithms start from the same constrained optimisation problem:

$$
\max_{\pi_\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}\!\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr]
-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl[\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)\,\|\,
      \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr].
$$  

When we know the *true* sentiment classifier $r(x,y)$ (the “ground‐truth’’ reward used in the sentiment experiment), Eq.(1) becomes an exact target for both methods.



### 2. How PPO solves (1)

PPO treats Eq.(1) as a **reinforcement‑learning** problem:

* It samples trajectories $(x,y)$ from the current policy $\pi_\theta$.
* It computes an **advantage estimate**

$$
\hat A(x,y)=r(x,y)-V_{\psi}(x),
$$  

where $V_{\psi}$ is a learned value network that tries to approximate $\mathbb{E}_{y'\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}}[r(x,y')]$.  

* The policy update uses a surrogate loss

$$
L_{\text{PPO}}=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[
      \min!\bigl(
     \rho_\theta\,\hat A,\;
      \operatorname{clip}(\rho_\theta,\!1-\epsilon,\!1+\epsilon)\,\hat A
      \bigr)
   \Bigr],
   \qquad
  \rho_\theta=\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\textold}(y|x)} .
$$  

Both steps are **stochastic**:  
the Monte‐Carlo sample $y$ introduces variance into $r(x,y)$;  
the learned baseline $V_{\psi}$ adds bias whenever it is inaccurate;  
and the clipping operator further distorts the true gradient unless $\epsilon$ happens to be optimal.  

Even when we replace $r$ by a perfect sentiment classifier (PPO‑GT), we still have to *estimate* the advantage from finite samples and a learned baseline. The authors point out (§ 5.2) that this “soft value function’’ term,

$$
f(r,\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta)=r(x,y)-\beta\,\log
   \sum_{y'}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)\,
          \exp\bigr(r(x,y')/\beta\bigr),
$$  

is essential for low variance but must be **approximated** (typically by a separate critic). Any mis‐estimate propagates directly into the policy gradient.
