# The Attention Bottleneck: Why Transformers Struggle with Long Contexts

Transformer models have become the de‑facto standard for natural language processing, code generation, vision‑language tasks, and more. At the heart of every Transformer lies **self‑attention**—a mechanism that lets each token look at every other token in a sequence and decide how much “attention” it should pay to them. While this idea is elegant and powerful, it comes with a hidden cost that grows dramatically as we try to feed longer sequences into the model. In this post we unpack the mathematics behind self‑attention, explain why its **quadratic** cost creates a bottleneck for long contexts, and glimpse how recent systems such as **FlashAttention‑3** are beginning to break through that barrier.

---

## 1. Self‑Attention in a Nutshell

Given an input sequence of length $N$ and a hidden size $d$, a single attention head first projects the input into three matrices:

| Symbol | Meaning | Shape |
|--------|---------|-------|
| $\mathbf{Q}$ | queries | $\mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ |
| $\mathbf{K}$ | keys    | $\mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ |
| $\mathbf{V}$ | values  | $\mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ |

The attention computation proceeds in three steps:

1. **Score matrix**  
   $$\mathbf{S} = \alpha\,\mathbf{Q}\,\mathbf{K}^\top,$$  
   where $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{d}$ rescales the dot‑product.
   $\mathbf{S}$ has shape $N\times N$—every token now has a raw similarity score toward every other token.

2. **Softmax** (row‑wise)  
   $$\mathbf{P} = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$$  
   This turns raw scores into probabilities that sum to one across each row.

3. **Weighted sum**  
   $$\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{V},$$  
   producing the output representations $\mathbf{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$.

The same pattern repeats across multiple heads and batches, then the heads are concatenated and projected back into the model’s hidden space.

---

## 2. Where Quadratic Scaling Comes From

The dominant work in attention is the two matrix multiplications:
- $\mathbf{Q}\,\mathbf{K}^\top$ computes an $N\times N$ score matrix.
- $\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{V}$ multiplies that $N\times N$ matrix by another $N\times d$ matrix.

Both multiplications involve $O(N^2 d)$ floating‑point operations (FLOPs). Moreover:

* **Memory footprint** – Storing $\mathbf{S}$ or $\mathbf{P}$ explicitly needs $N^2$ entries.
* **Data movement** – Even if we could compute on‑the‑fly, each block of $\mathbf{S}$ must be read/written back to global GPU memory unless we fuse operations tightly together.
* **Latency** – The softmax step needs a per‑row maximum (`rowmax`) and sum (`rowsum`) before applying exponentials—additional passes over $N^2$ data.

If you double the sequence length from $1\,024$ tokens to $2\,048$, both FLOPs *and* required memory quadruple! This quadratic explosion quickly overwhelms modern GPUs once sequences exceed a few thousand tokens—a regime increasingly needed for document‑level reasoning, source‑code understanding, high‑resolution vision tasks, or long audio streams.

---

## 3. Real‑World Consequences

Long contexts matter because many applications involve more than short sentences:

- **Document summarization** or question answering over multi‑page PDFs.
- **Code completion** where models ingest entire files or even whole repositories.
- **High‑resolution image/video** processing that translates into thousands of patches.
- **Conversational agents** preserving extensive dialogue histories or planning over long horizons.

When attention becomes the bottleneck:

1. **Training slows down dramatically**, inflating compute costs.
2 and 3️⃣ **Inference latency skyrockets**, making it impractical to serve large models in production.
4️⃣ **Memory limits are hit**, forcing developers to truncate inputs or resort to costly model parallelism tricks.

Thus reducing the quadratic burden is critical if we want Transformers to scale beyond today’s typical context windows (often $≤ 4\,096$ tokens).

---

## 4. Early Workarounds

Researchers have tackled this problem from two angles:

### Approximation Techniques
Sparse attention patterns (e.g., locality‑sensitive hashing or sliding windows) drop many entries from $\mathbf{S}$ so only $O(N\log N)$ or $O(N)$ scores are computed. While effective for some workloads, approximations may sacrifice model quality if important long‑range dependencies are pruned away.

### Software Optimizations
Even exact attention can be sped up by smarter kernels:

- **Standard attention** materializes $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ in global memory—slow due to limited DRAM bandwidth.
- **FlashAttention** introduced a tiling strategy that fuses all steps into a *single* GPU kernel, keeping intermediate results in fast shared memory and thereby eliminating most global reads/writes.
- **FlashAttention‑2** added parallelism over the sequence dimension and better load distribution across GPU streaming multiprocessors (SMs).

These tricks already gave noticeable speedups on earlier GPUs (e.g., NVIDIA Ampere). However, there remained a hidden inefficiency: on newer Hopper GPUs (e.g., H100), FlashAttention‑2 only attained roughly **35 % hardware utilization**, far below what optimized dense matrix multiplication kernels achieve (80–90 %). The gap hinted at untapped architectural features—namely *asynchrony* and *low‑precision* support—that could be leveraged more aggressively.

---

## 5. The Hardware Perspective

A modern GPU consists of several memory tiers:

1. **Global Memory (HBM)** – large capacity but relatively low bandwidth.
2. **L2 Cache**, then **Shared Memory (SMEM)** – fast on‑chip buffers accessible by all threads within an SM.
3. **Registers (RMEM)** – fastest but very limited per thread.

Computation units also specialize:

| Unit | Primary Role |
|------|---------------|
| CUDA cores | General arithmetic & integer ops |
| Tensor Cores / WGMMA | High‑throughput matrix multiplication |
| Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) | Asynchronous bulk copies between GMEM and SMEM |
| Multi‑Function Unit (MFU) | Special functions like exponentials |

Crucially:

- **Asynchrony** means data movement via TMA can proceed while Tensor Cores execute GEMM operations—no need to stall one for the other.
- **Warp specialization** lets different warps within a thread block focus exclusively on either data loading *or* computation.
- Hopper adds native support for **FP8** Tensor Core operations—roughly twice as fast as FP16/BF16 when using registers efficiently—but requires careful layout handling.

Harnessing these features demands redesigning attention kernels beyond “just fuse everything together.”

---

## 6. FlashAttention‑3: Turning Asynchrony & Low Precision Into Speed

The latest incarnation—**FlashAttention‑3**—combines three engineering ideas to finally squeeze out most of the Hopper hardware’s potential:

### 6.1 Producer–Consumer Asynchrony via Warp Specialization
Instead of letting every warp juggle both loads *and* GEMM calls, FlashAttention‑3 splits roles:
- A *producer* warp group lives solely on loading blocks of $\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}$ from global memory into SMEM using TMA.
- A *consumer* warp group immediately consumes those blocks with WGMMA Tensor Core instructions while another block is being loaded in parallel.
  
This “pipeline” hides both memory latency and instruction‐issue delays behind useful work—a classic producer‑consumer pattern now realized at warp granularity on GPUs.

### 6.2 Overlapping Softmax With Blockwise GEMMs (“Ping‑Pong” Scheduling)
Softmax involves relatively cheap compute (exponentials) compared to dense GEMMs but still consumes valuable cycles because MFU throughput is far lower than Tensor Core throughput (~4 TFLOPs vs ~1000 TFLOPs on H100). FlashAttention‑3 interleaves softmax of block $j$ with GEMM on block $j+1$:
```
(softmax j) ←→ (GEMM Q·Kᵀ j+1)
```
Using synchronization barriers (`bar.sync`), each warp group alternates between “softmax mode” and “GEMM mode,” keeping Tensor Cores busy while MFU handles exponentials concurrently—a “ping‐pong” dance that pushes overall utilization toward *75 %* of theoretical peak FP16 performance (~740 TFLOPs/s).

### 6.3 Low‐Precision FP8 With Block Quantization & Incoherent Processing
FP8 halves the precision compared with FP16 but doubles raw Tensor Core throughput (~1.2 PFLOPs/s). Naïve quantization corrupts outlier values common in large language models—producing unacceptable numerical error. FlashAttention‑3 addresses this via two tricks:

| Technique | What It Does |
|-----------|--------------|
| **Block Quantization** | Instead of one global scaling factor per tensor, each block (the same tiles used by the kernel) receives its own scale term—capturing local dynamic range without extra cost. |
| **Incoherent Processing** | Multiply $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix before quantizing; because orthogonal transforms preserve inner products ($\mathbf{Q}\!\mathbf{M}\,\big(\mathbf{K}\!\mathbf{M}\big)^\top = \mathbf{Q}\,\mathbf{K}^\top$), they spread out outlier magnitudes across many entries, reducing quantization error dramatically.|

With these steps FlashAttention‑3’s FP8 implementation achieves *2.6× lower RMSE* than a baseline per‑tensor FP8 attention while still delivering near‐PFLOP performance—a compelling tradeoff between speed and accuracy.

---

## 7. What This Means for Long Contexts

By increasing raw throughput nearly twofold (FP16) or threefold (FP8) compared with prior FlashAttention versions—and doing so without sacrificing numerical fidelity—FlashAttention‑3 effectively raises the ceiling on feasible sequence lengths:

- Training runs that previously stalled at $4\,096$ tokens can now comfortably target $8\,192$–$16\,384$, opening doors for document‐level pretraining or multi‐modal fusion.
- Inference latency stays within acceptable bounds even when serving very large inputs—a boon for code assistants or chatbots needing extended conversation history.
- Energy efficiency improves because higher utilization reduces idle cycles on expensive tensor cores; FP8 further trims power consumption per FLOP thanks to smaller arithmetic units.

In practice these gains translate into *real*, measurable time savings on H100 GPUs—a major workhorse in today's AI clusters—and set a precedent for future architectures where asynchronous execution units become standard fare.

---

## 8️⃣ Takeaway & Outlook

The quadratic nature of self‑attention has long been recognized as an obstacle when extending Transformers beyond short passages. While algorithmic approximations can trade accuracy for speed, pure engineering optimizations also play an outsized role:

1️⃣ Fusion of all attention steps eliminates unnecessary global memory traffic (the original FlashAttention insight).  
2️⃣ Exploiting hardware asynchrony through producer–consumer warp specialization hides data movement latency behind compute work ("ping‐pong" scheduling).  
3️⃣ Leveraging emerging low‐precision formats like FP8—with careful blockwise quantization and orthogonal preprocessing—doubles raw computational density while keeping errors under control.

FlashAttention‑3 showcases how marrying algorithmic awareness with modern GPU capabilities can push exact attention forward dramatically—a crucial step toward truly *long‐context* Transformers without compromising performance or precision. As newer generations of GPUs continue to expand asynchronous pipelines and ultra low‐precision operators (e.g., FP4 on upcoming Blackwell chips), we can expect further breakthroughs along this same line: smarter kernels that let every transistor do useful work while preserving—and perhaps even improving—the quality of our models’ predictions.

# From FlashAttention‑2 to FlashAttention‑3: What Changed Under the Hood?

## Introduction

Since its debut, **FlashAttention** has become the go‑to way of implementing exact self‑attention on GPUs. The original version eliminated the costly round‑trip to global memory by fusing the three attention steps—*QK* multiplication, softmax, and *PV* multiplication—into a single kernel. **FlashAttention‑2** pushed this idea further by parallelizing over the sequence dimension and improving occupancy on Ampere‑class GPUs.  

Yet when we switched to NVIDIA’s Hopper H100 GPU, FlashAttention‑2 left a lot of performance on the table: it only reached about **35 %** of the raw Tensor Core throughput, while a hand‑tuned GEMM can saturate **80–90 %**. The reason isn’t a lack of raw compute power; it’s that the algorithm still assumes a *synchronous* execution model and never exploits Hopper’s new asynchronous hardware units or its native FP8 support.

**FlashAttention‑3** rewrites the kernel from the ground up to match Hopper’s capabilities. In this post we walk through the three main algorithmic tricks that turned a modest implementation into a **1.5–2×** speedup for FP16 and close to **1.2 PFLOP/s** for FP8:

1. **Warp‑specialized producer–consumer pipelines**  
2. **Ping‑pong scheduling that overlaps softmax with blockwise GEMMs**  
3. **Block‐wise GEMM/softmax overlap (two‑stage pipeline)**  

We’ll see how each change reduces idle cycles, improves memory bandwidth utilization, and ultimately lets us squeeze more work out of each H100 SM.

---

## A Quick Recap of FlashAttention‑2

FlashAttention models multi‑head attention as three matrix operations:

\[
\mathbf{S} = \alpha \,\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top,\qquad
\mathbf{P} = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{S}),\qquad
\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V},
\]

with \(\alpha=1/\sqrt{d}\) and \(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}\) for a single head (\(N\) = sequence length, \(d\) = head dimension).  

FlashAttention‑2 tiles both *QK* and *PV* along the sequence dimension:

- The input query block \(\mathbf{Q}_i\) (\(B_r\times d\)) stays resident in shared memory.
- It streams over key/value blocks \(\{\mathbf{K}_j,\mathbf{V}_j\}_{j=0}^{T_c-1}\) (\(B_c\times d\)) one at a time.
- For each pair \((i,j)\) it computes an *SS‑GEMM* (query × keyᵀ), applies a rowwise max/subtract trick to keep softmax numerically stable, performs exponentiation/reduction locally, then does an *RS‑GEMM* (softmax × value).

All three steps run in one monolithic kernel, which already avoids writing intermediate \(\mathbf{S}\) or \(\mathbf{P}\) back to HBM. However:

- The kernel is **synchronous**: after each *SS‑GEMM* we wait for the softmax before launching the next *RS‑GEMM*.
- All warps in a CTA share the same responsibilities (load + compute).  
- Hopper’s new **Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA)** for async copies and **WGMMA** instructions for asynchronous Tensor Core launches are not used.

Because of these constraints, early stages of computation sit idle while later stages wait for data movement or special‐function units (exponential). This is where FlashAttention‑3 makes its biggest gains.

---

## FlashAttention‑3: What Changed?

### 1. Warp‑Specialized Producer–Consumer Pipelines

Hopper introduces two important primitives:

| Primitive | What it does | Why it matters |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|
| **TMA**   | Asynchronously copies data from HBM → shared memory without stalling CUDA cores | Hides global‑memory latency |
| **WGMMA**| Launches Tensor Core GEMMs that read directly from shared memory and run asynchronously | Overlaps compute with other instructions |

FlashAttention‑3 splits a CTA into two *warp groups*:  

- **Producer warpgroups** dedicate all their warps to issuing TMA loads (`Q_i`, `K_j`, `V_j`) and then signaling completion via lightweight barriers.
- **Consumer warpgroups** own all Tensor Core lanes; they pick up freshly loaded blocks from a circular shared‐memory buffer and run WGMMA kernels.

Because producer warps never execute arithmetic beyond issuing TMAs, they can discard registers that would otherwise be needed for GEMMs (`setmaxnreg`). Conversely, consumer warps can claim extra registers to keep intermediate results (`S_cur`, `P_tilde`) in registers instead of spilling to shared memory. The net effect is:

- **Memory latency is fully overlapped with Tensor Core work**, raising occupancy from ~35 % (FlashAttention‑2) toward >70 %.
- The barrier overhead is tiny compared with the cost of a global‐memory fetch or an exponential call.

### 2. Ping‑Pong Scheduling: Hiding Softmax Behind GEMMs

Even after decoupling load/calc responsibilities there remains a bottleneck: *softmax* consists mainly of exponentials and reductions whose hardware throughput is far lower than that of Tensor Cores (e.g., H100 offers ~3.9 TFLOP/s for special functions vs ~989 TFLOP/s for FP16 matrix multiply).  

FlashAttention‑3 schedules softmax work on one warp group while the other warp group is still busy executing its next *SS‑GEMM*. Concretely:

1. Warpgroup A computes `S_cur = Q_i K_jᵀ` (asynchronous WGMMA).  
2. As soon as `S_cur` lands in registers, warpgroup A starts its local softmax (`exp`, row reduction).  
3. Meanwhile warpgroup B already launched `S_next = Q_i K_{j+1}ᵀ`.  

When both groups finish their respective steps they swap roles – “ping‑pong”. Because exponentials take roughly half the runtime of an FP16 GEMM at these dimensions, keeping them busy during every Tensor Core cycle yields a noticeable uplift in measured TFLOP/s (e.g., from 570 → 620–640 TFLOP/s in FP16 with head dim 128).

The implementation uses explicit `bar.sync` instructions to enforce ordering: “all SS-GEMMs for iteration j must finish before any RS-GEMM for iteration j+1 starts”. In practice NVIDIA’s compiler respects this ordering after modest pragma annotations.

### 3. Blockwise GEMM/Softmax Overlap (Two‑Stage Pipeline)

The previous ping‑pong strategy hides *softmax* behind an *SS-GEMM*. To squeeze out even more performance FlashAttention‑3 also overlaps the second GEMM (*RS-GEMM*) with softmax from the *next* iteration—a classic producer–consumer pipeline across iterations:

| Stage | Operation | Overlapped With |
|-------|-----------|------------------|
| **Stage 0** | Compute `S_cur = Q_i K_jᵀ` | – |
| **Stage 1** | Softmax over `S_cur`, produce `P̃_cur` | While computing `O_i += P̃_cur V_{j−1}` |
| **Stage 2** | `O_i += P̃_cur V_{j−1}` (RS-GEMM) | While computing `S_next = Q_i K_{j+1}ᵀ` |

The pseudocode in Algorithm 2 shows that after launching `S_next` asynchronously we *commit but do not wait*, immediately start `O_i += P̃_cur V_{j−1}`, then later synchronize on whichever operation finishes last before moving to the next iteration. This “two‐stage” pipeline requires only one extra register buffer (`S_next`) but gives us effectively continuous Tensor Core utilization:

- Each loop iteration hides one exponentiation batch behind two independent WGMMA calls.
- Register pressure stays manageable because only one extra tile (`B_r × B_c`) lives in registers at any time.
- A three‐stage variant is possible but quickly runs out of registers on large tiles; experiments show diminishing returns beyond two stages.

---

## Low‑Precision Support: FP8 with Block Quantization & Incoherent Processing

While not strictly an “algorithmic trick,” gaining access to Hopper’s FP8 Tensor Cores was essential for hitting the **~1.2 PFLOP/s** figure reported in the paper.

Two challenges arise:

1. **Operand layout:** FP8 WGMMA only accepts k-major formatted matrices in shared memory, forcing an in‐kernel transpose of each value tile (`V_j`). FlashAttention‑3 uses LDSM/STSM instructions (the same hardware that moves data for half precision) to perform these transposes without additional memory traffic.
2. **Quantization error:** FP8’s 4‐bit exponent + 3‐bit mantissa can blow up if outliers dominate a tensor level scaling factor. The authors mitigate this by:
   - **Block quantization:** scaling each tile (`B_r × d` or `B_c × d`) independently rather than using one scalar per entire tensor.
   - **Incoherent processing:** multiplying both `Q` and `K` by a random orthogonal matrix (`±1` diagonal × Hadamard) before quantization—this spreads out extreme values while preserving \(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top\).

Together these steps deliver *2.6× lower RMSE* compared with a naïve FP8 baseline that uses per‐tensor scaling only.

---

## Performance Gains at a Glance

| Precision | Baseline (FlashAttn‑2) | FlashAttn‑3 | Speedup | TFLOP/s (peak Util.) |
|-----------|-----------------------|-------------|---------|----------------------|
| FP16      | ≤ 370 TFLOP/s         | ≤ 740 TFLOP/s | 1.5–2×   | 75 % |
| FP8       | —                     | ≈ 1.20 PFLOP/s | —     | — |

Key observations from the benchmarks:

- For sequence lengths ≥ 1k across head dimensions 64–256, FlashAttention‑3 consistently beats cuDNN’s proprietary attention kernel despite cuDNN being highly tuned for H100.
- In backward passes where five GEMMs are required per token, the same pipeline gives ≈ 1½× speedups.
- The ping-pong plus two-stage overlap accounts for most of the extra TFLOPs—removing either component drops performance back toward FlashAttention‑2 levels (≈ 570 TFLOPs/s).

---

## Takeaways

FlashAttention‑3 demonstrates that algorithmic redesign can be just as valuable as raw hardware upgrades:

1. **Separate concerns via warp specialization**—let some warps focus solely on async data movement while others drive Tensor Cores.
2. **Exploit hardware asynchrony**—use TMA + WGMMA together with lightweight barriers so that no unit sits idle waiting for another.
3. **Pipeline across iterations**—by rearranging dependencies we can overlap cheap softmax work with expensive matrix multiplies.
4. **Adapt low precision carefully**—block quantization + incoherent processing makes FP8 practical without sacrificing numerical fidelity.

The result is an attention primitive that pushes Hopper close to its theoretical FLOP ceiling while staying exact (no approximations). As GPUs continue adding async units—e.g., Blackwell’s upcoming Tensor Memory Accelerator—and newer reduced formats like FP4 appear, we expect similar design patterns will become standard practice across deep learning kernels.

In short, moving from FlashAttention‑2 to FlashAttention‑3 is less about “more cores” and more about “more clever orchestration”. By letting producers feed consumers through asynchronous pipelines and by carefully hiding low-throughput operations behind high-throughput GEMMs, we finally let modern GPUs do what they were built for: keep every arithmetic unit busy while shuffling data silently in the background.



# Asynchrony on Hopper GPUs: Turning Latency Into Speed  

*How the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) and asynchronous Tensor Cores let FlashAttention‑3 hide memory and compute latencies using a producer‑consumer warp model.*

---

## The latency problem in exact attention  

When a Transformer computes self‑attention it must form the score matrix  

\[
\mathbf{S}= \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\;\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top},
\qquad
\mathbf{P}= \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{S}),\qquad
\mathbf{O}= \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V},
\]

with $\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$.  
Even though the matrix‑multiplications are “just” GEMMs, the quadratic $N^2$ term makes attention dominate both runtime and memory traffic. 

**FlashAttention‑2** tackled this by fusing the three ops into a single kernel so that intermediate matrices never touch global memory. The trick worked well on older GPUs but only achieved about **35 %** compute utilization on NVIDIA’s Hopper H100—far below the ~80 % hit rate of a handcrafted GEMM kernel.  

Why? Two things changed on Hopper:

1. **Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA)** – a dedicated unit that moves data between HBM (global memory) and shared memory *asynchronously*.
2. **Asynchronous Tensor Core WMMA** – the new `WGMMA` instruction can start a matrix multiply while still fetching its operands from shared memory; it does not stall the rest of the warp.

If we can schedule data movement **and** computation so they overlap, those hidden latencies disappear and the kernel finally runs at its theoretical speed.

---

## Hopper’s async building blocks  

| Component | What it does | Why it helps |
|-----------|--------------|--------------|
| **TMA**   | Issues non‑blocking loads/stores between global memory (HBM) and shared memory (SMEM). | The GPU can keep doing work while a block of $\mathbf{K}$ or $\mathbf{V}$ streams in. |
| **Tensor Core (`WGMMA`)** | Performs a GEMM directly from SMEM operands without waiting for previous instructions. | Matrix multiply becomes a fire‑and‑forget operation that can run in parallel with other arithmetic such as exponentials. |
| **Warp groups** | A CTA is split into several *warp‑groups* (typically 4 warps each). | By dedicating some warps solely to loading (`producer`) and others solely to computing (`consumer`), each group can stay busy without stepping on each other’s toes. |
| **`setmaxnreg`** | Dynamically re‑allocates registers between warp‑groups at launch time. | Consumer warps get extra registers for larger GEMM tiles; producer warps keep only what they need for TMA calls. |

These concepts are already present in Hopper’s programming guide—they just weren’t exploited by earlier FlashAttention kernels.

---

## Producer–consumer warp specialization  

The core idea of FlashAttention‑3 is a **circular SMEM buffer** managed by two kinds of warp‑groups:

1. **Producer warps** – issue TMA loads for $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ into SMEM slots.
2. **Consumer warps** – read already‑loaded tiles from SMEM and run `WGMMA` to form partial scores $\mathbf{S}^{(j)}$ and partial outputs $\mathbf{O}^{(j)}$.

A simplified sketch (inspired by Algorithm 1 of the paper) looks like this:

```text
# Producer side
for each tile j of K/V:
    TMA_load(K_j)                     // async, no stall
    TMA_load(V_j)
    signal_consumer(j)               // notify that slot j is ready

# Consumer side
for each tile j:
    wait_for_producer(j)             // barrier until slot j filled
    S_cur = WGMMA(Q_i, K_j^T)         // async GEMM
    softmax_update(S_cur)            // exp, row‐max etc.
    O_i += WGMMA(P_cur, V_j)          // second async GEMM
    release_slot(j)                  // let producer reuse it
```

Key points:

* The producers *never* wait for any GEMM result—they merely stream data into SMEM.
* Consumers *never* touch global memory—they only read from the already resident SMEM tiles.
* A lightweight barrier (`signal_consumer` / `wait_for_producer`) synchronizes just enough to preserve data correctness while letting the hardware pipeline run continuously.
* `setmaxnreg` gives consumer warps more registers so they can hold intermediate results (`S_cur`, `P_cur`) without spilling, while producers shrink their register footprint because they only need a handful of registers for a TMA launch.

Because TMA is truly asynchronous, the first *s* stages of the circular buffer fill up without any stalls; only when the buffer wraps around does a producer have to pause until its earlier slot is reclaimed by a consumer. In practice this yields near‑perfect overlap between data movement and computation.

---

## Hiding softmax under asynchronous GEMMs  

Even after we overlap loads with matrix multiplies, there remains an obvious sequential dependency: **softmax needs the scores** $\mathbf{S}^{(j)}$, while the second GEMM needs *post‑softmax* probabilities $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{(j)}$. On Hopper those two steps have wildly different throughputs:

* FP16 matmul throughput ≈ 989 TFLOPs/s  
* Exponential / special‑function throughput ≈ 3.9 TFLOPs/s

In other words, while Tensor Cores are churning through billions of FLOPs per cycle, the scalar “exp” pipeline sits idle most of the time.

FlashAttention‑3 solves this with **ping‑pong scheduling** across two consumer warp‑groups:

1️⃣ Warp group A finishes its `WGMMA(Q·Kᵀ)` for tile *j*, then immediately starts computing `exp` on those scores (the softmax part).  
2️⃣ While group A works on `exp`, warp group B launches its own `WGMMA(Q·Kᵀ)` for tile *j+1*.  
3️⃣ When group A finishes `exp`, it proceeds to its second GEMM (`P·V`) using results from tile *j*, while group B now moves onto its own softmax stage.

The two groups exchange roles each iteration—hence “ping‑pong”. By inserting explicit CUDA barriers (`bar.sync`) we enforce that *all* `WGMMA`s belonging to one group are issued before any softmax instructions from the other group execute. The net effect is that **every cycle contains both a high‑throughput matmul and a low‑throughput exponential**, eliminating idle time for either unit.

Real measurements confirm this intuition: flashing from 570 TFLOPs/s (no overlap) up to 620–640 TFLOPs/s for FP16 forward passes at head dimension 128 and sequence length 8192—roughly an extra 10 % boost just from intra‑warpgroup pipelining.

---

## From 35 % to 75 % utilization  

Putting producer–consumer specialization together with ping‑pong softmax yields surprising speedups:

| Precision | Peak TFLOPs/s (H100 SXM5) | Observed Utilization | Speedup vs FlashAttention‑2 |
|-----------|--------------------------|----------------------|-----------------------------|
| FP16      | ~990 TFLOPs              | ~75 % (≈740 TFLOPs/s) | 1.5–2×                      |
| FP8       | ~1.2 PFLOPs              | comparable           | ≈1× (but doubles raw throughput because FP8 Tensor Cores are twice as fast) |

FlashAttention‑2 topped out at roughly 35 % because its kernels were built around synchronous “load → compute → write” steps that left both TMA units and Tensor Cores underutilized most of the time. By redesigning the algorithm around Hopper’s asynchronous execution model we finally achieve hardware utilisation comparable to hand‑tuned GEMM kernels—without sacrificing exactness (the algorithm still computes true softmax).

A few practical notes from our implementation:

* **Register pressure:** The two‑stage pipeline needs an extra copy of $\mathbf{S}_{\text{next}}$ kept in registers, which pushes occupancy limits at very large block sizes.
* **Compiler reordering:** NVCC sometimes shuffles instructions; we verified through SASS inspection that our barriers indeed enforce the intended order.
* **Scalability:** The approach works for any sequence length where we can tile Q/K/V into blocks $B_r$/$B_c$; larger blocks give higher arithmetic intensity but also raise register usage—so profiling remains essential.

---

## Key takeaways  

- **Asynchrony is free if you schedule it right** – Hopper’s TMA lets you pull data into shared memory while Tensor Cores are already busy on previous tiles.
- **Separate roles = higher occupancy** – dedicating some warps just to loading frees up registers for the compute warps, letting them use larger GEMM tiles without spilling.
- **Overlap cheap ops with expensive ops** – ping‑pong scheduling hides softmax’s low‐throughput exponentials behind high‐throughput matrix multiplies.
- **Result:** FlashAttention‑3 reaches up to 75 % device utilisation on H100s, delivering 1½–2× faster exact attention than FlashAttention‑2 while keeping numerical error identical (FP16) or even improving it dramatically when combined with FP8 block quantization techniques.

---

## Looking ahead  

The producer–consumer pattern isn’t limited to attention—it can accelerate any kernel where large tensor loads precede heavy compute (e.g., convolutional backbones or vision transformers). Moreover, while our experiments focused on NVIDIA’s Hopper architecture, any accelerator offering separate data‐movement units and compute units could benefit from a similar software pipeline.

FlashAttention‑3 is open source (`github.com/Dao-AILab/flash-attention`) and will soon be integrated into PyTorch/Hugging Face pipelines, so developers can plug in these speedups without rewriting models. As GPUs continue adding more specialized async engines (e.g., tensor‐core versions for FP4/FP6), refining producer–consumer pipelines will remain a key strategy for squeezing every ounce of performance out of modern hardware—turning latency into speed rather than a bottleneck you have to live with.

# FP8 Low‑Precision Attention: Twice the Throughput Without Losing Accuracy  

**TL;DR:** By redesigning the attention kernel around Hopper’s native FP8 Tensor Cores and adding two clever tricks—block‑wise quantization and “incoherent” processing—FlashAttention‑3 reaches almost 1.2 PFLOP/s while keeping numerical error comparable to full‑precision baselines.

---  

## Why Attention Still Holds Up the Transformer Train  

The self‑attention operation at the heart of every Transformer scales as \(O(N^{2}d)\) with sequence length \(N\) and head dimension \(d\). For long contexts—think whole documents or multi‑minute audio—this quadratic cost quickly eclipses everything else.  

Early software hacks (e.g., approximations or kernel fusions) helped, but the bottleneck is fundamentally the two large matrix multiplications:

\[
\mathbf{S}= \alpha\,\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}\in \mathbb{R}^{N\times N},
\qquad
\mathbf{O}= \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{S})\,\mathbf{V}\in \mathbb{R}^{N\times d},
\]

where \(\alpha = 1/\sqrt{d}\). Even if we hide the softmax’s cheap arithmetic behind fast GEMMs, we still pay a huge price in data movement and raw FLOP count.

FlashAttention‑1 showed that fusing these steps into a single kernel eliminates intermediate writes to global memory, yielding a massive speedup on earlier GPUs. FlashAttention‑2 added parallelism over the sequence dimension, yet on NVIDIA’s Hopper H100 it only managed about **35 %** tensor‑core utilization—far below the 80–90 % seen for pure GEMM kernels. The missing piece? Hopper’s new **asynchrony** and **FP8** support weren’t being exploited.



## The Promise of Native FP8  

Hopper’s Tensor Cores expose a new instruction set (`WGMMA`) that can compute matrix multiplies directly in **FP8** (`e4m3`). In raw throughput terms this means roughly **twice** as many FLOPs per SM compared with FP16/BF16:

| Precision | Peak matmul throughput (H100) |
|-----------|-------------------------------|
| FP16/BF16 | ≈ 989 TFLOP/s |
| **FP8**   | **≈ 1.9 PFLOP/s** |

If we could simply switch the two GEMMs inside attention from FP16 to FP8, we would instantly halve execution time—*unless* numeric errors explode.



## Two Obstacles When Going Low‑Precision  

1️⃣ **Layout restrictions** – The `WGMMA` instruction expects its operand matrices in a *k‑major* layout (contiguous along the inner reduction dimension). For attention we need `V` in “row‑major” order because it is multiplied after softmax, which conflicts with the hardware rule for the second GEMM (`softmax · V`).  

2️⃣ **Quantization error** – FP8 only carries **3 mantissa bits** and **4 exponent bits**. In large language models many activations are small, but a few “outliers” can be orders of magnitude larger (e.g., spikes from rare tokens). A single global scaling factor (`per‑tensor`) would either saturate those outliers or waste precision on the bulk of values.



## FlashAttention‑3’s Two Accuracy‑Preserving Tricks  

### 1️⃣ Block Quantization – “shrink the scale locally”

Instead of a single scale per whole tensor (the usual *per‑tensor* approach), FlashAttention‑3 splits each input matrix into the same blocks that the kernel already processes (`B_r × d` or `B_c × d`). Each block gets its own floating‑point scale factor before being cast down to FP8:

```
for each block:
    compute max_abs = max(|block|)
    scale = max_abs / max_fp8_value
    fp8_block = round(block / scale)
```

Because the softmax step works on a product `QKᵀ`, we can *apply* these scales directly when forming `S`; no extra arithmetic is required beyond multiplying by the stored scalars at runtime. The net effect is a **dynamic range adaptation** that preserves most of the original information even when some entries are huge.



### 2️⃣ Incoherent Processing – “scramble outliers before they get quantized”

Even with per‑block scales an outlier may still dominate a small tile. The authors solve this by first multiplying both queries and keys by a *random orthogonal matrix* \(\mathbf{M}\):

\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}, 
\qquad 
\widetilde{\mathbf{K}} = \mathbf{K}\mathbf{M},
\qquad
\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}\widetilde{\mathbf{K}}^{\top}
=
\mathbf{Q}\underbrace{\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{\top}}_{=\,I}
\mathbf{K}^{\top}
= \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}.
\]

Since \(\mathbf{M}\) is orthogonal (\(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{\top}=I\)), it does not change the underlying attention scores—but it *spreads* each original entry across many positions (each entry becomes a sum of many signed components). In practice \(\mathbf{M}\) is built from cheap Hadamard transforms interleaved with random sign flips (+/– 1). This operation costs only \(O(d \log d)\) per head and can be fused into existing preprocessing steps such as rotary embeddings without extra memory traffic.

The result is that after scrambling, every tile’s values are closer together statistically, making them easier to represent accurately in only three mantissa bits.



## Making It All Fit on Hopper  

Even after solving accuracy concerns we still need to satisfy hardware layout constraints:

* **In‑kernel transpose of `V` tiles:** Using `ldmatrix`/`stmatrix` instructions (LDSM/STSM), a warp can load a tile from shared memory into registers and write it back transposed—all while other warps are busy executing GEMMs. This “ping‑pong” schedule hides both compute and data movement thanks to Hopper’s asynchronous Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA).

* **Accumulator ↔ Operand permutation:** After computing `S = QKᵀ` in FP8, its accumulator resides in an `FP32` format whose register ordering differs from what the next GEMM expects for `V`. A lightweight byte‑permute reshuffles registers (`d0 d1 d4 d5 …`) so that when `softmax·V` runs it sees exactly what it needs without extra copies.

Together these low‑level tricks let FlashAttention‑3 run a completely fused forward pass entirely in fp8 every bit of the way.

## Why Attention Still Holds Up the Transformer Train

\[
\mathbf{S}= \alpha \,\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\!\top},\qquad
\mathbf{P}= \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{S}),\qquad
\mathbf{O}= \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V},
\]

with $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{d}$ and $N$ the sequence length.  
The quadratic term $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\!\top}$ makes attention the dominant cost when $N$ grows beyond a few thousand tokens – exactly the regime where large language models (LLMs) start showing new capabilities (long‑document reasoning, codebase navigation, video processing, etc.).

## What FlashAttention‑3 Changes

FlashAttention‑3 adds three hardware‑aware tricks that together lift the bottleneck:

| Technique | What it does | Why it helps |
|---|---|---|
| **Producer–consumer asynchrony (warp specialization)** | Splits a CTA into “producer” warps that only issue TMA loads and “consumer” warps that only run Tensor Core WGMMAs. | Memory loads and matrix multiplies can proceed in parallel instead of being serialized by CUDA synchronizations. |
| **GEMM–softmax pipelining (two‑stage overlap)** | While one warpgroup finishes $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\!\top}$ for block *j*, another warpgroup already starts the exponential/softmax for block *j–1* and then immediately multiplies $\widetilde{\mathbf P}^{(j)}$ with $\mathbf V^{(j)}$. | Softmax is bandwidth limited (≈ 4 TFLOP/s of special functions) whereas GEMM runs at ≈ 989 TFLOP/s (FP16). Overlap hides most softmax latency behind the massive Tensor Core work. |
| **Hardware‑accelerated FP8 GEMM + block quantization** | Uses Hopper’s FP8 Tensor Cores via WGMMA and performs an in‑kernel tile transpose so layout constraints are met without extra memory traffic. Each block is quantized separately and an orthogonal “incoherent” matrix spreads out outliers before quantization. | FP8 doubles raw Tensor Core throughput while keeping numerical error comparable (≈ 2.6 × lower than naïve per‑tensor FP8). |

All three pieces are implemented with CUDA primitives (`setmaxnreg`, `TMA`, `WGMMA`) and compile down to a single fused kernel – just like FlashAttention‑2 but now fully asynchronous and low‑precision aware.

## Decoding TFLOP/s & Utilisation for Practitioners

### What is “TFLOP/s”?

A *teraflop* = $10^{12}$ floating‑point operations per second. Modern GPUs quote separate peaks for different precisions because each Tensor Core instruction processes a different number of bits:

* **FP16 / BF16:** Peak ≈ 989 TFLOP/s on Hopper.
* **FP8:** Peak ≈ ~2000 TFLOP/s (the kernel reports ∼1200 TFLOP/s after accounting for overhead).

When we say “FlashAttention‑3 hits 740 TFLOP/s”, we mean the code actually *executes* arithmetic equivalent to $7.4\times10^{14}$ ops each second—roughly three quarters of what the hardware could do if every cycle were spent on matrix multiplication alone.

### Why does “utilisation” matter?

Utilisation = measured FLOP rate ÷ hardware peak FLOP rate.

*Higher utilisation* indicates that most cycles are spent doing useful work rather than waiting on memory or control flow stalls.
For LLM training this matters because:

* **Wall‑clock time ↓** – A higher utilisation reduces epoch time proportionally when attention dominates.
* **Energy efficiency ↑** – Tensor Cores consume roughly constant power when busy; idle cycles waste electricity without doing work.
* **Cost efficiency ↑** – On cloud platforms you’re billed per second of GPU time; halving runtime translates directly into lower monetary cost.

FlashAttention‑3 lifts utilisation from ~35 % (FlashAttention‑2) → ~75 %, meaning you get *more than twice* as much compute out of every dollar spent on an H100 instance when running long‐context workloads.

## 2️⃣ Swapping the Default Attention Kernel  

### A. Bare‑bones PyTorch (`torch.nn.MultiheadAttention`)  

FlashAttention‑3 ships a drop‑in function `flash_attn_func`. It expects packed QKV tensors in *shape* \((B,\;N,\;3d)\) where \(B\) is batch size, \(N\) sequence length and \(d\) head dimension per head.

```python
import torch
from flash_attn import flash_attn_func

def flash_mha(query, key, value,
              causal=False,
              dropout_p=0.,
              softmax_scale=None,
              fp8=False):
    """
    Mirrors torch.nn.functional.scaled_dot_product_attention but runs
    completely on the fused FlashAttention‑3 kernel.
    """
    # pack Q,K,V along last dim → shape (B,N,3*d)
    qkv = torch.stack([query, key, value], dim=-1).reshape(
        query.shape[0], query.shape[1], -1)

    if fp8:
        # convert inputs to fp8_e4m3fn – see note below
        qkv = qkv.to(torch.float8_e4m3fn)

    out = flash_attn_func(qkv,
                         dropout_p=dropout_p,
                         causal=causal,
                         softmax_scale=softmax_scale,
                         return_unscaled=False,
                         fp8=fp8)
    return out
```

Replace calls like

```python
attn_output, _ = self.mha(query, key, value)
```

with

```python
attn_output = flash_mha(query, key, value,
                        causal=self.is_decoder,
                        dropout_p=self.dropout.p,
                        softmax_scale=1./(self.head_dim**0.5),
                        fp8=False)   # set True for FP8 inference
```

> **Why it works:** Internally the function launches the same kernel described in the paper — producer warps pull blocks of \(\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}\) from HBM via TMA while consumer warps run asynchronous WGMMAs that compute \(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}\), apply rowwise max/subtract/exp (`softmax`) and immediately multiply by \(\mathbf{V}\). No intermediate \(\mathbf{S}\) or \(\mathbf{P}\) ever touch global memory.

| Issue | Fix / Recommendation |

| **GPU < sm90** – no native FP8 | Use `fp8=False`; you’ll still benefit from async GEMM–softmax pipeline (+≈ 30 % perf gain). |

| **Debugging wrong results after patching Transformers** | Call `model.eval()` before benchmarking; otherwise dropout may mask speed differences and introduce nondeterministic values which look like “bugs”. |

# Looking Ahead: How Faster, More Accurate Attention Enables New AI Applications  

The attention layer sits at the heart of every Transformer model we use today—from giant language models that generate code to vision systems that parse high‑resolution images. Yet it remains a performance bottleneck because computing the full attention matrix scales quadratically with sequence length. **FlashAttention‑3** tackles this bottleneck head‑on on NVIDIA’s Hopper GPUs by marrying two powerful ideas—*asynchronous execution* and *low‑precision arithmetic*—into a single kernel that is both **faster** and **more accurate** than previous implementations. In this post we unpack what makes FlashAttention‑3 tick, translate its raw numbers into practical benefits, and sketch out research avenues that could further amplify its impact.

---

## What FlashAttention‑3 Brings  

FlashAttention‑3 builds on the exact‑attention paradigm introduced by FlashAttention‑1/2, which already eliminated costly intermediate writes to global memory by fusing query–key multiplication, softmax, and value multiplication into one kernel. FlashAttention‑3 pushes this fusion further by exploiting architectural features unique to Hopper:

### 1️⃣ Producer–Consumer Asynchrony via Warp Specialization  
Hopper’s Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) moves data between HBM and shared memory *asynchronously*, while its Tensor Cores (exposed through the WGMMA instruction) can start a matrix multiply before the data load completes. By assigning one warpgroup as a **producer** (only issuing TMA loads) and another as a **consumer** (only issuing WGMMA computes), FlashAttention‑3 creates a software pipeline where data movement and computation overlap perfectly. The result is a dramatic rise in compute utilization—from roughly **35 %** seen with FlashAttention‑2 to **≈ 75 %** on H100 for FP16 workloads.

### 2️⃣ Hiding Softmax Behind Block‑wise GEMMs  
The softmax step includes expensive operations such as exponentials and reductions that run at only a few TFLOPs per second—orders of magnitude slower than Tensor Core matmuls (~990 TFLOPs FP16). FlashAttention‑3 interleaves *softmax on one block* with *GEMM on the next block* (“ping‑pong” scheduling). While one warpgroup computes $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, another simultaneously evaluates the exponential and normalisation for the previous block’s scores $\mathbf{S}$. This two‑stage pipeline shrinks idle cycles on both Tensor Cores and special-function units, boosting observed throughput up to **740 TFLOPs /s** (FP16) and approaching **1.2 PFLOPs /s** when using FP8.

### 3️⃣ Hardware‑Accelerated FP8 GEMM + Accuracy Tricks  
FP8 offers roughly twice the raw matmul throughput of FP16 thanks to Hopper’s dedicated FP8 Tensor Cores. However, naïve per‑tensor quantisation would inject intolerable error—especially when large outlier values appear in language model activations. FlashAttention‑3 resolves this mismatch through:
* **Block quantisation** – each $B_r\times d$ query block or $B_c\times d$ key/value block gets its own scale factor, eliminating the “one scale fits all” problem.
* **Incoherent processing** – before quantisation we multiply $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ (implemented as sign flips combined with a Hadamard transform). Since $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top = \mathbf{I}$, the attention output stays unchanged while outlier magnitudes are spread across entries, reducing quantisation error dramatically.

Together these tricks make FP8 FlashAttention‑3 **2.6× more accurate** than a baseline per‐tensor‐quantised FP8 implementation, while still delivering near–peak tensor core throughput.

## Why Faster Attention Matters  

### 🚀 Long‐Context Reasoning in Large Language Models  
Self‐attention costs grow quadratically with sequence length $N$ ($\mathcal{O}(N^2)$). By shaving off memory traffic and increasing arithmetic density, FlashAttention‑3 makes it feasible to double or triple $N$ without increasing wall‐clock time proportionally. This directly benefits use cases such as:

Vision Transformers treat each image patch as a token; higher resolutions translate into far more patches—precisely where attention becomes prohibitive. With an attention kernel that can sustain >750 TFLOPs/s in FP16 or ~1 PFLOP/s in FP8:
* High‐resolution image classification (e.g., $>1024^2$ pixels) becomes tractable at inference time,
* Video models can attend over longer temporal windows without hitting memory limits,
* Audio or speech tasks that sample at high rates benefit from denser frame representations.

### 🏎️ More Efficient Inference – Lower Latency & Power  
Switching from FP16 to optimized FP8 cuts materialisation time roughly in half while preserving accuracy thanks to block quantisation & incoherent processing. For production services this translates into:
* Smaller GPU footprints per request,
* Reduced electricity costs,
* Ability to serve larger batches within tight latency SLAs—a crucial factor for real‐time assistants or recommendation engines.
