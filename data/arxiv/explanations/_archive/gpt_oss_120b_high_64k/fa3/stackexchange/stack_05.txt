\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### Why does exponential throughput become a bottleneck despite its lower FLOP count?
Question:
"The H100 has 989 TFLOPS of FP16 matmul but only 3.9 TFLOPS of special functions such as exponential" – given that softmax involves far fewer FLOPs than the matmul (e.g., 512× less), why can exponentials still dominate half of the execution cycles? How does the difference between FLOP count and unit throughput lead to this situation, especially when moving from FP16 to FP8 where matmul throughput doubles but exponential throughput stays constant?
Answer:
The short answer is that “FLOP count” tells you how many arithmetic operations an algorithm needs, whereas “TFLOPS” tells you how many **operations per second** each functional unit on the GPU can deliver.  
On Hopper these two numbers are wildly different for matrix‑multiply versus special‑function units, so an operation that performs far fewer FLOPs can nonetheless consume a comparable fraction of the wall‑clock time.

### How many FLOPs does attention really do?

In a single head the forward pass computes  

\[
\mathbf{S}=QK^{\top}\qquad 
\mathbf{P}= {\operatorname {softmax}}\!\bigl(\mathbf{S}\bigr)\qquad 
\mathbf{O}=PV .
\]

If we tile the computation into blocks of size \((B_{r}\!\times d)\) for \(Q\) and \((B_{c}\!\times d)\) for \(K\) (the same tiling is used for \(V)), then  

* One block‑wise GEMM \(QK^{\top}\) performs \(2\,B_{r}dB_{c}\) floating‑point multiply‑adds.  
* The second GEMM \(PV\) performs another \(2\,B_{r}dB_{c}\).

Counting a multiply‑add as **two** FLOPs (the convention used throughout the paper), the two GEMMs together require  

\[
\vphantom{\underbrace{}{}}\underbrace{4\,d}_{\text{factor}}\;\times B_{r}B_{c}
\]

floating‑point operations.  

The softmax itself needs only one exponential per element of \(\mathbf{S}\), i.e.\ \(B_{r}B_{c}\) exponentials plus inexpensive subtract‑and‑divide steps.  

Consequently the **ratio** between GEMM FLOPs and exponentials is  

\[
\frac{\text {GEMM FLOPs}}{\# {\rm exp}}=4d .
\]

With a common head dimension \(d=128\) this gives a factor of **\(4\times128=512\$** – exactly what the authors report in § “Pingpong scheduling”.

### Why do those $\displaystyle \approx 500\times$ more FLOPs not dominate runtime?

Peak throughputs on an H100 are not uniform:

| Unit                                      | Peak TFLOPS (FP16) |
|-------------------------------------------|---------------------|
| Tensor‑Core matrix multiply               | $\displaystyle \approx 989$ |
| Special‑function unit (exp, log, …)      | $\displaystyle \approx 3.9$ |

Thus a single Tensor‑Core can finish about  

```tex
\[ 
\dfrac{989}{3.9}\;\approx\:255
``` 

times as many floating‑point operations per second as the special‑function unit can finish exponentials.

If we write the wall‑clock time spent on each part as  

```tex
\[ 
T_{\text{GEMM}} = \frac{\!\# {\rm FLOPs}_{\,{\rm GEMM}}\!}{989},
   \qquad
T_{\exp }       = \frac{\!\# {\rm exp}\!}{3.9},
``` 

and substitute  

```tex
\[ 
\!\# {\rm FLOPs}_{\,{\rm GEMM}} = 512\, \!\# {\rm exp}
``` 

we obtain  

```tex
\[ 
T_{\text G E M M}= 
   \frac{512\, \!\# {\rm exp}}{989}
   \;\simeq\:2.1\,T_{\exp } .
``` 

In other words **the GEMM part only runs about twice as long as the exponential part**, even though it does five hundred times more arithmetic work.  
Because everything else in softmax (the max reduction, subtraction, division) costs negligible cycles compared with an exponential, the exponential accounts for roughly one half of the total execution cycles in practice – exactly what Table&nbsp;1 and Fig.&nbsp;6 of the paper observe for FP16 with \(d=128.\)

### What happens when we switch from FP16 to FP8?

Hopper adds native FP8 tensor‑core instructions which double matrix‑multiply throughput while leaving the special‑function unit unchanged – it still delivers only $\displaystyle \approx 3.9\$ TFLOPS for exponentials because there is no separate “FP8 exp” path. Consequently the disparity widens:

* **FP8 GEMM peak** $\displaystyle (\;2\times 989)\;\approximately\:1978\$ TFLOPS,
* **Exp peak**                 $\displaystyle \approximately\:3.9\$ TFLOPS.

The ratio between attainable rates jumps from $\displaystyle (\;\approximately\:255)\;$to $\displaystyle (\;\approximately\:510)\;$.
Even though the absolute number of exponentials stays identical, its share of wall–clock time therefore grows: now almost all of the forward pass time is spent waiting for those slow exponentiations unless we hide them behind GEMMs.

### How FlashAttention‑3 deals with it

The authors exploit exactly this imbalance:

1\. **Warp–specialised producer/consumer pipelines** let one warpgroup load data while another issues tensor–core WGMMA instructions.  
2\. **Two–stage pipelining** overlaps a block’s softmax (including its exponentials) with the next block’s GEMM so that every cycle spent in a tensor core is paired with work on a special–function unit.  
3\. When using FP8 they retain this overlap; however because exp throughput does not increase, “hiding’’ becomes even more valuable—otherwise exponentials would dominate > 60 % of cycles.

Even with perfect overlap you cannot exceed the slower unit’s capacity; therefore after all possible hiding has been performed,
the overall speed is bounded by whichever unit runs longest—in practice still close to half of the cycles are spent in exponentiation for FP16 and an even larger fraction for FP8 unless additional hardware support for low–precision special functions appears.

### Take–away

The apparent paradox stems from confusing **operation count** with **hardware rate**: attention requires $\displaystyle \approximately\:500\$ fewer exponentials than matrix–multiply FLOPs, but Hopper’s tensor cores are $\displaystyle \approximately\:250\$ times faster than its special–function unit, making those few exponentials consume roughly half of execution time at FP16 precision and an even larger share at FP8 precision. FlashAttention‑3 mitigates this by tightly interleaving softmax with asynchronous GEMMs, but it cannot eliminate it entirely because the underlying hardware limits remain unchanged.​
