\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 1: Motivation and Challenges in Efficient LLM Fine‑tuning  

# The role of instruction fine‑tuning in enhancing model performance  

Instruction fine‑tuning has become the de facto method for endowing large language models (LLMs) with reliable conversational abilities. By presenting the model with paired examples of an “instruction” (a user prompt) and an appropriate “response”, the pretrained network learns to map arbitrary textual commands to task‑specific outputs. Empirically this process yields dramatic gains across a wide spectrum of downstream tasks \citep{min2021metaicl, wei2021finetuned, ouyang2022training, wang2022super, wang2022self, liu2022few}. Moreover, it provides a controlled mechanism for adding desirable behaviors—such as factual grounding or politeness—and for removing unwanted ones — for example toxic or biased language \citep{ouyang2022training,askell2021general,bai2022training}. In practice, publicly released instruction‑tuned models such as Alpaca \citep{alpaca} and Vicuna \citep{vicuna2023} have demonstrated that relatively modest supervised datasets can lift a raw pretrained LLM into a capable chatbot rivaling proprietary systems. This observation motivates the research community to invest heavily in refining the instruction‑fine‑tuning pipeline: higher quality or more diverse instruction datasets repeatedly translate into better conversational fluency, factual correctness, and alignment with human preferences.

# Memory consumption of conventional BF16/FP16 fine‑tuning  

The gains from instruction fine‑tuning come at an extreme computational price when the base model is very large. A typical modern practice is to store and update all parameters in 16‑bit floating point (BF16 or FP16) precision during back‑propagation. While this halves the memory required compared with 32‑bit precision, the reduction is insufficient for models that contain tens of billions of parameters. Consider LLaMA \citep{touvron2023llama} with $65\text{B}$ parameters: each parameter occupies $2$ bytes at BF16 precision, leading to a raw weight footprint of $\approx130$ GiB. Adding optimizer state (often another two copies of the weight matrix) and activation buffers multiplies this requirement further. In practice the authors report that naïve BF16 fine‑tuning of LLaMA‑65B consumes more than $780$ GB of GPU memory—far beyond the capacity of any single modern graphics card. Even models at $33\text{B}$ parameters exceed typical high‑end consumer GPUs ($24$–$48$ GB), forcing researchers either to resort to multi‑node distributed training or to abandon full fine‑tuning altogether. Consequently, scaling instruction fine‑tuning to truly massive language models has been limited by hardware rather than algorithmic insight.

# Why existing post‑training quantisation methods break during gradient computation  

Quantisation reduces the number of bits required to store each weight by discretising continuous values into a smaller set (e.g., converting FP32 into 8‑bit integers). Recent advances have demonstrated that 4‑bit or 8‑bit post‑training quantisation can preserve inference quality for many LLMs \citep{dettmers2022llm, dettmers2022case, frantar2022gptq, xiao2022smoothquant}. However these techniques were designed solely for forward passes; during backward propagation they encounter fundamental obstacles.

In blockwise $k$‑bit quantisation (Section “Block-wise k-bit Quantization”), each block receives its own scaling constant $c_i$. This strategy mitigates outliers by scaling each block individually so that its dynamic range matches the low‑bit target range. Yet during gradient computation one must differentiate through both the dequantisation operation and the scaling constants themselves. Outlier values – which were deliberately isolated by using per‑block scales – produce highly non‐linear gradients when mapped back from a coarse grid (e.g., four discrete levels in 4‑bit). As shown empirically by \citet{wortsman2023stable}, these gradients become noisy enough that training diverges or leads to severe degradation in downstream performance. Moreover, many post‑training schemes assume static quantisers computed once on a frozen weight tensor; they cannot accommodate updates because re‑estimating optimal thresholds at every optimisation step would be prohibitively expensive.\footnote{The “quantile quantisation” approach estimates distribution percentiles but incurs high computational cost whenever weight statistics change.\citep{dettmers2022optimizers}} As a result, although low‑bit models are tractable for inference, they are practically unusable for standard stochastic gradient descent unless one redesigns the quantisation pipeline to remain stable under weight updates.

# Parameter‑efficient approaches such as LoRA that keep most weights frozen  

A complementary line of research tackles memory consumption by reducing the number of *learnable* parameters rather than compressing the stored parameters themselves. Low‐Rank Adaptation (LoRA) \citep{hu2021lora} augments each linear transformation $\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times o}$ with an additive pair of low‐rank matrices $\mathbf{L}_1\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times r}$ and $\mathbf{L}_2\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times o}$:

\[
\mathbf{Y}= \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}+ s\,\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2,
\]

where $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times h}$ denotes the input activations, $r\ll \min(h,o)$ controls the rank reduction, and $s$ is a scalar that rescales the adapter contribution. Crucially **the original weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$ remains frozen** throughout training; only $\mathbf{L}_1$, $\mathbf{L}_2$, and $s$ are updated via backpropagation. Since $\mathbf{L}_1$ and $\mathbf{L}_2$ together contain only $r(h+o)$ parameters—typically less than $0.5\%$ of $\lvert \mathbf{W}\rvert$—the additional memory required for storing gradients is negligible compared with activation buffers.

The memory profile favours LoRA because activation gradients dominate total consumption during training (see “Memory Requirement of Parameter‐Efficient Finetuning”). Even if we increase the number of adapters—for instance inserting them after every linear layer—the total parameter count scarcely rises while activation storage stays unchanged if gradient checkpointing is employed \citep{chen2016training}. Therefore LoRA enables us to preserve most expressive power from the massive base model while keeping learnable components small enough to fit within limited GPU budgets.

# The objective of achieving full BF16 performance while fitting training on a single GPU  

Given these observations—a clear need for instruction fine‐tuning, prohibitive memory demand under conventional BF16 updates, instability of naïve low‐bit backpropagation, and promising parameter‐efficient adapters—the central goal crystallises: **design an end–to–end fine‐tuning pipeline that matches full BF16 performance yet requires less than one typical high‑end GPU’s memory**.

QLoRA answers this challenge by marrying three innovations:

1. **4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4)** – an information–theoretically optimal representation for normally distributed weights~(Section “4-bit NormalFloat Quantization”). By aligning both data type range (scaled to $[-1,1]$) and distribution (standard normal), NF4 dramatically reduces quantisation error compared with generic Float4 or Int4 encodings.
2. **Double Quantization** – further compresses per‐block scaling constants $c_i$, turning their 32‑bit storage into an 8‑bit float followed by an auxiliary second‐level scale. This saves roughly $0.37$ bits per parameter without compromising forward fidelity.
3. **Paged Optimizers** – leverage NVIDIA Unified Memory so optimizer states can be swapped between GPU RAM and host RAM on demand; thereby eliminating occasional out–of–memory spikes caused by long sequences under gradient checkpointing.

Within QLoRA each base weight tensor is stored as NF4 plus its doubly compressed scales; at run time it is dequantised into BF16 (`dequant` → `doubleDequant`) before participating in matrix multiplication:

\[
\mathbf Y^{\text {BF16}} = \mathbf X^{\text {BF16}}\, \text {doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text {FP32}},c_2^{k{\text{-bit}}}, \mathbf W^{k{\text{-bit}}}) 
+ \,\underbrace{\bigl(\mathbf X^{\text {BF16}}\!\! \mathbf L_1^{\text {BF16}}\!\! \mathbf L_2^{\text {BF16}}\bigr)}_{\text {LoRA adapters}} .
\]

Only gradients w.r.t.\ $\mathbf L_{1}, \,\mathbf L_{2}$ are materialised; gradients w.r.t.\ $\mathbf W$ are never computed because $\mathbf W$ stays frozen at its NF4 representation throughout training. Consequently **the total memory footprint drops from > 780 GB down to < 48 GB** even for a $65\,\text B$ model—precisely enough to fit on a single professional GPU—while empirical results show no loss relative to a fully finetuned BF16 baseline (\S “QLoRA vs.\ Standard Finetuning”).  

Thus QLoRA satisfies the objective laid out at the beginning: it delivers full mixed–precision training quality using only four bits per base parameter and modest additional overhead from adapters and optimizer paging—making large‐scale instruction fine­-tuning accessible to researchers equipped with commonplace GPUs rather than specialised clusters.
