# Demystifying QLoRA: How 4‑Bit Quantization Makes LLM Fine‑tuning Accessible  

*An introductory guide to why fine‑tuning huge language models has been so hard—until now.*

---

## The Fine‑tuning Bottleneck  

Large language models (LLMs) such as LLaMA‑65B have shown impressive capabilities, but unlocking their full potential usually requires **fine‑tuning** on a downstream task or instruction set. The catch? Memory.  

* A naïve 16‑bit fine‑tune of LLaMA‑65B consumes **more than 780 GB** of GPU memory—far beyond what any single consumer or even most workstation GPUs can provide.  
* Because the bulk of memory usage comes from activations and optimizer states, simply “shaving off” a few layers is not enough; the entire weight matrix still lives in high precision during training.  

Consequently, only research labs with multi‑GPU clusters could afford to adapt these models, leaving the rest of the community out of the loop.

---

## Quantization: From Inference to Training  

Quantization is the process of representing tensors with fewer bits:

\[
\mathbf{X}^{\text{int8}} = \text{round}\!\Bigl(c \cdot \mathbf{X}^{\text{fp32}}\Bigr),\qquad 
c = \frac{127}{\text{absmax}(\mathbf{X}^{\text{fp32}})} .
\]

When inference only is concerned, converting a model from fp16/ bf16 down to **8‑bit or even 4‑bit** can cut memory dramatically while preserving most of the predictive quality.

Training is another story. Back‑propagation needs gradients w.r.t. each weight; low‑bit representations introduce large rounding errors that quickly destabilize learning. Previous work therefore restricted low‑bit formats to inference only—until **QLoRA** showed it’s possible to train *through* a quantized model without sacrificing performance.

---

## Low‑rank Adapters (LoRA) – A Quick Refresher  

LoRA sidesteps the need to update billions of parameters by adding tiny “adapter” matrices to each linear projection:

\[
\mathbf{Y}
= \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}
+ s\,\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2,
\]

where  

* \(\mathbf{W}\) – the frozen pretrained weight matrix,  
* \(\mathbf{L}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{h\times r}\) and \(\mathbf{L}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times o}\) – low‑rank trainable matrices (\(r \ll h,o\)),  
* \(s\) – a scaling factor.

Because only the adapter parameters are updated, their memory footprint is tiny (often <0.2 % of the original model). The heavy lifting—forward passes through \(\mathbf{W}\)—still happens unchanged; gradients simply flow through \(\mathbf{W}\) into the adapters.

---

## QLoRA’s Three Tricks  

QLoRA combines **fourteen pictures** of clever engineering:

### 1️⃣ 4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4)  

Most pretrained weights follow an approximately zero‑centered normal distribution \(N(0,\sigma^2)\). NF4 exploits this by *quantile quantizing* a standard normal into exactly \(2^k\) bins (for \(k=4\)). In practice:

1. Compute the exact quantiles \(q_i\) of \(N(0,1)\).  
2. Scale each weight so its distribution fits into \([-1,1]\).  
3. Encode each scaled value using the precomputed quantile thresholds.

Because every bin receives an equal expected number of values, NF4 is **information‑theoretically optimal** for normally distributed data—meaning less error than naïve int4 or fp4 schemes while still fitting into 4 bits per weight.

### 2️⃣ Double Quantization  

A blockwise k‑bit format also stores a *scale constant* per block (the \(c\) in the formula above). With blocksize 64, storing these constants at fp32 adds about **0.5 bits per parameter**, which translates into roughly **3 GB** extra for a 65 B model.

Double Quantization tackles this overhead:

* The first‐level constants (\(c_2^{\text{fp32}}\)) are themselves quantized using an **8‑bit floating format** (FP8).  
* A second‐level scale (\(c_1^{\text{fp32}}\)) dequantizes those FP8 values during runtime.

Result: average overhead drops from ~0.5 bits/param to **≈0.127 bits/param**, shaving off another gigabyte or so without hurting accuracy.

### 3️⃣ Paged Optimizers  

Even with efficient storage, gradient checkpointing can create *temporary spikes* when processing long sequences—a classic out‑of‑memory failure mode for large models. QLoRA leverages NVIDIA’s unified memory:

* Optimizer states are allocated as **paged memory**, automatically swapping unused pages between GPU and host RAM.  
* When a spike occurs, surplus pages migrate off the GPU; when needed again they return instantly.

This technique eliminates OOM crashes while keeping overall throughput comparable to “regular” optimizers.

---

## Training a 65 B Model on One GPU  

Putting everything together yields an elegant pipeline:

| Step | What Happens |
|------|--------------|
| **Loading** | Weights are stored in NF4 + double‑quantized scale constants (~13 GB total). |
| **Adapter Injection** | LoRA modules are added *at every transformer layer*. |
| **Dequant & Compute** | Each forward pass dequantizes NF4 → BF16 on‐the‐fly, performs matrix multiplication in BF16, then discards the dequantized copy immediately after use. |
| **Backward Pass** | Gradients flow through the frozen dequantized weights into LoRA adapters only; original NF4 parameters stay untouched. |
| **Optimization** | Adam/Adafactor states live in paged memory so occasional spikes never exceed GPU capacity. |

The net effect? Fine‐tuning LLaMA‐65B requires **<48 GB** of GPU RAM—well within a single RTX 3090 or A6000 card—while preserving *exactly* the same predictive power as a full‐precision (~16 bit) run.

---

## Does Low Precision Hurt Performance?  

The authors evaluated QLoRA across several axes:

| Setup | Metric | Full‑precision baseline | QLoRA (NF4 + DQ) |
|-------|--------|------------------------|------------------|
| RoBERTa / GLUE | Accuracy | — | ↔︎ |
| T5 / Super-NaturalInstructions | Exact match | — | ↔︎ |
| LLaMA / MMLU (5-shot) – models 7–65 B | % correct | — | ↔︎ |
| FP4 variant | — | — | ≈ −1 % |

Across GLUE, instruction tuning benchmarks and massive multitask language understanding (MMLU), QLoRA’s NF4 version **matched** full‐precision LoRA and even full-model fine–tuning scores almost exactly. The only noticeable dip appeared when using plain FP4 instead of NF4—a clear indication that NF4’s normal‐distribution awareness matters in practice.

---

## Guanaco – A State‑of‑the‑Art Chatbot Built With QLoRA  

To showcase QLoRA’s real–world impact, the team trained a suite of chat models called **Guanaco**:

* **Guanaco‑7B** runs within *5 GB* RAM on a consumer laptop yet outperforms many larger open models by >20 pts on the Vicuna benchmark.  
* **Guanaco‑33B** reaches **97.8 %** of ChatGPT’s Vicuna score after ≈12 h on a single consumer GPU.  
* **Guanaco‑65B** tops out at **99.3 %** of ChatGPT’s Vicuna performance after just *24 h* on a professional GPU—essentially closing the gap while still fitting inside one device’s memory budget.

All models were trained with **only cross‐entropy supervision**, no RLHF tricks required, highlighting how powerful an efficient adapter + quantization combo can be when data quality is high (e.g., OASST1 instruction set).

All code—including custom CUDA kernels for NF4—and the trained adapters have been released under an open license and integrated into Hugging Face Transformers, making replication straightforward for anyone with a modest GPU.

---

## Why This Matters  

* **Democratizing research:** Academic groups or hobbyists no longer need multi–GPU clusters to explore instruction tuning at billions–parameter scale.  
* **Lower carbon footprint:** Fewer GPUs mean less energy consumption per experiment—a win for sustainability.  
* **Edge deployment:** The same NF4 representation that enables single–GPU training also shrinks inference footprints dramatically; future work could even fine‐tune on mobile devices (a few million tokens per night on an iPhone!).  
* **Open ecosystem:** By releasing both models and kernels, QLoRA invites community scrutiny and further innovation—important for responsible AI development.

---

## Takeaways  

1️⃣ Fine-tuning massive LLMs has been blocked by sheer memory demand rather than algorithmic limitations.  
2️⃣ Quantizing weights down to **four bits** using a *normal-distribution aware* format (NF4) keeps representational fidelity high enough for training when paired with LoRA adapters that absorb all learnable gradients.  
3️⃣ Packing those scales again (**double quantization**) eliminates most of the extra bit overhead introduced by blockwise quantization itself.  
4️⃣ Using NVIDIA’s unified paging prevents occasional OOM spikes without sacrificing speed—critical when training at scale on limited hardware.  
5️⃣ Empirically, QLoRA recovers full *16-bit* performance across classification, reasoning and chatbot tasks while slashing required VRAM from >780 GB → <48 GB for a 65 B model.

In short, QLoRA shows that “big models + modest hardware” is no longer an oxymoron—it’s now an everyday setup reachable by anyone with access to a modern GPU.

# Inside NF4 and Double Quantization: The Technical Magic Behind QLoRA’s Memory Savings  

Large language models (LLMs) have become the workhorse of modern NLP, but their sheer size makes fine‑tuning a nightmare on ordinary hardware. QLoRA cracks this problem wide open by **quantizing** a frozen pretrained model to 4 bits while still allowing gradients to flow through it into tiny LoRA adapters. Two clever tricks make this possible:  

1. **NormalFloat (NF4)** – a 4‑bit data type that is information‑theoretically optimal for the *normally distributed* weights that LLMs naturally exhibit.  
2. **Double Quantization** – a second‑level compression that shaves the scaling constants (the “quantization constants”) down to just a few bits.  

In this post we unpack the intuition, math, and empirical payoff of each innovation. By the end you’ll see why these seemingly tiny engineering details translate into **gigabytes** of saved GPU memory without any loss in downstream performance.

---

## 1. Why 4‑bit Quantization Needs Careful Design  

Standard block‑wise k‑bit quantization works by splitting a weight tensor into small blocks (often 64 elements) and representing each block with two pieces of data:

* **Quantized values** – stored with *k* bits per element (here k = 4).  
* **Scaling constant** *c* – the factor that maps the integer code back to floating point during de‑quantisation.

The forward pass simply does  

\[
\mathbf{W}^{\text{FP16}} \;\approx\; \frac{\mathbf{W}^{\text{int}_k}}{c},
\]

and back‑propagation uses the de‑quantised float matrix while only updating LoRA parameters.

If we naïvely choose the integer representation (e.g., unsigned Int4 or IEEE Float4), we waste bits on values that rarely appear in LLM weights—especially outliers—because those distributions are far from uniform. This is where **NF4** shines.

---

## 2. NormalFloat (NF4): Matching the Weight Distribution  

### 2.1 The Core Insight  
Empirically, pretrained transformer weights are *zero‑centered* and closely follow a normal distribution \(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\). In such a case an *information‐theoretic* optimum is to allocate equal probability mass to each quantization bin—exactly what **quantile quantisation** does.

Intuitively:

* If each bin contains roughly the same number of values, we use every bit pattern efficiently.
* The bin boundaries are placed where the underlying density changes little, minimizing average distortion.

### 2.2 Constructing NFk (k = 4)  

1. **Compute theoretical quantiles** of a standard normal \(N(0,1)\).  
   For a k‑bit code we need \(2^k+1\) breakpoints \(\{q_i\}\), where  

   \[
   q_i = Q_X\!\left(\frac{i}{2^k+1}\right), \qquad i = 0,\dots ,2^k,
   \]

   and \(Q_X(\cdot)\) is the normal quantile function.
2. **Create symmetric bin centers** by averaging consecutive quantiles:

   \[
   v_i = \frac{1}{2}\!\Bigl(Q_X\!\bigl(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1}\bigr) + Q_X\!\bigl(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1}\bigr)\Bigr), \quad i=0,\dots ,2^k-1.
   \]

3. **Force an exact zero representation** – because many tensors contain padding or bias terms that are exactly zero, we need one codeword to map precisely onto zero. We therefore split the positive/negative halves asymmetrically (using \(2^{k-1}\) negative codes and \(2^{k-1}+1\) positive codes) and drop one duplicate zero entry.
4. **Normalize into \([-1,1]\)** – both the weight block and the codebook are linearly scaled so their ranges match before clipping/clamping to the nearest code.

All together this yields **k‑bit NormalFloat (NFk)**; for our setting \(k=4\), giving us **NF4** with sixteen well‑placed levels that mirror a Gaussian PDF.

### 2.3 Why NF4 Beats Float‑Int Quantisation  

| Data type | Average distortion on Gaussian data | Empirical perplexity (LLaMA family) |
|-----------|------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| Int8 (8 bits) | Baseline | — |
| FP8 (8 bits) | Slightly higher | — |
| **Int4 / Float4** | ≈ 2× worse than NF4 | Higher PPL |
| **NF4** | Minimal – matches theoretical optimum | Lowest PPL among all 4‑bit variants |

The paper reports *significant* gains over both Int4 and Float4 across many models (OPT, BLOOM, Pythia, LLaMA), confirming that aligning quantisation bins with the weight histogram pays off in practice—not just theory.

---

## 3. Double Quantization: Squeezing Out Scaling Overhead  

### 3.1 The Hidden Cost of Scaling Constants  

A typical block size of 64 elements means each block needs its own scaling constant *c*. If we store *c* as a full‑precision float32 we incur an extra  

\[
\frac{32\,\text{bits}}{64}=0.5~\text{bits per weight}
\]

—roughly **3 GB** for a 65B‑parameter model! That overhead dwarfs even our compact NF4 representation.

### 3.2 Two‑Stage Compression  

Double Quantization treats these *c* values as another tensor to be quantised:

1. **First level:** Original weights → NF4 + float32 scaling constants *c₂*.  
2. **Second level:** Quantise each *c₂* itself using an **8‑bit float** format (FP8) with a larger block size (e.g., 256). Because scaling constants are always non‑negative we subtract their mean first so they become centred around zero—allowing symmetric FP8 bins.
3. Store both:
   * The FP8 codes for *c₂*.  
   * A “meta” scaling factor *c₁* (still float32) that rescales groups of FP8 entries back to real values.

Mathematically,

\[
c_{{\text{real}}} = c_1^{\text{FP32}} \times c_2^{\text{FP8}},
\]

where \(c_2^{\text{FP8}}\) is obtained via dequantisation from its stored codebook.

### 3.3 Memory Savings in Numbers  

For a blocksize of 64:

* Original overhead: \(0.5~\text{bits/param}\).  
* After double quantisation:
  
  \[
  \underbrace{\frac{8\,\text{bits}}{64}}_{0.125}
   + 
   \underbrace{\frac{32\,\text{bits}}{64\times256}}_{0.002}
   \;\approx\;0.127~\text{bits/param}.
  \]

That’s a reduction of **≈ 0.37 bits per parameter**, translating into ~3 GB saved on a 65B model—a decisive margin when your GPU only has 48 GB of RAM left for everything else.

Crucially—empirically—the authors observed *no degradation* in downstream tasks (Vicuna benchmark scores remain unchanged), confirming that FP8 resolution is more than sufficient for scaling constants.

---

## 4. Putting It All Together: QLoRA’s End‑to‑End Pipeline  

When fine‑tuning an LLM with QLoRA:

1️⃣ Load the pretrained checkpoint → apply **NF4** blockwise quantisation (blocksize = 64).  
2️⃣ For each weight block store its NF4 codes plus **double‑quantised scaling constants** (FP8 + meta‐scale).  
3️⃣ Insert LoRA adapters at *every* linear layer (the memory cost here is negligible compared to activations).  
4️⃣ During forward/backward passes:
   * De‑quantise on-the-fly from NF4 → BF16 using both levels of scalars.
   * Compute gradients only w.r.t LoRA parameters; base weights stay frozen.
5️⃣ Optimiser state tensors are kept in NVIDIA’s unified memory via **paged optimisers**, eliminating occasional OOM spikes caused by gradient checkpointing on long sequences.

The result? A single GPU can fine‑tune a **65 billion parameter model** within <48 GB while achieving *identical* performance to full ‑16‑bit fine‑tuning—something previously requiring >780 GB!

---

## 5. Empirical Impact Across Model Scales  

| Model | Bits used after QLoRA | GPU RAM needed | Vicuna relative score vs ChatGPT |
|-------|-----------------------|----------------|-----------------------------------|
| LLaMA 7B | NF4 (+ DQ) | ~5 GB | ≈ 97% |
| LLaMA 33B | NF4 (+ DQ) | ~21 GB | ≈ 98% |
| LLaMA 65B | NF4 (+ DQ) | ~48 GB | ≈ 99% |

Across >1,000 experiments covering sizes from 80M up to 65B parameters, QLoRA **consistently reproduces full‑precision results**, confirming that:

* The *information loss* introduced by NF4 is fully recovered by LoRA adapters.
* Double Quantisation’s compression does not harm convergence or final quality.
* Memory savings scale linearly—larger models benefit even more because scaling constants dominate their storage cost at higher dimensions.

---

## 6️⃣ Takeaways & Future Directions  

* **Data‑aware quantisation beats naïve fixed bins** – aligning bit patterns with Gaussian weight statistics yields maximal utilisation of each codeword.
* **Double Quantisation shows how “meta” compression can unlock otherwise hidden savings** – once you compress the compressors you shave off enough space to fit massive models on modest hardware.
* The combination enables researchers without multi‑GPU clusters to experiment with instruction tuning at scales previously inaccessible.
* Open questions remain:
  - Could we push beyond four bits? Preliminary work suggests three‑bit base models plus LoRA might still recover quality.
  - Are there alternative PEFT methods (e.g., IA³ or prompt tuning) that synergise even better with NFk?
  - How does NFk behave for non‑Gaussian weight distributions such as those after heavy regularisation or pruning?

The answer may reshape how we think about “training large” models: perhaps it’s not about throwing ever more GPUs at full precision matrices but rather about smarter numeric encodings coupled with lightweight adapters—just like QLoRA demonstrates today.

--- 

**TL;DR:** By tailoring the four‐bit representation to match the Gaussian shape of LLM weights (**NF₄**) and compressing per‐block scaling factors through a second layer (**Double Quantisation**), QLoRA trims >0.35 bits per parameter—a multi‐gigabyte saving—while preserving every ounce of downstream accuracy. This technical wizardry turns “fine‑tune a trillion‐parameter beast” from an exclusive corporate privilege into something anyone with a single modern GPU can attempt.



# Paged Optimizers Explained: Tackling Gradient Checkpointing Spikes in Large‑Scale Fine‑tuning  

Fine‑tuning today’s massive language models pushes the limits of every GPU on the market. Even after compressing a 65 billion‑parameter model down to 4 bits per weight (the **NF4** format introduced by QLoRA), training can still run out of memory because **gradient checkpointing** creates short-lived “memory peaks.”  
QLoRA solves this problem with a surprisingly simple trick: *Paged Optimizers*. By letting CUDA’s **Unified Memory** automatically swap optimizer states between GPU VRAM and system RAM, QLoRA keeps those spikes under control without sacrificing speed.  

In this post we walk through why those spikes happen, how traditional solutions try (and often fail) to handle them, and exactly how QLoRA’s paged optimizers make single‑GPU fine‑tuning of gigantic models possible.

---

## Why Gradient Checkpointing Still Causes Memory Spikes  

When you train a transformer you typically need three kinds of data living at once:

| Item | Typical Size | Where it lives |
|------|--------------|----------------|
| **Model weights** | Hundreds of gigabytes (full precision) | GPU VRAM |
| **Activation buffers** | Proportional to batch × sequence length | GPU VRAM |
| **Optimizer state** (e.g., Adam’s momentum & variance) | ≈ 2 × number of parameters × 4 bytes | GPU VRAM |

Storing *all* activations throughout the forward pass would blow up VRAM even for a 7 B model. **Gradient checkpointing** alleviates this by discarding most intermediate tensors and recomputing them later during back‑propagation. The trade‑off looks clean—less static memory—but it introduces **transient peaks**:

1️⃣ The forward pass finishes → activations are freed.  
2️⃣ The backward pass starts → previously discarded layers are *recomputed* while gradients from earlier layers are still alive.  
3️⃣ Right before an optimiser step runs, both **recomputed activations** *and* **optimizer moments** coexist in VRAM.

For long sequences or larger batch sizes these temporary allocations can easily exceed the available device memory—even if your base model fits comfortably after quantisation.  

---

## A Quick Primer on NVIDIA Unified Memory  

CUDA’s *Unified Memory* creates a single virtual address space shared between CPU RAM and GPU VRAM. From the programmer’s point of view you allocate a tensor once (`managed` allocation) and let the driver decide where it lives at any moment:

* If a kernel needs a page that currently resides in main memory, the driver **page‑faults** into VRAM over PCIe automatically.  
* When VRAM becomes scarce, rarely accessed pages are evicted back to RAM without any explicit copy call from your code.

Think of it as “swap” for GPUs—exactly what operating systems have been doing for CPUs forever—but tightly integrated into the hardware scheduler so latency stays low enough for high‑performance workloads.

---

## How QLoRA Turns the Optimiser into a “Paged” Object  

The idea behind *Paged Optimizers* is straightforward: store all **optimizer moments** (Adam’s `m` and `v`, or any other auxiliary buffers) in *managed* CUDA memory instead of permanent device memory. Here’s what happens step‑by‑step during a typical iteration:

1️⃣ **Initial allocation** – When constructing the optimiser QLoRA requests managed tensors:
```python
# pseudo‑code illustrating the idea
optim = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=5e-5)
optim = qlora.PagedOptimizer(optim)   # wraps moments into managed memory
```
   Under the hood each moment tensor is created with `torch.cuda.memory_managed()` (`torch.empty(..., device='cuda', pin_memory=False)` plus a flag), which tells CUDA “this buffer may migrate”.

2️⃣ **Forward pass** – Only the forward computation touches dequantised weights (`BF16`). No optimiser state is required yet, so all managed buffers stay dormant in RAM.

3️⃣ **Backward pass & checkpoint recompute** – As soon as a layer’s gradients become available CUDA checks whether there is enough free VRAM:
   * If needed, portions of the optimiser moments are **paged out** automatically onto host RAM.
   * The recomputed activations occupy VRAM temporarily without exceeding capacity because some optimiser pages have already been displaced.

4️⃣ **Optimizer step** – Right before updating parameters PyTorch issues reads/writes over every moment tensor:
   * CUDA detects that these pages live in host RAM → it **pages them back** into VRAM.
   * Because an optimiser step touches every parameter once linearly, paging incurs essentially one bulk transfer per iteration—a cost that is dwarfed by the matrix multiplications themselves.

5️⃣ **Repeat** – After applying updates all moments go back into managed storage, ready for the next iteration’s potential page‑outs again.


### What Makes This Work Remotely Better Than Manual Offloading?

| Approach | Code complexity | Multi‑GPU support | Overhead pattern |
|----------|-----------------|-------------------|------------------|
| Manual `.to('cpu') / .to('cuda')` offload | High (explicit bookkeeping) | Needs custom collective ops | Variable spikes each time you flip |
| DeepSpeed ZeRO Stage 3 | High (process groups & partitioning) | Designed for many GPUs | Communication cost dominates |
| **QLoRA Paged Optimizer** | Minimal wrapper around any existing optimiser | Single‑GPU focused (no inter‑node sync) | One bulk copy per iteration → negligible |

In short: by relying on a feature already baked into modern CUDA drivers you avoid reinventing complex sharding mechanisms while still gaining protection against OOM crashes on a single consumer/professional card.

---

## Real‑World Impact – Training “Guanaco” on One GPU  

The authors released a family of instruction-tuned chatbots called **Guanaco**, ranging from 7 B up through 65 B parameters. Training details illustrate why paged optimizers matter:

* **Memory budget:** < 48 GB VRAM thanks to NF4 weight storage + double quantisation (`c₂` constants compressed from 0.5 bits/param → ≈ 0​.​127 bits/param).  
* **Batch size:** A modest batch size of 1–16 was used depending on model scale; nonetheless long prompt sequences (> 1024 tokens) triggered checkpoint recompute spikes that would have exceeded available VRAM without paging.
* **Throughput:** For LLaMA‑65B (`batch_size=16`) benchmark runs showed *no measurable slowdown*: training speed matched that of an identical configuration using regular (non‑paged) AdamW because paging only occurs during short update windows.
* **Result:** The largest Guanaco‐65B model reached **99.3 %** of ChatGPT performance on Vicuna while being fine‑tuned in ≈ 24 hours on a single RTX A6000 (48 GB). Without paged optimisers the job would have crashed repeatedly under identical hyperparameters.



---

## How Much Host RAM Do You Need?

Even though optimiser moments live mainly in system RAM most of their data fits comfortably thanks to two tricks introduced alongside paging:

1️⃣ **Double Quantisation** compresses per‑block scaling factors from 32 bits → FP8 (`c₂`) then applies another tiny blockwise quantisation (`c₁`). This saves roughly $0.\!127$ bits per parameter instead of $0.\!5$ bits—a reduction equivalent to ~3 GB for a 65 B model.
2️⃣ Only adapter parameters (**LoRA**) receive gradient updates; base‐model weights remain frozen after dequantisation, so their own Adam moments are never allocated at all.

Consequently ‑–a typical server equipped with ≥ 256 GB DDR4 will comfortably host all required moment buffers even for the biggest public LLMs supported by QLoRA.



---

## Quick Checklist if You Want To Try Paged Optimisers

| ✅ Item | Why It Matters |
|------|----------------|
| Use a recent CUDA driver (> 11.x) that fully supports Managed Memory page faulting.| Older drivers may fall back to synchronous copies causing stalls or OOMs.| 
| Allocate your optimiser through `qlora.PagedOptimizer` (or set `optim="paged_adam"` if using 🤗 Transformers Trainer).| Guarantees moments live in managed space.| 
| Enable `gradient_checkpointing=True` in your trainer config.| Without checkpointing you’ll already be at peak usage even before paging helps.| 
| Keep an eye on host RAM utilisation (`htop`, Windows Task Manager).| Managed tensors reside permanently in host RAM—even if they’re idle.| 
| Test with short sequences first before scaling up.| Guarantees your pipeline works before hitting edge cases.|

That’s essentially all you need—no exotic distributed setup nor custom kernel modifications.



---

## Limitations & Open Questions  

While paged optimisers dramatically increase robustness, they aren’t a silver bullet:

* **Latency spikes:** If your workload performs many tiny optimiser steps (e.g., micro-batching), frequent page‐ins could become noticeable because each step forces another roundtrip over PCIe.
* **Hardware dependence:** Unified Memory works best on recent NVIDIA GPUs that support full *on-demand page migration*. Older cards may fall back to slower software faults.
* **Memory pressure on CPU:** Very large models (>100 B) would push host requirements into multiple terabytes—a scenario where additional techniques such as hierarchical paging or swap-to-disk become necessary.
* **Multi‑GPU extensions:** Uniﬁed Memory currently stays within one physical device context; extending this idea across several GPUs would require coordinated paging strategies or adopting ZeRO‐style sharding alongside our approach.



---

## Takeaways  

1️⃣ Gradient checkpointing saves static activation memory but introduces brief peaks where recomputed tensors coexist with optimiser moments—a common cause of OOM failures at scale.  
2️⃣ QLoRA solves this by placing ALL optimiser state into CUDA’s *managed* memory pool so pages can be evicted automatically whenever VRAM runs low—a technique dubbed **Paged Optimizer**.  
3️⃣ The method needs only a lightweight wrapper around ordinary PyTorch optimisers; no extra software stacks nor multi‑GPU orchestration are required.  
4️⃣ Empirically it lets researchers fine‑tune LLaMA‑65B on a single RTX™ A6000 (48 GB) without slowing down training—the resulting Guanaco models compete head‑to‑head with commercial chatbots such as ChatGPT/GPT‑4 despite being trained solely on open datasets.  

If you’ve ever watched your notebook crash midway through an epoch because “CUDA out of memory,” give paged optimisers a try—the next generation of large‐scale fine­tuning may simply be a few lines of code away.

# Step‑by‑Step Guide: Fine‑tune a 65 B LLM on One 48 GB GPU Using QLoRA  

*TL;DR – With QLoRA you can take a frozen 65 B‑parameter LLaMA checkpoint, compress it to the new NF4 (“NormalFloat‑4”) data type, attach low‑rank LoRA adapters at **every** linear layer, and finish an instruction‑following finetune in roughly one day on a single 48 GB GPU while keeping full‑precision quality.*

---

## 1️⃣ Why QLoRA makes a 65 B finetune possible  

| Traditional 16‑bit finetune | QLoRA (4‑bit) |
|------------------------------|---------------|
| > 780 GB GPU RAM for LLaMA‑65B | < 48 GB GPU RAM |
| Full model gradients must be stored | Only LoRA gradients (+ activations) |
| Requires multi‑GPU clusters | Fits on a single RTX A6000 / RTX 4090 (48 GB) |

QLoRA achieves this by three tricks described in the paper:

1. **NF4 quantization** – an information‑theoretically optimal 4‑bit format for normally distributed weights.
2. **Double quantization** – the per‑block scaling factors themselves are stored as FP8 (≈ 0.13 bits/parameter), shaving ≈ 3 GB off a 65 B model.
3. **Paged optimizers** – optimizer states live in unified memory and spill to CPU only when needed, eliminating the occasional OOM spikes caused by gradient checkpointing.

All three are implemented in the open‑source codebase (`artidoro/qlora` + `TimDettmers/bitsandbytes`).  

> **Bottom line:** you keep the base model *frozen* in NF4 while only updating a tiny set of LoRA matrices; gradients flow through the dequantized weights during backpropagation, so no degradation is observed even on complex benchmarks like Vicuna or MMLU.

---

## 5️⃣ Defining LoRA adapters  

QLoRA ships a thin wrapper around HuggingFace’s PEFT library.  
Below is an example *YAML* configuration (`lora_config.yaml`) that mirrors what produced the best “Guanaco” models:

```yaml
r: 64                 # rank – default works well across scales
lora_alpha: 16        # scaling factor
target_modules:
  - q_proj            # attention query
  - k_proj            # key
  - v_proj            # value
  - o_proj            # output projection
  - gate_proj         # feed‑forward gate (if present)
  - up_proj           # feed‑forward up-projection
  - down_proj         # feed‑forward down-projection
lora_dropout: 0.05
bias: none            # keep biases frozen
task_type: CAUSAL_LM   # decoder‐only model like LLaMA
```

> **Why every linear layer?** The paper observed that restricting LoRA only to query/value matrices hurts performance significantly at large scales; adding adapters “at every network layer” almost eliminates any accuracy gap between full‐precision finetuning and QLoRA.

## 6️⃣ Enabling NF4 + Double Quant + Paged Optimizer  

All three switches are exposed through model loading flags in 🤗 Transformers + BitsAndBytes:

```python
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM

model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(
    "meta-llama/Llama-2-65b-hf",          # any compatible checkpoint works
    load_in_4bit=True,
    bnb_4bit_quant_type="nf4",           # <-- NF4 datatype from QLoRA paper
    bnb_4bit_use_double_quant=True,      # <-- double quantization of scaling factors
    bnb_4bit_compute_dtype="bfloat16",   # compute precision = BF16 as recommended
)
```

When using **paged AdamW**, simply request it via PEFT’s optimizer factory:

```python
from peft import get_peft_model, PeftConfig
from bitsandbytes.optim import PagedAdamW8bit   # uses unified memory internally

peft_cfg = PeftConfig.from_pretrained("path/to/lora_config.yaml")
model = get_peft_model(model, peft_cfg)

optimizer = PagedAdamW8bit(
    model.parameters(),
    lr=1e-5,
    betas=(0.9,0.999),
    eps=1e-08,
)
```

The optimizer automatically pages gradients/optimizer states between GPU and CPU whenever RAM runs low – exactly what Section 3 calls **Paged Optimizers**.

## 7️⃣ Launching the training job  

Below is a minimal `train_qlora.py` wrapper (the repo already contains one – feel free to adapt):

```python
import argparse, os
from transformers import Trainer, TrainingArguments
from datasets import load_dataset

def main():
    parser = argparse.ArgumentParser()
    parser.add_argument("--model_name_or_path")
    parser.add_argument("--dataset_path")
    parser.add_argument("--output_dir")
    parser.add_argument("--per_device_train_batch_size", type=int, default=1)
    parser.add_argument("--gradient_accumulation_steps", type=int, default=16)
    parser.add_argument("--learning_rate", type=float, default=1e-5)
    parser.add_argument("--max_seq_length", type=int, default=2048)
    parser.add_argument("--num_train_epochs", type=int, default=3)
    args = parser.parse_args()

    # -------- Load model with NF4 ---------
    from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM
    model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(
        args.model_name_or_path,
        load_in_4bit=True,
        bnb_4bit_quant_type="nf4",
        bnb_4bit_use_double_quant=True,
        bnb_4bit_compute_dtype="bfloat16",
        device_map="auto"
    )

    # -------- Attach LoRA ---------
    from peft import get_peft_model, LoraConfig
    lora_cfg = LoraConfig.from_pretrained("lora_config.yaml")
    model = get_peft_model(model, lora_cfg)

    # -------- Dataset ---------
    ds = load_dataset("json", data_files=args.dataset_path)["train"]
    
    def tokenize(batch):
        tokenized = tokenizer(batch["instruction"] + "\n" + batch.get("input","") + "\n" + batch["output"],
                              truncation=True,
                              max_length=args.max_seq_length,
                              padding="max_length")
        tokenized["labels"] = tokenized["input_ids"].copy()
        return tokenized
    
    tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(args.model_name_or_path)
    ds = ds.map(tokenize, batched=True)

    # -------- Trainer ---------
    training_args = TrainingArguments(
        output_dir=args.output_dir,
        per_device_train_batch_size=args.per_device_train_batch_size,
        gradient_accumulation_steps=args.gradient_accumulation_steps,
        learning_rate=args.learning_rate,
        fp16=False,
        bf16=True,
        max_steps=-1,
        num_train_epochs=args.num_train_epochs,
        logging_steps=10,
        save_total_limit=2,
        evaluation_strategy="no",
        report_to="none",
        dataloader_pin_memory=False,
        dataloader_num_workers=2,
        warmup_ratio=0.03,
        disable_tqdm=False,
        run_name="qlora_65b_oasst1"
    )
    
    trainer = Trainer(
        model=model,
        args=training_args,
        train_dataset=ds,
        optimizers=(optimizer, None),   # optimizer defined earlier with PagedAdamW8bit
     )
    
     trainer.train()
     trainer.save_model(args.output_dir)

if __name__ == "__main__":
   main()
```

**What really happens under the hood?**

* Each forward pass dequantizes NF4 → BF16 on‑the‑fly (`doubleDequant`).  
* Only gradients w.r.t. LoRA matrices are kept; base‐model gradients are discarded after backpropagation – this yields < 48 GB total usage even though there are > 65000 M parameters frozen in “NF4”.  
* PagedAdamW streams optimizer slots between GPU and CPU automatically; no manual checkpointing is needed beyond standard torch‐checkpointing used for activations (`trainer.use_gradient_checkpointing=True`).

## 9️⃣ Common Pitfalls & Quick Fixes  

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---------|--------------|-----|
| `CUDA out of memory` after few steps | Gradient accumulation too low *or* sequence length too long | Reduce `--gradient_accumulation_steps` or lower `--max_seq_length`. Remember that activations dominate memory once checkpointing is enabled – try `trainer.use_gradient_checkpointing=True`. |
| BitsAndBytes complains “unsupported compute capability” | Using an older GPU (< 7.x) | QLoRA needs at least compute capability 7.0 because NF4 kernels rely on tensor cores; upgrade hardware or fall back to FP8+FP16 which defeats most benefits. |
| Model loads but stays *fp32* despite flags | Missing environment variables (`BITSANDBYTES_FORCE_KERNELS=TRUE`) or outdated bitsandbytes version | Reinstall bitsandbytes from source (`pip install git+https://github.com/TimDettmers/bitsandbytes.git`) ensuring it compiles against your CUDA toolkit version. |
| Training extremely slow (> 30 tokens/sec) | FlashAttention not enabled & no mixed precision | Install flash-attention (`pip install flash-attn`) and set `--bf16` / `"torch_dtype": "bfloat16"` in trainer arguments as shown above.|

# Meet **Guanaco**: The Open‑Source Chatbot That Rivals ChatGPT  

*How a clever mix of quantization and adapters turned a 65‑billion‑parameter LLM into a state‑of‑the‑art chatbot that can be tuned on a single GPU.*

---

## Why “another” LLM is worth talking about  

Large language models have become the workhorse behind today’s conversational agents, but fine‑tuning them still feels like an exclusive club reserved for cloud giants.  
A regular 16‑bit fine‑tune of the **LLaMA‑65B** model needs **more than 780 GB** of GPU RAM – far beyond the capacity of even the most powerful consumer cards.

Enter **QLoRA** (Quantized LoRA). By keeping the bulk of the pretrained weights in a **4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4)** representation while training *only* tiny low‑rank LoRA adapters, QLoRA shrinks the memory footprint to **under 48 GB**. In practice this means you can:

* fine‑tune a **65 B** LLM on a single RTX 4090 (or equivalent)  
* finish training within **24 hours**  
* preserve the same task performance you would get from a full‑precision 16‑bit run  

The result is the **Guanaco** family of models – open‑source chatbots that sit at the top of every publicly available leaderboard we could find.

---

## A bird’s‑eye view of QLoRA’s tricks  

| Innovation | What it does | Why it matters |
|------------|--------------|----------------|
| **NF4 quantization** | Uses a data type that is *information‑theoretically optimal* for normally distributed weights (the distribution most pretrained LLM weights follow) | Gives higher fidelity than ordinary FP4 or INT4 quantization at the same bit width |
| **Double Quantization** | Quantizes the *quantization constants* themselves (FP8 + a second scale) | Saves ≈ 0.37 bits per parameter → roughly **3 GB** saved in a 65 B model |
| **Paged Optimizers** | Leverages NVIDIA’s unified memory so optimizer states can spill onto CPU RAM when needed | Prevents out‑of‑memory spikes caused by gradient checkpointing in long sequence training |
| **Full‑layer LoRA adapters** | Inserts low‑rank adapters *(L₁L₂)* after *every* linear transformation in each transformer block | Allows the tiny adapter set (≈ 0.2 % of parameters) to recover any loss from aggressive quantization |

Together these components let us treat the frozen base model as a read‑only “weight warehouse” stored in NF4 while back‑propagating gradients *through* it into the LoRA matrices. The forward pass dequantizes each block to BF16 only when needed, so computation stays fast and numerically stable.

---

## Building Guanaco  

1. **Base model** – LLaMA checkpoints at sizes 7 B, 13 B, 33 B and 65 B.  
2. **Training data** – The open‐source multilingual conversation set **OASST1** (≈ 9k high‑quality multi‑turn dialogues). The authors deliberately avoided any proprietary data such as ShareGPT or ChatGPT outputs.  
3. **Fine‑tuning recipe** –  
   * NF4 + double quantization for the frozen weights  
   * LoRA adapters (`r = …`, `s = …` as in standard LoRA) placed on *every* linear layer  
   * Cross‑entropy loss only – no RLHF or other reinforcement tricks  
   * Batch size & learning rate scaled with model size (e.g., halve LR for 33 B/65 B).  
4. **Compute budget** –  
   * **7 B** finishes in < 12 h on a consumer GPU (≈ 24 GB VRAM)  
   * **33 B** trains in ~12 h on a professional card (≈ 24–32 GB VRAM)  
   * **65 B** reaches peak quality after ~24 h on a single 48 GB GPU  

All models and the custom CUDA kernels are released under an open licence, ready to be dropped into Hugging Face’s 🤗 Transformers stack.

---

### Benchmark numbers

The authors ran two parallel evaluation pipelines:

# Rethinking Chatbot Benchmarks: Human vs. GPT‑4 Evaluation & Elo Ratings  

Large language models (LLMs) have become powerful chat assistants, yet measuring **how good** a chatbot really is remains surprisingly hard. The recent *QLoRA* work (“Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs”) tackles this problem head‑on by pairing **human judgments** with **GPT‑4 based ratings**, then turning the pairwise outcomes into a single **Elo score** for each model. In this post we walk through their evaluation pipeline, highlight where humans and LLMs agree—or clash—and explain why classic single‑score benchmarks can be misleading for chatbot quality assessment.

---

## 1. A two‑pronged evaluation design  

QLoRA’s authors did not rely on a single metric. Instead they built **parallel pipelines**:

| Aspect | Human side | LLM side |
|-------|------------|----------|
| **Datasets** | • Vicuna prompt set (≈ 80 varied instructions) <br>• OpenAssistant (OA) validation set (953 user messages) | Same two sets |
| **Generation settings** | Nucleus sampling with $p\!=\!0.9$, temperature $0.7$ | Identical settings |
| **Annotation task** | Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT): <br>– 2 annotators when comparing a model against ChatGPT <br>– 3 annotators for head‑to‑head model pairs | GPT‑4 prompted to score or pick winners using the same question/answer triples |
| **Scoring formats** | • *Relative* score out of ten (model vs. ChatGPT) <br>• *Pairwise* three‑way label {A wins, B wins, Tie} | • Same relative scoring <br>• Same three‑way labeling |

Both pipelines feed the same raw model outputs into two separate “judges”. This symmetry lets us ask directly: *Do humans and an advanced LLM rank chatbots similarly?*  

---

## 2. Human annotations versus GPT‑4 ratings  

### 2.1 Relative scoring against ChatGPT  

For each prompt the judge sees three items: the prompt itself, ChatGPT’s response, and the candidate model’s response. The judge assigns a score $s\in[0,10]$ to each answer and an optional explanation. The **overall performance** of a model is reported as  

\[
\text{Relative\%} = \frac{\displaystyle \sum_{i}s^{\text{model}}_i}{\displaystyle \sum_{i}s^{\text{ChatGPT}}_i}\times100,
\]

so a value above 100 % indicates that the candidate even outscored ChatGPT on average.

#### Ordering bias in GPT‑4  
When the researchers swapped the order of the two responses—putting the candidate first versus second—they observed a systematic advantage for the *first* answer in GPT‑4’s scores. To neutralize this artifact they report **the mean of both orderings** for every model.

### 2.2 Pairwise head‑to‑head judgments  

Instead of assigning absolute scores, judges choose which of two answers is better (or declare a tie). This reduces subjectivity because it forces a direct comparison rather than an absolute quality estimate.

### 2.3 How well do humans and GPT‑4 agree?  

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| System‑level Kendall $\tau$ | **0.43** |
| System‑level Spearman $r$ | **0.55** |
| Example‑level Fleiss $\kappa$ (human vs. GPT‑4 majority) | **0.25** |

A Kendall $\tau$ of 0.43 means that when we rank models by their average scores, humans and GPT‑4 produce **moderately similar orderings**, but there are notable divergences—particularly for smaller models such as Guanaco 7B. At the level of individual answers (Fleiss $\kappa=0.25$), agreement drops substantially, signaling that *both* judges still inject personal preferences into fine‐grained decisions.

#### What this tells us  

* **GPT‑4 is a useful proxy:** its rankings track human judgments well enough for large‐scale screening where crowdsourcing would be costly.
* **But it isn’t perfect:** ordering bias and lower example‐level agreement remind us that LLM evaluators inherit their own quirks—and should be calibrated or combined with human checks when precision matters.

---

## 3. From pairwise wins to an Elo rating  

The raw pairwise outcomes form a **tournament graph**: each edge tells us which model won over another on a particular prompt. To collapse this dense graph into a single scalar per model we can reuse a classic idea from competitive games—the **Elo rating system**.

### 3️⃣ How Elo works (in brief)

1️⃣ Each model starts with an initial rating $R_0 = 1000$.  
2️⃣ For a match between models $A$ and $B$, compute the expected win probability for $A$:  

\[
E_A = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_B-R_A)/400}} .
\]

3️⃣ After observing the actual result $S_A\in\{1,\tfrac12,0\}$ (win/tie/loss), update $A$’s rating:

\[
R_A \leftarrow R_A + K\,(S_A-E_A),
\]

where $K=32$ controls how aggressively ratings react to new evidence.

Because every match contributes an update, repeating the tournament many times (the authors ran **10 000 random seedings**) smooths out order effects—the final Elo values reflect each model’s *overall skill* across all pairings rather than a single noisy measurement.

### Why Elo beats raw averages  

* **Scale independence:** A model can rank higher even if its absolute scores are lower overall—as long as it consistently beats stronger opponents.
* **Robustness to variance:** Ties or close calls only shift rankings modestly; outlier victories have limited impact because expectations are already factored in.
* **Interpretability:** An Elo difference of $\Delta R=400$ predicts roughly a $10{:}1$ win odds ratio intuitively understandable by anyone who has watched chess or esports tournaments.

In QLoRA’s experiments, Elo rankings placed **Guanaco 33B/65B** just behind ChatGPT/GPT‑4 on both Vicuna and OA benchmarks—a far more granular picture than “99 % of ChatGPT’s score” alone could convey.

## 4. Why classic chatbot benchmarks can be deceptive  

### 4.1 Overlap between training data & test prompts  

The Vicuna benchmark consists of user prompts drawn from public ShareGPT conversations—a source also used by many open models during finetuning (e.g., Vicuna itself). This overlap can inflate scores without reflecting genuine generalization ability.

### 4.2 Dataset–task mismatch  

QLoRA’s extensive experiments revealed systematic *orthogonality* between two popular metrics:

| Benchmark | What it measures | Typical strong performer |
|-----------|------------------|--------------------------|
| **MMLU** (5‑shot) | Knowledge across academic subjects | FLAN‐v2 finetuned models |
| **Vicuna/OA chat** | Conversational instruction following | OASST1 finetuned Guanaco models |

A model that aces MMLU may still flounder on interactive dialogue tasks because the finetuning data (“knowledge” vs “instruction”) does not align with what the benchmark probes.

### 4.3 Wide confidence intervals & ambiguous scales  

Even after extensive testing, many models’ Vicuna relative scores overlap heavily (e.g., Guanaco 33B vs Vicuna 13B). The underlying reason is twofold:

1️⃣ The *absolute* ten‐point scale used by evaluators lacks clear anchors—“8/10” can mean different things across annotators or prompts.
2️⃣ Small sample sizes amplify variance; some prompts dominate ranking shifts because they are inherently easier/harder for certain models.

These issues motivate moving away from single‐score tables toward **pairwise tournament + Elo**, which naturally accounts for uncertainty through repeated random seedings and provides error bars derived from rating variance.

---

## 5. Takeaways for researchers and practitioners  

* **Pairwise judging + Elo offers a more stable leaderboard** than raw accuracy or single‐score benchmarks—especially when dealing with conversational agents where “correctness” is fuzzy.
* **Automated LLM evaluators like GPT‑4 are valuable**, but they must be calibrated (e.g., averaging across orderings) and complemented with human checks for high‐stakes deployments.
* **Benchmark choice matters:** choose an instruction dataset that resembles your target use case; otherwise you risk optimizing for an irrelevant metric.
* **Report uncertainty:** confidence intervals or rating variance should accompany any claimed superiority—otherwise readers can’t gauge whether differences are statistically meaningful.
* **Open resources help reproducibility:** QLoRA releases its adapters, CUDA kernels, and full paired comparison data so anyone can rerun tournaments or experiment with alternative rating schemes.

By rethinking how we evaluate chatbots—favoring relative wins over absolute points—we get closer to answering *“Which assistant really feels better in conversation?”* while keeping our assessments transparent and reproducible.
