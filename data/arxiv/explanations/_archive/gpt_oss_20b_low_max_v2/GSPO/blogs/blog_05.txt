# Breaking Down the Mathematics of Importance Sampling in RL for LLMs

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become indispensable for pushing the limits of large language models (LLMs). Yet the path is littered with unstable training dynamics, especially when former algorithms apply **token‑level** importance ratios that clash with the sequence‑level rewards of LLMs.  
In this post we unpack the key equations from “Group Sequence Policy Optimization” (GSPO) to show why **sequence‑level** sampling and clipping win the day, how length normalization dampens variance, and where clipping bounds should live.

---

## 1. The Core Backdrop: RL for Autoregressive Language Models

An autoregressive LLM parameterised by \(\theta\) is simply a policy:
\[
\pi_\theta(y \mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x,y_{<t}),
\]
where \(x\) is a query and \(y\) a generated response.  
We obtain a scalar reward \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) from an external verifier.

The goal of policy optimisation is to improve \(\pi_\theta\) so that higher‑reward responses become more likely.

---

## 2. Importance Sampling in RL: A Recap

When we sample trajectories from an old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) but optimise a new policy \(\pi_{\theta}\), we need to correct for distribution mismatch:

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}} f(z)
=\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}
    \left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\, f(z)\right].
\]

*Key property*: The weight must be averaged over many independent samples from the behaviour distribution; only then does it act as a trustworthy reweighting factor.

---

## 3. Token‑Level vs Sequence‑Level Ratios

### 3.1 Token‑Level (GRPO)

GRPO applies the importance weight at every token:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
   \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
        {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]
with an advantage estimate
\[
\widehat A_{i,t}=\widehat A_i
   = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\mu}{\sigma},
   \qquad
   \mu = \operatorname{mean}\{r(x,y_j)\},~
   \sigma=\operatorname{std}\{r(x,y_j)\}.
\]

The GRPO objective (neglecting KL terms) is

\[
J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
   \mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}\Bigg[
     \frac1G\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac1{|y_i|}
          \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
          \min\!\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i,\,
          \text{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)
               \widehat A_i\bigr)
   \Bigg].
\]

**The abuse**: each token’s weight uses only that token’s next‑token distribution—no averaging—and still feeds into a variance‑heavy gradient.

### 3.2 Sequence‑Level (GSPO)

GSPO collapses all tokens into one ratio:

\[
s_i(\theta)=
   \left(
     \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}
   \right)^{1/|y_i|}
 =  e^{\,{\displaystyle {1}/{|y_i|}
           }\sum_t
            \log w_{i,t}(\theta)} .
\]

Notice that we **divide by the sequence length** to obtain a *length‑normalised* weight.  

The GSPO objective becomes

\[
J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
  \mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}\Bigg[
      \frac1G\,\sum_{i=1}^{G}
      \min\!\bigl( s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,
            \text{clip}(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon_s,
                               1+\varepsilon_s)\,\widehat A_i\bigr)
  \Bigg].
\]

Here the same advantage is broadcast across all tokens in \(y_i\), matching the reward’s scope.

---

## 4. Why Token Importance Weights Cause Trouble

### 4.1 Variance Accumulation

For a single token,
\(w_{i,t}(\theta)\approx e^{A_t}\) can explode or vanish if the new policy changes sharply.  
During a long response (\(|y|\approx 2000+,\)) these multiplicative factors accumulate exponentially:

- If many tokens receive \(w<0.5,\) their product drifts toward zero → gradient vanishes.
- Conversely, if many tokens receive \(w>2,\) the product blows up → unstable updates.

GRPO’s per‑token clipping tries to tame this but it only limits the *local* weight range; it does not remove the exponential drift because each step still multiplies a potentially extreme factor.

### 4.2 Misaligned Unit of Reward

Rewards are evaluated on an *entire* response:
\(r(x,y)\).
Applying per‑token corrections forces us to interpret “how well did this single token do?”—an ill‑posed question.

Sequence–level weights respect the natural unit: they tell us how much more (or less) likely the whole response is under the new policy relative to reward we actually saw.

---

## 5. Length Normalisation: Taming Variance on Paper

The raw sequence ratio
\(S_i=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\)
scales roughly as \(e^{|y|\cdot c}\), where \(c=\mathbf E[\log w]\).
By raising it to exponent \(1/|y|\):

- We map \(S_i^{\,1/|y|} = e^c,\)
- Independent of sequence length.
  
Thus all responses, regardless of length, get weights in roughly the same numerical band (e.g., [0.9, 1.3]).

This length normalisation dramatically shrinks variance and allows us to pick meaningful clipping ranges that are comparable across tasks.

---

## 6. Clipping Bounds: From Tokens to Sequences

In GRPO we have:
\<br>Left clip: ~0.8 – 0.9  
Right clip: ~1.2 – 1.3  
*(example values, often tuned via validation)*

Because token weights can hover widely even after clipping—some still far below or above unity—variance remains high.

In GSPO, after normalisation most weights cluster near *one*. Therefore we can afford **narrower bounds**, e.g.:

- Left: \(3\times10^{-4}\)
- Right: \(4\times10^{-4}\)

These tiny offsets guarantee that only responses whose likelihood has drifted markedly (shown by “score” differences in log space beyond ≈\(10^{-4}\)) are clipped away.
Counterintuitively, even though GSPO clips *more tokens* overall, those clips are on *less informative* tokens because the sequence weight already absorbed most evidence about reward quality.

---

## 7. Variance Analysis Snapshot

Let’s approximate variances under both methods:

### Token Weight
\(w_t = e^{v_t},~ v_t=\log w_t.\)

Suppose \(v_t ~ N(0,\sigma^2)\). Then
\(w_t~ = e^{v_t}\).  
Variance:
\
\(Var[w_t]=e^{2\sigma^2}-e^{\sigma^2}.\)

For long sequences (\(|y|\)), product of these weights yields variance that explodes at rate \(e^{O(|y|\sigma^2)}.\)

### Sequence Weight
\(s = e^{({1}/{|y|})V}, V=\sum_t v_t.\)

If each \(v_t~N(0,\sigma^2)\), then \(V ~ N(0,\ |y|\sigma^2).\)

Thus 
\( s = e^{V/|y|} ~ N(0,\sigma^2/|y|).\)

Variance shrinks proportional to \(1/|y|\).  
Hence longer sequences stabilize our estimator!

---

## 8. Practical Takeaways for Data Scientists

1. **Align importance weights with reward granularity** – Use sequence weights when rewards are holistic; token weights introduce needless noise.
2. **Apply length normalisation** – This reduces variance in high‑entropy models and unifies clipping ranges across tasks.
3. **Clip on sequences** – Reset bounds appropriately; narrower intervals work because sequence ratios are already close to one.
4. **Check variance growth empirically** – For any new objective tweak, compute expected weight variance over sample lengths; if it scales with length then you’re likely reintroducing instability.
5. **Beware of expert activation volatility** – In MoE models token-level ratios become unreliable due to routing changes; sequence-level ratios sidestep this issue naturally.

---

## 9. Wrapping Up

The shift from token‑level to sequence‑level importance sampling in GSPO isn’t just a fancy tweak—it is grounded in classical importance sampling theory and tackled head‐on variance and stability problems that plagued earlier RL algorithms for LLMs like GRPO.

By carefully deriving sequence weights, normalising for length, and adopting tighter but well‐calibrated clipping bounds, GSPO delivers:

- More stable training dynamics,
- Higher sample efficiency,
- Elimination of complex tricks (Routing Replay),
- Simpler integration between training and inference engines.

For anyone building RL pipelines around large language models—or simply exploring your next objective tweak—make sure your importance ratios keep pace with your rewards’ granularity and keep those variance numbers under control!
