# MoE Meets RL: Why GSPO Eliminates Routing Replay

Large language models are increasingly built with **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)** architectures to keep parameter counts manageable while scaling compute. In an MoE model, each token traverses a sparse set of experts – a routing decision that depends on the current policy. When we add *reinforcement learning* (RL) on top of such a model, we run into a surprisingly subtle problem: **expert‑activation volatility**. Below I walk through what that means, why the old fix—*Routing Replay*—was cumbersome, and how the newest RL algorithm, **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**, solves the problem entirely.

---

## 1. The MoE/‑RL Conundrum

A MoE Transformer splits its computations across many expert networks. For a given token, the controller chooses *which experts* to activate; all other experts are bypassed for that step. 

When we train RL policies using algorithms like **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, we need an *importance ratio* for each token:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\, .
\]

GRPO uses these token‑level ratios inside a clipping mechanism to keep updates within a “proximal” region. The algorithm assumes that the denominator and numerator are computed over the **same set of active experts** so that the ratio stays faithful to how much probability mass has actually shifted.

In practice, however, applying RL to a deep MoE model causes the set of activated experts to change dramatically after every gradient step. Even for the *same* response \(y_i\) generated from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), \(\pi_{\theta}\) may route some tokens to entirely new experts. Knowledge workers who have tried this reported that **10 % of experts per token can change** after a single update in a 48‑layer Qwen3‑30B‑A3B-Base model.

That volatility wreaks havoc with those token‑level ratios:
- The denominator and numerator see very different network activations → \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) becomes noisy.
- The clipping drastically limits useful gradients or throws away too many samples.
- Training stalls or collapses – precisely what happened in many earlier RL runs.

---

## 2. Routing Replay: A Workaround

To circumvent expert volatility, researchers introduced **Routing Replay**:

| Step | What’s done? | Why it helps |
|-------|--------------|--------------|
| 1 | Store the expert set used by \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) when sampling each rollout token. | Locks in the routing decisions for the batch. |
| 2 | When computing \(\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\), *force* \(\pi_{\theta}\) to use exactly the same expert set as \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). | Guarantees that denominator and numerator share identical activations, so \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) is reliable. |

While Routing Replay works – it brings GRPO back into convergence – it comes with heavy penalties:

1. **Memory overhead** – every token’s routing mask must be cached.
2. **Communication bandwidth** – in distributed training you often need to ship these masks across workers.
3. **Capacity constraints** – you’re effectively limiting each forward pass to one subset of experts; you lose access to the full modelling power.

In short, Routing Replay technically fixes a symptom but adds engineering complexity and throttles performance.

---

## 3. GSPO’s Sequence‑Level Insight

The root cause of GRPO’s fragility turns out to be its *token‑level importance weighting*. The RL reward is always awarded on entire sequences; applying an off‑policy correction at each next‑token step introduces unnecessary variance and instability.

GSPO rethinks this at two levels:

1. **Unit of optimization matches unit of reward**  
   - Instead of weighting each token individually, GSPO weights an entire sequence:
     \[
     s_i(\theta)=\left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_i|}.
     \]
   - This ratio reflects how much *overall* probability mass has changed between old and new policies for that exact response.

2. **Clipping at the sequence level**  
   - Entire responses are clipped or discarded if their likelihood ratio strays outside \((1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon)\).
   - No per‑token importance ratios → no reference to expert sets for individual tokens.

Because \(\pi_\theta(y_i|x)\) is computed over the whole sequence, any routing changes affect only one aggregate number rather than tens of token‑specific scalars. Even if some experts flip their routing choices mid‑sequence, the *total* likelihood rarely drifts as wildly as individual token ratios would suggest.

Consequently:
- **GSPO ignores expert volatility** by not inspecting per-token routing.
- The algorithm no longer needs any special handling or replay buffers.
- Gradient estimates become cleaner and more consistent across updates.

---

## 4. What Happens in Practice?

Experiments on Qwen3 variants highlight two key effects:

| Metric | GRPO + Routing Replay | GSPO (no replay) |
|--------|-----------------------|------------------|
| Training stability | Often stalls without replay; otherwise noisy if replay skipped | Stable from epoch 0 |
| Reward curve | Requires careful clipping tuning | Smooth ascent with broader clipped range |
| Model performance | Slightly lower due to capacity loss from fixed routing | Higher Pass@1 on AIME’24 & LiveCodeBench |
| Infrastructure simplicity | Extra buffers + extra communication | Pure sequence-level likelihood computation |

Also noteworthy: despite discarding more tokens overall (GSPO clips whole answers more aggressively), it usually trains *faster per update* because it uses only reliable gradients—doubling effective sample usage compared with noisy GRPO samples churned out by volatile expert activations.

---

## 5. Broader Benefits Beyond MoE

Because GSPO removes per-token importance ratios entirely:

- It becomes tolerant to *precision discrepancies* between training engines (Megatron) and inference engines (SGLang/vLLM). You can compute likelihoods directly on inference engines rather than recomputing them via expensive distributed training code.
- It scales naturally to multi‑turn RL or partial rollout settings where you already generate long sequences with significantly different per‑token routes.
- Infrastructure overhauls are simplified: no special routing memory hooks or custom kernels needed—just regular distributed logits accumulation.

---

## 6. Takeaway

> **MoE models make RL hard because every token might switch experts after each gradient step; GRPO’s per-token importance ratios become useless without an extra “routing replay” trick that perplexes engineers and hurts capacity.**

> **GSPO sidesteps all this by stepping back: it treats an entire answer as one object for risk estimation and clipping. That sequence‑level focus tolerates expert changes harmlessly and delivers steadier gradients, higher rewards, and simpler training code—all without losing MoE expressivity.**

If you’re building large‐scale RL pipelines on sparse Transformers, ditching Routing Replay isn’t just a convenience—it’s now standard practice thanks to GSPO’s elegant sequence‐level design.
