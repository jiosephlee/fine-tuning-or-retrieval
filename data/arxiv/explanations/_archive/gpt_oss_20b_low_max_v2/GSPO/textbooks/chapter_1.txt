## 1 Introduction to Reinforcement Learning for Language Models

In recent years the practice of scaling language models has shifted from a purely supervised–learning–driven mindset to one in which reinforcement learning (RL) is woven into the training loop. This chapter explains why RL has become indispensable for large language models (LLMs), how it unlocks higher‑level reasoning abilities, and what pitfalls have arisen when applying standard RL algorithms to these enormous, often sparse, architectures. By the end of this section you will understand the motivation that drives the development of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO).

### 1.1 Historical evolution of RL in NLP

The first steps toward RL for NLP appeared in early *In‑context learning* settings, where a language model was asked to adapt quickly to a new task without additional gradient updates. Soon after, research advanced toward *self‑play* and *behavioral cloning* approaches that fine‑tuned models on their own generated samples under reward signals such as safety or instruction alignment. The turning point came with the publication of algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and, more specifically for language models, GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization). These works introduced off‑policy corrections via importance sampling and a clipping mechanism that bound policy updates within a stable neighborhood.

While these early efforts yielded notable progress—enabling LLMs to solve algebraic problems or automate code generation—they also revealed fundamental limitations. The most noticeable issue is **model collapse**: during RL training a model can abruptly lose its ability to generate coherent text and never recover, even after extensive attempts at hyper‑parameter tuning or checkpoint restoration. Moreover, the trend toward *Mixture‑of‑Experts* (MoE) architectures—where only a subset of expert sub‑modules is activated per token—has provoked additional stability difficulties that are exacerbated by conventional token‑level importance weights.

### 1.2 Capabilities enabled by RL: reasoning, math, programming

The practical payoff of RL in NLP is evident when we examine tasks that require deep reasoning over multiple steps of inference. For instance:

- **Mathematics**: RL fine‑tuning on algebraic problem sets leads LLMs to produce stepwise proof traces that are correct with significantly higher frequency than purely supervised models. The learned reward often prizes the correctness of each logical step rather than merely matching ground truth labels.
  
- **Programming**: Code generation benefits from an RL signal derived from an automated judge that evaluates whether a submitted program passes all tests. This converts the training objective into a measure of *functional correctness* rather than token‑wise fluency.

In each case the reward is naturally attached to entire responses—a window into how well the model can orchestrate an entire sequence of tokens, not just individual words.

### 1.3 Common failure modes: catastrophic collapse, instability in MoE

When scaling up to billion‑parameter models or deeper MoE variants, two failure modes dominate:

| Failure Mode | Description | Root Cause |
|--------------|-------------|------------|
| **Catastrophic Model Collapse** | Sudden loss of coherent generation capabilities that cannot be recovered by checkpointing or hyper‑parameter search | Inappropriate use of token‑level importance weights leading to high variance in gradient estimates; clipping mechanisms amplify this variance; absence of a value function compounding errors |
| **MoE Instability** | Dramatic fluctuations in expert activation patterns between consecutive policy updates | Token‑level importance ratios are highly sensitive to which experts are active; small changes in activation lead to large probability shifts; no guarantee that old and new policies activate the same experts |

The underlying theme is that conventional RL algorithms treat each token independently and then aggregate rewards across tokens. Yet rewards are inherently *sequence\-level*. This mismatch drives the theoretical problems that GSPO later resolves.

### 1.4 “What is the problem we solve?”: Motivation for a new algorithm

The central observation underpinning GSPO is that **the unit of optimization must match the unit of reward**. In GRPO—and related token‑level methods—the importance ratio is computed at each token position:
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]
Because this ratio depends on only one sample drawn from an on‐to‐policy distribution \(\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}\), it fails to serve as an effective off‑policy correction; instead it injects uncontrolled noise into gradients. When sequences grow very long (hundreds or thousands of tokens), this per‑token miscalibration compounds until training diverges completely.

Conversely, if we compute an importance ratio every *entire response*, we obtain:
\[
s_i(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_i\mid x)},
\]
which properly corrects sampling bias at the same granularity as the reward \(r(x,y_i)\). Moreover, normalizing \(s_i(\theta)\) by sequence length removes variance driven by token count differences.

In addition to improving statistical efficiency—since fewer low‐quality samples are discarded—this sequence‑level perspective stabilizes MoE training: expert activations vary slowly when we evaluate full sequence likelihoods rather than token probabilities tied tightly to specific routing decisions.

Therefore GSPO answers three essential questions:

1. **How can we avoid high variance caused by token‑level importance sampling?** By shifting clipping and weighting to sequence likelihood ratios.
2. **Why does MoE training become tractable under GSPO?** Because sequence likelihoods remain stable regardless of which experts actually fire.
3. **Can we simplify RL infrastructure without sacrificing performance?** Yes—sequence likelihoods tolerate numerical precision disparities between training and inference engines.

With this motivation firmly grounded in both theory and empirical evidence, GSPO emerges as a principled replacement for GRPO in large‐scale language model reinforcement learning.
