\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 3: # 3. Reinforcement Learning Optimizers: PPO, GRPO, and Their Limitations

Large language models (LLMs) are typically trained with proxy objectives such as maximum likelihood estimation (MLE).  When we want the model to behave in accordance with external criteria—human preferences, task‑specific metrics, or domain‑specific verification functions—reinforcement learning (RL) is invoked.  The RL training loop for LLMs follows the familiar pattern of *behavior* versus *target* policies and off‑policy correction by means of importance sampling weights.  Two algorithms that dominate modern LLM RL pipelines are Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).  PPO is a generic algorithm that has been widely adopted across domains.  GRPO, while simplifying a number of engineering challenges in large‑scale language model settings, exhibits inherent mathematical deficiencies that become catastrophic when applied to gigantic models or very long generation horizons.  In this chapter we unpack the two methods in detail, revisit their objective formulations, and explain the root causes of the instability that plagues GRPO.

---

## 3.1 Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO): Clipping Mechanism and Token‑wise Importance Ratios

Proximal Policy Optimization \citep{ppo} is arguably the industry standard for RL when the policy is a neural network with a large number of parameters.  The core idea of PPO is to keep successive updates close to each other in parameter space so as to avoid policy collapse due to drastic changes in the action distribution.  This principle is implemented with an on‑policy objective that still uses data collected from a *behavior* policy denoted \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  Key components of the PPO objective for an autoregressive language model are:

1. **Token‑wise importance ratios**  
   For every token \(y_{t}\) produced at time step \(t\) in a generated response \(y\) given a query \(x\), we compute
   \[
   w_{t}(\theta)
   \;=\;
   \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x, y_{<t})}
        {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{t}\mid x, y_{<t})},
   \]
   which is simply the ratio of the target policy's probability to that of the old policy for that specific token at that specific context.

2. **Advantage estimates**  
   The advantage \(\widehat{A}_{t}\) quantifies how much better or worse taking action \(y_{t}\) at time \(t\) is compared to an expectation under a value function baseline:
   \[
   \widehat{A}_{t} = r(x, y) - V_{\phi}(x, y_{<t}),
   \]
   where \(V_{\phi}\) is an auxiliary value network and \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) is given by an external verifier or reward function.

3. **Clipping**  
   PPO replaces the usual policy gradient surrogate
   \[
   w_{t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_{t}
   \]
   with a clipped surrogate
    \[
    \min\!\left(
      w_{t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_{t},\;
      \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(w_{t}(\theta),~1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,
      \widehat{A}_{t}
    \right),
    \]
    where the clipping interval controlled by hyperparameter \(\varepsilon\) (typically small, e.g., \(0.2\)) enforces that large deviations from the old policy do not dominate learning.

4. **Full objective**  
   Averaging over all tokens in all rollouts gives:
   \[
     \mathcal{J}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)
     = 
     {\mathbb E}_{ x\sim\mathcal D,\; y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
     \!\left[
       \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
         \min( w_{t}(\theta)\widehat A_t,\;
               {{\rm clip}(w_t(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)}\,\widehat A_t )
     \right].
   \]

**Intuition**:  
If a particular token’s probability under the current policy has spiked dramatically relative to the old policy (i.e., \(w_t>1+\varepsilon\)), PPO will clip it so that gradient contributions from this token do not overstress that direction of update; similarly for tokens whose probability has dropped below \(1-\varepsilon\). The clipping strategy hence enforces *proximal updates* in distribution space: the new policy’s likelihood ratio cannot stray far from unity.

## 3.2 PPO’s Reliance on a Value Network: Memory Cost & Variance Issues

The value network sits at the heart of PPO’s advantage estimation.  It predicts expected cumulative reward from a partial sequence:
\[
V_{\phi}(x,y_{<t})
 = 
{\mathbb E}_{y'\sim\pi_\theta}\!\bigl[r(x,y')\,\big|\,x, y_{<t}\bigr].
\]
In practice this predictor requires a neural architecture comparable in size to the language model itself so as to capture subtle dependencies across tokens and context.  

Two major drawbacks emerge:

1. **Memory and compute overhead**  
   Adding yet another gigantic network nearly doubles GPU memory usage during training iterations—an expensive proposition when hundreds or thousands are being trained concurrently on modern distributed systems.

2. **Statistical variance**  
   The value estimator must be _accurate_ across diverse contexts; any bias or high variance propagates multiplicatively through \(\widehat A_t = r - V_\phi\). Since rewards are bounded but highly idiosyncratic (e.g., +1 if a verifier flags correctness), even moderate imprecision in \(V_\phi\) can lead to noisy gradients that destabilize learning.

Hence practitioners often switch to value‑free algorithms such as GRPO which sidestep these issues albeit at cost of other pitfalls.

## 3.3 GRPO Overview: Group Advantage Estimation & Token‑wise Importance Weights

Group Relative Policy Optimization [GRPO] was introduced as a lightweight alternative designed specifically for large language models.  Instead of deploying a value network it crafts advantages directly within each mini‑batch by comparing multiple responses generated from *the same query*.  

Given:
- A query set \(\mathcal D=\{x_k\}\),
- A group size \(G\): for each query \(x_k\), generate \(G\) responses
  \(y_1,\dots,y_G\xleftarrow{\;\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x_k)}{}\);

GRPO defines:

- Token importance ratios identical to PPO but evaluated under each generated sequence:
  \[
    w_{i,t}(\theta)
      = 
      \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x_k,y_{i,<t})}
           {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x_k,y_{i,<t})},
  \]
- Sequence rewards computed by verifier:
  \(r(x_k,y_j)\in[0,1]\).

The group advantage for *token* $y_{i,t}$ multiplies across its host response:
\[
  {\widehat A}_{i,t}
    = 
    {\widehat A}_i
    =
    
    
    {\rm Std}^{-1}\!
        {\rm mean}^{-1}
      [
        r(x_k,y_i)-{\overline r},
        {\rm std}(r)
      },
```
where
`${\,{\overline r}={{\(1}/{G}}\sum_j r(x_k,y_j)}}$`
and ${\,{\rm std}(r)}$ denotes the standard deviation over response rewards inside the same group.

The resulting GRPO objective becomes:
\[
{
\nabla_\theta
&
(\textit{{GRPO}})
= 
{\mathbb E}_{x,k,\{y_i\}}\!
        {\Bigg[}
         &0&\\
            &{
              {G}^{-1}{\,|y_i|}^{-1}
                }
                &\\
          &&\\
          &{
             w_{i,t}(\theta){\,{\widehat A}_i}
             }\,
               &&
             {\bm?}
                  ~~~?~?
}{]
}
```
*(Equation~(8) in paper)*

In words: GRPO averages importance ratios over all tokens in every response and applies them separately than token’s advantage estimate come from its host response's relative reward compared against peers in its group.

## 3.4 Illustration of GRPO’s Objective Function

CRUCIAL TO UNDERSTANDING THE FLAW IS THE PRECISE FORM OF THE OBJECTIVE.

Let us write out all terms explicitly:

- For query $x$, after generating $G$ responses $\{y_i : i=1,\dots,G\}$ from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ we compute per‑token importance ratios $w_{i,t}$.  
- Since all tokens inside one response share *the same advantage*, ${{{\widehat A}_i}}$, we can move it out of inner summation:
  
~~~
J_GRPO(θ)=E_x,E_y[ G^{-1} Σ_i |y_i|^{-1} Σ_t min( w_i,t·Ȧ_i,
                                   clip(w_i,t,[…])·Ȧ_i ) ].
~~~

Notice two key patterns:

- **Token level weighting**: every token receives its own weight $w_{i,t}$ even though they contribute collectively to sequence credit.
- **Per-token advantage alignment**: because adv original came from comparing *sequences*, applying it at token level dilutes coherence between reward and update direction.

The geometric mean of ’importance ratios’ is built token‑wise rather than sequence‑wise – an approach that will later surface as problematic due to high variance compounded over long sequences.

## 3.5 Sources of Instability in GRPO

### High‑variance importance weights

When token probabilities differ drastically between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, their ratio can grow very large or shrink near zero.
The product over all tokens inside one response produces an aggregate weight equal essentially to:
\( s_i(\theta)=w^{+...+}_i=\prod_t w_{i,t}\).
But GRPO discards this aggregate quantity entirely and relies on *average* per-token weights instead.
Consequently:

- In slices where early tokens already diverge widely between old and new policies,
the variance of subsequent per-token ratios explodes,
propagating into noisy gradients.
- The clipping term only bounds individual terms but does not regulate their collective product.
Hence during training on long responses ($|y|\gg30$), errors accumulate steadily until difference grows beyond tolerable magnitude — leading inevitably to “model collapse” whereby internal LM scores become nonsensical.

### Expert‑activation volatility

Large MoE LLMs break up compute into sparse experts activated conditioned on input tokens.
When transitioning from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ to $\pi_\theta$, many tokens become routed through completely different experts.
This volatility makes token likelihoods highly discontinuous between successive steps:
\(p_\theta(y_t|\cdot)\neq p_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t|\cdot)\).
Because $w_{i,t}$ uses exactly these quantities linearly, its denominator becomes very unstable — sometimes approaching zero if an expert was never active before.
Thus high variance propagates catastrophically into gradients even for modestly sized MoE models (48 layers, above).

In practice one mitigates this issue by “routing replay”, which freezes routing decisions drawn from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ while computing new probabilities under $\pi_\theta$. This workaround adds overhead but only masks underlying theoretical deficiency: token–level corrections fail when off-policy behavior changes discontinuously across expert boundaries.

### Misaligned unit of optimization vs reward

Rewards are _sequence‐level_ scalars because verification functions examine complete generation outputs.
However GRPO’s gradient treats each token independently but shares a flow‐scaled advantage taken from sequence comparison.
This _mismatch_ leads to learning signals that do not respect how rewards propagate along generation horizons.
So while batch outcomes may be correct overall, intermediate tokens throw up spurious signals which accumulate into bad local optima — especially severe when query sets expand or generations lengthen during scaling experiments.

### Consequences observed empirically

During extensive RL training cycles:

- Marginal clipping intervals needed for stability change radically as model size grows; small clips compensated by token compensation becomes insufficient.
- After even modestly long training runs (>∼10M steps), loss curves exhibit rapid divergence altogether unabated by hyperparameter sweeps.
- Retrieval-based rollback fails: attempting to resume training with prior checkpoint does not recover once clobbering occurs; reconvergence absent even after recomputing environment rewards due to changed behaviors downstream ([references] note irreversibility).

These phenomena underline why GSPO was invented – aligning importance sampling doctrine with true sequence‐level guarantees eliminates these irregularities completely.

--- 

**Takeaway:**  
While PPO remains robust because it explicitly matches reward granularity with log-probability corrections (token-wise), its reliance on a value network imposes prohibitive cost at scale.  
GRPO removes this deficit but introduces severe statistical pathologies through its token-wise importance weighting scheme and misalignment between reward unit and optimization focus.  
Addressing both concerns concludes our discussion preceding introduction of GSPO – an algorithm designed precisely around sequence-level importance weights yielding stable scaling up-sides for huge MoE-based language models such as Qwen3.*
