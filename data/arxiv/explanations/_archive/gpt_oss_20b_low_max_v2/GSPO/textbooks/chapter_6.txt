Chapter 6: # 6. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)

## 6.1 Introduction

Reinforcement learning for large language models (LLMs) hinges on an objective that balances exploitation of high‑reward responses with a safeguard against drifting too far from the behaviour policy that generated the data. Classic on‑policy methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) achieve this compromise by clipping token‑level importance ratios derived from the policy’s forward passes during generation. Existing off‑policy approaches for LLMs, most notably Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), also clip at the token level but replace the value‑based advantage with a group‑relative estimate to avoid training an additional value network.

In practice, however, token‐level importance sampling is ill‑suited to autoregressive language generation. The importance ratio of a single next‑token is computed from a single sample of the next‑token distribution and therefore carries very high variance. For long responses—hundreds or thousands of tokens—this variance accumulates, leading to erratic gradient estimates and catastrophic model collapse. GRPO attempts to tame this instability with clipping, but because each token is weighted by its own importance ratio, the clipped signal remains noisy and unreliable.

GSPO addresses these shortcomings by realigning the optimisation units: instead of manipulating individual token log‑probabilities, it normalises sequence likelihoods and applies importance sampling at the sequence level. The entire draft response is treated as one “sample,” and its likelihood ratio becomes the single multiplier that rescales its reward into a policy gradient term. By clipping this ratio rather than each token’s ratio, the algorithm gains a stable, low‑variance estimate that remains faithful to the reward’s natural unit—an entire sequence.

The rest of this chapter dissects each element that constitutes GSPO’s objective: the sequence‑level importance ratio with length normalisation, the group advantage computed over multiple responses to a single query, and the clipping mechanism applied at the response level. We also discuss practical hyperparameter choices, specifically how to set left and right clipping ranges for sequences.

## 6.2 Sequence‐Level Importance Ratio \(s_{i}(\theta)\) and Length Normalisation

Given a query \(x\) and a sampled response \(y_{i}\) drawn from an old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), we define
\[
s_{i}(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i}\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_{i}|},
\]
where \(|y_{i}|\) is the number of tokens in response \(y_{i}\).

The exponent \(1/|y_{i}|\) serves two crucial purposes:

1. **Theoretical Alignment with Importance Sampling**  
   In standard importance sampling, reweighting a single sample \(z\) drawn from \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\) requires multiplying by \(\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\). Here \(z\) is an entire sequence; when we take expectations over responses, we must divide by \(|y_{i}|\) to normalise for fair comparison across sequences of different lengths. The exponent effectively divides log‑likelihood ratios by \(|y_{i}|\), so that longer sequences do not receive disproportionately large or small weights merely by virtue of their length.

2. **Variance Reduction**  
   Without length normalisation, even modest changes in early tokens could exponentially distort \(\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid x)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i}\mid x)\). Dividing by \(|y_{i}|\) attenuates these effects, yielding importance ratios whose magnitudes cluster around unity across diverse response lengths. This mitigates extreme clipping events that would otherwise arise if a few tokens dominated the weight.

Note that an unnormalised ratio
\[
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)} = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|} w_{t,\!i}(\theta)
\]
would explode or vanish exponentially in \(|y_i|\). Length normalisation cushions these extremes while preserving relative comparison between policies across all tokens jointly.

## 6.3 Group Advantage Estimation from Multiple Responses

A hallmark of GRPO was its “group” advantage:
\[
\widehat{A}_{i}= \frac{r(x,y_i)-\overline r}{\sigma_r},
\]
where \(\overline r=\mathbb E[r]\), \(\sigma_r=\mathbb E[(r-\overline r)^2]^{1/2}\), and \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) is an externally supplied verifier score for query–response pair \((x,y)\). GRPO simply reweights each token in response \(y_i\) with this scalar advantage.

GSPO preserves this formulation but clarifies that every token shares **the same** advantage because rewards are allocated per sequence:
- We generate \(G\) responses per query.
- Each response receives a verifier score.
- We compute mean and standard deviation over these scores **within each batch**.
- Standardised deviations serve as advantages for all tokens in that response.

Because every token receives identical weighting outside of its own positional importance ratio (see next section), this group advantage supplies sufficient granularity for most RL tasks while remaining computationally cheap: no separate value network or Monte Carlo rollouts are required.

## 6.4 Clipping Across Entire Responses vs Token‐Wise Clipping

The central novelty lies in where we apply clipping:

### Token-Wise Clipping (PPO / GRPO)

PPO’s objective clips each predicate
\(w_t(\theta)\widehat A_t\).
GRPO does similarly but now multiplies token-wise by
\(w^{{GRPO}}_{t,i}(\theta)=
    {\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{<t})}/{ 
    \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{<t}) }\).
Both methods protect against extreme off‐policy updates at each greedy sample step so that no single new decision can move drastically away from what generated data did.

### Sequence-Wise Clipping (GSPO)

GSPO instead teams up all tokens in a response into a single importance ratio formulated earlier:
\(s_i(\theta)\).
Clipping replaces any such ratio falling outside
\([1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon]\)
by its nearest endpoint:
\[
\mathrm{clip}(s_i,\;1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon)
=
\times~
{\begin{cases}
1-\varepsilon & s_i<1-\varepsilon\\
s_i           & |\,s_i-1\,|\leqslant \varepsilon\\
1+\varepsilon & s_i>1+\varepsilon
}
}
.\]
This has several implications:

- **Uniformity**: Either a whole response gets fully clipped or not; no partial truncation within it.
- **Reduced Noise**: The gradient estimate for each response is multiplied either by its raw “average” weight or by one of two constant values close to 1; erratic token‑level fluctuations are eliminated.
- **Interpretability**: Clipping operates directly on how far downstream policy has departed from old policy over an entire trajectory—exactly what rewards measure.

Because token‑wise ratios can wildly fluctuate due to expert activation changes or early-token mispredictions—especially problematic for Mixture-of-Experts models—sequence-clipping offers robust stability even in sparse architectures.

## 6.5 Hyperparameter Setting: ε Left/Right Ranges for Sequences

The additive–multiplicative asymmetry between current policies manifests as shifted likelihood ratios:
- If larger likelihood is subtracted hypothetically (\(s<1/\!\!>\)), gradients may be dampened adversely.
- If we clip too narrowly around 1 (e.g., \(\varepsilon=0.005\)), many good responses are discarded.
- If we clip too loosely (e.g., \(\varepsilon=0.05\)), unstable off‑policy updates reappear.

Empirically GSPO benefits from asymmetric bounds because posterior probabilities distribution over sequences often deflates slightly:
setting left bound (`ε_l`) lower than right bound (`ε_r`) accommodates mild declines while resisting sharp increases caused by new expert activations or large generation steps.

Example hyperparameters adopted in large MoE models:

| Distribution | Left bound   | Right bound |
|--------------|--------------|-------------|
| Sequence L.R.| $3\times10^{-4}$ | $4\times10^{-4}$ |

These bounds seem remarkably small compared with token‑wise clipping ranges (\([0.2,\;0.27]\)) used in GRPO due to unit differences: with sequence-level ratios typically centered tightly around 1 after length normalisation, even tiny deviations can signify significant policy change across vast generator networks.

During tuning:
- **Left bound** (\(ε_l\)) focuses on penalising policies whose predicted probability mass has contracted beyond acceptable limits on an entire response.
- **Right bound** (\(ε_r\)) guards against exploding probabilities attributable to rare event bursts—a common concern when generating long responses where tail probabilities behave wildly.

When training MoE models—or any sparsely activated architecture—it is advisable to monitor expert activation volatility: if observed drops in performance coincide with increases in clipped fractions under interior bounds, consider tightening `ε_l`. Conversely, if many high‑reward sequences are discarded due to right boundary violations, move `ε_r` closer to `ε_l` until more responses survive clipping without destabilising downstream gradients.

## 6.6 Practical Example

Consider two responses generated under old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ having lengths 10 and 20 tokens respectively:

| Response | Length (\(|y|\)) | Old Likelihood |\_\(_{\text{old}}\)| New Likelihood |\_\(_{\theta}\)| Raw Ratio |
|----------|------------------|-----------------|-------------------|--------------|
| A        | 10               | $2\times10^{-9}$ | $4\times10^{-9}$   | $\approx$ 2 |
| B        | 20               | $3\times10^{-18}$ | $6\times10^{-18}$ | $\approx$ 2 |

The naive unnormalised ratio would scale both responses equally (~2). However suppose new policy improves early tokens drastically but hurts late tokens: raw ratio might inflate excessively altering relative balance across partial sub-trajectories.

Length normalisation calculates following:

For A:
\( s_A = (2)^{\,1/10}= e^{(\ln 2)/10}\approx e^{0.0693}\approx 1.072 .\)

For B:
\( s_B = (2)^{\,1/20}= e^{(\ln 2)/20}\approx e^{0.0347}\approx 1.035 .\)

Hence after normalisation both lie comfortably within [0.9998 – 1.0008] under typical $\epsilon$ range like [−0.001 , +0.001], ensuring neither will be unnecessarily clipped while still representing true improvement.

## 6.7 Summary

Group Sequence Policy Optimization innovates RL training for LLMs by harnessing the familiar bootstrap machinery of PPO—clipping importance weights—but rephrasing everything at the natural granularity of full responses:

- **Sequence‑level importance ratios** capture how far new policy deviates from old policy on entire drafts, normalized to control variance over arbitrary lengths.
- **Group advantages** derived from multiple sampled responses aggregate subjective verification signals into a clean standardised scalar per generation.
- **Sequence-wide clipping** removes per-token volatility introduced by sparse activation dynamics or early-token noise.
- **Carefully tuned asymmetrical $\epsilon$ ranges** provide strong guarantees of stability without discarding useful gradients.

By aligning objectives directly with how rewards are measured and how policies produce data, GSPO unlocks more robust learning dynamics: it thrives even for trillion‑parameter Mixture-of-Experts models where earlier objectives collapse under graduate scaling demands—a key reason behind recent strides in Qwen 3 family performance.^
